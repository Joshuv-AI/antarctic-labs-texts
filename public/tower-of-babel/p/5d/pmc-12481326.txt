Body mass index-related participation limitations in daily life activities among survivors of childhood cancer: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Müberra Tanrıverdi, Sedef Sahin, Kardelen Yıldırım, Guleser Guney Yılmaz, Ceren Davutoglu, Fatma Betul Cakir, Özgün Kaya Kara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study compared participation limitations in daily life activities among childhood cancer survivors across different Body Mass Index (BMI) categories.DesignProspective cross-sectional study.SettingThe study data were collected from two separate oncology outpatient clinics.Participants116 children with cancer aged 10.07±3.83 were included in the study. The mean follow-up time after cancer treatment turnover was 36.28±6.43 months.Primary outcome measuresParticipation levels were assessed using the Pediatric Outcomes Data Collection Instrument (PODCI) as the primary outcome of this study, and they were categorised by BMI.ResultsThe mean z score was 0.64 ± 1.12, and the average BMI was 17.76±3.56. The PODCI global functioning score averaged 80.25±15.23. There were no significant associations between the global functioning score and age, chemotherapy, or surgery (p>0.05). However, significant relationships were found between the global functioning score and gender, BMI, and radiotherapy (p<0.05). Significant functional differences were observed in the upper extremity, basic mobility transfer, happiness and overall functioning (p<0.05). No significant differences were noted in sports and physical functioning or pain/comfort across different BMI categories (underweight, normal/healthy, overweight) (p>0.05).ConclusionsSurvivors experience participation restrictions in daily living activities after completing treatment. Systematic evaluation of feedback received from patients/parents regarding physical function, sports, transfer and participation highlights the importance of BMI in childhood cancer survivors. Addressing these factors is key to achieving better health-related quality of life in functionality and participatory outcomes for survivors.

Introduction

As a positive outcome of improved treatment modalities, the number of individuals who survive from childhood cancer is increasing.1 Normal range of motion, muscle strength, muscle tone, flexibility and coordination are essential for maintaining physical function for children with cancer.2 Unfortunately, childhood cancer survivor (CCS) patients are often at risk of long-term adverse effects such as cardiovascular, neurological, musculoskeletal, pulmonary complications and insulin resistance. The most common situation they face concerning with these adverse effects is physical dysfunction.3 4 In addition, it is noted that approximately half of adults with CCS are particularly prone to weight-related problems due to low levels of physical activity.5

It is reported that the effects of chronic diseases such as cancer may persist throughout life. When we examine these effects in terms of physical activity and weight-related problems, for example, the prevalence of obesity (defined as Body Mass Index (BMI) >30 kg/m²) in CCS is 36%, while in the matched general population in terms of age, gender and race, this rate is 31.6%. However, the prevalence among childhood acute lymphoblastic leukaemia survivors was found to be as high as 43%.6 Leukaemia survivors with early diagnosis age, female gender and cranial radiation therapy are reported to be at risk for conditions such as overweight or obesity in their future adult lives.712 In contrast, survivors of Hodgkin’s disease and Wilms tumour are reported to be at risk of being underweight.13 For brain tumour survivors, previous studies have yielded conflicting results regarding the risk of being underweight or overweight. However, BMI is similar to that of the general population.10 14 15

Weight abnormalities in survivors may cause problems with participation in daily life activities during childhood and adulthood.16 These effects increase the risk of decreased physical performance, activity limitations and participation limitations in childhood cancer survivors, which may negatively affect their participation in daily life.1620 For example, metabolic syndrome, characterised by abdominal obesity, dyslipidaemia and insulin resistance, is a significant health concern among CCS and can negatively affect their daily functioning. It is also known that physical fitness and endurance decrease regardless of the period of discharge from the hospital21 and BMI abnormalities may increase the risk of physical performance, activity limitation and participation restriction in childhood cancer survivors; thus, these risks may lead to adverse effects on participation in daily life.16 17 It has been stated that the most effective intervention for CCS with low and high BMI is a follow-up process that includes lifestyle changes combining diet, physical activity and behavioural strategies. This is considered the most effective approach to managing this condition. Such interventions improve metabolic health, supporting physical performance and participation in daily life activities.2023

CCS are at risk for weight-related issues, including both obesity and underweight conditions, which can significantly impact their physical function, activity participation and overall quality of life. While previous studies have explored BMI-related health risks in CCS,6 there is limited evidence on how BMI influences participation in daily activities. Since participation is a key component of functional independence and well-being, understanding its relationship with BMI is crucial for developing targeted interventions. For our purpose, participation in daily life, including upper extremity, transfer, mobility, sports, physical function, pain/comfort, happiness and general functional status in childhood cancer survivors, was evaluated according to BMI Z scores.

Methods

Patient and public involvement

This cross-sectional study received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the University, Local Ethics Committee (approval ID: E-54022451-050.05.04-43888). Researchers verbally apprised children and their parents prior to the commencement of the study. Then, individuals who voluntarily consented to participate were enrolled in the study. All participants provided written consent to the principles stated in the Helsinki Declaration.

Participant sample

The present study was conducted at two paediatric oncology outpatient clinics. Three to 17-year-old children diagnosed with cancer and treated at the outpatient clinics were included in this cross-sectional study. Families were informed about the study through phone calls and in-person consultations during routine outpatient visits. Those interested were provided detailed information about the study’s purpose, procedures and potential benefits. After obtaining verbal consent, a scheduled appointment was arranged for further evaluation. Participants were then assessed at the outpatient clinics following standardised procedures. Affect the child’s participation in daily living activities, such as severe impairments in cardiovascular, pulmonary, cognitive, emotional, neurologic or locomotor systems, nutrition-related health problems (obesity, underweight, etc) and the inability to cooperate were excluded from the study. Children outside the 3rd–97th percentile were not included in the study.

Measures

The children’s demographic characteristics (age, gender, body weight, height, BMI, Z scores and percentiles) were recorded. The researchers administered the assessments and related measurements. Medical data (cancer subtype, treatment status) were obtained from the hospital records.

The Pediatric Data Collection Instrument (PODCI) was used to evaluate participation in CSS.24 The PODCI consists of five main subsections about participation in daily living activities: upper extremity functions, transfer and basic mobility, sports and physical function, pain/comfort, and happiness. While four of the six parameters included in this tool (upper extremity function, transfers and basic mobility, sports and physical function, pain/comfort) are related to functionality, one parameter gives the happiness score, and the last gives the overall functionality score. The scores vary from zero to 100 points, and higher scores indicate a better participation level. The percentage of the scores assessed in children compared with the age and sex-matched normative was calculated. The PODCI has good reliability and validity to determine activity and participation limitations in children with different diagnoses.2527 The PODCI survey was administered in two ways, depending on the participant’s age. For children between 2 and 10 years, the parent completed the questionnaire. Adolescents between 11 and 18 years completed the questionnaire independently.28

The scores were compared with the instrument developers’ normative data previously collected and standardised. The average normalised score for each functional assessment is 50±10. Any participant with a normalised score more than one SD below the average (<40) is considered to have a significant impairment in that function. Z scores and percentile values were calculated with the relevant formula: Z score = (measured value – median value for age and gender) / SD for age and gender.29

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Statistical Program for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27. The significance level was set at 0.05 for all statistical analyses, and the results are expressed as a percentage (%) or mean±SD with minimum and maximum range. The sample size participating in the study was calculated according to the results obtained from the study, which used PODCI in childhood cancer Yıldız Kabak et al in the literature using the G* Power programme. Accordingly, 110 participants should be included in the study with a 95% CI and 5% error margin. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to determine the normality of the distribution. Continuous variables showing normal distribution were shown as mean and SD; variables not showing normal distribution were shown as median and interval of quartiles (IQR). Nominal variables were presented as numbers and percentages. BMI values were converted to age-adjusted and sex-adjusted Z scores using the WHO growth standards, and participants were classified into underweight, normal weight and overweight/obese groups based on these Z scores. Intergroup comparisons of Body Mass Index (underweight, normal/healthy, overweight) and PODCI scores were calculated by Analysis of Variance(ANOVA) and the Kruskal-Wallis H test. The χ² test was used to compare nominal variables between groups. The Pearson correlation coefficient test assessed associations between the outcome measures.

Results

Out of 192 potential patients who met the inclusion criteria, 150 patients were available at the standardised time for testing and were therefore asked to participate. 26 patients declined to participate due to insufficient time and transfer problems. Two participants were excluded from the study because they were below the third percentile, and six were above the 97th percentile. 116 (68 boys; 48 girls) children with cancer, with a mean age of 10.07±3.83, were enrolled in the study (figure 1).

Discussion

This study evaluated daily life participation in CCS according to BMI in terms of upper extremity, transfer, mobility, sports, physical function, pain/comfort, happiness and overall function. All PODCI scores were found to be lower than the normative values. Specifically, it was determined that scores for sports, physical function and pain/comfort were more affected than other PODCI subscales in participation in daily living activities. Children’s participation differed according to BMI, except for the PODCI subscales ‘Sports and Physical Function’ and ‘Pain/Comfort.’ Body composition correlates with all PODCI participation parameters except ‘Pain/Comfort.’

BMI is a suitable parameter for children older than 2 years of age that shows the child’s nutritional status.30 Abnormal BMI values affect physical functions negatively by causing both muscular and mental problems. According to our study’s results, most participants were in the not normal/healthy group according to their BMI values. Being obese, African American ethnicity, and being older than 11 years are limited but significant risk factors for functional impairment.31

In childhood cancer, gender can have various effects on children’s physical characteristics and functional levels.32 Boys may experience more noticeable muscle strength loss and growth delays, while girls face a higher risk of bone density loss and early or delayed puberty. Cancer treatment can negatively impact physical endurance, daily functioning and social interactions in both genders. Additionally, girls may experience more social anxiety due to physical changes, while boys might face challenges in social relationships due to restrictions on physical activity. Therefore, it is crucial to consider gender-specific effects during treatment and support both the physical and psychosocial development of children. Survivors with abnormal Body Mass Index have participation restrictions in the earlier period of diagnosis.33 In the current study, the underweight group demonstrated slightly higher PODCI scores than the normal weight group. Although this finding was unexpected, none of the participants reached the maximum possible score in any subdomain, indicating the presence of participation limitations across all BMI groups. This result may be attributed to individual differences in how survivors perceive and adapt to their physical capacities, daily routines and social expectations. It also highlights the complexity of participation outcomes, which may not always correspond directly with body composition. Additionally, in our study, although the study population had just completed their treatment, their physical activity participation was not found to be as healthy as possible. For this reason, it should be kept in mind that being underweight can create serious risks and limitations in participation in their future lifestyles. Meacham et al reported that the prevalence of low weight in CCS increased.13 It was found that most of the CCSs in our study were underweight.

In our study, we found that the functional scores of children with cancer, as assessed by the PODCI, were lower than the normative values, indicating that these children experienced participation restrictions. Similar to the literature source we drew inspiration from, our study by Yildiz Kabak et al also shows that PODCI can assess physical function and participation in children with cancer.34 35 It has also been used to evaluate children’s physical activity levels during and after cancer treatment, helping to encourage greater physical activity. Analysis revealed that PODCI scores were significantly lower in adolescents who had completed their cancer treatment more recently compared with those who had finished treatment a longer time ago.36 This suggests that health-related physical fitness is impaired in survivors of childhood cancer, with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) survivors particularly affected. While the type of cancer may influence changes in physical fitness, information on survivors of other paediatric malignancies and related risk factors is limited.37 In our study, we included patients with three different cancer types: brain tumours (65.5%), lymphoma (18.9%) and acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (15.5%). Notably, patients with brain tumours had significantly lower PODCI scores compared with those with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia. These findings suggest that cancer type may affect participation levels, likely due to differences in disease characteristics, treatment regimens and survivorship outcomes.

There could be other reasons for participation restrictions, such as fatigue, pain, depression and general health, which have often been described as barriers to physical activity for CCS. Most of them have not reached recommended physical activity levels. In a study investigating the relationship of fatigue, physical function/mobility, BMI and depression in childhood cancer survivors after around 6 years off treatment, while scores of fatigue, BMI and depression were improved after years, the physical function and mobility scores remained low.38 In the results of our study, a moderate relationship is found between the PODCI ‘sports and physical functioning’ score and ‘happiness’ scores. Treating the symptoms of depression might improve physical activity. On the other hand, CCS experiences late outcomes that affect physical activity and functionality. Smith et al found that adult survivors of childhood cancer self-reported 14.1% limitations in their physical performance.39 In addition, being physically active during hospitalisation increases physical performance participation after treatment.40 However, it is clinically reported that patients with childhood cancer experience weakness, muscle wasting, peripheral neuropathy, weak muscle endurance, and impaired flexibility and functional mobility.41 In an adult survivors of childhood study, participation in sport values was significantly lower than those of the healthy controls, but their BMI scores were within normal/healthy values.42 It is stated that it is important to examine the long-term health outcomes of oncologic paediatric treatments since more than 4/5 of children treated for childhood cancer are expected to survive.20 For this reason, we indicate that our study is instructive in examining the participation in daily living activities in childhood cancer survivors.

Conclusion

This study highlights that CCS face significant restrictions in their participation in daily living activities, as evidenced by their lower scores on the PODCI compared with normative values. In particular, sports, physical functioning and pain/comfort parameters are the most affected subdomains. BMI correlated with participation in most areas, emphasising its role in physical functionality and overall well-being. The majority of participants in this study were underweight, further underscoring the need to address nutritional status during and after treatment.

Cancer type was also a determining factor, with survivors of brain tumours showing greater participation limitations than those with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia. This suggests that the effects of cancer type, treatment modalities and survivorship processes may influence long-term functionality; however, the present study did not specifically assess variables such as cranial irradiation dose, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), or limb surgery, which may also have a significant impact.

Given the enduring impacts of cancer and its treatments on physical functionality and participation, comprehensive rehabilitation strategies targeting physical activity, nutritional support and psychological well-being are essential. Early intervention and long-term monitoring are critical for improving participation outcomes and enhancing the quality of life in this population. This study addresses these multidimensional factors to promote better functional and participatory outcomes in childhood cancer survivors.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its cross-sectional design captures only one point post-treatment (approximately 36 months), limiting the ability to observe longitudinal changes or potential participant recovery trends. Second, although using PODCI normative values allowed for comparison with standard reference data, the absence of a contemporaneous healthy control group restricts contextual interpretation, particularly regarding sociocultural factors influencing participation. Third, the reliance on self-reported or parent-reported PODCI scores may introduce reporting bias, as older children may tend to present themselves more favourably, while parents may overstate impairments. Although PODCI is a valuable instrument encompassing physical, social and psychological dimensions, the multifactorial nature of participation suggests the need for complementary objective functional measures (eg, 6 min walk test, grip strength) to strengthen validity. Fourth, the study included only survivors of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, brain tumours and Hodgkin’s lymphoma, based on available follow-up data at our centre; this limits generalisability to other cancer types such as Wilms tumour or non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, whose treatment regimens and late effect profiles differ substantially. Fifth, potentially important confounding variables—such as fatigue, depression, socioeconomic status, cognitive impairments, family support and school reintegration—were not quantitatively measured or analysed. Finally, while some participants were overweight, no obese individuals (BMI >30 kg/m²) were included, limiting conclusions about the full spectrum of BMI-related effects. Despite these limitations, to our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate participation levels according to body composition in childhood cancer survivors, addressing multiple dimensions of participation. Future longitudinal studies should examine how body composition and participation evolve concerning treatment exposures and survivorship care.

Future directions

The current findings of this study highlight various aspects for future research on the CSS population. The following directions are proposed for future studies: longitudinal follow-up studies should be planned to monitor functional and participation outcomes at multiple turning points after treatment (eg, 1 year, 3 years, 5 years) to shed light on recovery patterns or emerging deficits. Objective functional measures such as timed up-and-go, gait analysis and strength testing should be integrated to validate or complement the self-reported PODCI findings. To enhance the generalisability of the findings, future studies should include CSS with a broader and more diverse range of cancer types (eg, Wilms tumour, neuroblastoma, non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma) to assess whether BMI and participation relationships are consistent across diagnostic groups. In addition to functional findings related to participation, psychosocial variables such as fatigue, mood, family support and cognitive functions, including attention, memory and orientation, should be quantitatively measured to evaluate their independent or synergistic contributions to participation. In CCS, an interdisciplinary team should design and implement diagnosis-specific targeted intervention programmes (eg, occupational therapist, physiotherapist, nutritionist and psychological counsellor). Future research should include individuals representing a wider range of BMI categories, namely underweight, normal/healthy and overweight, to understand how the BMI gradient affects functionality. In addition to quantitative research, conducting interviews or focus group meetings with CCS and their families would allow for a more detailed exploration of lived experiences, meanings, perceptions and barriers to participation that are not captured through quantitative methods.
