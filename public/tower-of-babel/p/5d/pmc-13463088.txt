A dynamic nomogram for predicting the need for invasive interventions in patients with tubo-ovarian abscess
Authors: Suqiong Xu, Xianqian Chen, Jingjing Cai, Yi Chen, Xiuxia Chen, Zhicong Wu, Saihua Zheng, Zhenhong Wang, Limin Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTimely identification of tubo-ovarian abscess (TOA) patients who will fail antibiotic therapy is crucial for guiding early intervention. This study aimed to develop and validate a dynamic nomogram to predict the need for invasive intervention in TOA patients. In this retrospective cohort study of 167 TOA patients from a single center, multivariate logistic regression identified four independent predictors of antibiotic treatment failure: fever (OR = 3.117, 95% CI: 1.408–6.901), elevated C-reactive protein (OR = 1.007, 95% CI: 1.001–1.013), larger lesion diameter (OR = 1.349, 95% CI: 1.145–1.591), and poor ultrasonic transmission within the lesion (OR = 7.254, 95% CI: 2.943–17.881). A nomogram integrating these factors demonstrated good predictive performance, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.844 (95% CI: 0.785–0.903). The model showed good calibration and clinical utility in decision curve analysis. An online calculator was developed to facilitate clinical use. Introduction

Tubo-ovarian abscess (TOA), a severe complication of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), primarily affects women of reproductive age and has an incidence rate ranging from 15% to 35%1,2. The etiology of TOA is multifactorial, and its diverse clinical manifestations often contribute to delays in diagnosis and treatment. In the absence of timely intervention, the condition may rapidly progress to complications such as abscess rupture, diffuse peritonitis, or even septic shock. Moreover, TOA can result in long-term sequelae, including infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and chronic pelvic pain, significantly impairing patients’ reproductive health and quality of life1–5.

Current treatment strategies for TOA primarily involve antibiotic therapy6,7, and invasive interventions, such as percutaneous drainage or surgical procedures8–11. Although antibiotic therapy constitutes the cornerstone of initial management and is generally preferred, its efficacy varies widely, ranging from 34% to 88%6,7, with certain patients demonstrating a suboptimal response to pharmacological treatment alone and ultimately requiring invasive measures. Therefore, the timely identification of high-risk patients with antibiotic resistance is critical for optimizing personalized clinical management in TOA. Previous studies have identified several risk factors associated with antibiotic treatment failure in TOA patients, such as body mass index, leukocyte count, cancer antigen 125 (CA125), procalcitonin, abscess size, bilateral abscess formation, and prior surgical history12–16; however, these factors are not yet fully elucidated, warranting further investigation to identify additional clinically meaningful factors.

Therefore, this study aimed to analyze risk factors associated with antibiotic treatment failure in patients with TOA and to develop a clinical prediction model for identifying high-risk patients who may require invasive interventions, thereby facilitating individualized treatment decisions.

Materials and methods

Study population

Patients diagnosed with TOA who initially received antibiotic therapy at the First Hospital of Putian between January 2014 and November 2025 were included in this retrospective cohort study. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 2024 − 203) of the First Hospital of Putian and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective nature of the study. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) meeting the diagnostic criteria for PID as defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention17; (2) a confirmed diagnosis of TOA via ultrasound imaging; (3) age ≥ 18 years; (4) non-pregnant status; and (5) receipt of an antibiotic treatment regimen consistent with current clinical guidelines17. Exclusion criteria included: (1) discharge against medical advice or premature discontinuation of treatment; (2) abscess rupture upon admission requiring immediate surgical intervention; (3) secondary pelvic abscess resulting from acute suppurative appendicitis or other non-gynecological conditions; and (4) incomplete study data.

Patient management

All patients received standardized intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics, typically a combination of a third-generation cephalosporin and doxycycline. Traditional Chinese medicine was used as adjunctive therapy on an individual basis but was not standardized across all patients.Given the 12-year retrospective study period (January 2014 to November 2025), specific antibiotic doses and Traditional Chinese medicine formulations were not consistently documented. Antibiotic treatment failure was defined as the absence of significant improvement in body temperature or inflammatory markers, or no relief of clinical symptoms (such as fever and abdominal pain) after 48 to 72 h of empirical intravenous antibiotic therapy. According to guidelines from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and current clinical standards, the indications for invasive intervention, including ultrasound-guided drainage, posterior fornix puncture, open surgery, or laparoscopic surgery were as follows: (1) persistent fever lasting > 48 h despite appropriate antibiotic therapy; (2) worsening abdominal pain or tenderness; (3) increase in abscess diameter on follow-up ultrasound; (4) signs suggestive of impending abscess rupture (e.g., localized peritonitis, increasing abdominal distension, or sonographic thinning of the abscess wall). Based on whether invasive intervention was required, patients were divided into two groups: the invasive group and the non-invasive group.

Data collection

Variables potentially associated with antibiotic treatment failure in patients with TOA were collected upon admission and categorized as follows: (1) clinical and demographic variables: age, fever (axillary temperature ≥ 37.5 °C), number of pregnancies, previous antibiotic therapy, and history of abortion; (2) laboratory indicators: leukocyte count, absolute neutrophil count, absolute lymphocyte count, C-reactive protein (CRP), and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR); and (3) ultrasonographic findings: laterality of the lesion, lesion diameter (maximum transverse diameter), lesion morphology, and internal echogenicity of the lesion. Transvaginal ultrasound served as the primary imaging modality; transabdominal ultrasound was performed adjunctively when the lesion exceeded the field of view of the vaginal probe, extended into the upper abdomen, or when transvaginal ultrasound was contraindicated or poorly tolerated. All ultrasound examinations adhered to a standardized acquisition and reporting protocol. Images were independently interpreted by two board-certified sonographers, each with over 10 years of dedicated gynecologic ultrasound experience, who were blinded to other clinical data. “Poor internal echogenicity” was defined as the presence of homogeneous or heterogeneous punctate low-level echoes within the abscess cavity, accompanied by reduced acoustic transmission and absence of posterior acoustic enhancement, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1. Diagnostic discrepancies were resolved through consensus review.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R (version 4.5.1; https://www.r-project.org/). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were reported as mean ± SD and analyzed using the t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were reported as median (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and analyzed using the chi-square test.

Univariate logistic regression identified variables with P < 0.05, which were then included in a multivariate model using backward stepwise elimination (with a retention criterion of P < 0.05) to determine independent risk factors for antibiotic treatment failure in TOA. Optimal cut-off values were determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and the Youden index, with corresponding sensitivity and specificity calculated. Restricted cubic spline analysis was used to assess the nonlinear association between continuous predictors and the requirement for invasive intervention. A nomogram for predicting the need for invasive intervention was developed based on the final multivariate logistic regression model and deployed as an online risk calculator. The nomogram’s discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility were evaluated using the ROC curve, calibration curve (assessed with the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and the Brier score), and decision curve analysis (DCA), respectively. Internal validation of the nomogram was performed using 1000 bootstrap resamples to calculate optimism-corrected AUCs. The areas under the ROC curves (AUCs) of the two models were compared using the DeLong test. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 167 patients with TOA were included in this study (Supplementary Fig. S2). Of these, 83 (49.7%) received non-invasive management and were clinically cured and discharged following antibiotic therapy, while 84 (50.3%) underwent invasive intervention, including 25 laparoscopic surgeries; the remainder received percutaneous drainage or posterior fornix puncture. Risk factors of antibiotic treatment failure

Univariate logistic regression analysis of 14 variables revealed significant associations between antibiotic treatment failure and several risk factors, including fever, leukocyte count, neutrophil count, CRP, NLR, lesion diameter, and poor internal echogenicity of the lesion (all P < 0.05). In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, these factors were reduced to four independent risk factors for antibiotic treatment failure: fever (odds ratio [OR] = 3.117, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.408–6.901, P = 0.005), CRP (OR = 1.007, 95% CI: 1.001–1.013, P = 0.037), lesion diameter (OR = 1.349, 95% CI: 1.145–1.591, P < 0.001), and poor internal echogenicity of the lesion (OR = 7.254, 95% CI: 2.943–17.881, P < 0.001) (Table 2). The adjusted OR for CRP was 1.007, indicating a 0.7% increase in the odds of requiring invasive intervention per 1 mg/L rise; to contextualize this in clinical practice, the odds increase by approximately 7% for every 10 mg/L rise in CRP level.

Restricted cubic spline analysis revealed distinct association patterns between two continuous predictors and the need for invasive intervention (Supplementary Fig. S3). A statistically significant nonlinear association was observed between lesion diameter (reference value: 7.2 cm) and the outcome (overall P < 0.001; nonlinearity P < 0.001). In contrast, CRP level (reference value: 66.2 mg/L) exhibited a highly significant overall association with the outcome (overall P < 0.001), but the test for nonlinearity did not reach statistical significance (nonlinearity P = 0.096).

Performance of independent risk factors for predicting invasive intervention

The comparative analysis of independent risk factors for predicting invasive intervention in patients with TOA is presented in Table 3. Lesion diameter demonstrated the highest discriminative ability, with an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.756 (95% CI: 0.681–0.830), sensitivity of 73.8%, and specificity of 68.7% at an optimal cut-off value of 6.75 cm. CRP and fever showed moderate predictive performance, with AUC values of 0.695 (95% CI: 0.616–0.775) and 0.671 (95% CI: 0.600–0.740), respectively; the optimal cut-off for CRP was 67.15 mg/L. In contrast, poor internal echogenicity of the lesion exhibited the lowest sensitivity (46.4%) but the highest specificity (89.2%), with an AUC of 0.678 (95% CI: 0.610–0.740), indicating it is a highly specific but less sensitive predictive factor.

Construction and performance of prediction models

Based on the results of the multivariate analysis, two prediction models (with and without poor internal echogenicity of the lesion) were developed to predict the likelihood of invasive intervention following antibiotic treatment failure in patients with TOA. Compared to the basic model based on previously reported factors (fever, CRP, and lesion diameter), adding poor internal echogenicity of the lesion to the model (full model) significantly improved discriminative performance (AUC, 0.844 [95% CI: 0.785–0.903] vs. 0.796 [95% CI: 0.728–0.864]) (P = 0.027) (Fig. 1). At their respective optimal probability thresholds (full model: 0.489; basic model: 0.379), the full model demonstrated significantly higher specificity (78.3%; 95% CI: 69.9–86.7%) compared with the basic model (60.2%; 95% CI: 49.4–71.1%), albeit with a modest decrease in sensitivity (78.6%; 95% CI: 70.2–86.9% vs. 86.9%; 95% CI: 79.8–94.0%).

Comparison of discriminative performance between the basic model and the full model. AUC = area under the receiver operating characteristic curve; CI = confidence interval.

Using the full model, a nomogram was constructed to facilitate individualized risk prediction (Fig. 2). After internal validation with 1000 bootstrap resamples, the nomogram yielded an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.829 (95% CI: 0.827–0.845), with a sensitivity of 78.6% and a specificity of 78.3% (Fig. 3). Calibration analysis revealed good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities of invasive intervention (Fig. 4A). The calibration slope was 1.00, the calibration intercept was 0.00, and the Brier score was 0.160, indicating both accurate probability calibration and good overall predictive accuracy. These findings were corroborated by the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (chi-square value of 3.87, P = 0.869). Apparent DCA indicated that the nomogram provides favorable clinical utility across a wide range of threshold probabilities, outperforming the strategies of treating all patients or none (Fig. 4B).

Web deployment of nomogram

To enhance clinical utility, the nomogram was converted into a dynamic format and deployed on the shinyapps.io platform (https://fuslon.shinyapps.io/XU-TOA) to predict the need for invasive intervention in individuals with TOA. The tool allows users to input the four predictive variables into designated fields using a computer or mobile device. After submission, the system processes the data and generates a result; the output page displays the estimated probability of invasive intervention, along with the 95% CI and model parameters.

Discussion

This study identified risk factors associated with antibiotic treatment failure in patients with TOA and constructed a dynamic nomogram to predict the need for invasive interventions. In our cohort, 50.3% of patients ultimately required invasive interventions, a proportion consistent with previously reported rates of invasive intervention requirements18. Specifically, fever, elevated CRP, larger lesion diameter, and poor internal echogenicity of the lesion were identified as potential key risk factors for antibiotic treatment failure, offering a basis for early risk stratification in TOA patients. The web-based risk calculator derived from these predictors further enhances the clinical applicability of the nomogram in routine practice.

TOA is most commonly caused by ascending infections of the genital tract13,19. Fever and elevated CRP levels are key clinical indicators of the severity of systemic inflammation and the potential need for invasive intervention15,20. Fever, as a systemic response to inadequately controlled infection, often reflects insufficient antimicrobial suppression of pathogen-driven inflammation. As reported by Brahma et al.15, the prevalence of fever was significantly higher in patients undergoing invasive management compared to those managed non-invasively, suggesting more extensive systemic involvement and frequently larger or more complex abscesses. Consistent with these findings, our study observed fever in 61.9% of patients receiving invasive intervention, compared to 27.71% in the non-invasive group, further supporting the association between fever and the need for invasive procedures. Similarly, CRP—a sensitive and specific acute-phase reactant16—closely reflects the extent of tissue injury and infectious burden. Ribak et al.21 emphasized that elevated CRP levels are significantly associated with antibiotic treatment failure in TOA. Higher CRP levels, which reflect a more intense systemic inflammatory response, were associated with an increased likelihood of antibiotic treatment failure. Although the effect size of CRP (OR = 1.007 per 1 mg/L increase) is modest, CRP may serve as a useful adjunctive marker to identify patients at higher risk of requiring invasive intervention when interpreted alongside other clinical and imaging predictors. The concurrent presence of fever and elevated CRP generally signifies poorly controlled infection and a pronounced inflammatory response.

This study identified poor internal echogenicity of the lesion and lesion diameter as significant imaging predictors of the need for invasive intervention in TOA. TOA typically presents as a heterogeneous structure composed of necrotic tissue, organized debris, and viscous pus21–23, which appears on ultrasound as reduced translucency with dense punctate echoes24. These sonographic features reflect high pus viscosity and extensive tissue organization, factors that not only impede spontaneous abscess resolution but also limit antibiotic penetration into the abscess core. Moreover, abscess diameter is a critical determinant of treatment strategy: larger lesions are associated with decreased efficiency of antibiotic diffusion within the cavity and a higher risk of conservative treatment failure12,14,15. The study by Dewitt et al.18 reported that a lesion diameter exceeding 8 cm is associated with prolonged hospitalization and a conservative management failure rate as high as 43%, thereby supporting our finding that increased abscess size elevates the likelihood of requiring invasive intervention. Collectively, poor internal echogenicity and larger lesion diameter indicate an advanced infectious state characterized by marked tissue organization and diminished responsiveness to antimicrobial therapy, often necessitating invasive management. Therefore, these two variables are essential components in developing a predictive model for clinical decision-making in TOA.

This study developed and compared two predictive models to identify high-risk patients with TOA who may require invasive intervention following antibiotic therapy, and evaluated the predictive value of the sonographic feature “poor internal echogenicity of the lesion.” The full model, which incorporated this feature in addition to conventional predictors, demonstrated significantly superior discriminative performance compared to the basic model that excluded it. Decision curve analysis revealed that the full model provided a greater net clinical benefit than both the basic model and the strategies of intervening in all or no patients. Calibration curves further indicated excellent agreement between the predicted probabilities from the full model and the observed outcomes, confirming the reliability of its predictions. A dynamic nomogram derived from the full model, integrating clinical, laboratory, and imaging variables, represents a valuable tool to support individualized treatment planning in TOA management.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective cohort study, it is susceptible to selection bias, and treatment protocols were not standardized over the 12-year period. Important predictive factors for TOA outcomes, such as CA125 and body mass index, were also not analyzed12,14. Second, the assessment of the ultrasound feature “poor internal echogenicity” remains subjective. Although images were reviewed independently using predefined criteria, the retrospective design precluded a formal evaluation of interobserver agreement. Third, the developed nomogram has an events-per-variable ratio of 21, nearing the lower acceptable limit and raising potential overfitting concerns. While internal validation via bootstrapping was performed, external validation in larger prospective cohorts is essential. Finally, the lack of systematic follow-up data limited the evaluation of long-term outcomes by treatment strategy, and the sample size, though adequate for model development, was insufficient for robust external validation. Future multicenter prospective studies are needed to confirm the model’s clinical utility.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified four independent risk factors associated with the need for invasive intervention in patients with TOA following antibiotic treatment failure. The full model, which included poor internal echogenicity of the lesion, demonstrated significantly improved discriminatory power compared to the basic model that only included fever, CRP, and lesion diameter. The dynamic nomogram constructed based on the full model offers clinical convenience and exhibits good calibration performance and potential applicability, which may aid in individualized treatment decisions for TOA patients. However, multi-center external validation studies are still required to confirm its predictive stability and generalizability before this nomogram can be widely adopted in clinical practice.
