Incidence of RSV-Acute Respiratory Infection in Adults 18–64 Years With High-Risk Conditions: A Prospective Community-based Surveillance Study
Authors: Wendelyn Bosch, Katherine S King, Chung-Il Wi, David Watson, Colin L Colby, Traci L Natoli, Brandon H Hidaka, Randy Foss, Lisa J Speiser, Pouya Saeedi, Jean-Yves Pirçon, Andrew G Allmon, Paul Y Takahashi, Robert J Pignolo, Young J Juhn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundData on respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) acute respiratory infection (ARI) among community-dwelling U.S. adults aged 18 to 64 years, particularly those with high-risk conditions (HRC), remain limited. This study estimated incidence and attack rates of RSV-ARI and RSV lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD) in adults with and without HRCs.MethodsA multistate, community-based prospective cohort of adults aged 18 to 64 years with and without HRCs was followed from October 2022 to September 2024. Incidence rates and annual attack rates of RSV-ARI and RSV-LRTD were calculated by HRC presence and age group.ResultsOf 3375 adults with at least 1 HRC, the RSV-ARI incidence rate was 17.9 per 1000 person-years (attack rates: year 1, 1.87%; year 2, 1.56%), compared with 14.8 per 1000 person-years (year 1, 1.33%; year 2, 1.50%) among adults without HRCs. RSV-LRTD incidence was 10.0 per 1000 person-years in adults with HRCs versus 5.7 per 1000 person-years in those without. Asthma, congenital immunodeficiency, and immunosuppressive medication use were associated with increased incidence of RSV-ARI and RSV-LRTD; whereas adults with solid organ transplant and chronic kidney disease had a significantly higher incidence rate of RSV-LRTD compared to those without these conditions. Adults aged 18 to 49 years with HRCs had significantly higher RSV-LRTD incidence (9.6 vs 4.3 per 1000 person-years). After adjustment for age and sex, having ≥1 HRC was associated with a 62% increased RSV-LRTD risk, rising to 117% among adults aged 18 to 49 years.ConclusionsAdults with HRCs—particularly asthma, congenital immunodeficiency, chronic kidney disease, immunosuppressive medication use, and solid organ transplant—experienced higher RSV-ARI and RSV-LRTD incidence. The elevated RSV-LRTD burden was most pronounced in adults aged 18 to 49 years with HRCs.

In U.S. adults ≥50 years, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) accounts up to 12% of medically attended acute respiratory infections (ARI) with an estimated 6% to 8% in-hospital mortality and the third most common viral ARI etiology in hospitalized patients >65 years [1–3]. Persons who are immunocompromised or have conditions such as diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, pulmonary disease, chronic kidney or liver disease, chronic hematologic disease or severe obesity are at increased risk for severe RSV infection [1–10]. To our knowledge, the most recent and largest U.S. prospective, community-based RSV surveillance study that included individuals younger than 60 years with high-risk conditions (HRC) was published in 2005 [5].

To provide updated estimates of RSV incidence and epidemiology in U.S. populations, we recently reported the incidence of RSV-ARI and RSV-lower respiratory tract disease (LRTD) during 2 RSV seasons in October 2022–April 2023 and October 2023–April 2024. This prospective cohort study of community-based adults, aged 18 to 64 years (n = 7501), established post–COVID-19 pandemic RSV incidence, before and after introduction of RSV vaccines [11]. We found the incidence of RSV-ARI for year 1 was 27.71 per 1000 person-years with 49% having RSV-LRTD (13.53 per 1000 person-years) and year 2 was 26.39 per 1000 person-years with 47% having RSV-LRTD (12.43 per 1000 person-years). However, the scope of our previous study did not include RSV-ARI and RSV-LRTD incidence rates based on HRC status. The primary aim of this study was to determine the incidence and attack rates of RSV-ARI and RSV-LRTD in a large prospective cohort surveillance study of community-dwelling adults with and without HRC, aged 18 to 64 years. Our additional aims were to estimate RSV incidence by age groups, specific HRCs, and population attributable fraction of RSV incidence for adults with at least one HRC.

METHODS

Study Setting and Design

This 2-year large community-based prospective cohort study (previously reported [11]) assessed the incidence and attack rates of RSV in adults aged 18 to 64 years with and without HRC (see definition in High Risk Conditions section), starting on 1 October 2022. Participants were recruited from Mayo Clinic primary care patients residing in 4 states: Mayo Clinic Rochester and Mayo Clinic Health System in Minnesota and Wisconsin (Midwest), Mayo Clinic in Florida, and Mayo Clinic in Arizona. Participants who met ARI case definition (Supplementary Table 1) collected their own nasal and oropharyngeal swab specimens at home on onset of symptoms, which were picked up by a courier service. The surveillance period for year 1 was 1 October 2022–30 September 2023, and year 2 was 1 October 2023–30 September 2024.

Patient Consent Statement

Those who met the inclusion criteria received a mailed or electronic (Technology-Enabling Subject Recruitment System) invitation for participation. Once the inclusion criteria were confirmed via electronic survey or institutional review board–approved scripted phone interview, participants provided consent by remote electronic consenting and DocuSign technology or mail. This study followed the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guideline [12, 13]. The Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB # 22-003660) approved the study (NCT218644).

Study Cohort and Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

Participants were selected from our electronic health record (EHR) of eligible patients with at least 1 primary care clinic visit within 3 years before study enrollment (370 000 participants) using a stratified random sampling approach by region and status of HRC [11]. We aimed to recruit approximately 50% of participants with HRC. Our inclusion and exclusion criteria has previously been reported [11]. Briefly, inclusion criteria included the following: (1) adults aged 18 to 64 years at the time of consent with residency in southeast Minnesota, southwest Wisconsin, Florida (Jacksonville area), Arizona (Scottsdale area); (2) active Mayo Clinic primary care patient with at least 1 primary care visit within 3 years before enrollment; (3) authorization to use the EHR for research in Minnesota [14]; and (4) written informed consent. After study enrollment, the U.S. Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices recommended use of RSV vaccination in adults ≥60 years in July 2023 [15]. We did not exclude participants that received RSV vaccine during surveillance period as we are reporting population RSV incidence rates.

Baseline Data Collection

We collected baseline data via patient reported information from phone interviews during study enrollment and EHR review. Data included sociodemographic information, the Charlson Comorbidity Index [16–18] and self-reported race and ethnicity. Demographics were pulled from the EHR at the start of the surveillance period (1 October 2022, or the date the subject completed baseline measurements after October 1). We assessed socioeconomic status (SES) via a standardized and objective individual housing- property data-based measure (Housing-based Socioeconomic Status [HOUSES] Index) [19–22]. A greater HOUSES quartile index indicates a higher SES. Rurality was determined based on the U.S. Census Bureau's urban/rural classification [23].

High Risk Conditions

Participants with HRC were defined as those with any of the following conditions within 3 years before study enrollment that may increase the risk for severe RSV disease: cardiac arrhythmias, coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cystic fibrosis, diabetes (type 1 or 2), chronic liver disease, chronic kidney disease, congenital immunodeficiency disorders, immunosuppressive medications, cancer (solid or hematologic), and solid organ transplant [1–10]. Participants with HRC were identified by the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes (Supplementary Table 2). Medication order review was performed via EHR to confirm receipt of immunosuppressive medications. If the patient self-reported an HRC, but it was not identified by ICD-10 code, the study coordinators verified their medical history via EHR to confirm presence of HRC within the 3 years before study enrollment.

ARI Case Definitions and Specimen Collection

The process of nasal and oropharyngeal specimen collection and RT-PCR testing (Simplexa Flu A/B and RSV Direct assay or Panther Fusion Flu A/B and RSV Assay) for RSV during a participant's ARI episode has been previously described [11]. Supplementary Table 1 delineates the case definitions used for ARI, RSV-ARI, and RSV-LRTD. Among RSV-ARI and RSV-LRTD cases, health care utilization (based on chart review) within 30 days after onset of symptoms was described.

Statistical Analysis

We define rate measures and describe analyses of 2 types of RSV infections: RSV-ARI and RSV-LRTD. Annual attack rates were reported for each year and were defined as the percentage of participants with at least 1 RSV illness episode each year among those at risk at the start of the year. Confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated with the Clopper-Pearson exact method [24]. Incidence rates across 2 years were computed using the number of first RSV infections per 1000 person-years and reported with exact Poisson 95% CIs. The follow-up period began at the start of the surveillance until either symptom onset of the first RSV infection, end of the surveillance period (30 September 2024), or the last follow-up (ie, death, residing out of the study region >2 weeks during October-April and/or >4 weeks during May-September, time when opted out of the study, or moving out of the region), whichever came first [24]. To estimate the increased risk of RSV illness for having at least 1 HRC and specific HRCs, we calculated incidence rate ratios (IRR) with 95% CIs using Poisson regression with an offset term for person-years and adjusting for prespecified characteristics of age and sex. A sensitivity analysis was performed for IRRs that adjusted for additional factors that were (post hoc) associated with high-risk conditions (at the 5% significance level). The population attributable fraction for RSV cases was calculated with exposure defined as having at least 1 HRC. RESULTS

Participant Characteristics

A total of 7504 participants were enrolled. Over 2 years, 295 participants withdrew from the study, 107 in year 1 and 188 in year 2. Reasons for withdrawal are summarized in Supplementary Table 3, mostly related to pregnancy (41%). The number of participants included in the cohort data analysis with HRC (45%) and without HRC (55%) for year 1 was 7501 (3 withdrew before the start of year 1) and for year 2 was 7397. Participants with HRC were 3375 in year 1 (35.1% immunocompromised) and 3327 in year 2 (35.1% immunocompromised) as 48 withdrew. The characteristics of study participants are described in Table 1. There were 42 (0.6%) participants in the overall cohort who received an RSV vaccine before year 2 (28 with HRC and 14 without HRC). In year 2, an additional 223 (3.0%) participants received an RSV vaccine (151 with HRC and 72 without HRC).

Incidence of RSV-ARI

In year 1, there were 3480 ARI episodes, of which 397 did not have swab samples (eg, due to a call of 7 days after onset) and 11 had invalid RSV test results (ie, swabbed and tested but no valid positive or negative RSV-PCR). Among the episodes with a valid RSV test result, 118 participants had a confirmed RSV-ARI episode with 63 (53.4%) having 1 ≥ HRC. In year 2, the overall cohort had 3382 ARI episodes, of which 605 had no swab samples and 4 had invalid RSV results. Among the episodes with a valid RSV test result, 113 participants had a confirmed RSV-ARI episode with 52 (46.0%) having 1 ≥ HRC. None of the participants over the full 2 years had >1 RSV episode. Among RSV-ARI participants, there were 3 and 8 COVID-19 co-infections in years 1 and 2, respectively, and 1 influenza A co-infection in year 2.

The incidence and attack rates of RSV-ARI for year 1 and 2 combined in participants with and without HRC are summarized in Table 2. The incidence of RSV-ARI in the HRC group was 17.9 (95% CI, 14.8–21.5) per 1000 person-years (years 1 and 2 attack rates of 1.87% and 1.56%, respectively). In those without HRC, there were 55 participants in year 1 and 61 participants in year 2 with RSV-ARI. The incidence of RSV-ARI without HRC was 14.8 (95% CI, 12.3–17.8) per 1000 person-years (year 1 and 2 attack rates of 1.33% and 1.50%, respectively). Overall, there were no statistically significant differences in RSV-ARI between those with any HRC and without HRC over 2 years (IRR 1.18 [95% CI, 0.90–1.55]), although several individual HRCs were associated with significant differences.

The RSV-ARI incidence for the top 3 nonimmunocompromising conditions included congestive heart failure (34.1 [95% CI, 9.3–87.3] per 1000 person-years), asthma (22.9 [95% CI, 17.5–29.5] per 1000 person-years), and chronic kidney disease (22.0 [95% CI, 11.7–37.6] per 1000 person-years). The RSV-ARI incidence for the top 3 immunocompromising conditions included solid organ transplant (43.2 [95% CI, 11.8–110.6] per 1000 person-years), congenital immunodeficiency disorders (40.2 [95% CI, 17.4–79.3] per 1000 person-years), and those on immunosuppressive medications (31.6 [95% CI, 19.8–47.9] per 1000 person-years). Incidence rates of RSV-ARI were significantly higher in adults with congenital immunodeficiency disorders (IRR 2.49 [95% CI, 1.21–5.1]), those on immunosuppressive medications (2.02 [95% CI, 1.29–3.16]), and adults with asthma (IRR 1.56 [95% CI, 1.16–2.09]) compared with individuals without those specific conditions. Sensitivity analysis of IRRs with additional adjustment factors did not differ substantially from these results (Supplementary Table 4). The population attributable fraction of RSV-ARI incidence for any HRC was 7.6%, meaning 7.6% of RSV-ARI cases in the study population would have been prevented if no individual had HRCs.

Incidence of RSV-LRTD

The incidence rate and attack rate of RSV-LRTD for years 1 and 2 combined in participants with and without HRC are summarized in Table 3. In the HRC group, there were 36 participants in year 1 and 29 participants in year 2 with RSV-LRTD. The incidence rate of RSV-LRTD in the HRC group was 10.0 (95% CI, 7.7–12.8) per 1000 person-years (years 1 and 2 attack rates of 1.07% and 0.87%, respectively). In the group without HRC, there were 21 participants in year 1 and 24 participants in year 2 with RSV-LRTD. The incidence rate of RSV-LRTD in those without HRC was 5.7 (95% CI, 4.2–7.6) per 1000 person-years (years 1 and 2 attack rates of 0.51% and 0.59%, respectively). RSV-LRTD incidence rate for the HRC group was significantly higher relative to the group without HRC with an IRR of 1.62 (95% CI, 1.08–2.41).

The population attributable fraction of RSV-LRTD incidence for any HRC was 22.6%, meaning 22.6% of RSV-LRTD would not have occurred if there were no HRCs within this cohort.

Incidence of RSV by Age Groups

We estimated the incidence rate of RSV-ARI and RSV-LRTD for those with and without HRC by age groups (Table 4). The overall incidence rates of RSV-ARI were similar within the 3 age groups (18–49 years, 50–59 years, and 60–64 years) when comparing those with and without HRC. However, the RSV-LRTD incidence rate within the age category of 18 to 49 years in the HRC group (9.6 [95% CI, 6.4–13.8] per 1000 person-years) was significantly higher relative to those without HRC (4.3 [95% CI, 2.6–6.5] per 1000 person-years) of the same age group (IRR 2.17 [95% CI, 1.23–3.84]). Among the age groups of 50 to 59 years and 60 to 64 years, participants with at least 1 HRC were more likely to have an RSV-LRTD than those without HRC, but the effects were smaller and not statistically significant.

RSV Related Health Care Utilization Prevalence

During the 2-year study period, a total of 55 participants (HRC group: n = 34, 66%) sought medical care within 30 days of RSV-ARI signs/symptom onset. Incidence rates of medically attended RSV-ARI were 5.2 (95% CI, 3.6–7.3) per 1000 person-years among those with HRCs and 2.6 (95% CI, 1.6–4.0) among those without HRC. Health care utilization included 51 outpatient visits (63% HRC), 12 emergency department visits (75% HRC), 2 hospitalizations without intensive care unit admission (100% HRC), and no hospitalization with intensive care unit admission (utilization frequencies are not mutually exclusive). No participants required mechanical ventilation or died during the 30-day follow-up. Among the 41 participants with HRC who required medical attention, 15 (37%) were 18 to 49 years of age, 12 (29%) were 50 to 59 years of age, and 14 (34%) were 60 to 64 years of age. In those 21 participants without HRC who required medical attention, 13 (62%) were 18 to 49 years of age, 2 (9%) were 50 to 59 years of age, and 6 (29%) were 60 to 64 years of age (data not shown).

DISCUSSION

This prospective cohort study, the largest to date in community-dwelling adults aged 18 to 64 years after the COVID-19 pandemic, found that overall RSV-ARI rates did not differ significantly between participants with any HRCs and those without; however, congenital immunodeficiency, use of immunosuppressive medications, and asthma was each associated with significantly higher incidence of RSV-ARI. By contrast, RSV-LRTD incidence was significantly higher in participants with any HRC, aligning with the current Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices vaccine recommendation for adults with HRC for RSV prevention [26]. For RSV-LRTD, significantly higher incidence was specifically observed in adults with asthma, chronic kidney disease, congenital immunodeficiency, immunosuppressive medication use, and solid organ transplant compared with adults without these conditions. Notably, adults aged 18 to 49 years with any HRC experienced more than a 2-fold higher RSV-LRTD rate than those without HRC, a rate similar to adults aged 50 to 64 years. Because younger adults with HRCs are often underrepresented in RSV surveillance, these results can support the value of considering prevention strategies in this age group. From a public health perspective, expanding the CDC Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices RSV vaccine recommendations to adults aged 18 to 49 years with HRC may be justified as more evidence and cost-effectiveness data become available.

We present the population attributable fraction to describe the burden experienced among people with at least 1 HRC. For example, the population attributable fraction for RSV-LRTD suggests that 22.6% of such cases would not have occurred in the absence of an HRC. We do not ascribe a causal interpretation to an HRC nor suggest a hypothetical intervention that could remove HRCs from the population. Rather, we view the population attributable fraction as a useful summary measure that combines IRR and prevalence of HRCs to express the burden of disease. This summary has important implications for RSV vaccine recommending committees as it would suggest that adults with HRC may benefit from RSV vaccination to prevent complications from RSV-LRTD without age limitations in adults ≥18 years.

In the past 20 years [5], no large, prospective, community-based surveillance studies of RSV exists in the United States that included adult participants with HRC who are younger than age 50 years, especially during or after the COVID-19 pandemic, which affected respiratory infection epidemics [27]. A 2005 study described the incidence of RSV in 540 adults aged ≥21 years with chronic heart or lung disease in a prospective surveillance study of 4 consecutive winters in 1999–2003 and found an annual infection of 4%–10% with an incidence rate of 1.5 cases per 100 person-months [5]. Although this study was not specifically poised to assess incidence rates by age categories, the majority of the HRC group (73%) were ≥65 years, and only 10% were ≤54 years [5]. Comparing these results with ours is difficult as they implemented paired RSV serology with RT-PCR, whereas our RSV incidence estimates relied only on RSV test via nasal and oropharyngeal PCR [5, 28]. An additional prospective, community-based study in Wisconsin assessed the RSV seasonal incidence of medically attended ARI episodes in participants ≥50 years from 2006 to 2010 [29]. They included some participants with HRC (4.3% had congestive heart failure or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), but this study was focused on the RSV incidence rate in adults aged ≥50 years and did not report RSV incidence rates by HRC [29]. Recently, this group published a retrospective study (2015–2019) on seasonal incidence of medically attended RSV infection in adults aged 18 years and older with high-risk conditions, detailing findings by age and HRC [30]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 7 community-dwelling U.S. and European studies of adults with HRC, estimated a combined RSV-ARI incidence rate of 30.3 per 1000 person per year/season [10]. However, 1 of the studies included was retrospective [31] and 2 studies did not include participants <50 years of age [10, 29, 32]. Thus, to our knowledge, this is the first and largest prospective surveillance study of community-dwelling U.S. adults with HRC that estimates the RSV incidence and attack rates in adults aged 18 to 64 years by age groups, particularly noting that 47% of our participants were between 18 to 49 years age.

Patients with HRC are known to be at increased risk to RSV infection progression from the upper respiratory tract to the lower respiratory tract, leading to increased morbidity and mortality [1, 3, 6, 8, 33, 34]. RSV-associated hospitalization rates are typically higher in patients with HRC, and exacerbations of underlying pulmonary diseases are more frequently encountered in those with RSV than those with influenza [34]. For example, a prospective, population-based study in New York between 2017 and 2020, found RSV incidence rates for hospitalized adults ≥18 years with COPD, coronary artery disease, and congestive heart failure to be more than 3 times higher compared to people without those conditions [4]. When we evaluated each specific HRC to determine if any of them contributed significantly to the risk of developing RSV-LRTD compared to those who did not have the specific condition, we found a significantly increased risk of 5.48 in solid organ transplant, 3.72 in congenital immunodeficiency disorders, 2.43 in those on immunosuppressive medications, 2.09 in asthma, and 1.99 in chronic kidney disease. Although participants with congestive heart failure in our study had the highest incidence of RSV-LTRD among the nonimmunocompromising conditions, congestive heart failure was not statistically significant as an individual risk. This may be related to the small sample size (n = 63) of this condition in our cohort as evidence suggests cardiovascular diseases have a significant role in RSV pathophysiology [33]. It is important also to note that in our participants without HRC, those that did n't have the HRCs of interest, might have had other underlying medical conditions that we did not assess in this study.

Our study has several strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first prospective cohort study that reports the estimated incidence rates of RSV-ARI and RSV-LRTD in community-dwelling U.S. adults aged 18 to 64 years with and without HRC. Specifically, we had the largest cohort of HRC participants in the age category of 18 to 49 years (n = 1582), knowing that this is an age group that is underrepresented in RSV incidence studies. Furthermore, our study did not rely on medically attended or hospitalization surveillance data; rather, the participants collected their own nasal and oropharyngeal swab specimens at home. Additionally, our decentralized study design consisted of a cohort of primary care empaneled patients from 4 U.S. states of different climates and geographic regions that may capture the epidemiologic diversification of RSV in the United States.

Our study had several limitations. There is a possibility of RSV incidence underascertainment because of suboptimal sensitivity of common diagnostic methods [6] and participants missing the 7-day window for specimen collection after symptom onset. Other potential study limitations include the single-institution study design, and exclusion of pregnant participants. Additionally, the definitions of HRCs considered in the study were based on the scientific understanding and guidance available at the time of its design, and these definitions are continuously evolving, as it is also evident from the updated CDC guidance from 2023 onward. Participants that enrolled in this study were not necessarily representative of the target population of paneled patients. We had a predominance of female participants across all sites, and although this imbalance has been shown in a previous community-based study [35], the enrollment sex imbalance is not clearly understood. A recent study has demonstrated that sex bias among clinical trial enrollees vary by disease category and clinical trial features, with female enrollment predominance associated with preventive intervention trials [36]. Additionally, our previous report showed that enrolled participants were likely to be older, non-Hispanic White, and higher SES (as measured by HOUSES) than the target population [37]. As such, the generalizability of our study findings to other settings of racially and SES diverse communities should be cautiously interpreted.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, adults with asthma, congenital immunodeficiency, and use of immunosuppressive medications had a significantly higher incidence of RSV-ARI compared with adults without these conditions. For RSV-LRTD, significantly higher incidence was observed overall in adults with any HRCs, and specifically in those with asthma, chronic kidney disease, congenital immunodeficiency, immunosuppressive medication use, and solid organ transplant compared with adults without these conditions. In age-stratified analyses, a >2-fold higher RSV-LRTD incidence was observed in the 18 to 49 year age group with any HRC versus those without HRCs. Findings may support expansion of RSV vaccine recommendations to adults 18 to 49 years with HRC.
