Cholangiolocellular Component Predicts a Biologically Distinct Subgroup of Mass‐Forming Intrahepatic Cholangiocarcinoma
Authors: Naoto Kubota, Ken Yamazaki, Yasuhito Arai, Minoru Esaki, Nobuyoshi Hiraoka, Hirofumi Shirakawa, Moriaki Tomikawa, Tatsuhiro Shibata, Michiie Sakamoto, Hidenori Ojima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCholangiolocellular carcinoma (CLC) is a histopathological variant of primary liver tumor with unique morphologies, and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinomas (iCCAs) frequently contain a CLC component; however, the biological characteristics of iCCA with CLC remain undescribed. In this study, 36 mass‐forming iCCAs (MF‐iCCAs), histologically small‐duct type iCCA, were classified into CLC(+) iCCAs and CLC(−) iCCAs by the presence/absence of the CLC component. Two genetic subgroups were generated using highly expressed genes in CLC(+) iCCA and CLC(−) iCCA. As the results of clinicopathological and genetic analyses, CLC(+) iCCA had better overall survival and upregulation of stromal‐ and oxidation‐related genes, whereas CLC(−) iCCA showed upregulation of proliferation‐ and hypoxia‐related genes. Two genetic subgroups of iCCA were identified: iCCA‐G1, which was related to CLC, and iCCA‐G2, which was unrelated to CLC. iCCA‐G1 comprised all 14 CLC(+) iCCAs [CLC(+)G1] and 7 of 19 CLC(−) iCCAs [CLC(−)G1], whereas iCCA‐G2 was composed only of CLC(−) iCCAs [CLC(−)G2]. CLC(+)G1 and CLC(−)G1 exhibited similar patterns of somatic gene alterations compared with CLC(−)G2. Angiogenesis‐related genes were upregulated in CLC(+)G1, and the number of tumor vessels was larger in CLC(+)G1, followed by CLC(−)G1, compared with CLC(−)G2. Further, SPP1 (encoding osteopontin) was identified as a highly expressed angiogenesis‐related gene in CLC(+) iCCA. Immunohistochemical expression of osteopontin was high in CLC(+) iCCA, showing apical and/or cytoplasmic expression patterns, which should facilitate the histopathological classification of iCCA‐G1 and iCCA‐G2. CLC component is useful for predicting a distinct genetic subgroup of MF‐iCCA with better prognosis, high angiogenesis, and different gene alteration patterns, indicating different carcinogenic pathways of MF‐iCCA.

Introduction

The incidence of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA) is increasing worldwide; however, the 5‐year overall survival rate for iCCA patients remains extremely low at approximately 10%, even in developed countries [1, 2]. Consequently, there is a need to develop novel therapeutic approaches against iCCA. Molecular subclassification of cancers based on upregulated gene/protein expressions or specific gene alterations has proven a successful method for developing therapeutic strategies for conditions such as breast cancer, lung cancer, and colon cancer [3, 4, 5]. Several molecular subclassifications of iCCA using multiple genes/proteins have been reported, but have not yet been applied in the clinical setting, except for the several genetic alterations targeted by specific drugs [6, 7, 8]. For example, fusion of the FGFR2 gene and point mutations of IDH1/2 are promising candidates for stratifying patients for the application of molecular‐targeted drugs. However, the number of patients with these genetic alterations is limited, and the acquisition of resistance after molecular‐targeted treatment remains unresolved [9]. Therefore, the creation of useful iCCA subclassifications is indispensable for gaining novel insights into anti‐iCCA therapies.

Histopathological subclassification is a classical and cost‐effective method compared with molecular subclassification. Several histopathological subgroups have proven useful for predicting the molecular subtypes of a tumor. For example, the presence of pseudo‐glandular features in hepatocellular carcinoma predicts CTNNB1 mutation in the tumor [10]. Currently, iCCA is characterized using two histopathological subtypes, i.e., the small‐duct type and the large‐duct type, based on the size of tumoral ducts [11]. These histological differences are considered to reflect the origin of the tumor: small‐duct iCCA arises from peripheral bile duct showing a mass‐forming pattern in its gross appearance, whereas large‐duct iCCA arises from perihilar large bile duct and has a periductal infiltrating pattern or an intraductal growth pattern [12]. Additionally, cholangiolocellular carcinoma (CLC) is considered to be a different histological subtype of liver tumor currently classified as a part of well‐differentiated iCCA. CLC was originally described by Paul E. Steiner et al. in 1959 as a rare histological variant of primary liver tumor with small cancer ductules resembling canals of Hering or cholangioles [13]. Previously, CLC was classified as a histological subtype of combined hepatocellular–cholangiocellular carcinoma with stem cell features because of its unique histological structures: CLC tumor glands resemble cholangioles, where hepatobiliary stem cells likely reside [14]. Several studies indicate that CLC is unique in its genetic and protein expression patterns, such as the downregulation of several integrin molecules [15, 16, 17, 18]. In the latest version of histological classification, CLC is categorized as a small‐duct iCCA, based on its similarity to well‐differentiated iCCA in terms of immunohistochemical and molecular features [11, 19, 20]. Pathologists often observe the presence of a focal CLC component in small‐duct iCCA lesions; however, the clinicopathological features of such lesions have not been described in detail [21]. In this study, we focused on the CLC component in mass‐forming iCCA (MF‐iCCA) with small‐duct histology and identified a robust molecular subgroup of iCCA with unique biological features based on the existence of a CLC component. This approach could form the basis for stratification of therapeutic strategies for iCCA.

Material and Methods

Patients and Samples

Thirty‐six MF‐iCCA cases surgically resected between 2003 and 2010 at the National Cancer Centre Hospital in Tokyo, Japan, were included in this study. All MF‐iCCA histologically confirmed as small‐duct iCCA with available fresh frozen samples were consecutively selected. Fresh tissue samples (up to 10 mm square) were collected from tumor areas of 33 patients immediately after evaluation of the gross tumor appearance by two pathologists (H.O. and N.H.). Whole resected tissues were fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin to undergo general histopathological evaluation, and the small fresh tumor pieces were embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (O.C.T.) compound and stored in a freezer at −80°C. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)‐stained slides (4 μm thick) from formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks were evaluated histologically. Frozen samples underwent RNA extraction and transcriptome analysis after taking several frozen‐sections slices from which the histological characteristics of the samples could be confirmed. In addition, 21 MF‐iCCA cases surgically resected at the Tochigi Cancer Center between 2009 and 2023 were used as a validation cohort.

Clinicopathological Evaluation

Histopathological Determination of the CLC Component

We evaluated the CLC component in MF‐iCCA lesions using H&E‐stained slides from FFPE tissue blocks according to the morphological definitions of CLC in the WHO classification of tumors, 2019 [14]. We determined the presence of CLC components according to the following histological criteria: (i) smaller cuboidal cells with round to oval nuclei, fine chromatin, and scant cytoplasm; (ii) innocuous‐appearing ductular tumor formations resembling reactive bile ductules; and (iii) hyalinized fibrotic stroma. The diagnosis of small duct‐type and large duct‐type iCCAs was also made in accordance with the WHO classification. Small duct‐type iCCAs are characterized by tubular formations with distinct lumina lined by cuboidal to low columnar tumor cells with scant cytoplasm; they may also exhibit small tubular, cord‐like, or spindle‐shaped cell formations with slit‐like lumina. In contrast, large duct‐type iCCAs consist of larger ductal structures that resemble the histology of perihilar and extrahepatic CCA. In addition, because CLC components can occasionally be observed in combined hepatocellular–cholangiocarcinoma (cHCC‐CCA), we carefully confirmed the absence of any hepatocellular carcinoma component within each lesion when extracting the cases for our iCCA cohort. Tumors with more than 5% of CLC component were classified as CLC(+) iCCAs, and the others as CLC(−) iCCAs as previously reported [21]. Two pathologists (N.K. and H.O.) evaluated the tumors independently and classified them as CLC(+) iCCA or CLC(−) iCCA, with consensus agreement. For background liver, we evaluated the fibrosis stage and metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) status using the nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) activity score (NAS) [22].

Clinicopathological Features

From patients' pathological records, we extracted general histopathological data, i.e., tumor size, portal vein invasion, hepatic vein invasion, lymphatic vessel invasion, perineural invasion, intrahepatic metastasis, lymph node metastasis, and the fibrous status of background liver. Clinical information, including age, sex, presence of hepatitis B and C virus infection, serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19–9 (CA‐19‐9) levels, and the 5‐year post‐surgery recurrence and survival statuses, was also extracted from patients' clinical records.

RNA Extraction and Genetic Analysis

Total RNA was extracted from each fresh‐frozen tumor sample using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Good‐quality RNA samples (RIN > 7.5) were submitted to microarray analysis (SurePrint G3 Human Gene Expression Microarray v1 (8x60K), G8458A#28004, Agilent, California, U.S.A.). Data processing and normalization were performed according to the vendor's protocols. After data normalization, all probes were collapsed to the gene level by median value using the corresponding chip file (GPL14550‐9757) and then converted to the latest‐version gene symbols using the following website: https://www.syngoportal.org/convert [23]. For three tumors, two frozen samples were submitted to microarray analysis. For each pair, the data were highly correlated; consequently, we used the mean value of each gene for further analysis. The microarray data are available in the GEO database (GSE277546). Whole‐exome sequencing data were generated using the same methods as described in previous studies [24].

Immunohistochemical Analysis

Serial sections (4 μm thick) of representative H&E‐stained slides from FFPE tissue blocks of each patient were used for immunohistochemical analysis. After deparaffinization in xylene, each FFPE slide was treated with 0.3% H2O2 in 95% methanol for 10 min to deactivate intrinsic peroxidase, followed by antigen retrieval at 120°C for 10 min in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking, the slides were treated with primary antibody followed by a secondary antibody (Table S1) using the manufacturers' recommended conditions. Color development was achieved using 3, 3′‐diaminobenzidin solution (Table S1), and nuclear staining was performed using hematoxylin. For evaluation of the number of vessels within the tumor region, h‐caldesmon was used to identify unpaired arteries and the arteries in the portal tract, as previously described [25]. h‐caldesmon‐positive arteries associated with portal vein and bile duct were considered as the residing portal tracts in the tumor area, whereas those not associated with portal vein and bile duct were as unpaired arteries. Angiopoietin 1, which showed positive for blood vessel endothelial cells, was used as a marker of small vessels [26]. The number of unpaired arteries and the number of portal areas were counted in the whole tumor area in each slide, and the number of small vessels were counted within 10 randomly placed regions of interest (ROIs) in each tumor lesion. The size of the ROI for small vessel counting was 970 μm in diameter, which is almost the same as the 200× field commonly used in light microscopy [27]. The number of vessels per unit area underwent further analysis. Immunostained slides were scanned using a digital slide scanner (NanoZoomer S210, Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu, Shizuoka, Japan, for the original cohort, and Aperio GT 450, Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany, for the validation cohort), and the tumor area measurement was performed using viewing software (NDP. view2, Hamamatsu Photonics). In the analysis of osteopontin expression, positive staining patterns inside the lumen of the tumor ducts was defined as the “apical pattern”, and positive staining of the tumor cytoplasm was defined as the “cytoplasmic pattern”. Of note, nontumoral bile ducts showed apical osteopontin pattern positivity, as previously reported, and we considered this staining pattern to be a positive control within the slide [28]. When we evaluated the proportion of osteopontin‐positive cells on each slide, cases with more than 5% positivity tended to exhibit a discernible regional distribution, whereas cases with less than 5% showed only scattered positive cells. These two patterns were readily distinguishable. Therefore, based on consensus discussions among multiple pathologists, a cutoff value of 5% was adopted for this study.

Statistical Analysis

For clinicopathological analysis, Student's t‐test was used to compare continuous data between two groups, and the chi‐squared test was used for comparing categorical data. Prognostic analysis was performed using the log‐rank test and Kaplan–Meier survival curves for recurrence‐free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS). Student's t‐test was also used to compare the gene expression levels between two groups. The frequencies of genetic alterations between two groups were analyzed using the chi‐squared test. Gene signature enrichment analysis was performed using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) software, and all genes were ranked by t‐statistics between two categories [29, 30]. Gene sets were from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) and gene ontology [31]. Hierarchical clustering and heatmap generation were performed using Morpheus software (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus). Comparisons of the number of vessels per unit area were done using the Mann–Whitney U test. p values smaller than 0.05 were considered to be significant, unless otherwise specified. All statistical calculations, other than those based on GSEA and Morpheus software, were performed using R software (version 4.3.1).

Clinicopathological analysis revealed that CLC(+) iCCA was more frequently found in female patients (p = 0.041), and that the log‐transformed serum CA19‐9 levels were significantly low in CLC(+) iCCA patients (p = 0.035). Although the number of cases with hepatic steatosis was limited, steatosis was observed more frequently in CLC(−) iCCA (p = 0.032) (Table 1). CLC(+) iCCA Belongs to a Distinct Genetic Category With Better Prognosis

First, we analyzed the differentially expressed genes in CLC(+) iCCA and CLC(−) iCCA and identified 209 and 329 genes highly expressed in CLC(+) iCCA and CLC(−) iCCA, respectively (p < 0.01, fold change > 2, Table S2). Hierarchical clustering of these 538 genes revealed two distinct molecular subgroups of MF‐iCCA: iCCA‐G1 and iCCA‐G2 (Figure 2A). iCCA‐G1 was a CLC‐related subgroup composed of 14 CLC(+) iCCAs [CLC(+)G1] and 7 CLC(−) iCCAs [CLC(−)G1], whereas iCCA‐G2 was a non‐CLC‐related subgroup composed of 12 CLC(−) iCCAs [CLC(−)G2] and no CLC(+) iCCAs (p = 0.0002) (Figure 2B).

To elucidate the transcriptome features of CLC(+) iCCA vs. CLC(−) iCCA and iCCA‐G1 vs. iCCA‐G2, GSEA analysis was performed on these two sets of subgroups. CLC(+) iCCA was characterized by enrichment of the gene signatures related to bile duct cells, fibrous stroma, immune cells, complement, oxidative metabolism, and osteopontin, whereas CLC(−) iCCA was characterized by signatures related to cell proliferation, DNA repair, hypoxia, glycometabolism, KRAS signaling, TP53 signaling, and WNT signaling. The results of the comparison between iCCA‐G1 and iCCA‐G2 were essentially similar to the comparison between CLC(+) iCCA and CLC(−) iCCA; however, aerobic/oxidative metabolism was more significantly enriched in iCCA‐G1 than in CLC(+) iCCA (Figure 2C, Table S3).

Further, Andersen cholangiocarcinoma class gene signatures [gene sets originally identified from the transcriptome data of hilar and peripheral cholangiocarcinoma for classifying lesions into two genetic subtypes to predict prognostic feature (Table S4) [32] were applied to our cohort. The original Andersen cholangiocarcinoma class genes were composed of ANDERSEN_CHOLANGIOCARCINOMA_CLASS1 (63 genes) for good prognosis and ANDERSEN_CHOLANGIOCARCINOMA_CLASS2 (175 genes) for poor prognosis, cited from MSigDB. We found that the class 1 gene set was significantly enriched in CLC(+) iCCA, whereas the class 2 gene set was significantly enriched in CLC(−) iCCA (Figure S1). Interestingly, the two Andersen iCCA clusters in our cohort generated from hierarchical clustering of Andersen's gene sets perfectly overlapped our CLC‐related genetic subgroups (p < 0.0001, Figure S2).

Prognostic analysis revealed that patients with iCCA‐G1 tumors had significantly better overall survival and recurrence‐free survival than iCCA‐G2 patients (Figure 2D), and this result was validated in a public data set composed of 30 iCCA cases (GSE107943, Figure S3) [7].

Pattern of Gene Alterations is Correlated With Transcriptome Subgroups of MF‐iCCA

Among the 36 lesions in our cohort, 30 had whole‐exome sequencing data that had been previously published [13 CLC(+) and 17 CLC(−) iCCAs] [24] In total, 1360 genetic alterations were observed, and the total number of genetic alterations and missense mutations were significantly higher in iCCA‐G2 than in iCCA‐G1 (p = 0.009, 0.010, respectively) (Figure 3A). Thirteen genes, namely, MUC4, BAP1, TP53, FGFR2, EPHA2, AKT3, MUC16, MYCBP2, TTN, COL12A1, EGFR, KRAS, NRAS, were identified as frequently altered genes, i.e., genes with a total number of alterations ≥ 3 in our cohort. Only one ARID1A alteration was observed in the present study, although iCCA with ductal plate malformation is potentially associated with ARID1A mutations [33] Figure 3B. Summarizes the distributions of these genetic alterations and also shows two IDH1 missense mutations. Among these commonly altered genes, MUC4 missense mutations were the most frequently observed genetic abnormality [three in CLC(+)G1, three in CLC(−)G1, and one in CLC(−)G2]. Missense or nonsense mutations of TP53 were significantly more frequently detected in CLC(−) iCCA than in CLC(+) iCCA (p = 0.032), and this difference was also significant when comparing iCCA‐G1 and iCCA‐G2 (p = 0.001). KRAS mutations were more frequently observed in iCCA‐G2 than in iCCA‐G1 (p = 0.010). Hierarchical clustering of the frequency of genetic alterations of the 13 frequently altered genes revealed that CLC(+)G1 and CLC(−)G1 were more closely related to each other than to CLC(−)G2, suggesting that our transcriptome‐based classification of iCCA‐G1 and iCCA‐G2 was related to genetic alterations (Figure 3C). Moreover, to visualize the relationship between genetic alterations and gene expression, we evaluated the activation of pathways associated with representative genetic alterations (Figure S4). CLC Component in MF‐iCCA has Angiogenic Features and a Larger Number of Tumor Vessels

To identify in more depth the features of the CLC component in CLC(+) iCCA, we compared gene enrichment in CLC(+)G1 and CLC(−)G1. GSEA analysis indicated that CLC(+)G1 had higher enrichment of gene signatures related to cell orientation, fibrous stroma, and angiogenesis (Figure 4A, Table S5). To clarify the biological implications of the upregulation of angiogenesis in CLC(+)G1, we compared the number of tumor vessels in each lesion histopathologically. The number of small vessels was significantly higher in CLC(+) iCCA than in CLC(−) iCCA (p < 0.001, Figure 4B,C), especially in the CLC component of CLC(+) iCCA, followed by the non‐CLC area of CLC(+) iCCA, compared with CLC(−) iCCA. Similar trends were observed in the numbers of unpaired arteries (Figure 4C). Between iCCA‐G1 and iCCA‐G2, both the number of small vessels and the number of unpaired arteries were significantly larger in iCCA‐G1 (p < 0.001 and p = 0.024, respectively) (Figure 4C). The number of small vessels and the number of unpaired arteries were positively correlated in all histological components, suggesting that both small vessels and unpaired arteries proliferate as a result of upregulation of angiogenesis (Figure S5). Interestingly, the number of remaining portal areas in the tumor area was significantly larger in the CLC component of CLC(+) iCCA than in the non‐CLC area of CLC(+) iCCA (p = 0.011, Figure 4C). Of note, the number of portal areas and small vessels showed a negative correlation trend in the CLC component (Figure S5).

CLC(+) iCCA has a Unique Osteopontin Expression Pattern

Considering the difficulty in regular clinical practice of both whole‐transcriptome sequencing and vessel counting, we aimed to identify key genes/proteins of CLC(+) iCCA that could be evaluated using immunohistochemical analysis; the aim was to facilitate implementation of our findings in the clinical setting. By considering the intersects of three groups of genes, i.e., (i) genes highly expressed in CLC(+) iCCA in our cohort (209 genes), (ii) the Andersen class 1 gene set (63 genes), and (iii) angiogenesis‐related genes from the hallmark gene set of angiogenesis in MSigDB (36 genes), we identified SPP1, which encodes osteopontin protein, as a key factor of CLC(+) iCCA (Figure 5A) [31]. Immunohistochemical staining analysis of osteopontin revealed that iCCA tumor cells showed three distinct patterns: (i) an apical pattern (positive on the lumen side of tumor ducts), (ii) a cytoplasmic pattern (positive in cytoplasmic areas of tumor cells), and (iii) negative (Figure 5B). Interestingly, both positive patterns were significantly more frequently observed in CLC(+) iCCA than in CLC(−) iCCA, and in iCCA‐G1 than in iCCA‐G2 (p < 0.001, Figure 5C). The sensitivity and the specificity of osteopontin positive patterns to distinguish iCCA‐G1 and iCCA‐G2 were 94.4% and 100% respectively, suggesting that osteopontin staining can be utilized to identify the genetic subgroup of iCCA with better prognosis during pathological diagnosis. Furthermore, the apical pattern was more frequently observed in CLC(+) iCCA, and apical pattern was specifically found in CLC component (Figure 5C). In the validation cohort, all MF‐iCCA cases with CLC components were osteopontin‐positive, and osteopontin immunoreactivity stratified patient prognosis. While the CLC(+) group showed only a trend toward better outcomes, osteopontin‐positive cases exhibited significantly favorable prognosis (Figure S6, Table S6). Discussion

We comprehensively elucidated the relationships among the clinical, morphological, and genetic characteristics of iCCA with a CLC component; moreover, we developed a molecular subclassification of small‐duct iCCA related to the existence of a CLC component (i.e., iCCA‐G1 and iCCA‐G2). Transcriptome analyses revealed that CLC(+) iCCA is related to less aggressive features such as less enrichment of cell proliferation‐related genes and DNA repair/synthesis genetic signatures, which explains the better overall survival for patients with CLC(+) iCCA and iCCA‐G1 tumors. This result is consistent with previous studies that indicated improved prognostic features of CLC‐related MF‐iCCA [6, 21]. Investigation of the genetic alterations in each subgroup showed that TP53 and KRAS mutations were frequently observed driver gene alterations in iCCA‐G2, which can explain the aggressive biological phenotype of iCCA‐G2. Importantly, although CLC(+) iCCA and iCCA‐G1 showed better overall survival compared with CLC(−) iCCA and iCCA‐G2 respectively, CLC(+) iCCA and iCCA‐G1 have an unignorable recurrence risk. This fact indicates that both CLC(+) iCCA/iCCA‐G1 and CLC(−) iCCA/iCCA‐G2 require therapeutic interventions. Considering the genetic differences between CLC(+) iCCA/iCCA‐G1 and CLC(−) iCCA/iCCA‐G2, the best therapeutic approaches for these subgroups may be different. Of note, FGFR2 and IDH1 abnormalities were observed mainly in iCCA‐G1, both of which could represent therapeutic targets. In fact, FGFR2 fusion and IDH1 mutation are well‐known genetic changes in iCCA [34], and targeted drug therapy for these gene alterations is currently undergoing clinical trials [35]. However, it should be noted that, despite certain associations being observed between genetic alterations and the corresponding pathway activities, these patterns were not always concordant. This observation suggests that additional factors—such as epigenetic modifications or influences from the tumor microenvironment—may also play a role in regulating gene expression in iCCA.

The question as to whether iCCA‐G1 can progress to iCCA‐G2 remains unresolved; however, our data indicate that several gene alterations are mutually exclusive between iCCA‐G1 and iCCA‐G2, whereas CLC(+)G1 and CLC(−)G1 share several gene alterations. In addition, the CLC and non‐CLC components were frequently observed to merge into one another in a continuous morphological transition. Consistently, osteopontin immunostaining revealed a gradual shift from an apical pattern in the CLC component to an apical–cytoplasmic pattern, and ultimately to a cytoplasmic pattern in the non‐CLC areas. Taken together, the CLC and non‐CLC components appear to be largely continuous, representing a morphological spectrum rather than discrete entities. These findings suggest that the two distinct genetic subgroups iCCA‐G1 and iCCA‐G2 are less likely to interconvert. In contrast, CLC(+)G1 and CLC(−)G1 could be sequential entities, and CLC(−)G1 may arise from CLC(+)G1.

Our histological evaluation of tumor vessels and portal areas revealed that the number of small vessels and unpaired arteries is higher in CLC(+) iCCA and iCCA‐G1. Considering the activation of aerobic metabolism in CLC(+) iCCA and iCCA‐G1, the vessels in these subgroups are necessary to supply adequate oxygen to the tumor cells. In contrast, the activation of hypoxic/glycometabolism in CLC(−) iCCA and iCCA‐G2 indicates that tumors in these subgroups can survive with a smaller number of vessels and low oxygen supply. Angiogenesis is known generally to increase with tumor malignancy; however, it is highly interesting that iCCA exhibited a different pattern. Whereas angiogenesis in most cancers is associated with hypoxia and fibrotic stroma, angiogenesis in iCCA‐G1 appears to reflect intrinsic tumor characteristics. Compared with iCCA‐G2, iCCA‐G1 may be closer in phenotype to hepatocellular carcinoma, which is well known for prominent angiogenesis. Moreover, our observations revealed that the number of portal areas and small vessels showed a tendency for negative correlation in the CLC component. This result suggests that the CLC component invades liver parenchyma while initially preserving the original portal structure of the liver; however, as it becomes more aggressive with the activation of angiogenesis and loss of cell polarity, it destroys portal structures along with the overall progression. This scenario is consistent with several studies suggesting that hyper vascular iCCA is an early stage of iCCA [36, 37, 38].

As a result of our findings, we propose the utility of immunohistochemical evaluation of osteopontin to identify the subgroup of iCCA with improved prognosis. Osteopontin is reported to be consistently overexpressed in iCCA [39] and its decreased expression is reportedly related to tumor aggressiveness and clinical outcomes [40]. Recently, single‐cell analysis of iCCA revealed that SPP1 was a marker for peripheral small‐duct iCCA and was related to CLC features [41]. The current study added new insight into osteopontin expression patterns in iCCA, finding the apical osteopontin pattern in the CLC area and the cytoplasmic osteopontin pattern in the non‐CLC area of iCCA‐G1 and CLC(−)G1 tumors. These patterns may indicate the progression of CLC to non‐CLC along with diminished cell polarity in iCCA‐G1. Additionally, osteopontin staining could be a new method for identifying CLC component through the apical osteopontin pattern, while indicating the better prognostic subgroup of CLC(−) iCCA through its cytoplasmic osteopontin pattern. To date, the apical pattern expression of epithelial membrane antigen (EMA) and polyclonal CEA have been widely utilized for identifying CLC component immunohistochemically [42]; however, they have not been evaluated as markers of genetic subgroups. Immunostaining is a lower‐cost method compared with multiple‐gene evaluation; therefore, osteopontin staining can be easily implemented to carry out molecular classification of MF‐iCCA in the clinical setting. Because many gene alterations and genetic pathways are related to our molecular subgrouping, in the future, this subtyping could be applied to indicate the most appropriate molecular/pathway‐targeted therapies.

In this study, we performed validation using a cohort from another institution and a publicly available dataset to compensate for the limited number of samples; nevertheless, the relatively small sample size remains an important limitation. Furthermore, our transcriptomic analyses were conducted using RNA extracted from bulk tissue. Because bulk specimens comprise a heterogeneous mixture of cell types—including tumor cells, inflammatory cells, and stromal cells—the proportion of tumor‐derived RNA within the total RNA may vary across samples. No significant differences in tumor cell content were observed between the major comparison groups in this study [i.e., CLC(+) iCCA vs. CLC(−) iCCA and iCCA‐G1 vs. iCCA‐G2], suggesting that any influence of tumor cell proportion is likely limited. Nonetheless, further accumulation of single‐cell transcriptomic data in iCCA may help delineate the biological characteristics of each group with greater precision.

In this study, we found that iCCA with a CLC component is a histological subtype of MF‐iCCA with better prognosis. The genetic subgroups indicated by these histological subtypes proved to be distinct molecular subgroups of MF‐iCCA. Investigation of the biological implications of CLC(−) lesions in the better prognostic genetic subgroup (iCCA‐G1) indicated different carcinogenic pathways for MF‐iCCA. Further, we suggest the utility of osteopontin staining for identifying the molecular subgroups.
