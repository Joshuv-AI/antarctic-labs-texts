A Categorical Machine Learning Approach to Predicting Areas of Shallow Coastal Groundwater
Authors: Patrick Durney, Matt Dumont, Christo Rautenbach
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoastal lowlands are increasingly vulnerable to threats from sea‐level and associated groundwater rise. This study introduces a categorical modeling framework that redefines groundwater depth estimation as a classification problem rather than a continuous prediction task. By dividing groundwater occurrence into multiple depth thresholds (0.9–2.0 m), the approach explicitly quantifies prediction uncertainty through Type I (false positive) and Type II (false negative) errors. A national‐scale ensemble model developed at 100 m resolution using the Random Forest algorithm was trained on New Zealand's comprehensive depth‐to‐water database. Thirty‐seven predictor variables, derived via PCA (97.5% variance retained) from 199 base predictors, were incorporated to capture the complex interactions influencing groundwater depth. The model demonstrates strong performance, with ROC–AUC values ranging from 0.823 to 0.962, and accuracy improves with increasing depth. This categorical framework addresses challenges associated with data imbalance and enhances uncertainty quantification compared to traditional regression methods. Probabilistic predictions allow stakeholders to set customizable risk thresholds and manage acceptable error levels based on specific coastal management contexts. By bridging the gap between advanced numerical modeling and practical adaptation planning, the approach provides a robust tool for evidence‐based decision making in the face of rising sea levels.

Introduction

Coastal lowlands worldwide face unprecedented challenges due to rising sea levels and associated tidal stage propagation. These phenomena are leading to elevated water tables and a host of related risks, particularly concerning coastal aquifers and infrastructure. New Zealand typifies this global concern as it is a small island nation in the South Pacific with an extensive coastline of approximately 18,200 km (Lawrence et al. 2018). A significant portion of its population centers are situated in low‐lying coastal areas, making the country particularly vulnerable to sea‐level rise.

Historical data shows a relative sea‐level rise of 0.2 m in New Zealand from 1900 to present, with projections indicating an additional 0.3 to 0.4 m rise in the coming 30 to 40 years (Lawrence et al. 2018). By 2100, sea levels are expected to rise between 0.5 and 1 m, depending on global greenhouse gas emissions (Stephens et al. 2017). This rise poses significant risks to coastal communities and infrastructure, with Lawrence et al. (2018) estimating that the replacement value of buildings on land within 0.5 m of spring high tide is $3 billion, rising to $19 billion for land within 1.5 m.

The implications of relative sea‐level rise (RLSR) are severe, including increased frequency and depth of coastal flooding, exacerbated coastal erosion, and more frequent flooding in areas that currently experience only occasional inundation. Of particular concern is the phenomenon of groundwater rise, where sea‐level rise causes a corresponding increase in the water table as a response to changes in coastal boundary elevations. In extreme cases, this can lead to groundwater flooding if it reaches the land surface (Morgan 2024). Groundwater rise associated with RSLR is becoming increasingly recognized for its impacts on agriculture and infrastructure. Further, groundwater rise presents increased risks for liquefaction Monk et al. (2016). As a result, coastal communities will be forced to adapt to these changing conditions, with both Lawrence et al. (2018) and Stephens et al. (2017) emphasizing the urgent need for adaptive measures.

To enable successful adaptation, a thorough understanding of the areas specifically at risk is required. The first step is to identify areas at a national scale where groundwater is already shallow, and therefore most at risk of groundwater rise. While global scale gradient models are available, these have known accuracy problems (Reinecke et al. 2020). Consequently, other approaches are needed. In recent years, machine learning (ML) approaches like Random Forests (RF) (Breiman 2001) and deep learning techniques have emerged as powerful alternatives to traditional physically‐based models, demonstrating efficacy across scales from local to continental mapping.

Recent studies have demonstrated both the potential and limitations of these approaches. Ma et al. (2024) showed the capability of RF in estimating water table depth across the contiguous United States at 1 km resolution, outperforming previous physically based models. Their study highlighted the importance of location and elevation as predictors while quantifying model uncertainty using quantile regression forests. Similarly, Koch et al. (2019, 2021) showcased the evolution of ML approaches for high‐resolution groundwater mapping in Denmark, progressing from RF at 50 m resolution to gradient boosting decision trees achieving 10 m resolution mapping. Deep learning techniques have also been applied to groundwater modeling, for example, Zhang et al. (2018), Baek et al. (2020). Several studies have explored hybrid and ensemble approaches (Sahoo et al. 2017; Pham et al. 2022). The application of ML to groundwater management challenges is typified by Rohde et al. (2021), who used RF to model long‐term groundwater trends for ecosystems across California. A review by Ahmadi et al. (2022) analyzed 197 studies from 2010 to 2020, noting the prevalence of Artificial Neural Networks in groundwater level prediction. This study highlighted the importance of input data quality and quantity over specific ML algorithms and identified opportunities for integrating ML with physics‐based models.

Despite these advancements, significant challenges remain in groundwater table modeling. Sun and Scanlon (2019) pointed out issues in big data management, the need for more labeled training data, and the importance of developing interpretable and physically consistent models. In addition, the majority of these studies have focused on the temporal prediction of time series at specific sites. Comparatively few studies have attempted to model the water table spatially across large areas. These studies typically approach spatial water table modeling as a regression problem, which is effective for overall groundwater level prediction. However, the regression approach faces significant challenges predicting shallow groundwater, in particular the data sparsity in these depth ranges. This is a known problem in groundwater modeling, as highlighted by the work of Ma et al. (2024). Wells tend to be drilled for water supply or for monitoring a region's water resources; few are developed to identify and characterize the water table. Regression‐based approaches, while offering good performance overall, can struggle in areas where data is limited. As a result, regression models may fail to accurately capture the depth to water of very shallow (<2 m deep) water tables and their groundwater dynamics. Figure 1 adapted from Ma et al. (2024) and Koch et al. (2019) provides an example of the Type II error in shallow groundwater depth prediction. The models have a false tendency to predict deeper water tables than observed for observations less than 1 m. A Type II error in this context underestimates the risk of water table rise and can lead to maladaptive strategies (e.g., investment which exacerbates rather than mitigates hazards) Schipper (2020).

Example of Type II error (highlighted inside ellipse) in shallow groundwater depth prediction adapted from (1) Figure 4 in Ma et al. (2024) and (2) Figure 3 in Koch et al. (2019). It should be noted that the original regression models are robust for their intended purposes, which differ from the current study's objective of predicting very shallow groundwater levels. The highlighted error is not intended as a critique of the original studies but rather emphasizes the necessity for alternative modeling approaches in very shallow and data‐sparse groundwater contexts.

Data sparsity also impacts physically based models. Where such models are optimized to existing groundwater measurements, objective function approaches often prioritize fitting the more dominant class over the minority class. This issue can be addressed to some extent by modifying observation weightings; however, such weightings are often inherently qualitative and judgment‐based Leaf et al. (2024). Additionally, physically based models can introduce compounding structural errors Doherty and Hunt (2010), Schneider et al. (2022). For example, it is common practice to include drainage at the land surface (e.g., DRN package) to represent small‐scale surface water features not explicitly modeled. However, unrealistic conductance terms can truncate residual distributions, capping groundwater elevation at the model surface. These truncated residuals can drive optimization techniques toward minimum‐error solutions that may not reflect reality. Careful constraints on surface drainage flux can mitigate this, but estimates of diffuse surface drainage are typically poor or nonexistent.

Instead of pursuing precise depth predictions, this research focuses on determining groundwater occurrence within specific depth thresholds; a shift from a regression to a classification approach. Classification techniques can mitigate data sparsity impacts through the use of existing robust oversampling of minority populations techniques. In addition, the approach directly addresses the core question of risk assessment: What is the probability of shallow groundwater occurrence? Finally, it facilitates explicit determination of Type I (false positive) and Type II (false negative) errors. By explicitly considering Type I errors (falsely predicting shallow groundwater) and Type II errors (failing to predict shallow groundwater), decision‐makers can better balance the trade‐offs between over‐preparation and under‐preparation based on their specific contexts and resources.

This research develops a high‐resolution, national‐scale shallow groundwater extent model using RF techniques, incorporating comprehensive predictor variables and adopting categorical depth classes to improve shallow groundwater prediction accuracy. The insights from this study will inform coastal management and climate adaptation in New Zealand, with potential benefits for other coastal regions facing similar challenges worldwide.

Methods

Study Area

This research encompasses the coastal and lowland areas of New Zealand, where explicitly defined as mapped aquifers by White et al. (2019) and below 100 m elevation NZVD2016 (New Zealand Vertical Datum 2016). The 100 m elevation threshold was, somewhat arbitrarily, chosen to ensure comprehensive coverage of all potentially affected areas, while excluding higher elevation regions where groundwater rise due to sea‐level rise is extremely unlikely. The study area covers approximately 46,000 km2 of the total land mass of New Zealand (c. 268,500 km2) International studies have generally limited the plausible inland elevation extent of relative sea‐level rise affects elevation up to 10 m above mean sea level (Kulp and Strauss 2019; Hill et al. 2023). The conservative approach adopted here allows capture of all areas that could potentially be at risk, including those that might be indirectly affected through connected hydrological systems and provides additional training data (e.g., those areas not at risk but with shallow groundwater). The study area was discretised into 100 m grid cells. This resolution was selected to provide detailed spatial information while remaining computationally feasible, and limiting false precision interpretations by end users. Figure 2 shows the coastal aquifers subject of interest to this study. The study areas include a diverse range of geological settings ranging from relatively impermeable volcanic deposits to highly permeable alluvial gravels (Westerhoff et al. 2019) with data availability ranging from data‐rich regions with extensive groundwater monitoring networks to data‐sparse areas with minimal direct groundwater observations. The diversity in both data density and physical character precludes some traditional approaches, such as geostatistics, but provides an ideal testbed for the adopted modeling methodology.

Modeling Approach

Existing approaches to estimate depth to water include numerical models, geostatistical interpolation, and machine learning methods, each with their own strengths and limitations (detailed analysis available in Appendix S3). This study adopts a RF machine learning approach, which directly addresses the core research questions while explicitly accounting for prediction uncertainty (Pedregosa et al. 2011). Random Forests (Breiman 2001) combine multiple decision trees through bootstrap aggregation, offering robust predictive capabilities across varied hydrogeological settings. This method has demonstrated success in national‐scale hydrological applications (Koch et al. 2019, 2021; Ma et al. 2024), particularly in handling diverse data types and sparse observations.

Machine learning models serve two main approaches to predictive modeling: classification and regression. While regression models are traditionally used for groundwater depth prediction due to the continuous nature of the target variable, analysis revealed specific characteristics of the data that favor a classification approach. The key consideration in the methodological choice was the highly skewed distribution of our target variable. This imbalance presents particular challenges for regression models, which tend to optimize for the majority population, potentially at the expense of accurate predictions for the minority class. While techniques such as Synthetic Minority Over‐sampling Technique for Regression (SMOTE‐R) exist to address such imbalances in regression contexts, these methods can experience overfitting and may not fully address the fundamental challenge of skewed data distributions (Torgo et al. 2013; Branco et al. 2017). In contrast, classification models offer well‐established and robust methods for handling class imbalances through various oversampling techniques (Chawla et al. 2002; He et al. 2008). Further, a classification approach benefits the study use case. Regression techniques attempt to minimize the error between predicted and observed values, which implicitly assumes that an over or under‐prediction is an “equally bad” outcome. Risk, hazard, and vulnerability assessments often require a more nuanced approach, as the potential cost of under‐prediction (Type II error, e.g., failing to identify shallow groundwater) can be significantly higher than over‐prediction (Type I error). The approach chosen here adopts a binary classification scheme, with an ensemble of models trained at 0.1 m increments from 0.5 to 2 m (and 0.5 m increments from 2 to 5 m) below surface. Depth increments were chosen pragmatically: data shallower than 0.9 m are too sparse to train the model, while depths >5 m have negligible effect on pasture yields and rural building foundations. The ensemble enables explicit analysis of the trade‐offs between Type I and II errors at different depths, providing flexibility for depth‐specific applications. Examining these tradeoffs helps to address the challenge of predicting very shallow groundwater while maintaining accuracy and allows estimates of the probability of shallow groundwater occurrence, providing a direct and explicit measure of risk.

Groundwater Depth Data

This study utilizes depth‐to‐water measurements from the Komanawa New Zealand Depth to Water Database (Durney et al. 2024), which contains approximately 5.7 million observations from 113,526 locations spanning 1899 to 2024. For alignment with climate predictor variables, the data was subset to post‐1950 observations. The database aggregates water level data from 17 regional councils, city councils, and unitary authorities, along with data from the New Zealand Geotechnical Database (Howe et al. 2020). Of the approximately 2.5 million observations from wells less than 30 m deep (79,000 locations), 16% record groundwater levels less than 1 m below surface, representing only 11% of monitoring sites. While the database provides comprehensive temporal data, over 90% of sites have fewer than 10 readings, highlighting the sparse temporal coverage across the monitoring network. Figure 2 shows the distribution of monitoring sites across New Zealand. Data from sites flagged as artesian in the original data, and those with water levels more than 1 m above the land surface (assumed artesian) were excluded from the analysis. Measurements slightly above the land surface (0 to 1 m) were retained to account for potential inaccuracies in elevation data, which are particularly prevalent in locations without LiDAR‐derived elevations, where less accurate national DEM products were used to infer the likely ground surface elevation (Durney et al. 2024).

Data Preprocessing

The data processing pipeline merged temporal and static predictor data on a 100 m resolution grid. The merged dataset underwent cleaning steps including removal of records with missing hydrogeological system information and adjustment of depth categories based on each site's maximum depth to water. Missing data were handled through scikit‐learn imputation techniques.

Daily climate variables (e.g., precipitation, PET) were first extracted into day, month, and year components to capture seasonal patterns. For each groundwater‐depth observation on date D, the same‐day predictor values were paired with rolling‐window summaries (7 day, monthly, quarterly, and annual mean, minimum, and maximum) computed over the interval D − n to D. This ensures that every depth record reflects both contemporaneous conditions and recent antecedent variability. Data was then aggregated to the 100 m grid, with each grid cell containing a time series of predictor values for each depth observation.

Second, the data was split by group to ensure all observations from individual sites remained in either training or testing sets. This process is essential to prevent data leakage (where information from the test dataset is used to train the model). Third, predictor datasets were transformed (e.g., scaling); further details are provided in the Appendix S4. Instead of manually computing cross‐correlations, PCA was relied upon to remove redundancy automatically. PCA analysis reduced the number of predictor variables from 199 to 37 while retaining 97.5% of the variance in the data (Pedregosa et al. 2011). The dimensionality reduction addresses multi‐collinearity, improves computational efficiency, and reduces the risk of overfitting as the lost variance (2.5%) cannot speciously inform the prediction. An optional hold‐out validation set was implemented for additional model performance assessment to confirm whether overfitting was a problem. The pipeline maintained spatial integrity throughout by preserving site‐specific relationships and ensuring temporal consistency in the predictor‐response relationships; full details are given in Appendix S4.

Model Training Process

The training process addressed three key challenges: class imbalance, model optimization, and depth‐specific prediction requirements, which are summarized here, with full details given in Appendix S5. Using a 70/30 train‐test split, ADASYN (He et al. 2008) oversampling was implemented to handle the significant imbalance in shallow groundwater observations. Observational weighting was also evaluated to address class imbalance but performed worse than ADASYN oversampling. ADASYN was applied exclusively to the training data; the test and holdout datasets were left unchanged to ensure unbiased model evaluation.

This approach adaptively generates synthetic samples based on data distribution, focusing on harder‐to‐learn examples. Model hyperparameters were optimized through manual tuning with five‐fold cross‐validation, using ROC‐AUC as the primary performance metric. The final RF configuration used 500 trees, a maximum depth of 20, a minimum 10 samples per leaf, and a minimum 4 samples per split. These parameters balanced model complexity with generalization ability. Balanced class weights and the Gini impurity criterion were employed for node splitting, with both cross‐validation and out‐of‐bag scoring providing unbiased accuracy estimates. Model robustness was verified through stochastic testing with multiple random test/train splits to ensure performance stability, and performance testing against the holdout data.

Model Evaluation and Performance Metrics

Model performance was assessed on the test split with eight complementary metrics: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1, F2, ROC‐AUC, Powers Kappa, and MCC. Their formal definitions are listed in Appendix S2, while Appendix S1 is a glossary of commonly used terms.

Accuracy is reported, however, due to class imbalance, a naive model predicting all records as “deep” would appear highly accurate while missing the minority shallow cases (Chawla 2005). Therefore, ROC‐AUC was used as the primary selection criterion because it is prevalence‐independent and reflects ranking quality for the minority class. Precision and Recall quantify false‐positive and false‐negative trade‐offs; their F‐scores (F 1 and recall‐heavy F 2) summarize this balance. MCC and Powers Kappa add symmetric, bias‐corrected agreement measures that remain reliable under skewed class ratios (Powers 2012; Chicco and Jurman 2023) and have been used here to assess model performance across all depth thresholds.

Confusion‐matrix inspection then guided the choice of a decision threshold that optimized the recall‐precision balance for shallow‐water detections. The final hyper‐tuned models were refitted on the complete dataset to generate national‐scale DTW ensemble predictions.

Principal component analysis (PCA) reduces dimensionality and multicollinearity but at the expense of direct interpretability. To recover feature‐level insights, all predictors were standardized (zero mean, unit variance) before PCA, and the 37 principal components explaining 97.5% of total variance were retained. Each component's importance I j was then assessed using the Random Forest Gini impurity metric, which measures the total decrease in node impurity (weighted by the probability of reaching that node) across all trees. National‐Scale Predictions: Application to 70‐Year Averaged Day‐of‐Year Transient Simulations

The key output of the model is a series of (100 m by 100 m resolution) national spatial arrays presenting the probability of the depth to water being less than a certain threshold. To generate these maps, the trained models were reduced to those with adequate performance metrics and applied to a typological day‐of‐year transient simulation, which was used to capture the seasonal variability and a representative annual cycle. The uniform grid was applied in the New Zealand Transverse Mercator (EPSG:2193) coordinate reference system, which has minimal distortion and edge effect across New Zealand. This day‐of‐year typology was produced by averaging the transient predictor variables on each day of the year (1 to 365, leap days excluded) for a 70‐year period. These layers were then predicted for each depth threshold across the study area and can be used to identify areas at risk of groundwater rise due to sea‐level rise.

Variables of Importance

Using the inverse importance, which represents the spatial importance, calculated from the RF weightings and PCA loadings, of the 199 predictor variables, 10 variables consistently emerged as most influential across all models. These 10 remained consistently important across all the investigated depth thresholds rankings with minor shifts in relative positions. Well depth category ranked among the top three variables for all models from 0.9 to 2.0 m depth thresholds. Distance to wetland, both current and pre‐human, consistently appeared in the top 10 variables, reflecting its persistent relevance. Hydrogeological systems—representing mapped hydrological units—remained among the top 10 variables for all but one depth threshold. Similarly, the distance to the nearest river consistently ranked within the top 10 variables across all depth increments. Groundwater porosity and annual mean precipitation (derived from ERA5‐Land datasets) regularly appeared in the upper tier of influential predictors. Soil drainage ranked higher in models for shallower depths (0.9 to 1.7 m) than for deeper thresholds, suggesting that near‐surface conditions have a particular sensitivity to drainage properties. Finally, groundwater flow classes also emerged as important, although with more variable rankings. Full variable importance tables are provided in Appendix S5.

Type I and II Errors with Depth Threshold

The analysis of false positive rates (Type I error) and false negative (Type II error) rates in the test data set reveals distinct patterns across depth and probability thresholds. These data are indicative of the expected performance of the model in predicting shallow groundwater occurrence at different depth thresholds where no data is currently available for model training. Figure 3 shows the Type I error rate (Type II error rates are the inverse proportions). As expected, error rates vary systematically with both probability and depth to water thresholds. By definition, a probability threshold of 0.0 (all sites are shallow) means a false positive rate approaching 1 and a false negative rate approaching 0.

False positive rate (Type I error) with depth and probability threshold.

The relationship between depth threshold and error rates is more complex. In the mid‐probability range (0.15 to 0.4), Type I error rates peak at 0.4 to 0.5 in the middle depth range (1.3 to 2.0 m), forming a dome‐shaped pattern with lower values at both shallow (0.5 to 0.8 m) and deep (>3.0 m) thresholds. The lowest Type I error rates (<0.05) occur with probability thresholds above 0.85, regardless of depth threshold. Type II error rates increase with both depth and probability thresholds, showing marked linearity between 1.0 and 2.5 m depths. For depth thresholds above 2.5 m, Type II error rates exceed 0.7 when probability thresholds surpass 0.8. A notable inflection point occurs at approximately 0.9 to 1.0 m depth threshold, where Type II rates begin increasing more rapidly.

The trade‐off between Type I and II errors is quantified by the trade‐off analysis (Figure 4). Models using depth thresholds between 0.5 and 0.8 m show consistently higher error rates, with the 0.5 m threshold model's error curves intersecting other depth thresholds. In contrast, depth thresholds above 0.8 m demonstrate superior performance in both error metrics. Based on this analysis, models with depth thresholds between 0.5 and 0.8 m were excluded from further analysis, focusing instead on depth thresholds between 0.9 and 2.0 m which showed optimal error trade‐offs.

National Scale Predictions

The practical implications of model performance are reflected in the national scale predictions. Figure 5 illustrates the relationship between predicted shallow groundwater areas and Type II error rates across depth thresholds ranging from 0.9 to 2.0 m. The area is calculated by summing the area of all grid cells predicted to have shallow groundwater at the probability threshold associated with the accepted Type II error rate. The relationship between predicted area and Type II error (false negative rate) exhibits a consistent exponential decay across all depth thresholds. The steepest decline in predicted area occurs within the 0 to 20% Type II error range for all depth thresholds, showing an approximate 60% reduction (from approximately 46,000 to 18,000 km2). This sharp decrease indicates that minimizing false negatives requires accepting a substantially larger spatial extent and consequently more false positives. Finally, for Type II error rates above 60%, the prediction curves converge to identify approximately 2000 km2 of high‐confidence shallow groundwater areas. These regions represent locations where the model achieves optimal true positive rates despite high false negatives, effectively identifying the most confidently predicted shallow groundwater areas while sacrificing the detection of less certain shallow groundwater zones.

Change in modeled area (nation‐wide) with accepted Type II error for each depth threshold.

To visualize the spatial distribution of predictions at different Type II error thresholds we use three local case studies (Figure 6). Similar to the national results a reduction in acceptable Type II error directly results in more area being classified as shallow groundwater, which includes both new correct classifications (previously false negatives) and incorrect classifications (Type I errors). The western declines in predicted shallow groundwater areas, seen in the Waimakariri and Kaituna study areas, is explained by the increasingly steep groundwater gradient. For Dunedin the topography is a key driver of groundwater conditions, with the city area being characterized by low topography and shallow predicted groundwater levels. The statistical model incorporates this relationship using the “distance to coast” and “elevation” predictors. These results align with the established understanding of general groundwater conditions in these areas. Dunedin is known for its high groundwater tables and associated flooding risks Cox et al. (2023). Kaituna is recognized as a region with persistently high groundwater levels, necessitating extensive artificial drainage to support farming activities Savoldelli et al. (2020). Similarly, Waikamakiri is also characterized by consistently high groundwater levels Dodson et al. (2012).

Discussion

Model Performance

The RF models demonstrate robust performance across multiple evaluation metrics, effectively addressing the challenges of imbalanced data in groundwater prediction. The systematic improvement in discriminative ability with depth, evidenced by increasing ROC‐AUC values, reflects the models' enhanced reliability at greater depths where data representation is stronger. This pattern aligns with known data sparsity issues in shallow groundwater monitoring. The observed trade‐off between precision and recall, particularly at shallow depths, has important implications for risk assessment. The higher recall values at shallow depths indicate a conservative approach that prioritizes identifying potential risk areas, even at the cost of false positives. This trade‐off is particularly valuable in groundwater rise risk assessment, where failing to identify at‐risk areas (false negatives) could have significant consequences. The consistent improvement in Powers Kappa and MCC with depth provides evidence that the models are making increasingly informed predictions as depth increases, rather than simply reflecting dataset characteristics. This multi‐metric evaluation approach is especially valuable given the implications of classification errors in groundwater management, where misidentification of shallow groundwater conditions can have substantial practical consequences.

Physical Basis of Model Predictions

The alignment between the statistically derived importance variables and known physical drivers of groundwater systems provides evidence that the statistical model is robustly emulating physical processes in its predictions. The consistent emergence of well depth category as a key predictor reflects the established understanding of vertical flow stratification in groundwater systems. Similarly, the high importance of surface water features (wetlands and rivers) aligns with established theory on groundwater‐surface water interactions and recharge‐discharge processes. The prominence of hydrogeological systems, porosity, and flow classes variables demonstrates the model's ability to capture subsurface characteristics that control groundwater movement and storage.

The alignment between statistical importance and physical drivers supports the model's predictive capabilities and enhances confidence in its application to practical decision‐making contexts. It suggests that the numerical processes (PCA and RF) are effectively capturing real‐world hydrogeological relationships, despite being purely data‐driven. This approach suggests that the investment in detailed geological and pedological mapping has provided real benefits to the model's predictive capabilities and thus to resource and hazard management. Further, it suggests significant potential for developing hybrid models that combine statistical and process‐based approaches, which could further enhance the model's interpretability and predictive power. Finally, the importance of climate variables in the model further suggests that groundwater inundation is likely to be influenced by the intersection of meteorological and relative sea level rise impacts of climate change, highlighting the importance of considering all aspects of the hydrological cycle in adaptation planning.

Depth and Probability Threshold Analysis: Insights and Implications for Risk Assessment and Policy Making

The analysis of the modeled depth thresholds provides crucial insights into the model's performance and limitations, particularly given the training dataset's imbalanced nature. As detailed in the Introduction and Methods, and illustrated in Table 1, the training dataset is significantly biased toward deeper groundwater levels, with few wells targeting shallow depths. The training data imbalance, despite robust oversampling, poses challenges in model performance, especially for shallower depth thresholds.

A trade‐off analysis of Type I and Type II errors (Figure 4) provides an explicit representation of the trade‐offs between precision (e.g., depth threshold) and error rates. Trade‐off analysis shows how different probability thresholds can be set to prescribe the acceptable Type II (false negative) threshold for a given use‐case. This analysis is inversely related to Youden's J statistic (Youden 1950), which maximizes sensitivity and specificity. The trade‐off curves for the models with depth thresholds less than 0.9 m are relatively flat and do not follow the typical convex shape of a trade‐off analysis front. This confirms the models' struggle to reliably predict shallow groundwater at shallow depth thresholds. This could be due to either data scarcity or model non‐linearity, but regardless, it suggests that statistical models cannot reliably predict shallow groundwater below 0.9 m using the datasets available in this study. Physical models are more likely to be able to predict groundwater shallower than 0.9 m as they rely on simplified physical principles and not data availability. However, physical models are subject to bias, and predictions at this scale are unlikely to be able to be validated unless they are produced in local data‐rich areas. Consequently, any model predictions (physical or statistical) of depth to water below 0.9 m in New Zealand should be treated with extreme caution due to a significant risk of model bias and false precision.

Returning to the trade‐off, the 1:1 line represents the point where Type I and Type II errors are equal, providing a balance point between sensitivity and specificity. Moving toward the upper right of the 1:1 line on the trade‐off corresponds to selecting depth thresholds that allow for more precise depth determinations due to finer depth increments. However, this increased precision results in higher Type I and Type II error rates, leading to reduced overall model reliability. Conversely, moving toward the lower left reduces error rates and increases overall reliability by using broader depth increments, but at the expense of depth precision.

The most balanced model (i.e., the point closest to the 1:1 line) is often not the best model for a given use‐case as the consequences of a Type I (false positive) and Type II (false negative) error are rarely equal. As an example, consider a coastal management scenario where a false positive (Type I error) could result in additional consenting and design costs, while a false negative (Type II error) could lead to critical infrastructure exposure to groundwater inundation. Here, the cost of a Type II error is far greater than a Type I error, and the model should be selected to reduce Type II errors, even at the expense of increased Type I errors.

The classification approach, coupled with trade‐off analysis, offers a clearer quantification of uncertainty and risk than traditional regression, directly linking model performance to the costs of Type I and Type II errors. By presenting a categorical ensemble and its full error trade‐off curve, the method bridges complex numerical modeling and policy practice: decision‐makers can place their own operating point on the trade‐off, balancing false positives, false negatives, depth precision, and unavoidable model error to match their objectives. A flood‐control agency, for instance, might minimize Type I errors to avoid costly unnecessary works, while an insurer could prioritize reducing Type II errors to limit financial exposure. Scientists thus supply transparent tools and evidence; rather than prescribing thresholds—leaving stakeholders, who best understand industry‐specific factors, to define acceptable risk.

Limitations of the Study

Despite the advantages of our approach, several limitations warrant consideration. First and foremost, the model lacks a mechanism to directly predict the impacts of future sea‐level rise scenarios, limiting its applicability in climate change adaptation planning. Additionally, while the model performs well for current conditions, it may struggle to represent the impacts of increased meteorological variability under climate change and is unlikely to be able to predict the impacts of extreme events.

The data drive nature of the approach means that the model is limited by the existing datasets. In data‐sparse regions, there may be other factors or processes that are not captured in the model, as there is simply no data to inform the existence of those processes. As an explicit example, the model struggles to predict shallow groundwater below 0.9 m; this is likely due to data scarcity in this depth range. Physical models may be better placed to predict these processes; however, the fundamental issue of data scarcity remains, which may preclude adequate validation of these models.

Additionally, the categorical framework used in this study, while effective for identifying areas of shallow groundwater potential, only predicts whether depth is above or below a threshold and does not discern depth within or beyond those thresholds. Future work could explore hybrid classification‐regression models to characterize uncertainty near decision thresholds and enable continuous‐depth mapping where data permit.

Predicted probabilities are used as a ranking mechanism to optimize classification performance, not as direct estimates of event frequencies. Due to oversampling in training, raw probabilities are biased, and attempts at calibration degraded discrimination, which is critical for this application. Future work may explore calibration methods that preserve ranking ability.

Finally, it is emphasized that this tool is intended as an initial screening approach. Users should confirm any flagged “shallow” locations with site‐specific field measurements. Model application is restricted to mapped aquifer extents that include hydrogeological predictors (e.g., estimated hydraulic conductivity, specific yield) to mitigate, but not eliminate, the risk of misclassification in atypical or newly reclaimed coastal zones.

Conclusions

This study has produced a national scale model ensemble of shallow groundwater extent at 0.1 m depth increments between 0.9 and 2 m for New Zealand and explicitly quantified the relationship between Type I and II errors and depth precision between 0.5 and 5 m depth thresholds. The models consistently achieved strong performance metrics (ROC‐AUC values of 0.823 to 0.962) and predictor variables aligned with known hydrogeological drivers, further enhancing confidence in the model's predictions. Notably, analysis revealed that model performance improves with increasing depth thresholds, with models predicting depth to water at thresholds lower than 0.9 m being highly unreliable. It is suggested that this is a fundamental limitation of the available data, and while physical models can provide some guidance, the data limitations are likely to prevent adequate validation of the models at these depths.

The categorical ensemble approach effectively addresses many limitations of traditional regression models. In particular, it reduces false precision, bias toward majority classes, and inadequate uncertainty quantification. Past studies have shown a high potential for Type II errors using regression techniques to predict shallow groundwater. Type II errors (predicting deep groundwater where the groundwater is shallow) can have significantly higher consequences than Type I errors. This study demonstrates that a trade‐off analysis of Type I and Type II errors provides a powerful framework for optimizing model utility. This approach enables stakeholders to select appropriate depth and Type II error thresholds based on specific risk tolerances, consequences, and management objectives. Enabling direct inclusion of stakeholder values represents a significant advancement in linking statistical modeling directly to policy decisions. This approach demonstrates how machine learning techniques can be effectively leveraged not just for improved predictive accuracy, but also for enhanced decision support in complex environmental management scenarios. By facilitating a direct link between model outputs and policy decisions, while explicitly accounting for uncertainty and risk tolerance, the methodology presents an alternative approach for more informed and nuanced environmental management strategies in the face of climate change.
