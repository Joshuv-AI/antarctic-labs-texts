Molecular‐Based Risk Prediction Models for Recurrence After Curative Treatment of Early‐Stage Lung Cancer: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Aaron Ling, Evangeline Samuel, Rahini Mahendran, John Zalcberg, Rob G. Stirling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecurrence after curative treatment remains a major challenge in early‐stage non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Molecular‐based prediction models may improve risk stratification and support personalized surveillance strategies. To identify and evaluate molecular‐based risk prediction models for recurrence and survival following curative treatment of early‐stage NSCLC. A systematic review and meta‐analysis were conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines and a published PROSPERO protocol. MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library were searched for studies published between January 2000 and December 2023. Eligible studies reported performance metrics of molecular‐based models predicting recurrence‐free survival, cancer‐specific survival, or overall survival following curative treatment for NSCLC. Data extraction was performed using the CHARMS framework, risk of bias was assessed using PROBAST, and model performance metrics were pooled using random‐effects meta‐analysis. Of 2447 records identified, five studies met the inclusion criteria. All models used Cox proportional hazards regression. Molecular predictors included mRNA expression profiles (n = 3), long noncoding RNAs (n = 1), and DNA methylation biomarkers (n = 1). Internal validation studies reported AUC values ranging from 0.66 to 0.89, with a pooled AUC of 0.77 (95% CI = 0.66–0.90; I 2 = 88.4%). Two externally validated models reported AUC values of 0.68–0.74, with a pooled AUC of 0.72 (95% CI = 0.68–0.75; I 2 = 0%). Two studies were considered high risk of bias due to inappropriate handling of missing data. Molecular‐based prediction models demonstrate moderate‐to‐good discriminative performance for recurrence and survival in early‐stage NSCLC. However, broader external validation and improved methodological rigor are required before routine clinical implementation. Trial Registration: PROSPERO identifier: CRD42024599641

Introduction

Lung cancer is the most common cause of cancer‐related deaths globally, with age‐standardized mortality rates in 2019 reported at 37.3 per 100 000 for men and 14.9 for women [1, 2]. The 5‐year relative survival rate remains low, at just 21% [3]. It is primarily divided into two categories: non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), accounting for 85% of cases, and small cell lung cancer (SCLC), comprising the remaining 15% [4].

Recent developments in the treatment of NSCLC, such as targeted therapy and immunotherapies, have greatly enhanced patient outcomes. Prognostic prediction, as highlighted by the American Joint Committee on Cancer, is essential for informing clinical decisions [5, 6]. Prognosis is mainly assessed using the tumor, node, and metastasis (TNM) staging system, which classifies patients into Stages I–IV based on clinical and pathological factors [6].

Patients with Stages I–III NSCLC generally undergo curative treatments, including surgery, stereotactic radiotherapy, or radical chemo‐radiotherapy, as advised by international guidelines from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) [7] and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) [8].

Despite these advancements, NSCLC still has a high rate of recurrence after curative surgery, with 30%–55% of patients experiencing a relapse [9]. Treatment failure can lead to both locoregional recurrence and distant metastasis and is the leading cause of mortality among patients with lung cancer [10, 11]. Locoregional recurrences are described as new disease sites confined to the ipsilateral regional lymph nodes, the bronchial stump, the staple line, or the remaining lung lobe when a sublobar resection had been done. Any recurrences occurring elsewhere, such as in pleural or pericardial effusions, were considered distant recurrences [12]. The prognosis is worse in patients presenting with distant metastasis as a recurrence pattern than in those presenting with locoregional recurrence [13]. Therefore, it remains crucial to recognize and risk‐stratify those especially at risk of disease recurrences.

There is no agreed‐upon standard for the optimal frequency or method of post‐treatment surveillance imaging, resulting in differing guidelines from various oncology organizations. These recommendations are often based on lower‐quality evidence and expert opinions [14]. The NCCN suggests a chest computed tomography (CT) every 6 months for 2–3 years, followed by annual scans for patients with Stages I–II NSCLC who have had surgery [15]. The American Academy of Chest Physicians recommends bi‐annual CTs for 2 years, then annual scans [16]. The American Association for Thoracic Surgery advises bi‐annual CTs for the first 4 years after surgery, followed by annual scans until the age of 79 [17]. The European Society for Medical Oncology recommends at least annual CT scans, or bi‐annual if salvage therapy may be required [18]. The heterogeneity across multiple guideline recommendations reflects the uncertainties around gold standard surveillance and further justifies the formulation of risk‐stratified surveillance approaches.

Prognostic models that incorporate multiple factors to estimate patient outcomes have the potential to enhance personalized surveillance strategies. By combining variables such as age, gender, disease stage, and tumor characteristics, these models can help predict outcomes like overall survival (OS) or disease progression. However, before being applied in clinical practice, these models must undergo validation for both performance and generalizability through internal and external validation [19, 20, 21].

As lung cancer screening and curative treatments continue to grow, personalized surveillance strategies are becoming increasingly important [20, 22]. Ongoing progress in molecular research has led to new and effective approaches for assessing individual lung cancer risk. Biomarkers consist of detecting unusual concentrations of certain molecules (such as single‐nucleotide polymorphisms [SNPs], proteins, or hormones) in blood, other body fluids, or tissues, which can indicate the presence or progression of disease [23]. Molecular‐based models are defined as models incorporating tumor‐derived molecular biomarkers (e.g., genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, or epigenetic features), either alone or in combination with clinical and pathological variables. For the purpose of this study, purely molecular‐based models are included within this definition.

This systematic review aimed to assess and compare the effectiveness of molecular‐based risk prediction models for recurrence or survival following curative treatment in early‐stage or locally advanced NSCLC. The goal was to identify, describe, and evaluate models developed to predict OS, recurrence‐free survival (RFS), or cancer‐specific survival (CSS) in patients with early‐stage (Stages I and II) NSCLC who have received curative treatment.

Methods

We conducted our review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analyses (PRISMA) guidelines for evaluating prediction model performance [24], with the published protocol registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024599641). Results are reported according to the TRIPOD‐SRMA guidelines [25].

Information Sources and Search Strategy

We performed a systematic search of Medline, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library for studies published between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2025, using keywords related to “NSCLC/lung cancer,” “recurrence, survival, or prognosis,” “prediction models or scores,” and “risk assessment, nomograms, or clinical decision support tools.” Additionally, we manually reviewed reference lists and carried out citation searches of the included studies.

Inclusion Criteria

Studies were included if they were peer‐reviewed and evaluated the performance of risk models in predicting outcomes for patients who received curative treatment for Stages I and II NSCLC. The rationale for not including Stage III disease is that there has been rapidly emerging evidence of novel approaches to its management over recent years, such as neoadjuvant treatment, which can lead to higher levels of heterogeneity and reduce the precision of combined results in our study. The time‐to‐event outcomes assessed in these studies included OS, CSS, and RFS, with RFS covering events such as disease recurrence, progression to metastatic disease, local recurrence, and metastasis‐free survival.

Eligible studies involved patients aged over 18 years with Stages I and II NSCLC (any histological subtype) who underwent curative‐intent treatment. This included surgery alone or in combination with chemotherapy or radiotherapy, stereotactic radiotherapy, or radical radiotherapy. In addition, only models that underwent internal or external validation were included, and those reporting on a performance metric like C‐index, ROC (receiver operating characteristic curve), or AUC were included. Although both the C‐index and AUC quantify how well a model discriminates between outcomes, they are not fully interchangeable. The C‐index is tailored for time‐to‐event data that include censoring, whereas the AUC is generally used for binary outcomes or outcomes assessed at a fixed time point [26]. One of the advantages of utilizing ROC AUC as the performance metric is that it provides a single summary value that allows tests to be compared without separately balancing sensitivity and specificity. It is often promoted as reflecting the overall diagnostic performance because it represents an average over every possible diagnostic threshold [27]. Only studies reporting C‐index, ROC, or AUC were included to ensure a consistent and comparable measure of model discrimination across studies, thereby enabling quantitative synthesis through meta‐analysis.

Exclusion Criteria

Patients diagnosed with Stage III or IV NSCLC, SCLC, carcinoid cancer, mesothelioma, thymoma, or non‐lung cancer metastases were excluded. The review also excluded studies focusing on purely clinico‐pathological variables, including only models with molecular‐based predictor variables.

Data Items and Collection Process

Two authors, A.L. and E.S., independently extracted data into structured tables. Any discrepancies between their entries were discussed and resolved through consensus. Data extraction for each prediction model was guided by the Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modelling Studies (CHARMS) checklist [28]. Extracted information included: (1) author and year, study design, setting, study population, participant selection (where applicable), variables included in the model, and the criteria used for their selection; and (2) model performance metrics (such as discrimination and calibration) and any reported impact of the prediction model on clinical decision‐making.

Risk of Bias (RoB) and Applicability Assessment

The PROBAST tool was implemented to independently evaluate for bias in each model [29]. Key data for assessing RoB were gathered simultaneously, and any disagreements were resolved jointly by the two authors (Table 2). PROBAST consists of four domains: participants, predictors, outcome, and analysis. A prediction model was categorized as having a “low ROB” when a low ROB was observed in every domain, whereas a prediction model was classified as having a “high ROB” when a high ROB was detected in at least one domain.

Synthesis Methods

Models were compared descriptively based on the types of variables used, the outcomes predicted, and the performance metrics reported. Data were grouped according to performance indicators (e.g., C‐statistic, ROC curve, and AUC curve) and outcome categories (e.g., RFS, OS, and CSS).

Model Performance

Measures of Predictive Model Performance

There are some guidelines for conducting meta‐analyses to assess how well prognostic models predict outcomes. For example, Debray et al. [30] suggested a guideline based on discrimination and calibration, as well as investigating heterogeneity across studies. Moons et al. [31] established the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guideline, which includes a 22‐item checklist outlining key elements necessary for the proper reporting of studies that develop or validate multivariable prediction models. Similarly, our review adapts to a model utilizing calibration and discrimination as measures of performance. Calibration is essential to prevent overestimation or underestimation of events that could lead to misguided clinical decisions [32]. Discrimination is quantified using the concordance index (c‐index). Values of 0.7, 0.8, or 0.9 represent strong discriminatory performance, whereas a c‐index of 0.5 indicates no discrimination (equivalent to random chance), and a value of 1.0 signifies perfect discrimination, meaning the model accurately ranks individuals by their outcome risk [19].

Meta‐Analysis

To estimate the pooled model performance metrics, we performed a random‐effect meta‐analysis. First, all reported AUC values of eligible studies were transformed using the logit method, and pooled estimates were calculated using a random‐effects model to account for between‐study heterogeneity. Among the six included studies, three [24, 33, 34] did not report confidence intervals (CIs). For these, the standard errors (SEs) and 95% CIs were derived using the Hanley and McNeil method [35]. The approach relies on the mathematical equivalence between the AUC and the Wilcoxon (Mann–Whitney U) statistic. In this framework, the AUC is interpreted as the probability that a randomly chosen diseased individual is assigned a higher level of suspicion than a randomly chosen non‐diseased individual [36]. It presumes that the data used for classifying individuals can be arranged along a single continuum, where higher scores typically support classifying as “abnormal.” In applying this approach, it was assumed that approximately 30% of the study population survived the event during the follow‐up period [37]. It offers a closed‐form formula that enables fast computation, bypassing the need for intricate iterative curve‐fitting or maximum likelihood estimation [35]. The meta‐analysis results presented in the main manuscript are based on this 30% survival‐rate assumption. If the true survival rate across the combined studies deviates substantially from 30%, the Hanley and McNeil method may underestimate the necessary sample size, resulting in underpowered analyses for the pooled data [38]. To evaluate the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were also conducted assuming 20% and 40% survival rates.

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.3) using the “meta” and “metafor” packages for meta‐analysis.

Results

Study Selection

We identified 2447 articles. After manually screening titles and abstracts, 2200 were removed. Of the remaining 247, 98 duplicates were removed using the Covidence PRISMA flowchart (Figure 1). Of the 149 studies screened, 143 were excluded due to factors like incorrect intervention, patient population, study design, or setting. The final list included five studies with molecular‐based variables available for this review.

Risk Factors

Table 1 includes the various molecular‐based predictors utilized, ranging from messenger ribonucleic acid (mRNA) expressions, long noncoding RNAs, to deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) methylation biomarkers. Out of five studies, mRNA expression was investigated as a predictor in three studies [39, 41], long noncoding RNAs in one study [34], and DNA methylation biomarkers in one study [41]. The mRNA expression model in Bueno et al. [39] was a 50‐gene prognostic signature developed by the University of Michigan (UM) based on Affymetrix U133A mRNA profiling. It combines the UM signature alongside clinico‐pathological variables to generate a risk score, which correlated with OS. Liu et al. [41] identified a metabolic signature (RAMS) represented by two genes (ACADM and RPS 58), which were negative prognostic markers and were involved in tumor progression. There is quite substantial evidence supporting the role of ACADM as a tumor suppressor and prognostic biomarker across various malignancies such as hepatocellular carcinoma, pancreatic cancer, and clear cell renal cell carcinoma [42, 43, 44]. On the other hand, there has not been much literature data on the significance of RPS 58 beyond Bueno et al. [39].

Zhang et al. [34] identified an eight‐gene model based on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) that was associated with recurrence of lung adenocarcinoma following curative surgery. One of the eight genes, MYBPH, is identified as a transcriptional target of thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF‐1), which is not only a lung development regulator that regulates lung epithelial morphogenesis, but also a highly sensitive marker for the distinction of lung adenocarcinomas in clinical practice [37].

Lissa et al. [40] aimed to validate a promoter methylation of HOXA9 and its association with the prognosis of NSCLC. The model showed that a high HOXA9 promoter methylation corresponds to a poorer prognosis in NSCLC; however, the mechanisms of effect on survival remain unclear.

Studies With External Validation

Two out of five studies had external validation. Bueno et al. [39] established a risk prediction model for OS after curative resection of early‐stage squamous cell lung carcinoma. The model looked at mRNA expression based on Affymetrix U133A microarrays and developed a 50‐gene signature using the UM data set.

Peng et al. [33] developed a risk prediction model for OS after curative resection of early‐stage lung adenocarcinoma. The model looked at long noncoding RNAs based on the Genetic Expression Omnibus (GEO) microarray and found significance in six long noncoding RNAs (SCAT1, MIR193BHG, LINC01671, MIR223HG, LINC00261, and AC115099.1).

Pooled Model Performance Metrics

Figure 2 reviews the performance and outcome metrics from each study based on a forest plot. Based on Figure 2, four studies with internal validation reported AUC ranging from 0.66 to 0.89, with a pooled AUC of 0.77 (95% CI = 0.66–0.90) and a high heterogeneity (I 2 = 88.4%, p < 0.0001). On the other hand, two studies with external validation reported AUC ranging from 0.68 to 0.74, with a pooled AUC of 0.72 and a low heterogeneity (I 2 = 0%, p = 0.4159). Due to the small number of studies, subgroup analyses or meta‐regression were not performed. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the pooled model performance metrics, as AUC estimates remained stable despite changing the survival rate, although heterogeneity in internal validation persisted. Sensitivity analyses results, conducted assuming 20% and 40% survival rates, are presented in Figures S1 and S2.

Liu et al. [41] shaped a model accomplishing the highest AUC of 0.89 for OS in 243 patients with Stage 1 lung adenocarcinoma who underwent curative surgery. The study predicts OS based on two mRNA expression gene signatures (ACADM and RPS8).

The results from Bueno et al. [39] demonstrated a risk of death at 3 years achieved a C‐index of 0.744 (95% CI = 0.684–0.804) in internal validation and 0.683 (95% CI = 0.610–0.756) in external validation, based on the combination of UM 50‐gene signature and risk factors such as age, sex, and tumor classification (American Joint Committee on Cancer Eighth ed.) [45]. However, the potential for selection bias due to selected inclusion criteria preventing all patients from providing genetic samples from the original specimen banking study may limit broader clinical application.

The results from Peng et al. [33] showed that the 3‐year ROC curve analysis was 0.659 in internal validation and 0.708 in external validation based on the six long noncoding RNAs selected. Nonetheless, factors such as the limited information on somatic mutations and multi‐institutional patient cohort may restrict practical implications.

The dataset available includes only AUC and C‐index values, and the consistency of reported calibration metrics could not be identified across the included studies. Therefore, a quantitative synthesis of calibration outcomes could not be performed or justified. The inability to pool data for calibration metrics could result in reduced statistical reliability of the model's true performance.

Discussion

Key Findings

This systematic review examined five risk prediction models assessing recurrence and survival outcomes in early‐stage NSCLC after curative therapy. Pertinent findings of at least moderate to strong discrimination models are demonstrated, with an area under the curve (AUC) value ranging from 0.66 to 0.89. The pooled AUC values of 0.77 and 0.72 in the internally validated and externally validated studies, respectively, highlight good to excellent discrimination, further enhancing the stability of model performance. The best model achieved an AUC of 0.89 for OS. Two out of five models utilized mRNA expressions as molecular‐based predictors but with no overlap of gene signatures. The fact that only two out of the five studies had external validation can impact the generalisability of the models.

Model Generalizability

External validation was performed in two out of the five models. Three out of five studies are based in China [33, 34, 41], whereas the other two studies originated from the United States [39, 40] The small number of included studies, as well as the limited external validation, does impact the generalizability of the performance models. The fact that no gene signatures overlapped between the mRNA‐based models can also limit comparability between performance models. Future models should account for the increasing adoption of neoadjuvant immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy for actionable mutations. Strong and large‐scale external validation studies are essential to confirm the generalizability of these models. One [39] out of five models looked at early‐stage lung carcinoma of only squamous cell histology, whereas the remaining four models [33, 34, 40, 41] looked at early‐stage lung carcinoma of only adenocarcinoma histology. Studies that contain both histologies and other entities of non‐small cell lung carcinomas, such as large cell, would improve their clinical applicability by making them more inclusive and easier to use.

The high heterogeneity observed in internal validation (I 2 = 88.4%) may be attributed to differences in study characteristics, including variations in model development approaches, study populations, and small sample sizes, such as Lissa et al. [40] and Liu et al. [41] leading to statistical outliers. Although most studies were conducted in similar populations (e.g., lung adenocarcinoma in China and the United States), which could limit applicability to other regions or ethnic groups, the methodological differences across studies may have contributed to variability in model performance.

Availability of Risk Factors

A key factor for clinical implementation is how readily the required risk factors for model calculation can be accessed. These molecular‐based predictors involve extraction processes that are not readily available and require a huge investment of time and effort. Studies focusing on purely clinic‐pathological variables or risk factors are excluded based on the exclusion criteria of this review.

Recommendations

This review underscores the important role of risk prediction models in forecasting recurrence and OS, particularly given the stage shift resulting from global lung cancer screening initiatives. Nonetheless, it also points out that, despite their promise, no single model has yet emerged as the clear frontrunner. Future studies should focus on creating an inclusive model specifically designed for Stages I–IIIA NSCLC after curative resection. This model should incorporate easily obtainable predictors, including clinicopathological and molecular factors, as well as radiomic features. In addition, future models should also account for patients undergoing neoadjuvant treatments with emerging data supporting various options, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy, to improve the wider generalizability of data. Moreover, validation across diverse patient populations from different geographic regions is essential to strengthen its generalizability and reliability.

Strengths and Limitations

The review specifically includes studies that integrate molecular‐based predictors, excluding those relying solely on clinicopathological factors. It employs clearly defined inclusion criteria and the PROBAST tool for bias assessment. Designed by methodological experts, PROBAST provides a rigorous framework for quality evaluation and is widely used across multiple research domains.

However, the study faces several limitations. The absence of CIs in some studies could lead to potential RoB. The pooled AUC cannot be generalized for the disease. A statistical method based on Hanley and McNeil [35] was generated to provide a more meaningful interpretation of AUC in studies without reported CIs using different event rates. The limitation regarding the genetic sample availability of Bueno et al. [39] could lead to potential bias in the estimation of the AUC/C‐index. The restriction to English‐language publications also contributes to the review's limitations. Furthermore, only two of the six studies included were externally validated, which reduces the generalizability of the findings. Moreover, inadequate transparency regarding the handling of missing data in some of these studies would have also increased the RoB. Besides, the limited number of externally validated models, as well as the lack of ethnic, geographic, and healthcare system diversity, could impact the overall discriminative performance of the risk prediction models. The predominance of studies from the United States and China, especially, may limit applicability to other regions or ethnic groups. In addition, the studies did not report other performance metrics such as calibration or clinical impact, as they primarily focus on discrimination, which could lead to publication or selection bias.

Future Directions

Future studies should focus on aligning comprehensive risk adjustment strategies with real‐world clinical practice. Although advanced models incorporating molecular and radiomic features offer strong predictive accuracy, their use in everyday clinical settings is often limited by the complexity and expense of data acquisition. Therefore, it is essential to strike a balance between predictive power and the practicality of obtaining the required variables. Potential strategies could include integrating molecular data with routinely collected clinical data.

Furthermore, future studies assessing the model performance in this field should report AUC or C‐index with 95% CI for accurate assessment. This enables researchers and clinicians to make more confident conclusions about the suitability of a diagnostic test for clinical use [46].

The patient selection criteria should also encompass those who received neoadjuvant systemic therapy rather than those who underwent upfront surgery only, given the rapidly evolving landscape of treatment in early‐stage lung cancer.

Promoting equity in healthcare should also be a key consideration. Developing globally applicable models that reflect differences in available resources and include participation from low‐ and middle‐income countries is vital. Since roughly 60% of lung cancer cases occur in these regions, creating inclusive models that function effectively across diverse healthcare contexts will enhance their worldwide relevance and impact.

In summary, overcoming practical challenges, ensuring global relevance, and maintaining standardized reporting of model performance with 95% CIs will help advance personalized treatment and surveillance approaches for NSCLC.

Conclusion

This systematic review identified six studies that examined risk prediction models for recurrence following curative treatment of early‐stage NSCLC, using molecular‐based variables. Evaluating the real‐time performance of these models in clinical practice would be valuable. To ensure the reliability of future risk prediction models, upcoming research should emphasize standardized methods and consistent reporting of model performance. Additionally, future model development should aim to combine clinicopathological characteristics with molecular predictors, broaden inclusion criteria to patients receiving neoadjuvant systemic therapy, and incorporate real‐time data from electronic health records. By integrating these elements, future models could deliver more accurate and personalized recurrence risk predictions, ultimately improving patient outcomes. Overall, the review highlights a promising field but underscores the need for larger, well‐validated, and transparent studies.
