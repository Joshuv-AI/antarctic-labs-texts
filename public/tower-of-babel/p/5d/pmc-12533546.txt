Biophysically constrained dynamical modelling of the brain using multimodal magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Siddharth Bansal, Bradley S Peterson, Chaitanya Gupte, Siddhant Sawardekar, Maria J Gonzalez Anaya, Maricar Ordonez, Deepa Bhojwani, Jonathan D Santoro, Ravi Bansal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe propose a Biophysically Restrained Analog Integrated Neural Network (BRAINN), an analog electrical network that models the dynamics of brain function. The network interconnects analog electrical circuits that simulate two tightly coupled brain processes: (1) propagation of an action potential, and (2) regional cerebral blood flow in response to the metabolic demands of signal propagation. These two processes are modeled by two branches of an electrical circuit comprising a resistor, a capacitor, and an inductor. We estimated the electrical components from in vivo multimodal MRI together with the biophysical properties of the brain applied to state-space equations, reducing arbitrary parameters such that the dynamic behavior is determined by neuronal integrity. Electrical circuits were interconnected at Brodmann areas to form a network using neural pathways traced with diffusion tensor imaging data. We built BRAINN in Simulink, MATLAB, using longitudinal multimodal MRI data from 20 healthy controls and 19 children with leukemia. BRAINN stimulated by an impulse applied to the lateral temporal region generated sustained activity. Stimulated BRAINN functional connectivity was comparable (within ±1.3 standard deviations) to measured resting-state functional connectivity in 40 of the 55 pairs of brain regions. Control system analyses showed that BRAINN was stable for all participants. BRAINN controllability in patients relative to healthy participants was disrupted prior to treatment but improved during treatment. BRAINN is scalable as more detailed regions and fiber tracts are traced in the MRI data. A scalable BRAINN will facilitate study of brain behavior in health and illness, and help identify targets and design transcranial stimulation for optimally modulating brain activity.Keywords: Anatomical MRI; MR spectroscopy; Perfusion MRI; Diffusion tensor imaging; Control system, Dynamical brain model; System stability; Eigenmodes; System controllability; PBH test; Gramian matrix

Introduction

Complementary information from multimodal MRI of the living brain has advanced our understanding of normative brain development as well as the neural bases in disease conditions. Anatomical MRI provides measures of morphology, regional volumes, and cortical thickness (Peterson et al., 2009); diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) provides measures for white matter microarchitecture and anatomical connectivity (Le Bihan et al., 1986); perfusion MRI measures regional cerebral blood flow (rCBF), a marker for regional metabolic activity (Petcharunpaisan et al., 2010); resting-state fMRI quantifies functional connectivity (Rogers et al., 2007); and MR spectroscopy measures metabolites (Buonocore and Maddock, 2015), including N-Acetyl Aspartate (NAA), a marker for the density of healthy neurons (Arnold et al., 2001); and choline, a marker for membrane turnover (Miller et al., 1996). DTI and fMRI data have been used for developing dynamical models for brain functioning.

Brain simulation models can be characterized as bottom-up or top-down. Neuromorphic computing, a bottom-up approach, is inspired by brain connectomics and simulates in silico the functioning of a single neuron and its synaptic communications (Poon and Zhou, 2011). Top-down approaches, including dynamic causal model (DCM) (Kiebel et al., 2008; David and Friston, 2003), The Virtual Brain (TVB) (Sanz Leon et al., 2013), and Virtual Brain Twins (VBT) (Wang et al., 2024), parcellate the brain into regions and simulate how information is transmitted and processed by anatomically connected brain regions. Anatomical connectivity among regions is determined using DTI data (Gu et al., 2015; Beynel et al., 2020; Stiso et al., 2019), whereas functional connectivity from fMRI, EEG, or MEG data has generated a Bayesian dynamic causal model (DCM) for brain activity on hidden states. Anatomical connectivity-based studies (Gu et al., 2015; Beynel et al., 2020; Stiso et al., 2019) employed a state-space model x˙(t)=A⋅x(t)+B⋅u(t) for dynamic changes in activity x(t) across brain regions in response to control inputs u(t) applied to a set of regions selected by the input matrix B. The state transition matrix A is estimated as the adjacency matrix of brain regions that are interconnected by fiber tracks traced in diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) data. The eigenmodes (i.e., eigenvalues λi and eigenvectors νi) of the matrix A are used to infer system controllability (Beynel et al., 2020) and for designing control input (Stiso et al., 2019) that transitions brain functioning from a current state to a desired state. Such models using anatomical connectivity have high test-retest reliability because neuronal projections are stable across individuals (Gu et al., 2015). The model using anatomical connectivity, (Gu et al., 2015) however, assumed that regional activation instantaneously affects the states of all connected regions and, therefore, cannot model differing lengths of neural pathways or pathological white matter changes that alter the propagation velocity of action potentials. TVB and VBT (Sanz Leon et al., 2013), however, modeled the delays in propagation of brain activity from one region to another region, thereby yielding a more realistic model for brain activity.

There are several limitations of the prior models. First, the generalizability of TVB and VBT is limited by the large number of parameters estimated from training samples, making them susceptible to model degeneracy and overfitting (Wang et al., 2024). Second, neuromorphic computing is trained for a specific task; hence, it cannot study how brain functioning is disrupted in illness. Third, extant models cannot account for variations in propagation speed of the action potential that aging or disease process may produce. Aging and disease processes affect tract properties, such as myelin thickness and neuronal health, which in turn alter the transmission of action potentials and post-synaptic potentials (Hamada et al., 2017; Petkoski et al., 2023). Disruptions in the transmission of action potentials due to dys- and de-myelination have been implicated in several illnesses (Haroutunian et al., 2014), including multiple sclerosis, autism spectrum disorder, schizophrenia, and depression. VBT (Petkoski et al., 2023) models a linear decline in propagation velocity with normative aging that is assumed to be age-related changes in myelination (Chapman and Hill, 2020). However, it assumes equal propagation velocity for all neural pathways, declining with age equally across all neural pathways. Finally, current models are computationally intensive (Sanz Leon et al., 2013; Triebkorn et al., 2024), especially when simulating the brain at an increasingly detailed region parcellation and detailed tracking of neural pathways.

We propose herein a new analog electrical network, BRAINN, which simulates brain function by integrating electrical circuit models of neural pathways. An electrical circuit models two tightly coupled brain processes using resistors, capacitors, inductors, and metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistors (MOSFETs) (Bhol et al., 2022). The electrical components are estimated as nonlinear functions of in vivo MRI-derived brain measures, and the arbitrary constants in those relations are restrained by brain biophysics. The proposed BRAINN model consequently has only one arbitrary constant and adaptively models the propagation speed of action potentials using MRI-derived brain measures, thereby yielding a realistic electrical network model that is unique for the function of the brain in each individual. The proposed BRAINN is scalable and can simulate brain activity in real-time. Individual-specific BRAINN can help understand brain dynamics in vivo, how disease conditions disrupt brain functioning, and design control inputs for optimally steering brain processes.

Methods

An electrical circuit model for a neural pathway comprises two parallel branches of resistors, capacitors, and inductors, and simulates two tightly coupled dynamical processes: (a) propagation of action potential and (b) cerebral blood flow to support the metabolic demands. The electrical components are nonlinear functions of the MRI-derived brain measures. We developed coupled, nonhomogeneous second-order differential equations that describe the state-space dynamics of the electrical circuit, which are constrained by the biophysics of brain dynamics, and yield simplified nonlinear relations by eliminating arbitrary constants. Electrical circuits are interconnected at Brodmann areas (Jacobs, 2011) using a MOSFET (Bhol et al., 2022) to form an analog electrical network or the BRAINN. We derived analytical functions that compute individual- and tract-specific values for the resistor, propagation velocity, and pulsation frequency, yielding unique BRAINN for each participant in our real-world, longitudinal multimodal MRI dataset (Fig. 1). We built BRAINN in Simulink, MATLAB (The MathWorks Inc.., 2022), using healthy values from the literature for propagation velocities of action potential and pulsation frequencies and studied its response when stimulated by an impulse applied to the lateral temporal region of the brain. We expected a BRAINN built using healthy values would generate activity that would not differ from brain activity in healthy individuals. We therefore statistically compared functional brain connectivity in the stimulated BRAINN with real-world, resting--state functional connectivity using resting-state fMRI data from 20 healthy participants (age 12.9 ± 4 years, 18 males). We studied the stability of the BRAINNs and tested whether controllability in patients differed from that in healthy participants over the course of chemotherapy. Finally, using real-world data, we recommend a resistor value that maximizes variance, thereby eliminating all unknowns and designing a BRAINN that is restrained only by brain biophysics and component values determined by in vivo multimodal brain MRIs.

Electrical circuit model for a neural pathway

The electrical circuit model assumes that the propagation speed of the action potential is proportional to neuronal health and myelination (Hamada et al., 2017; Hsu et al., 1964), and that rCBF responds to both basal and transient changes in metabolic demands (sFig.1). We assumed that the electrical components are nonlinear functions of MRI-derived brain measures (detailed derivations in Supplementary-Methods 1): (a) the resistor R1=f1(NAA) is an inverse function of NAA, as higher NAA levels are associated with higher conduction velocities (Hall, 2010; Mascalchi et al., 1998) or lower resistance. We consequently modeled resistance R1, an inverse of conduction, to relate inversely to NAA. (b) The capacitor C1=f2(FA) is an inverse function of fractional anisotropy (FA), as the capacitance decreases with increasing myelination. The diffusion of water molecules perpendicular to the long axis of fiber bundles is constrained by neuronal membranes, especially by the myelin sheath. More cell membranes and myelin-sheathing axons impose greater constraints on the direction of diffusion, thereby increasing FA values. More myelination also increases the propagation velocity of action potentials because it decreases membrane capacitance; therefore, ions diffuse faster along the lengths of the axons. That is, thicker myelination increases transmembrane impedance and decreases membrane capacitance, thereby permitting faster saltatory conduction by enabling faster diffusion of ions (Sumner, 2014). Capacitor C1 charges with a time constant R1C1, which model the conduction of an action potential along neural pathway. (c) The capacitor C2=f3(CH) as a function of choline concentration because membrane turnover is a large contributing factor for basal metabolic needs (Warren and Glick, 1968). A larger capacitor C2 permits greater current flow in the RLC branch, representing greater blood flow required for housekeeping functions in tissues with greater cellular density. (d) The inductor L1=f4(rCBF) presenting impedance to regional blood flow. Vessels with greater diameters present smaller impedance and have higher blood flow (Sutera and Skalak, 1993). The inductor L1 models impedance to changes in blood flow with increased pulsation for increased blood flow. Pulsating impedance at the resonance frequency ω0=1L1C2 models pulsating of cerebral blood flow (sFig.1&2). The phase velocity v of the blood flow (Spahr et al., 2015; Laloy-Borgna et al., 2023) in an artery of diameter d pulsating at frequency f was estimated to be v=0.1*(f*d)0.8. The diameter d changes proportionally with the basal metabolic Mb needs of the tissue; (Reglin et al., 2017) or, v=0.1*f*β*Mb0.8..rCBF is phase velocity v multiplied by the total cross-sectional area A of capillary bed in a specified volume of tissue: rCBF=β′*v*A=β′*0.1*f*β*Mb0.8*A; or, f*β*Mb=10β′*A1.25*rCBF1.25. Hence, the pulsation frequency f=δ*(rCBF)1.25Mb. Because membrane turnover is a large contributing factor for basal metabolic needs, we used choline as a marker for basal membrane turnover, Mb=γ*CH. Consequently, the pulsation frequency f=δγ*CH*1rCBF1.25=β′′CH*1rCBF1.25. We therefore modeled pulsation frequency as directly proportional to rCBF, but inversely proportional to choline levels (detailed derivations in Supplementary-Methods 1).

The resistor R1 modulates the charging of the capacitor C1 with a time constant R1C1; that is, action potential propagates slower in neuronal pathways with greater neuronal impairments (higher R1) and higher capacitance due to leaky membrane. The R1,C2, and L1 branch models regional blood flow at the resonance pulsation frequency ω0=1L1C2, which increases to support greater blood perfusion. The capacitor C1 also modulates the current flow into the RLC branch; larger C1, or less myelination, slows the propagation of the action potential, thereby reducing the rate of increase, but not the amount of blood flow required to support metabolic processes.

State-space equations

The dynamic response of the electrical circuit is defined by its three state-space variables: (1) the voltage v1 across the capacitor C1, (2) the voltage v2 across the capacitor C2, and (3) the current i2 through the inductor L1 (Fig. 2). We derive nonhomogeneous state-space equations and then employ the biophysical properties of the brain to relate arbitrary constants α1,α2,α3, and α4, leaving only one arbitrary constant α1. Equivalently, the electrical components R1,C1,C2, and L1 are derived as a function of the resistor R1; consequently, density of healthy neurons determines the constrained dynamical behavior of the electrical circuit. We finally show that the eigenmodes, but not the Gramian energy, are invariant to R1.

Building a network of electrical circuits

We integrated electrical circuits at Brodmann areas to form BRAINN. The voltage V1(t)=0.63V0 across the capacitor C1 represents the action potential has propagated along the entire neural pathway, which can trigger the transmission of downstream action potential. The increase in the voltage from 0.63V0 to 0.975 V0 represents the action potential propagating down the terminals (Section 2.3.1). To ensure that the downstream electrical circuit(s) do not present an electrical load that causes the voltage to fall below 0.63 V0 and disrupt transmission, we employed a MOSFET (Sedra et al., 2020) device at the input of electrical circuits. MOSFET is a voltage-gated device with three terminals: gate (G), source (S), and drain (D) (sFig. 3). The voltage across the capacitor C1 is the gate voltage VGS of the downstream fiber tract(s). As the capacitor C1 charges, VGS increases, changing the drain current iD. To ensure that iD changes do not affect the dynamic response, we connected a drain resistor RD≪R1≪R, thereby providing a constant voltage for the downstream electrical circuit (detailed derivation in Supplementary-Methods 1.3).

The source resistor R≫R1 ensured that the capacitor C1 had a constant charging voltage to model the propagation of the axon potential. Relating the arbitrary constants

If these parameters are not eliminated, then the proposed network cannot realistically model brain function. We eliminated arbitrary constants using state space equations for the homogeneous and nonhomogeneous system response and biophysical properties to yield BRAINN that is a realistic model for the brain functioning, leaving only R1 as an unknown variable for each neural pathway.

Individual- and tract-specific electrical components

We estimated individual- and tract-specific proportionality constants for resistance, propagation velocity, and pulsation frequency using data for fibers through the body of the CC in healthy participants. The proportionality constants for propagation velocity and pulsation frequency were estimated from values for healthy individuals reported in the literature. We reduced the four unknown parameters α1,α2,α3, and α4 in the electrical circuit by employing biophysics for the transmission of action potential (Section 2.1), thereby leaving only one unknown parameter that determines the resistor R1 in the electrical circuit (Section 2.5). We used fibers through the corpus callosum(CC) as the reference neural pathway for computing tract- and individual-specific values, leaving only the value of one resistor R1 as an unknown parameter for all participants in our real-world cohort. The estimated proportionality constants were then used for all participants in our real-world data. We chose CC as the reference because it comprises a large number of fibers. Averaging across a large number of fiber tracts decreases the variance in the estimated values. Furthermore, the CC is located in the middle of the brain, and the fiber tracts through the CC body are located away from the scalp. Consequently, the MRS signal and hence the NAA levels in the CC fibers are not contaminated by the MRS signal from macromolecules in the scalp.

Neural pathways connecting Brodmann areas

We affine transformed (Viola and Wells, 1995) the Brodmann atlas (MRIcron, RRID:SCR_002403) (Rorden and Brett, 2000) for defining Brodmann areas in participant anatomical MRI and rs-fMRI MRI. We then used the detailed in vivo neural pathways traced in DTI data for 1065 healthy participants (Yeh, 2022) from the Human Connectome Project database (Glasser et al., 2016). Fiber tracts between the Brodmann areas were traced by applying the deterministic fiber-tracking algorithm (Yeh, 2022; Yeh et al., 2013) with uniformly distributed seeds across the entire brain and grouped into 24 major pathways (Fig. 3). We considered a neural pathway projected to or received projections from a Brodmann area if that area was connected to the neural pathway in > 80 % of the 1065 participant brains (Yeh, 2022).

Combined Brodmann areas

Neural pathway received fibers or terminated into contiguously neighboring Brodmann areas, which we combined into a single area (Fig. 3). For example, Brodmann areas BA1/3 and BA2 were combined as a somatosensory area whereas BA44 and BA45 were combined as Broca’s area (sTable 1) (Schmahmann and Pandya, 2006). Neural pathways in BRAINN interconnected combined Brodmann areas. For example, the frontal aslant tract (FAT) interconnected the somatosensory cortex, premotor/motor cortex, lateral superior frontal gyrus, and Broca’s area (Supplementary-Methods 1.5).

BRAINN stability and controllability

A dynamical system with a system matrix A=TDT-1 is stable, that is converges to a finite value, if the real part of all eigenvalues is negative (Supplementary-Methods 1.7). A system is “controllable” for the input U(t) if it can steer the system from an initial state X0 to any desired state X1. Mathematically, a system is controllable if the controllability matrix (DiStefano et al., 1995; Kirk, 2004) CAB=BABA2B⋯An-1B is full-rank. The Popov-Belevitch-Hautus (PBH) test for controllability shows that the maximum rank deficiency of the matrix A-λiI is the minimum number of control inputs required for the system to be controllable. to assess the controllability of a state X(t). The controllability of the state X(t) is defined as the Gramian energy X′W-1X, with larger values indicating a larger control energy required for steering the system to that state (Pasqualetti et al., 2014). If state X is an eigenvector of the matrix W-1, then X′W-1X is its eigenvalue, and therefore, if eigenvalues are large then the system cannot be easily steered to certain states (Pasqualetti et al., 2014). If certain states are more desired to be reachable, then the Gramian matrix can provide insights into the control input configuration B that makes the state reachable.

Control inputs configurations

System controllability is computed for a specific configuration of the control inputs. We studied BRAINN controllability for increasing complexity of control inputs.

(1) B=vec1=[100100…100]′; that is, one input controls the voltage V1 by the same amount in all neural pathways. (2) B=vec2=[111111…111]′; that is, one input controls all state-space variables by the same amount in all neural pathways. (3) An 81 × 3 control matrix Mat3=[100;010;001;…;100;010;001] with three different inputs, each controlling the same state-space variable in all neural pathways. (4) An 81 × 81 identity matrix MatIdent =[100…0;010…0;…;000…1] with 81 different inputs, each independently controlling one state-space variable. (5) An 81 × 81 matrix of random 0 or 1 (MatRand81), denoting 81 different inputs, such that a linear sum of randomly selected inputs controls a state-space variable.

Real-world dataset

We collected T1-weighted anatomical MRI, multi-shell DTI, perfusion MRI, MR Spectroscopy, and resting-state fMRI data on a 3.0 T Philips Achieva MRI scanner equipped with a 32-channel phased array head coil. Multimodal MRI data were collected in 19 patients (age 13.3 ± 6.3 years, 16 males) with newly diagnosed acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) and 20 age- and sex-matched healthy participants (age 12.9 ± 4 years, 18 males). Data were collected longitudinally at four time points: (1) baseline before chemotherapy and then during chemotherapy at (2) week 9, (3) week 22, and (4) year 1. All participants were of ages 5–20 years. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of CHLA, and written informed consent/assent was obtained from all participants. MR pulse sequences and data processing are detailed in Supplementary-Methods 1.8.

Multimodal tractometry data

We traced fibers in each participant DTI data by applying the automatic fiber tracking (AutoTrack) algorithm within DSI Studio, which grouped fibers into neural pathways (Supplementary-Methods 1.8) (Yeh, 2020). Neural pathways are lists of 3D coordinates in the DTI space, which we normalized into the participant MR spectroscopy and perfusion MR data to compute average values for fiber pathway length, fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity, axial diffusivity, radial diffusivity, NAA, CH, and rCBF. The multimodal tractometry data were used to build a unique BRAINN for each participant.

Building and simulating BRAINN in MATLAB

We built BRAINN from MRI data as follows. First, we computed MRI-derived data for neural pathways interconnecting Brodmann areas (Section 2.11). Second, we used MRI data for computing resistance R1,j,F, pulsation frequency fj,F, and propagation velocity θj,F for neural pathway F in participant j (Section 2.6). Third, these values subsequently are used for computing capacitor C1,j,F, capacitor C2,j,F, and inductor L1,j,F for simulating neural pathway F in participant j (Section 2.5). Finally, these neural pathways interconnected combined Brodmann areas (Section 2.7) as reported previously using DTI data for 1065 participants (Section 2.7) (Yeh, 2022).

We simulated BRAINN using Simulink, MATLAB, (sFig.6) (The MathWorks Inc, 2022) and visualized its dynamic response by applying a pulse input to the “lateral temporal area”. We elected to apply the stimulus to the lateral temporal area because it subserves semantic cognition network of the brain. Consequently, extracranial repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation (rTMS) has been applied to the lateral temporal lobe to study how stimulation alters functioning of the semantic cognition network (Binney and Ralph, 2015), processing of semantic content in pictures (Woollams et al., 2017), encoding and retrieval of memory (Boggio et al., 2009), and processing of feature discrimination (Zhou et al., 2021). The activation and deactivation time-courses for each area yielded a stimulated connectivity matrix. We also computed resting-state functional connectivity matrices for healthy participants in our real-world data and tested whether the stimulated connectivity differed from resting-state functional connectivity. We simulated only one hemisphere of the brain by applying a four-second pulse with 10–14 s delays because of the computational demands for solving large numbers of differential equations, leading to convergence difficulties in Simulink, MATLAB. Our real-world data showed that the functional activity of the homologous regions in the two hemispheres was significantly correlated. Therefore, simulating activity in one hemisphere alone demonstrates the feasibility of modeling brain function using BRAINN.

Results

We first evaluated whether anatomical connectivity is consistent with the resting-state functional connectivity between brain regions in our real-world data (Section 3.1). Second, we assessed whether a BRAINN stimulated by a pulse applied to the lateral temporal region generates sustained activity across brain regions, and whether simulated functional connectivity is comparable with resting-state functional connectivity in our real-world rs-fMRI data (Section 3.2). Third, we charted how MRI-derived brain and BRAINN properties changed longitudinally from baseline to year 1 in our real-world cohort of patients and healthy controls (Section 3.3). Fourth, we confirmed longitudinal stability of BRAINNs for all participants (Section 3.4). Fifth, we studied how BRAINN stability, rank deficiency, and Gramian control energies varied for varying values of R1, which permitted us to select an optimal value of R1 in our cohort of participants (Section 3.6). Finally, we selected as a reference the BRAINN for one healthy participant and computed its eigenvalues and eigenvectors for calculating Gramian energies for all participants. We compared stability and statistically tested whether controllability in patients differed from that in healthy participants at baseline and how controllability changed during treatment in patients (Section 3.7).

Generalizability of anatomical connectivity

If previously traced neural pathways were generalizable to participants in our cohort, then we expected high resting-state functional connectivity among regions connected by those pathways. We therefore computed functional connectivity matrices among the Brodmann areas controlled for age effects using our real-world rs-fMRI data. The averaged resting-state connectivity matrix was threshed at 0.7 or 0.65: Brodmann regions were functionally connected (p < 10−18) if their correlation coefficient was greater than 0.7 (or 0.65). Other thresholds were not informative as for thresholds > 0.7 very few Brodmann areas were functionally connected, whereas for threshold < 0.6 each area was connected to all other areas.

The resting-state functional connectivity matrix threshed at 0.7 or 0.65 did reveal anatomical connection between the Brodmann areas (Supplementary-Results 2.1). In particular, the following Brodmann areas had functional connectivity greater than 0.7. (1) Superior frontal cortex (BA8, BA9, BA10, BA47) was functionally connected with Wernicke’s region (BA40, BA39), Broca’s area (BA44, BA43), the lateral temporal cortex (BA20, BA21, BA22), and the temporal pole (BA38). These functionally connected regions are anatomically connected by AF, UF, and SLF-II pathways. (2) Superior parietal cortex (BA5) was functionally connected with occipital cortex (BA17, BA18, BA19) and fusiform cortex (BA37, BA20), which are anatomically connected by the VOF pathway. (3) Superior parietal cortex (BA7) was functionally connected with the occipital cortex (BA19) and lateral temporal cortex (BA20, BA37), which are also connected by the VOF pathway. (4) The temporal pole (BA38) was functionally connected with the parahippocampal (BA48) and the lateral temporal cortex (BA21, BA22), which are anatomically interconnected by the ILF pathway. And (5) Superior frontal cortex (BA8, BA9) was functionally connected with Broca’s region (BA44, BA45, BA47), which are anatomically connected by the FAT pathway.

However, resting-state functional connectivity matrices did not reveal all anatomical neural pathways, perhaps because their functional connectivity was below the selected thresholds, or because multiple anatomically connected regions alter the function of a single brain region, or both. Nevertheless, the consistency between functionally and anatomically connected areas provides strong support for the generalizability of the previously detailed anatomical connectivity to our real-world data.

BRAINN response to pulse stimulation

We tested whether BRAINN stimulated by an impulse generates activity that is comparable to the resting-state fMRI activity in healthy participants. BRAINN interconnected regions that were interconnected in DTI data from 1065 healthy individuals (Yeh, 2022). If BRAINN generated from the prior DTI data is generalizable, then we expected that stimulating a single region would generate sustained activity across brain regions and that the stimulated activity would be comparable to the brain activity in our real-world rs-fMRI data.

We stimulated BRAINN by applying a four-second pulse to the lateral temporal area, which propagated the action potential to downstream neural pathways, leading to sustained activation and deactivation of BRAINN areas. We used these time courses to compute the stimulated functional connectivity matrix (sTable 2) and compared it with the resting-state functional connectivity for 20 healthy participants (sTable 3). We considered simulated functional connectivity within ±1.3 standard deviations to be comparable with the resting-state functional connectivity. More than 20 % healthy controls would have functional connectivity outside a ± 1.3 standard deviation equivalence bounds. That is, stimulated functional connectivity outside ±1.3 standard deviations has a 20 % probability that it in fact is comparable with resting-state functional connectivity.

Using the ±1.3 standard deviation bound, simulated functional connectivity was comparable to resting-state functional connectivity between 40 of the 55 pairs of brain regions. When the BRAINN was stimulated by applying a four-second pulse to the superior frontal region, simulated functional connectivity was comparable (within ±1.3 standard deviation) to the resting-state functional connectivity between 38 of the 55 pairs of brain regions for healthy participants (sTable 16). Simulated connectivity differed from resting-state functional connectivity due to (a) noisy real-world fMRI data, (b) BRAINN models a brain hemisphere partitioned among 14 regions, and (c) limitations of simulating BRAINN using software Simulink, MATLAB, which necessitates solving thousands of differential equations for signal propagation, a four-second stimulation pulse, and ten to fourteen second delay post activation of a brain region. Nevertheless, sustained, simulated functional connectivity was comparable (within ±1.3 standard deviations) to the resting-state functional connectivity across 73 % of brain regions.

Within- and between-participant analyses showed that simulated functional connectivity was positively associated with resting-state functional connectivity, but correlations were not statistically significant for all participants (sFig. 9 &10). The trend-level positive correlations are likely because simulated functional connectivity is not affected by the variability in fMRI data due to noise and diurnal physiological changes in brain functioning. Consequently, although simulated connectivity varied across individuals, simulated connectivity was positively correlated with resting-state functional connectivity across participants (sFig.11).

MRI-derived and BRAINN properties

For each neural pathway fiber length, we computed axial diffusivity (AD), radial diffusivity (RD), fractional anisotropy (FA), perfusion, choline, and NAA. MRI-derived brain measures were used to calculate tract- and individual-specific values for propagation velocity, pulsation frequency, and resistor R1, for longitudinally collected data. We applied multiple linear regression with age as a covariate to test whether MRI-derived and electrical properties in patients differed from those of healthy participants. These analyses showed that the length of the neural pathways in patients did not differ from those in healthy participants, except for the corticospinal tract and posterior thalamic radiations (sTable 4). Differing tract lengths possibly could be due chance or from disease process affecting white matter and, consequently, brain morphology.

MRI-derived brain measures (sTable 5 & 17) were significantly correlated with network electrical properties (sTable 6). At baseline, patients differed significantly (p < 0.05) from healthy participants on RD, rCBF, choline, and NAA for a few neural pathways (sTable 7). Consequently, the propagation velocity, resistance, and pulsation frequency in patients differed from those in healthy participants (sTable 7). By week 9, perfusion and choline were abnormal in fewer than two neural pathways; however, patients had AD and RD abnormalities and impaired neuronal health across a large number of pathways, which caused significant differences in resistance and pulsation frequency (sTable 8). By week 22, perfusion and choline levels in patients did not differ from those in the healthy participants. However, AD, RD, and NAA were abnormal across almost all neural pathways (sTable 9). These abnormalities in AD, RD, and NAA persisted into Year 1 (sTable 10). Despite abnormalities in MRI-derived brain measures, patients did not differ from healthy participants on estimated propagation velocities at week 9 and week 22. By Year 1, however, patients had significantly slower propagation velocities due to disruption and loss of myelination and neuronal health. Thus, MRI-derived brain measures and propagation velocities became increasing abnormal in patients relative to healthy controls.

BRAINN stability

BRAINN was stable in all participants as the real part of eigenvalues was always negative. Volcano plots visualized how patients differed from healthy participants on eigenvalues (sFig.7). At baseline, eigenvalues in patients compared with healthy participants were significantly more negative, suggesting that patients had faster propagation of action potentials and greater damping of rCBF in posterior thalamic radiations (sTable 11). However, eigenvalues for Cingulum-Fronto Parahippocampal (C_FPH) at Year 1 were significantly more negative in patients, suggesting their faster propagation of action potentials (sTable 14). However, during chemotherapy, eigenvalues for patients relative to healthy participants were significantly less negative in Arcuate Fasciculus (AF), Cortico-Spinal Tract (CST), Thalamic Radiation Posterior (CTh_P), Middle Longitudinal Tract (MdLF), and Superior Longitudinal Fasciculus III (SLF-III) at week 9 (sTable 12), mdLF at week 22 (sTable 13), and C_FPH, CST, Cortico-Striatal Tract Superior (CStr_S), Frontal Aslant Tract (FAT), mdLF at Year 1 (sTable 14), suggesting a disruption in the propagation of action potential and rCBF. Thus, despite progressive abnormalities in patients over the follow up period, BRAINNs were stable for all participants in our real-world cohort.

Rank deficiency in PBH test and controllability matrix

The maximum rank deficiency of BRAINN was four, which did not differ between patients and healthy participants, suggesting that four or more inputs are required to steer BRAINN to a desired state. The rank deficiency was invariant to the resistive element R1, that is, the configuration of the control inputs required for steering BRAINN was independent of the one arbitrary constant in the network.

Sensitivity of stability and controllability to R1

The eigenvalues did not change as R1 was varied from 0.5 Ω to 5 Ω, suggesting that BRAINN stability was invariant to R1. The standard deviation in the average Gramian energy needed for steering BRAINN was the maximum for R1=1.5Ω (sTable 15). Maximizing variance reduces the statistical power to test whether controllability differed in patients from that in healthy participants. We therefore used R1=1.5Ω to test whether BRAINN controllability in patients differed from that of healthy participants.

BRAINN controllability

We computed the Gramian energy X′W-1X required for steering BRAINN to the eigenstates Xi(t),i=1,…,N of the Gramian matrix W for a healthy participant. Because any state is a unique sum of eigenstates, a comparison of Gramian energies for one set of eigenstates informs the controllability of BRAINN to any desired state. The control energies required for the patients did not differ from those of the healthy participants with input configurations Vec1, Vec2, and Mat3. The control energies (~ 1010a.u.) and their variances were large, which generated large between-group fold change (Fig. 4). For the input configurations MatIdent and MatRand81, the Gramiam energy was small (~ 50a.u.), and consequently, the between-group fold change was small (Fig. 4). Volcano plots showed that the number of states with different control energy increased during chemotherapy (Fig. 4), suggesting that chemotherapy disrupted BRAINN controllability. Volcano plots also showed that the between-group fold change was negative, suggesting that patients relative to controls required less control energy, or equivalently, patient BRAINNs were easier to control than healthy BRAINNs (sTables 12–14). Patient BRAINNs were more controllable likely because propagation speeds were slower in patients relative to healthy controls (Supplemental Section 2.4).

However, the control energy required in patients differed from that of healthy participants for steering to a greater number of states at Week 9, Week 22, and Year 1, thereby suggesting a disruption in the dynamic control of BRAINN in patients during chemotherapy.

Discussion

We proposed BRAINN, a biophysics-constrained analog electrical network, to simulate the dynamic behavior of a living brain (Chen, 1999a). We built a unique BRAINN for each individual from their in vivo multimodal MRI collected longitudinally, permitting us to study how brain functioning in patients differs from healthy participants during chemotherapy. By constraining BRAINN using the biophysics of action potential propagation and arterial pulsation, we eliminated arbitrary constants that related electrical elements to MRI-derived brain measures. The BRAINN response depends on one resistor R1 estimated from NAA; therefore, its dynamic response is determined by the density of healthy neurons. Individual- and tract-specific values for R1 were estimated using fibers through the body of the corpus callosum as the reference, which yielded individual BRAINN dependent upon one arbitrarily specified value across all 39 participants. We subsequently suggested R1 that maximized the variance for Gramian energy in our real-world data. BRAINN, built using MRI-derived brain measures, was stable at all time points, generated sustained functional activity when stimulated, and its stimulated functional connectivity did not differ from resting-state functional connectivity in our real-world data. The biophysics-constrained BRAINN is largely independent of arbitrary constants and can be built using analog electrical elements. Consequently, BRAINN is scalable and does not incur any additional computational costs as more detailed brain regions and neural pathways are traced. BRAINN therefore can be used for studying the dynamic functioning of a living brain in both healthy and disease states. BRAINN can determine the strength of control inputs and their configuration required for steering the brain to a desired state, such as in transcranial magnetic stimulation procedures applied for treating neuropsychiatric and neurological disorders (Ozturk and Venugopal, 2022).

Our real-world data showed that NAA was inversely correlated with perfusion along the superior longitudinal fasciculus, suggesting blood perfusion and metabolism for maintaining the energy state of axons (sTable 6) (Peterson et al., 2019). Blood perfusion, however, was positively correlated with choline, a marker for membrane turnover, suggesting greater metabolic support is needed to support greater tissue turnover. Creatine, although positively correlated with NAA, was not significantly correlated with R1 because R1=α1NAA is a nonlinear function of NAA. Furthermore, the length of neural pathways in patients did not differ from those in healthy controls (sTable 4). Nevertheless, MRI-derived brain measures became increasingly abnormal during chemotherapy, which persisted one year into treatment. Propagation velocity, however, did not differ until week 22, and then it slowed significantly in patients relative to healthy controls due to declining neuronal health. Slower propagation speed was represented by a smaller magnitude of eigenvalues for the state-transition matrix, which in turn lowered the Gramian energy required to control patient BRAINNs compared to the energy required to control healthy BRAINNs. This finding suggested that patient BRAINNs were more controllable than healthy BRAINNs. Nevertheless, even though patient BRAINNs require less control energy, the number of control inputs required to steer the BRAINN to a desired state for patients did not differ from those required for healthy controls. Furthermore, the BRAINNs were stable at all times for all participants in our real-world cohort. Slower propagation velocities, greater damping of rCBF, and increased BRAINN controllability suggest that patients relative to healthy controls can be steered to a desired state with lower intensity external stimulation, for example through repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation.

On the other hand, greater controllability in the presence of greater brain abnormalities also suggests that interfering inputs, such as noise, may have a greater influence on brain functioning. Alternatively, a brain with fewer abnormalities and low controllability would be less affected, and would perform equally well, in the presence of greater amounts of noise and interfering inputs.

Our proposed BRAINN using analog components provides an opportunity for physically building a brain in printed circuit board, rather than simulating a living brain in silico. We demonstrated that BRAINN when stimulated generates sustained brain activity and that simulated functional connectivity was positively correlated and comparable (within ±1.3 standard deviations) with resting-state functional connectivity for 40 of the 55 pairs of brain regions. Simulated connectivity differed from resting-state functional connectivity due to limitations of in silico simulation using software Simulink, MATLAB. Simulating the brain in silico is also computationally expensive. For example, TVB required 66 min of compute time when simulating 1 min of brain activity at a time resolution of 0.01 ms across 64 brain nodes (Sanz Leon et al., 2013). The computational cost was higher for simulating a network with more than 512 nodes (Sanz Leon et al., 2013). Although the compute time would decrease as faster computers with more memory become available, a recent simulation required 35 min for simulating brain activity in 64 nodes (Triebkorn et al., 2024). Similarly, simulating 5 min of BRAINN activity using Simulink, MATLAB, took about 15 min of compute time. BRAINN built using physical components is scalable as more detailed regions and tracts are traced. Furthermore, analog electrical circuits function at gigahertz speeds, and therefore a physical BRAINN presents an opportunity to simulate the brain in real time.
