Treatment With Ashwagandha‐Derived Exosome‐Like Nanovesicles Up‐Regulates VEGF‐A Production and Promotes Human Hair Growth Ex Vivo
Authors: Alizée Le Riche, Janina Nienhaus, Marta Silva e Sousa, Hanieh Erdmann, Ilaria Piccini, Luisa Z. Collins, Richard Barfoot, Pedro L. Oseliero‐Filho, Deepikka Senthilkumar, Meiyappan Lakshmanan, Marta Bertolini, Janin Edelkamp, Giorgio Dell‘Acqua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman‐derived exosomes show promise for hair loss treatment but may pose safety concerns, including inflammatory responses and pathogen transmission. Here, we investigated whether plant‐derived exosomes, whose isolation can be standardized, and which show lower compositional complexity and no immunogenicity, represent a valid alternative. We isolated and characterized exosome‐like nanovesicles from the Ashwagandha plant (Ash‐ELNs), which is traditionally used to relieve stress, and pre‐clinically explored their hair growth‐promoting potential. Multi‐omic approaches demonstrated low variations in the content of seven different Ash‐ELN preparations, and cellular vesicle uptake was confirmed in human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs). In a scratch assay of human dermal papilla fibroblasts (HDPCs), Ash‐ELNs significantly increased the production/secretion of the hair growth promoters VEGF‐A, PLGF1, FGF2 and LIF. Application of Ash‐ELNs to healthy human microdissected hair follicles significantly prolonged anagen and increased hair shaft production, while hair matrix keratinocyte proliferation and ‐apoptosis remained unaltered. Ash‐ELNs significantly upregulated VEGF‐A protein expression in the bulb mesenchyme, corroborating our in vitro results. Finally, Ash‐ELNs promoted tubulogenesis in an HDF–human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) assay, suggesting VEGF‐A‐induced angiogenesis as a possible mechanism underlying the anagen promoting effect of Ash‐ELN. Thus, Ash‐ELN treatment offers a novel adjuvant strategy or non‐drug agent to reduce hair shedding.

Background

The interest in plant‐derived cosmetic adjuvants and natural non‐drug agents to improve hair growth and prevent/treat hair loss is continually increasing [1]. Extracellular vesicles, including exosomes and exosome‐like nanovesicles (ELNs), carry biologically active molecules used for intercellular communication [2, 3]. Exosomes have great potential as nano‐carrier systems [4, 5, 6], and various pre‐clinical and clinical studies demonstrated a beneficial effect of human‐derived or engineered exosomes on hair growth promotion [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. While human‐derived exosomes have several drawbacks [6, 13], plant‐derived exosomes show potential as biologically active substances in cosmeceuticals, nutraceuticals or drug agents, also for human therapeutic applications [6, 14, 15]. Yet, to our knowledge, only two studies assessed the effect of plant‐derived exosomes on human hair follicle (HF) function in vitro [16], and ex vivo [17], with differing outcomes.

We assessed ELNs derived from the adaptogenic Ashwagandha plant, Withania somnifera , used in traditional medicine for over 5000 years [18]. Nowadays, supplementation with Ashwagandha is popular as an anti‐stress remedy [19, 20, 21] and believed to enhance overall health and longevity [20]. Stress mediators can induce hair loss in mice in vivo [22, 23, 24] and human HFs ex vivo [25, 26, 27], therefore, it has been suggested that Ashwagandha might also mitigate (stress‐induced) hair loss in humans [28]. Supporting this, application of Ashwagandha extract increased β‐catenin and telomerase reverse transcriptase expression in human dermal papilla (DP) cells, indicating anagen‐prolonging and proliferative effects, respectively [29]. Additionally, a randomized double‐blind, placebo‐controlled study demonstrated that topical application of an Ashwagandha root extract serum to adults with mild to moderate hair loss significantly reduced hair shedding and improved hair density, growth, and thickness [30]. Also, Ashwagandha, when applied in combination with other ingredients in a nutraceutical supplement, was effective in reducing hair loss in women with self‐perceived hair thinning [31].

This initial evidence highlights Ashwagandha's promise to combat hair loss. However, the specific effects of ELNs derived from Ashwagandha seeds (Ash‐ELNs) on HF function and hair growth have not yet been investigated.

Questions Addressed

We aimed to (1) isolate and characterize Ash‐ELNs prior to their experimental use, and (2) pre‐clinically explore their potential to promote hair growth in healthy human HFs ex vivo.

Experimental Design

Detailed M&M in Supplement.

ELN Extraction

Ash‐ELNs were isolated from Ashwagandha seeds at Zen Bio (Durham, NC, USA), Figure 1a.

Multiomics Analyses

Proteomic Analysis

After proteolysis the samples were quantified (OD280) and capillary HPLC was performed to separate the samples. Afterwards, the samples were analysed by mass spectrometry with a Q Exactive HF‐X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, USA).

Transcriptomic Analysis

RNA was extracted using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Germany). RNA integrity was assessed via Qsep100 detection (Biooptic Inc., Taiwan). RNAseq analysis was performed with the PE150 sequencing protocol, using fastqc to evaluate the quality value of the sequencing library.

Lipidomic Analysis

The samples were separated by the Nexera LC‐30A ultra‐high performance liquid chromatography system and analysed by mass spectrometry using a Q Exactive series mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA). Electrospray ionization positive and negative ion modes were used for detection.

Metabolomic Analysis

The samples were separated by Agilent 1290 Infinity LC UHPLC. An AB Triple TOF 6600 mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX) was used to collect the primary and secondary spectra of samples and the positive and negative ion modes of ectrospray ionization were used for detection.

Scratch Assay

Human dermal papilla cells (HDPCs; ScienCell Research Laboratories, CA, USA) were cultured in 12‐well plates for 3 days at 37°C with 5% CO2 in DMEM +1% fetal calf serum medium until confluent. A scratch running the diameter of the well was induced. Cells were treated with vehicle (1× PBS), 1.0 × 108, 1.0 × 109 or 1.0 × 1010 Ash‐ELN vesicles/mL. Secreted growth factor protein expression was quantified by protein immunoassay (MAGPIX, Luminex, USA).

HDF‐HUVEC Co‐Culture

Human dermal fibroblasts (HDF; Zen Bio, NC, USA) were cultured in 96‐well plates for 3 days at 37°C with 5% CO2 in DMEM containing 10% FBS and antibiotics until confluent, after which 2.5 × 104 human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs; Zen Bio) were seeded on top. After 24 h, media were replaced with endothelial growth media plus vehicle (0.5% FBS), Ash‐ELN at 1.0 × 107, 1.0 × 108 or 1.0 × 109 vesicles/mL, or VEGF‐A at 0.1 or 1.0 ng/mL. Seven days later, cells were fixed and stained with CD31‐Alexa Fluor 647 (BD Biosciences, NJ, USA) and imaged with network analysis on an ImageXpress Pico (Molecular Devices, CA, USA).

VEGF‐A ELISA

HDFs were cultured in 12‐well plates for 3 days at 37°C with 5% CO2 with DMEM containing 10% FBS and antibiotics until 70% confluent. Afterwards, cells were serum‐starved for 18 h in standard growth medium with 0.5% FBS and treated for 48 h with 1.0 × 107, 1.0 × 108 or 1.0 × 109 Ash‐ELNs or vehicle (0.5% FBS). After 48 h, VEGF‐A was measured in the conditioned media using a Quantikine ELISA (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN).

Fluorescent Vesicle Uptake in HDFs

Ash‐ELNs were labelled with a fluorescent lipid dye (Vybrant DiD 650 nm, Thermo V22887). Labelled vesicles were enumerated using ZetaView Particle NanoTracking Analysis. HDFs were cultured as described above and the next day, labelled vesicles (0, 1.0 × 106, 1.0 × 107, 1.0 × 108, 1.0 × 109 or 1.0 × 1010 vesicles/mL) were added and incubated at 4°C or 37°C and 5% CO2 for 15 min to allow nuclear dye to accumulate in the cells. The plate was imaged after 0, 16, 24 and 72 h.

Donor Material and Information

Occipital HFs from FUE were obtained from 4 healthy male donors (27–55 y/o) after informed, written patient consent and ethics committee approval (Medical Association of Westfalen‐Lippe and Westfälischen Wilhelms University, no. 2020‐954‐f‐S). Experiments on human tissue were performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki principles.

HF Organ Culture and Treatment

Amputated microdissected human anagen VI scalp HFs were cultured at 37°C with 5% CO2 in Williams Complete Media (WCM) [32, 33, 34, 35]. After 24 h, HFs were treated with 2.0 × 109 Ash‐ELNs/mL or vehicle (0.2% PBS) for 5–8 days. Following culture termination, HFs were embedded in an OCT cryomatrix (Thermo Fisher) and sectioned into 6‐μm sections (Leica Biosystems, Germany).

HF Elongation/Hair Shaft Production

Each HF was measured from the end of the connective tissue sheath (CTS) to the end of the distal outer root sheath (ORS) at Days 1, 3 and 5 ex vivo [32, 33, 36] with a digital light microscope at 50× magnification (VHX900; Keyence Corporation, Japan).

Histochemistry, Immunofluorescence Staining

To visualize melanin, the Masson–Fontana staining was performed [37, 38], and to stain apoptotic and proliferating cells, a Ki‐67/TUNEL double staining was performed [33].

For VEGF‐A, cryosections were incubated overnight at 4°C with rabbit monoclonal anti‐mouse/human VEGFA primary antibody (1:100, ab52917, abcam, UK). The secondary antibody, goat anti‐rabbit Alexa 546 (A11030, Life Technologies, USA), was incubated for 45 min at RT.

Counterstaining with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) (Roche GmbH, Germany) was performed to visualize nuclei.

Quantitative (Immuno‐)histomorphometry

Images were taken using a Keyence fluorescence microscope (BZ9100; Keyence Corporation), maintaining a constant set exposure time throughout imaging for further analysis. Staining immunoreactivity (IR) or the number of positive cells was counted in the corresponding reference areas depicted in the representative images. Analyses were carried out with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Hair Cycle Staging and Scoring

Hair cycle staging and scoring were performed at the end of the culture, using Masson‐Fontana and Ki‐67/TUNEL staining [32, 33, 39].

Statistical Analyses

Data were analysed and visualized with Graph Pad Prism9 (GraphPad Software Inc.). Data were tested for Gaussian distribution using D'Agostino & Pearson normality test followed by an unpaired student's t‐test or Mann–Whitney‐U test, *p < 0.05 were considered significant. Three or more groups were analysed using one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's or Dunnett's post hoc test, #p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Ash‐ELNs were extracted from Ashwagandha seeds through stepwise centrifugation and filtration (Figure 1a). SEM revealed round vesicles with a diameter of around 100 nm (Figure 1b). The diameter was confirmed by dynamic light scattering showing an average hydrodynamic diameter of d̄ number 103 ± 15 nm (Figure 1c; Figure S1a). Small‐angle X‐ray scattering identified vesicles with a bilayer thickness of 38 ± 4 Å, and an inner compartment with different scattering length density than the dispersant, suggesting a distinct material composition (Figure 1d). Ash‐ELN contained 75.61 ± 13.74 ng/mL RNA and 0.89 ± 0.29 μg/mL protein (mean ± SEM, n = 7). Hsp70, Hsp90, and Tet8 protein expression confirmed the plant origin of the vesicles and revealed variable protein content across three preparations (Figure S1b). Notably, human/mammalian anti‐HSP70 antibodies have been employed before for reliable detection of plant‐HSP70, owing to the high sequence conservation of HSP70 proteins across eukaryotes [40, 41, 42].

Understanding the pharmacokinetics, safety, stability, efficacy and content reproducibility of Ash‐ELN is crucial to minimizing adverse effects, maximizing therapeutic efficiency, and clarifying its biological impact [43]. Thus, we applied multi‐omic analyses and found 62%, 39%, 71% and 60% of variance in the first two principal components (PCs) of the miRNA, proteomic, metabolomic and lipidomic data, respectively, of seven analysed Ash‐ELN samples (Figure 1e), indicating a relatively consistent molecular profile across samples, particularly in miRNA, metabolomics, and lipidomics samples, whereas proteomic data exhibited greater variability and less distinct clustering. Nevertheless, 911 common proteins were found in six out of the seven samples expressing more than 1000 proteins in total (Figure 1f). More specifically, KEGG pathway enrichment showed pathways such as Ribosome, Carbon metabolism, Citrate cycle (TCA cycle), and Purine metabolism had similar expression across all samples with the potential to support hair growth by promoting cell energy production, protein biosynthesis, and cell proliferation/differentiation [29, 44].

Ash‐ELNs were efficiently taken up by HDFs, used here as a well‐established and less specialized screening model for vesicle uptake given their biological comparability with HDPCs [45, 46], at concentrations of 1 × 109 and 1 × 1010 vesicles/mL when incubated at 37°C for 16, 24 and 72 h (Figure 1g). Although the highest uptake was observed after incubation with 1 × 109 and 1 × 1010 vesicles/mL for 72 h, the vesicle uptake was > 70% after 16 h of incubation (Figure 1g). When HDFs were incubated at 4°C minimal uptake was observed (Figure 1g). Incubation with increasing Ash‐ELNs concentrations of up to 1 × 109 vesicles/mL for 72 h exerted no cytotoxicity (Figure S3a). These results demonstrate a temperature and concentration‐dependent uptake of Ash‐ELNs, further emphasizing their applicability in human systems with promising prospects also for in vivo utilization.

We next assessed the functional impact of Ash‐ELNs in human‐derived cellular models. Treatment of HDPCs in a scratch assay (Figure 2a), chosen for its mechanistic overlap with hair growth promotion [47], revealed that Ash‐ELNs dose‐dependently trigger the secretion of growth factors associated with hair cycle promotion, namely vascular endothelial growth factor‐A (VEGF‐A) [48], placental growth factor 1 (PLGF1), basic fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) [49] and leukaemia inhibitory factor (LIF) [50] (Figure 2b). These findings are the first to indicate that plant‐derived exosomes can positively affect the release of hair growth promoting factors.

Next, we assessed the functional consequences of Ash‐ELN treatment in the preclinical, clinically predictive [36, 51] HF organ culture model (Figure 2c). Ash‐ELN maintained significantly more HFs in anagen than the vehicle, accompanied by a significant decrease in hair cycle score, as assessed by Ki‐67/TUNEL and Masson‐Fontana staining (Figure 2d,e). Additionally, treatment with Ash‐ELNs significantly increased hair shaft production at Days 3 and 5 ex vivo (Figure 2f). Neither HM keratinocyte proliferation, nor apoptosis were affected by Ash‐ELN treatment (Figure S3b,c).

Given the increased VEGF‐A release in HDPCs treated with Ash‐ELN (Figure 2a), and considering that VEGF‐A is known to boost DP regenerative potential in vitro [52, 53], we quantified VEGF‐A expression in the mesenchymal compartments of human HFs ex vivo. Ash‐ELN enhanced VEGF‐A expression in the DP, as well as significantly in the dermal cup (DC) and bulbar CTS, also when only HFs remaining in anagen at the end of the culture were analysed (Figure 2g,h). These findings suggest VEGF‐A as one potential mediator of the Ash‐ELN's beneficial effects on anagen maintenance and hair shaft production.

In addition to a direct effect on the DP [52, 53], VEGF‐A also promotes angiogenesis [54], which is tightly connected to anagen maintenance and hair shaft production [48, 55]. We here assessed tubulogenesis, serving as marker for angiogenesis [56], in a HUVECs/HDFs co‐culture, an established in vitro angiogenesis model [57]. Initially, we confirmed that VEGF‐A secretion was increased by treatment with 1 × 109 Ash‐ELN vesicles/mL (Figure 3b). Interestingly, the same concentration also increased total tubule length and area in the HUVECs/HDFs co‐culture, exceeding the effect of VEGF‐A alone for the latter parameter, indicating Ash‐ELNs' pro‐angiogenic effects are mostly, yet not exclusively, mediated by VEGF‐A release from HDFs (Figure 3c,d). Thus, the pro‐angiogenic and pro‐migratory properties of Ash‐ELNs suggest a promotion of regenerative effects in human HFs ex vivo.

Conclusion and Perspective

Here, we present preliminary in vitro and ex vivo data indicating a beneficial effect of Ash‐ELNs on hair growth promotion. Treatment with Ash‐ELNs maintained anagen and increased hair shaft production in the clinically relevant human HF ex vivo model [36, 51, 58], effects likely mediated, at least in part, by VEGF‐A stimulation. Although clinical studies are essential for confirmation, our findings support Ash‐ELNs as a potential adjuvant to combat hair loss.
