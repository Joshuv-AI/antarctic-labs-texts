Response to Letter to the Editor Regarding A Rapid Systematic Review of U.S. Food and Drug Administration-Authorized Coronavirus Disease 2019 Treatments
Authors: Margaret A Maglione, Jeffrey Klausner, Susanne Hempel
Published: 2025

To the Editor—Thank you for bringing to our attention the letter by Sullivan and colleagues [1] about our rapid systematic review of US Food and Drug Administration–authorized coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) treatments [2]. We appreciate their thoughtful comments and have taken them into careful consideration. Below, we provide a point-by-point response, followed by a new sensitivity analysis that incorporates the authors’ feedback.

INITIAL CERTAINTY OF EVIDENCE ON THROMBOTIC RISK WITH COVID-19 CONVALESCENT PLASMA

The authors state that our abstract “erroneously noted limited certainty for thrombotic risk with CCP [COVID-19 convalescent plasma] instead of no evidence of risk, as noted in the text” [1]. However, the text of our review states that “Certainty of evidence is limited due to conflicting results, lack of statistical significance, and wide confidence intervals” [2]. This aligns with the language used in the abstract, which characterized the certainty of evidence as limited.

GROUPING OF SERIOUS ADVERSE EVENTS

We acknowledge the authors’ concern regarding serious adverse event (SAE) categories; the “thrombotic events” category includes deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, embolic stroke, and other thrombotic SAEs. We also grouped all serious infections including sepsis, and all arterial and venous bleeding SAEs. As noted in our article, we grouped these events together per prior work conducted for the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality [3]. The expedited nature of a rapid review precluded contacting study investigators to determine the extent of patient overlap. For some studies, it was impossible to determine overlap from the publications and Clinicaltrials.gov records. As such, the protocol assumed a worst-case scenario for SAE calculations, wherein events in the same SAE category were each counted as an independent event, not accounting for the possibility that multiple events could occur in the same patient.

With the understanding that the rapid review methods may overestimate SAEs in both intervention and control groups, we have conducted a sensitivity analysis for convalescent plasma. We calculated risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each specific type of SAE, presenting these results in footnotes to Table 2. Below is an example of additional analysis, based data on serious thrombotic events from Ortigoza et al [4].

ABSTRACTION ERRORS AND CONTROL GROUP DATA

We appreciate the identification of numerical errors in our table on convalescent plasma [2, Table 2]. We apologize for these and have corrected the data in Table 2, a revised version of our earlier table. Corrected rows are displayed in boldface. Please note that the numbers transposed did not systematically favor the control group or the intervention.

FINDINGS FROM THOMPSON ET AL

Our table on convalescent plasma [2, Table 2] displayed data for the unmatched controls in the non–randomized controlled trial by Thompson et al [5], rather than the propensity-matched controls. The text described important data from the matched controls. Revisions included in Table 2 reflect analysis using the matched control group data, and these rows are bolded.

POOLING RESULTS OF MULTIPLE STUDIES

In their letter, Sullivan et al state that “Grouping all the CCP studies using the data from Table 1 in Maglione, despite the 5 hospital studies and 2 outpatient studies, results in 49 thrombotic events in the CCP group out of 2609 total and 83 in the control group out of 2964, which translates into a significantly lower risk for CCP relative to controls (relative risk of 0.67 with a P = .025 and 95% CI of .473 to .9510) [1].” Conducting meta-analysis (ie, pooling all CCP studies together) was beyond the scope of the rapid review and not appropriate given the goal. As described in our review [2], we instead used a tool created to assess certainty of evidence on adverse events [6] and differentiated the following levels: high confidence, defined as ≥2 studies with negligible methodologic limitations that are consistent in terms of the direction of the effect; moderate confidence, a single study with negligible methodologic limitations or a collection of studies generally consistent in terms of the direction of the effect; limited or low confidence, defined as a single study or a collection of studies lacking precision or consistency; and insufficient, defined as no epidemiologic studies of sufficient quality.

NEW ANALYSIS

In Table 2 we have revised our original table on convalescent plasma [2, Table 2]. with our updated analyses. The conclusions are described below. For bleeding, our original article reported moderate certainty specifically for patients with hematologic cancers [2], based on Thompson et al [5], using data for convalescent plasma compared with unmatched controls. Using matched controls, the RR is 2.67 (95% CI, 1.07–6.62), so our conclusion remains unchanged.

For infection, including sepsis, our original article also reported moderate certainty specifically for patients with hematologic cancers [2], based on Thompson et al [5] (unmatched controls). Using data from matched controls results in an RR of 1.81 (95% CI, 1.26–2.61), so our conclusion remains unchanged.

For thrombotic events, as noted in our article [2], we designated certainty as limited because although the risk was elevated in 5 of the 7 studies, it was not statistically significant in any study, CIs were wide, and most studies provided little or no detail on the type or consequences of the events. (We identified most of these events from clinicaltrials.gov, as few studies reported them in their corresponding published articles).

Based on 2 grade 3 or 4 events in the CCP group and none in the control group, Sullivan et al [7], had the highest risk (RR, 4.97 [95% CI, .24–103.4]). Our sensitivity analyses found RRs of 2.93 (95% CI, .12–69.90) for embolism in Bar et al [8], 5.05 (.24–105.0) for embolic stroke in Ortigoza et al [4], and 1.25 (.61–2.58) for thrombotic events using matched controls for Thompson et al [5]. Although 2 studies showed reduced risk (not statistically significant) the sensitivity analysis supports our conclusion of limited certainty of thrombotic events according to the criteria described above.

We agree that the risks and benefits of CCP treatment must be assessed on a case-by-case basis. In the United States, as of 2024, CCP is approved only for immunocompromised patients, as the Food and Drug Administration has determined that the known and potential benefits of convalescent plasma do not outweigh the risk for immunocompetent patients.

Again, we are thankful for the opportunity to respond to the important concerns raised by Sullivan et al [1]. We hope that the new analyses and revisions are acceptable to them and that they will not hesitate to contact us with any questions or additional feedback.
