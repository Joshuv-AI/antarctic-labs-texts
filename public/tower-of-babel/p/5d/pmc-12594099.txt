Interdisciplinary management of vaginismus: Case series on psycho-behavioral interventions by mental health and gynecological teams
Authors: Swapnajeet Sahoo, Shreyashi Koner, Aashima Arora, Bharti Joshi, Eepsita Mishra, Sandeep Grover
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Vaginismus, categorized under ICD-11 as a sexual pain-penetration disorder, is often overlooked in clinical practice, particularly in culturally conservative societies where stigma, lack of awareness, and gendered misconceptions delay help-seeking. This case series examines the interdisciplinary management of vaginismus.Cases:Five women (ages 22–35) with lifelong or acquired vaginismus, diverse comorbidities (e.g., dysthymia, childhood trauma, infertility distress), and delayed help-seeking due to the sociocultural barriers were managed through an interdisciplinary model. Gynecological assessments ruled out organic causes, followed by psychiatric evaluation, and tailored interventions: pharmacotherapy (Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), duloxetine, propranolol), psychoeducation, cognitive-behavioral techniques (Jacobson’s relaxation, sensate focus), and vaginal dilator therapy. Joint sessions with spouses and graded desensitization were emphasized. Outcomes were tracked over 3–8 months. All patients achieved successful penetrative intercourse and reported improved psychological well-being. Key facilitators included spousal support, trauma-informed therapy, and combined psychiatric-gynecological care. Interventions reduced anticipatory anxiety, resolved pelvic floor dysfunction, and restored marital harmony. Two patients conceived post-treatment.Conclusion:Interdisciplinary collaboration between mental health and gynecological teams is critical for managing vaginismus, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where stigma delays care. The case series highlights the need for enhanced training of gynecologists and mental health professionals to recognize and manage vaginismus as a treatable disorder rather than a taboo subject. This patient series adds to the limited literature on vaginismus from LMICs and serves as an educational resource for clinicians and trainees encountering female sexual dysfunctions in similar sociocultural settings.

INTRODUCTION

In ICD-11, vaginismus has been included under “Sexual pain-penetration disorder” (HA20) in females and is characterized by recurrent and marked fear and anxiety about vulvo-vaginal or pelvic pain in anticipation of, during or as a result of penetration/sexual activity.[12] The exact data on prevalence of vaginismus is not available, however, clinic-based studies have reported a higher prevalence rate of 5%–7%.[34] It often leads to persistent or recurrent difficulties with penetration, which includes involuntary tightening of pelvic floor muscles during sexual intercourse and leads to various psycho-social consequences such as depression, marital discord, separation, and even divorce.[5] Childhood trauma, sexual abuse, negative attitude about sexuality, poor sexual knowledge and education, and relationship problems have been reported to some of the etiological explanations for causation of vaginismus.[6] This is also one of the common causes of unconsummated marriage, yet less researched and less discussed.

Several psychosocial interventions/therapies have been explored for the management of vaginismus such as sex-education, relaxation techniques, couple sex therapy, systematic desensitization, therapist-aided exposure therapy, vaginal penetration exercises, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), and so on.[57] However, these interventions are based on few observational studies and these studies had not taken into account various psycho-social-cultural phenomena. Treatment drop-out and unsuccessful treatment rates are usually high. Further, women presenting with vaginismus from countries that are rigid culturally sex restrictive societies (Middle East Asia, Islamic countries, South Asian countries of Indian subcontinent, African countries)[8] often present late due to shame and embarrassment and continue to suffer in silence. In majority of cases, the women usually consult gynecologists for these complaints and are often unaware of the fact that mental health professionals treat vaginismus. Many a times, it is seen that gynecologists are themselves unaware of prompt referral to mental health professionals in such scenarios, ultimately leading to treatment drop-outs and increase in suffering of the females with vaginismus.

In this regard, we present a case series of five patients with vaginismus, who were managed with collaborative and interdisciplinary care by mental health professionals and gynecologists leading to successful outcome.

Study setting/methodology

The Department of Psychiatry, PGIMER, Chandigarh had been running the Marital and Psychosexual clinic (MPC) since June 1987, which caters to the marital and sexual issues of the patients. Patients with sexual problems and marital discord are registered in the MPC clinic at the walk-in level and subsequently undergo detailed evaluation and treatment. All the patients discussed in this case series were seen in the MPC special clinic and were thoroughly evaluated. The team of psychiatrists and gynecologists were involved in the care of these patients. These patients were treated over a period of l year that is, 2024. Written informed consent was taken from each patient regarding publication of the details of therapy and history and due care had been taken to maintain confidentiality and privacy of the patients discussed. The ICD-11 diagnostic criteria of Sexual pain-penetration disorder (HA20) was used in all cases to diagnose vaginismus and/or any comorbid psychiatric disorders in the patients.

CASE SERIES

Patient 1: Comorbid dysthymia in vaginismus

A 29-year-old woman, married for four years, presented with sadness of mood since adolescence, low self-esteem along with lifelong non-consummation of marriage due to severe perineal tightness and pain. Despite preserved sexual desire, she experienced anticipatory anxiety and avoidance of penetrative attempts. After exclusion of organic etiologies by the gynecologist, she was referred for psychiatric evaluation, suspecting vaginismus. Developmental history revealed early psychosocial adversity, including parental separation at age one, and upbringing in a restrictive household dominated by caregiving responsibilities toward elderly and disabled family members. She started to internalized internalize feelings of sadness and worthlessness. Peer relationships and friendships remained superficial and transient. She secretly got married to a younger partner (4 years younger) due to lack of paternal approval, further exacerbating her guilt and self-esteem deficits. After marriage, the couple have been unable to consummate as she would perceive extremely severe tightness and pain in her perineal region even during the foreplay, despite sexual desire. This further increased her sadness and anxiety, especially before having an attempt for intercourse. As a result, she developed self-deprecating thoughts and carried a persistent sense of failure across her life. A diagnosis of dysthymic disorder (6A72) and vaginismus/sexual pain penetration disorder (HA20) was made as per ICD-11. Beck Depression Inventory score was 18.

Pharmacological management with Paroxetine (up to 37.5 mg/day) resulted in mood improvement. Psycho-education, ventilatory sessions, and Jacobson’s relaxation techniques preceded behavior therapy. Sensate focus and graded vaginal desensitization were implemented with adjunctive anxiolytics and topical anesthetic. However, even after 3 months of this treatment strategy, she continued to have anticipatory anxiety leading to failure in penetrative sexual act, resulting in marital discord. Joint sessions with the couple were taken by mental health professionals along with gynecologists on board. The Gynecologist took three sessions of vaginal dilator therapy assisted using lubricants. Gradually, the patient felt more relaxed and confident to undergo penetrative sexual activity. After 8 months of combined efforts by the gynecologists-mental health team, the couple achieved successful intercourse, and the patient subsequently conceived. She delivered a healthy female baby later and had significant improvement in her sexual and marital life.

Patient 2: Supportive spousal role in vaginismus

A 22-year-old woman, married for 1.5 years via an arranged marriage and with limited premarital sexual knowledge, presented with persistent difficulties in achieving vaginal penetration. She described involuntary vaginal muscle contractions and a pronounced fear of pain during attempted intercourse, resulting in avoidance of intimacy and significant emotional distress. Notably, her husband remained empathetic and non-critical, actively encouraging help-seeking behavior and offering consistent emotional reassurance.

Six months prior to presentation to our psychiatry set-up, she was diagnosed with pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), treated successfully with antibiotics. Post-infection, her apprehension regarding sexual activity intensified, and involuntary vaginal tightening became more prominent. Despite her husband’s encouragement to attempt self-exploration, this yielded minimal benefit. There was no premarital sexual activity, significant medical comorbidity (other than PID), or prior surgical history. A diagnosis of vaginismus was established. Psychosexual stressors, particularly her mother-in-law’s persistent pressure to conceive and critical behavior, contributed to her emotional burden. Nevertheless, the marital relationship remained strong and supportive.

Therapeutic intervention included reassurance, ventilatory sessions addressing interpersonal stressors, and promotion of open spousal communication. Jacobson’s progressive muscle relaxation and pelvic floor exercises were introduced to reduce anticipatory anxiety, along with low-dose propranolol (20 mg) before planned intercourse. Sensate focus exercises emphasizing non-genital touch were employed to foster trust and decrease performance pressure. Re-evaluation by gynecologists’ team was done to rule out any existing PID. The gynecologist team explained finger insertion techniques for self-exploration by doing per-vaginal (PV) examination with lubricants. Further, gradual, and slow exploration of vagina by spousal finger insertion was advised. Over 2–3 months, she reported substantial reduction in anxiety and improved emotional bonding with her partner. Within six months, she successfully achieved penetrative intercourse and subsequently conceived.

Patient 3: Vaginismus linked to childhood trauma

A 24-year-old unmarried woman presented with an 8-year history of lower abdominal pain radiating to the vaginal region, consistently triggered by exposure to sexual content (e.g., intimate scenes in media) or strong emotions such as anger, anxiety, or sadness. She reported a history of sexual abuse by cousins at age seven, which did not involve penetration but resulted in persistent psychological distress, aversion to sexual stimuli, and fear surrounding sexual activity. Attempts at masturbation, encouraged by peers, were unsuccessful and provoked similar pain responses. She had never attempted penetrative intercourse and actively avoided gynecological examinations. Despite being in a romantic relationship for over a year, she refrained from sexual intimacy due to anticipatory fear of pain. While sexual content initially evoked pleasurable sensations, it was invariably followed by sharp pelvic discomfort, leading to feelings of guilt and emotional conflict.

She was referred to psychiatry after gynecological evaluation ruled out organic causes. A provisional diagnosis of vaginismus in the context of childhood sexual abuse was made. Pharmacotherapy with duloxetine 20 mg was initiated but adherence was poor. Psychotherapeutic intervention included masturbation training, psychoeducation, and Jacobson’s progressive muscle relaxation. Following regular practice of relaxation techniques, the patient reported a reduction in the frequency and intensity of pain, particularly when exposed to sexual content. Two sessions were taken by Gynecologist team in which she was taught gentle vaginal self-exploration following relaxation training. After 5 months of these strategies, she reported significant improvement in anxiety, had no more pelvic discomfort when exposed to sexual content and had successful penetrative sexual activity with her partner.

Patient 4: Unconsummated marriage, collaborative healing

A 29-year-old woman, married for 4 years via an arranged marriage, presented with inability to consummate the marriage due to intense pelvic pain and anxiety during penetration attempts. Although she had basic awareness about intercourse and conception prior to marriage and reported adequate arousal during foreplay, any penile contact with the vulval area triggered severe anxiety, involuntary thigh muscle contraction, and pain, often resulting in crying and cessation of the act. Repeated failed attempts over the years, coupled with her husband’s criticism, led to significant emotional distress and frustration. She had never engaged in masturbation and initially sought gynecological evaluation for infertility. With no identifiable organic cause, she was referred to psychiatry.

A multidisciplinary approach was adopted. She was initiated on Escitalopram 10 mg/day and Propranolol 10 mg as needed before intercourse. Couple psycho-education and ventilatory sessions addressed performance anxiety and self-blame. Jacobson’s progressive muscle relaxation and pelvic floor exercises were introduced. Concurrently, gynecological interventions focused on vaginal desensitization, including graded self-insertion with lubricated dilators under guidance. Regular gynecology-psychiatry collaboration helped track progress and tailor interventions. Over 5–6 months, she experienced reduced anxiety, improved comfort with genital touch, and was eventually able to achieve penetrative intercourse.

Patient 5: Silent struggles of intimacy: A case of undiagnosed vaginismus and infertility

A 35-year-old woman presented with primary infertility after 8 years of unconsummated marriage. Despite numerous fertility consultations and hormonal therapies, no organic cause was identified. The couple had limited awareness of sexual health; they believed their inability to consummate was untreatable. Cultural stigma and fear that society would blame the husband for presumed impotence prevented them from seeking help earlier. The patient reported intense pelvic pain and anxiety during every penetrative attempt since marriage. Despite this, her husband remained supportive and never forced intercourse, prioritizing her emotional comfort throughout. She experienced persistent low mood, tearfulness, sleep disturbances, and feelings of hopelessness. Though initially considered an adjustment disorder, her symptoms had persisted for years, suggestive of a depressive episode related to prolonged psychosexual dysfunction.

Psychiatric evaluation led to the diagnosis of vaginismus. A multidisciplinary treatment plan involving gynecologists and mental health professionals was initiated. Therapy included treatment with Escitalopram up to 20 mg/day, psychoeducation, pelvic floor exercises, progressive muscle relaxation, and graded desensitization. Her spouse actively participated in non-demand sensate focus exercises, which enhanced emotional intimacy and reduced anxiety. Over 6 months, she achieved successful penetration and conceived naturally.

DISCUSSION

Female sexual dysfunctions are considered as one of the difficult to treat areas. Vaginismus remains a significantly under-recognized sexual dysfunction, particularly in culturally conservative societies where shame, stigma, and lack of awareness hinder timely intervention. This case series underscores the complexity of vaginismus as a biopsychosocial disorder, necessitating nuanced interdisciplinary approaches that blend psychosexual therapy with gynecological expertise. Each case presented highlights distinct psychosocial pathways to vaginismus, including early trauma, psychiatric comorbidities, interpersonal dynamics, and sociocultural pressures.

One key observation from this series is the importance of individualized and collaborative management. The presence of comorbid dysthymia (Case 1) required parallel mood stabilization before behavioral therapy could be effective, emphasizing the need for comprehensive psychiatric evaluation. This aligns with findings from Maseroli et al. 2018,[5] who noted that success in vaginismus treatment is significantly influenced by addressing psychiatric comorbidities and emotional readiness alongside physical desensitization.

The supportive role of spouses (Cases 2 and 5) emerged as a protective factor. In contrast to traditional expectations of male sexual assertiveness, the empathetic stance of these partners promoted emotional safety, engagement with therapy, and ultimately successful outcomes. This echoes prior work emphasizing spousal cooperation as a critical determinant of treatment success.[910] However, the inverse was seen in Case 4, where repeated failures and perceived criticism by the partner exacerbated anxiety and performance pressure, reaffirming the need for couple-focused interventions and psychoeducation.[10]

Cultural stigmatization, as in Case 5, reveals the hidden burden of female sexual pain disorders in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The belief that sexual non-consummation reflects male impotence, coupled with limited sex education, delayed help-seeking for nearly a decade. Similar barriers were highlighted in a recent studies on sex education and help-seeking behavior in patients with sexual dysfunctions (either gender) in developing South Asian countries[1112] as well as in developed countries.[1314] Importantly, all cases benefited from sensitively delivered psychoeducation and active engagement of both gynecologists and psychiatrists—an interdisciplinary model that enhanced patient trust, reduced attrition, and supported sustained outcomes.

Another salient theme is the link between childhood sexual trauma and vaginismus, as seen in Case 3. The psychophysiological encoding of sexual stimuli with fear and pain, even in non-penetrative abuse, underlines the need for trauma-informed care in sexual dysfunctions. Here, gradual desensitization and relaxation therapies (e.g., Jacobson’s) supported neurophysiological decoupling of arousal and pain responses, a method consistent with evidence from cognitive behavioral model of vaginismus.[1516]

This series also demonstrates that pharmacological agents—SSRIs, propranolol, duloxetine—while not primary treatments, play a valuable adjunctive role in managing anticipatory anxiety, depressive symptoms, and pain modulation. However, the cornerstone of treatment remains behavioral therapy, pelvic floor relaxation, and progressive desensitization, tailored to each patient’s psychological and relational context. CONCLUSIONS

This case series reinforces the need for interdisciplinary, culturally sensitive, and trauma-informed models in managing vaginismus. For mental health trainees, it highlights critical diagnostic, therapeutic, and ethical considerations in addressing sexual dysfunction in women. There is a need to train both psychiatrists and gynecologists to assess and treat sexual pain penetration disorders with a holistic approach.
