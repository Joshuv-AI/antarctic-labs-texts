Spatially Resolved ABA Signalling Reveals Dual Role in Tomato Yield
Authors: Fu‐An Wen, Fu‐Dong Mai, Ji‐Bin Xiao, Hong Zhang, Xuan Zhou, Xin‐Yu Yang, Yelena Sterlin, David Karpovsky, Ke‐Ting Li, Juan Wang, Jia Liu, Shi‐Hao Su, Assaf Mosquna, Yu‐Fei Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAbscisic acid (ABA) is a central hormonal regulator of plant adaptation to stress and developmental progression. However, its contribution to agronomic traits such as yield remains unclear due to the pleiotropic nature of ABA signalling. To disentangle distinct physiological functions of ABA, we employed a receptor‐specific gain‐of‐function strategy in tomato, activating downstream signalling independently of endogenous hormone levels in an ABA‐deficient background. This approach revealed two separable ABA‐responsive modules that contribute to yield formation: one enhancing drought resilience through improved stomatal regulation, and another restoring reproductive success by promoting pollen viability and anther development. These physiological responses, governed by spatially distinct sites of ABA action, partially mitigated the yield penalties associated with ABA deficiency, although neither alone was sufficient to fully restore productivity. Our findings establish a dual functional framework by which ABA signalling coordinates water conservation with reproductive fitness, providing mechanistic insight into how hormone signalling integrates stress adaptation with crop yield. This modular understanding lays the foundation for targeted manipulation of ABA pathways to improve agricultural resilience under climate stress.Keywords: abscisic acid signalling, PYL receptor, reproduction, stomata, yield

Introduction

As plants transitioned from aquatic to terrestrial habitats, the ability to regulate water loss and respond to osmotic stress became critical for survival while maintaining competitive growth. Abscisic acid (ABA) emerged as a central regulator of these processes, integrating environmental cues with quick responses and developmental programs. This functional role created selective pressure for refining ABA signalling into a ligand‐dependent system, enabling dynamic control of plant physiology under adverse conditions. Over the course of evolution, land plants have become increasingly dependent on ABA as an integrator of stress and developmental cues (Anderegg 2015; Cuming and Stevenson 2015; de Vries et al. 2018; Delwiche and Cooper 2015). This dependency is evident in ABA‐deficient or signalling‐impaired mutants, which fail to withstand dehydration, cannot delay germination under suboptimal conditions, and show marked reductions in both yield quantity and quality (Groot and Karssen 1992; Miao et al. 2018; Tan et al. 1997; Zhao et al. 2018). Thus, ABA signalling was not merely retained from early lineages; it was co‐opted, expanded, and embedded as a foundational component of terrestrial plant physiology.

ABA perception is initiated by PYR/PYL receptors, which, upon binding the hormone, inhibit clade A PP2C phosphatases (Fujii et al. 2009; Ma et al. 2009; Park et al. 2009). This releases SnRK2 kinases from repression, with full activation requiring phosphorylation by additional kinases, including group B RAF‐like kinases, calcium‐dependent protein kinases (CDPKs), and BOTRYTIS‐INDUCED KINASE 1 (BIK1) (Li et al. 2024, 2025; Lin et al. 2020; Soma et al. 2020; Takahashi et al. 2020). Activated SnRK2s phosphorylate diverse downstream targets: ion channels to regulate turgor in guard cells (Fujii et al. 2011; Geiger et al. 2009; Lee et al. 2009; Sato et al. 2009) and transcription factors to modulate gene expression (Furihata et al. 2006; Yoshida et al. 2015, 2010). Members of each signalling tier, PYLs, PP2Cs, SnRK2s, and downstream effectors, belong to multigene families, and their interactions vary with expression levels, biochemical specificity, and cellular context. Spatial distribution of signalling components and downstream factors likely shapes tissue‐specific responses. Thus, translating this modular organization to physiology is challenging to resolve.

While PP2C phosphatases and SnRK2 kinases are conserved in streptophyte algae, PYL‐type ABA receptors likely evolved from START‐domain proteins that originally acted as ABA‐independent PP2C inhibitors in the common ancestor of land plants and streptophyte algae (Hauser et al. 2011; Lind et al. 2015; Sun et al. 2019; Zimran et al. 2025). During evolution, PYL receptors diversified across plant lineages to sense distinct ranges of ABA, with dimeric receptors emerging in angiosperms and unique‐gate variants appearing in leafy mosses. This functional diversification, along with the aforementioned complexity of the signalling cascade, contributed to the emergence of a complex regulatory hub that coordinates both immediate stress responses and long‐term growth patterns.

Understanding how these diverse and context‐dependent signalling outputs translate into coordinated plant physiological output necessitated a compromise in resolution. Rather than relying on ectopic expression of individual genes, here, we activated receptors at a relatively high point in the ABA cascade to capture its aggregate effects. By activating a module governed by a specific receptor, we were able to partition the overall ABA‐related physiological outputs. We apply this logic to resolve ABA‐related contributions to yield production. We developed a gain‐of‐function strategy using constitutively active (CA) variants of tomato PYL receptors expressed in an ABA‐deficient background. By artificially activating ABA signalling in the absence of endogenous hormone input, this approach revealed the physiological determinants that link specific ABA responses to yield formation. We demonstrate that ABA promotes yield through at least two separable functional arms: one regulating shoot water balance, and another essential for reproductive fitness, which involves controlling pollen development.

Results

Constitutively Active Tomato ABA Receptors Exhibit ABA‐Independent PP2C Phosphatase Inhibition

To dissect the physiological responses mediated by ABA signalling, we employed a gain‐of‐function strategy using constitutively active mutations predicted to activate tomato PYL receptors. To select suitable candidates, we prioritized PYLs from the same phylogenetic clade, anticipating shared biochemical properties and consistent responses to CA mutations. We further narrowed our focus to receptors with comparable expression levels and, critically, broad expression across both vegetative tissues and fruit. Based on these criteria, three tomato PYLs, SlPYL1, SlPYL3, and SlPYL10, were selected for functional analysis.

Transcriptional profiling of tomato PYL family members revealed that Subfamily I genes, SlPYL1, SlPYL2, SlPYL3, and SlPYL10, are broadly expressed in fruits, with SlPYL1, SlPYL3, and SlPYL10 showing marked upregulation during fruit development (10, 20 and 30 days post‐anthesis) (Figure S1a). In leaves, SlPYL10 expression was downregulated by ABA, whereas SlPYL1 and SlPYL3 remained unchanged, highlighting isoform‐ and tissue‐specificity divergence within Subfamily I (Figure S1b).

This CA mutant engineering strategy builds on our earlier work in Arabidopsis, where mutations H60P, V83F, M158I, and F159V in AtPYR1 conferred ABA‐independent inhibition of clade A PP2Cs (Mosquna et al. 2011). To translate this approach to tomato, we first aligned the amino acid sequences of the selected SlPYLs with AtPYR1 and identified the corresponding residues (Figure S2a). Notably, the histidine at position 60 in AtPYR1 (H60), which is critical for PYL oligomeric state, is naturally substituted by proline in these three tomato receptors. We therefore introduced mutations analogous to AtPYR1V83F M158I F159V into the tomato orthologs: SlPYL1V82F Y157I F158V; SlPYL3V70F Y145I F146V; and SlPYL10V78F Y153I F154V (Figure S2b), which are now referred to as PYL CA mutations.

To assess whether the engineered SlPYLs CA mutants exhibit enhanced basal activity in the absence of ABA, we initially performed yeast two‐hybrid assays to quantify their interactions with key SlPP2C phosphatases. The wild‐type SlPYL1, SlPYL3, and SlPYL10 exhibited detectable basal interactions with SlPP2Cs even without ABA, limiting the resolution of this system for distinguishing CA‐specific effects (Figure S3a) (Feuer et al. 2022). Therefore, we turned to in vitro phosphatase inhibition assays, which offer a more precise biochemical readout. We expressed His‐tagged recombinant SlPYL1/3/10CA proteins and tested their ability to inhibit PP2C activity in the absence of ABA. Under ABA‐free conditions, the three SlPYLCA effectively suppressed the phosphatase activity of SlPP2C6 and HAB1, achieving inhibition levels equivalent to those of wild‐type receptors saturated with 10 μM ABA at the same molar ratios (Figure 1a, Figure S3b). For SlPP2C2, however, the CA mutants exhibited partial inhibition in the absence of ABA (Figure S3b), indicating differential efficacy across PP2C members. These results suggest that the CA mutations stabilize the active receptor conformation, enabling ABA‐independent PP2C inhibition and potentially activating downstream signalling in vivo.

SlPYLsCA Differentially Rescues ABA‐Deficient Vegetative Growth Defects

To investigate the in planta functions of SlPYL1/3/10 CA mutants, we generated transgenic tomato lines (M82 background) expressing proSlPYL::SlPYL CA ‐HPB constructs, where the CA variants of SlPYLs were driven by their native promoters and tagged with an HPB epitope (Figure S4a). Two independent homozygous lines were identified for each receptor. Wild type (WT) and SlPYL CA lines displayed comparable stature with no morphological abnormalities under standard conditions (Figure S5a,b). Drought stress assays also showed similar wilting severity across the different genotypes after 7 days of drought treatment (Figure S5a), and detached leaf water loss rates for transgenic lines are also in line with WT (Figure S5c). Furthermore, stomatal aperture measurements demonstrated that WT and SlPYL CA lines maintained comparable stomatal openings before or after drought treatment (Figure S5d,e). These results suggest that constitutive activation of SlPYL1/3/10 alone does not enhance ABA signalling outputs under either non‐stressed or drought conditions. This raises questions about the in vivo activity of the PYLs. However, it could also result from natural ABA levels being sufficient to elicit signalling, thereby masking any additional contribution from the CA receptors.

Given that the sitiens (sit) mutant, an ABA‐deficient background, exhibits pronounced defects across all tested physiological parameters, we reasoned that this context would provide a more sensitive system to assess the functional impact of SlPYLCA receptors. Therefore, we introgressed SlPYL1/3/10 CA alleles into the sit mutant background through genetic crosses (Figure S4). SlPYL3 and SlPYL10 were sufficient to suppress the sit mutant dwarf phenotype (Figure 1b,c). Constitutive activation of SlPYL3 and SlPYL10 substantially rescued growth and developmental defects characteristic of the sit mutant, with their leaf area and shoot dry weight reaching levels comparable to WT (Figure 1d,e). In contrast, sit/SlPYL1 CA exhibited only partial phenotypic recovery in leaf area (Figure 1d). We next evaluated whether ABA signalling also affects below‐ground performance. Compared to the stunted roots of the sit mutant, both sit/SlPYL1 CA and sit/SlPYL3 CA partially rescued root fresh and dry weights, with SlPYL1 CA showing the most substantial effect. In contrast, sit/SlPYL10 CA showed no significant improvement in root biomass (Figure S6). These results validate the in vivo activity of the three SlPYLCA receptors but, more importantly, the activation of ABA signalling in different organs establishes a distinct ABA‐related physiological signature, enabling a more detailed breakdown of its contribution to yield‐associated traits.

Selective Activation of ABA Receptors Leads to Variable Levels of Yield Rescue

We reasoned that the distinct morphological outcomes resulting from activation of different PYLs provide a means to dissect the physiological determinants underlying yield production. To explore this, we analysed fruit development and yield parameters in sit/SlPYL CA lines. The sit mutant exhibited reduced fruit number and total yield per plant (Figure 2a,c,d), as well as decreased fruit size and single fruit weight (Figure 2b,e). This is consistent with ABA‐related physiological deficiencies that were shown to reduce yield. Introduction of SlPYL3 CA and SlPYL10 CA into the sit background substantially restored these yield‐related traits, both in individual fruit metrics (Figure 2b,e) and overall yield (Figure 2a,c,d), although not to wild‐type levels. Notably, in one parameter, fruit numbers, sit/SlPYL10 CA lines performed comparably to WT (Figure 2a,c), while sit/SlPYL3 CA showed intermediate recovery. In contrast, SlPYL1 CA failed to improve sit mutant yield deficiencies, as sit/SlPYL1 CA plants showed no statistically significant recovery in fruit number, size, or total yield. In the sit background, expression of SlPYL1 CA and SlPYL3 CA improved fruit quality, as reflected by increased soluble solids content (Brix), whereas carotenoid levels were not enhanced by any PYL (Figure S7). Notably, expression of SlPYL10 CA restored carotenoid content to wild‐type levels. In light of the distinct yield‐related phenotypes observed across the CA lines, these genotypes serve as a model to explore the physiological drivers of ABA‐related yield responses.

SlPYL3 Promotes Yield via Stomatal Regulation

To further dissect ABA's contribution to yield regulation, we focused on water‐related parameters in the shoot. While root systems influence yield, they were likely not a limiting factor in our growth conditions. This is supported by the divergent phenotypes observed: SlPYL1 CA substantially rescued root biomass but failed to improve yield, whereas SlPYL10 CA significantly enhanced yield without restoring root growth (Figure 2, Figure S6). These contrasting outcomes suggest that ABA‐dependent yield regulation in our system is largely uncoupled from root developmental pathways and instead mediated by shoot‐based processes.

Given these findings, we next examined whether improved shoot water status, particularly via stomatal regulation, underlies the yield enhancements seen in specific CA lines. Under drought stress, sit/SlPYL3 CA plants outperformed the sit mutant, showing delayed wilting and sustained turgor (Figure 3a). This was supported by slower water loss in detached leaf assays (Figure 3b,e) and constitutively reduced stomatal apertures under both control and drought conditions (Figure 3c,d). Thermal imaging confirmed reduced transpiration, with elevated leaf temperatures in sit/SlPYL3CA (Figure S8). In contrast, sit/SlPYL1 CA and sit/SlPYL10 CA showed only marginal improvements across all assays relative to the sit mutant (Figure 3, Figure S8). Transcriptomic analysis of public datasets revealed that SlPYL3 is the most highly expressed PYL receptor in tomato guard cell (Shohat et al. 2020), which may help explain its distinct phenotypic effects (Figure S9). These consistent improvements in water retention and transpiration dynamics support a mechanistic basis for the yield‐promoting effects observed in SlPYL3 CA lines.

This physiological profile aligns with improved water‐use efficiency and yield stability, supporting that ABA‐dependent modulation of stomatal behaviour plays a central role in sustaining productivity under water‐limited conditions. In contrast, the yield improvements conferred by SlPYL10 activation, which lack stomatal ABA‐regulation, are likely mediated through distinct, non‐stomatal physiological processes.

SlPYL10 Enhances Yield by Restoring Pollen Viability and Anther Development

While differences in stomatal behaviour and water‐use efficiency explained some of the phenotypic variation among CA lines, they did not fully account for the observed SlPYL10 improvements in yield. We therefore examined whether these lines also impact reproductive processes that contribute to yield outcomes.

In common practice, propagation of sit mutants requires either exogenous ABA application or mechanical stimulation of flowers to ensure successful seed set. Suggesting that the reproductive organs are functional, but fertilization is inefficient. This is usually a case of male organ impairment, as their high numbers mean that at least some pollen grains would be expected to achieve fertilization. Additionally, Pollen quality is a critical determinant of crop yield, especially under drought conditions where impaired pollen development can exacerbate productivity losses. To assess whether ABA receptor activation influences male reproductive traits, we examined anther morphology and pollen viability in the sit/SlPYL CA lines. Anthers of sit/SlPYL10 CA plants were morphologically intact and well‐developed, in contrast to the shrunken, underdeveloped anthers observed in sit mutants (Figure 4a). Statistical analyses confirmed that sit/SlPYL10 CA anthers were significantly longer than those of sit and sit/SlPYL1 CA , and wider than both sit and sit/SlPYL1/3 CA lines (Figure 4b, Figure S10c). Reproductive defects in sit were evident in reduced seed production, largely rescued by SlPYL10 CA but not by SlPYL1 CA or SlPYL3 CA (Figure 2b).

To evaluate pollen viability, we used 2,3,5‐triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining, a redox‐based assay that marks metabolically active cells. sit/SlPYL10 CA pollen showed intense TTC staining, comparable to WT, while sit/SlPYL1 CA and sit/SlPYL3 CA exhibited diminished staining intensity, indicative of reduced viability (Figure 4c, Figure S10a). Histological analysis further revealed that SlPYL10 CA specifically rescued developmental defects during the bicellular stages in the sit mutant (Figure 4d), but not at earlier stages (Figure S10b), suggesting a key role in supporting mitotic progression during pollen maturation. Supporting this, in situ hybridization showed SlPYL10 expression was highly localized to developing microspores, with no detectable signal in surrounding anther tissues or other floral organs. In contrast, SlPYL3 showed broad expression across floral structures, while SlPYL1 was undetectable (Figure 4e, Figure S10d).

To gain mechanistic insight into how SlPYL10CA restores anther development and pollen viability, we performed transcriptomic analysis of anthers. Principal component and differential expression analyses showed that sit/SlPYL10 CA partially reverts the sit transcriptional profile toward WT, with substantial recovery of genes associated with reproductive development (Figure 5a–d). Notably, genes involved in pollen wall assembly, sporopollenin biosynthesis, and phenylpropanoid metabolism were significantly reconstituted, alongside key transcription factors and enzymes required for anther and pollen maturation (Figure 5e).

The exclusive enrichment and functional activity of SlPYL10 in developing pollen, together with its ability to restore viable pollen and normal anther morphology, establish its non‐redundant role in ABA‐dependent reproductive development. This is further supported at the transcriptional level by the specific reconstitution of pollen development programs. Given that sit/SlPYL10 CA also conferred the strongest recovery in fruit set and total yield, these findings demonstrate a direct connection between ABA‐mediated signalling in reproductive organs and improved reproductive fitness, which in turn contributes to enhanced yield outcomes.

Discussion and Conclusion

Our findings establish that ABA contributes to yield formation through distinct and receptor‐specific physiological modules, water regulation and reproductive development, mediated by SlPYL3 and SlPYL10, respectively. SlPYL3 activation primarily enhanced shoot water status via tighter stomatal control, improving drought resilience and vegetative biomass. In contrast, SlPYL10 uniquely restored pollen viability and anther development by activation of anther‐specific transcriptional programs, leading to improved fruit set and total yield. The spatial expression patterns of these receptors, with SlPYL3 enriched in guard cells and SlPYL10 localized to developing microspores, provide a mechanistic basis for this functional divergence (Figure 6).

Physiological modules mediate ABA‐dependent yield restoration. Schematic representation of phenotypic and physiological responses in WT, ABA‐deficient sit mutant, and sit/SlPYL3 CA and sit/SlPYL10 CA transgenic tomato lines. The sit mutant displays severe growth inhibition and sterility due to impaired ABA biosynthesis. Constitutive activation of SlPYL3 enhances stomatal regulation in guard cells, improving shoot water status and partially restoring vegetative growth and fruit production. In contrast, SlPYL10 activation rescues male reproductive development by restoring anther morphology and pollen viability, leading to a substantial recovery of fruit set and total yield. Spatially localized activation of ABA signalling in guard cells and developing microspores, mediated via SlPYL3 and SlPYL10, respectively, underlies the distinct contributions of water regulation and fertility to yield formation.

While both modules independently contributed to the partial recovery of sit mutant phenotypes, we cannot definitively determine which is more critical for overall yield. This likely reflects the modular nature of ABA signalling, where different receptors govern distinct physiological outputs. Moreover, given that our CA mutants restore only a single receptor function in an otherwise ABA‐deficient background, it is plausible that full yield potential depends on the coordinated action of multiple ABA receptors acting across tissues and developmental stages.

Additionally, we note that ABA's influence on yield may extend beyond the parameters directly measured here. For instance, while improved stomatal control likely supports gas exchange and photosynthetic stability, these aspects were not quantified and warrant future investigation. Similarly, while SlPYL10 CA restored fruit number and mass, further studies are needed to determine whether this reflects enhanced fertilization, fruit set, or reduced abortion under stress. Nonetheless, our study provides a receptor‐resolved framework for understanding ABA's dual role in vegetative and reproductive performance, offering a strategy to manipulate ABA pathways for improved crop resilience and productivity.

While core ABA signalling components are broadly conserved across plant species, their spatial expression patterns and functional specialization may vary between crops. Our findings provide a conceptual and experimental framework for translation, whereby candidate receptors can be identified based on tissue‐specific expression and functionally validated through targeted constitutively active variants, to dissect and optimize their contributions to yield‐related traits.

Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions

Tomato ( Solanum lycopersicum cv. M82) was used as the wild‐type background. Seeds of all genotypes were surface‐sterilized and germinated on moist filter paper in Petri dishes. After 3 days, uniformly germinated seedlings were transferred to pots containing a soil mixture of vermiculite:perlite:peat (1:1:2 by volume). Plants were grown under controlled environmental conditions in a growth chamber with a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod, 25°C/20°C day/night temperature and 60% relative humidity.

The sitiens (sit) mutant was generated using CRISPR‐Cas9‐mediated genome editing to disrupt the SITIENS gene in the M82 background. Guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed to target the sixth exon of SITIENS. The CRISPR‐Cas9 expression vector was constructed using the Golden Gate cloning method (Engler et al. 2014). The plasmid was transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and introduced into M82 tomato via Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation. Mutation in the SITIENS gene was confirmed by PCR amplification and sequencing of the targeted loci (Figure S4b). Transgenic free homozygous sitiens (sit/sit) mutants were identified in the next generation by genotyping and used for subsequent phenotypic and physiological analyses.

Constitutively active (CA) variants of SlPYL1, SlPYL3, and SlPYL10 were generated by introducing mutations analogous to those stabilizing the ABA‐bound conformation in AtPYR1 (Mosquna et al. 2011). Specifically, mutations V82F, Y157I, and F158V were introduced into SlPYL1; V70F, Y145I, and F146V into SlPYL3; and V78F, Y153I, and F154V into SlPYL10 (Figure S2). SlPYL CA genomic sequences fragments containing the CA mutations were chemically synthesized. For generation of SlPYL CA transgenic lines in M82 background, genomic sequences of each SlPYL CA were cloned downstream of their native promoters and fused to a C‐terminal biotinylation tag (HPB) (Figure S4a). Constructs were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 and subsequently used for stable transformation of M82 background. Two independent homozygous lines were established for each construct and designated as WT/SlPYL1 CA , WT/SlPYL3 CA , and WT/SlPYL10 CA . These lines were crossed with sit mutant plants to generate sit/SlPYL CA lines. Individuals homozygous for both the SlPYL CA transgene and the sit mutation were identified via PCR genotyping and Sanger sequencing. The resulting sit/SlPYL1/3/10 CA lines were subsequently used for all phenotypic and physiological analyses.

Yeast Two‐Hybrid Assay

The full‐length coding sequences of SlPYL1, SlPYL3, SlPYL10, and their respective constitutively active (CA) mutants were cloned into the pGBKT7 vector (Clontech), resulting in fusions with the GAL4 DNA‐binding domain (BD). The coding sequences of SlPP2C1/2/3/4/5/6/8 were cloned into the pGADT7 vector to generate fusions with the GAL4 activation domain (AD). Yeast strain Y190 was co‐transformed with combinations of BD‐PYL (wild‐type or CA) and AD‐PP2C constructs. Transformants were selected on SD/−Leu/−Trp dropout media. Individual clones were spotted onto SD‐Leu‐Trp glass plates containing 10 μM ABA and 0.1% DMSO as solvent control. Following incubation at 28°C for 2 d, colonies were chloroform lysed and stained to estimate β‐galactosidase accumulation (Park et al. 2009).

PP2C Phosphatase Inhibition Assay

The coding sequences of all PYLs and PP2Cs were cloned into the pET28a vector generating 6 × His‐PYLs and 6 × His‐PP2Cs. Proteins were expressed in BL21 (DE3) pLysS (Promega) E. coli strain as previously described (Zimran et al. 2025). Phosphatase assays were carried out in assay buffer containing 0.5 μM PP2C and 0, 0.5, 1, or 2 μM receptor and 33 mM Tris‐acetate (pH 7.9), 66 mM potassium acetate, 0.1% BSA, 10 mM MnCl2, 0.1% β‐ME, and 50 mM pNPP with or without 10 mM ABA. Hydrolysis of pNPP was monitored at A405. PP2C activity in the absence of the receptor was set as 100% activity. Assays were performed in triplicate with at least two independent protein preparations.

RNA‐Seq

Plants were watered with a 10 μM ABA solution, and samples were collected 14 h later. Total RNA was extracted from leaves using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen). Library preparation was performed using the Bulk MARS‐Seq protocol. RNA‐seq libraries were prepared at the Crown Genomics Institute of the Nancy and Stephen Grand Israel National Center for Personalized Medicine, Weizmann Institute of Science. A bulk adaptation of the MARS‐Seq protocol (Jaitin et al. 2014; Keren‐Shaul et al. 2019) was used to generate RNA‐Seq libraries for expression profiling of the samples. Counts for each gene were quantified using the ESAT toolkit (ESAT. Available online: https://github.com/garber‐lab/ESAT; accessed on 27 August 2021), using the BED file based on ITAG4.0 and the 3′ UTR region (1000 bases), and corrected for UMI saturation.

To investigate how SlPYL10CA restores anther development and pollen viability, anthers were collected from WT, sit, and sit/SlPYL10 CA plants at the same developmental stage. Each genotype included three independent biological replicates. Fresh anther samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and ground into a fine powder. Total RNA was extracted using the RNAeasy Isolation Reagent (Vazyme, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA quality and integrity were assessed prior to library construction. RNA samples were then delivered to LC‐Bio Technology CO. Ltd. (China) for cDNA library preparation and high‐throughput sequencing on an illumina Novaseq 6000 following the vendor's recommended protocol. Clean reads were obtained after quality filtering and used for downstream transcriptomic analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among the three genotypes were identified based on standard statistical criteria.

Measurements of Plant Growth Parameters

Plant height was measured from the base of the stem to the shoot apex using a ruler. Fully expanded leaves were imaged, and total leaf area was quantified using ImageJ software. For biomass analysis, shoots and roots were harvested separately, and fresh weights were recorded immediately. Samples were then oven‐dried at 65°C for 72 h before measuring dry weights.

Physiological Assays for Water Status and Drought Response

For drought stress treatment, plants were grown under well‐watered conditions for 6 weeks before imposing drought stress by withholding irrigation for 7 days. Morphological changes and wilting symptoms were documented photographically throughout the drought period. Stomatal apertures were measured before and after drought stress to evaluate guard cell responses. Imprint II Garant (3 M, Shanghai, China) impression material was promptly applied to the leaf surface. The imprint was then filled with 2‐Ton Epoxy adhesive (Devcon, Suzhou, China) to produce epoxy replicas, as described previously (Su et al. 2018). Impressions were observed under a light microscope. Stomatal width and length were quantified using ImageJ software, and aperture was calculated as the width‐to‐length ratio. Representative images were captured for each sample. To estimate water retention capacity, excised leaves were subjected to a time‐course desiccation assay. The sixth fully expanded leaf was detached from each plant at room temperature (~25°C) under ambient airflow. Leaves were immediately weighed (0 h) and subsequently at 0.5 h intervals over a 6‐h period. Water loss rate was calculated using the formula: Water loss (%) = [(Fresh weight at 0 h—Fresh weight at 6 h)/Fresh weight at 0 h] × 100%. Thermal images were captured from intact plants at 10:00 AM using a FLIR T630 thermal camera. Leaf surface temperatures were analysed using FLIR Tools v5.2.15161.1001 software.

Anther Morphology and Pollen Viability Assays

For anther morphology analysis, thirty mature anthers per genotype were randomly selected, and their length and width were measured using a vernier calliper. Pollen viability was assessed by 2,3,5‐triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining: flower buds were collected from plants grown under normal conditions. Fresh pollen grains released from excised anthers were incubated with TTC solution at 35°C for 15 min and scored under a light microscope based on staining intensity, with bright red indicating viability. Pollen grains were counted using ImageJ software.

Yield‐Related Trait Measurements

Tomato plants were grown in a growth chamber under normal conditions for 4 weeks, and then transferred to a greenhouse. Fruits were harvested 3 months after transfer. For yield‐related trait measurements, fruits were harvested from each individual plant, and the number of fruits and total fruit weight were recorded. For single fruit weight, around 30 fruits per genotype were weighed.

Determination of Fruit Carotenoids and Soluble Solids Content

Carotenoids were extracted from tomato fruit tissue and quantified spectrophotometrically as previously described with minor modifications (Val et al. 1986). Briefly, fresh pericarp tissue from ripe tomato fruits was homogenized and extracted with 80% acetone until the tissue became colourless. The extracts were then centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, and the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 440 nm using a spectrophotometer, with 80% acetone as the blank control.

Soluble solids content (SSC) of tomato fruits was measured using a Professional Pocket Brix & Acidity Meter (ATAGO PAL‐BXIACID3, Japan). Fresh fruit juice was obtained by squeezing the pericarp tissue, followed by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min. Subsequently, 300 μL of the supernatant was collected and used for SSC measurement according to the manufacturer's instructions. The SSC was expressed as °Brix.

Paraffin Sectioning and Staining

Paraffin embedding was performed following a standard protocol (Strable et al. 2020). Anthers were fixed in FAA solution (10% formalin, 5% acetic acid, and 50% ethanol), then dehydrated through an ethanol series and cleared with xylene. Tissues were embedded in Paraplast Plus (Sigma‐Aldrich), sectioned, and stained with 0.5% Fast Green before imaging under a Nikon ECLIPSE Ni microscope.

RNA In Situ Hybridization

For RNA in situ hybridization, flower buds were collected and fixed in FAA fixative. The buds were then embedded with Paraplast‐plus after treatment with multiple ethanol and Van‐Clear solutions. cDNAs of SlPYLs were amplified and cloned into the pTA2 plasmid (TOYOBO) before in vitro transcription. The probe labelling and the hybridization process were performed as previously reported (Su et al. 2023). The primers used here are listed in Table S1.
