Association of first-line combination antiretroviral therapy with malaria among adult HIV-infected persons in Jos, Nigeria: a pilot cross sectional study
Authors: Asukwo Onukak, Enoch Aninagyei, Agantem Ekuma, Simji Gomerep, Nathan Shehu, Samson Isa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection coexist in significant numbers in some geographic areas including sub-Sahara Africa (SSA). HIV-infected patients are a World Health Organization (WHO) recognized high risk group for increased malaria morbidity. Majority of HIV-infected patients undertaking treatment in SSA are on WHO recognized first-line combination antiretroviral therapy (cART). Considering the immunity-enhancing capacity of antiretroviral therapies on people living with HIV, this study aimed to explore the association between first-line combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) with malaria parasitaemia and antigenaemia in adult HIV-infected persons and to determine the predictors of malaria antigenaemia in adult persons living with HIV.MethodsThe study was conducted at the AIDS Prevention Initiative in Nigeria (APIN) Centre, Jos University Teaching Hospital, Jos, Plateau State, from August 2018 to February 2019. Epi Info statistical tool was used to determine the sample size and power of the study. The study population consisted of three groups. The first group comprised first-line cART-experienced adult HIV-seropositive subjects, the second group comprised ARV-naïve HIV-seropositive adults and the third group comprised HIV-seronegative adults. For this pilot study, 60 persons were recruited into each group via convenience sampling. Malaria rapid diagnostic test (RDT) was performed according to manufacturer’s instruction for all the study participants using SD Bioline Malaria Ag P.f (HRP2/pLDH) (Standard Diagnostics, Hagal-Dong, Korea). All the study participants also had thick and thin blood film malaria microscopy. Data collected was processed and analyzed using the Stata statistical software version 15 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas). Chi square was used to test the association between malaria and first-line cART exposure. Univariate and multivariate analysis were also done to identify factors that were independently associated with malaria antigenaemia.ResultsA total of 180 persons participated in the study and involved 60 participants recruited in each of the three study groups. Overall, the predominant study participants were females (56.67%), traders (27.78%), secondary school leavers (43.33%) and urban dwellers (88.89%). Their mean age and standard deviation was 37.07 ± 11.53 years. Using malaria microscopy, the prevalence of malaria parasitaemia in ARV-naïve HIV-infected persons was 5% and 0% in the first-line cART-experienced HIV-infected persons as well as the HIV-negative persons. Malaria RDT result was positive in 7/60 (11.67%) of the first-line cART experienced HIV-infected participants, 6/60 (10%) of the ARV-naïve HIV-infected group and 1/60 (1.67%) of the HIV-negative group. Of the seven positive malaria RDT results in those on first-line cART, five persons were receiving zidovudine/lamivudine/nevirapine (AZT/3TC/NVP) while the remaining two were receiving tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/lamivudine/efavirenz (TDF/3TC/EFV), thus making an antigenaemia proportion of 16.67% and 6.67% respectively. Being an HIV-infected person on first-line cART (OR = 16.20, p = 0.04), having a headache (OR = 6.21, p = 0.03) and non-usage of window nets (OR = 3.74, p = 0.05) were found to be predictors of malaria antigenaemia.ConclusionMalaria parasite burden in HIV-infected persons on first-line cART is lower than that observed in ARV-naïve HIV-infected persons. Our study suggests that TDF/3TC/EFV may be associated with lower malaria antigenaemia when compared with AZT/3TC/NVP and can be considered an alternative first-line antiretroviral regimen in malaria-endemic regions.Keywords: Malaria, P. Background

Over 0.5% of the world’s population is living with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)/acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS) with about 5000 new infections occurring daily. The global prevalence of HIV/AIDS continues to increase, with South Africa, Nigeria, Mozambique, India, Kenya and the United States having the highest burden [1]. Malaria is also a global challenge for which the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2021 estimated 247 million malaria cases and 625,000 malaria deaths in 84 malaria-endemic countries. The WHO African Region represented about 95% of the malaria cases and deaths [2].

Malaria and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection coexist in significant numbers in some geographic areas such as sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), South East Asia and South America [3]. Nigeria has been ranked third among the countries with the highest number of people living with HIV (PLHIV), with about 1.9 million PLHIV between the ages of 15–49 years [4]. Among other SSA countries, Nigeria (27%), the Democratic Republic of the Congo (12%), Uganda (5%) and Mozambique (4%) –contributed almost half of all cases worldwide in 2021 [2]. As a result of the high prevalence of malaria and HIV in SSA, co-infections abound with synergistic deleterious effects [5]. HIV-infected patients are a World Health Organization (WHO) recognized high risk group for increased malaria morbidity as are other high risk groups like infants and pregnant women living in malaria endemic areas [6]. The infection has been associated with higher malaria parasite densities and lower haemoglobin levels as well as with increased frequency of clinical malaria especially in individuals with reduced CD4 + cell counts. Majority of HIV-infected patients undertaking treatment in SSA are on WHO recognized first-line combination antiretroviral therapy (cART). The combination for adults comprises three antiretroviral medications, with two taken from the nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) class and one from the non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) class. The second line cART involves the switching of NNRTI for a protease inhibitor (PI) [7, 8]. Dolutegravir (DTG), an integrase inhibitor, has been recommended in place of NNRTIs as the first-line combination of choice in adult HIV-infected persons [9]. Experimental studies have demonstrated some antimalarial activity of some of the first-line cART medications both in-vitro and in animal models [10, 11]. However, there are only few human studies [12–14]. First-line cART has also been linked to the reduction in the frequency of malaria by immune reconstitution [15].

Commercially available rapid diagnostic tests (RDT) can achieve a sensitivity of ~ 100 parasites/µl of blood, similar to expert microscopy [16]. They also do not require specialized personnel and have a shorter turnaround time. A beneficial effect of first-line regimens on malaria could have significant impact on the management and control of malaria in HIV-infected persons. Considering the immunity-enhancing capacity of antiretroviral therapies on people living with HIV, this pilot study aimed to explore the association between first-line cART with malaria parasitaemia and antigenaemia in adult HIV-infected persons and to determine the predictors of malaria antigenaemia in adult persons living with HIV.

Methods

Study site and participants

The study was conducted at the AIDS Prevention Initiative in Nigeria (APIN) Centre, Jos University Teaching Hospital, Jos, Plateau State from August 2018 to February 2019. The APIN Centre, JUTH, was established in 2001 and provides ambulatory HIV treatment services to clients that come from different states of the north-central region of Nigeria. An estimated 340 persons get tested at the facility annually with about 50 of them being sero-positive and initially ART-naïve before being enrolled into care. There are currently about 7500 persons on cART.

The study population consisted of three groups. The first group comprised first-line cART-experienced adult HIV-seropositive subjects 18 years and above. The second group comprised ARV-naïve HIV-seropositive adults and the third group comprised HIV-seronegative adults who were recruited from the HIV Counselling and Testing unit of APIN, following a negative HIV test. The study populations, from which consenting participants were selected, came from various urban and rural areas within and outside Plateau State, Nigeria. For this pilot study, convenience sampling was used to recruit participants, who were then provided with information about the study.

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study of malaria antigenaemia and parasitaemia of the participants in the three groups.

Sample size determination

Using the Epi Info statistical tool [17] through StatCalc sample size and power (cohort or cross-sectional), the minimum sample size should be 108 per group with a 90% power and a 95% confidence interval. The malaria parasitaemia prevalence of 7.8% in first-line cART-experienced HIV-seropositive adults [12] was used for the exposed group and a prevalence of 24% in ARV-naïve HIV-seropositive adults [18] was used for the unexposed group.

However, a sample size of 30 per group has been recommended for pilot studies [19]. For this study, 60 persons were recruited into each group including a third group of HIV seronegative persons for better assessment of the preliminary data.

Inclusion criteria

Adults who were 18 years and above in each of the three groups were recruited. First-line cART-experienced HIV-seropositive adults had to have been on their medications for at least six months prior to recruitment. Persons living with HIV without clinically obvious debilitating opportunistic diseases like tuberculosis and malignancies were recruited irrespective of their WHO clinical staging [20].

Exclusion criteria

Excluded from the study were the following persons: Pregnant women; patients that have had to substitute any component of their first-line cART regimen since commencement of treatment; people who had received an antimalarial within the previous one month of the study; patients that had been on cotrimoxazole, clindamycin, tetracyclines, and macrolides within the previous one month of the study due to their antimalarial properties [21]; patients with clinically obvious opportunistic diseases like tuberculosis and malignancies; and patients on immunosuppressive therapy like cytotoxics.

Collection of participants’ demographic and clinical data

Each participant responded to questions from a structured questionnaire, which requested information regarding the patient’s biodata, symptoms, malaria control/treatment practices, HIV status and treatment regimen for adults living with HIV.

Blood sample collection

Blood samples were collected by experienced and trained phlebotomist, as previously published [22]. In brief, the antecubital fossa was disinfected with 70% ethanol and allowed to air dry. Using a vacutainer needle (BD vacutainer, USA), whole blood sample was collected into an ethylenediamine tetra-acetic acid vacuum tube (BD Vacutainer® EDTA Tubes, USA) and mixed gently while ensuring hemostasis on removing the needle.

Detection of malaria antigenaemia and parasitaemia

Malaria rapid diagnostic test was performed according to manufacturer’s instruction for all the study participants using SD Bioline Malaria Ag P.f (HRP2/pLDH) (Standard Diagnostics, Hagal-Dong, Korea). All the study participants also had thick and thin blood film microscopy carried out for malaria parasite quantification and species identification respectively. Thick blood film was prepared, air dried and stained with 10% Giemsa for about 10 min. The thin film was fixed with absolute methyl alcohol (Flinn Scientific, Illinois, USA) for 30 s after which the methyl alcohol was poured off and 10% dilute Giemsa stain immediately added. The stain was allowed to stand for 10 min after which it was rinsed in distilled water and air-dried [23].

Data analysis

Participants’ data was anonymized to ensure confidentiality. Data collected was processed and analyzed using the Stata statistical software version 15 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas). Normality of continuous variables was tested with Shapiro-Wilk test. Chi square was used to test the association between malaria and first-line cART exposure while Fisher’s exact test was done for variables with ≤ five counts per cell. Additionally, univariate and multivariate analysis were done to identify factors that were independently associated with malaria antigenaemia. In all, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study population

A total of 180 persons who met the inclusion criteria participated in the study, with 60 participants recruited into each of the three study groups. The mean age and standard deviation of the study participants was 37.07 ± 11.53 years; 44.75 ± 10.23 years for the first-line cART experienced HIV-infected group, 32.73 ± 9.14 years and 33.72 ± 11.15 years for the ARV-naïve HIV-infected group and the HIV-negative group respectively (p = ˂0.01). The study participants were predominantly female (n = 102, 56.67%) and resided in urban settings (n = 160, 88.89%). Malaria antigenaemia and parasitaemia among adults on different combinations of first-line cART

Malaria RDT result was positive in seven of the first-line cART experienced HIV-infected participants, six of the ARV-naïve HIV-infected group and one of the HIV-negative group giving a P. falciparum antigenaemia proportion of 11.67%, 10.00% and 1.67% respectively. The difference in prevalence was statistically significant (p = 0.04). P. falciparum was the only malaria parasite noted in the positive findings. Factors associated with positive malaria antigenaemia

Relevant clinical and laboratory findings were compared across the three study groups. Each variable was tested in a univariate analysis. Variables with biologic plausibility and those identified from literature review to show association with malaria were included in the final multivariate logistic regression model as shown in Table 3.

Discussion

From this pilot study, P. falciparum antigenaemia using the malaria RDT was 11.7% in the HIV-infected persons on treatment, 10% in the treatment naïve HIV-infected persons and 1.7% in the HIV negative persons. The differences were not statistically significant. This is at variance with the findings from malaria microscopy that showed statistically significant differences in malaria parasitaemia across the three groups, with 5% parasitaemia in the treatment naïve HIV-infected population. Hence, patients on first-line cART had a higher proportion of antigenaemia when compared to their treatment-naïve counterparts unlike what was observed from malaria microscopy. This may be due to false positive results from persistence of antigenaemia when using histidine-rich protein 2 (HRP2)-based RDT kits, that has been documented for up to 6 weeks following parasite clearance from antimalarials [24, 25].

Nakanjako et al. [14] had documented a 4% antigenaemia using RDT in HIV-infected persons on first-line cART in Uganda; though the study participants were also on cotrimoxazole prophylaxis which may explain their lower finding due to the antimalarial property of cotrimoxazole. Using PCR as the standard of assessment, the sensitivity of microscopy when compared to RDT varies in the literature, with some studies considering microscopy more sensitive [26, 27], while others consider RDT more sensitive [28, 29]. Though the current study demonstrated a higher proportion of antigenaemia using RDT when compared to malaria parasitaemia on microscopy, PCR would have enabled assessment of the accuracy of each diagnostic modality.

Malaria parasitaemia prevalence using microscopy was 5% in the treatment naïve group. HIV-infected persons on first-line ARVs had no detectable malaria parasitaemia by microscopy, similar to the HIV seronegative study group. Previous studies had noted much higher malaria prevalence of 21% [30] and 24% [18] in treatment naïve HIV-infected adults in Jos in 2005 and 2012 respectively, giving a reduction in prevalence of between 75 and 80% when compared to the current finding. These previous studies in Jos also noted a malaria parasitaemia prevalence of 11.6% and 9% respectively in the HIV seronegative population. The 2018 World malaria report [31] had noted an 18% reduction in the incidence rate of malaria between 2010 and 2017 and Africa was reported as recording a 40% reduction in malaria mortality in 2017. The marked reduction in malaria prevalence may have resulted from increasing awareness about malaria control and effective treatment [32, 33].

The non-detectable microscopic parasitaemia in the cART-experienced HIV-infected group in this study is also similar to the 2.1% prevalence of asymptomatic malaria parasitaemia noted in Benin, Nigeria [13], but lower than the 7.8% prevalence that was documented in Kogi State [12], which like Jos is also in north central Nigeria. However, some of the patients in the Kogi study were febrile. The absence of fever in the current study may also explain the low prevalence of microscopic parasitaemia in the study populace as individuals with a higher peripheral parasitaemia are at greater risk of being febrile [34].

Following multivariate logistic regression, being an HIV-infected person on first-line cART, having a headache and non-usage of window nets were significantly associated with a higher odds of malaria antigenaemia. The higher odds in the treatment-experienced study group may have been due to persistent antigenaemia from relatively recent use of poorly recalled antimalarials as well as antibiotics with antimalarial properties and possible antimalarial effects of the first-line regimens. Non usage of insecticide treated bed nets was not included in the regression analysis because none of the participants lacking an insecticide treated net had a positive malaria RDT across the three study groups. However, it has been validated in the literature as an important malaria control tool [15].

The ARV experienced HIV-infected group comprised 30 persons on TDF/3TC/EFV and 30 persons on AZT/3TC/NVP. Malaria parasitaemia by microscopy was not demonstrated in them. Animal studies by Akinyede et al. [11], following inoculation with P. berghei, had compared possible antimalarial properties between AZT/3TC/NVP and stavudine (d4T)/3TC/NVP, which are different first-line cART regimens. At doses extrapolated to therapeutic concentrations in humans, AZT/3TC/NVP, which was used in the current study, was associated with serial reduction and eventual clearance of the malaria parasite, while the mice that were on d4T/3TC/NVP had increasing malaria parasite density.

Given the overall low malaria microscopic parasitaemia noted in this study, there is need for larger PCR based studies to be able to assess submicroscopic malaria parasite densities, due to its higher sensitivity [29]. This will enable a better comparison of malaria parasite densities between TDF-based first-line regimens, which is the regimen of choice, with other first-line regimens. Any significant differences in the malaria parasite densities in HIV-infected persons on the different regimens may then be determined more objectively. However, using RDT, there was a lesser proportion of P. falciparum antigenaemia in persons on TDF/3TC/EFV (6.67%) when compared with those on AZT/3TC/NVP (16.67%). Though the difference was not significant (p = 0.42), this may suggest better activity of TDF-based regimens against malaria. A study by Azevedo et al. on the effect of antiretroviral drugs on the sporogonic and blood stages of P. berghei in vitro and in vivo found that TDF/3TC/EFV inhibited plasmodia oocyst development in vitro by 50% and there was also a significant reduction in the oocyst load on infected mosquitoes in vivo with the same regimen [35]. The regimen was mainly compared with AZT/3TC/lopinavir-ritonavir(LPVr), which is a second-line regimen in adults and contains a protease inhibitor (LPVr) with established antimalarial property and hence, showed a 90% reduction in oocyst formation. Our alternative first line regimen, AZT/3TC/NVP, was not evaluated in the study.

Limitations

One limitation of the study is that quantitative PCR assays for malaria parasitaemia was not done. Though being a more expensive investigation, it will have enabled submicroscopic malaria parasite detection and density assessment together with an objective determination of false positive malaria RDT. For our pilot study, the participants recruited were just about half of the estimated sample size; hence, it was not powered enough to conclusively detect a difference where present. This can also explain the wide confidence interval of the odds of the first-line cART-experienced group in being significantly associated with malaria antigenaemia on multivariate regression. There is therefore the need for preferably cohort studies with larger sample sizes appropriately powered to better determine temporal relationships between first-line combination antiretroviral therapy with malaria among adult HIV-infected persons.

Conclusion

Malaria parasite burden in HIV-infected persons on first-line cART is lower than that observed in ARV-naïve HIV-infected persons. Our study suggests that TDF/3TC/EFV may be associated with lower malaria antigenaemia when compared with AZT/3TC/NVP and can be considered an alternative first-line antiretroviral regimen in malaria-endemic regions.
