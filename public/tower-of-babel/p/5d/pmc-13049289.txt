Effects of stocking rate on grazing behavior, nitrogen and energy metabolism, and greenhouse gas emissions of Simmental cattle in saline meadow
Authors: Xuan Wang, Yueting Pan, Kaili Xie, Tianhai Yan, Fujiang Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA rational stocking rate balances grassland ecosystems and improves livestock productivity. This study examined how stocking rates affect Simmental cattle in saline meadow through analyses of grazing behavior, nitrogen (N) and energy metabolism, and greenhouse gas emissions, to inform targeted livestock management. A total of 24 male Simmental cattle (3-month-old, 104.72 ± 1.78 kg) were selected and assigned to stocking rates of 0.66, 1.33, and 2.66 cattle/ha (8 cattle/treatment). The experiment adopted a rotational grazing system, with three paddocks as replicates for each stocking rate, and lasted for 90 d. Each paddock was grazed for 10 d and rested for 20 d. The rumination rate (P = 0.002), chews per bolus (P = 0.017), and average daily gain (P < 0.001) were lower in the 2.66 cattle/ha than in the 0.66 and 1.33 cattle/ha. The stocking rate of 1.33 cattle/ha increased (P < 0.001) crude protein digestibility, retention N, retention energy, N and energy utilization, decreased urinary energy (P = 0.012), CO2 emission and intensity (P < 0.001), CH4 energy/digestible energy (P = 0.001), and CH4 energy/metabolizable energy (P = 0.001), and resulted in the lowest CH4 energy output. At this stocking rate, the CO2 or CH4 intensities increased by 0.390 or 0.018 g/kg metabolic body weight (BW0.75) per d, respectively, with an increase of 1 g/kg BW0.75 per d of dry matter intake (P < 0.001). The metabolizable energy requirement for maintenance (MEm) was 0.98% and 2.40% lower (P < 0.001), while the metabolizable energy maintenance utilization efficiency (Km) was 1.08% and 0.96% higher for 1.33 cattle/ha compared to 0.66 and 2.66 cattle/ha, respectively. Therefore, a stocking rate of 1.33 cattle/ha is optimal for saline meadow in arid regions, as it increases utilization of N or energy and Km, while decreases CO2 emissions and CH4 energy losses.Keywords: Grazing behavior, Energy requirement, CH4 emissions, Prediction equation, Saline meadow

Introduction

As an important terrestrial ecosystem, pasture provides multiple services such as essential material and environmental benefits for human beings, as well as high-quality forage for livestock (Crossley et al., 2023). Consumers are increasingly concerned about food production practices and prefer systems that allow livestock to live more naturally outdoors, which also aligns with the evolutionary needs of ruminants (Hötzel et al., 2017; Schuppli et al., 2014). Indeed, forage-based production systems were considered highly profitable (Dillon et al., 2008), but their success depends on efficient forage utilization. Numerous studies have examined strategies such as increasing stocking rates (Coffey et al., 2018; McCarthy et al., 2016) and improving pasture management (Evers et al., 2021) to maximize forage intake and utilization, thereby enhancing nutrient intake per ha. Therefore, the rational utilization of grassland ecosystems is essential for maintaining regional ecological security and ensuring sustainable, high-quality animal production.

Arid regions cover about 41% of the global land area (Zhou et al., 2023), account for 78% of the world’s rangelands (Maestre et al., 2022), are home to more than 38% of the population, and support approximately half of the world’s livestock (Reynolds et al., 2007). The Hexi Corridor, located in the west of Gansu, China, is characterized by a typical arid and semi-arid climate area. Grassland is its main vegetation type, accounting for approximately 35.6% of the entire region (Cheng et al., 2022). According to statistics, the desert grassland area in the Hexi Corridor accounts for 43.7% of the total grassland area (Cheng et al., 2022). Salinized wasteland accounts for 53% of the total wasteland area, while secondary salinized cultivated land constituents 17% of arable farmland (Li et al., 2019). Globally, soil salinization threatens nearly 20% of irrigated agriculture (Hassani et al., 2021), particularly in arid regions (Li et al., 2014), where around 30% of irrigated agriculture is saline land, which means that salinization as a part of the ecosystem has caused agricultural resource waste and economic losses (Daliakopoulos et al., 2016). Meanwhile, soil salinization exacerbates challenges of maintaining healthy pasturelands, thereby threatening both livestock production and the long-term sustainability of these ecosystems. In this context, as one of the important natural resources, natural forage is of great significance to the sustainable development of livestock and food security in arid areas. However, there is a lack of adequate studies on how much livestock carrying capacity can be maintained in saline meadows in arid regions without affecting livestock growth and improving animal welfare.

Additionally, a key factor in global warming is greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, with CH4 contributing 16% of total GHG and 33% of global anthropogenic CH4 emissions, with a warming potential 28 to 34 times that of CO2 (Lan and Yang, 2019; Scheehle and Kruger, 2006). 50.6% of total anthropogenic CH4 emissions originate from agriculture (Islam and Lee, 2019), particularly from ruminants, which account for 16% of global emissions (Lan and Yang, 2019). Emissions from ruminants originate mainly from enteric fermentation, with about 87% of the rumen and 13% of the hindgut (Murray et al., 1976). Improving the efficiency of animal energy utilization has been found to have the potential to reduce CH4 emissions (Thornton and Herrero, 2010). Losses of CH4 typically ranged from 2% to 12% of gross energy intake (GEI) (Johnson and Johnson, 1995), depending on factors such as feed intake, feed composition, and animal species (Hook et al., 2010). McPhee et al. (2024) found that managing pasture and sheep numbers to increase stocking rate not only increased productivity but also reduced CH4 emissions. However, increasing stocking rate has also been found to decrease forage intake (Fenger et al., 2023) and lying time (Crossley et al., 2023) in grazing livestock thereby compromising animal welfare. Other studies have similarly demonstrated a negative relationship between stocking rate and animal production (McCarthy et al., 2011, 2014). Therefore, both the welfare and energy requirements of grazing animals should also be taken into account when determining appropriate stocking rates.

Few studies have quantified the effects of stocking rates on grazing behavior, nitrogen (N) and energy utilization, and GHG in cattle grazing saline meadows in arid regions. The objectives of this study were: 1) to determine the stocking rates that would increase N and energy utilization and decrease GHG emissions from cattle grazing saline meadow in arid regions without negatively affecting grazing behavior; and 2) to determine the energy requirements of grazing cattle at different stocking rates.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All procedures undertaken involving cattle during the experiment were approved by the Laboratory Animal Management and Welfare Committee of Lanzhou University (No. 2010-1 and 2010-2).

Study site

This study was conducted at the Linze Pratacultural Research Station of Lanzhou University from July to September 2020, located in the core area of the Heihe Oasis in the Hexi Corridor, Linze County, Zhangye, Gansu, China (100°3′25″E, 39°14′30″N, 1400 m a.s.l.). The study station is located in the inland arid region of Northwest China, and has a temperate continental climate with long and harsh winters, and short and hot summers. The mean annual temperature is 9.3 °C, with a mean annual precipitation of 112.9 mm and a mean annual potential evaporation of 1830.4 mm. More than 60% of the rainfall occurs between July and September. The predominant agricultural systems used throughout the study site are intensive, highly specialized crop and livestock production systems with saline soil (Hou et al., 2008).

Experimental design

A total of 24 male Simmental cattle (3-month-old, 104.72 ± 1.78 kg weight) were selected and randomly divided into 3 treatments (8 cattle/treatment). Based on long-term data for forage production, cattle body weight (BW), and feed intake, cattle were grazed in paddocks of 200 m × 200 m, 200 m × 100 m, and 200 m × 50 m at stocking rates of 0.66, 1.33, and 2.66 cattle/ha, respectively. The experiment adopted a rotational grazing system, with three paddocks as replicates for each stocking rate and lasted for 90 d. Each paddock was grazed for 10 d and rested for 20 d. Before grazing, forage samples were collected from 5 randomly allocated grid quadrats of 1 m × 1 m in the paddock, each containing 100 points. Vegetation was recorded at each point where the grid lines crossed. The total number of plants recorded was divided by 100 to give the relative cover of vegetation (Shi et al., 2026). All forage in the grid quadrats were collected with scissors at a height of 5-cm above the ground, weighed freshly, and dried in a forced air oven at 65 °C to constant weight for determination of dry matter (DM) content and other nutrient levels (Table 1). Nutrient levels of forage were determined before grazing in each paddock. During the grazing experiment, cattle were allowed to enter the paddock at 07:00 and returned to a shaded pen at 19:00, with free access to fresh water throughout the period. The initial and final BW were recorded to calculate the average daily gain (ADG).

Behavior recording

Individual grazing behavior of the herd was continuously observed and recorded by three trained observers for three consecutive days from 07:00 to 19:00 daily. Observers were required to familiarize themselves with the cattle at least a week in advance so as not to disturb the herd. Observations were made three times per h for 10 min each time to record the cattle’s feeding activities and rumination activities (Baumont et al., 2004). Feeding behaviors mainly included daily intake time (min/daylight), daily resting time (min/daylight), feeding rate (bites/min), foraging speed (steps/min), bites per step (bites/step). Ruminating behaviors mainly included daily ruminating time (min/daylight), rumination rate (times/min), chews per bolus (times/bolus), chewing time per bolus (s/bolus), and the interval between boluses (s) (Du et al., 2017). During observations, the behavioral state of the herd was determined based on the activity of at least 80% of the individual cattle within the grazing area.

Feed intake measurement

The dry matter intake (DMI) of cattle was determined according to the method described by Xie et al. (2023). Grazing cages were arranged in a “W” shape within each paddock. Forage samples were collected from inside and outside the cages using a 50 cm × 50 cm quadrat at a distance of 5-cm above the ground before and after grazing using the hand clipping procedure (Cook, 1964). The collection of forage before grazing was conducted in the next grazing paddock, while the collection of forage after grazing was carried out immediately after the cattle had left the same grazing paddock. Fresh forage was weighed and then oven-dried at 65 °C to a constant weight. Feed intake was estimated as the difference in forage mass before and after grazing (Haydock and Shaw, 1975). Organic matter intake (OMI), crude protein intake (CPI), ether extract intake (EEI), neutral detergent fiber intake (NDFI), and acid detergent fiber intake (ADFI) were determined by multiplying DMI by the corresponding nutrient concentrations. Nitrogen intake (NI) was calculated by dividing CPI by 6.25. Digestible dry matter intake (DDMI) and digestible organic matter intake (DOMI) were calculated according to the formulae described by Xie et al. (2020).

Digestion and metabolism

After 21 d of grazing, the cattle were moved into metabolic cages for a 5-d digestion and metabolism experiment. Feces and urine were collected daily using the total collection method. Ten percent of the total feces and urine collected daily was used as a representative mixed sample and placed at −20 °C for subsequent analysis. Additionally, fresh feces samples (approximately 100 g) and urine (1% of the total daily excretion) were collected daily, and 10% H2SO4 was added to fix N for the determination of fecal nitrogen (FN) and urinary nitrogen (UN). Daily feed supply and residual forage were also recorded. Following the metabolism trial, the cattle were randomly assigned to the indirect open-circuit respiration calorimeter chambers (LZUCKY-SDXCLZ-002, Institute of Grassland and Livestock Production System, Lanzhou University, Lanzhou, Gansu, China) for the measurement of GHG, with 4 cattle/batch, and at least one from each treatment group. The experiment was conducted over six consecutive 3-d gas collection periods. CH4 and CO2 emissions for each cattle were determined following the methods described by Zhao et al. (2015) and Xie et al. (2020). Nutrient digestibility, digestible nitrogen (DN), retention nitrogen (RN), GEI, digestible energy (DE), metabolizable energy (ME), heat production (HP), retention energy (RE), and CH4 energy (CH4-E) were calculated according to the procedures described by Xie et al. (2020, 2022).

Chemical analysis

All forage and feces samples were dried in a forced-air oven (DHG-9240A, Shanghai Jinghong Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 65 °C to constant weight and stored at room temperature for subsequent measurements. The dried samples were then ground to pass through a 1-mm sieve for nutrient analysis. The DM content was determined according to the method 934.01 of (AOAC, 1990). The organic matter (OM) content was calculated as 100% minus ash content (method 942.05, AOAC, 1990), where ash was determined by incinerating the samples at 550 °C for 6 h in a muffle furnace (SX-G03173, Tianjin Zhonghuan Electric Furnace Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China). The crude protein (CP) content was determined by measuring total nitrogen (N) via the Kjeldahl method (method 955.04, AOAC, 1990) with a UDK159 analyzer (VELP Scientifica S.r.l., Usmate, MB, Italy), and calculated as N × 6.25. The ether extract (EE) content was determined via diethyl ether extraction (method 920.39, AOAC, 1990) with an ANKOM XT15i extractor (Ankom Technology Corp., Macedon, NY, USA).The dried samples were then ground to pass through a 1-mm sieve for analysis of DM, organic matter (OM), crude protein (CP), and ether extract (EE) (AOAC, 1990; methods 934.01, 942.05, 955.04, and 920.39, respectively). The neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acid detergent fiber (ADF) were measured using Ankom F57 filter bags in an Ankom200 fiber analyzer (Ankom Technology Corp., Macedon, NY, USA) according to the procedure reported by Van Soest et al. (1991) with sodium sulfite and heat-stable amylase (A3306, Sigma Chemical Co., St. Louis, MO, USA). Gross energy (GE), fecal energy (FE), and urinary energy (UE) were analyzed with a calorimeter (6400, Parr Instrument Co., Monmouth, IL, USA).

Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS statistical software (v23.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to study the effect of stocking rate on the behavior, digestion, and metabolism of cattle. Duncan’s multiple comparison was used to analyze the significance of differences between treatments, where the level of difference was determined at P < 0.05, and a trend was considered at 0.05 < P < 0.10. Using stocking rate as a fixed factor, the general linear model in SPSS was used to analyze the relationship between N intake and excretion, energy intake and excretion. Similarly, 0.66 cattle/ha tended to spend longer in the interval between boluses than 2.66 cattle/ha (P = 0.072).

CO2 and CH4 emissions

The 1.33 cattle/ha had lower CO2 emission (P < 0.001), CO2 intensity (P < 0.001), CO2/DMI (P < 0.001), CH4 emissions (P = 0.002), CH4 intensity (P = 0.022), CH4-E/DE (P = 0.001), and CH4-E/ME (P = 0.001) compared with 0.66 cattle/ha (Table 7). The CH4/DMI (P < 0.001) and CH4/OMI (P = 0.003) were increased by 5.15% and 10.9% respectively, at 2.66 cattle/ha compared to 0.66 cattle/ha.

Discussion

Grazing behavior

Although there was forage competition between cattle with high stocking rates, it did not statistically affect daily intake time and feeding rate in this study. This contrasts with the findings of Crossley et al. (2017), who reported that dairy cows increased their daily intake time under conditions of higher levels of feed competition. The rotational grazing measures adopted in this study may have been effective in preserving the vegetative composition of the pasture and provided a buffer time for vegetative restoration (Augustine et al., 2023). Compared to 0.66 cattle/ha, 2.66 cattle/ha increased daily resting time, while there was no difference in daily intake time. This may be related to increased standing time (Tucker et al., 2021). Grazing animals at high stocking rates require longer movements across limited pastures to access more forage, with a consequent increase in standing time (Tucker et al., 2021). Studies exploring the motivation of animals to rest found that cattle prioritize resting over feeding when both activities were constrained in some way (Schütz et al., 2015, 2019; Tucker et al., 2018). Tucker et al. (2018) found that daily intake time decreased by 32% due to a compensatory increase in daily resting time in dairy cattle. Therefore, daily intake and resting times did not vary synchronously in this study. Additionally, the rumination rate and chews per bolus at 2.66 cattle/ha were lower than those at 0.66 cattle/ha, and there was also a tendency for the interval between boluses to decrease. This may reflect greater “work efficiency” (Jensen et al., 2004, 2005). For instance, dairy heifers completed the set number of panel presses at a faster rate to get more rest time (Jensen et al., 2004). Similarly, cattle at 2.66 cattle/ha may have ruminated and chewed faster to maximize their resting periods.

Feed intake and nutrient digestibility

Compared to 0.66, 2.66 cattle/ha had a 23% lower ADG, consistent with the results of a previous study (Coffey et al., 2017). This reduction may be attributed to lower nutrient intake associated with increased daily resting time in cattle with high stocking rates (Tucker et al., 2021). Compared with 0.66 cattle/ha, DMI reduced by 6% and 11%, respectively, and OMI reduced by 5.8% and 13.0%, respectively at 1.33 and 2.66 cattle/ha. The DMI decrease was likely due to increased competition for feed resources (Munksgaard et al., 2005). In addition, high stocking rates that did not exceed ecological thresholds can increase species richness, leading to an increase in the number of low-growing and poisonous species in the pasture community. This shift may reduce livestock feeding activity, thereby reducing DMI and OMI (Xiao et al., 2020). Nutrient digestibility is closely related to forage nutritional quality and rumen microbial composition (Cui et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2017). At low stocking rates, forage tends to have a simpler botanical composition, sufficient quantity, and excellent quality. In contrast, excessively high stocking rates reduce the nutritional quality, density, and height of the forage, resulting in a reduction in the selectivity of livestock to the forage (Török et al., 2018; Xiao et al., 2020). Therefore, with a relatively balanced variety and quantity of forage consumed by cattle at a medium stocking rate (1.33 cattle/ha), rumen microorganisms can better adapt to and make full utilization of the forage, thus improving the digestibility of DM and CP.

Nitrogen balance

The relatively low N utilization efficiency of ruminants causes a large amount of N loss to the environment, resulting in resource waste and pollution. The conversion efficiency of dairy cow dietary N into BW gain or milk N is only 14% and 27% (Huhtanen et al., 2010), while the remaining 70% to 80% of N is not absorbed by the organism and was excreted in feces and urine (Külling et al., 2001). In this study, there was no difference in NI among the three stocking rates, but the cattle stocked at 1.33 cattle/ha reduced FN and total-excreted N, indicating that more N was deposited in the body or converted into DN, thereby improving N utilization efficiency. This finding may be attributed to reduced NDFI and increased DM and CP digestibility (Baudracco et al., 2011). The decrease in vegetation coverage, grassland productivity, and forage quality caused by soil salinization led to a shortened grazing season, thereby reducing forage utilization (Cheng et al., 2018). Appropriate stocking rates accelerated the growth of forage, allowing livestock to choose high-quality forage, which may inhibit the deamination of certain soluble proteins in the rumen, reduce the decomposition of proteins by rumen microorganisms, and reduce the ammonia content in the rumen, allowing entry into N content of the kidneys to be reduced, thereby reducing UN excretion (Huang et al., 2023). In this study, cattle at 1.33 cattle/ha likely consumed relatively high-quality forage, which improved the rumen environment, reduced UN excretion and improved N utilization indices such as DN/NI, RN/NI, and biological value. Therefore, the adoption of a grazing system of 1.33 cattle/ha in saline meadow in arid areas can enhance the recycling of N from excreta to grassland ecosystems, provide essential nutrients for plant growth, promote N cycling, and support the sustainable development of both livestock production and grassland ecosystems.

Energy balance

The stocking rate at 1.33 cattle/ha reduced UE, allowing more energy to be retained in the body whereas the stocking rate at 0.66 cattle/ha increased FE, UE, and CH4-E, decreased RE, and reduced energy available for metabolism. Excessive intake of forage and protein by cattle with low stocking rates might have resulted in increased FN and total-excreted N, which in turn increased excretion of FE and UE. Energy utilization is closely related to selective feeding behavior, in particular, the energy required for muscle work during selective foraging activities in grazing animals has a huge impact on utilization (Gonçalves et al., 2024). Grazing animals at low stocking rates preferentially feed on higher-quality forage with high nutritional value, low fibrous tissue, and thin, soft leaves. As stocking rates increase, grazing animals are forced to select lower-quality forage due to the limited availability of higher-quality forage (Török et al., 2018). In this study, compared with 0.66 cattle/ha, the FE, UE, and CH4-E excretion of the stocking rate of 1.33 cattle/ha were decreased by 23.3%, 11.9%, and 9.8%, respectively, while RE increased by 38.7%. Surprisingly, no linear relationship was observed between energy utilization and stocking rate. Instead, a single-peaked distribution relationship was observed, with the highest energy utilization occurring at a stocking rate of 1.33 cattle/ha. This pattern may be attributed to the strong grassland type dependence of the effect of stocking rate on forage species and functional diversity (Török et al., 2018). Therefore, an appropriate stocking rate reduced the availability of higher-quality forage, increased the likelihood of selecting lower-quality forage, and reduced selective feeding (Török et al., 2018), thereby increasing energy utilization.

In this study, NEm and MEm were calculated using ME, and the results showed that NEm and MEm of Simmental cattle grazing saline meadow ranged from 0.340 to 0.346 MJ/kg BW0.75 per d, 0.410 to 0.416 MJ/kg BW0.75 per d, respectively. These values were slightly lower than the MEm ranges (0.439-0.468 MJ/kg BW0.75 per d) for beef cattle measured by indirect calorimetry, as summarized by Freetly et al. (2023). This discrepancy may be related to the physiological status of the cattle, the breed, and measurement methods, as MEm of non-pregnant, non-lactating beef cattle measured by multiple regression and comparative slaughter methods ranged from 0.381 to 0.799 MJ/kg BW0.75 per d (Freetly et al., 2023). Moreover, compared with 0.66 and 2.66 cattle/ha, the MEm of cattle at 1.33 cattle/ha was 0.98% and 2.40% lower, respectively, while Km was 1.08% and 0.96% higher. This may be attributed to the abundance of forage with higher nutritive value at medium stocking rate, which helped the livestock to obtain more energy and reduced MEm and increased Km value (Török et al., 2018). In the present study, Km was 0.830 to 0.839, which was in the normal range (<2.5%) for ruminants (NRC, 2016). The highest Km value of 1.33 cattle/ha indicates a more efficient conversion of MEm to NEm, which in combination with increased energy utilization, further suggests that a medium stocking rate provides more available energy for maintenance and production. The search for forage and digestibility of forage by grazing animals decreased the Km value (de Lima Júnior et al., 2023), which may also contribute to the decreased Km of 0.66 and 2.66 cattle/ha in the present study. Therefore, in actual production, factors such as weight, age, forage growth, and grazing behavior of grazing livestock should be considered while estimating NEm and MEm in combination with feeding standards.

Nitrogen and energy ingested, excreted, and used for digestive metabolism by cattle per unit of grassland area increased with increasing stocking rate. Cattle with high stocking rates may have poorer quality forage intake due to competition or reduced activity due to spatial constraints, thus affecting digestibility. In aggregate, N and energy used for digestive metabolism per unit of grassland area will still increase with increasing stocking rate due to increased intake, even if digestibility is reduced. Therefore, understanding the N and energy cycle of grazing cattle on unit grassland areas can help evaluate the carrying capacity of pastures, optimize forage utilization and environmental protection. Mathematical modeling is an effective approach for assessing the relationship between N and energy intake and excretion in grazing livestock. In this study, both N and energy intake showed a linear relationship with excretion. Xie et al. (2023) also found a linear relationship between NI and N excretion, GEI and energy excretion in grazing sheep. The differences in slope and intercept indicated that the cattle at 1.33 cattle/ha had better digestive capacity, leading to reduced N and energy losses via excreta and greater nutrient absorption and utilization. These results provide valuable insights for developing sustainable grazing management strategies and improving the ecological efficiency of livestock production systems.

Greenhouse gas emission

Compared to 0.66 and 2.66, 1.33 cattle/ha reduced CO2 emissions by 19.40% and 7.55% and CO2 intensity by 18.50% and 9.62%, respectively. CH4 emissions and intensity were reduced by 11.20% and 9.73%, respectively, for 1.33 cattle/ha compared to 0.66 cattle/ha, which may have resulted in lower forage maturity and inhibited CO2 and CH4 emissions due to the reduced production of acetate and hydrogen in the rumen fermentation process (Wang et al., 2016; Khan et al., 2017). Feed intake is the main factor influencing CH4 emissions, therefore CH4/DMI was used to assess the effectiveness of mitigating CH4 emissions (Beauchemin et al., 2022). Estimating CH4 emissions based on the actual digestion ratio of ingested feed (i.e., the actual digestion amount per kg OM) could effectively reflect changes in ruminal fermentation conditions (Beauchemin et al., 2022). In this study, the highest CH4/DMI and CH4/OMI were observed at the high stocking rate (2.66 cattle/ha), while the lowest were at 0.66 cattle/ha, possibly because grazing cattle were often forced to forage less favored plant species, causing a reduction in intake as the stocking rate increases (Wang et al., 2025), which was in line with the findings of Hammond et al. (2011), who observed a decrease in CH4 emission (g/d) with increasing DMI. Given the strong effect of forage nutrient intake (especially digestibility) on CH4 emissions (Woodmartin et al., 2025), lower NDFI and higher CP digestibility may have led to the lower CH4 emissions (g/d) observed in this study. The rumen of grazing animals with increased forage diversity contained lower Methanobrevibacter and abundant Methanosphaera, while increased Methanobrevibacter was associated with higher CH4 emissions (Tapio et al., 2017). The rotational grazing system in this study also increased forage species diversity at 1.33 and 2.66 cattle/ha, which may have altered rumen methanogenic bacterial populations in grazing cattle, thereby reducing CH4 emissions. Grazing sheep on sown pasture with an increased number of forage species increased the relative abundance of Prevotella (Woodmartin et al., 2025), which decreased GHG by enabling rapid degradation and full utilization of substrates in the rumen, compared to sown pasture with a single forage species (Niderkorn et al., 2017).

Theoretically, an increase in ruminal volatile fatty acid (VFA) concentration could potentially increase CH4 emissions (g/d) (Woodmartin et al., 2025). However, Robinson et al. (2010) found that VFA concentration was not a good predictor of daily CH4 emissions in grazing sheep, with DMI exerting a much stronger influence (Robinson et al., 2010). Therefore, nutrient intake was used in this study to predict GHG. CH4 and CO2 intensities increased by 0.016 to 0.019 and 0.390 to 0.692 g/kg BW0.75 per d, respectively, for every 1 g/kg BW0.75 per d increase in DMI as stocking rate increased from 0.66 to 2.66 cattle/ha. In contrast, the intensity of CH4 and CO2 increased by 0.942 and 0.848 g/kg BW0.75 per d without considering the stocking rate (R2 = 0.887 or 0.720). This suggested that variation in DMI due to competition for forage resources strongly influences GHG emissions. Furthermore, the accuracy of predicting CH4 and CO2 intensities with multiple nutrient intakes was higher than with a single nutrient, which may be related to nutrient utilization by rumen microbes (Woodmartin et al., 2025; Tapio et al., 2017). Therefore, modeling CH4 and CO2 emissions from grazing livestock in saline meadows using multiple nutrient intakes can improve prediction accuracy. The relationship between intake, excretion and these predictive equations provides useful information for the scientific literature and may provide an alternative method for predicting energy and N utilization and GHG emissions from grazing cattle in the absence of actual metabolic data.

The proportion of energy released from enteric CH4 to the GEI, that is, the CH4 emission rate, is a key indicator for assessing the efficiency of energy utilization and the intensity of GHG in ruminants (Zhang et al., 2018). The lower the proportion, the more efficient the energy utilization, and the greater the potential for decreasing GHG (Zhang et al., 2018). Although there was no significant difference in CH4-E/GEI in this study, 1.33 cattle/ha had decreased CH4-E/DE and CH4-E/ME. This may be attributed to increased DM and CP digestibility which could shorten forage retention time in the rumen and slow fermentation, resulting in less energy being used to produce CH4 and an increase in DE and ME (Pragna et al., 2018). This demonstrated that the prediction of CH4 emissions should not only focus on the effect of the diet on the apparent digestibility of the feed but should also consider the effect of the diet on rumen fermentation and the balance of hydrogen in the rumen (Bannink et al., 2011).

Conclusions

In arid region saline meadow grazing systems, a stocking rate of 1.33 cattle/ha did not adversely affect grazing behavior but decreased N and energy excretion, increased retention and utilization, increased Km, and also demonstrated some advantages in GHG emission reduction. Assessing nutrient intake to evaluate GHG emissions offered a scientific foundation for developing effective grazing systems and emission reduction strategies.
