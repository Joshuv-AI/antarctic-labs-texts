Bilateral medial medullary infarction: A report of two cases with a review of unique MRI signs
Authors: Abel Tsehay Abebe, Mohamed Tantawi, Nihanth Palivela, Azin Aein, John Heymann, Huda Al Jadiry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBilateral medial medullary infarction (BMMI) is a rare type of brainstem ischemic stroke involving the anteromedial medulla oblongata bilaterally. Diagnosis is often delayed due to its rarity, non-specific clinical presentation, and non-revealing imaging in the early phases. MRI plays a crucial role in early detection by revealing characteristic patterns. We report two cases of BMMI, highlighting classic and additional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings and their anatomic correlations.Keywords: Medial medullary infarction, Stroke, Heart sign, Snake’s eye sign, Fan sign, Air pod sign, Y sign

Introduction

Bilateral medial medullary infarction (BMMI) is an extremely rare yet clinically significant condition that arises from compromised vascular supply to the anteromedial medulla bilaterally, often due to atherosclerotic disease. The anteromedial medulla is divided into three segments: ventral, middle, and dorsal, each associated with distinct neural structures and functions [1]. A thorough understanding of these anatomical features is essential for accurately diagnosing BMMI through magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) MRI sequence is used in detecting BMMI. Several imaging characteristics, including the ''snake’s eye'' sign, ''fan'' sign, ''heart'' sign, and ''letter Y'' sign, have been documented in the literature. These signs correlate with the extent of infarction and specific clinical presentations. We present two cases of BMMI featuring classic imaging signs and explore the radiologic neuroanatomy of the anteromedial medulla, the imaging findings associated with BMMI, and common underlying vascular abnormalities, particularly atherosclerotic disease of the vertebral artery (VA). Understanding these factors is vital for enhancing diagnostic accuracy and optimizing patient management.

Case 1

A 55-year-old male with a past medical history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and coronary artery disease presented with acute-onset asymmetric bilateral lower extremity weakness and numbness, more pronounced on the right side, leaving him unable to rise from a seated position. Over the next 24 hours, his symptoms progressed to involve the upper extremities. He also developed mild dysphagia and dysarthria. He presented to medical care two days after symptom onset and was transferred from an outside hospital with a presumptive diagnosis of Guillain-Barré syndrome.

On arrival to our institution, vital signs were stable, with a blood pressure of 135/90 millimeters of mercury (mmHg). Neurologic examination revealed right predominant extremity weakness and numbness, with a suspected sensory level at C5. Nonspecific myelopathy or myelitis was clinically considered. Blood laboratory results were significant for hyperlipidemia, and high hemoglobin A1C indicating poor glycemic control (Table 1). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was negative for infectious or inflammatory etiologies.

Non-contrast head CT and cervical spine MRI were unremarkable. Brain MRI performed two days after symptom onset revealed diffusion restriction in the bilateral medial medulla, demonstrating the classic ''heart'' sign on DWI and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) sequences (Figs. 1A and B), consistent with acute or subacute BMMI. CT angiography of the head showed moderate to severe focal stenosis of the distal right intradural (V4) vertebral artery (Fig. 2). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no intracardiac thrombus.

Thrombolytic therapy was not administered as the patient was outside the therapeutic window. He was managed with supportive care, including blood pressure and glycemic control, speech therapy, and physiotherapy. The patient was discharged home without improvement after a 14-day hospital stay with a plan of outpatient rehabilitation follow-up.

Case 2

A 70-year-old female with a past medical history of hypertension and poorly controlled diabetes complicated by neuropathy experienced a sudden fall and was unable to get up. She initially reported subjective generalized extremity weakness which felt more like fatigue and went to bed. The following morning, she developed vertigo, right-sided facial numbness, bilateral upper and lower extremity weakness, and dysarthria, prompting evaluation at an outside hospital (OSH). On presentation, her blood pressure was 200/140 mmHg. A non-contrast head CT at OSH was unremarkable. She was treated for a hypertensive emergency with clonidine, hydralazine, and nicardipine. Due to worsening mental status deterioration, she was transferred to our institution.

Upon arrival, the patient became obtunded, developed a stridor, and required intubation for airway protection, followed by admission to the intensive care unit (ICU). The physical exam was notable for quadriparesis and horizontal nystagmus. Blood work results were notable for leukocytosis, hyperlipidemia, and poor glycemic control (Table 1). Urinalysis was positive for leukocyte esterase and nitrite suggesting urinary tract infection. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was unremarkable.

Given concern for hypertensive or infectious encephalopathy, she was started on antihypertensive regimen, a loading dose of aspirin, and empiric intravenous antibiotics. Repeat head CT remained unremarkable. Brain MRI, performed one day after symptom onset, demonstrated diffusion restriction in the bilateral medial medulla, showing the ''letter Y'' sign on DWI and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) sequences—findings consistent with acute or subacute BMMI (Fig. 3). CT angiography of the head revealed occlusion of the distal intradural (V4) segment of the left vertebral artery and severe multifocal stenosis of the basilar artery (Fig. 4). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no intracardiac thrombus.

CT angiogram of the head demonstrates occlusion of the distal left intradural vertebral artery (blue arrows), seen as thrombosis on the axial image (A) and an abrupt cut-off on the 3D reconstruction (B). Thrombolytic therapy was not administered, as the patient presented outside the therapeutic window. In accordance with the wishes of the patient and family, comfort care measures were initiated. The patient passed away two days after admission, following extubation at the family’s request, with the likely cause of death being respiratory failure secondary to the brainstem stroke.

Discussion

Medial medullary infarction is a rare form of stroke, accounting for only 0.5-1.5% of all stroke cases [[3], [4], [5]]. The largest study of MRI detected medial medullary infarction to date, conducted by Kim et al., [2] included 86 cases, of which 14% were bilateral. Bilateral medial medullary syndrome is exceptionally rare, as the medulla’s paired arterial supply usually ensures adequate perfusion [1].

The anteromedial medulla oblongata can be anatomically divided into three segments: the ventral, middle, and dorsal portions (Fig. 5). The ventral segment contains the pyramids, the middle segment houses the medial lemniscus, and the dorsal portion includes the medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF), the hypoglossal nerve nucleus, and vestibular nucleus [1,2]. Clinical manifestations correlate with the specific anatomical segments involved: infarction of the pyramids leads to motor deficits; extension into the medial lemniscus causes sensory disturbances; involvement of the MLF results in nystagmus; hypoglossal nucleus involvement leads to lingual palsy; and vestibular nucleus involvement presents with dizziness or vertigo [1,2]. Most of the corresponding specific clinical presentations were observed in both of our cases. However, dysphagia was present in Case 1; facial numbness in Case 2; and dysarthria in both cases. These findings may suggest ischemia extending into the lateral medulla [2].

The diagnosis of BMMI is clinically challenging, as illustrated by both of our cases, due to its complex presentation and symptom overlap with other neurological conditions. CT head imaging is often nonrevealing given the small size of the infarct and limited sensitivity of the CT in detecting brain stem infarcts. MRI plays a crucial role in the diagnosis of BMMI. According to Fukuoka et al. [5], 3 out of 7 cases of medial medullary infarction were diagnosed only on the second MRI. A statistically significant shorter interval from symptom onset to the initial MRI was observed in the MRI-negative group (3.6 ± 1.5 hours) compared to the MRI-positive group (11.1 ± 2.1 hours), suggesting a potential delay in detectability in the early stages . In contrast, both of our cases were diagnosed on initial MRI performed approximately 24 and 48 hours after symptom onset for cases 2 and 1, respectively.

Three characteristic signs on axial DWI MRI sequences have been described as diagnostic of BMMI, each correlating with the extent of medullary involvement [2] (Fig. 6). In a retrospective study, Hu et al. [1] reported the only known case to date demonstrating the ''fan'' sign, while the remaining cases exhibited the ``letter Y'' sign and the ''heart'' sign, in ascending order of frequency. An additional sign, the ''snake’s eye,'' has been described in a single case on axial T2-weighted MRI obtained nine months after clinical presentation [6]. Isolated involvement of the ventral portion of the anteromedial medulla gives rise to the ''snake’s eye'' sign[6] when the infarct is limited in extent (Fig. 6A), and the ''fan'' sign [1] when the affected area is more extensive (Fig. 6B). Involvement of both the ventral and middle segments results in the classic, pathognomonic ''heart'' sign [1] (Fig. 6C). When all three segments, ventral, middle, and dorsal, are affected, the ''letter Y'' sign, also known as the ''air pod'' sign, is observed [1,7] (Fig. 6D).

The anteromedial portion of the superior medulla is primarily supplied by perforator branches from the vertebral arteries (VAs) and basilar artery, while the inferior portion is predominantly supplied by perforator branches from the anterior spinal arteries [8]. The proposed anatomical variants of these perforator branches include supply from both VAs or from a single VA, which can provide blood to the medial medulla bilaterally [1]. Underlying vascular etiologies described in BMMI include large artery atherosclerotic disease involving the vertebral artery resulting in at least 50% luminal narrowing or occlusion, cardioembolic sources, small vessel (perforator branch) disease, arterial dissection, or a combination of any of the mechanisms [1,9,10]. Among these, unilateral intradural vertebral artery stenosis was the most frequently encountered according to Hu et al. [1]. Both of our cases exhibited significant atherosclerotic risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. CT angiography revealed moderate to severe unilateral intradural vertebral artery stenosis in Case 1 and occlusion in Case 2. Transthoracic echocardiograms were unremarkable, excluding cardioembolic sources.

Overall, BMMI is associated with poor clinical prognosis [11]. Although no studies to date have specifically investigated prognostic factors in BMMI, a study by Kim et al. [2] involving predominantly unilateral medial medullary infarctions identified older age and severe motor dysfunction at presentation as the only significant predictors of poor outcome, among various factors evaluated, including infarct size and extent . However, Hu et al. [1] proposed that more extensive infarctions, such as the ''heart'' sign and ''Y'' sign, may be associated with a poorer prognosis, as the ischemia could extend to involve bulbar and respiratory centers.

Conclusion

Bilateral medial medullary syndrome poses a significant diagnostic challenge due to its rarity, variable clinical presentation, and possibly unrevealing early imaging findings. A high index of clinical suspicion, combined with a detailed neurologic examination, is crucial to avoid delays in diagnosis and management. Characteristic MRI findings—most notably the classic ''heart'' sign, along with other described patterns—correlate with specific anatomic involvement within the anteromedial medulla and are essential for accurate diagnosis.
