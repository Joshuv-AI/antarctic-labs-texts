Multi-omics reveal phosphatidic acid phosphatases modify Niemann-Pick type C disease severity
Authors: Andrew B Munkacsi, Gabriella E Conway, Keri Multerer, Michael D Jackson, Lauren Csaki, Tamayanthi Rajakumar, Jeffrey P Sheridan, Eliatan Niktab, Wuh-Liang Hwu, Yin-Hsiu Chien, Mark Walterfang, Cintya Del Rio Hernandez, Robin B Chan, Bowen Zhou, Renu Nandakumar, Peixang Zhang, Gilbert Di Paolo, Robert A Maue, Karen Reue, Stephen L Sturley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryNiemann-Pick type C (NP-C) disease is a fatal, neurodegenerative disorder caused by lysosomal lipid accumulation with variable symptomatic penetrance at the primary disease locus encoded by the NPC1 gene. We identified genetic modifiers of disease progression by integrating genetic, genomic, and lipidomic analyses across yeast, mice, and human patients. A yeast screen identified 45 candidate modifiers of disease severity, including phosphatidic acid hydrolase (PAH1), a key enzyme in triacylglycerol (TAG) synthesis. Lipidomic profiling of liver, cerebral cortex, and cerebellum from Npc1−/− mice at multiple ages demonstrated that dysregulation of TAG metabolism strongly correlates with disease progression. Deletion of the murine PAH1 orthologues Lpin1 or Lpin2 in Npc1−/− mice reduced lifespan, accelerated Purkinje cell loss, and increased hepatic lipid accumulation. In NP-C patients, two LPIN3 variants were associated with early childhood onset. These findings identify lipins as modifiers of NP-C disease and expand our understanding of lipid metabolism in neurodegeneration.Subject Areas: Disease, Neurogenetics, Genomics, Lipidomics, Model organism

Introduction

The fundamental processes of lipid synthesis, transport, and storage are required for the optimal growth of all eukaryotic organisms ranging from yeast to humans.1,2,3 Accordingly, defective lipid metabolism is associated with numerous human syndromes both prevalent and rare, including cardiovascular,4 metabolic,5 and neurodegenerative6,7 diseases. Altered lipid homeostasis is often reflected in the lipidome, the product of the highly regulated crosstalk between genes, proteins, and lipids.8 The increasing application of lipidomic technology has revealed novel alterations and potential therapeutic targets for lipid-related diseases (e.g., cardiovascular) as well as neurodegenerative diseases indirectly associated with lipidosis, such as Alzheimer’s disease,6,9 Parkinson’s disease,10 and Huntington’s disease.11

Niemann-Pick type C (NP-C) disease is a fatal, lysosomal lipid storage disease that results in the accumulation of multiple major lipid classes in all tissues.12 Enigmatically, the pace and characteristics of progression of this rare autosomal recessive monogenic disease can be diverse. Hepatosplenomegaly is the dominant pathology in the viscera, while neurological symptoms include hypotonia, loss of motor skills, supranuclear gaze palsy, and ataxia due to Purkinje cell loss in the cerebellum, as well as seizures, dysphagia, dysarthria, and dementia associated with changes in other brain regions.13,14 The disease typically culminates in loss of life during adolescence, while neonatal onset of NP-C generally causes patients to succumb within 6 months of birth due to visceral organ failure.13 In rare instances, adult onset NP-C disease can present with only neurological disease, sometimes as late as the sixth decade.13,14 With the exception of loss-of-function mutations in the NPC1 and NPC2 genes that underlie 95% and 5% of NP-C disease cases, respectively, it is clear that genetic variation at these loci does not always predict disease severity, suggesting that genetic modifiers play a role in determining prognosis for affected individuals.13

The hallmark pathology of NP-C disease is the multi-tissue accumulation of unesterified cholesterol, sphingolipids (sphingomyelin and sphingosine), and glycosphingolipids (glucosylceramide, lactosylceramide [LacCer] and the gangliosides GM2 and GM3) within late endosomes/lysosomes (LE/L).14,15 In the majority of patients, cholesterol, and sphingomyelin accumulate predominantly in peripheral tissues, while glycosphingolipid storage predominates in the brain.13,14 Besides these classical storage lipids, other lipid classes (phosphatidylethanolamines, phosphatidylcholines, phosphatidylglycerols, ceramides, free fatty acids, triacylglycerol [TAG], and diacylglycerol [DAG]) have been found altered in NP-C patient plasma as well as Npc1−/− mouse liver tissue.16,17 Recently, the phospholipid bis(monoacylglycero)phosphate (BMP) has also been shown to dramatically increase in NP-C disease across tissues in LE/L; however, this may reflect a compensatory response, as BMP supplementation reduced cholesterol accumulation and other NP-C disease phenotypes.18,19

Developing therapeutic strategies for NP-C disease has progressed somewhat slowly,20 in part due to a historically “cholesterol-centric” treatment approach and an incomplete awareness of proteins and lipids associated directly or indirectly with NPC1 and the NPC1-dependent pathway trafficking cholesterol through the lysosome. Despite this, however, Npc1−/− mouse has been a valuable model organism in the development and testing of the candidate therapeutic compounds, particularly those that act via mechanisms independent of the activity of this pathway (e.g., 2-hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin). The yeast model of NP-C disease,21,22 a strain in which NCR1 (a functional and structural ortholog of human NPC1) is deleted (ncr1Δ), has also been useful in identifying candidate compounds. A genetic interaction study with this strain was instrumental in the identification of Vorinostat, a clinically approved anti-cancer histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor, as a therapeutic that ameliorates the lysosomal accumulation of cholesterol and sphingolipids in NP-C patient-derived human fibroblasts.23,24,25

To simultaneously investigate lipidomic and genetic modifiers of this pathway, we adopted an integrated genetic, genomic, and lipidomic approach exploiting the evolutionary conservation of lipid metabolism pathways between yeast, mice, and humans. An unbiased genome-wide screen in yeast identified a key component of TAG synthesis, phosphatidic acid (PA) hydrolase (PAH1), as a modifier of the NP-C pathway. We tested the prediction that TAG homeostasis is altered in Npc1−/− mice, and that the mammalian PAH1 orthologs, encoded by the lipin gene family, would influence the course of disease in mice and humans. Our findings reveal TAG homeostasis as a potential modifier of NP-C disease severity and suggest a novel pathway for therapeutic intervention in treating NP-C disease.

Results

Synthetic genetic array analysis reveals genes required for sphingolipid homeostasis in the yeast model of NP-C disease

In previous studies, the phenotypes of a dominant mutation in the sterol-sensing-domain of the yeast NCR1 gene led us to propose that the primordial function of NPC1 proteins is to recycle sphingolipids. We proposed that defects in this process in multicellular eukaryotes secondarily result in cholesterol accumulation,21 a hypothesis supported by the time course of subcellular lipid accumulation in cultured mammalian cells.26 However, an understanding of the genetic mechanisms underlying sphingolipid dysregulation in NP-C disease remains incomplete. To investigate genetic interactions mediating the sphingolipid component of NP-C disease, we conducted a genome-wide screen for conditional synthetic lethality with the NPC1 pathway using ncr1Δ yeast cells in which sphingolipid metabolism was further impaired by treatment with myriocin. Myriocin inhibits serine palmitoyltransferase, the rate-limiting step in sphingolipid biosynthesis in yeast and humans, thus impacting the bioavailability of sphingolipids as occurs in NP-C disease. Our aim was to identify genes and pathways that when aberrant exacerbate lethality of ncr1Δ strains (i.e., genes and pathways that in their normal state compensate for reduced Ncr1/NPC1 function) specifically in the presence of myriocin and thereby target the sphingolipid component of NP-C disease.

We exploited a unique characteristic of the yeast NP-C disease model: absence of a growth defect in the ncr1Δ deletion strain in a variety of environmental conditions including perturbed sterol and sphingolipid biosynthesis.21,23 Using synthetic genetic array (SGA) methodology,27 we generated haploid double mutants with deletions in NCR1 and additional deletion mutations in each of 4,800 nonessential genes. Consistent with previous results,23 no growth defect was detected in the double mutants under standard growth conditions. We treated each double mutant with 250 ng/mL myriocin, a concentration that did not inhibit the growth of WT or ncr1Δ cells. Consequently, we identified 45 genes required for viability of myriocin-treated yeast ncr1Δ cells (Figure 1; Table S1), where 34 of these 45 genes are conserved in humans and thus may act to modify NP-C disease.

Mutations in 12 yeast genes that conferred myriocin sensitivity in ncr1Δ also conferred anaerobic inviability of ncr1Δ (Table S1), consistent with our previous findings on the established role of sterol homeostasis in the NP-C pathway.23 This concordance highlights the intricate link between sterol and sphingolipid metabolism,29 as well as the often-inseparable accumulation of sterols and sphingolipids characteristic of NP-C disease. The converse to this observation is also critical in that 33 genetic interactions were unique to the myriocin treatment. Our myriocin SGA thus implicates these diverse processes in mediating or responding to the intracellular transport of sphingolipids in the yeast model of NP-C disease.

Network analysis reveals importance of phosphatidic acid hydrolase in the ncr1Δ network

Networks of interacting genes are functional entities that determine cellular phenotypes mediating human diseases.30 We thus mapped the 45 myriocin-specific yeast genes epistatic with NCR1 and their interacting genes within a comprehensive gene network derived from the entire yeast genome; this myriocin-specific ncr1Δ network comprised 797 nodes (genes) and 955 edges (interactions) (Figure 2A). The more central a gene is to a network, the more biological relevance it has to the phenotype.32,33,34,35,36 Three centrality measurements (“betweenness centrality” representing the shortest path length between two nodes, “closeness centrality” representing the shortest path length between one and all other nodes, and “degree centrality” representing the number of neighbors) were thus calculated for each gene (node) in the network (Figure S1; Table S2). The osmoregulating protein kinase HOG1 was ranked consistently high in all three centrality measurements, which is a proof-of-principle result that reflects the previously described importance of the HOG pathway in the response of yeast cells to impaired sphingolipid biosynthesis.37 Thirteen additional genes were identified as central to the network. Notably, YAF9 is a member of the NuA4 histone acetylase (HAT) complex, which was the complex that interacted with NCR1 in an anaerobic (sterol auxotrophy) condition23 that led to HDAC inhibitors being candidate therapeutic agents in NP-C patient cells,23,24,25,38,39,40,41 NP-C mice,42,43,44 and NP-C patients in a clinical trial. The prevalence of these genetic modifiers previously implicated in NP-C disease provides validation of our network analysis and demonstrates the myriocin-specific ncr1Δ network is representative of genes and pathways involved in this disorder.

Since subnetworks are modules (communities) of interacting genes that are tightly co-regulated to conduct specific biological functions in response to drug treatments and human diseases,30,32,33,34,35 community analysis was conducted to partition the NCR1 interactome into functional modules that are non-randomly interconnected. We distinguished 26 statistically significant modules (false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05) that permit yeast to tolerate the combined loss of the NP-C pathway (via NCR1-deficiency) and disrupted sphingolipid metabolism due to myriocin (Figure 2A; Table S3). Given the disrupted lipid metabolism of NPC disease, we prioritized glycerophospholipid metabolism enriched in module 20 (Figure 2B) for further study. This 12 gene unit includes the DGA1- and LRO1-encoded terminal acyltransferases required for TAG synthesis, and the diacylglycerol kinase DGK1 that converts DAG to PA. A key node of this module was the PAH1 gene encoding one of the most highly regulated enzymes in lipid metabolism in yeast, a PA hydrolase that catalyzes the dephosphorylation of PA to yield DAG, the immediate precursor of TAG.45 Notably, two genes that function in the same pathway, PAH1 and PHO80, were each epistatic with NCR1 (Figure 1) which would be consistent with PHO80 encoding a component of the Pho80-Pho85 cyclin-dependent kinase complex that phosphorylates and regulates the activity of PAH1.28 Interestingly, we previously identified PAH1 and PHO80 as modifiers of sterol metabolism in NCR1-deficient yeast23 and the mammalian ortholog of PHO80 (CDK5) is dysregulated in the murine model of NP-C disease.46

Lipidomic analysis identifies importance of triglycerides in NP-C disease progression

The exacerbated growth defect of the yeast model of NP-C disease due to loss of PA phosphatase (3-sn-phosphatidate phosphohydrolase, EC 3.1.3.4) activity (PAH1 deletion) and regulation (PHO80 deletion) prompted the hypothesis that variation in PA, diacylglycerol or TAG might be key components of NP-C disease. To assess these predictions, we characterized the developmental lipidomic profile of three different tissues in Npc1−/− mice. Specifically, given Npc1−/− mice exhibit symptoms of NP-C disease (weight loss, ataxia, tremor) by ∼postnatal day 40 (P40) followed by death at ∼P7047 we characterized the lipidome of the cerebral cortex, cerebellum, and liver of Npc1−/− mice at P30 (presymptomatic), P50 (symptomatic), and P70 (late-stage/preterminal), ages in line with those analyzed in a previous survey of sphingolipids in Npc1−/− mice.48 We identified and quantified 396 lipid species across 27 lipid subclasses (Table S4). Each lipid subclass was well represented, with 196 phospholipid, 91 sphingolipid, and 109 neutral lipid species (Figure S2), representing a diverse and comprehensive lipidome for investigating the progression of NP-C disease in the murine model.

Lipidomic profile changes in Npc1−/− mice were visualized as volcano plots, via a comparison of –log10 p value and log2 fold change relative to control animals (Figure 3). At all ages (P30, P50, and P70), there were multiple changes to the hepatic lipidome, including the predicted elevations in BMP and LacCer species.14,15 Impressively, 39 of 42 TAG species were decreased in the liver across all time points in Npc1−/− compared to Npc1+/+ (Figure S3). At P30 in the cortex, among the alterations were increased levels of dehydroxysphingomyelin (dhSM) and decreased levels of phosphatidylethanolamine phosphate (PEp). In addition to the anticipated increase in GM3 at P70 in the cortex,14,15 PA was increased, consistent with our findings that dysregulated PAP activity modifies the yeast NPC related pathway. In the P70 cerebellum, levels of TAG and PEp were reduced while BMP and glucosylceramide (GlcCer) were elevated, consistent with prior observations in Npc1−/− cells and tissues.14,15 Overall, the temporal changes in the Npc1−/− lipidome (dysregulation of multiple lipid species at P30 in the liver arising before numerous changes in cortex and cerebellum at P70) presents a strong association with disease progression in Npc1−/− mice, and is consistent with NP-C disease pathology, in that the liver is most often the first tissue to be affected.14

Deletion of the mammalian PAH1 orthologs exacerbates disease severity in Npc1−/− mice

Together the Npc1−/− associated chronological changes in murine TAG metabolism and the exacerbated viability of PAH1-NCR1 deficient yeast prompted us to hypothesize that genetically based deviations from optimal TAG synthesis would act as modifiers of NP-C disease. PA phosphatase plays a key role in TAG metabolism in all eukaryotes, and in mammals the PAH1 orthologs, known as lipins, are critical for lipid homeostasis.49 Additionally, variation in mammalian lipins influences the adverse effects of cholesterol-lowering statin drugs. Therefore, to extend our genetic findings in yeast to mammals we tested whether variation at Lpin gene loci are modifiers of disease in the Npc1−/− mouse, predicting that deletion of these PAH1 orthologous genes in mice would exacerbate NP-C disease severity.

In yeast, a single PAH1 gene is responsible for PA phosphatase (PAP) activity,45 whereas in mammals the three members of the Lpin gene family (Lpin1, Lpin2, and Lpin3) are responsible for this enzyme activity. The three corresponding lipin proteins all exhibit PAP enzyme activity, but only Lipin1 and Lipin2 are expressed at appreciable levels in mouse liver and brain, the key tissues affected in NP-C disease.50 Therefore, we crossed Npc1+/− mice with either Lpin1+/− mice or Lpin2+/− mice to generate Npc1−/− Lpin1−/− and Npc1−/− Lpin2−/− animals, and corresponding control genotypes (WT, Npc1−/−, and Lpin1−/− or Lpin2−/−).

Lipin-1 deficiency was highly detrimental to Npc1−/− mice. Viable Npc1−/− Lpin1−/− animals were generated at significantly less than the expected ratio, with only ten of an expected 18 live animals recovered from 299 births (p = 0.01, χ2 test for deviation from Mendelian ratio). The occurrence of lethality at a higher frequency than observed for single Npc1−/− mutants alone51 suggests that lipin-1 deficiency augments the early, developmental-associated lethality in Npc1−/− mutant mice.52 The ten Npc1−/− Lpin1−/− mice that were recovered all exhibited rapidly progressing weight loss and reduced mobility, and had a notably abbreviated lifespan (3–6 weeks) compared to Npc1−/− mice (10–12 weeks) and Lpin1−/− mice (>1.5 years) (Figure 4A). We examined Npc1−/− Lpin1−/− animals for evidence of neurological pathology that is characteristic of NP-C disease. Npc1−/− mice typically exhibit loss of cerebellar Purkinje cells, coincident with impaired motor function,53,54 with an almost complete absence of these neurons by ∼P70. We assessed the Purkinje cell layer in viable Npc1−/− Lpin1−/− and age-matched Npc1+/+ Lpin1+/+, Npc1−/−, and Lpin1−/− mice at P44. Notably, Npc1−/− Lpin1−/− animals had virtually no detectable Purkinje cells (Figure 4B) whereas all other genotypes had relatively normal cerebellar morphology. Likewise, Npc1−/− Lpin1−/− animals had increased hepatic lipid accumulation compared to all other genotypes (Figure 4C).

Lipin-2 is a significant contributor to PAP activity in tissues that are affected in NP-C disease, including cerebellum and liver, and Lpin2−/− mice develop ataxia as they age (onset at ∼7 months of age).55 We, therefore, tested whether lipin-2 might also act as a modifier of NP-C disease. Npc1−/− Lpin2−/− mice were born at the expected Mendelian ratio but had a shorter lifespan (6–9 weeks) than Npc1−/− animals (Figure 4A). In addition to slightly reducing the lifespan of Npc1−/− mice, lipin-2 deficiency also led to accelerated motor deficits, as assessed by a balance beam assay. In weekly assays between the ages of 4–9 weeks, we measured the time animals could remain on a balance beam up to a maximum period of 180 s (Figure S4). Consistent with literature,47 Npc1−/− mice exhibited a significant decline in their ability to remain on the beam at 7–8 weeks of age compared to Npc1+/+ Lpin1+/+ and Lpin2−/− mice. Notably, impaired balance was accelerated in Npc1−/− Lpin2−/− mice at 5–6 weeks, supporting our hypothesis that Lpin2 deletion exacerbates NP-C disease severity. Combined with the data from Npc1−/− Lpin1−/− mice, these results suggest that variation in the Lpin1 and Lipin2 genes act as NP-C disease modifiers.

Lipin-1 deficiency exacerbates macroautophagic defect in murine NP-C disease

Autophagy is a fundamental cellular process in which cytosolic proteins and organelles are engulfed by an autophagosome in the cytoplasm and degraded in the lysosome. Both NPC1 and lipin-1 influence autophagy, with deficiency in either protein leading to impaired autophagic flux; the autophagosome-specific microtubule-associated protein-1 light chain 3 (LC3-II) accumulates in tissues of Npc1−/− and Lpin1−/− mice as a result of impaired autophagosome-lysosome fusion and/or autophagosome degradation.56,57 We hypothesized that lipin-mediated exacerbation of NP-C disease may be associated with impaired autophagy beyond that observed in either NPC1 or lipin deficiencies. We quantified autophagy induction by examining levels of LC3-II in liver and cerebellum. LC3-II levels are influenced by both synthesis and degradation. As reported previously,56 we observed an increase in levels of LC3-II compared to the non-lipidated LC3-I form in liver and cerebellum of Npc1−/− mice (Figures 4D and 4E). Loss of both Npc1 and lipin-1 led to an increase in LC3-II in the liver and cerebellum, compared to the sole loss of either Npc1 or lipin-1 (Figure 4D). Similarly, loss of both Npc1 and lipin-2 led to an increase in LC3-II in the liver and cerebellum, respectively, compared to the loss of Npc1 or Lpin2 alone (Figure 4E). These data suggest that NPC1 and lipin genes may influence different stages of autophagic flux such that the combined deficiency has an additive effect on autophagosome regulation. More broadly, the additional impairment of cerebellar autophagy in Npc1−/− mice that occurs in the setting of lipin deficiency may contribute to accelerated disease course observed in the double mutant mice.

Single nucleotide variants in LPIN3 are associated with NP-C disease onset in humans

The conservation of metabolic pathways between species has allowed for the utilization of model organisms such as yeast and mice to predict the effect of mutations of genes within conserved pathways in humans. Furthermore, observed variation in NP-C disease pathology and lifespan in mouse models of NP-C disease with different genetic backgrounds suggests that there are genetic modifiers of NP-C severity.58 As such the reduced viability of PAH1-NCR1-deficient yeast, the murine lipidomic evidence of altered DAG and TAG metabolism, and the exacerbated disease severity in Npc1−/− Lpin1−/− mice led us to hypothesize that variation at Lipin loci in humans with NP-C disease may modify disease severity. To explore this hypothesis, we analyzed the three genes of the lipin protein family, Lpin1, Lpin2, and Lpin3, in 22 patients with NP-C disease. The range of neurological onset within the patient sample was broad, from early infantile (2 years) to adult (48 years) (Table S5), which allowed us to test for associations between identified lipin variants and age of neurological onset.14 While only the effects of LPIN1 and LPIN2 deletions were studied in our murine model of NP-C disease due to their significant expression in tissues most affected by the disorder, LPIN3 was included in our human genomic analysis due to observed interactions within the Lipin family. Previous studies showed that Lipin-3 interacts with Lipin-1 and Lipin-2 to influence PAP activity and/or lipid concentrations.59,60 Furthermore, in contrast to mice, in human liver it is Lipin-3, not Lipin-1, which exhibits the next highest level of expression after Lipin-2.50

Across the 22 patients, 14 unique variants of interest were identified across the three Lipin loci, of which ten occurred within exons and the remainder within splice regions (Table S6). Of the ten exon variants, two belonged to LPIN1, one to LPIN2, and seven to LPIN3. Visual inspection of genotypes for each patient showed a compelling pattern for LPIN3 variants rs2072969 (G > A, minor allele frequency in ALFA = 0.05) and rs12625565 (A >C, minor allele frequency in ALFA = 0.24), which result in p.(Leu687 = ) and p.(Gln680His) in the translated protein, respectively (Figures 5A and 5B).

For rs2072969, individuals carrying both A alleles of rs2072969 had early or late infantile onset (x¯ = 5.3 ± 3.4 years) while those carrying both G alleles had late juvenile to adult onset (x¯ = 23.2 ± 12.5 years) (Figure 5A). Comparison of age of onset between those with at least one major allele (GG and GA) and those with both minor alleles (AA) found a significant difference in age of onset (p = 4.97 × 10−5). Similarly, all individuals that carried both C alleles of rs12625565 had either early or late infantile onset of NP-C disease (x¯ = 5.8 ± 3.3 years), while most individuals carrying both A alleles had late juvenile to adult onset (x¯ = 26.0 ± 10.1 years). Statistical comparison of age of onset in all individuals carrying an A allele (AA and AC) and those carrying both C alleles (CC) found the difference in age of onset to be significant (p = 3.26 × 10−5). The minor C allele of rs12625565 is not rare in the general population, which suggests it may have no harmful effect for most healthy individuals. However, consistent with the phenomenon of epistasis, it may be that this variant is only impactful in combination with a deleterious mutation in NPC1.

To assess this pattern further, principal component analysis (PCA) of the effect of the 14 variants on age of onset was performed to separate individuals based on their genotypes for each variant. PC1 loading scores were obtained, where these can vary between −1 (negative correlation) and +1 (positive correlation), thus indicating the variants that contribute most to separation along the PC1 axis. Of all variants, the previously identified rs2072969 and rs12625565 had markedly higher PC1 loading scores, at 0.75 and 0.63, respectively; the other 12 variants had PC1 loading scores between −0.1 and 0.075 (Figure 5B; Table S5). These loading scores validate our observation that LPIN3 variants are associated with age of neurological onset of the disorder.

Discussion

The conservation of many aspects of lipid metabolism throughout eukaryotic evolution prompted us to pursue an integrated combination of genetic, genomic, and lipidomic analyses in yeast and mice to provide insight into human NP-C disease. The established, but essentially circumstantial, paradigm with respect to the mechanistic role of lipid dysregulation in NP-C disease focuses on lysosomal toxicity due to accumulation of cholesterol and other lipids. Our studies provoke an alternate possibility that the accumulation of certain lipids in NPC1-deficient cells may be cytoprotective. This concept is predicated by two key observations in our current work. Firstly, an extensive temporal lipidomic analysis of murine NP-C disease revealed that triglycerides and their metabolic precursors such as DAG accumulates as a reflection of disease progression. Secondly, a genetic screen in yeast that was extrapolated to the murine NP-C model indicated that loss and/or misregulation of a key component of DAG/TAG biosynthesis (PA hydrolysis), severely exacerbated NP-C disease. It is critical to note that this genetic interaction is reflected in the severely reduced viability associated with loss of both pathways in the yeast and murine models, where in the most extreme case many of the doubly compromised Npc1−/− Lpin1−/− animals do not survive embryogenesis.

Sterols participate in a molecular “hand-off” from NPC2 to NPC1 in mammals61 and from NPC2 to NCR1 in yeast.22 However, the precise functions of NPC1/NCR1 especially with regards to their interacting genes and the impact of these genetic interactions on sphingolipids and the rest of the lipidome are not well understood. Via the identification of 45 sphingolipid-specific genetic interactions with NCR1, we constructed an NCR1 interactome in yeast. We identified a single module (out of 26 modules) in the yeast model that was enriched in lipid metabolism, and experimentally validated the functionality of this module in mice. Module 20 depicts components of sterols and neutral lipid biosynthesis that highlight the connection between PAH1, PHO80 and NCR1 previously reported by our group.23 This conjunction of yeast NCR1 function with neutral lipid and PA metabolism has been implicated as a component of NP-C pathophysiology in studies beyond the yeast model. In the same manner by which the PHO80 kinase regulates PAH1 activity in yeast, the mammalian ortholog of PHO80, CDK5, is dysregulated in brain axons of mouse models of neurodegenerative diseases including NP-C and Alzheimer’s disease.46,62,63 The remaining modules from our pathway enrichment analysis identify several key aspects of membrane homeostasis including the autophagy/SNARE/endocytosis/mitophagy pathways (modules 2, 3, 5, 17, and 22) that have already been associated with NP-C disease in mice and humans,64,65,66,67 suggesting the orthologues of the yeast genes in these modules may also be epistatic with NPC1 and involved in NP-C disease. Our analysis also revealed pathways enriched in the network that have yet to be associated with NP-C disease, including the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway (module 23), cell cycle regulation (modules 9, 13, 19, and 21), lysine degradation (modules 5, 15, 22, and 23), longevity (module 22) and DNA mismatch repair (module 13).

NP-C disease has long been considered a disease of cholesterol accumulation. However, unbiased lipidomic analyses in the past decade have proven that the disease implicates a much broader swathe of the lipidome than cholesterol alone. It is plausible that the heterogeneity of the toxic metabolic stockpile, which may in part reflect “lipid hitchhiking” (e.g., the known affinity of sphingomyelin for cholesterol) promotes a varied progression of NP-C disease. Sphingolipids, phosphoinositides, and fatty acids have been shown to be significantly dysregulated in late-stage NP-C disease.14,15,16,17,48,68,69 Our study across three tissues and three stages of disease progression highlights the importance of also considering TAG species with respect to disease severity. The concurrence of genetic data in yeast with murine lipidomic data provided compelling evidence that NP-C disease is accompanied and perhaps worsened by dysregulated TAG metabolism that likely precedes the appearance of neurological symptoms. We observed the hallmark preponderance of multiple lipids, including cholesterol, sphingolipids and BMP; however, a PCA identified TAG as a major component of the differences between control and mutant animals that worsened with the time course of the disease. While the importance of TAG has been previously suggested in livers of Npc1−/− mice,17,70,71 here we show TAG metabolism (i.e., TAG as well as PA) is also dysregulated in the brain. Since the loss of either Lipin-1 or Lipin-2 compromised Npc1−/− mice, albeit to different magnitudes, we hypothesize that the PAP activity of the lipin proteins is a fundamental mechanism in modifying NP-C disease. The greater impact of Lipin-1 versus Lipin-2 could be a consequence of their relative levels of expression in tissues critical to NP-C pathology, particularly during embryogenesis.

The observation of naturally occurring variation in disease severity in humans with NP-C and consequent identification of LPIN3 variants associated with age of neurological onset of NP-C disease contributes to the accumulating evidence that TAG homeostasis has a significant role in NP-C disease severity, with PAP enzyme activity as a key mediator. It remains possible that additional LPIN3 variants or perhaps LPIN1/LPIN2 variants would also impact NP-C disease dynamics. The prediction of these lipin modifiers would be supported by the reduced viability of ncr1Δ pah1Δ yeast strains and exacerbated disease severity in our Npc1−/− Lpin1−/− and Npc1−/− Lpin2−/− murine models.

The precise mechanism by which loss of PA hydrolase activity exacerbates the viability of cells lacking the NP-C pathway likely reflects either the accumulation of PA or a deficiency in synthesis of DAG and its products. The metabolic pathways, both anabolic and catabolic, that contribute to the steady state levels of these lipids are complex. Since the lipin substrate PA regulates autophagic flux in mammalian cells72 and the yeast lipin (PAH1) regulates vacuole homeostasis,73 we determined if Lpin1 or Lpin2 deletion impacted autophagy in the Npc1−/−mouse. In line with previous studies of the Npc1−/− mouse,53,56 we identified an activation of autophagy in the liver and cerebellum prior to onset of neurological disease. In particular, LC3-II expression was upregulated in the Npc1−/− mouse compared to Npc1+/+ Lpin1+/+ mice. Intriguingly, the lipin-1 or lipin-2 deficiency exacerbated this autophagic defect.

Neurodegenerative conditions are rarely curable. Even monogenic neurodegenerative conditions, such as NP-C disease, where the biochemical and genetic etiology is relatively well defined, are tragically recalcitrant to therapy, probably because even monogenic diseases are complex in their interaction networks. Here we used the power of unbiased genomics and lipidomics to reveal novel therapeutic targets in yeast and mammalian models of NP-C disease. PA hydrolase (lipin) is the first fundamental lipid metabolism gene shown to modify NP-C disease severity across two taxonomically diverse models. In essence, paired deleterious variants in these gene-gene combinations (NCR1-PAH1 in yeast and NPC1-LPIN1/2 in mice) appear to recapitulate the variation in the severity, including age of neurological onset, of NP-C disease. We hypothesize that ameliorating phosphorylation of DAG to PA with an inhibitor of diacyglycerol kinase (DGK, e.g., R59022) might compensate the lipid deficiencies in the Npc1−/− mice and subsequently delay NP-C disease progression. It is also possible that our findings in the lipin pathway are a consequence of overcompensation for defects in the phospholipase D (PLD) pathway, the other major route of PA synthesis. Thus, a potential strategy to normalize PA levels could be to treat the Npc1−/− mice with inhibitors of PLD, which has been considered for other brain diseases.6 Finally, in the studies presented here, we exacerbated NP-C disease severity via lipin deletions, which suggests that lipin overexpression may be a potentially effective therapeutic strategy for human NP-C disease via an “exacerbate-reverse” strategy.23 Although NP-C disease is a rare disorder, the process of cholesterol and sphingolipid egress from the lysosome is a critical component of cellular lipid homeostasis in all eukaryotes. Ultimately, variation at the NPC1 locus is associated with multiple fundamental pathologies in medicine, including aging, neurodegeneration, obesity, and viral infections,20 making a complete understanding of NPC1 interactions integral to unraveling the role of dyslipidemia in a wide spectrum of disorders.

Limitations of the study

Prompted by a well-controlled genome wide-screen in yeast, our study provides compelling evidence that LIPIN-encoded PA hydrolases act as modifiers of NP-C disease severity. We did not resolve a mechanism by which members of this multigene family act independently or in combination to influence the severity of the disease. This likely reflects the relative tissue-specific contributions of each gene to PA hydrolase activity, a process that should be studied in greater depth. Our genotype-phenotype association study in humans was complicated by a small sample size inherent to any study of rare genetic disorders. Given the central roles of Npc1 and Lpin genes in lipid metabolism, examining lipid changes in Npc1−/− mice with altered Lpin expression, either reduced or overexpressed, would be an informative direction for future research.

Method details

Genome-wide conditional synthetic lethality analysis

Synthetic Genetic Array (SGA) analysis was performed using Saccharomyces cerevisiae as previously described.27 The query strain for the SGA analysis was Y3656 (MATα can1Δ::MFA1pr-HIS3-MFα1pr-LEU2 ura3Δ0 leu2Δ0 his3Δ1 met15Δ0 lys2Δ0; with the addition of ncr1::NATR that was introduced by PCR-mediated gene disruption. To validate interactions, we isolated haploids of double mutants and compared the growth of double mutants to the parental single mutants on agar in the presence and absence of myriocin. Colonies in 384-format were imaged in quadruplicate every 8 hours for 40 hours and colony sizes were determined using gitter.74 Growth functions were then estimated using the Gompertz model75 for each genotype and the Synthetic Viability score was calculated by subtracting definite integrals of the growth function of treated and control single parental mutant strains from the double mutant strain over the 40-hour interval. Annotations of yeast genes were derived from the online Saccharomyces Genome Database76 with orthologous human proteins identified using Alliance of Genome Resources.77

Gene network analysis

Genes required for normal viability of myriocin-treated yeast ncr1Δ cells were evaluated for enrichment within a functionally annotated genetic interaction network.78 Genes interacting with NCR1 were annotated for three measurements of network centrality as previously described.79 Seed genes and their interacting genes one path length apart were mapped in a first-order network within an established yeast interaction network, specifically the STRING interactome80 with a confidence cutoff of 900 and requirement for experimental evidence.

Animals

Npc1 and Lpin1 mutations were genotyped as previously described.81,82 Offspring were monitored for cannibalization of pups prior to being genotyped at P14 and monitored until death. Perfused tissue was collected at P44; Purkinje cells were visualized in the cerebellum and hepatocytes in the liver as previously described.44,55 In brief, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin blocks, and sections were stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin to reveal tissue morphology. Autophagy was monitored by comparing the relative levels of LC3-I and LC3-II as previously described.56 Proteins were extracted from cerebellum and liver samples, electrophoresed on NuPAGE Bis-tris gels, transferred to membranes and detected with an anti-LC3 antibody (Novus) and an anti-GAPDH antibody (GeneTex).

Lipid analyses

Lipids were extracted from frozen liver, cortex or cerebellum using a modified Bligh/Dyer extraction procedure as previously described.9 Tissue was homogenized in 2 mL pre-chilled chloroform:methanol (1:2), of which 650 μL was extracted with 300 μL chloroform and 200 μL 1M KCl, followed by a second extraction with 200 μL 1M KCl. The two organic layer phases were pooled, dried and stored at -80°C. The lipid extracts were spiked with internal standards and analyzed using an Agilent 1200 HPLC system coupled with an Applied Biosystems Sciex 3200 QTRAP triple quadrupole/linear ion trap mass spectrometer as previously described.9 Polar lipids (i.e. phospholipids and sphingolipids) were separated by normal phase HPLC using a Phenomenex Luna 3μ silica column (inner diameter 150 x 2.0 mm), mobile phase A (chloroform:methanol:ammonium hydroxide, 89.5:10:0.5) and mobile phase B (chloroform:methanol:ammonium hydroxide:water, 55:39:0.5:5.5) with a flow rate of 300 μL/min and the following: 5% mobile phase B for 3 min, then linearly changed to 30% mobile phase B over 24 min and maintained for 5 min, then linearly changed to 70% mobile phase B over 5 min and maintained for 7 min, and then the gradient was changed back to the original ratio over 5 min and maintained for 6 min for column re-equilibration. Neutral lipids (i.e. glycerolipids and sterols) were separated on an Agilent Zorbax XDB-C18 column (i.d. 4.6 × 100 mm) using an isocratic mobile-phase chloroform:methanol:0.1 M ammonium acetate (100:100:4) at a flow rate of 300 μL/min.83 The instrument capillary voltage, sheath gas flow rate, temperature and nebulizer pressure were set to 3000 V, 12 L/min, 300°C and 35 psi, respectively. Multiple reactions monitoring transitions were used for quantitative lipid analysis, with individual lipid species semi-quantified relative to spiked internal standards (Table S7). PC 14:0/14:0; PE 14:0/14:0; PS 14:0/14:0; PA 14:0/14:0; PG 15:0/15:0; BMP 14:0/14:0; LPC 17:0; LPE 14:0; SM d18:1/12:0; SM d18:0/12:0; Cer d18:1/17:0; GluCer d18:1/12:0; GalCer d18:1/12:0; LacCer d18:1/12:0; Sulf d18:1/17:0; TG 50:0 (d5); DG 4-methyl 16:0 diether; MG 17:0; CE 17:0; and Chol (d7) were obtained from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL). PI 16:0/16:0 and LPI 13:0 were obtained from Echelon Biosciences (Salt Lake City, UT). The concentration (number of moles) of each lipid was quantified as mol %, a value that represents the concentration of each lipid relative to the total lipid concentration, in order to correct for differences in total lipid amount between samples. The lipidomic profiles comparing -log10 p-value and log2 fold change between wild type and Npc1-/- mice were visualized in volcano plots using ggplot2.31

Human genome sequence analysis

Genomic DNA from venous blood was extracted and used for whole-genome sequencing using the Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-Free library preparation and Illumina HiSeq X Ten sequencer, generating 150 bp paired-end reads with a mean coverage of 30X for 95% of the genome at the Australian Genome Research Facility. Burrows-Wheeler Aligner84 was used to align reads against NCBI RefSeq GRCh38 genome assembly. Conversion of SAM to CRAM files and indexing was performed with SAMtools.85 DeepVariant86 was used to generate single-sample VCF files, variants that either didn’t pass RefCall or had a Phred-scaled quality score (QUAL) < 25 were removed using BCFtools for quality control. Variants that fell between genomic coordinates of Lpin1, Lpin2, and Lpin3 were extracted for annotation with Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP).87 For each variant, frequency in the 22 NP-C patients was calculated by the percentage of variant allele occurrence in sample ((No. heterozygous or homozygous variant genotype)/22) ∗ 100).

Principal component analysis of lipin variants

To assess separation of neurological age of onset by genotype we performed principal component analysis using the scikit-learn decomposition PCA library88 (version 1.2.0) which uses singular value decomposition (SVD) to project onto a lower dimensional space. Each patient was assigned a binary genotype (0=homozygous reference, 1=heterozygous, 2=homozygous variant) for each variant of interest. The default arguments were used in which LAPACK (Linear Algebra PACKage) was implemented with 80% of the variance explained with 3 components. Loadings for the variants were obtained by fitting a model with 3 components and transformed to calculate principal components for individuals. Loadings indicate how much each variant contributes to a particular principal component with larger loading scores indicating variants which contribute most to variability in neurological age of onset. A positive loading indicates that the variable contributes to the principal component, whilst a negative loading indicates that its absence contributes to the principal component. Loading score size for principal component 1 (PC1) for variants in Lipin-3 were visualized using trackViewer lollipopPlot.89

Quantification and statistical analysis

Growth of each yeast double mutant was quantitatively compared in quadruplicate after growth at 30°C for 2 d in the presence and absence of 250 ng/ml myriocin using ScreenMill.90 In brief, growth normalization was accomplished by dividing each colony size by the average growth on control media. The normalized growth of each quadruplicate was then averaged to quantify the growth of each strain. Differential growth in the yeast genome-wide analysis was quantitatively determined by using the log2 ratio between control and myriocin plates.

Degree centrality, closeness centrality and betweenness centrality values were calculated using NetworkAnalyzer.91 Functional modules (also referred to as subnetworks or communities) in the network were identified using the InfoMap algorithm92 implemented in NetworkAnalyst93 and statistically defined by their clustering significance (network connectivity) as per their significant recovery compared to random subnetworks using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Enrichment of biological pathways in modules was statistically evaluated (FDR <0.05) in Enrichr94 via comparisons with the KEGG pathway database. Pathway enrichment was statistically evaluated using an adjusted P-value with the Benjamini-Hochberg method for correction, a z-score reflecting the deviation of a Fisher’s exact test from an expected rank, and a combined score that is the product of the natural logarithm of the P-value multiplied by the z-score.
