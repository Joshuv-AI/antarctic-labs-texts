Factors influencing preference for short-term over long-term contraceptives among women in northern Uganda. A mixed-methods study
Authors: Betty Apio, James Okello, Denis Omiat, Andrew Odur, Humphrey Beja, James Acaye, Emmanuel Ekung, Edward Kumakech, Anna Grace Auma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionModern contraceptives have contributed to reduction of unintended pregnancy among the women of reproductive age (15 to 49) globally. Use of long-term reversible contraceptives is low (13%) in Uganda yet they are cost-effective. This study aimed to identify factors influencing preference of short-term reversible contraceptives over long-term methods among women of reproductive age attending the family planning clinic at Lira Regional Referral Hospital (LRRH).MethodWe performed a mixed-methods cross-sectional study, in which structured interviews were administered to 220 family planning users from December 2022-February 2023, data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 and cleaned prior to analysis. Descriptive statistical analyses were performed to summarize participant characteristics and contraceptive preferences, and results were presented using tables and figures. Frequencies, percentages, and cross-tabulations were computed.Bivariate analysis using binary logistic regression was conducted to assess associations between independent variables and preference for short-term versus long-term reversible contraceptive methods. Crude odds ratios (CORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Variables with a p-value ≤ 0.05 at bivariate analysis were included in a multivariate binary logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of contraceptive method preference. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% CI were calculated, and statistical significance was set at p-value ≤ 0.05.Qualitative data were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using applied thematic analysis. Atlas.ti version 23 was used to support data organization and coding. Analysis followed an iterative process involving open coding, development of summary memos, and construction of a structured codebook. Related codes were grouped into overarching domains, with sub-themes used to organize emerging patterns. Coded transcripts were reviewed and synthesized, and representative participant quotes were selected to provide contextual understanding of factors influencing women’s contraceptive method preferences. Discrepancies in coding and interpretation were resolved through consensus discussions among the research team.Ethical approval was obtained from Gulu Research and Ethics Committee (GUREC), and administrative clearance from Lira Regional Referral Hospital and Informed consent /assent from study participants.ResultsOf 220 women 63.6% preferred short term, 36.4% preferred long-term reversible contraceptive methods. Age between 25-34 years were 47.7%, 91.8% were married, and 70.5% attained primary education. Reasons for preference of short-term contraceptives were; less side effects 50%, protection for a short period of time 21.4%, confidentiality 28.6%. Partners approval (AOR: 0.253, 95% CI: 0.194-0.680, P=0.006), was significantly associated with short term preference. Providers cited confidentiality, quick return to fertility, and of methods availability as influence for short-term preference.ConclusionThis study found that women of reproductive age predominantly preferred short-term reversible contraceptive methods. Despite the effectiveness and cost-efficiency of long-acting contraceptives, their uptake remains low due to socio-cultural factors, misconceptions, and health system barriers. Strengthened client-centered counseling, consistent method availability, community education, and male engagement are essential to improve informed choice and increase uptake of long-acting reversible contraceptives.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04662-1.Keywords: Reversible contraceptives, Short term contraceptive, Myths and misconceptions, Contraceptive preference, Uganda

Introduction

Globally, approximately 77.6% of women of reproductive age (15 to 49 years) have their need for modern contraceptive methods satisfied [1]. However, utilization remains substantially lower in sub-Saharan Africa, where only about 58% of women use modern contraceptives [1]. In Uganda, modern contraceptive prevalence among women aged 15 to 49 years was estimated at 30–31% by 2020, with unmet need for family planning remaining high at 17–20%. During the 2018–2021 period, only about 60–63% of the total demand for modern contraception was satisfied [2–4].

Unmet need for contraception exposes women to unintended pregnancies, unsafe abortions, and sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, as some modern methods such as condoms also provide protection against disease transmission [5, 6]. Although Uganda demonstrated notable progress between 2016 and 2020 in increasing contraceptive use through policy commitment, community-based service delivery, and donor support under the FP2020 initiative [7]. uptake of long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) remains low. Nationally, only about 13% of women use LARCs, despite their high effectiveness, cost-efficiency, and long duration of protection ranging from three to ten years [5, 8, 9].

To address persistently high fertility and unmet need, the Government of Uganda, in partnership with development partners, set targets to reduce the annual population growth rate from 3.2% to 2.4% by 2040 and to lower unmet need for family planning from 17% to 15% [4, 10]. However, regional disparities persist. Northern Uganda continues to lag behind the national average, with modern contraceptive prevalence estimated at approximately 16% in the Lango sub-region and about 10% at LRRH [11].

Contraceptive use and preferences are shaped by a combination of individual, socio-cultural, and health system factors, including fertility intentions, partner influence, cultural norms, access to information, perceived side effects, and availability of preferred methods [5, 6, 12]. When women are unable to access their preferred methods or have concerns about side effects, non-use or discontinuation becomes more likely, increasing the risk of adverse reproductive health outcomes [5, 13, 14].

Contraceptive preferences vary widely across settings, with injectables, implants, pills, and condoms being dominant in different populations [14–16]. Method choice is influenced by attributes such as effectiveness, duration of action, ease of use, and side effects [5, 8, 14, 17]. LARCs, including intrauterine devices and implants, provide highly effective, long-term protection with minimal user dependence, whereas short-acting reversible contraceptives such as oral pills, condoms, vaginal rings, and injectable contraceptives require regular or repeated use and are more susceptible to typical-use failure due to adherence challenges [5, 14, 15, 18].

Women’s contraceptive choices are further influenced by socio-cultural and informational factors, including myths and misconceptions, prior experiences, partner approval, and fertility intentions [19]. Socio-demographic characteristics such as education level, parity, residence, and media exposure have also been associated with contraceptive use patterns [20]. Nonetheless, misconceptions and fears about health effects remain significant barriers to the uptake of modern contraceptives, particularly among young women [21].

Although several studies have examined contraceptive preferences in different contexts [22, 23], evidence from Northern Uganda, particularly at LRRH, remains limited. Understanding the factors influencing preference for short-term versus long-acting reversible contraceptives is essential for improving informed choice and contraceptive uptake. Therefore, this study aimed to assess factors influencing preference for short-term reversible contraceptives over long-acting methods among women of reproductive age and to explore healthcare providers’ perspectives at LRRH.

Methodology

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study using a convergent mixed method of data collection from December 2022 to February 2023 among women of reproductive age attending the family planning clinic and family planning providers at LRRH, located in Lira City in northern Uganda. LRRH is a public hospital located about 340 km by road from Kampala, the capital city of Uganda. The hospital has highly qualified service providers (doctors, midwives and nurses) and offers many health care services including family planning. About 4,800 women of reproductive age accessed family planning services at LRRH in 2021, averaging approximately 400 clients per month according to Health Management Information System (HMIS 2021). During the study period (December 2022 to February 2023), a total of 400 women attended the family planning clinic and constituted the sampling frame for this study. The healthcare providers were purposively selected as key informants for qualitative data collection because they have the expertise and in-depth understanding of the contraceptive methods they offer as they interact regularly with users.

Study population

The study population included women of reproductive age (15 to 49 years) attending family planning clinic and 12 family planning providers in family planning clinic in LRRH, lira city. The family planning providers are the experts who provide information to their clients and they receive feedback from them suitable qualitative participants.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Women of reproductive age (15 to 49 years) attending the family planning clinic of LRRH, Lira City, Northern Uganda.

Family planning providers at the LRRH family planning clinic.

Exclusion criteria

Women of reproductive age (15 to 49 years) attending the family planning clinic who declined to participate in the study.

The health care providers at LRRH who declined to be interviewed.

Healthcare workers on leave or off duty on data collection days.

Study variables and operational definitions

Dependent variable

The dependent variable was contraceptive method preference, defined as the type of contraceptive method the woman was currently using or preferred at the time of the interview. This variable was categorized into two groups:

Short-term contraceptive methods: Methods that require frequent user action or regular resupply and provide protection for a relatively short duration. These included injectable contraceptives (e.g., Depo-Provera), oral contraceptive pills, and male or female condoms.

Long-term contraceptive methods: Methods that provide extended protection against pregnancy for several years with minimal user intervention. These included intrauterine devices (IUDs) and contraceptive implants.

For quantitative analysis, contraceptive preference was coded as a binary outcome variable (short-term methods = 1, long-term methods = 0).

Independent variables

The independent variables were factors hypothesized to influence women’s preference for short-term over long-term contraceptive methods and were grouped as follows:

Socio-demographic variables

Socio-demographic variables

These included age, marital status, level of education, occupation, religion, and parity (number of living children), measured using self-reported responses.

Socio-cultural variables

Socio-cultural variables

These referred to social and cultural influences on contraceptive decision-making, including partner or spousal approval, cultural beliefs, religious beliefs, and community norms related to fertility and contraceptive use.

Client-related variables

Client-related variables

Perceived side effects: Women’s self-reported experience or fear of undesirable physical effects associated with contraceptive use, such as menstrual changes, weight gain, headaches, or reduced libido.

Desire for future fertility: Women’s intention to have more children in the future, measured as a yes/no response.

Perceived duration of protection: Women’s understanding or belief regarding how long a contraceptive method provides protection against pregnancy, categorized as short-term or long-term.

Confidentiality: Women’s perception that their contraceptive use could be kept private from partners, family members, or the community when accessing or using a particular method.

Service-related variables

Service-related variables

These included availability of contraceptive methods at the health facility, quality of counseling received, provider attitude, privacy during service provision, waiting time, and perceived cost of accessing family planning services.

Qualitative variables

In the qualitative component, variables were explored as emergent themes derived from in-depth interviews with family planning providers. These included providers’ perspectives on reasons for women’s preference for short-term methods, misconceptions about long-term methods, health system constraints, and client–provider interaction factors influencing contraceptive choice.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated using the Yamane formula for finite populations [24] at a 95% confidence level and a margin of error of 5%. The study used a target population of 400 women of reproductive age, which represented the actual number of clients who attended family planning services at Lira Regional Referral Hospital during the study period (December 2022 to February 2023). This figure is different from the annual attendance estimate used for planning purposes. Using the formula n = N / [1 + N(e²)], the minimum sample size was calculated as 200. After adding 10% to account for possible non-response, the final sample size was 220 women.

Twelve (12) family planning providers were purposively selected and interviewed. Data saturation was reached at the 10th interview, where no new themes were emerging. However, two additional interviews were conducted to confirm that saturation had been achieved and to ensure completeness of the data. No further interviews were conducted thereafter.

Sampling procedure

Systematic sampling was employed. The sampling interval was determined by dividing the total number of women attending the family planning clinic during the study period (N = 400) by the required sample size (n = 220), resulting in an interval of approximately 1.8, which was rounded down to 1. After selecting the first participant using a simple random (lottery) method, all subsequent eligible women were consecutively enrolled until the target sample size was reached.

Data collection tool and procedure

Quantitative data were collected using a pretested semi-structured interviewer-administered questionnaire. adapted from a previous study and modified to suit the aim of the study and the local context [25]. The qualitative data collection tool was designed by the researchers for this study. The data collection tools were initially developed in English and then translated into Lango, the indigenous language of the study participants.

Data was collected in both English and Lango depending on participant’s preference by four research assistants and one supervisor who was the study principal investigator. Data collectors explained to the participants the purpose of the study and how to complete the questionnaire after obtaining a written informed consent, validity and reliability of the data collection tool were calculated, and the validity index was 0.81. This was done by subject experts reviewing the questionnaire and tools. The data collection tools (the number of valid questions 31 out of 38) were realized. Data was collected on socio-demographic, socio-cultural, clients and service-related factors. The questionnaire was pre-tested on 10% of the total sample size (220) before the start of data collection, at Gulu Regional Referral Hospital located out of the study area and had similar results with the final data. Completed questionnaires were checked daily to ensure completeness and consistency.

Key informants interview guides were used to collect data from 12 family planning providers in English by two research assistants in a quiet environment. Interviews were audio recorded. Each interview took approximately 15 min to complete.

Data analysis

Participant questionnaires were checked for completeness, then data were entered into a computer using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and cleaned for analysis. Descriptive statistics were generated, and the results were presented in tables and figures. Frequency distributions and cross tabulations were calculated. Binary logistic regression was used to determine associations. The strength of the association was determined using odds ratio (OR), With confidence interval of 95%, P-value ≤ 0.05. Multivariate analysis was done for factors and a bivariate analysis with a P-value of ≤ 0.05.

Qualitative data were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using applied thematic analysis. Atlas.ti version 23 (ATLAS.ti Scientific Software Development GmbH, Berlin, Germany) was used to support data organization and coding. Analysis followed an iterative process involving open coding, development of summary memos, and construction of a structured codebook. Related codes were grouped into overarching domains, with sub-themes used to organize emerging patterns. Coded transcripts were reviewed and synthesized, and representative participant quotes were selected to provide contextual understanding of factors influencing women’s contraceptive method preferences. Discrepancies in coding and interpretation were resolved through consensus discussions among the research team.

Ethical approval and consent to participate in the study

This study was approved by Gulu University Research and Ethics Committee (GUREC-2022-318).

Administrative clearance to collect data was obtained from the Hospital Director of LRRH. The in-charge of the family planning clinic of LRRH was informed before the commencement of data collection. Participants were informed of the study by research assistants, written informed consent was then obtained from the study participants who accepted to participate in the study. To indicate consent, those who were not able to write gave a right thumb print as their signature. For those under 18, who came with their guardians were provided informed assent and consent. All participants were informed of their right to refuse to participate in the study or withdraw at any time without any penalty. The data provided by each and every participant was coded and kept confidential under lock and key.

Results

We conducted a convergent mixed method study, merging both quantitative and qualitative result at discussions. Quantitative study wing had 220 participants who were family planning users with response rate of 100%. We also interviewed 12 health care providers as key informants to explore the factors influencing preference of short-term reversible contraceptives over long-term contraceptive methods.

Quantitative result

Socio-demographic characteristics of respondents

A total of 220 women of reproductive age participated in the study. The mean age of the respondents was approximately 28.0 years (SD = 7.0), with an estimated age range of 16–49 years. Nearly half of the respondents, 105 (47.7%), were aged 25–34 years, while 77 (35.0%) were aged 16–24 years and 38 (17.3%) were older than 34 years. A large majority of the respondents were married or cohabiting (91.8%, n = 202). Seven in every ten (70.5%, n = 155) had completed primary education, more than half were employed (52.5%, n = 115), about two-thirds were of the Anglican faith (67.4%, n = 147), and over half (58.2%, n = 128) had at most two children. Contraceptive preferences

Most of the respondents 62.3% prefers injectable contraceptives, 28.2% preferred the implant, and 8.2% preferred an Intrauterine device (IUD) as a contraceptive method (Fig. 1).

Preference of different contraceptives methods among women of reproductive age in Lira, northern Uganda, December 2022 to February 2023 (N = 220)

Overall, nearly two-thirds of respondents (140/220; 63.6%) preferred short-term reversible contraceptive methods (injectables, oral pills, and condoms), while just over one-third (80/220; 36.4%) preferred long-term reversible methods (implants and intrauterine devices).

Reasons for preference of contraceptive methods

Among the 140 participants who preferred short-term contraceptive methods, 50.0% preferred them because of fewer side effects, 21.4% because of the shorter duration of protection, and 28.6% because of confidentiality (Fig. 2).

Reasons for contraceptive preference of short-term reversible contraceptives among women of reproductive age in Lira, northern Uganda December 2022 to February 2023 (N = 220)

Association between socio-demographic characteristics, economic status and preference of short term over long term reversible contraceptive methods

Bivariate analysis showed no statistically significant association between socio-demographic characteristics (age, marital status, education level, parity, religion, employment status) or economic status (income) and preference for short-term versus long-term reversible contraceptive methods (p > 0.05 for all).

Bivariate analysis of Social and cultural factors affecting contraceptive choice

At the bivariate level, partner-related factors were significantly associated with contraceptive method preference. Women who reported that contraceptive decisions were made jointly with their partner had higher odds of preferring short-term contraceptive methods compared with those who made the decision alone (COR = 1.93; 95% CI: 1.18–2.41; p = 0.041).

In contrast, women whose partners approved the use of contraceptives or family planning methods were less likely to prefer short-term methods compared with those whose partners did not approve (COR = 0.28; 95% CI: 0.12–0.66; p = 0.004), indicating a greater likelihood of long-term method preference in this group.

Other social and cultural factors, including prior discussion with a partner about contraceptive methods and religious beliefs against contraceptive use, were not significantly associated with contraceptive method preference (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Qualitative results from providers

Twelve (12) health care providers were interviewed as key informants to explore the factors influencing preference of short-term reversible contraceptives over long-term contraceptive methods. The qualitative interview data indicated that peer influence played a major role in contraceptive choice, and providers did not commonly observe side effects as a reason for preference of short-term methods.

During the key informant interviews, probes were used to explore whether health care providers attempted to address misconceptions related to long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs), including misconceptions about duration of use, peer influence, and prevailing myths. Providers were asked follow-up questions on how they respond when women express fears or misinformation about LARCs, the type of counselling provided, and whether corrective information influenced method choice.

Although providers were generally aware of common misconceptions and peer-driven misinformation, most reported that women often arrived at facilities with pre-formed preferences for short-term methods, strongly influenced by peers, partners, family members, and religious beliefs. Providers indicated that counselling to address myths was routinely provided; however, these efforts were frequently constrained by time limitations, staff shortages, and client insistence on preferred methods. As a result, counselling aimed at correcting misconceptions about LARC duration and safety did not always translate into uptake of long-term methods.

Factors influencing contraceptive methods preference

Confidentiality

Health providers noted in our interviews that some women prefer short term methods because of privacy. They explained that some women say they are hiding from their spouses, who do not want them to use any modern family planning methods as quoted below:

“… It’s confidential, no one can tell that you are using any method of family planning” (Participant 1, health care provider).

“…most of them prefer short term some of their husbands don’t allow them to use modern family planning method so they think if they inject themselves their husband will not know they have the method unlike for the implant where it can be seen from the body.” (Participant 4, health care provider).

“…Women go for short term in hiding because they do not want other church members to know that she is using what is against the bible.” (Participant 11, health care provider).

Easy to discontinue

Providers observed that short-term contraceptive methods allow women to discontinue easily and return to fertility at any time they wish. The participating providers mentioned that women love to rest for a short time then return to fertility as supported by the quotes below:

“Women in early reproductive age prefer short term methods because they want to rest for a short time and conceive quickly.” (Participant 9, health care provider).

“Short term is preferred because the mothers will not take long before conceiving….” (Participant 12, health care provider).

“And then also some of them want to conceive after a short duration of time like 2 years compared to long term like Jadelle that can take 5 years and IUD that takes over 10 years.” (Participant 6, health care provider).

During the interviews, providers were further probed to clarify whether women were informed that LARC methods can be removed at any time after insertion, regardless of their maximum duration of use. Most providers reported that they routinely explained the option of early removal during counselling sessions. However, they indicated that this information did not substantially alter women’s choices, as many clients remained concerned about delayed return to fertility, feared side effects, or were influenced by peers, partners, and family members. As a result, even when informed about the reversibility of LARCs, many women continued to opt for short-term contraceptive methods.

Peer influence

Some women presented to the clinic with preconceived preferences for specific family planning methods, largely shaped by information and recommendations from peers and friends. Health providers reported that peer experiences particularly positive testimonials strongly motivated women’s initial method choices, especially for injectable contraceptives.

“Some clients come when they already have what methods in mind, they heard from their peers have told them about e.g. most of them come for injectable because their peers are using” (Participant 10, health care provider)

“Some clients are influenced to take a method by peers, so they go straight to get contraceptives especially injectable” (Participant 2, health care provider).

During the interviews, providers were probed to explore whether women who presented with preconceived contraceptive choices were still counselled on the full range of available methods. Most providers reported that standard practice involved comprehensive counselling on all contraceptive options before service provision. However, they noted that this approach rarely altered women’s initial choices, as peer influence, religious beliefs, and partner or family expectations strongly shaped decision-making. In addition, high client load and limited time for counselling further reduced opportunities to influence method choice beyond the client’s original preference.

Against the bible/religion

Religion was found to influence women’s choice of contraceptive methods. The providers who were interviewed explained how the opinions of both Christian and Muslim religious leaders influence the use of contraception among their religious communities, as demonstrated by the quotes below:

“Some denominations don’t promote family planning especially modern methods, some say it killing babies so women prefer short term for confidentiality because church members will not know” (Participant 5, health care provider).

“Catholics says FP is killing for example condoms” (Participant 7, health care provider).

“Catholic Church prefer natural family planning methods; therefore, women tend to use methods that cannot be seen like short term” (Participant 8, health care provider).

Family demand for children

In the qualitative interviews, providers reported that mothers-in-law do not support use modern contraceptive methods because they want many children for their sons which made their daughter-laws to use contraceptives silently as illustrated by the quotes below:

“Mother in-laws don’t want their daughter in laws to use FP method because they want many grandchildren so because of these daughters in law prefer short term because they are not seen” (Participant 5, health care provider).

“Lack of male involvement, mother in-laws want very many grandchildren so they don’t support their daughters’ in-laws to use FP methods so short term is preferred for confidentiality” (Participant 10, health care provider).

Some providers explained that the male partners consider family planning a woman’s issue and didn’t take part in family planning as seen by men not participating in family planning activities. This is backed by participants’ quotes below:

“Lack of male involvement, …… men say that family planning is for women, they say there is no family planning method for men so why would they go for it” (Participant 8, health care provider).

“Men do not come with women because they think they have no business with family planning since it is prepared for women only” (Participant 1, health care provider).

Number/availability of health workers

Participating providers reported that staff shortages and lack of interest in providing other contraceptive methods were factors affecting whether a provider gave a woman her contraceptive method of choice. As noted in the quotes below:

“Health workers are few, and sometimes only one available and she is to give services to over 10 clients she would rather give short term method to save time for herself and clients as well” (Participant 6, health care provider).

“Yes, at times it is true because if you get this health worker who does not want to explore other methods and also has other clients along the line, so the one who come for short term she would prefer to give first. Depends also on health worker because short term does not require a lot of preparation compared to long term” (Participant 5, health care provider).

Availability of short-term methods

Providers mentioned the availability of short-term contraceptive methods and stock out of long-term contraceptives, as factors affecting the choices of contraceptive methods. As highlighted in the quotes below

“The availability of short-term methods also contributes to preference because it’s always available and eligible client cannot go back without a method” (Participant 4, health care provider).

“It is rare that you find short term method out of stock. It’s always available everywhere even clinics and most of them start from somewhere and when they come to the clinic, they say the methods they have been on …” (Participant 5, health care provider).

“Short term is always available compared to long term and this makes it possible for every woman who comes to the clinic to go home with a short-term method” (Participant 6, health care provider).

During the interviews, providers were further probed to determine how they managed situations where long-acting reversible contraceptives were out of stock. Providers reported that stockouts most commonly affected implants and intrauterine devices. When such stockouts occurred, women were counselled on alternative available methods, most often short-term contraceptives, to ensure that they did not leave the facility without protection. Although some providers reported referring women to nearby or higher-level facilities where LARCs were available, referrals were not consistently utilized due to challenges such as distance, transport costs, and client preference to receive services at the point of care. Providers indicated that these stockouts occurred intermittently, but even temporary unavailability of LARCs influenced women’s choices toward short-term methods.

Myths and misconceptions

Myths and misconceptions influence contraceptive method preference and these findings are backed by comments from qualitative interviews with service providers, as quoted below:

“Some women believe that long term methods like IUD disappears in the uterus and causes infertility and cancer and implants goes to the heart and causes death” (Participant 11, health care provider).

“…. and also, there is a belief that after implant and IUD staying on their bodies for long, they cause the uterus to be redundant even after conceiving the uterus fail to contract resulting to difficult delivery” (Participant 5, health care provider).

During the interviews, providers were further probed to explore how they responded to myths and misconceptions regarding long-acting reversible contraceptives. Most providers reported that they addressed these misconceptions through routine counselling, explaining the safety, effectiveness, and reversibility of implants and intrauterine devices. However, providers indicated that correcting misinformation did not consistently influence women’s contraceptive choices, as deeply entrenched beliefs, peer influence, and partner or family expectations often persisted. As a result, many women continued to opt for short-term methods despite receiving corrective information.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative findings

In this convergent mixed-methods study, quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated to examine factors influencing women’s preference for short-term over long-term contraceptive methods. Both data strands consistently showed that short-term methods particularly injectables were preferred by most women. Confidentiality and partner dynamics emerged as key convergent factors, with quantitative associations supported by qualitative explanations of covert use, limited male involvement, and family opposition. Complementarity was observed regarding fertility intentions, as qualitative findings expanded on quantitative results by highlighting women’s desire for quick return to fertility. Divergence was noted in perceptions of side effects, which were frequently cited by women but less emphasized by providers. While quantitative analysis found no significant service-related associations, qualitative findings revealed health system constraints influencing method choice. Integration of findings indicates that women’s contraceptive preferences are shaped by intersecting individual, social, cultural, and health system factors.

Discussion

This study examined the factors influencing preference for short-term reversible modern contraceptive methods among women of reproductive age (15–49 years) attending the family planning clinic at LRRH in northern Uganda. Both the quantitative and qualitative findings aligned, revealing that socio-cultural, client-related, and health service factors significantly influenced contraceptive choice.

The majority of participants (63.6%) preferred short-term reversible contraceptives, mainly injectables, followed by oral contraceptive pills, emergency pills, and condoms. Long-term reversible contraceptive (LARC) methods were less preferred (36.4%), despite being more efficacious, cost-effective, and independent of user adherence. LARCs also promote better child spacing and reduce pill burden, which could help address the unmet need for contraception in low-resource settings [26].

The reasons most cited for preferring short-term methods included fewer perceived side effects, confidentiality, and limited duration of protection. These findings are consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia, where injectables were also the most preferred methods due to perceived safety and minimal side effects [27], and in Guinea, where adolescents and young women preferred short-term methods because of perceived absence of side effects [28] However, a study in Germany reported contraceptive pills and condoms as the most preferred methods reflecting how socio-cultural and contextual differences shape contraceptive choices [29].

Interviews with service providers in the current study provided additional insights. Providers reported that clients often select methods based on availability at the clinic. When LARCs are out of stock, even eligible women are forced to opt for short-term alternatives. This finding underscores the need to strengthen supply chains and ensure consistent availability of all modern contraceptive methods to support individualized and informed choice [30].

Confidentiality emerged as a major determinant of short-term method preference. Many women reported using contraceptives privately to avoid disapproval from partners or family members. This finding aligns with studies from Ethiopia, which showed that women preferred injectables because they could be used discreetly without the partner’s knowledge [31]. Similarly, perceived or experienced side effects were major deterrents to the adoption of certain methods. Systematic reviews have reported that women’s negative experiences or fears of side effects significantly influence contraceptive discontinuation and method switching [32, 33]. In contrast, a study from Northwest Ethiopia reported higher preference for long-term methods, especially implants, among women who had adequate knowledge and counseling about their use [34]. This highlights the importance of providing comprehensive and accurate contraceptive education to women of reproductive age.

Partner approval was found to significantly influence the preference for short-term contraceptive methods in this study. This may be due to men’s preference for early return to fertility and greater familiarity with short-term methods. Similar findings were reported in the Democratic Republic of Congo, where women’s perceptions of their partner’s approval were strongly associated with modern contraceptive use, highlighting the importance of partner support and couple communication in method choice and uptake [35]. Likewise, a Ugandan study conducted in Kampala found that male approval was associated with higher short-term method use, emphasizing the need to strengthen male involvement in family planning initiatives [36]. These results were also echoed by provider interviews, which identified partner influence as a key determinant of contraceptive choice.

While the logistic regression analysis showed no significant association between service-related factors and contraceptive preference, providers in the qualitative interviews reported periodic stockouts of long-term methods. These qualitative insights reflect perceived service delivery challenges but do not provide statistical evidence that stockouts account for the preference for short-term methods. Similar study found that uptake of long-acting reversible contraception in rural south-western Uganda was hindered by health system barriers including insufficient trained providers and limited access to LARC methods, among other factors [37], . A study in Burundi also identified gaps in provider skills and shortages of trained personnel as barriers to LARC provision [38]. Strengthening supply systems and investing in provider training on long-term contraceptive methods could therefore enhance access and utilization.

This study also uncovered widespread myths and misconceptions surrounding long-term reversible methods. Participants reported beliefs that implants and intrauterine devices could disappear in the body, cause infertility, congenital abnormalities, excessive bleeding, cervical cancer, divorce, and complicated labor or delivery. These misconceptions reveal a substantial knowledge gap among women and communities. Similar myths have been documented in Kenya, where respondents associated modern contraceptives with infertility, birth defects, promiscuity, and reduced sexual freedom [21]. In Nigeria, men perceived condoms as reducing sexual pleasure, which may explain their low preference among participants in the present study [39]. Such beliefs perpetuate fear and misinformation, thereby limiting uptake of effective methods.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The mixed-methods design enabled an in-depth exploration of factors influencing contraceptive method preference, allowing triangulation of quantitative and qualitative findings. The quantitative data provided key associations, while the qualitative interviews with healthcare providers offered additional insights and information power regarding service provision and factors influencing method choice. The sample size was adequate to identify some important factors.

The study was conducted at a single health facility and data were collected at one point in time, which may have limited statistical power and generalizability.

The cross-sectional nature of the study also restricts the ability to capture changes in contraceptive preferences over time or establish causal relationships. Conducting research across multiple health facilities, including urban and rural settings, would improve representativeness and enhance applicability to broader populations.

In addition, the qualitative component was limited to healthcare providers and did not include male partners, community members, or women not currently using family planning services, which may have restricted the range of perspectives on socio-cultural influences and decision-making processes. Future studies should involve larger and more diverse samples and extend qualitative data collection to these groups to obtain a more comprehensive understanding. Although myths and misconceptions were also identified through provider interviews, the study did not formally assess how providers’ counselling responses influenced women’s contraceptive decision making. This limits our ability to draw conclusions about the effectiveness of counselling in overcoming misconceptions and shaping contraceptive method choice.

The study may also have been influenced by social desirability and recall bias, as participants may have provided responses they perceived as acceptable to the researchers or healthcare providers. To minimize these biases, participants were assured of confidentiality, interviews were conducted in private settings, and questions focused on experiences relevant at the time of the interview.

Conclusion

The study found that women of reproductive age predominantly preferred short-term reversible contraceptive methods over long-acting reversible contraceptives, influenced by perceptions of fewer side effects, confidentiality, quicker return to fertility, partner approval, and prevailing myths and misconceptions. Uptake of long-acting methods remained low and was further affected by health facility factors, particularly stockouts, as well as peer influence and misinformation. Strengthening client-centered contraceptive counseling, ensuring consistent availability of all methods especially long-acting options and promoting male partner involvement are essential to support informed contraceptive choice and improve utilization of long-acting reversible contraceptives.

Implication statement

The preference for short-term reversible contraceptive methods among women of reproductive age highlights critical gaps in awareness, access, and acceptance of LARC. This highlights the need for targeted, evidence-based contraceptive counselling that emphasizes the safety, efficacy, and long-term benefits of LARC. Male partners involvement in contraceptive decision-making processes could enhance support for LARC uptake and promote shared responsibility contributing to improved contraceptive outcomes and reduced rates of unintended pregnancies.
