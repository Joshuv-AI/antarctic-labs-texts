Knowledge, practices, and perception of predatory journals among North-African Dental University Hospital Doctors
Authors: Abir Mannai, Islam Khadmi, Ibtissam Grira, Mehdi Khemiss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPredatory journals are referred to illegitimate journals that threaten scientific integrity by publishing low-quality research. This study aimed to assess the knowledge, practices, and perceptions regarding these journals among Dental University Hospital Doctors (DUHD) in Tunisia.Material and methodsA cross-sectional and descriptive study was conducted from September 1 to 26, 2025, at the Faculty of Dental Medicine, University of Monastir (Tunisia). All DUHD, including academic staff and residents from various dental specialties, were invited to complete the validated Predatory Journals Knowledge and Practices Assessment Questionnaire via Google Forms. DUHD were not given a time limit for completing the questionnaire but they received standard follow-up emails reminder after receiving their original invitation to participate.ResultsAmong 339 potential participants, 178 (77% were women) completed the questionnaire. Over half (53.9%) reported no prior training on predatory journals. While more than 50% of DUHD correctly identified predatory journals in most questions, residents demonstrated lower knowledge than academic staff. Regarding practices, 52.2% had never submitted to predatory journals and 57.3% had never published in them. However, only 16.9% reported checking blacklists before submission. Most respondents perceived predatory journals as deceptive (74.7%) and believed they mainly attract academics seeking rapid promotion (75.9%).ConclusionAlthough DUHD showed relatively good awareness of predatory journals, significant gaps persist, especially among residents. Targeted training programs and institutional policies are needed to prevent unintentional submissions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-08895-4.Keywords: Ethics, Predatory journals, Scientific misconduct, Tunisia

Introduction

“Predatory journals are exploitative academic publications that charge authors fees without providing legitimate peer review, adequate editorial oversight or rigorous quality control” [1]. Several terms, such as “illegitimate journals”, “deceptive journals”, “dark journals”, and “journals operating in bad faith”, have been proposed to designate this type of journal [2]. The word “predatory” has been used in different ways with several related meanings [1]. For instance, the term “predatory publishing” was introduced by Jeffrey Beall in 2012 [3]. It gained a formal definition only in 2019, when an international consensus group described it as journals that often prioritize self-interest over scholarship integrity [4]. In fact, predatory journals are characterized by dishonesty, insufficient of transparency, poor quality, a primary focus on profit, invalid or non-existent peer review, false indexing claims and the publishing of low-quality or unverified research [1, 4]. For these reasons, predatory journals are often regarded as fake or trash journals [5]. They represent a serious threat to scientific integrity since they undermine researchers’ efforts, damage the credibility of scientific findings, and hinder the advancement of the entire research community [6]. It is difficult to determine their exact number, but in 2021, the estimate exceeded 15.000, and this number is expected to be significantly higher by 2025 [7]. The rise of predatory journals is not only caused by unethical publishers’ deceitful practices but also worsened by the involvement of some researchers whether knowingly or unknowingly.

Tackling this problem is not just the duty of publishers or regulators; above all, it requires ethical awareness and careful judgment from individual researchers [7]. It seems that there are differences between specialties to explain why scientists publish in predatory journal. Dentistry, as in other medical disciplines has not been immune to these trends [8]. Since the critical importance of evidence-based practice in dentistry, the spread of low-quality studies published by predatory journals can cause clinical uncertainty and potentially harmful outcomes [8]. In order to maintain high standards of scientific rigor, it is crucial to assess the knowledge, practices and perception of predatory journals among Dental University Hospital Doctors (DUHD) as well as the strategies used by them to recognize and avoid such journals. While some researchers deliberately take advantage of these journals, others may unknowingly become victims. Regardless that, it is essential that DUHD acknowledge and take responsibility for their part in combating this challenge [6]. Therefore, effective interventions as well as appropriate policies will be taken to prevent future submissions in this type of journals by researchers.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the level of awareness of DUHD in Tunisia regarding predatory journals, as well as to investigate the methods they employ to identify and steer clear of these deceptive publications.

Material and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Faculty of Dental Medicine of Monastir (FDMM), University of Monastir (Tunisia) during the academic year 2025/2026. Data collection took place over a three-week period between September 1, 2025 and September 26, 2025.

Study was led in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki and its revision. Permission from the Research Ethics Committee of the FDMM was obtained (Approval number 2025038). Participants had no direct benefits. Data were anonymized and stored securely. Details regarding the research was provided to the participants, ensuring their understanding of the study objectives. Informed consent to participate in the present study was indicated by the voluntary completion and the return of the questionnaire.

Study population

The study targeted to include all DUHD enrolled at the FDMM during the 2025–2026 academic year across various Hospital-University establishments in Tunisia. For the specific academic year under investigation, the total number of academic staff (Professors, Associate Professors, and Assistants) and dental residents (Postgraduate students currently perusing their specialty training) were 159 and 180, respectively.

The study sample included all the DUHD in Prosthodontics (Fixed or Removable Prosthodontics), Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics, Periodontics, Pediatric Dentistry, Orthodontics, Oral Surgery, and Basic Sciences.

Participants with incomplete questionnaire responses were excluded.

Applied questionnaire

The “Predatory Journals KP Assessment Questionnaire” was used [10]. It included 46 questions, divided into four sections. The first focused on gathering general characteristics of the participants, including age (in years); sex (i.e. ; male/female); discipline such as Basic Sciences, Oral Surgery, Endodontics, Prosthodontics, Orthodontics, Pedodontics, and Periodontology; current job (i.e. ; differentiating academic staff and residents); PhD degree (i.e. ; yes, no); prior training about predatory journals (i.e. ; yes, no); number of published articles during the three last years; number of published articles during the three last years as a corresponding author; and reasons for publishing in predatory journals (aspirations toward promotion, incentive allowance payment linked to number of publications, fear of job loss and the “publish-or-perish” pressure, failure to publish in indexed journals, inadequate research skills, competition among colleagues, desire to score higher and gain respect, lack of awareness, and institutional pressures). The second section of the questionnaire is related to the knowledge towards predatory journals. It comprises 13 questions. Each one could be answered by “Yes”, “No”, or “Not sure”. The third one featured on the practices towards predatory journals. It comprises 16 questions. Each question provided four answer choices (i.e.; always, often, rarely, never). The last one explored the perception towards predatory journals with nine questions. Each one uses a five-point Likert scale, from “totally disagree” to “totally agree”.

The “Google Forms” application was used to prepare the used questionnaire. A pilot test involving 10 DUHD randomly selected indicated no need for any adjustments to it. Academic staff were invited to participate to the study through institutional e-mails and departmental outreach. DUHD emails were obtained from the FDMM. An email with online link was sent to participants by the first author (AM in the authors’ list) using a standardized recruitment script.

DUHD were not given a time limit for completing the questionnaire but they received a standard follow-up emails reminder one week and two weeks after the original invitation to participate. When the number of participants reached 180, the analysis of data began.

Statistical analysis

All mathematical computations as well as statistical procedures were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (version 26). Significance was set at the 0.05 level. Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality test was used in order to assess the normal distribution of numerical variables. Variables without a normal distribution were expressed as median [Minimum-Maximum]. Categorical variables were expressed in term of frequencies and percentages. The Chi-Square test was used to examine relationships between categorical variables. The Mann-Whitney test was used to compare ranks.

Results

Online link was sent to 339 potential participants. A total of 180 DUHD responded to the questionnaire. Two were excluded for incomplete responses giving a response rate of 52.5%. The final sample size was equal to 178 (Fig. 1).

Perception towards predatory journals

Table 4 provides the perception of the DUHD toward predatory journal categorized by their objective level of agreement. DUHD agree that predatory journals deceive inexperienced authors (74.7%) and attract frustrated academics wanting speedy promotions (75.9%). That’s why, knowing about predatory journals is currently a priority for 62.9% of them. Discussion

In the present study, the level of awareness of Tunisian DUHD about predatory journals was investigated and the methods they employ to identify and avoid them were assessed. To the best of authors’ knowledge, this is the first study conducted in North Africa. The most striking finding of our results was that the majority of DUHD correctly identify predatory journals.

Discussion of the results

More than 50% of DUHD correctly identified predatory journals. However, a difference between the academic staff and the residents was observed. The latter showed a reduced ability to identify characteristics of this type of journals. Our finding was in accordance with a previous study examining author profiles for predatory journals comprising medical students and physicians [11]. A lack of knowledge and awareness of predatory publishing practices among medical students and trainees was observed compared to physicians. It was also reported that researchers that publish in predatory journals tend to be young, inexperienced, and originating from developing countries [11]. Resident are considered as postgraduate students in the Tunisian system. They demonstrated lower awareness and knowledge regarding predatory journals. This percentage was higher than that reported in a previous study conducted among Jordanian researchers [12]. In fact, 34% of them reported a high ability to identify predatory journals, while 27% reported a low ability to identify them. An Indian study revealed that 40% of participants across different academic disciplines and positions to an online survey did not recognize predatory journals [13]. The most alarming finding came from Latin American medical students. Only 30% of students could identify characteristics of a predatory journal [14]. This may confirm the fact that researchers engaged in clinical practice rather than academia are not aware of predatory journals [15, 16]. Nevertheless, Richtig et al. [17] found that predatory journals were known by 70% of German and Austrian residents in oncology. However, only 27.7% of them identified Beall’s list. This finding was also observed in the present study; only 16.9% and 20.8% checked “blacklist” and “whitelist” of predatory Publisher before submitting their manuscript, respectively. In fact, it is crucial to be aware of the Directory of Open Access Journals, the Journal Citation Reports, the Open Access Scholarly Publishers Association, the Beall’s list, the Think-Check-Submit initiative, and/or any other platform to assess target journal credibility. The APC’s predatory journals were also unclear for the majority of the participants. It has been noted that predatory journals often reveal only the existence of APC’s after the acceptance of the article [18]. The lack of transparency in how predatory journals present their publishing fees makes comparison with other OA and traditional journals difficult [19].

Geographically, authors who published in predatory journals are based primarily in Asia and Africa [11, 20]. However, a majority of the Tunisian DUHD never submit in predatory journals. Among the 97 who recognized having published in a predatory journal, they reported the lack of awareness and the failure to publish in indexed journals as main reasons. This finding is in contrast with the previous studies [13, 21, 22]. For instance, 70% of Indian authors reported having pressure to publish and 80% of Canadian general surgery residents reported “keeping the program director happy” as the main reason to conduct research [22]. In addition, a study conducted among anesthesiology residents showed that participants’ reason to publish was the program requirements and the career advancement [21]. Since there is a growing concern that the motivation to publish is related to academic advancement [23], it is crucial to consider how to incorporate curricular requirements that facilitate scientific research without posing undue publication pressure on trainees [24].

Discussion of the methodology

This study aimed to describe the awareness of a defined population (DUHD) about predatory journals at one particular time, without regard to any causal or other hypotheses. The primary advantage of descriptive studies lies in its ability to make generalizations and predictions about a population based on sample data. Furthermore, information collected may help generate hypotheses which can then be verified using more complex design. Nevertheless, measurement must be obtained using an appropriate tool [25]. For this purpose, the “Predatory Journals KP Assessment Questionnaire” was used. It is a valid and reliable questionnaire to assess and measure the researcher’s knowledge and perceptions of predatory journals in the clinical and health disciplines [10]. Unlike all the previous instruments, based on interviews or self-reporting questionnaires, it is the first valid, reliable and verified measurement tool that serves a guide to assess researcher’s knowledge and perceptions about predatory journals. It was developed, confirmed and validated by 304 registered researchers identified by the institutional review board list at King Fahad Medical City in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The construct validity and reliability were established using exploratory factor analysis with principal axis factoring and varimax rotation as well as confirmatory factor analysis for perception construct [10].

The development process of this Questionnaire involved three main steps: item generation through theoretical framework development and extensive literature review; validity testing, in terms of face, content, and construct validity for perceptions as well as knowledge, by a panel of experts in the area of medical and clinical research from different countries and institutions, possessing knowledge and experience in research and publishing ethics; reliability testing using two methods including internal consistency and test-retest reliability in terms of Cronbach’s alpha, Kuder-Richardson, item-to-total correlations, corrected item-to-total correlations, and Cronbach’s alpha if the item was deleted [10].

Recommendations

The existence of predatory journals is more and more increasing. This constitutes a challenge for researchers to publish their work in indexed journals that uphold rigorous editorial standards and ethical practices. However, by taking some actions, it is possible to address predatory journals and prevent future submissions in this type of journals. This likely requires additional inquiry but addressing these challenges involve resources. Institutional funders should indicate their interest in funding investigations into identifying predatory journals and strategies for recognizing reputable publishers. We also recommend funders to give more serious consideration to develop relevant policy that control and regulate research quality and impose a must peer review process in predatory journals.

The results of this study highlight the importance of implementing strategies to prevent publishing in predatory journals. The following recommendations can be considered:

Organizing research program addressed to authors to raise their awareness regarding the existence of predatory publishers and train them in the principles and practice of research [1, 26];

Limitations

A major limitation was related to the survey’ language. This is because English is not the first language for respondents, limiting responsiveness or leading to response error especially for professors and late carrier seniors. This may explain the low response rate from both associate professors and professors and reduce the generalizability of our study. In addition, the length of the survey, the nature and the content of the questions as well as the term “Predatory journals” may induce cognitive bias influencing the quality of the results. Finally, the analysis was predominantly descriptive and bivariate. That is due to the design of the questionnaire used which did not provide a validated global score that could be used for multiple linear regression. Moreover, there was no single general binary outcome suitable for binary logistic regression. Future studies applying multivariate methods, using different languages, and including smart questions that minimize or avoid cognitive bias are recommended.

Conclusion

Assessing knowledge, practices, and perception among Tunisian DUHD is critical in order to be able to develop effective interventions to prevent future submissions. Although they showed relatively good awareness of predatory journals, significant gaps persist, especially among residents. Targeted training programs and institutional policies are urgently needed to enhance knowledge and prevent unintentional submissions, thereby safeguarding the quality and credibility of dental research in Tunisia.
