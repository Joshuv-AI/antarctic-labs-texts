Psychological wellbeing and occupational stress among public secondary school teachers in Beni Municipality, Nepal: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Pujan Dhungana, Aayush Joshi, Anisha Chalise, Shishir Paudel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe teaching profession, while rewarding, presents numerous challenges that can adversely affect teachers’ psychological well-being and occupational stress. This cross-sectional study aimed to assess the psychological well-being and occupational stress among secondary school teachers in Beni Municipality, Nepal, and identify its associated factors.MethodsA total of 231 secondary school teachers were included through a census approach. Data were collected via face-to-face interviews using structured questionnaires, including the General Health Questionnaire-12 (GHQ-12) and Teacher Stress Scale (TSS). Bivariate analyses and multivariable logistic regression were performed to identify factors associated with psychological well-being and stress at a 5% level of significance.ResultsThe study found that 60.2% of the teachers experienced psychological distress, while 51.1% reported occupational stress. Factors significantly associated with better psychological well-being included designated teaching level at the secondary level (aOR = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.15–4.56), satisfaction with job description and responsibilities (aOR = 2.37, 95% CI: 1.33–4.24), and occupational stress (aOR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.16–3.78). In regard to occupational stress, teachers aged 50 years and above (aOR = 3.04, 95% CI: 1.15–8.01), dissatisfaction with salary (aOR = 2.78, 95% CI: 1.10–7.02), and perceiving their job as highly demanding (aOR = 6.50, 95% CI: 2.14–19.76) were significantly associated with higher levels of occupational stress.ConclusionThis study reveals that a significant proportion of secondary school teachers in public schools of Nepal experience psychological distress and occupational stress, with key factors including job satisfaction, salary dissatisfaction, and perceived job demands associated with them. The findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to improve teachers’ well-being, such as improving salary structures, clarifying job roles, offering professional development, providing access to mental health support, and fostering a supportive school environment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24677-x.

Introduction

The teaching profession is one of the most rewarding yet challenging professions. Teachers play a crucial role that shaping young minds. However, this responsibility often comes with a higher level of occupational stress. Occupational stress can be defined as the negative mental and physical response that results from an imbalance between job demands and the degree of control a person has over meeting these demands [1, 2]. Psychological well-being, on the other hand, refers to a person’s emotional and mental health, encompassing their overall happiness, life satisfaction, and ability to manage both positive and negative emotions [3].

Teachers’ stress is characterized by the manifestation of negative emotions such as anxiety, frustration, burnout, and disappointment, which can arise from the demanding nature of their roles [4]. In addition to teaching, educators of today must keep up with new technologies, interact with parents, and manage complex classroom dynamics, all of which can make teaching a particularly stressful occupation [5–7]. Evidence suggests that there are multiple contributing factors leading to occupational stress and psychological distress among teachers, including high job demands, low job control and autonomy, insufficient support from colleagues and supervisors, low procedural justice, and effort-reward imbalance [5, 8–11]. These stressors can significantly lower motivation, induce feelings of alienation, and contribute to high turnover rates within the profession [8, 12]. In this context, the psychological well-being of the teachers is crucial not only for their fulfillment but also for their professional effectiveness, as it directly impacts their ability to foster students’ learning and development [13, 14].

Globally, teachers’ mental health has emerged as a significant public health concern, transcending national economics and geography. In the Global West, in countries like the UK, USA, and Canada, teachers are found to experience high levels of stress, anxiety, burnout, and depression, more frequently than the general population [6, 15–18]. In Eastern Europe, 44.6% of teachers report high perceived stress, whereas in Ireland, the prevalence of work-related stress among teachers was reported to be 45.0% [19, 20]. The burden of stress among teachers is also significant In Africa. Studies from Ethiopia show a prevalence rate of 58.2%, while in Libya, 40.5% of the teachers report experiencing work-related stress [21, 22]. In the Global South, countries like India, Japan, and Nigeria report similar levels of psychological distress among teachers, with prevalence rates ranging from 39.6–76.0% [16, 23–25]. For instance, a study based in neighboring country India suggested that 42.7% of teachers in Tamil Nadu reported having high-stress levels [25].

In Nepal, the education system faces its own set of challenges. The overall schooling structure covers basic education (grades 1–8), and secondary education (grades 9–12) and the deployment of teachers is done in three categories; primary teachers (grades 1–5), lower secondary teachers (grade 6–8), and secondary teachers (grade 9–12) [26]. After the COVID-19 pandemic, the education sector of Nepal has been seriously affected, including infrastructural damages, low enrollment, changes in teaching modalities, transformation in policies, and a halt in teaching-learning activities, calling for coordinated efforts to recover the loss [27]. Teachers in Nepal also face multiple constraints such as large class sizes, inadequate resources, curriculum dissatisfaction, and limited opportunities for career development, all of which could contribute to their stress [28]. Recent studies suggest that nearly half of all teachers in Nepal experience stress, and many report dissatisfaction with their jobs [29, 30]. For instance, a study conducted by Karki et al. in the Lamjung district reported that 47.2% of secondary school teachers were experiencing occupational stress [29]. Similarly, a study in the Kaski district found that 9.2% of school teachers experienced occupational stress, with inadequate resources, poor job promotion, pupil misbehavior, inadequate salary, and large class sizes identified as major sources of stress [31]. The prevalence of occupational burnout among teachers in Nepal was found to be high, with moderate to high symptoms of emotional exhaustion (31.0%), depersonalization (35.0%), and personal accomplishments (27.0%) [32]. These findings underscore the urgent need to address the mental health and occupational challenges faced by teachers in Nepal. However, many of these studies focus broadly on stress without delving into its specific contributing factors or its impact on psychological well-being, leaving a critical gap in understanding these issues.

Over the past few years, teachers in Nepal, mostly those engaged in public schools, have also been involved in protests, demanding better working conditions, job security, and recognition of their rights. These protests have often been linked to broader educational reforms and government policies affecting the education sector. For instance, in the mass teachers’ protest of 2023, demands included sector reforms, revision of the educational bill, concerns over wages, job security, the teacher leaves, responsibilities, and challenges in their incentives and benefits [33, 34]. Given these circumstances, it is clear that teachers in Nepal face significant occupational stress, and their psychological well-being is under strain. However, there is limited research on the specific factors contributing to occupational stress, but its impact on the psychological well-being of teachers is unexplored [29–31]. Identifying the factors contributing to these issues is essential for designing effective interventions aimed at improving teachers’ well-being, job satisfaction, and overall productivity. Understanding these factors is also critical for informing policies that address the unique challenges faced by teachers in Nepal’s public education sector, ultimately fostering a more supportive and sustainable teaching environment. This study aims to fill this gap by assessing the level of occupational stress and psychological well-being among secondary school teachers in Nepal and identifying their associated factors.

Method

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted among public secondary school teachers of Beni Municipality. The data was collected between July to August 2024. Beni Municipality lies in the Gandaki Province of Nepal. This municipality contains 8652 households in total, and the total population is 32,697 according to the 2021 national census [35]. There are a total of 16 public secondary schools with 236 secondary school teachers, within ten wards of Beni Municipality [36].

Census and data collection

This study employed a complete enumeration approach, meaning that all teachers working in 16 available public secondary schools within Beni Municipality were included in the study. Out of the 236 teachers employed in these 16 schools, 231 participated in face-to-face interviews. Despite multiple attempts, five teachers could not be interviewed due to absenteeism. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted either in the participants’ respective offices or in other locations that ensured privacy. All teachers were approached and given a brief introduction to the study, and their written informed consent was obtained. Every teacher gave their consent and provided complete responses. A structured questionnaire was used as the interview guideline, consisting of four sections. The first section consisted of the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants, the second section consisted of job-related information, the third section consisted of occupational stress, and the last section consisted of a scale for psychological well-being.

The questionnaire consisted of two types of questions: some were developed by the research team, while others were adapted from standardized tools, including the Teacher Stress Scale (TSS) and the General Health Questionnaire-12 (GHQ-12). The research team compiled the questionnaire in English, incorporating both the newly developed items and the standardized tools, and then translated it into Nepali to ensure it was culturally appropriate and understandable for the participants. To ensure translation validity, it was back-translated into English. A pretest of the tool was conducted with 23 secondary school teachers serving at government schools in Kathmandu district to identify any issues and to refine the questionnaire. The finalized tool used in the study is attached as a supplementary file (Supp 1).

Outcome variables

The study assessed two outcome variables, i.e., psychological well-being and the teacher’s occupational stress. Psychological well-being was assessed using the General Health Questionnaire-12 (GHQ12), a standard screening instrument developed by David Goldberg [37]. The GHQ-12 is widely used in occupational settings to detect psychological distress and well-being [38] and has been validated in multiple languages, including the Nepali language. It consists of 12 items with a four-point Likert scale ranging from “Better than usual”, “Same as usual”, “Less than usual”, or “Much less than usual”. A cutoff ≥ 4 was used as the binary scoring method [39], classifying participants into two groups: psychologically well and psychologically distressed. In the context of Nepal, previous studies have shown the sensitivity and specificity of GHQ 12 to be 85.5% and 74.7%, respectively [40].

Occupational stress was measured using the Teacher Stress Scale (TSS), a validated tool for assessing stress among teachers [41]. It is a seven-item questionnaire, and these scale items are administered on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Neutral, 4 = Agree, and 5 = Strongly Agree). The cutoff point for the presence of occupational stress was determined after conducting a normality check. Using the median as the cutoff point, teachers were categorized into two groups: “stressed” and “not stressed”. Based on the pretesting data, the internal consistency of both GHQ12 and TSS tools was assessed through the calculation of Cronbach’s alpha, which was found to be 0.77 for GHQ12 and 0.75 for TSS, indicating good internal consistency for both instruments.

Statistical analysis

The data were entered using EpiData (V.3.1) and exported to Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS V.26) for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics such as mean, frequency, and percentage were calculated. Pearson chi-square test and binary logistic regression were applied to assess the association between different independent and dependent variables at a 95% confidence interval and 5% level of significance. The variables found to be statistically significant in bivariate analysis were included in the final model for multivariable analysis to calculate the adjusted odds ratio (aOR). Although this study covered all the public schools’ teachers from all secondary schools inside the municipality, regression analysis was performed to identify and quantify the factors most strongly associated with psychological well-being and occupational stress. The variance inflation factor (VIF) test was performed among selected independent variables to be subjective to the regression model. The VIF greater than five was taken as an indication of multicollinearity. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test for goodness-of-fit was also performed.

Results

Out of 231 teachers, nearly two-thirds (67.1%) were male, and one-third (32.9%) were female. The age of the participants ranged from 22 years to 59 years, with a mean age of 40.7 ± 10.23 years. More than one-third of the participants belonged to Brahmin/Chhetri/Thakuri ethnicity (77.5%), followed by Janajatis (16.5%) and Dalits (6.0%). Economic status distribution, based on the Kuppuswamy classification, indicated that over half (54.5%) of the participants belonged to the lower middle class (Table 1).

For multivariable analysis, the VIF test among the independent variables was performed, where the highest reported VIF was 1.685, indicating that there was no issue of multicollinearity. Multivariable analysis indicated that the teachers designated to the secondary level have twice the odds of reporting better psychological well-being as compared to those designated to the primary level (aOR = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.15–4.56) while keeping other factors constant. Similarly, teachers highly satisfied with their job duties reported significantly better psychological well-being (aOR = 2.37, 95% CI: 1.33–4.24) in the adjusted model. Additionally, those without occupational stress were twice as likely to report better psychological well-being (aOR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.16–3.78) after adjusting for all other factors (Table 5).

In the multivariable analysis of factors associated with occupational stress, several factors were found to have statistically significant association with occupational stress after adjusting for other variables. In regard to age, the teachers aged more than 50 years had three times higher odds of experiencing occupational stress compared to those aged 30 years or younger (aOR = 3.04, 95% CI: 1.15–8.01), while keeping other factors constant. Regarding occupational characteristics, dissatisfaction with salary remained a significant factor for occupational stress in the adjusted model, with teachers who were dissatisfied with their salary having a nearly three-fold increase in the odds of experiencing stress (aOR = 2.78, 95% CI: 1.10–7.02). Similarly, the perceived nature of the job was another critical factor. Teachers who viewed their job as highly demanding were over six times more likely to experience stress (aOR = 6.50, 95% CI: 2.14–19.76), while those perceiving moderate demands had twice the odds (aOR = 2.07, 95% CI: 1.02–4.20), even after adjusting for other variables. Additionally, psychological well-being was found to be a significant risk factor, with distressed individuals having more than twice the odds of experiencing occupational stress compared to those reporting good psychological well-being (aOR = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.16–3.78) after adjusting for all other factors (Table 6).

Discussion

This study revealed that roughly two-thirds of the secondary school teachers were psychologically distressed, while about two-fifths were psychologically well. Similarly, nearly half were found to have occupational stress. To our knowledge, there are no studies published to date assessing psychological distress among this vulnerable population in Nepal to make a compatible comparison, while few have investigated their occupational stress. This lack of literature underscores the importance of the current study, which provides a much-needed exploration of these issues within the unique socio-cultural and educational context of Nepal. The observed rate of psychological distress in this study is higher than the rate of psychological distress reported among adult populations in Lalitpur, Nepal, in 2021, suggesting 32.8% had psychological distress [42]. This finding suggests that teachers in Nepal may experience greater psychological distress than the general population due to the unique demands of their profession, such as large class sizes, inadequate resources, and a lack of institutional support. Additionally, a 2011 study from Kaski, Nepal indicated higher stress scores among school teachers [28]. These findings highlight the significant mental health burden among teachers, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions such as mental health programs specifically designed for teachers, including awareness campaigns, psychoeducation, and the establishment of accessible mental health services within schools.

The limited availability of Nepal-specific studies is a significant research gap. However, the observed prevalence of psychological distress in this study aligns with similar rates reported in neighboring countries, such as India, where in 2018, 68.8% of school teachers in Karnataka were noted to have experienced psychological distress [43]. Likewise, in another study conducted in India in 2022, it was found that about 76.0% of teachers had a deficit in psychological well-being [24]. These findings are also supported by a study from Sri Lanka, which suggested that teachers are subject to high psychological distress [44]. This higher prevalence of psychological distress has been reported by studies throughout the world. For instance, in Japan, 57.8% of female teachers and 47.8% of male teachers experienced psychological distress [45], while in Nigeria, 69.9% of teachers were found to report distress [16], and 56.0% of Canadian teachers reported experiencing psychological distress [46]. A study from South Africa in 2008 suggested that 31.0% of teachers had psychological distress [47]. These variations may be attributed to differences in assessment tools, teacher autonomy, societal respect for teachers, job culture, and study methodologies. Despite these variations, the observed prevalence in this study highlights its public health significance and need for integrating stress management workshops and training into professional development programs for teachers.

In this study, the school teachers assigned to the secondary level were found to have twice the odds of reporting psychological well-being as compared to the teachers assigned at the primary level. This finding aligns with prior research suggesting that higher-level teaching roles might be associated with better psychological outcomes due to factors like greater autonomy and professional recognition [48]. In Nepal, primary-level teachers often face larger class sizes, limited teaching resources, and less recognition compared to secondary-level teachers, which may contribute to this disparity. Addressing these inequities is critical to improving the psychological well-being of teachers across all teaching levels. Moreover, teachers who expressed satisfaction with their job descriptions and responsibilities were more likely to experience psychological well-being than those who were dissatisfied. However, there remains a significant gap in the literature exploring job-related characteristics, such as designated teaching levels, satisfaction with job responsibilities, and their relationship with psychological well-being, though a systematic review has suggested these factors could contribute to occupational stress [49]. On the contrary, this finding contradicts the findings of a study from India, which found no association between low role conflict and psychological well-being [43]. The discrepancy may be attributed to differences in personal traits, teaching environments, existing policies related to teacher welfare, or broader contextual factors. In Nepal, policies regarding teacher welfare, such as salary structures, access to professional development opportunities, and job security provisions, are often limited or inconsistently implemented. The limited studies and observed discrepancies highlight the need for further research to clarify these associations and explore the underlying mechanisms. Moreover, policymakers should consider implementing targeted support programs for primary-level teachers, such as allocating additional teaching resources, reducing class sizes, and creating opportunities for career development to improve their job satisfaction and psychological well-being.

Similarly, those who were free from occupational stress were twice as likely to enjoy psychological well-being. This observation is supported by the findings from neighboring countries such as Pakistan [50], India [51], and Malaysia [52], all of which demonstrate the significant negative impact of occupational stress on psychological well-being. The consistency of these findings across different countries underscores the inherently stressful nature of the teaching profession, where emotional labor, heavy workloads, and the interplay of these factors often contribute to occupational stress. Addressing these challenges is crucial to improving the psychological well-being of teachers globally.

In regard to occupational stress, it was observed that teachers aged 50 years or older were more than three times as likely to experience occupational stress compared to those under 30 years of age. This finding is consistent with previous research, such as a study from Malaysia in 2022, which indicated that middle-aged teachers experienced higher work stressors than younger and older teachers. Similarly, a study from India indicates that older teachers show higher levels of stress [53]. The consistency across studies underscores the influence of age on stress levels, with older teachers possibly facing unique stressors such as job stability concerns, increased responsibilities associated with seniority, health challenges, or approaching retirement. These factors, combined with potential health challenges, highlight the need for targeted interventions such as flexible working arrangements and wellness programs for older teachers to alleviate their stress and psychological distress.

In this study, teachers dissatisfied with their salaries were nearly three times more likely to experience occupational stress. This result aligns with studies from India, which reported a significant association between insufficient wages and high-stress levels, with more than five-fold increase in stress among those teachers reporting insufficient wages [53, 54]. However, the literature on the relationship between salary dissatisfaction and occupational stress in teachers is limited, suggesting a need for further research to provide more conclusive evidence. Understanding this dynamic is crucial, as salary dissatisfaction can compound existing stressors, negatively impacting both teacher performance and well-being. Improving teacher salary structures and creating transparent career advancement pathways might address dissatisfaction and reduce occupational stress.

Perceived job demands were another critical factor for occupational stress, with teachers who perceived their jobs as highly demanding being six times more likely to experience occupational stress. This finding aligns with results from studies in India, suggesting that a heavy workload, work pressure, and job demands exacerbate stress levels among teachers [51, 53, 54]. These findings highlight the importance of reinforcing interventions to address workload and provide clear job descriptions to mitigate stress among teachers. Work pressure is a universal stressor across diverse teaching contexts, underscoring the need for interventions that address workload management and job expectations to improve teachers’ psychological well-being.

The findings of this study hold important implications for educational policymakers, school administrators, and mental health professionals. The high prevalence of psychological distress and occupational stress among teachers suggests an urgent need for targeted mental health interventions within the school system. Programs aimed at stress management, mental health awareness, and support services should be prioritized, particularly for older teachers and those assigned to primary-level positions, who appear to be at greater risk. Addressing job demands through workload management, clearer job descriptions, and improved salary structures could significantly reduce occupational stress. This study highlights the necessity of creating a supportive work environment that acknowledges the unique challenges faced by teachers, which, in turn, can enhance both their psychological well-being and their performance in the classroom.

Although this study is one of the few to assess psychological well-being, stress, and associated factors among secondary school teachers in Nepal, it is not free from limitations. Despite efforts to conduct a comprehensive enumeration of teachers to enhance generalizability, the study was limited to a specific municipality and public schools. Given Nepal’s rich diversity in terms of culture, geography, and urban-rural settlements, caution should be taken when interpreting these findings, as such contextual factors may influence psychological well-being and stress differently across regions. The study relied on self-reported data, and while efforts were made to ensure participants felt comfortable providing honest responses, there remains the possibility of response biases, such as social desirability or recall bias. Teachers may have underreported their stress levels or psychological distress due to concerns about stigma or fear of negative repercussions. Additionally, although the study focused on occupational factors specific to the teaching profession due to time constraints and the length of the questionnaire, other potential contributors, such as work-life balance, family responsibilities, or institutional support systems, which might influence occupational stress, were not explored. Future research could explore these aspects to offer a more comprehensive understanding of stress and psychological well-being among teachers.

Conclusion

This study found that two-thirds of teachers experienced psychological distress, while half reported occupational stress. Factors influencing psychological well-being and stress included designated teaching level, satisfaction related to job description and salary, and perceived job demands. To address these issues, targeted interventions should include improving salary structures, clear job descriptions, and providing professional development opportunities for better job satisfaction. Additionally, reducing workload through administrative support and conducting regular mental health screenings can help mitigate stress and improve psychological well-being. By addressing these specific factors, policymakers and school administrators can create a supportive environment that promotes teachers’ well-being and enhances overall education quality.
