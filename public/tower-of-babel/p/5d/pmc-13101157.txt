Investigating the impact of multinational collaborations on cultural understanding, health disparities, biomedical innovations, and professional development through project-based learning
Authors: Breanna Kilgore, Nickolas A Gustavison, Alexandra Guiterrez-Vega, Ernesto Ibarra-Ramírez, Luis Estrada-Petrocelli, Jay Jesus Molino, Shilpa Drakshi, Anil S Bilimale, Devanshi Somaiya, Abhishek Appaji, Rogelio Garcia Contreras, Raj R Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe criticality of global health was put on full display following the COVID-19 pandemic and its devastating effects on communities, irrespective of their socioeconomic status. Biomedical innovations that have been actively developed over the years played a critical role in combating the crisis and bringing communities together. However, even with significant technological advancements, solving global health issues with realistic solutions requires an intimate understanding of culture, socioeconomics, and educational competency, among other factors. The Biomedical Innovations for Global Impact course at the University of Arkansas, Fayetteville, provides students with the opportunity to focus on specific healthcare system problems and work collaboratively with students from other disciplines and other countries. By collaborating with institutions in Panama and India, students are provided the opportunity to address health care system challenges under the advisement of clinical and entrepreneurial mentors. This study focused on evaluating the course via pre- and post-course surveys completed by the students, focusing on their educational experience, personal growth, professional development, and cultural understanding.ResultsStudent feedback on educational experience indicated a strong agreement that the course enhanced understanding of the design process as well as infrastructure challenges. Post-course analysis revealed an improvement in research skills, interdisciplinary communication, and real-world applications of both business and biomedical engineering principles. Approximately 85% of students agreed that the course improved their ability to engage in interdisciplinary groups while enhancing their understanding of global health. No significant shifts in the pre- and post-course responses were observed, which indicated that the high expectations of students were met. While most students recognized the value of engineering and entrepreneurial partnerships, some expressed interest in the application of these lessons on an international stage. The students’ cultural understanding prompts revealed a high agreement on its importance in global innovation. However, results revealed challenges in cross-cultural engagement and internal team communication, and active engagement.ConclusionQualitative responses revealed that students grew in empathy, cultural competence, and awareness of global health disparities. Despite the challenges that students experienced, many students appreciated the opportunity to apply their knowledge to explore diverse ideas and engage with real-world challenges as part of the course. The multicultural teams encouraged creativity and allowed students to broaden their horizons. In addition, the students had to learn to re-evaluate prior assumptions and increase their empathy, adaptability, and inclusiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-026-00646-9.Keywords: Student engagement, Biomedical engineering, Global health, Entrepreneurship, Student learning, Global partnership, Project-based learning, Professional development, Cultural understanding, Personal growth

Introduction

Internationalization and promotion of global understanding are important areas of focus for educators with interdisciplinary approaches emphasizing international health issues, causes, and technology-based solutions to global health issues. To effectively solve global health issues, people from different backgrounds must come together to develop innovative solutions. However, before addressing the problem, gaining a basic understanding of cultural differences and collaborating with people from different backgrounds is an essential skill to develop [1], [2–5]. This understanding can be gained in the classroom to prepare students to be global changemakers and leaders in the global health and technology field [6–10]. Previous studies have indicated that international service-learning opportunities via study abroad programs contribute to the development of intercultural competencies and promote increased awareness of global health issues [11–19]. Many of the students indicated that these experiences transformed their personal and professional lives and proved to be beneficial to the students’ overall understanding, cultural competency, and personal growth [12, 18].

Acknowledging the interdependence of our world, we have designed a “Biomedical Innovations for Global Impact” course that connects participating students with a global and local network of students, faculty, community partners, and mentors, and invites them to develop viable solutions to pressing health care or sanitation-related challenges in different locations around the world. We hope that creating a global collaborative classroom environment will encourage students to develop cultural understanding in conjunction with the tools for product development. The course focuses on specific problems triggered by healthcare challenges and allows students to work creatively with students from different countries and disciplines. The course is designed to include students from the United States, Panama, and India. Participating faculty and mentors instruct students on the importance of the design process during product development and how global health needs impact the process. The novel contributions of this study relate to promoting educational experiences at the intersection of design, entrepreneurship, and biomedical innovations to address healthcare challenges that impact our local and global communities.

Materials and methods

Course structure

The course was offered over one semester as a 3-credit elective course for students enrolled at the University of Arkansas. The students participating in the course from India and Panama received credit at their respective institutions. Five iterations of the course were conducted from 2020 to 2025. All participating students were proficient in the English language, as it was the chosen language of instruction in their respective institutions. In addition to the use of different features of Google Workspace as the learning management system, communication platforms for students and faculty involved Zoom. This study employed students who partook in a group project consisting of students (both undergraduate and graduate) from different countries (Panama, India, and the United States) and different majors (biomedical engineering, entrepreneurship, and public health). After creating a video introducing their culture and region, students were then provided with a listing of select problem statements to address specific healthcare challenges to simulate real-world product development from problem identification to product development. The first project was done in pairs, conducted in a hackathon format over 5 days, and was assigned based on location and knowledge base, wherein students were tasked with designing novel approaches to healthcare problems faced by the communities. This approach was taken to facilitate a quick understanding of the process that would be adopted by the students over the semester. Subsequently, the students were assigned to different groups, where the specific project to be tackled over the semester was assigned to the different student groups comprising at least three-four students from different countries. The first major step with the groups was to find at least ten professionals of different backgrounds related to biomedical entrepreneurship and conduct interviews to find gaps in the market or new problems previously not discussed. Armed with the advice of interviewees, the groups then had to report on the financial viability of their product, which required an understanding of market strategies, advertising, and cost analysis.

Groups gave their first attempt at a business proposal presentation, accompanied by a proposal idea report, to mentors. The presentations generally lasted ten minutes and were followed by five minutes of questions from their peers and mentors. With the feedback given, students were expected to refine their presentations, pitches, and timing. The last major component of the course was to give their modified presentations to the same mentors and to the class; the setup was the same as the first, and it was an opportunity for students to show the growth of their ideas. Following the presentation, the students were expected to write an individual retrospective on the evolution of their ideas, the changes due to feedback, the interviews, and the group collaboration/dynamics.

Course design project

For the semester-long project, students were tasked with designing a viable solution to a healthcare problem impacting different communities. This had to be done in the context of starting a viable business, which implied that they had to include items such as cost structure, financial viability, investors, and scalability as part of the design solution. These solutions would be presented to specialists in various aspects of the medical field, public health field, and business leaders, with students expected to answer scrutinizing questions on their product idea’s feasibility in current global markets. The students were also tasked with interviewing various professionals from different backgrounds to gather new ideas and perspectives on the problems being faced. This allowed students to practice building new connections and work on their interviewing skills, such as question development, scheduling, and note-taking. The students were given two rounds of presentation practice before the final presentation to provide feedback on professionalism, content, and quality, which thus allowed the teams to understand their expectations and practice development. Feedback throughout the semester was provided by assigned mentors for each team, selected from the compiled cohort of mentors representing experts from healthcare startups, non-governmental organizations, academics, healthcare administrators, and investors.

Student assessment

The course assessed student performance by a series of project benchmarks to simulate events and steps taken in the design process. Starting with a cultural awareness video (10%) as an introductory project followed by the assigning of teams and topic selection (5%), this was then followed up by the design process: idea validation and customer discovery report (10%), financial viability report (10%), preliminary presentation (10%), attendance (5%), and concluded with a final presentation (20%), final report (20%) and retrospective essay (10%). The assessment was performed by a multinational cadre of instructors representing each of the participating institutions from India, Panama and the USA.

Course evaluation surveys and data analysis

This project collected qualitative and quantitative data through 30-question pre-course and post-course surveys administered using the Qualtrics system. Over the five years, 175 students participated in the course. Although the survey was carried out for all participants, it was not mandatory. Not all students needed to respond to all questions, while many students chose not to participate in the survey. Based on the number of responses to each question and in each category, we analyzed the data to arrive at meaningful conclusions. The survey collected data related to gender, race, ethnicity, and age (Figs. S1-S4).

The surveys also collected qualitative data through short-answer questions focused on four themes: personal growth, professional development, educational experience, and cultural understanding. The pre-course survey was administered right after the first class, while the post-course survey was administered after the final project submission at the end of the semester. This data was collected at the beginning and end of the course to observe the growth the students experience throughout the semester. Quantitative data were collected using Likert-scale questions focused on the same four categories to determine whether the course outcomes met course expectations. Students representing three countries (Panama, India, and the US) participated in the study. The data collected from the survey were exported and graphed using GraphPad. For Likert questions, percentages of each response were calculated and grouped together by country to be graphed. To ensure scientific rigor and reproducibility, an ANOVA design accounting for pre- and post-survey data that was nested within groups was used to identify differences. Post-hoc Tukey HSD tests were used to identify differences among specific groups. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed using the GraphPad Prism 10 program (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). All questions were grouped according to their associated category to arrive at conclusions. Responses to different short-answer-type questions were compiled and investigated for commonalities and generalities found in responses. To evaluate team dynamics and student interactions, a 360-degree student peer evaluation was also conducted, which provided insights into student engagement with other team members, quality of student work, communication competency, dependability, continuous engagement, reliability to contribute to team needs, and overall team management.

Results

Educational experience

To measure educational experience, 14 Likert-scale questions were used. These questions and the student response distributions are shown in Fig 1 and Table 1 and Fig. S5. In further evaluating responses to question 28, we noted that 73% of students agreed and 27% strongly agreed that they understood the process behind the design process from idea to prototype. For responses to question 13, 75% of students agreed that they learned how to better determine the way in which limited infrastructure and resources are capable of meeting (or not) a growing demand. Only 15% of students disagreed with the learning objective, indicating that the methods of instruction used in the course were effective. Overall, the majority of the students agreed that the course contributed to positive educational experiences.

In the post-course survey, students were asked, “How did this course contribute to your educational experience?” and this question was used to obtain more qualitative data from the students. In response to this question, we wanted students to expand on their educational experience in the course. Students responses included “develop more as a researcher”, “business emphasis”, “expanding general knowledge outside of textbook/classroom knowledge”, as testimonials to the education experience. Students also commented on the product development process and how this course contributed to their overall understanding of biomedical innovators and designers. Business students commented on how they learned how to “communicate with engineers better” and apply their unique skills to the project to help understand product viability and feasibility.

Personal growth

To measure personal growth, 3 Likert-scale questions and 1 open-response question were used during the student surveys. Table 2 and Fig. 3 and S6 display the specific questions used in the pre- and post-course surveys along with their responses. For all three questions, approximately 50% of all students strongly agreed that the course had a positive impact on their personal growth. A major objective of the course was to enable the students to work across disciplines and across cultures. Approximately 85% of students either agreed or strongly agreed that they could confidently communicate with interdisciplinary teams after working with students in different countries. For responses to question 23, just over 5% of students disagreed that the course contributed to an increased understanding of global health; meanwhile, approximately 85% of the students agreed or strongly agreed with the course’s contribution.

Discussion

When evaluating all the different components of the survey, we noted that the responses in the majority of the categories indicated a positive experience for all students participating in the course. The educational experience and professional development categories had at least 70% of students either “Strongly Agree” or “Agree” as answers on the Likert scale. Statistical analysis was also calculated for each category, and significant differences were observed in responses to question 28. We observed a significant increase in responses to Question 28, inquiring about understanding the process of prototyping, thus indicating that the course successfully explained the process of bringing their ideas to market. As to the other questions, the lack of change indicates that each of the course objectives was met based on the responses in the pre-course survey. Most students agreed that their knowledge in educational experience and professional development categories grew because they participated in the course.

The variation in responses seen in some of the cultural understanding pre- and post-survey questions was further analyzed. When looking closer, the majority of the significant differences in the data were attributed to the responses by the students from the United States (Fig. S10). The students tended to have a lower mean score for question 2, which queried the impact working with other students had on their cultural understanding. This response could partially be explained in a follow-up questionnaire (Fig. 10), which queried the team dynamics throughout the course. The total number of responses (n = 163) is a composite of a 360-degree peer evaluation of all the student responses that were obtained. In this assessment, each student was asked to provide feedback on each of their team members. In most cases, each student provided feedback on two or three of their team members. Since over half of the responses were neutral or worse, this would indicate that a lack of internal communication and collaboration seemed to be the problem behind poor cultural understanding. Factors such as time zone differences and language barriers could also be contributing variables to misunderstandings that arose in certain teams. Figure S6 also displays a significant difference in pre-course and post-course survey responses for the Panamanian student community within the course. Question 34 queried about working in an international team to complete a common goal for improving product quality. When looking closer at the data, there was a small percentage of students responding from “Strongly Agree” to “Agree” as the mean answer in the surveys. Overall, the analysis of cultural understanding indicated an overall positive experience for the majority of the students, but also presented an opportunity to improve in the future to better the experience for all participating students from different countries. Promoting cultural competency across different countries during the design process involves integrating cultural knowledge, self-awareness, and inclusive practices that include active learning, diverse team building, and empathetic communication skills into the design process. As part of future course offerings, we plan to not only incorporate modules to ensure students learn more about different cultural practices, values, and worldviews, but also use cultural context to frame the healthcare problems to spark context-specific biomedical innovations.

Students’ responses to questions related to team dynamics and collaboration. Each question is displayed to the left, and responses are displayed to the right as a percentage (n = 163)

In the personal growth category, only 15% of the student population responded with “Disagree” and “Neither Agree or Disagree” for question 23. This response variation was indicated in the statistical analysis of the US student population, where a slightly significant difference between the pre-course and post-course survey responses was observed. It is useful to note that Question 23 asked if the course contributed to an increased understanding of common global health issues. The observed variance could be attributed to the course’s focus on product development and market assessment, versus focusing solely on healthcare challenges globally.

We also had all students rank their ideal career choice in a list from 1 to 5, most likely to least likely. Most students chose industry as their most desired career field. This response could be due to specific mentorship they received in the course and the fact that most of the course material focused on companies, start-up culture, and entrepreneurship. Students were exposed mostly to these types of careers in the course, and the professors focused heavily on product viability. Government/Policy was ranked as the least desirable amongst the respondents out of all the options. This could be a result of not being made aware of how engineering and product development relate to government and policy. Similarly, there could also be a stigma against government positions due to either political affiliations or public distrust. It is, however, useful to note that during the course offering, there weren’t many guest speakers from this field, and most of the material did not explain what role the information would play in policy or governmental capacity.

We recognize that use of Likert-type statistics has its limitations that include response biases, ceiling effects, and self-report subjectivity challenges. We thus complemented survey results with qualitative written responses. As an example, the results from the surveys were further substantiated by the written responses, many of which expressed frustrations, especially in the lack of communication within groups. Even with this challenge, many students still expressed that the experience of working across cultures with students from different nationalities gave them a newfound appreciation for different cultures’ approaches to problems and how they challenge or cause re-evaluations of ideas. This was also the first time many students had the ability to test their skills, leadership, and understanding in a real-world scenario; it allowed them the opportunity to take a step back and view their career choices and options. The project-based approach also helped “bridge the gap between theory and practice” that other courses usually fell short of accomplishing. When asked specifically about the experience of working in multicultural teams, many responded that they felt more creative and freer to explore different thoughts and ideas, as they felt they otherwise could not. They also mentioned higher empathy, better adaptability, and a higher degree of cultural sensitivity, which leads to positive outcomes and challenges to preconceptions.

Future directions and conclusions

After the semester design project was completed, students were asked to provide more information on the group dynamics; all students were required to work in interdisciplinary/international teams to complete the project. Most students expressed concerns and problems they experienced with a significant lack of communication and collaboration throughout the design/development process. In Fig. 10, each question posed is shown along with the percentage of responses. For the second question listed, over 50% of the students responded with an answer lower than “sometimes,” indicating a clear lack of team communication and preparation. Moving forward, implementation of different communication platforms like Google Meet and WhatsApp and more professor check-ins could help with communication difficulties, as this would allow for the essential collaboration and interactions necessary to ensure the common goal of a completed, quality product. In future surveys, students can also be asked for their opinions on the communication and check-in changes, as well as thoughts on how to improve issues they encountered during the course.

In the future, we plan on engaging with non-governmental organizations to provide valuable insights and perspectives on real-world challenges that are experienced in clinical settings across the globe. This would help broaden students’ understanding of healthcare disparities and help develop skills for working in interdisciplinary/international teams to solve global healthcare challenges. We also hope to enter student teams into innovation and product design competitions such as ‘Science and Tech-based Innovations to Advance the SDGs’ sponsored by the UN. These competitions will expose students to different avenues of product development and entrepreneurial skills to assist with team collaboration. It will also expose people to how different groups approach a problem, how to pitch the idea to a panel, and how to defend the idea against critiques. Introducing seed money for each team will allow them to prototype their device to further develop the course as an interactive and involved opportunity to apply knowledge.

It is useful to note that the response rate was limited to the number of students who had completed the course, which was the objective of the study. Respondents were from different backgrounds (business, biomedical engineering, health care, etc.), which limits potential cognitive bias by taking different backgrounds into account. Though care was taken to avoid leading questions and the responses were anonymized, there is a possibility that students may have felt pressured to respond positively. However, it is useful to note that the post-course survey was administered after the final project and final grades were assigned for the course.

The results of this study provide evidence on how effective project-based learning approaches are at addressing global healthcare needs through biomedical innovation and contribution to student learning. In our analysis of educational experience through the Likert-scale survey questions, we noticed that all students, irrespective of their location, had similar responses for each survey question with no significant difference between the pre-course and post-course responses. This consistency indicates that the course objectives were fulfilled, and student expectations were met. The questions in the educational experience category gauged students’ understanding of product development, implementation, viability, and scalability. Many of the students commented on learning about the financial and business models for product development as an important component of the course, indicating the usefulness of this component in helping students develop better, low-cost, and sustainable solutions with potential for broad impact across diverse populations. This study will help enhance the biomedical engineering curriculum by shaping courses that foster empathy, solidarity, and collaboration across disciplines, sectors, and borders; the goal is to ensure that shared challenges are met with unified solutions. The implementation of the Biomedical Innovations for Global Impact course is an example of how universities can collaborate not only across disciplines but across the globe for the benefit of learners in both universities and their respective communities. Students across different disciplines gained a valuable understanding about their unique roles and built a network of peer learners and community support. In the end, different communities thrive when social entrepreneurs collaborate to develop practical solutions for urgent, real-world healthcare issues. It is useful to note that the findings in this country are context-specific, based on information gathered from participants from three countries (India, Panama, and the USA). Although the findings cannot be universally generalizable, our study demonstrated the significant potential of collaborations across countries to analyze opportunities to bring innovations to market and possibly coordinate transnational efforts to implement and scale the development and impact of innovations to address pressing social issues.
