Beyond First Events: A Closer Look at VOYAGER PAD
Authors: Jen‐Kuang Lee
Published: 2025

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) is a global vascular condition associated with substantial morbidity, mortality, and functional decline. The VOYAGER PAD (Efficacy and Safety of Rivaroxaban in Reducing the Risk of Major Thrombotic Vascular Events in Subjects With Symptomatic Peripheral Artery Disease Undergoing Peripheral Revascularization Procedures of the Lower Extremities) trial established that vascular‐dose rivaroxaban, when added to aspirin, significantly reduced a composite end point of acute limb ischemia, major vascular amputation, myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, or cardiovascular death in patients following lower extremity revascularization. 1 However, as in many cardiovascular trials, the use of composite end points poses a challenge in interpreting the true clinical relevance of individual event reductions.

In this prespecified exploratory analysis in this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Hogan and colleagues leverage 3 advanced analytic approaches—the global rank method, win ratio, and net clinical benefit—to reevaluate the VOYAGER PAD composite outcomes, prioritizing events by clinical severity and evaluating treatment efficacy against bleeding risk. 2 Their findings provide critical insights into the benefit–risk profile of low‐dose rivaroxaban and offer a model for future cardiovascular end point interpretation.

CONTRIBUTION AND STRENGTHS

This article meaningfully advances the interpretation of composite end point trials in vascular medicine. First, it rigorously addresses a key limitation of time‐to‐first event analyses: the equal weighting of events that differ in severity. By applying the global rank method and win ratio—both prespecified and clinically justified—the authors demonstrate that rivaroxaban not only reduces events but also delays or prevents the most severe ones (eg, cardiovascular death and stroke). 3 , 4

Second, the net clinical benefit analysis integrates both efficacy and safety outcomes, showing that the ischemic benefits of rivaroxaban outweigh the bleeding risks across multiple definitions. Notably, the number needed to treat ranged from 24 to 32, supporting a favorable risk–benefit ratio and reinforcing rivaroxaban's therapeutic value in this high‐risk population with PAD. 1 , 5

Finally, the study demonstrates consistent superiority across multiple analytic strategies, increasing confidence in the robustness of the findings. The methodology is well explained and appropriately referenced, making this a valuable contribution not only to the PAD literature but also to the broader domain of cardiovascular clinical trial methodology. 3 , 6

METHODOLOGICAL INNOVATIONS

The authors highlight the limitations of conventional time‐to‐first event end points—particularly in trials where high‐frequency but less severe outcomes can drive the overall result. 7 In VOYAGER PAD, acute limb ischemia was the most frequent event and contributed substantially to the observed treatment benefit. By contrast, events like cardiovascular death or stroke may have greater long‐term clinical consequences but occur less frequently.

The global rank method and win ratio address these issues by assigning greater weight to more clinically important events. In both analyses, rivaroxaban demonstrated superiority to placebo (P=0.0158 and P=0.0167, respectively). 2 The win ratio of 1.16, although modest, underscores the incremental benefit in preventing severe events. 4 , 8

The net clinical benefit analysis further strengthens the findings by demonstrating a consistent reduction in adverse composite outcomes, including both ischemic and bleeding events. The consistency across 4 different definitions (incorporating cardiovascular versus all‐cause death and varying bleeding criteria) adds credibility to the overall benefit–risk assessment. 5 , 9

LIMITATIONS AND INTERPRETIVE CONSIDERATIONS

Despite the strengths, several limitations warrant discussion. First, these analyses, although prespecified, remain exploratory and thus hypothesis generating. The hierarchy of clinical event severity, although reasonable, is inherently subjective. Some patients may prioritize quality of life or limb preservation over survival, potentially challenging the universality of the ranking schema. 10 , 11

Second, the high proportion of ties (72%) in the win ratio analysis may limit its discriminatory power and suggests that many patient pairs did not experience events or experienced them at the same time. This could attenuate the perceived effect size and highlights a potential limitation of pairwise comparison methods in trials with low event rates or homogeneous timing of events. 8 , 12

Third, although the findings are robust in the trial population, generalizability to broader populations with PAD (eg, women, racial minority groups, or patients with atypical symptoms) remains uncertain. The cohort was predominantly male and White, and subgroup analyses were not presented in this paper.

Lastly, although net clinical benefit analyses are informative, they depend on predefined thresholds of harm and benefit and may not fully capture individual patient preferences or clinical heterogeneity. 13

CLINICAL AND RESEARCH IMPLICATIONS

These findings have practical implications for shared decision‐making in PAD management. Clinicians often struggle to balance ischemic protection with bleeding risk, especially in the setting of polyvascular disease. This analysis reassures that low‐dose rivaroxaban offers net clinical benefit even when severe outcomes are prioritized.

From a research perspective, the article exemplifies how advanced analytic tools can improve the interpretation of composite end points. Future trials—particularly those in vascular medicine where multiple event types co‐occur—should consider prespecifying hierarchical and net benefit analyses to enhance interpretability and patient‐centered relevance. 6 , 12 , 14

Moreover, these findings may guide clinical guideline updates by supporting more nuanced risk stratification and personalized antithrombotic strategies. Further real‐world studies, including diverse patient populations and preference‐based modeling, are needed to confirm these findings in broader settings.

CONCLUSIONS

Hogan et al. 2 deliver a compelling, methodologically innovative, and clinically relevant reanalysis of VOYAGER PAD. Their use of the global rank method, win ratio, and net clinical benefit demonstrates that rivaroxaban provides not just numerical but clinically meaningful benefit in patients with PAD post lower extremity revascularization. The consistency across multiple analytic approaches strengthens the conclusion that vascular‐dose rivaroxaban plus aspirin should be considered a preferred strategy in appropriate patients.

This article contributes to evolving best practices in trial design and interpretation and should be considered an important reference for clinicians, researchers, and policy makers in cardiovascular medicine.
