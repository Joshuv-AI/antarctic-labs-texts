Mapping of fermionic lattice models for Ising solvers
Authors: Lakshya Nagpal, Aditya Kumar, S R Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present an end-to-end, symmetry-aware pipeline that converts interacting fermionic and quantum-spin models into annealer-ready QUBOs while preserving low-energy physics. The workflow combines Bravyi–Kitaev encoding, exact symmetry tapering, Xia–Bian–Kais (XBK) diagonalization to a Z-only form, and local quadratization, with ground energies recovered via a Dinkelbach fixed-point over the resulting Ising objective. We validate the approach across a complexity ladder: (i) a frustrated 2D Ising model run on a D-Wave Advantage QPU reproduces the known ferromagnet–stripe transition; (ii) finite-temperature checks on 1D Ising recover standard finite-size trends; (iii) a genuinely quantum spin target (XXZ) matches exact diagonalization (ED) on small chains; and (iv) interacting fermions (t–V) in 1D (rings ) show ED-level energies and the expected kink near , with a 2D cluster tracking ED slopes up to a uniform offset. Introduction

Interacting quantum lattices—fermions, spins, and bosons—exhibit superconductivity, quantum magnetism, correlated metals, and topological order. Predictive simulation is hard because Hilbert spaces grow exponentially and quantum statistics impose long-range constraints1–3.

Classical solvers each cover part of this space: exact diagonalization (ED) is definitive but small-scale; quantum Monte Carlo (QMC) reaches larger sizes but suffers from the sign problem in frustrated/fermionic regimes4; tensor networks, especially DMRG, excel in 1D and some quasi-2D cases but degrade with entanglement and topology1,2,5.

Quantum hardware offers a complementary route. Gate-based processors can implement the full operator algebra but are currently limited by qubit counts, fidelities, and depth6. Quantum annealers already provide thousands of qubits with persistent couplings and directly minimize Ising/QUBO cost functions7–10, provided physical Hamiltonians—typically noncommuting and sometimes multi-body—are translated to a diagonal, two-local Ising form without losing essential physics.

From fermions to qubits: where BK and XBK fit.

Fermion-to-qubit encodings map second-quantized operators to Pauli strings. Jordan–Wigner (JW) and Bravyi–Kitaev (BK) produce a Pauli (spin/qubit) Hamiltonian usable on gate-based devices (Trotterization or VQE)11–19, but present hardware remains resource-limited6. In higher-dimensional (higher-D) settings, JW parity strings grow long; BK distributes parity/occupation to reduce typical operator weight (from toward ), especially with lattice linearizations(e.g., Hilbert-curve orderings, a class of space-filling curves) that keep geometric neighbors adjacent in index space20,21. For annealers the Pauli form is not yet “ready,” because X/Y terms are nondiagonal. The Xia–Bian–Kais (XBK) method embeds the problem in a replicated register and rewrites all X/Y structure as Z-only couplings—with sector bookkeeping—yielding a diagonal Ising Hamiltonian at the cost of replication22,23.

What is new here.

We assemble a deterministic, symmetry-aware pipeline tailored to lattice models and current annealing hardware: (i) BK(or direct spin form) for a locality-friendly Pauli Hamiltonian12,13; (ii) exact tapering beforediagonalization to remove qubits without approximation and fix the symmetry sector24; (iii) XBK to obtain a Z-only Hamiltonian with sector bookkeeping22; (iv) two reduction with correctness-preserving penalties to form a QUBO25,26; (v) sector energies via a discrete Rayleigh quotient solved by Dinkelbach iteration for monotone convergence diagnostics27. We use hardware-aware choices (lattice ordering, penalty scales, and Pegasus-compatible minor-embedding/graph shaping)8.

Positioning relative to alternatives.

JW+XBK without tapering inflates nonlocal strings and replicas on higher-D lattices13,23; parity mappings with tapering remove global symmetries but typically yield longer strings than BK under standard 2D orderings. Direct quadratization of nondiagonal Pauli terms (skipping XBK) forces large penalties and risks spurious minima7,28,29. Gate-based routes avoid Ising-only reduction but currently face depth/fidelity limits6. Our BK tapering XBK two pipeline balances fidelity, variable growth, and embeddability for extended lattices.

We benchmark three models: a frustrated classical Ising system, the spin- XXZ chain, and the spinless fermionic t–V model. In each case the resulting two-local Ising Hamiltonian is annealer-ready. The frustrated classical Ising benchmark was executed on a D-Wave Advantage QPU to validate end-to-end embedding and readout, whereas the XXZ and spinless t–V studies used the D-Wave Ocean DIMOD simulators (no sustained QPU access for these cases) these were further compared with neal (simulated annealing) packages to verify QUBO acceptance and embedding. Reported sizes for XXZ and t–V therefore reflect simulator limits rather than QPU capacity. Although our focus is on lattices, the pipeline is portable; we also apply it to a small molecular Hamiltonian (benzene) in Appendix E, and summarize a lightweight GUI in Appendix F.

The primary contribution of this work is the construction and validation of a unified, end-to-end mapping pipeline rather than the demonstration of speedup or near-term performance advantage. We explicitly implement the pipeline across multiple representative problem classes, including classical Ising models, quantum spin chains, fermionic lattice Hamiltonians, and a molecular electronic structure example. This breadth is intended to demonstrate robustness and generality of the construction, and to establish formal compatibility with annealing-based solvers across a wide class of Hamiltonians.

Model selection

We consider two complementary model families that together span classical and fully quantum regimes while exercising the benchmarks used in this work: a fermionic lattice Hamiltonian and a spin (qubit) Hamiltonian. The former stresses fermion-to-qubit encodings and symmetry structure; the latter isolates noncommuting spin dynamics without fermionic statistics.

This section fixes the model families and parameter conventions used throughout. The complete mapping pipeline—fermion-to-qubit encoding, exact tapering, XBK diagonalization, and reduction to QUBO—is presented in Sec. III.

Bravyi–kitaev mapping (BK)

Fermionic Hamiltonians in second quantization must be recast into qubit operators to run on quantum hardware. The BK transformation offers an efficient compromise between Jordan–Wigner (local updates but long parity strings in higher-D) and parity encodings (short parity, heavy updates), reducing many operator weights from to by distributing occupation/parity information across a Fenwick tree (binary indexed tree)12,13.

Ordering in higher dimensions.

Although the BK sets depend only on L and j, the ordering of modes is induced by the lattice geometry. Row-wise or column-wise enumerations preserve nearest neighbors in only one direction, whereas space-filling curves(e.g., the Hilbert curve) keep horizontal and vertical neighbors close in index space, shortening parity strings and improving hardware compatibility20,21. Results and discussion

Our validation strategy follows a complexity hierarchy: (1) purely classical spin models with well-established behavior; (2) genuinely quantum spin systems with noncommuting terms; and (3) interacting fermionic Hamiltonians. This progression benchmarks each stage of the mapping–embedding–optimization pipeline on problems of increasing physical richness and helps isolate potential systematic biases before tackling the full fermionic targets. As emphasized in the Introduction, our pipeline is designed for D-Wave annealers and yields annealer-ready QUBO instances; in this study, however, only the frustrated classical Ising benchmark was executed on a D-Wave Advantage QPU. The XXZ and spinless t–V studies were run using D-Wave’s publicly available Ocean simulators, so the reported system sizes for those models reflect simulator and runtime limits rather than QPU capacity. Finally, to demonstrate portability beyond lattices, we also apply the same pipeline to a molecular Hamiltonian (benzene); the end-to-end mapping and results are summarized in Appendix D.

Benchmark I: Frustrated 2D Ising model at

The first benchmark targets a classical ground-state problem: the square-lattice frustrated Ising model. Competing ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic couplings generate a highly degenerate manifold in the intermediate frustration regime, making this a stringent test of optimization quality.

Previous works have implemented this model directly on D-Wave processors for large lattices, including the case7,36. Here, we use it to assess how well the QPU (quantum processing unit) resolves the known phase boundary when the problem is provided in full QUBO form through our pipeline. We adopt the same lattice size as Ref36. to enable a direct comparison. The goal is not new physics, but a baseline annealer benchmark on a well-characterized classical instance before moving to quantum systems.

We embedded the QUBO representation of the problem onto a D-Wave Advantage QPU to sample its lowest-energy spin configurations. The system was analyzed by sweeping the frustration ratio , and for each value of R, we collected a large set of annealed samples. The raw samples from the QPU were then subjected to a classical post-processing step; this involves using local search algorithms to verify that each low-energy configuration is a true local minimum of the objective function. From these verified configurations, we computed three physical observables: the magnetization M, the Binder cumulant U, and the structure factor .

The crossover phenomenon: why annealing is initially slower

A central performance question is: At what problem size does a general-purpose quantum annealing (QA) workflow become competitive with a specialized classical solver such as DMRG?1,6,32 For small L, QA is less efficient due to mapping overheads12,22,25,28,29. Asymptotically, however, different exponents can produce a computational crossoverwhere QA becomes faster32,38–42.

ED scales as and is a small-Lreference35. DMRG achieves for gapped 1D systems1,2,5,43. For QA, after BK–XBK and reduction, and empirical TTS often grows roughly as (with instance-dependent prefactors)32,38–40. On current devices, the crossover point lies at –, beyond present qubit counts/connectivity6,8,44.

In the near term, QA’s advantage is model-agnostic applicability rather than beating DMRG at small L7,32. Hardware scaling plus overhead reductions (e.g., tapering) decrease prefactors and push accessible sizes upward22,24.

Path to scalability and near-term priorities

The classical mapping (BK tapering XBK quadratization) is a one-time cost; once a QUBO is assembled, the annealer produces samples with fixed per-read anneal time (s–ms)32,39. Time-to-solution depends on reads for a target success probability, minor-embedding overhead, and postprocessing, captured by standard scaling fits38,44. This contrasts with iterative gate-based approaches (e.g., VQE)6,45–50.

Conclusions, summary, and outlook

We introduced a deterministic, symmetry-aware workflow that maps lattice Hamiltonians—fermionic or spin—to an annealer-compatible two-local Ising/QUBO form:

The ordering is chosen to expose and remove exact symmetries before diagonalization, keep locality favorable in higher-D via space-filling orderings, and produce Pegasus-conscious QUBOs. Energies are recovered with a discrete Rayleigh quotient solved by Dinkelbach iteration, providing a monotone sector-by-sector diagnostic.

We stress that the objective of this work is not to demonstrate a regime of quantum advantage over gate-based quantum computers or classical algorithms. Rather, the goal is to establish a formally consistent and practically implementable mapping pipeline that enables quantum and fermionic Hamiltonians to be expressed in annealer-compatible form. The present results delineate the regime in which such mappings are currently feasible, and highlight the resource overheads that must be addressed for larger-scale applications. This positioning clarifies the complementary role of annealing-based approaches alongside gate-based quantum simulation methods.

Outlook

Mapping efficiency. Reduce via locality-optimized encodings (BK variants/parity–BK hybrids) and automated symmetry discovery to maximize tapering gains; prefer higher-D orderings (e.g., Hilbert curves) that cut string lengths.

Quadratization and penalties. Employ tighter gadgets and principled, instance-adaptive penalty schedules to shrink while preserving correctness; monitor spurious minima with feasibility checks.

Embedding and annealing. Improve minor-embedding (shorter chains, topology-aligned graphs), tune chain strengths and gauges, use pauses/reverse anneals, and calibrate effective temperature for robust post-processing.

Hybrid workflows. Combine annealer outputs with classical refinement (parallel tempering/local search) and lightweight gate-based subroutines when helpful (e.g., sector identification).

Physics targets. Extend to spinful fermions (Hubbard-type), frustrated magnets, and topological phases; add excited-state and finite-T observables; broaden molecular set beyond benzene (Appendix E).

Scaling and crossover. Systematically track time-to-solution and accuracy versus r, , and embedding overhead on newer hardware to empirically locate the performance crossover with tensor-network and QMC baselines.

In short, the BK–tapering–XBK–quadratization chain provides a reproducible route from operator-level models to annealer-ready QUBOs that preserve low-energy physics. While today’s device constraints limit sizes, the demonstrated fidelity on spins and fermions, portability to molecules, and clear engineering levers suggest that increasingly capable annealers—and better mappings—can bring scientifically relevant lattice scales within reach. We note that the auxiliary-variable overhead incurred during quadratization and embedding is not fundamental. Established constructions, such as Ishikawa-type reductions for higher-order terms and Sierpinski-triangle-based embeddings for structured lattices, can substantially reduce the number of auxiliary variables required in practice. In the present work we adopt generic constructions to emphasize generality; systematic optimization of auxiliary counts remains an important direction for future improvement.

Discussion: regime of applicability and resource considerations

The primary objective of this work is to establish a unified and formally consistent pipeline for mapping quantum spin and fermionic Hamiltonians into annealer-compatible Ising and QUBO formulations, rather than to demonstrate near-term performance advantage or scalability to physically large system sizes. We explicitly implement this pipeline across a diverse set of representative problem classes, including classical Ising models, quantum spin chains, fermionic lattice Hamiltonians in one and two dimensions, and a molecular electronic structure example. This breadth is intended to demonstrate robustness and generality of the construction, and to establish formal annealer compatibility across a wide class of Hamiltonians. A central concern raised by this mapping approach is the resource overhead incurred through XBK replication, auxiliary variables introduced during quadratization, and subsequent minor-embedding onto hardware graphs. We emphasize that the resulting logical QUBO size should not be interpreted as a direct measure of physical qubit requirements.

Quadratic couplers encode connectivity constraints between logical variables and do not themselves correspond to qubits; the mapping from logical variables to physical qubits is determined by the embedding, where multiple logical interactions may be realized using chains on shared physical qubits. Accordingly, reported QUBO sizes reflect logical problem complexity rather than direct hardware qubit counts. Moreover, the auxiliary-variable overhead incurred during quadratization and embedding is not fundamental. Established constructions, such as Ishikawa-type reductions for higher order interactions and Sierpinski-triangle-based embeddings for structured lattice graphs, are known to substantially reduce auxiliary-variable counts in practice. In the present work we adopt generic constructions to emphasize generality and clarity; systematic optimization of auxiliary overheads remains an important direction for future development and scaling.

An important structural distinction between the proposed annealing-based pipeline and gate-based quantum simulation approaches lies in compilation structure. The final QUBO encodes all Hamiltonian parameters simultaneously, allowing parameter sweeps (e.g., interaction strengths or chemical potentials) without recompilation or reoptimization. In contrast, circuit-based approaches such as variational quantum eigensolvers, Trotterized time evolution, or sampling-based diagonalization typically require circuit updates or retraining for each parameter set. We stress that this distinction concerns compilation and workflow structure rather than asymptotic resource scaling, and does not imply an intrinsic advantage in qubit count or runtime. All quantum spin and fermionic benchmarks reported in this work are executed using classical simulators for the annealing process, with the exception of the classical Ising benchmark executed on physical annealing hardware.

Consequently, reported wall-clock runtimes reflect classical simulation cost and should not be interpreted as indicative of performance on quantum annealers. Direct runtime comparisons between classical simulators and physical annealing hardware are therefore not meaningful within the scope of this study. Finally, we emphasize that the present work is not intended to establish a regime in which annealing-based approaches outperform gate-based universal quantum devices. Rather, it delineates the regime in which a formally consistent annealer-compatible representation of quantum Hamiltonians can be constructed and studied. The results highlight both the flexibility of the mapping pipeline and the resource overheads that must be addressed for larger-scale applications. In this sense, the proposed approach is best viewed as complementary to gate-based quantum simulation methods, providing a structured pathway for exploring annealer compatibility and hybrid workflows as hardware capabilities continue to evolve.

C. 8 Physical interpretation

The XBK method encodes logical qubit operators in terms of Ising couplings between a reference copy and its replicas, with sectors enumerating possible alignment patterns. The embedding enlarges the search space so that an Ising solver can reproduce the original quantum ground state without using X or Y operators. The price is an increase from m to rm qubits before quadratization, but the procedure is fully systematic and symmetry-aware.

Appendix D: Application to molecular systems : benzene case study

To demonstrate the practical utility of the pipeline on complex chemical systems, we performed a series of ground state energy calculations on the benzene molecule. D. 1 Benzene: multi-parameter ground state optimization

We performed a detailed study of the ground state energy surface for benzene using Dwave DIMOD (CH), focusing on the interplay between C–C and C–H bond length variations. These calculations provide insight into how structural optimization in aromatic systems is reflected in the underlying quantum energy landscape.
