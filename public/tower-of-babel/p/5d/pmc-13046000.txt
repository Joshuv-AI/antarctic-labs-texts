Recurrent pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma following a 7-year disease-free interval: A case report and literature review
Authors: Chorouq Mehdaoui, Abdelkrim Zazour, Anass Haloui, Imane Kamaoui, Ghizlane Kharrasse, Zahi Ismaili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma (PACC) is a rare malignant pancreatic tumor, accounting for only 1% of all pancreatic neoplasms. It has distinctive clinical, radiological, and histological features.Case presentation:Here, we report a case of PACC diagnosed 7 years after surgical treatment for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). The recurrence was suspected during routine follow-up via abdominal computed tomography scan, which showed a local mass without biliary ducts dilation, vascular involvement, or any specific radiological signs. However, clinical examination and laboratory workup, including tumor markers (CA 19-9 and carcinoembryonic antigen), were normal. By comparing samples obtained via endoscopic ultrasound fine-needle aspiration of the pancreatic mass to the histopathological findings of the prior duodenopancreatectomy, a diagnosis of local recurrence of previously misdiagnosed PACC was established.Clinical discussion:PACC is a rare pancreatic epithelial malignancy (1%), for which radical surgical resection remains the treatment of choice. However, it is usually diagnosed at an advanced stage (with metastasis). The most common differential diagnoses include pancreatic adenocarcinoma, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, solid pseudopapillary neoplasms, and pancreatoblastoma in the pediatric population. Histopathological examination combined with immunohistochemical staining remains the cornerstone for establishing the diagnosis. In our case, ultrasound-guided fine needle aspiration of the pancreatic mass, compared to the previous duodenopancreatectomy, led to the diagnosis of a local recurrence of a previously misdiagnosed initially as PDAC, with the longest survival (84 months) ever described in the literature.Conclusions:Due to its rarity, PACC remains a diagnostic challenge. It has a better prognosis than PDAC; radical resection is recommended.

Introduction and importance

While acinar cells make up >80% of the pancreas, pancreatic acinar cell carcinoma (PACC) is a rare malignant tumor, accounting for only 1% of all pancreatic tumors. PACC generally has a poor prognosis, although it is relatively better than pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), which constitutes 90% of pancreatic tumors and is projected to become the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality by 2040[1].

PACC is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, usually presenting with liver metastases[2]. Despite the lack of specific clinical symptoms, PACC has distinct biological, radiological, and histological characteristics. However, its diagnosis is challenging and is often mistaken for other pancreatic malignancies, such as PDAC, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PNETs), and pancreatoblastoma (PB) in children.

Given the lack of standardized treatment guidelines, surgical resection remains the treatment of choice for resectable tumors including recurrences. Accordingly, we report a case initially diagnosed as PDAC, which was later identified as PACC, to highlight the diagnostic challenges and clinical management of this rare pancreatic malignancy.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist (2025).

Case presentation

A 54-year-old male patient with a history of duodenopancreatectomy performed in 2016 for pancreatic head adenocarcinoma and without any family history of malignant tumors was admitted to our department. The surgery achieved R0 resection margins, as confirmed by anatomopathological analysis. Following surgery, he underwent gemcitabine-based adjuvant chemotherapy. In 2023, radiological follow-up via computed tomography (CT) revealed an isolated hypodense mesenteric mass without evidence of additional suspected lesions (Fig. 1). Figure 1.CT scan imaging. This finding suggested either a local pancreatic recurrence, a mesenchymal abdominal tumor, an extra-digestive gastrointestinal stromal tumor, or an adenopathy. The patient was asymptomatic with a normal clinical examination. Tumor markers [carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (Ca19-9) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA)], as well as liver and renal function tests, were within normal limits. While plasma lipase levels were initially normal, they gradually increased to 300 UI/mL without any clinical signs of hyperlipasemia syndrome. A 18F-fludeoxyglucose-18 positron emission tomography scan identified a hypermetabolic left lateroaortic lesion (SUV max = 2.5) without other foci of hepatic, pulmonary, or skeletal hypermetabolism. Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) further characterized the lesion as a well-demarcated, oval, encapsulated tissue formation involving the pancreatic tail. The lesion contained microcystic lesions, T1 and T2 hyposignal, and diffusion hypersignal with apparent diffusion coefficient reduction, with a homogeneous enhancement after gadolinium injection (Fig. 2). No suspicious focal liver lesions were detected. MRI findings were initially interpreted as consistent with either a solid pseudopapillary tumor or a local recurrence of the patient’s prior tumor. An endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) confirmed the presence of a well-defined heterogeneous lesion measuring 34 × 45 mm with central anechoic areas, appearing contiguous with the residual pancreatic parenchyma and distinct from surrounding structures. The lesion was located at a safe distance from major vessels, including the celiac trunk, superior mesenteric artery, and splenic vessels. Afterward, a trans-gastric fine-needle (19 G) aspiration was performed (Fig. 3). Figure 3.Linear endoscopic ultrasound. (A) Well-defined heterogeneous mass with central anechoic areas. The tumor cells exhibited moderate cytonuclear atypia with enlarged hyperchromatic nuclei and prominent nucleoli. The cytoplasm was abundant and eosinophilic. A comparison of the obtained sample with the histopathological and immunohistochemical findings from the patient’s initial duodenopancreatectomy specimen revealed notable differences. The original tumor cells demonstrated moderate atypia, uniform hyperchromatic nuclei, nucleoli, and abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm. The tumor stroma was fibroinflammatory without vascular emboli or perineural invasion. Immunohistochemical staining was negative for chromogranin A, synaptophysin, β-catenin, cyclin D1, and vimentin, excluding neuroendocrine carcinoma and solid pseudopapillary neoplasm (Fig. 6). The lesion was diagnosed as a local recurrence of PACC, which had been initially misdiagnosed due to nonspecific histopathological features. Following a multidisciplinary team discussion, the patient underwent a total pancreatectomy.

Clinical discussion

The pancreas is predominantly composed of exocrine tissue (≈90%), including 85% acinar and 5% ductal cells. Nevertheless, PACC is a rare epithelial malignancy, accounting for only 1% of all pancreatic malignancies. Although it can occur in any part of the pancreas, the pancreatic head is the most common site[3,4]. PACC has a higher prevalence in males and shows a bimodal age distribution, with peaks in childhood (8–15 years) and adulthood (around 60 years)[5–7].

The majority of PACC cases have metastases at diagnosis[8]. The liver is the most common site of metastasis (68%), followed by the peritoneum (19%) and distant lymph nodes (14%)[9]. Elevated lipase levels, frequently associated with PACC, can lead to Shmid’s triad (hyperlipasemia syndrome), which includes fat necrosis, polyarthralgia, and peripheral eosinophilia. This phenomenon is often observed in patients with liver metastases. Furthermore, lipase levels can serve as tumor markers, particularly in surgical candidates, as they may predict therapeutic success[5,6,8,10,11]. In contrast, traditional tumor markers such as Ca19-9 and CEA are usually low or negative in PACC, as seen in this case[7,12,13].

Radiological imaging often misdiagnoses PACC as PDAC, PNETs, or solid pseudopapillary neoplasms (SPNs). Typical imaging findings for PACC include well-circumscribed exophytic masses, often located in the pancreatic head, with cystic areas due to necrosis and occasional calcifications. The median tumor size is 45–54 mm[5,6,14–16]. Local infiltration of the duodenum, large vessels, stomach, kidney, peritoneum, or spleen is found in 45% of cases[17]. Unlike PDAC, which frequently leads to ductal obstruction and is usually invasive, PACC rarely causes ductal obstruction. However, jaundice occurs in only 12% of patients with PACC. In fact, PACC causes a mass effect and displaces those organs but does not infiltrate them, even in patients with pancreatic head tumors[11,18]. Similar to PACC, PB, which is encountered seldomly in adulthood, appears radiologically as a large (ranging from 7–10 cm), circumscribed, and exophytic mass with or without central cystic areas due to necrosis and occasional calcifications; hence, it has a heterogeneous multiloculated appearance in 30% of cases[19].

The tissue structure of PNETs closely resembles that of PACC. However, PNETs typically demonstrate greater enhancement in imaging than PACC due to their hypervascularity. Additionally, non-functioning PNETs may present as well-marginated masses with features such as internal cystic degeneration, necrosis, and hemorrhage. Occasionally, these tumors may exhibit ill-defined boundaries, with or without pancreatic duct dilation and metastasis[13,16]. Additionally, SPNs and PNETs frequently have capsules and calcifications. SPNs, characterized by a combination of solid and cystic components, are more common in young and middle-aged women, particularly for tumors located in the pancreatic tail[16,20,21].

EUS is a valuable diagnostic tool for PACC, which may appear as a hypoechoic, isoechoic, or slightly hyperechoic lesion with lobulated features and potential calcifications. Contrast-enhanced EUS and EUS-elastography findings vary depending on the microscopic structure of the tumor but often show a pattern resembling PDAC[22–24]. PACC is commonly confused with PNETs, which have almost the same age prevalence (40–60 years) and no sex predisposition[25]. On EUS, PNETs are hypo- or isoechoic, hyperenhancing in 77% of cases on contrast-enhanced EUS, with a hard pattern in 61% of cases on EUS-elastography[26].

SPN, a rare tumor predominantly affecting young women, shares clinical similarities with PACC and can arise anywhere in the pancreas, with a slight predisposition (41.5%) for the pancreatic head. It typically presents as a bulky mass with a mean size of 6–8 cm[5,27–29]. While EUS lacks pathognomonic features to establish a definitive imaging diagnosis, endoscopic ultrasound fine-needle aspiration (EUS-FNA) proved conclusive in 97.2% of 103 cases studied by Pawlak et al, demonstrating its efficiency and safety[28]. This diagnostic tool is particularly useful given inconclusive CT/MRI. However, age and gender, in combination with imaging findings, are often sufficient to strongly suggest the diagnosis in many cases[30].

Histologically, PACC is characterized by large, polygonal tumor cells with moderate cytoplasmic vacuolization, irregular nuclear membranes, and prominent nucleoli[31–33] (Table1). Additionally, immunohistochemical staining is a cornerstone for differentiating PACC from other pancreatic tumors. PACC typically stains positively for amylase, trypsin, and chymotrypsin with a high sensitivity (95%)[34], but it is well known that they significantly differ in terms of sensitivity (Table 2). Another antibody directed against the COOH-terminal portion of the BCL-10 protein, which recognizes the COOH-terminal portion of carboxyl ester lipase, an acinar specific product, is a useful tool for detecting ACCs, because of its high specificity and sensitivity in both histological and cytological specimens. In this case, negative staining for markers such as MUC1, MUC2, synaptophysin, and chromogranin A helped to exclude PDAC, SPN, and PNETs, while high Ki-67 levels (80%) confirmed the aggressive nature of the tumor.

Increased MUC1 expression occurs in PDAC with a high expression of MUC1 glycoforms positively modulating the tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis[42,43]. MUC 5AC and MUC2 are never expressed in normal pancreatic tissue, whereas MUC2 is not expressed in PDAC[44].

PACC expresses vimentin and CK7 in 82.1% and 53.8% of cases, respectively, although both markers were negative in our case[14,19]. In contrast, PDAC frequently expresses CK20, which is associated with poorer survival outcomes in patients with R0-resected[45]. Additionally, PDAC is immunoreactive for CK5/6 in 50% of cases, a useful marker for excluding pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinoma in poorly differentiated malignancies with negative staining[46].

On the other hand, PNETs are immunoreactive for endocrine markers chromogranin A and synaptophysin in >75% of tumor cells, as do mixed acinar-endocrine tumors when one component exceeds 30% of the total tumor cell population[47,48]. However, focal labeling for chromogranin A and synaptophysin can also occur in a considerable proportion of PACC, necessitating the use of definitive acinar markers to avoid PACC misdiagnosis as a neuroendocrine tumor or a mixed acinar-neuroendocrine tumor[5]. In our case, staining for chromogranin A, synaptophysin, and CK5/6 was negative.

For SPNs, immunohistochemical staining for cyclin D1 was positive in 50% of cases reported in the literature, although it was negative in our case[49]. A retrospective analysis (1997–2020) of EUS-FNA samples revealed that SPNs were immunopositive for β-catenin, CD10, CD99, and progesterone receptor in 93.7%, 87.5%, 83.3%, and 66.6% of patients, respectively. Tumor cells were immunonegative for chromogranin A in all cases, and Ki67 expression was <2%. These findings align with the largest current SPN review, which analyzed 484 studies involving 2744 patients[20,50]. In our case, immunostaining was positive for CD10 and CD99 – markers consistently expressed in SPNs[51,52]. However, CK7 staining was also positive, which is atypical, as CK7 is either absent or only focally expressed in SPNs[29]. Additionally, β-catenin, a marker with a 100% sensitivity, a 92.3% specificity, a positive predictive value of 0.929, and a negative predictive value of 1 for SPNs[40], was negative in this case. Furthermore, Ki67 expression was 80% in our case, which is inconsistent with the typical diagnostic profile of SPNs. Despite sharing share β-catenin positivity with PB, SPNs do not stain for pancytokeratin or only show focal staining, unlike PB[37].

PACC has frequent aberrant DNA methylation and chromosomal abnormalities with targetable genetic alterations in genes such as BRCA1/2, BRAF, RAF1, NTRK1, ALK-KANK4, PALB2, and MSH2[5,6,59,60]. Genomic studies have demonstrated that PACC rarely has KRAS, TP53, SMAD4, or CDKN2A mutations, which are common in PDAC. Instead, mutations in BRCA1/2 and ATM are significantly higher in PACC (11.4/15.9%) than in PDAC (2.5/3.7%)[61]. Mutations in PALB2 and mismatch repair genes have also been reported, suggesting potential for targeted therapies[6]. PARP inhibitors, ALK inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown promise in cases with specific genetic alterations[62]. Similarly, in a large pan-cancer cohort, a high frequency of BRCA2 mutations (35.5%) was observed, suggesting that PACC should be considered part of the BRCA-related cancers spectrum[63].

Radical surgical resection remains the cornerstone of PACC management for non-metastatic disease[64]. While the role of adjuvant chemotherapy is still debated, emerging molecular data may refine treatment strategies[65]. Chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted therapies may be considered in unresectable or metastatic cases. Misclassification of PACCs as PNETs could lead to favorable bias in survival statistics and may explain the observed discrepancies[66]. PACCs are characterized by high rates of metastasis and recurrence, with recurrence-free survival of 24.5 months for PACC compared to 11.6 months for PDAC (P = 0.023)[67]. Notably, recurrence-free survival was significantly longer (84 months) in our case.

Prognosis is better in females than in males (31 months vs. 20 months, respectively; P = 0.01), and younger age is associated with improved outcomes[64]. However, tumor size is not linked to overall survival[6]. Negative prognostic indicators for PACC may include a high Ki-67 index and lymphovascular invasion[65].

While radical surgery represents an independent positive prognostic factor[64,65], the role of adjuvant therapy remains controversial, with no consensus on its effectiveness[64,68,69]. Consequently, prospective studies are needed to assess the efficacy of various adjuvant regimens in improving outcomes for PACC patients.

To note, repeated pancreatectomy has been shown to improve the survival outcomes in selected patients, potentially in association with neoadjuvant chemotherapy[70]. Conclusions

PACC is a rare and challenging pancreatic malignancy with a high recurrence rate yet a better prognosis than PDAC. Accurate diagnosis often requires EUS-FNA with immunohistochemical staining due to its overlapping clinical and radiological features with other pancreatic tumors. Radical resection remains the primary treatment modality. Moreover, advancements in molecular diagnostics and targeted therapies are expected to play a pivotal role in improving outcomes for patients with PACC.
