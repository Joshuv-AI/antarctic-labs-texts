RRM2 contributes to pathogenic phenotype of fibroblast-like synoviocytes by activating NF-κB signaling and inhibiting ferroptosis in rheumatoid arthritis
Authors: Linqi Zhang, Zhibing Wang, Jingjie Zheng, Qiulin Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe development and pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) are associated with ferroptosis. This study aims to investigate the regulatory role of ribonucleotide reductase subunit M2 (RRM2) in ferroptosis and the pathogenic phenotype of fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLSs) in RA.MethodsTranscriptomic datasets associated with rheumatoid arthritis were analyzed to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), which were then intersected with known ferroptosis-related genes using a Venn diagram to determine overlapping candidates. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was utilized to evaluate the diagnostic value of key genes. The expression of RRM2 was silenced using short hairpin RNA transfection. Cell viability, motility, and invasive capacity were evaluated through the CCK-8 assay, scratch assay, Transwell, and ELISA assay, respectively. Inflammatory cytokines and ferroptosis-associated indicators were quantified using ELISA and specific biochemical detection kits. Additionally, the transcriptional and protein levels of genes linked to FLS function were analyzed.ResultsRRM2 was upregulated in tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α)-induced MH7A cells. Knockdown of RRM2 significantly inhibited TNF-α-induced cell proliferation, migration, invasiveness, and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in MH7A cells. Additionally, RRM2 knockdown induced ferroptosis, as evidenced by increased reactive oxygen species (ROS), ferrous iron (Fe2+), and malondialdehyde (MDA), alongside decreased expression of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11). Further mechanistic analysis revealed that RRM2 led to nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling activation.ConclusionRRM2 inhibits ferroptosis and enhances the pathogenic behavior of RA FLSs through activation of the NF-κB pathway, highlighting its pivotal contribution to RA development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06417-4.

Introduction

As a long-lasting autoimmune disorder, rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is featured by inflammation, mainly targeting the synovial joints and resulting in gradual joint damage, disability, and reduced quality of life [1]. With a global prevalence of approximately 0.5–1%, RA imposes a significant socioeconomic burden [2]. Clinically, RA is characterized by persistent synovitis, systemic inflammation, autoantibody production, and extra-articular manifestations. The synovial membrane becomes hyperplastic, forming pannus tissue that invades cartilage and bone, ultimately resulting in joint deformity [3]. The pathogenesis of RA is multifactorial and involves genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers, and dysregulated immune responses [4, 5]. A key feature of RA pathogenesis is the accumulation of T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, and macrophages that release proinflammatory cytokines. These mediators perpetuate inflammation, leading to joint tissue injury and structural changes [6].

Fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLSs), the primary mesenchymal cells within the synovium, are important in the pathological processes of RA due to their abnormal activation and aggressive behavior. In RA, FLS acquire a transformed phenotype marked by hyperproliferation, resistance to apoptosis, increased migration and invasion, and excessive secretion of inflammatory mediators and matrix-degrading enzymes [7]. These pathogenic features make RA-FLS key contributors to joint damage and chronic inflammation. Emerging evidence suggests that ferroptosis, a regulated, iron-dependent cell death process marked by lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage, is involved in RA pathogenesis [8]. While some evidence suggests that ferroptosis contributes to synovial tissue remodeling and inflammation, the regulatory mechanisms underlying ferroptosis in RA remain poorly understood.

Ribonucleotide reductase subunit M2 (RRM2), a key component of the ribonucleotide reductase enzyme complex, is essential for DNA synthesis and cellular proliferation [9]. Beyond its classical roles, RRM2 has been implicated in modulating ferroptosis, particularly by regulating reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism and antioxidant defenses [10]. Although RRM2 has been studied in RA [11], its specific role in RA and its relationship to ferroptosis in RA-FLS have not been fully elucidated. In addition, nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling plays a fundamental role in orchestrating inflammatory reactions and modulating immune system activity, which is known to be hyperactivated in RA and contributes to FLS activation, cytokine production, and joint destruction [12]. Whether RRM2 interacts with the NF-κB pathway to regulate ferroptosis and RA-FLS pathogenesis has not been previously investigated.

In this study, we hypothesize that RRM2 contributes to the pathogenic phenotype of RA-FLS by modulating ferroptosis via NF-κB signaling. By uncovering the molecular crosstalk among RRM2, ferroptosis, and NF-κB signaling within RA-FLS, our findings may provide novel insights into RA pathogenesis and identify RRM2 as a potential therapeutic target for RA treatment.

Materials and methods

Differential analysis and visualization of RA-related genes

RA-related gene expression was retrieved from GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Two datasets were used: GSE55235, which includes 10 RA synovial tissue samples and 10 normal control (NC) synovial tissue samples, and GSE55457, which includes 13 RA and 10 NC synovial tissue samples. Using GEOquery [13] and limma package [14], data were imported and normalized, followed by differential expression analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened based on the criteria |log fold change (logFC)| >1 and adjusted p-value (adj.P.Val) < 0.05. The ggplot2 package was used to construct volcano plots for visualization of DEGs.

Acquisition of ferroptosis-related genes

Ferroptosis-related genes, encompassing drivers, suppressors, and markers, were retrieved from the FerrDb V2 database [15]. All acquired genes were merged, duplicates removed, and non-coding RNAs excluded to generate a refined list of ferroptosis-related genes for subsequent analysis.

Identification and visualization of ferroptosis-related DEGs

The jvenn online tool [16] was utilized to identify the overlap between DEGs (from GSE55235 and GSE55457) and ferroptosis-related genes, resulting in ferroptosis-associated DEGs. Expression of these genes in RA versus NC synovial tissues were extracted from the datasets. Pheatmap package was used for visualization of gene expression patterns.

Diagnostic value of the target genes

Expression of the gene were extracted from GSE55235 and GSE55457 datasets for both RA and NC samples. Based on the corresponding clinical grouping (RA vs. NC) and using the pROC package in R. ROC curve evaluation was performed, while corresponding AUC was computed to evaluate the gene’s capability to differentiate between groups, with AUC values closer to 1 indicating better diagnostic performance.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the target gene and all other genes in the expression matrices of GSE55235 and GSE55457. Genes showing a correlation coefficient greater than 0.6 in absolute value and a p-value below 0.05 were identified as significantly positively associated with the target gene. These selected genes were subsequently analyzed for pathway enrichment via KEGG using the clusterProfiler package. The enrichKEGG function was used with the species set to “Homo sapiens” and adj.P.Val < 0.05 as the threshold to identify significantly enriched signaling pathways.

Cell culture and grouping

Human RA-FLS cell line MH7A (SC2335; YuchiCell, Shanghai, China) was cultured in DMEM containing 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and 10% FBS (Gibco), with media changed every 2–3 days, at 37 °C in 5% CO₂.

Based on previous studies [17, 18], 40 ng/mL TNF-α was applied to stimulate MH7A cells for 24 h to establish an RA-FLS model. Lentiviruses encoding negative control shRNA (shNC) and RRM2 (shRRM2) were purchased from Yunzhou Biotech (Guangzhou, China; Table 1). MH7A cells were grouped as the NC group (untreated MH7A cells); the TNF-α group (MH7A cells treated with 40 ng/mL TNF-α for 24 h); the TNF-α + shNC group (MH7A cells transfected with shNC for 24 h, followed by 40 ng/mL TNF-α stimulation for another 24 h); the TNF-α + shRRM2-1/2/3 group (MH7A cells transfected with shRRM2-1/2/3 for 24 h, then stimulated with 40 ng/mL TNF-α for 24 h); and the TNF-α + shRRM2 + Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) group (MH7A cells transfected with shRRM2-3 for 24 h, then stimulated with 40 ng/mL TNF-α and 0.5 µM PMA for 24 h each). PMA (HY-18739) was obtained from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

MH7A cells exposed to different experimental conditions were harvested, and total RNA was extracted utilizing TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in accordance with the supplier’s instructions. The quality and quantity of the isolated RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was generated from total RNA through reverse transcription using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara, Japan), following the manufacturer’s recommended procedure. Quantitative PCR was carried out with SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (Takara) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). Gene expression was standardized using GAPDH as an internal reference control and calculated using the Comparative Ct method. Primer sequences are provided in Table 2.

Western blot (WB)

Cells were disrupted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) (ST506, Beyotime) and centrifuged at 4 °C to collect total protein from the supernatant. Protein concentration was determined, and 30 µg of protein was loaded and resolved on 10% SDS-PAGE gels, followed by transfer onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The membranes were then blocked using 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 to 2 h before being incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies as anti-RRM2 (A3424, ABclonal), anti-GPX4 (67763-1-Ig, proteintech), anti-xCT (A13685, ABclonal), anti-phospho-NF-κB p65 (S536) (AP1294, ABclonal), anti-NF-κB p65 (80979-1-RR, proteintech), anti-IκBα (A19714, ABclonal), and anti-GAPDH (A19056, ABclonal), all at dilution of 1:1000. After primary incubation, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h: goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) (ZB2301, 1:10000, ZSGB-BIO, Shanghai, China) or goat anti-mouse IgG (ZB2305, 1:10000, ZSGB-BIO). Protein bands were studied using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and grayscale intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software. GAPDH was used as the loading control.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)

The viability of MH7A cells subjected to various treatments was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. MH7A cells were seeded at 5 × 10³ cells per well in a 96-well plate and cultured for 24 h to allow adherence. Each well was then incubated with complete medium containing CCK-8 reagent (C0038, Beyotime) for 2 h. With a microplate reader, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured.

Scratch assay

Following different treatment conditions, MH7A cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured until they achieved over 90% confluency. A linear wound was then created across the cell monolayer using a sterile pipette tip to initiate the scratch assay. Images were taken at 0 h and 24 h, and wound width was measured using ImageJ to calculate cell migration rate.

Transwell

Cell invasion was evaluated using Transwell chambers (pore size 8.0 μm, Corning, New York, USA) that were pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). A total of 1 × 10⁵ pretreated MH7A cells were suspended and added to apical chamber, while the basolateral chamber was filled with 20% FBS-contained medium. Following an incubation period of around 24 h, a cotton swab was used to eliminate the non - invading cells present on the upper surface. The cells that had invaded were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime) and then stained with crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime). Subsequently, the number of invaded cells was counted by observing them under a microscope.

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (D6470, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Following treatment, MH7A cells were loaded with DCFH-DA (10 µM) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Post-incubation, cells were washed thrice void of light to prevent photobleaching. Fluorescence microscopy was performed to visualize and capture images. ImageJ software was utilized for quantitative analysis of relative fluorescence intensity.

Measurement of ferrous ion (Fe2+) and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels

MH7A cells were cultured overnight to allow adherence. Following experimental treatments, the culture medium was removed, and cells were rinsed twice with PBS. A suitable volume of lysis buffer was added, followed by sonication of the samples on ice. Afterward, the lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min to obtain the supernatant. Cellular Fe2+ and MDA levels were determined according to the manufacturer’s instructions using commercial kits (Fe2+: A039-2-1; MDA: A003-4-1; both from Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

ELISA

The supernatants of treated MH7A cells were collected to assess the levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-6 (PI325, Beyotime) and IL-1β (PI305, Beyotime) using human ELISA kits. The absorbance was detected at 450 nm with a microplate reader.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 9.0. Statistical evaluation between two experimental groups was conducted using the Student’s t-test, while comparisons involving more than two groups were analyzed through one-way ANOVA. A p-value below 0.05 was deemed to indicate statistical significance. Each experiment was carried out independently no fewer than three times to ensure reproducibility.

Results

Identification of overlapping genes between RA DEGs and ferroptosis-related genes (FRDEGs) in RA

Two RA datasets, GSE55235 and GSE55457, were obtained from the GEO database, and differential expression analysis was performed using the “limma” R package. Based on the criteria of adj.P.Val < 0.05 and |logFC| >1, a total of 1,057 (618 upregulated, 439 downregulated) and 370 (190 upregulated, 180 downregulated) DEGs were identified, respectively (Supplementary Table 1). Volcano plots clearly displayed the DEGs between RA and NC groups (Fig. 1A). A total of 420 ferroptosis-related genes were obtained from the FerrDb V2 database (excluding non-coding genes; Supplementary Table 1). By intersecting the RA-related DEGs with ferroptosis-related genes, nine FRDEGs were identified: GABARAPL1, DDR2, EGR1, JUN, RRM2, ZFP36, CDKN1A, NR4A1, and IDO1 (Fig. 1B). Using the “pheatmap” package, the expression of these 9 FRDEGs were visualized. The heatmap displayed dysregulated expression of all these genes in synovial tissues from RA patients. Notably, RRM2 and IDO1 were distinctly upregulated in RA patients, while the others were significantly downregulated. The expression trends of RRM2 and IDO1 were consistent across both GSE55235 and GSE55457 datasets, and sample groupings were clear, suggesting these genes may play important roles in ferroptosis mechanisms in RA (Fig. 1C). We further focused on RRM2, which was highly expressed in RA, and constructed ROC curves to evaluate its diagnostic value. As shown in Fig. 1D, the AUC values for RRM2 in the two datasets were 1 and 0.869, respectively, indicating strong diagnostic performance and potential as a biomarker.

RRM2 knockdown inhibits TNF-α-induced abnormal proliferation and inflammatory response in MH7A cells

Expression of RRM2 in RA was subsequently validated. An in vitro RA model was established by stimulating MH7A cells with TNF-α to mimic inflammatory conditions. RRM2 expression was assessed by qRT-PCR and WB. Results showed that, compared to the NC group, RRM2 was upregulated in TNF-α-stimulated MH7A cells (Fig. 2A/B, p < 0.001), which indicates that RRM2 may be critical for TNF-α-induced synovial cell activation.

To explore the function of RRM2 in TNF-α-induced MH7A cell behavior, RRM2 was knocked down using shRRM2. qRT-PCR and WB were employed to validate knockdown efficiency. Among the three shRNAs tested (shRRM2-1, -2, and − 3), shRRM2-3 showed the most effective knockdown (Fig. 2C/D, p < 0.05) and was selected for subsequent experiments. In RA cell model with RRM2 knockdown, we assessed the pathogenic phenotype, including proliferation, migration, invasion, and inflammation. The results demonstrated that RRM2 silencing significantly reduced cell proliferation in TNF-α-induced MH7A cell (p < 0.0001, Fig. 2E). Scratch assays showed a significant reduction in wound healing rate by over 40% (p < 0.0001, Fig. 2F). Transwell assays confirmed that cell invasion capacity was also significantly reduced (p < 0.001, Fig. 2G/H). Additionally, proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-1β levels were decreased in the shRRM2 group (p < 0.01, Fig. 2I/J). These findings suggest that silencing RRM2 inhibits TNF-α-stimulated proliferation, migration, invasion, and the release of inflammatory cytokines in FLS.

RRM2 knockdown induces ferroptosis in RA synovial cells

Previous studies have shown that RRM2 suppresses ferroptosis in cancer [19]. To determine whether RRM2 exerts a similar inhibitory role in RA synovial cells, we measured markers of ferroptosis after RRM2 knockdown in TNF-α-stimulated MH7A cells. Knockdown of RRM2 significantly reduced the expression of GPX4 and SLC7A11 (p < 0.001, Fig. 3A-E), while increasing intracellular Fe2+ (p < 0.0001, Fig. 3F), MDA (p < 0.001, Fig. 3G) and ROS (p < 0.0001, Fig. 3H/I) levels. These changes are consistent with ferroptosis activation, suggesting that RRM2 knockdown induces ferroptosis in RA FLS.

RRM2 knockdown inhibits NF-κB signaling pathway activity

To investigate the biological pathways potentially regulated by RRM2 in RA, Pearson correlation analysis was performed between RRM2 and all genes in the GSE55235 and GSE55457 datasets. Genes with |r| >0.6 and p < 0.05 were used for KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. As shown in Fig. 4A/B, the top 20 enriched pathways associated with RRM2-related genes were identified. Notably, 22 pathways were consistently enriched across both datasets, involving immune cell differentiation, inflammatory signaling, and infection responses. These include hematopoietic cell lineage, Th17/Th1/Th2 cell differentiation, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, JAK-STAT, NF-κB, and chemokine signaling pathways. The Rheumatoid arthritis pathway was also significantly enriched, implying a direct connection of RRM2 to RA pathogenesis. Enrichment of virus-related pathways (e.g., Epstein-Barr virus infection, Herpes simplex virus 1 infection, and Human T-cell leukemia virus 1 infection) suggested potential involvement of antigen presentation and immune activation mechanisms. Pathways such as PD-L1/PD-1 and p53 further indicated roles in immune escape and cell cycle regulation. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that RRM2 promoted RA FLS pathogenicity and inhibited ferroptosis via activation of NF-κB signaling. WB results supported this hypothesis, showing that RRM2 knockdown significantly decreased phosphorylated p65 (p-p65)/p65 ratio and increased IκBα levels (p < 0.001, Fig. 4C), indicating suppressed NF-κB signaling activity.

RRM2 knockdown induces ferroptosis and suppresses FLS pathogenicity by inhibiting NF-κB signaling

To validate whether RRM2 functions through NF-κB signaling in RA synovial cells, we treated MH7A cells with both shRRM2 and the NF-κB activator PMA. WB analysis revealed that RRM2 knockdown reduced p-p65/p65 ratio and increased IκBα expression, confirming inhibition of NF-κB signaling. However, co-treatment with PMA reversed these effects, elevating p-p65 levels and reducing IκBα (p < 0.0001, Fig. 5A), indicating reactivation of the NF-κB pathway. Subsequently, shRRM2 reduced MH7A cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and inflammatory cytokine (IL-6, IL-1β) secretion, while promoting ferroptosis (evidenced by increased ROS, MDA, Fe2+ levels and decreased GPX4, SLC7A11 expression). These effects were significantly reversed by PMA co-treatment, which restored cell proliferation (Fig. 5B), migration (Fig. 5C), invasion (Fig. 5D/E), and inflammatory cytokine levels (Fig. 5F/G), and inhibiting ferroptosis (evidenced by decreased ROS (Fig. 5H/I), MDA (Fig. 5J), Fe2+ (Fig. 5K) levels and enhanced GPX4, SLC7A11 expression) (Fig. 5L-N). In conclusion, RRM2 knockdown induces ferroptosis and inhibits the pathogenic phenotype of RA FLSs by suppressing NF-κB signaling. These findings provide mechanistic insights and a theoretical basis for RRM2 as a potential therapeutic target in RA.

Discussion

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death characterized by lipid peroxidation, has emerged as a significant player in the pathogenesis of RA [20]. In RA, elevated iron levels and oxidative stress within the synovial microenvironment contribute to the induction of ferroptosis in various cell types, including FLSs and macrophages [21]. This process exacerbates synovial inflammation and joint destruction by promoting inflammation [22]. In our study, we uncover a novel role of RRM2 in the pathogenesis of RA, thereby adding a novel mechanistic link between ferroptosis regulation and chronic synovial inflammation in RA.

At the beginning of this study, we identified RRM2 as a key gene upregulated in RA synovial tissues through bioinformatics analysis of datasets GSE55235 and GSE55457. This finding aligns with previous research demonstrating elevated RRM2 expression in various malignancies, including glioblastoma [23], breast cancer [24], and retinoblastoma [25], where RRM2 contributes to disease progression and poor prognosis. Our functional assays revealed that RRM2 knockdown in FLSs significantly suppressed cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and inflammation. These results are consistent with studies indicating that RRM2 promotes oncogenic phenotypes in various diseases and that its inhibition can alleviate disease progression [9]. Notably, RRM2 silencing has been shown to reduce the pathogenic behavior of RA-FLSs [26], which is also in line with our results. Our data further demonstrated that RRM2 knockdown attenuated inflammatory responses in RA-FLSs, as evidenced by decreased levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. These cytokines are well-established mediators of inflammation in RA [27]. Coincidentally, previous study reports that RRM2 expression is elevated in clinical RA samples and synovial cells stimulated with TNF-α and IL-1β, while silencing RRM2 suppresses MH7A cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [11], further supporting its role in RA pathogenesis. Importantly, we observed that RRM2 silencing induced ferroptosis in RA-FLSs, characterized by enhanced ROS, MDA, and Fe2+, along with reduced GPX4 and SLC7A11 levels. These markers are indicative of ferroptotic cell death and have been associated with ferroptosis in RA [28]. Our results indicate that RRM2 is crucial for RA pathogenesis by linking its expression to FLS pathogenic phenotypes, inflammatory responses, and ferroptosis.

Mechanically, our study showed that RRM2 knockdown inhibited NF-κB signaling pathway activity in RA. Previous research has established a connection between RRM2 and NF-κB signaling. For instance, overexpression of RRM2 has been shown to promote invasiveness in gastric cancer cells via the AKT/NF-κB pathway [29]. Furthermore, RRM2 has been implicated in enhancing cellular invasiveness in a NF-κB-dependent manner [30]. NF-κB signaling is critically involved in the development of RA, particularly in FLSs [31]. Activation of NF-κB in these cells leads to increased proliferation, resistance to apoptosis, and production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, contributing to synovial hyperplasia and joint destruction [32]. Specifically, we observed a significant decrease of p-p65/p65 ratio and increase of IκBα, indicative of NF-κB signaling suppression. Our findings align with existing knowledge about NF-κB signaling: in the canonical pathway, IκBα binds to the p65 subunit, retaining NF-κB in the cytoplasm and preventing its transcriptional activity. Upon stimulation, IκBα is phosphorylated and degraded, allowing p65 to translocate to the nucleus and activate target genes [33]. The p-p65/p65 ratio serves as an indicator of NF-κB activation, with increased phosphorylation correlating with enhanced transcriptional activity [34]. By demonstrating that RRM2 knockdown led to decreased p-p65/p65 ratio and increased IκBα levels, our study suggested that RRM2 positively regulated NF-κB signaling in RA-FLSs.

In summary, this study highlights RRM2’s effects on modulating inflammation and pathogenic behavior in RA. However, there are several limitations in our study. (1) The molecular interactions between RRM2 and components of the NF-κB signaling pathway were not fully delineated, leaving the direct regulatory mechanisms unclear. (2) We did not assess the proliferation, migration, invasion, or ferroptosis of MH7A cells without TNF-α stimulation as a control for the TNF-α–induced RA model, which limits a more comprehensive evaluation of RRM2 function and its underlying mechanisms. (3) Although shRRM2 transfection exhibited only a modest knockdown efficiency in TNF-α–induced RA-FLS, significant functional alterations were still observed. Several explanations may account for this phenomenon. First, it is possible that RRM2 levels partially recovered to baseline after knockdown; however, since we did not monitor RRM2 expression at multiple time points following transfection, this cannot be definitively concluded. Second, RRM2 is highly expressed in RA-FLS, and although knockdown did not completely reduce it to a “low” level, even a partial reduction from a high baseline may be sufficient to disrupt intracellular metabolic and signaling homeostasis. Furthermore, NF-κB signaling represents a classic amplification cascade, whereby modest changes in upstream regulators can be amplified to produce profound functional consequences. These factors may collectively explain why limited RRM2 knockdown still led to marked cellular phenotypic changes in our study. (4) Although our in vitro findings in MH7A cells provide strong evidence, further validation using primary RA-FLSs and in vivo RA models is necessary. In future research, we aim to explore the upstream regulators of RRM2 in RA, dissect its direct binding partners in the NF-κB pathway, and evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of targeting RRM2 in animal models of RA. Systematic investigations into the role of RRM2 in RA will help to advance its potential clinical application. Evidence indicates that siRNA represents a highly selective therapeutic strategy that specifically targets pathological inflammatory processes. On the one hand, siRNA can be employed to identify genes and molecules implicated in RA pathogenesis; on the other hand, it can serve as a precise therapeutic intervention [35]. Therefore, siRNA-based approaches may hold considerable promise for facilitating the clinical translation of RRM2-targeted therapies in RA.

Conclusion

This study systematically elucidates the pathogenic role of RRM2 in RA-FLSs. We found that RRM2 expression was significantly upregulated in TNF-α–stimulated RA-FLSs. Knockdown of RRM2 inhibited cell viability, migration, invasion, and proinflammatory cytokine secretion, while inducing ferroptosis. Mechanistically, RRM2 exerted its pathogenic effects at least in part by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway. Collectively, our findings identify RRM2 not only as a key regulator of ferroptosis in RA-FLS but also as a critical driver of their proinflammatory and pathogenic phenotype.
