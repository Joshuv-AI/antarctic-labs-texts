Human imprints on global riverfronts
Authors: Fanxuan Zeng, Chunqiao Song, R Iestyn Woolway, Linghong Ke, Jida Wang, Dongmei Feng, George H Allen, Peirong Lin, Kai Liu, Tan Chen, Pengfei Zhan, Chenyu Fan, Hui Zhang, Lin Huang, Tamlin M Pavelsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRivers are profoundly shaped by human activity along their water-land interfaces (riverfronts), yet the global distribution and drivers of these imprints remain poorly understood. Here we present a high-resolution global map of 7.52 million kilometers of riverfronts using satellite imagery and deep learning. We find that nearly 20% of global riverfronts are anthropogenically modified, primarily by agriculture (13.43%) and built-up areas (6.29%). A distinct modification belt spans parts of Africa and Eurasia, accounting for ~60% of global alterations, with imprint densities six times higher than elsewhere. This lateral fragmentation represents a pervasive human pressure that differs fundamentally from dam-induced longitudinal fragmentation, and is closely linked to deteriorating water quality and biodiversity threats. Environmental constraints and region-specific socio-economic dependencies further shape these spatial patterns. Our findings inform riverfront management and underscore the urgent need to reconcile development with the ecological integrity of these transitional zones.Subject terms: Hydrology, Geomorphology, Geography, Water resources

Introduction

Rivers are a crucial component of Earth’s water cycle, supporting both human societies and natural ecosystems1–3. For millennia, civilizations have flourished along rivers, such as those of the Nile, Indus, Yellow River, and ancient Mesopotamia, relying on them for irrigation, transportation, trade, and energy4–6. Today, rivers remain central to global food and energy security, supplying between 1900 and 3800 km3 of water annually for irrigation7,8 and generating over 16.6% of the world’s electricity9. Beyond their economic significance, rivers play a vital ecological and hydrological role, linking terrestrial and marine systems. They provide habitat for an estimated 13% of all known species10 and transport roughly 40% of global land precipitation11, along with substantial portions of organic carbon12, nitrogen, and phosphorus13 to the oceans. Culturally, rivers define nearly one quarter of all international boundaries14, shape migration patterns15, and have influenced the rise and fall of civilizations16–20. These diverse roles underscore both the status of rivers as socio-ecological keystones and the urgent need to monitor and manage them in the face of escalating human pressure.

Satellite-based remote sensing now allows for systematic monitoring of global river systems, bypassing the logistical challenges of in situ data collection and the political barriers to transboundary data sharing. Recent studies have mapped river extents and channel belts, revealing widespread morphological and hydrological changes linked to dam construction, land-use change, and climate variability1,21–28. Approximately 20% of global rivers show signs of channel shifting23, and human infrastructure has disrupted the free-flowing status of nearly two-thirds of global rivers2,23. Such changes alter not only flow regimes but also biogeochemical cycles29, influencing carbon fluxes12, nutrient retention30,31, and greenhouse gas emissions32. While dams have been extensively evaluated as a primary stressor, anthropogenic pressures on river systems are multifaceted, encompassing pollution, water transfers, and sediment dredging33. Existing assessments disproportionately focus on factors acting directly on the river itself, paying limited attention to adjacent riverfront zones and the dynamic transition areas between aquatic and terrestrial systems. These zones are critical socio-ecological interfaces: they not only host human settlements and agricultural activity but also buffer aquatic ecosystems from land-based impacts34–36. For example, agricultural expansion near riverbanks degrades water quality via nutrient and pesticide runoff13,37, while vegetated buffers can filter pollutants and stabilize soils38–41. Consequently, any restoration framework that overlooks anthropogenic stresses in the lateral dimension is fundamentally incomplete, as reconciling these cross-scale stressors is essential for the holistic recovery of riverine ecosystem functions. Despite their importance, comprehensive global-scale assessments of riverfronts remain limited. Existing broad-scale land cover datasets offer invaluable insights but face inherent resolution constraints when capturing the fine-scale heterogeneity and dynamic inundation characteristics of riverfronts. Similarly, conventional pixel-based mapping methods encounter challenges in fully delineating complex spatial patterns, primarily due to localized landscape variations and inconsistent segment definitions42. This has limited our ability to assess human modification and ecological function in these critical zones.

Here, we address this gap by developing a deep-learning framework to produce a globally consistent, high-resolution map of riverfront landscapes. We introduce the Global Riverbank rOUting Dataset (GROUD), which delineates the maximum inundation extent for rivers wider than 30 m, covering 7.52 million kilometers of river length. Using 200,000 hand-labeled 2 m-resolution image patches (Supplementary Fig. 1), we train a convolutional neural network (CNN) to classify riverfronts into six categories: artificial, agricultural, wooded, grassy/tundra, semi-barren, and barren (see “Methods” and Supplementary Text, Table 1). Applying the model to nearly 8.7 million scenes of meter-level satellite imagery, we provide a spatially explicit assessment of human imprints and natural heterogeneity across global riverfronts. Our results reveal geographic hotspots of anthropogenic modification and the influence of hydroclimatic and socioeconomic gradients, offering a basis for managing riverine transitions under global change.

Results

Global patterns of riverfront landscape categories

We classified global riverfronts into six categories at a 1-km river segment size (Fig. 1a), achieving an overall classification accuracy of 93% (see Supplementary Text, Tables 2 and 3 for accuracy assessment). Wooded riverfronts are the most prevalent globally, accounting for 43.27% of riverfront length (95% confidence interval: 41.29–45.58%). They form five major global hotspots: the Malay Archipelago (76% of 2.62 × 105 km riverfronts), the Amazon Basin (74% of 1.03 × 106 km riverfronts), the Congo Basin (59% of 2.55 × 105 km riverfronts), Scandinavia-Siberia in Eurasia (57% of 1.71 × 106 km riverfronts), and Alaska-Newfoundland in North America (51% of 7.43 × 105 km riverfronts). Together, these five regions comprise 75.50% of the global wooded riverfront extent (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Their distribution shows two distinct latitudinal peaks at 50°–70° N and 0°–10° S, corresponding to extensive taiga (42.09% of global wooded riverfront) and rainforest (17.85%), respectively (Fig. 1). Grassy/tundra riverfronts make up 14.95% of riverfronts (95% confidence interval: 13.86–16.43%) and exhibit strong zonal patterns. Over half (56.45%) occur in the Arctic, with additional concentrations across the Eurasian steppe (15.08%), African Savanna and Veld (6.11%), South America’s pampas (2.79%) and llanos (2.47%), and North American prairies (2.24%) (Supplementary Fig. 2b). These areas are typically shaped by permafrost, low temperatures, or arid conditions that restrict tree growth, favoring grass/tundra-dominated riverfronts.

Semi-barren riverfronts represent 16.03% of the global total (95% confidence interval: 13.97–18.29%) and often occur in transition zones between vegetated and arid landscapes. Notable regions include the southern margin of the Sahara and the East African Highlands (15.14%), the Brazilian Plateau and Andes (8.48%), Central Asian Drylands (13.46%), the Great Basin in North America (7.35%), and Australia’s Great Artesian Basin (2.94%) (Supplementary Fig. 2c). These zones are shaped by complex interactions among climate aridity, riparian topography, and hydrological inputs. Barren riverfronts, comprising 6.03% of global riverfronts (95% confidence interval: 5.48–6.46%), are mainly confined to cold or arid high-altitude regions. The Arctic hosts 29.52% of the global barren total, while the Tibetan Plateau contributes 27.33% (Supplementary Fig. 2d), driven by harsh conditions, such as permafrost, low insolation, and poor soil fertility.

In contrast to these natural landscape types, artificial and agricultural riverfronts are predominantly found in mid-latitude human-dominated regions. Agricultural riverfronts constitute 13.43% (95% confidence interval: 12.31–14.03%) and artificial riverfronts 6.29% (95% confidence interval: 6.08–6.39%) of the global total. Both types are highly concentrated between 10° N and 50° N, together accounting for over three-quarters of their respective global totals (76.74% and 78.03%). The southern half of Asia alone hosts 56.73% of artificial and 61.00% of agricultural riverfronts. Other key regions include the contiguous United States (15.71% artificial, 8.90% agricultural) and Western-Central-Southern Europe (9.36% and 8.63%, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 2e, f). The Eastern Hemisphere overwhelmingly dominates in both categories, containing 76.15% of global artificial and 78.45% of agricultural riverfronts. A particularly dense band between 75° E and 130° E contains nearly half of global artificial (48.32%) and agricultural (48.41%) riverfronts (Fig. 1).

One-fifth of global riverfronts are imprinted by human activities

To support analyses of riverfront management and ecological services, we grouped the six riverfront categories into three primary classes: human-imprinted (artificial and agricultural), well-vegetated (wooded and grassy/tundra), and poorly-vegetated (semi-barren and barren) (Fig. 2). Globally, 60.51% of river basins (HydroBASINS Level 4) exhibit a human imprint, defined as having over 1% of riverfront length classified as artificial or agricultural, spanning 66.58% of the global land surface (excluding Antarctica). Among these human-affected basins, 28.69% are dominated by artificial riverfronts (exceeding agricultural riverfronts in length), while 71.31% are dominated by agricultural riverfronts. These correspond to 16.56% and 50.02% of the Earth’s land surface, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3b). In total, we identified 192 highly human-imprinted basins, in which artificial and agricultural riverfronts together exceed 50% of total riverfront length. These basins, covering 12.58% of the global land area, are concentrated in a contiguous belt we term the “low- and mid-latitude Afro-Eurasia corridor (LAEC)”. Spanning North Africa, Europe, West Asia, South Asia, and East Asia (Fig. 2b), the LAEC alone contains 60.34% of the world’s human-imprinted riverfronts. Within this corridor, artificial and agricultural riverfronts account for 18.48% and 41.33%, respectively, roughly three times the global average and six times the proportion found outside the LAEC.

River basins that hosted early civilizations, including the Indus Valley, Yellow River, Mesopotamia, and Nile River, still show high degrees of human imprint, with 52.14%, 50.80%, 48.03%, and 19.58% of riverfronts classified as artificial or agricultural, respectively (Fig. 1b). These basins are predominantly agricultural in character, with agricultural-to-artificial ratios (length ratios of agricultural to artificial riverfronts), an indicator of the relative dominance of diffuse agricultural modification over localized artificial infrastructure, ranging from 2.74 to 4.11, notably higher than the global average of 2.14. However, several modern basins now surpass ancient centers in the extent of human-imprinted riverfronts. These include the Godavari-Krishna-Mahanadi (77.20%), Elbe-Rhine-Loire (75.76%), Ganges-Brahmaputra (66.93%), Danube (59.13%), Yangtze (57.79%), Xi (57.37%), and Yalu-Han (56.12%) (Fig. 1b). Socioeconomic trajectories also influence patterns of riverfront use: for example, the Volga, Xi, Savannah River-St. Johns River, and St. Lawrence basins are characterized by more artificial than agricultural development, with agricultural-to-artificial ratios ranging from 0.24 to 0.88 (Fig. 1b). At a national scale, countries that hosted ancient civilizations, including Iraq (66.90%), Pakistan (63.87%), China (45.10%), and Egypt (39.75%), continue to have high levels of human-imprinted riverfronts, with agriculture as the dominant use (Fig. 1c). Other countries, such as Madagascar (agricultural-to-artificial ratio: 20.76), Nigeria (9.14), South Africa (7.37), and Afghanistan (6.54), similarly show strong dominance of agricultural riverfronts. In contrast, Japan and Ukraine are dominated by artificial riverfronts, with respective agricultural-to-artificial ratios of 0.76 and 0.94. Conversely, basins with minimal human imprint, defined as having more than 50% well-vegetated or poorly-vegetated riverfronts, cover 42.77% and 21.06% of the Earth’s land surface, respectively (Fig. 2c, d).

Multifaceted drivers of human imprints on riverfronts

Natural, cultural, and economic drivers have long shaped human settlement along rivers, leaving distinct imprints on riverfront landscapes. To quantify this relationship, we defined “riverfront dependency” as the ratio of the human footprint index (HFI, a composite metric of land use, population density, and infrastructure43) within the riverfront zone to the national average (see “Methods” and Fig. 3). Our results reveal a spatially uneven distribution of this dependency (Fig. 3a–c). In arid regions, such as North Africa, West Asia, and Central Asia, acute water stress has historically concentrated human activity along rivers, a pattern reinforced by the legacy of river-based civilizations. Topography further modulates this reliance; in Japan, the Kanto and Osaka plains provide optimal conditions for urban expansion, intensifying riverfront dependency. In these high-dependency countries, HFI declines monotonically with increasing distance from the river (Fig. 3d). Conversely, in countries like Brazil and Peru, HFI often increases away from river systems, reflecting both a strategic pivot toward coastal industries and an adaptation to high-discharge44 or flood-prone45 inland environments.

Heightened water demand is closely associated with riverfront dependency, as population and infrastructure are often clustered near scarce aquatic resources. This relationship is most pronounced in regions with a significant “water gap”, the deficit between water supply and demand (Fig. 3e). Countries that hosted ancient river valley civilizations, including Pakistan (35.8 km3/yr), China (27.2 km3/yr), Iraq (23.8 km3/yr), and Egypt (18.8 km3/yr), face critical water shortages that are strongly linked to irrigated agriculture, which accounts for approximately 90% of total water use46. Notably, the LAEC accounts for 68% of the global water gap, totaling 311.1 km3/yr. This increasing dependency is strongly linked to landscape transformation: there is a significant positive correlation (r = 0.60) between national riverfront dependency and the proportion of human-imprinted riverfronts (Fig. 3f). Such patterns reflect a form of optimal self-organization, in which societies strategically expand into riverfront areas to exploit natural and geographic advantages47. However, future climate scenarios suggest that widening water gaps, particularly within the LAEC, may further amplify these dependency patterns46,48. At the same time, escalating risks, such as sea-level rise, droughts and floods, are expected to drive divergent trajectories in riverfront development, largely shaped by each nation’s capacity to invest in large-scale water management and protective infrastructure47,49.

Human imprints drive widespread fragmentation of riverfront landscapes

Human imprints, particularly agriculture and infrastructure development, have fragmented natural riverfront landscapes, disrupting once-continuous corridors of vegetation or barren land. To quantify this fragmentation, we use a continuity index (CI), which ranges from 0% (complete fragmentation) to 100% (full continuity) (see “Methods”). Globally, fragmentation patterns closely align with hotspots of human-imprinted riverfronts (Fig. 4a), with human activity reducing the average CI to 53.77%. Agriculture emerges as the dominant driver of this fragmentation. River basins dominated by agricultural land use show markedly lower continuity (average CI of 45.43%) compared to those dominated by artificial infrastructure (67.36%) (Supplementary Fig. 3c). In heavily cultivated basins, such as the Ganges-Brahmaputra, Mississippi, and Yangtze, continuity declines steeply to 18.09%, 16.29%, and 5.54%, respectively. In contrast, basins with minimal human impact (<6% imprint), such as the Yenisei (80.95%), Congo (77.93%), and Amazon (74.12%), maintain relatively intact riverfront landscapes (Fig. 4b). Fragmentation is especially severe in the LAEC, where human activity has reduced the regional average CI to just 13.54%, starkly contrasting with the global average of 53.77% (Fig. 4c). This extreme decline is indicative of the intense human imprint in the region, further highlighting the variability in fragmentation across different basins. Notably, the spatial configuration of human activities, not just their total extent, plays a key role in determining fragmentation. For instance, although the Ganges-Brahmaputra has a higher proportion of human-imprinted riverfronts than the Yangtze (66.93% vs. 57.79%), its more spatially concentrated development results in a higher CI, indicating less fragmentation overall.

Human activities impose multifaceted impacts on river systems, with the effects of lateral fragmentation, driven by human imprints on riverfronts, proving to be far more significant than those of longitudinal fragmentation, which is primarily influenced by dams and infrastructure development (Fig. 5a). Our analysis reveals that hotspots of lateral fragmentation affect approximately 17.62% of the global land area, nearly double the extent of longitudinal fragmentation (9.31%), highlighting the widespread influence of human-induced alterations to riverfronts (see “Methods” and Fig. 5b). This disparity is less pronounced in agriculturally dominant regions, such as the Indus and Yellow River basins, where lateral and longitudinal fragmentation occur simultaneously across 4.09% of the global land area. In these basins, the dual requirements for water storage and irrigation, coupled with extensive riverfront exploitation for agriculture and aquaculture, drive simultaneous longitudinal and lateral alterations50. The impact of lateral fragmentation extends across a river length 3.6 times greater than that affected by longitudinal fragmentation (Fig. 5c, d). This disproportionate extension, relative to the twofold difference in land area, reflects a fundamental divergence between river network architecture and human utilization patterns. Specifically, it suggests that lateral modifications are concentrated within more intricate, higher-order river networks that are inherently conducive to human development. This phenomenon is especially acute in larger river systems, where riverfront alterations disrupt ecological and hydrological functions more severely than in smaller reaches. Consequently, the synergistic pressures of lateral and longitudinal fragmentation, exacerbated by agricultural expansion and water management strategies, pose a critical threat to global river resilience and biodiversity.

Environmental and ecological consequences of human-imprinted riverfronts

The transformation of riverfront landscapes offers numerous advantages and social benefits, contributing significantly to economic growth and societal well-being8,16. The use of these areas for agricultural cultivation supports food production8, while the construction of ports and shipping routes facilitates trade and transportation51, driving regional and global commerce. Moreover, riverfront transformation often leads to improved infrastructure and enhanced access to vital resources. However, the regulation and development upon river systems incur an ecological price, primarily through the alteration of hydrologic and sediment mass fluxes. By disrupting the natural ecosystem’s ability to transport matter and energy, these modifications impair geomorphological stability and fundamentally reshape the biogeochemical cycles that sustain healthy aquatic and riparian habitats.

Riverfront landscapes play a critical role in regulating hydrological processes by retaining sediment, modulating flood dynamics, and shaping water flow through bio-geomorphic interactions (Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). Our analysis highlights that regions with low erosion rates, such as the Congo Basin and Siberia, are closely associated with intact, well-vegetated riverfronts, underscoring the importance of natural riparian vegetation in stabilizing riverbanks and maintaining geomorphological resilience45,52. In contrast, widespread human-driven fragmentation has significantly disrupted these natural systems, reducing the global average CI to just 53.77%. Among anthropogenic drivers, agriculture emerges as a more potent cause of fragmentation than urbanization. River basins dominated by agricultural riverfront landscapes have an average CI of 45.43%, substantially lower than the 67.36% observed in basins dominated by artificial (impervious) surfaces. This pressure is especially acute in major agricultural hubs, such as the Ganges-Brahmaputra (CI, 18.09%) and Mississippi (CI, 16.29%), where landscape fragmentation disrupts both lateral and longitudinal connectivity, diminishing sediment regulation and exacerbating flood risks53–55.

Anthropogenic transformation of riverfront landscapes alters water quality by disrupting the buffering, filtering, and retention functions of riparian zones at the land-river interface (Supplementary Fig. 5d). Conversion of vegetated riverfronts to agricultural fields, aquaculture ponds, and engineered surfaces fragments lateral continuity and reduces the capacity of riverfront soils and vegetation to intercept dissolved salts, nutrients, and organic matter before they enter river channels38. In addition, riverfront modification often involves bank reshaping and direct discharges associated with irrigation return flows and aquaculture effluents, leading to elevated solute loads and organic pollution, as reflected in increased total dissolved solids (TDS)56,57 and biochemical oxygen demand (BOD)58. The former represents increased solute loads, whereas the latter reflects enhanced organic inputs that stimulate microbial decomposition and oxygen consumption. Beyond TDS and BOD, water quality degradation associated with riverfront transformation is also reflected in elevated nutrients (e.g., nitrogen and phosphorus) and pesticide concentrations13. We find a strong spatial correspondence between hotspots of human-imprinted riverfronts, especially within the LAEC belt, and documented zones of river eutrophication and pesticide contamination (Supplementary Fig. 6). Consistent with this pattern, rivers within the LAEC exhibit significantly higher TDS and BOD concentrations than those outside the region (Fig. 6a, b). Similar patterns are observed in basin-scale comparisons, such as between the Indus and Yangtze basins, where a higher proportion of agricultural riverfronts (41.94% vs. 31.81%) coincides with elevated TDS and BOD levels despite differences in overall human imprint intensity (CI of 23.07% vs. 5.54%) (Fig. 6a, b). At the global scale, basins characterized by extensive riverfront modification and damming exhibit substantially higher TDS and BOD levels than near-natural basins. Notably, basins dominated by riverfront transformation show higher TDS and BOD levels than those dominated primarily by dam-induced fragmentation (Fig. 6c, d). A basin-scale control analysis further shows that the positive association between lateral fragmentation and both TDS and BOD persists after accounting for hydroclimatic, topographic, land-use, population, and basin-size covariates (Supplementary Fig. 7), supporting the interpretation that riverfront transformation is a robust correlate of water-quality degradation.

The consequences extend beyond water quality to biodiversity loss and habitat degradation59 (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Riverfronts serve as natural corridors that support species migration, foraging, breeding, and genetic flow across populations35,60. Since the 1970s, global populations of freshwater vertebrates have declined by more than 80%, and biodiversity has dropped in over 50% of the world’s rivers60,61. Despite the establishment of conservation frameworks, such as the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) protected area management categories, our results reveal substantial gaps in protection. We estimate that 7% of all protected riverfronts are already heavily modified by human activity, with this figure rising to 63.82% in Japan and 39.57% in Europe, underscoring the limited effectiveness of current protection strategies62. In this context, 2639 amphibians and 2854 freshwater fishes worldwide are severely threatened (classified as critically endangered [CR], endangered [EN], or vulnerable [VU], excluding regions without rivers) (Fig. 6e). This conservation gap is most acute in the LAEC, where conservation efforts are minimal: while 8.81% of global riverfronts are protected, only 0.92% of those in the LAEC fall under protection (Supplementary Fig. 8). This neglect directly contributes to the severe threat facing 34.02% of amphibians and 29.01% of freshwater fishes within the LAEC, totaling 1152 species (Fig. 6e). Our analysis further reveals differential taxonomic vulnerabilities: amphibians are primarily threatened by lateral riverfront transformation, with threat prevalence 8.80% higher than that of fishes in laterally dominated regions. In contrast, freshwater fishes exhibit higher susceptibility to longitudinal damming, with threat levels 4.44% above those of amphibians in longitudinally fragmented areas. When these anthropogenic stressors coincide at the intersection of lateral and longitudinal imprints, the proportions of threatened amphibians and fishes escalate to 20.73% and 16.60%, respectively (Fig. 6e). This finding reveals a critical nexus between riverfront landscape transformation and ecological degradation. Furthermore, these results suggest that traditional assessments that often omit riverfront dynamics profoundly underestimate anthropogenic pressure. It demonstrates the utility of our riverfront classification for systematically evaluating human impacts on freshwater environments.

Discussion

Rivers have long served as the foundation of civilizations, providing water for communities, enriching soils for agriculture, and supporting livelihoods and ecosystems. This deep-rooted cultural, economic, and ecological value has driven the widespread development of human infrastructure on riverfronts, reinforcing a strong global dependency on riverfronts. Our analysis shows that this dependency has transformed nearly 20% of global riverfronts through agriculture, urbanization, rural settlements and industrial expansion. These human imprints are especially concentrated in the LAEC, where riverfronts have become highly altered landscapes. The remaining approximately 80% of riverfronts are dominated by natural land cover types, including wooded areas (43.27%), grasslands and tundra (14.95%), semi-barren (16.03%), and barren landscapes (6.03%). These natural riverfronts show distinct spatial clustering patterns shaped by regional hydroclimatic and geomorphological conditions. Characterizing these patterns of riverfront transformation is essential for regulating hydrological processes, mitigating flood risks, and preserving ecological authenticity and integrity in river basins worldwide. Differentiating the typology and extent of human imprints facilitates the formulation of targeted strategies for sustainable management, habitat restoration, and climate resilience planning. Furthermore, our globally consistent classification provides a valuable resource that can support efforts toward advancing the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals, especially Goal 6 (clean water and sanitation), Goal 13 (climate action), and Goal 15 (life on land). By elucidating the spatial intensity of anthropogenic pressures, this framework supports the development of basin-scale, context-sensitive strategies that harmonize socioeconomic development with the ecological carrying capacity of fluvial systems. Ultimately, this research advocates for a shift from the unilateral exploitation of rivers to a model of co-evolution, ensuring the long-term sustainability of their vital functions.

Methods

Our methodology consists of five main steps (Supplementary Fig. 9). First, we developed the GROUD by extracting the river waterline from 10-m resolution Sentinel-2 satellite images to precisely define the riverfront zone at the water-land transition areas, with two-round rigorous quality control procedures of manual inspection and editing segment-by-segment. Second, we delineated the global riverfronts, identified the classification units by river segment, and established the classification scheme for different riverfront categories. Third, we extracted high-resolution (~2 m) image patches corresponding to each riverfront unit for subsequent classification using deep learning. Fourth, we prepared training samples, trained our deep learning classification model, and then applied it to classify high-resolution image patches across all global riverfront zones. Finally, based on the classification results, we performed spatial pattern analysis, landscape fragmentation evaluation, and a riverfront dependency assessment.

Development of the global riverbank routing dataset (GROUD)

To identify global riverfront zones, we developed the GROUD, which delineates the water-land boundary at maximum observed inundation for global rivers using multi-year Sentinel-2 imagery (Supplementary Fig. 10). River planforms and inundation extents were derived exclusively from Sentinel-2 observations, while existing river network datasets were used only to provide spatial constraints and improve extraction stability. We processed all available Sentinel-2 MSI observations from 2019 to 2021 using the Dynamic World dataset, which classifies valid pixels into water and eight non-water classes for each scene63. For each pixel, we calculated water occurrence frequency as the ratio of water observations to valid observations over the 3-year period (Eq. 1)64,65. Following established practice, a 10% water-frequency threshold was applied to delineate river water extent at maximum observed inundation66,67. This multi-year approach minimizes mapping noise caused by cloud contamination and extreme hydrological events and provides a representative snapshot of riverfront conditions around 202066. To ensure reliable mapping at the native 10-m resolution of Sentinel-2, our analysis was limited to river reaches wider than 30 m (approximately three pixels), as narrower channels cannot be consistently resolved. Small internal holes (e.g., ephemeral islands or bars) with areas less than 10 km2 were filled to enhance spatiotemporal stability and to focus the analysis on persistent geomorphological features. SWORD and GRIT datasets were incorporated only as spatial constraints to guide the identification of river channels, not to define river geometry. For exorheic basins (those that drain to the ocean), the SWORD dataset was used to constrain the spatial domain within which Sentinel-2 derived water pixels were interpreted as river channels68. For endorheic basins (those that do not drain to the ocean), where many rivers/streams have a width of less than 100 m and are not well represented in the Landsat-based SWORD data, we employed the GRIT dataset69, a valley line dataset derived from a 30-m DEM that does not necessarily represent inundation, as a reference to constrain the locations of endorheic rivers66. Lakes and reservoirs identified in either the SWORD or GRIT datasets were retained as part of the river network.

The extracted river water masks were manually verified by comparing them with high-resolution imagery from Google Earth and Esri. The quality control procedure primarily addressed four issues (Supplementary Fig. 10): (1) incorrect retention of non-river objects near the river masks, (2) river segments split by grid boundaries, (3) misclassification of hill-shaded areas, and (4) misclassification of flooded regions as river features. Areas identified as falling within one of these categories were removed from the water masks. After this quality control, the validated river masks were merged to achieve global coverage. Finally, by extracting the outlines of the global river mask, we delineated a total of 7.52 million kilometers of riverbanks on both sides of global rivers.

Global riverfront delineation and classification system

The GROUD was divided into 1-km line segments, which served as the basic unit for riverfront classification in this study (see Supplementary Text, Fig. 11). However, the precise delineation of riverfronts in waterline buffering width can vary across different research scopes and geographic regions34,70 (Supplementary Table 4). Previous studies have considered optimal riverfront widths of 100, 200, and 300 m, up to as much as 1000 m71–77. By testing the proportions of various land cover types across different buffer widths in different hydrologic and geographic regions worldwide by random sampling, we found that when the buffer zone exceeds 500 m, the proportions of land cover types tend to stabilize, and the residual sum of squares approaches zero (Supplementary Fig. 12). Based on this analysis, we consistently delineated a 500-m buffer zone as a compromise setting for the riverfronts in this study. A sensitivity experiment also demonstrated that a variation of up to 15% in the extent of the delineated riverfront zone does not systematically affect the classification results (Supplementary Fig. 13).

Our primary objective was to investigate the spatial distribution of human imprints along riverfronts, specifically focusing on agricultural and artificial surface development, and to understand their associated ecological and environmental impacts. Crucially, the types and densities of vegetation along riverfronts can mitigate the ecological impacts of human imprints, including soil erosion, river pollution, and habitat degradation38. To explore this, we developed a classification system for global riverfronts, as shown in Supplementary Table 1 and Fig. 14. The system comprises six categories: artificial riverfronts (dominated by man-made structures), agricultural riverfronts (extensively developed for farming), wooded riverfronts (naturally wooded areas), grassy/tundra riverfronts (natural grassland or tundra areas), semi-barren riverfronts (characterized by sparse vegetation), and barren riverfronts (areas with little to no vegetation).

High-resolution image patch clipping and sampling for riverfront classification

To classify global riverfronts, we acquired high-resolution imagery from Esri and Google Earth. Global high-resolution imagery is provided as RGB color patches and is accessible via a Web Map Service API. All high-resolution images were acquired during the period of 2018–2023 (circa 2020). We mosaicked the original patches and employed a sliding window strategy to clip image patches along the riverbanks represented in GROUD (Supplementary Fig. 15). Each image patch was cropped to 256 × 256 pixels at an approximate spatial resolution of 2 m. To ensure global representativeness, these patches were randomly selected from regions encompassing a wide array of climates, vegetation types, and land cover across the globe. Each collected patch was then manually labeled based on meticulous visual interpretation (Supplementary Fig. 1). Labels were assigned at the patch level rather than the pixel level, based on the overall characteristics of each patch. This patch-based approach facilitates the identification of meaningful objects, such as directly recognizing riverfront communities instead of isolated pixels categorized as “built-up area” or “vegetation”. In total, we amassed a dataset of 194,393 sample patches, with their global distribution shown in Supplementary Fig. 16. To balance the spatial representativeness and mapping complexity, the breakdown of these labels is as follows: 40,988 patches were classified as artificial riverfront, 38,186 as agricultural riverfront, 27,225 as wooded riverfront, 17,437 as grassy/tundra riverfront, 28,393 as semi-barren riverfront, and 36,695 as barren riverfront. Additionally, 5469 patches were identified as being contaminated by clouds or snow.

Of the labeled image patches, 80% were used for classifier training and the remaining 20% for testing. To further balance the dataset and increase the number of samples, we applied data augmentation techniques by randomly rotating the images by 90°, 180°, 270°, or 360°, and then randomly flipping the rotated images78. Given the inherent scale invariance of river systems, these augmentations preserve the characteristics of the landscape that define the riverfront while enhancing the model’s ability to predict across different scales. After augmentation, the number of training and validation patches for each category reached 50,000, while the number of testing patches for each category reached 10,000.

Global riverfront classification based on deep learning

To classify global riverfronts, we evaluated six CNN architectures and selected the best-performing model (Supplementary Fig. 17). The evaluated CNN architectures included DenseNet79, EfficientNet v280, Inception v381, MobileNet v382, NasNet83, and ResNet v284. During the training phase, 80% of the image patches were randomly assigned to the training subset, with the remaining 20% for validation. Each CNN model was trained for 100 epochs with a batch size of 16 until convergence was achieved (Supplementary Fig. 18). The Adam optimizer with default settings was employed for gradient-based optimization of the stochastic objective function, allowing for suboptimal local decisions during the search process85. All experiments were conducted using TensorFlow 2.6 and the Keras 2.6 deep learning library. The initial learning rate was set to 0.001, and the ReduceLROnPlateau module in Keras was used to dynamically adjust the learning rate.

We evaluated the performance of our trained riverfront classifiers independently using dedicated testing patches, assessing them with a confusion matrix (Supplementary Figs. 19 and 20, Table 2). Key evaluation metrics included overall accuracy (OA), Kappa coefficient, user’s accuracy (UA), and producer’s accuracy (PA). Among these, OA and Kappa were used to identify the best-performing model for global riverfront classification, while UA and PA were employed to assess the model’s accuracy across different categories. Finally, the EfficientNet v2 B3 model performed best and achieved the highest OA (93.19%) and Kappa coefficient (0.92).

Globally, 8,742,977 high-resolution riverfront image patches were automatically interpreted and assigned to their respective categories using the EfficientNet v2 B3 model. If an image patch was initially classified as invalid (e.g., due to cloud or snow cover), we first attempted to switch the data source between Esri and Google Earth. If the image remained invalid, we adjusted the image tile level to obtain a lower-resolution version, then applied resampling to maintain a consistent image size of 256 × 256 pixels. This process was repeated until all invalid images were successfully assigned to valid categories. Globally, a total of 24,207 invalid image patches were detected, representing 0.28% of all observations, with hotspots primarily in high-latitude and sparsely populated regions. Experimental evaluation further demonstrated that the use of degraded imagery resulted in only a minor reduction in model performance (overall accuracy decreased by 1.7% and Kappa by 0.02) (Supplementary Fig. 21). Finally, the classified image patches were matched with the 1-km line segments derived from GROUD to determine global riverfront categories and their spatial distribution (see Supplementary Text for accuracy assessment).

Estimation of river longitudinal fragmentation by damming

To quantify global longitudinal connectivity, we adopted the degree of fragmentation (DOF) metric established by Grill et al.2, which characterizes the reach-scale disruption of river systems by anthropogenic barriers. This indicator utilizes the Global Reservoir and Dam Database (GRanD) and Global geOreferenced Database of Dams (GOODD) datasets to pinpoint the locations of approximately 20,000 dams, integrated with the HydroSHEDS global hydrographic network. The DOF methodology assumes that the impact of a barrier propagates upstream and downstream based on the similarity in natural average discharge between the dam site and the affected reaches. This relationship is modeled through a logarithmic decay function, wherein the magnitude of fragmentation diminishes as the discharge of a given reach diverges from that of the barrier location. By accounting for natural discontinuities, such as waterfalls, to bound the extent of impact, the DOF provides a robust, discharge-based assessment of how infrastructure fundamentally partitions the global river network.

Building upon this reach-level foundation, we derived a basin-wide fragmentation index for each HydroBASINS Level 5 unit to align with our socio-economic and ecological analyses. First, the reach dataset was filtered to include only primary river segments by reserving records where the inclusion field was set to “INC = 1”, thereby ensuring the exclusion of redundant or minor drainage features2. To characterize the collective longitudinal pressure within each catchment, we calculated a length-weighted mean DOF. By weighting the DOF values of individual river segments by their respective lengths, we ensured that longer, more prominent stretches of the river network contributed more significantly to the final indicator. This approach effectively normalizes the longitudinal stressor across varying basin geometries, providing a standardized spatial metric that reflects the cumulative physical extent of fragmentation within global river basins.

Hotspots of bi-dimensional fragmentation patterns

To delineate the global epicenters of river system disruption, we performed a bi-dimensional spatial cluster analysis to identify basins dominated by longitudinal and lateral fragmentation. Using the hotspot analysis (Getis-Ord Gi*) tool implemented in ArcGIS, we identified statistically significant spatial concentrations of anthropogenic impact, where longitudinal fragmentation is driven by damming and lateral fragmentation stems from human-imprinted riverfronts. We defined hotspots as basins where high fragmentation values are clustered with statistical significance at a 95% confidence level, characterized by an absolute z-score exceeding 1.96 (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Assessment of water quality and biodiversity consequences

To quantify the environmental and ecological consequences of human-imprinted riverfronts, we integrated global riverfront classification results with independent water-quality and biodiversity datasets. All analyses were conducted at the HydroBASINS Level 5 spatial scale to ensure consistency across datasets while retaining basin-level heterogeneity.

River water quality was characterized using TDS and BOD, two widely applied indicators representing complementary dimensions of hydrochemical degradation. TDS reflects dissolved solute loads associated with salt mobilization, mineral dissolution, and anthropogenic inputs, whereas BOD indicates the oxygen demand generated by microbial decomposition of organic matter. TDS and BOD data were obtained from the DynQual global river water quality dataset (2019), which provides modeled monthly concentrations for major rivers worldwide based on a harmonized integration of observational records, watershed attributes, and process-based simulations. The DynQual dataset has been validated against in situ measurements, showing strong agreement between simulated and observed concentrations and robust performance in reproducing large-scale spatial gradients and basin-level variability in river water quality88. For each HydroBASINS Level 5 unit, annual mean TDS and BOD concentrations were calculated by averaging values across all river reaches intersecting the basin. Basins were subsequently grouped according to dominant fragmentation type (lateral, longitudinal, or combined) and the intensity of human-imprinted riverfronts.

To assess the robustness of the observed relationships between riverfront transformation and water quality to potential confounding factors, we conducted a basin-scale control analysis using a machine-learning approach. The analysis was performed at the HydroBASINS Level 5 scale. For each basin, we compiled a set of explanatory variables describing lateral fragmentation, longitudinal fragmentation, and key hydroclimatic, topographic, land-use, and demographic conditions. Hydroclimatic variables included mean annual precipitation, runoff, and aridity (defined as the ratio of precipitation to potential evapotranspiration) derived from the TerraClimate dataset (2020). Topographic conditions were represented by the mean slope derived from the Multi-Error-Removed Improved-Terrain (MERIT) DEM. Population density was obtained from the Gridded Population of the World dataset. Land-use variables included cropland and artificial surface proportions derived from ESA WorldCover (2020), and basin area was calculated from HydroBASINS geometries. Skewed variables (TDS, BOD, population density, and basin area) were log-transformed to reduce the influence of extreme values, and all predictors were standardized to enable comparison of effect sizes. Random forest regression models were applied to account for potential nonlinear relationships and interactions among variables. Model performance was evaluated using cross-validation, and variable importance was quantified using permutation importance metrics. To further interpret model behavior, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were computed to quantify the marginal contribution of each predictor to model outputs. Because riverfront modification, dam construction, climate, geology, and upstream pollution may covary spatially, these relationships should be interpreted as statistical associations rather than direct causal effects.

Biodiversity impacts were assessed using species-level threat data from the IUCN Red List, focusing on freshwater amphibians and freshwater fishes, which are taxa with strong ecological dependence on riverfront habitats. Species records were filtered to include only those with confirmed presence (presence = 1), native origin (origin = 1), and relevant seasonal occurrence (seasonal = 1 or 2). Species counts were aggregated to the HydroBASINS Level 5 scale, with each species counted once per basin. Threat status was classified according to IUCN categories: CR, EN, and VU. Basins were grouped by dominant fragmentation type to evaluate differences in taxon-specific vulnerability under lateral versus longitudinal human pressures.

While the analyses quantify statistical associations between human-imprinted riverfronts, water quality indicators, and biodiversity patterns, we emphasize that the relationships identified are correlational. Interpretation is guided by established eco-hydrological theory regarding riparian buffering, land-river material exchanges, and habitat connectivity, but no unidirectional causal pathways are inferred from the statistical analysis alone.

Quantifying the level of riverfront dependency

We propose a metric, the riverfront dependency level (RDL), to quantify the extent to which human activities are concentrated around rivers. Intuitively, in regions with high riverfront dependency, human activities are typically clustered near rivers, while in areas with low dependency, activities tend to occur further afield. To quantify this riverfront dependency, we calculated the difference in human activity intensity between the riverfront zone and the broader region. We derived human activity intensity from the human footprint index (HFI) dataset, a composite metric that incorporates factors such as built environments, cropland, pasture land, human population density, night-time lights, railways, roads, and navigable waterways43. Estimating uncertainties in global riverfront classification

Inaccuracies in riverfront classification primarily resulted from the spectral similarity between different riverfront categories and the heterogeneity within the same class. To mitigate this issue and enhance the reliability of our global estimates, we provided confidence intervals for the length of each riverfront type, based on the accuracy assessment of the classification model. A resampling strategy was employed to estimate confidence intervals for riverfront lengths across all categories89. Bootstrapping was performed by resampling the test samples over 1,000 iterations.
