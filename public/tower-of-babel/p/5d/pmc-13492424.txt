Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic Syndrome Staging and Cancer Risk
Authors: Xixi Xiao, Jiayong Li, Yu Ning, Yunyao Yang, Zhen Ou, Bin Dong, Yugang Dong, Yi Li, Chen Liu, Yilong Wang, Ruicong Xue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is an emerging integrative framework and a critical determinant of overall disease risk; however, its relationship with cancer risk and underlying mechanisms remains poorly understood.ObjectivesThis study sought to investigate the association between CKM staging and incident cancer and elucidate mediators underlying this association, thereby uncovering pathways and potential targets for risk mitigation based on multiomics analysis.MethodsUK Biobank participants were categorized into CKM stages 0 to 4 according to the American Heart Association staging framework based on International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes. Cox regression was conducted to evaluate associations between CKM staging and overall cancer incidence. Proteomic and metabolomic mediators linking adjacent stages and cancer were identified using Cox regression, logistic regression, and mediation analysis and underwent Gene Ontology enrichment and interaction network analyses.ResultsOverall cancer risk increased stepwise from CKM stages 1 to 3, with slight attenuation but persistent elevation in stage 4. Stage-specific mediators and mechanisms were identified: leukocyte/lymphocyte activation and cell-cell adhesion in stage 1; T cell–related immunity and emerging immune tolerance induction in stage 2; emerging natural killer (NK) cell tolerance induction and respiratory burst involved in the inflammatory response in stage 3; and dominant NK cell tolerance induction and tissue-resident chronic pathology in stage 4. Dominant metabolomic mediation evolved from high-density lipoproteins (HDLs) and triglyceride-rich lipoproteins (TRLs) to low-density lipoproteins (LDLs). Protein-metabolite interaction networks revealed distinct profiles for each stage, including angiopoietin-like protein 1/HDL and asialoglycoprotein receptor 1/TRL interactions in early stages and phospholipid transfer protein/HDL, apolipoprotein M/LDL, and fibroblast growth factor–binding protein 1/intermediate-density lipoprotein/LDL correlations in advanced stages.ConclusionsCKM staging is associated with incremental cancer risk and stage-specific immune-metabolic pathways, highlighting potential prevention targets.

Introduction

Cancer, cardiovascular disease, metabolic disorders, and renal disorders are leading causes of mortality worldwide.1 Accumulating evidence indicates these conditions often coexist and interact synergistically.2 Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome, a novel concept proposed by the American Heart Association (AHA), emphasizes the interaction between cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and metabolic disorders, such as diabetes and obesity, and their intertwined effects on organ dysfunction and adverse clinical outcomes.2 This integrative framework provides an important tool for early identification and risk stratification.

Previous studies have established individual links between components of CKM syndrome—cardiovascular disease, CKD, and metabolic disorders—and increased risk of cancer.3, 4, 5 However, these studies did not fully reflect the synergistic or cumulative effect of CKM as a syndrome on cancer risk, which requires further investigation. In addition, the mechanisms connecting CKM staging with cancer incidence are poorly understood. Therefore, using a large-scale prospective cohort with extensive omics data, we aimed to clarify this association and uncover pathways and potential targets for risk mitigation.

Methods

Participant selection and data source

The UK Biobank recruited more than 500,000 participants between 2006 and 2010. Baseline data were collected at recruitment, and health-related outcomes were obtained through linked electronic health records. The study protocol was approved by the North West Research Ethics Committee (reference: 11/NW/0382). We excluded participants with a cancer history at recruitment or who developed cancer within 1 year after recruitment to minimize reverse causation, leaving 448,323 participants for analysis (Figure 1A). The database provided proteomics (Olink Explore 3072 Proteomics platform; 2,923 proteins) and metabolomics (Nightingale Health high-throughput nuclear magnetic resonance platform; 249 metabolites). Outcome definition

Overall cancer incidence was defined as the first diagnosis of any of 15 common site-specific cancers more than 1 year after recruitment, according to International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes (Supplemental Table 3): esophageal, stomach, small intestine, colorectal, anal, pancreatic, liver, gallbladder, respiratory system (including lung, bronchus, trachea), urinary tract (including kidney, ureter, and bladder), nervous system, thyroid, breast, multiple myeloma, and other non–multiple myeloma hematologic malignancies.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared across CKM stages. Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using one-way analysis of variance; normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Non-normally distributed variables are described as median with 25th to 75th percentiles (Q1-Q3) and were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables are described as frequency and percentage and were compared using the chi-squared test. Missing values were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with five imputations.

We assessed associations between CKM stages and overall cancer incidence using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models and Gray regression models, with death considered a competing risk. Model results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. To address potential reverse causation, we performed sensitivity analyses by excluding participants who developed cancer within 2 years and 5 years after baseline, using the same analytical procedure. Given the controversial effects of statins on cancer risk, we performed a subgroup analysis by statin use to assess the consistency of the CKM-cancer association. To compare the discriminative ability of CKM staging vs its individual components, Uno's C-index was used (Supplemental Table 4). To assess longitudinal changes, we analyzed participants with a first follow-up visit using time-integrated metrics calculated from baseline and follow-up CKM stages—mean stage, cumulative stage (sum of the two), and weighted cumulative stage (70% weight to follow-up, 30% to baseline)—and evaluated their associations with cancer risk. All Cox proportional hazards models were adjusted for potential cancer-related confounders, including age, sex, current smoking, current alcohol use, family history of cancer, walking for longer than 10 minutes at least 4 days per week, Townsend deprivation index, aspirin use, and lipid-lowering therapy.

We identified cancer-associated proteins using Cox proportional hazards models with the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Stage-associated proteins between adjacent stages (1 vs 0, 2 vs 1, 3 vs 2, 4 vs 3) were identified using logistic regression models with the BH procedure. Proteins associated with both CKM staging and cancer underwent mediation analysis using the R mediation package with nonparametric bootstrapping (1,000 simulations) to estimate average causal mediation effects (ACMEs), average direct effects, total effects, and proportions mediated (Figure 1B). Proteins with an indirect-effect P value of less than 0.05 (FDR-adjusted ACME) were considered significant mediators and underwent Gene Ontology enrichment analysis. The same procedure was applied to metabolomics. BH-FDR correction was applied within each set: all proteins and metabolites for cancer associations and separately within each adjacent-stage comparison for stage associations and mediation analysis. We assessed mediator retention rates by repeating the mediation analyses in the study group excluding cancers diagnosed within 2 and 5 years after baseline.

To elucidate the interactive landscape between proteomic and metabolic mediators, integrated protein-metabolite networks were constructed for each adjacent-stage comparison. Spearman correlation analysis with BH correction was performed between all protein-metabolite pairs within each comparative set. To identify the main module, the following thresholds were applied: |ρ| > 0.15 for stage 1 vs 0 and |ρ| > 0.25 for the other comparisons. The main interaction module within each network was identified by extracting the subnetwork connected to the top five hub nodes ranked by degree centrality.

Statistical analyses were performed using R statistical software, version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, 2025), and Stata, version 17 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX). A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Participants were distributed across CKM stages 0 to 4 as follows: 12.4%, 12.3%, 48.7%, 18.6%, and 8.0%, respectively (Table 2). Compared with stages 0 to 2, stages 3 to 4 had older age, higher proportions of men and current smokers, and higher body mass index. Stages 3 to 4 had more adverse metabolic profiles, including higher glucose, hemoglobin A1c, and creatinine levels and lower estimated glomerular filtration rate. Lipid profiles were most dysregulated in stage 3, with the highest total triglyceride levels and the lowest HDL levels, both of which showed relative improvement in stage 4. Total cholesterol peaked at stages 2 to 3 but reached its lowest in stage 4. Consistent with the CKM framework, stage 0 had no CKM health risk factors; stage 1 reflected excess and/or dysfunctional adiposity or prediabetic glycemic measures without CKD, cardiovascular disease, or other metabolic risk factors; participants in stages 2 to 3 had metabolic comorbidities without established cardiovascular disease; and participants in stage 4 had cardiovascular disease. The association between CKM stages and cancer incidence

Among the 448,323 participants (median follow-up: 4.3 years [Q1-Q3: 2.1-5.0 years]), 27,251 developed cancer (incidence rate: 7.092/1,000 person-years). Multivariable Cox regression showed that the overall cancer risk increased stepwise from stages 1 to 3: stage 1 (HR: 1.12; 95% CI: 1.06-1.18; P < 0.001); stage 2 (HR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.15-1.25; P < 0.001); stage 3 (HR: 1.52; 95% CI: 1.44-1.60; P < 0.001), with slight attenuation but persistent elevation in stage 4 (HR: 1.51; 95% CI: 1.42-1.60; P < 0.001) (Table 3, Supplemental Table 5). Each one-stage increment was associated with an increased risk (P for trend < 0.001). Advanced CKM stages (3-4) were associated with a higher risk than early CKM stages (0-2) (HR: 1.30; 95% CI: 1.26-1.34; P < 0.001) (Supplemental Table 5). These associations remained robust in sensitivity analyses excluding participants who developed cancer within 2 or 5 years after baseline (Figure 1A, Supplemental Table 6) and in Gray regression In site-specific analyses, elevated cancer risks in esophageal, urinary tract, and nervous system were evident from stage 1. Risk elevations of most digestive cancers, respiratory cancers, and breast cancer generally emerged from stage 2 onward and peaked at stages 3 to 4 (Table 3). Subgroup analysis revealed that statin use did not modify the association between advanced CKM stages (3-4 vs 0-2) and cancer risk (P for interaction = 0.56), although significant interactions were observed for stages 2 to 4 (Supplemental Table 7).

We further compared the discriminative ability of CKM staging (model 1) against a model using cardiovascular disease, CKD, and metabolic disorders as separate binary variables (model 2) with Uno's C-index comparison (Supplemental Table 4). The C-index of model 2 was significantly lower than that of model 1 (Δ = −0.001413; 95% CI: −0.001415 to −0.001411; P < 0.001). Although this difference was statistically significant, the absolute difference was very small and likely not clinically meaningful.

In longitudinal analysis of 9,474 participants with available CKM staging data at their first follow-up visit, mean CKM stage (HR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.02-1.31; P = 0.022), cumulative stage (HR: 1.07; 95% CI: 1.01-1.14; P = 0.022), and weighted cumulative stage (HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.01-1.29; P = 0.040) were each associated with cancer risk (Supplemental Table 8).

Proteomic mediators linking CKM stage and cancer incidence

We identified 661 cancer-associated proteins and four subsets of stage-associated protein subsets for adjacent-stage comparisons (1 vs 0, 2 vs 1, 3 vs 2, 4 vs 3). Proteins associated with both cancer and CKM stage were candidates for mediation analysis. Discussion

Principal findings

Our principal findings were as follows: 1) The integrated CKM framework was associated with overall cancer risk. 2) Stage-specific immune-metabolic shifts mediated this association: cell-cell adhesion and leukocyte/lymphocyte activation with HDL dysfunction in stage 1; T cell–related immunity and emerging immune tolerance induction with dysregulated triglyceride metabolism, particularly in TRLs in stage 2; emerging NK cell tolerance induction and inflammatory respiratory burst with shift to large HDL in stage 3; and dominant NK cell tolerance induction, tissue-resident chronic pathology with consolidated LDL involvement in stage 4. 3) Core protein-metabolite interactions were implicated: ANGPTL1/HDL in stage 1, ASGR1/TRLs in stage 2, PLTP/HDL and APOM/LDL in stage 3, and FGFBP1/IDL/LDL in stage 4.

Integrated CKM staging framework and cancer risk

Beyond its role in cardiovascular disease risk stratification, CKM staging in our study was associated with a stepwise increase in cancer incidence from CKM stages 0 to 3, with a slight attenuation but persistent elevation in stage 4, in line with a recent UK Biobank study by Huang et al with similar stage distribution (stage 0, N = 44,895; stage 1, N = 40,416; stage 2, N = 235,100; stage 3, N = 3,068; stage 4, N = 27,760).12 Beyond these consistent findings, our study also showed that the integrated CKM framework provided a statistically significant but modest clinically meaningful difference in discrimination compared with individual components, underscoring that these factors may interact to potentiate cancer risk. Furthermore, significant associations with longitudinal CKM metrics suggest that cumulative CKM burden is relevant to oncogenesis, emphasizing dynamic risk management.

Of note, previous studies have reported conflicting results on statin use and cancer incidence,13 making it important to assess whether and how statin use influences the CKM-cancer relationship. In our study, associations remained significant in both statin users and nonusers across all stages, although HRs were consistently higher in statin users, with significant interactions at stages 2 to 4. This pattern may reflect indication bias: Statin users in advanced CKM stages likely represent a more adverse cardiometabolic phenotype, introducing residual confounding that may amplify stagewise associations.

Dynamic immune dysfunction mediating oncogenesis across CKM stages

Huang et al12 primarily identified PI3K/Akt and inflammation-related proteins contributing to stepwise cancer risks across stages 0 to 3. In contrast, our pairwise adjacent-stage mediation analyses involving all stages further uncovered a stage-ordered evolution of immune dysregulation.

In stage 1, adipose tissue dysfunction was associated with lymphocyte- and leukocyte-mediated inflammation, with enhanced cell-cell adhesion facilitating their infiltration.14 Although this may reflect an attempt to manage metabolic stress, it concurrently and potentially creates a proinflammatory niche that supports genomic instability and early tumorigenic transformation.15 In stage 2, T cell–related immunity was prominently and broadly enriched, with concurrent existence of T-cell activation marked by up-regulation of molecules involved in T-cell differentiation and activation, and emerging immune tolerance marked by increased inhibitory receptors (LILRB4, HLA-E, and NECTIN2)16, 17, 18 and exhaustion markers (PDCD1) known to suppress antitumor immunity,19 which indicated T-cell exhaustion. This dual state suggests a responsive adaptation to oncogenic metabolic stress, which subsequently transitions to immune tolerance and exhaustion, disrupting T-cell homeostasis and antitumor capacity.

In stage 3, with subclinical cardiovascular disease and peak cancer risk, mechanisms were multidirectional: Inflammatory respiratory burst may result in oxidative DNA damage that fosters mutagenesis,20 and NK cell–targeted immune tolerance may disable the innate antitumor barrier, compounding T-cell exhaustion initiated in stage 2. In stage 4, with established clinical cardiovascular disease, overall cancer risk was slightly attenuated compared with stage 3, yet mediators enriched in this transition revealed a qualitative shift: NK cell tolerance induction emerged as dominant, driven by mediators such as LGALS9, HAVCR2 (TIM-3), and HLA-E, which deliver inhibitory signals that suppress NK cell cytotoxicity and promote immune tolerance, thereby impairing NK cell–mediated antitumor surveillance.

Meanwhile, microglial migration, aberrant tissue repair, and antifungal responses collectively point to a transition from systemic immune activation toward localized, tissue-specific chronic tissue turnover. This shift may reduce systemic oncogenic drive, whereas persistent localized immune dysfunction sustains residual cancer risk. Notably, 15 universal mediators involved in the activation of multiple immune cells across stages supported chronic immune dysregulation as a common pathogenic thread linking CKM to cancer risk.

CKM stage–specific metabolic rewiring and cross talk with immunity

Mediation in stages 1 and 3 was dominated by HDL-related metabolites, but with distinct profiles. The stage 1 signature encompassed broad HDL components, indicating early generalized HDL dysfunction, which has been linked to oncogenesis.21 ANGPTL1, the key proteomic hub decreased in this stage, is a functional ligand for LILRB2,22 an immune receptor highly expressed on macrophages23 that regulate HDL biogenesis through cholesterol efflux. Based on these established relationships, together with the observed correlation between ANGPTL1 and HDL particles, we hypothesize that reduced ANGPTL1 may impair LILRB2-mediated signaling in macrophages, leading to diminished cholesterol efflux, dysfunctional HDL particles, and subsequent antitumor immunity. However, further studies are required to validate this hypothesis.

By stage 3, the HDL signature shifted to large HDL particles as subclinical cardiovascular disease emerges, and PLTP decreases and becomes the strongest negative hub. Reduced PLTP impairs HDL maturation, yielding large but dysfunctional particles with compromised cholesterol efflux.24 This promotes lipid accumulation and foam cell formation, activating proinflammatory signaling that drives inflammatory respiratory burst, a key feature of stage 3. Notably, PLTP is a p53 target gene with tumor-suppressive properties; its decline thus disrupts both HDL metabolism and direct antitumor capacity,25 linking PLTP-HDL correlation to peak cancer risk in this stage.

The onset of CKD and metabolic dysfunction at stage 2 results in marked triglyceride-related metabolic dysfunction, with observed elevated ASGR1, a regulator of triglyceride metabolism.26 ASGR1 deficiency exacerbates T cell–mediated hepatitis and leads to hepatic CD8+ T-cell accumulation,27 suggesting a role for ASGR1 in limiting T-cell responses. Notably, TRL directly induces a proinflammatory transcriptomic signature in CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.28 Thus, increased ASGR1-mediated T-cell suppression and TRL-induced T-cell reprogramming converge to disrupt T-cell homeostasis, contributing to increased cancer risk. This finding is consistent with the dual state presented in our proteomic analysis in stage 2. On the other hand, elevated carboxypeptidase M might only serve as a circulating marker of the underlying lipoinflammation because it is selectively upregulated in lipid-laden macrophages.29

In stage 3, LDL-related metabolites emerged alongside decreased APOM, whose catabolism is mediated by LDL.30 The enhanced clearance of APOM via LDL in this stage depletes its antitumor mechanism that drives ferroptosis and inhibits glycolysis, contributing to the peak cancer risk in stage 3.31,32 FGFBP1 promotes tumor progression through multiple mechanisms.33,34 Lower FGFBP1 levels in stage 4 may weaken this systemic protumorigenic effect, explaining the attenuated cancer risk, whereas residual risk may be sustained by tissue-resident chronic pathology. Despite its strong correlation with IDL/LDL components in stage 4, there is a lack of direct mechanistic explanation for this connection other than shared coregulation of upstream signals or simply accompanying variation.

Thus, the evolution of these immune-metabolic networks maps a dynamic biological escalation underlying the trajectory of cancer risk across advancing CKM stages.

Biological mediators across cancer types

Site-specific analysis revealed that biological mediators linking advanced CKM stages (3-4 vs 0-2) to cancer risk exhibited tissue specificity. Advanced CKM stages may be associated with non–multiple myeloma hematologic malignancies through a systemic inflammatory microenvironment. Digestive system cancers were associated with extracellular matrix proteins, suggesting disrupted epithelial integrity and tissue remodeling. CKM progression may fuel respiratory cancers through lysosomal and vesicular pathways. Urinary system cancers were characterized by negative regulation of antitumor immunity and monocyte differentiation. Breast cancer displayed a unique protein profile involving complement component C1q complex binding. These findings support the potential value of personalized cancer screening and prevention strategies in patients with CKM, tailored to specific CKM stage and cancer risk profile.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. The predominantly European cohort limits generalizability to other ethnicities. Because direct measures of subclinical cardiovascular disease were unavailable (eg, coronary artery calcium, cardiac biomarkers, echocardiography) or inadequate (urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, which was used for staging in the study by Huang et al 12), stage 3 was defined using the FRS rather than the PREVENT (Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease EVENTs) equations or true subclinical disease. This choice introduced heterogeneity into this stage but avoided sample loss and selection bias, possibly explaining interstudy differences. Moreover, FRS incorporates variables independently associated with cancer risk, including age, smoking, blood pressure, and cholesterol, complicating the biological interpretation of the stage 2 to 3 transition specifically.

Despite mediation analysis, the associations among biological processes cannot be interpreted as causal. Although we focused on 15 common cancers, inclusion of other cancers may reveal additional insights. Residual confounding, particularly from age, which is strongly correlated with both CKM stage and cancer risk, cannot be entirely excluded despite adjustment and may influence the observed risk gradient. For the stage 1 vs 0 comparison, complete loss of metabolomic mediators after excluding cancers within 5 years precludes robust causal mediation inference for the earliest transition, which should be interpreted as reflecting shared biological pathways or putative intermediates rather than definitive mechanisms. Finally, the mediating mechanisms require further validation in experimental and interventional studies.

Conclusions

In this large prospective cohort study, advancing CKM stages were associated with increased cancer risk, and the integrated CKM framework showed a statistically significant but not clinically meaningful difference in discrimination compared with its individual components. These associations were primarily explained by stage-specific immune-metabolic mechanisms: stage 1 by ANGPTL1-related broad HDL dysfunction coupled with cell-cell adhesion and leukocyte/lymphocyte activation; stage 2 by ASGR1-related TRL dysregulation accompanied by T cell–related immunity and emerging immune tolerance induction; stage 3 by LDL-mediated APOM clearance and PLTP-mediated HDL dysfunction alongside inflammatory respiratory burst and immune tolerance induction targeting NK cells; and stage 4 by FGFBP1-correlated IDL/LDL dysregulation with dominant NK cell tolerance induction and tissue-resident chronic pathology (Central Illustration).
