Design and architecting of a broadband bullet-diamond shaped integrated frequency selective meta-surface absorber for aero-stealth platform
Authors: Priyanka, Prashant S Alegaonkar, Himangshu B Baskey
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe work report on architecture of integrated frequency selective meta-surface (IFSMS) absorbers for aerospace stealth applications. Fabricated IFSMS comprised of a pattern metasurface integrated with dielectric interlayer and conducting ground. Initially, a supercell (2 × 2-unit cell: 24 × 24 mm2) was designed with a fourfold topological symmetry. Supercell produces impedances (R), inductances (L), and capacitances (C) in tune with design on its interaction with microwave. RC performance was tested at variable incident transverse electric/magnetic (TE/TM) modes over, Θ, 0°–60° and at the normal incidence (TE), against a planer, clockwise rotation over, Φ, 0°–90°. The mode stability and rotational invariance was analyzed for displacement current- and power-density distributions. The impedance behavior and phase reversal S11 reflection coefficient studies revealed the emergence of mid-band Fabry–Perot mode distinguishing LC behavior of the circuit. The meta-pattern was manufactured by mask lithography using a customized resistive micro-carbon ink and imprinted onto dielectric/ground tile (dimension: 30 × 30 cm2). Structure–property relationship of the ink material was investigated using SEM, XRD, FTIR, UV–visible spectroscopy to reveled surface properties of imprinted material. The absorber was subjected to the free space measurements over C (4–8), X (8–12), and Ku (12–18 GHz) bands, including pristine interlayer dielectrics. The simulated and experimental RC data was found to be in excellent agreement. The proposed IFSMS design is a potential candidate for the stealth application.Keywords: Microwave absorber, IFSMS, Frequency selectivity, Programmable surface, Radar cross-section (RCS), X and Ku bands, StealthSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Physics

Introduction

The echoless response of a flying object at a detection range is stealth. It’s a tactical act in the electronic countermeasures. Military aviation of any nation exploits a microwave electromagnetic (EM) region for the purpose of communication, tracking, and threatening. Among this, the frequency band of 2–18 GHz is typically reserved for the purpose of detection and ranging1. Modern strategies include redirecting the incident beam by either using a sharp geometrical shape of the aero structure or painting it with a radar absorber material (RAM) to evade the target detection. The classical techniques involved the development of a λ/4—thick Salisbury coating or dielectric incorporated Jaumann absorber. However, these techniques, could offer a limited absorption loss and a narrow bandwidth, including constraints on the thickness and weight of the designed absorber2,3. In this context, meta-material-based surface absorbers have, recently, been captivating interest among the researchers due to their tunable EM response.

Metamaterial is an artificially fabricated multi-layered medium that exhibits unique EM properties4. Metamaterials have not only been demonstrated for the microwave region5 but have also been shown their intriguing properties in the spectral range like radio6, millimeter wave7, terahertz8, mid-infrared9, near-infrared10, and even near optical11 regime. Fundamentally, such absorbers involved the architecture of a multi-layered macro-structure comprised of a metal inclusion over layer, dielectric interlayer, and conducting ground. The inclusion, typically, appears like an analog of a solid in which the sub-wavelength building blocks are imprinted periodically (similar to an atomic arrangement) on a host surface. The patterned surface, integrated with the dielectric interlayer and ground, behaves like an effective medium that has a tailored and independent resonance response towards the electric permittivity (ε(ω) = ε1 + iε2) and magnetic permeability (μ(ω) = μ1 + iμ2) functions. Particularly, manipulating ε1 and μ1 could lead to the opening of the EM band gap, emergence of a single, double negative refractive index, a cloaking effect, and extreme control over the incident EM/optical fields12. Whereas tailoring of the imaginary part of ε and μ functions exhibits backward wave propagation, reverse Doppler effect, perfect absorber action, etc.5. Moreover the imaginary part of the causal constitutive functions has a profound impact on the absorption properties of the surfaces. By matching the surface impedance with respect to the free space wave impedance that minimizes the reflectivity of the fabricated meta-surface. Such surfaces are termed as the frequency selective meta surfaces (FSMS).

FSMS, technically, requires architecture and control on numerous parameters like unit cell inclusions, its nature (conductive/resistive), dimensions, inter-cell distance, Bragg reflection characteristics, resonance frequency, loss levels (decibel: dB), bandwidth, its shape, fractional bandwidth @10 dB (~ 90% absorption) value, thickness, structural homogeneity, its response to the geometry of the wave incidence (normal/oblique), polarization, rotational performance, etc. All these parameters, integrated together, lead to the realization of a practical IFSMS.

Landy et al. demonstrated the first meta-absorber comprised of an electric resonator compounded with cut wire backing capable of absorbing power > 88% @ resonance ~ 11.5 GHz with a narrow bandwidth of ~ 4% over 09–14 GHz13. Compound resonance frequency absorber has been demonstrated by Ghosh et al. with an ultra-thin, symmetric, concentric ring, that were polarization insensitive, having bandwidth of ~ 1.15 GHz over ~ 9.50–10.50 GHz compounded with two absorptions peaks @ 9.66 and 10.26 GHz with 96% and 92.5% absorptivity, respectively14. Kundu et al. developed Giusepe Peano fractal arms with polarization insensitivity up to 60° with a bandwidth of 7.5% over 02–14 GHz15. In another study, Jain et al. demonstrated a square-shaped absorber having polarization insensitivity up to 90° and angular stability up to 60° at 3.36, 3.95, and 10.48 GHz. In another study carried out by Jain et al., a T, I, and split Jerusalem cross (SJC) shaped absorbers were proposed with 100% absorption @ 10.19, 9.32, and 9.32 GHz, respectively16,17. In similar fashion, Barde et al. proposed a double square shaped, compact broadband absorber having 90% absorption between 14.44 and 27.87 GHz with 75.8% FBW and absorption between 11.39 and 20.15 GHz with 55.55% FBW respectively18,19. In another study, Ghosh et al. investigated a swastika‐shaped meta-structure printed on dielectric to achieve absorption > 80% with polarization insensitivity > 50°, bandwidth > 0.60 GHz over 08–12 GHz20, whereas multi-layer absorber showed wide-angle polarization insensitivity, with a bandwidth 7.5%@10 GHz21. In order to enhance the bandwidth, Munk et al. designed and simulated a circuit analog (CA) absorber comprised of a conductive inclusion, dielectric interlayer, and conducting backing for implementing a high radar cross-section (RCS) meta-absorber22. In a study, Kundu et al. demonstrated a 2D conductive crossed dipoles array with top-mounted lumped resistor elements on FR4-backed metal having the ability to reduce RCS with thickness < 0.077λL (λL: lowest cutoff frequency), offered 10 dB default reduction compounded bandwidth > 70% over 5.3–11.2 GHz and > 100% over 4–12 GHz23. Nguyen et al. proposed a broadband meta-absorber for X-band applications having a novel design of eight resistive arm cells capable of achieving absorption > 90% up to 60° of TE oblique incidence over 08–12 GHz and 65° for TM over 09–12 GHz24. Sohrab et al. proposed a wideband AC absorber in X-band that consisted of two RO 4003 air-separated layers of 3 mm thick to attain—20 dB @ 40% bandwidth and ~ 80% optimality in frequency response and total thickness25. Shang et al. investigated a single-layer CA absorber having a design of a double square loop array that showed three resonances over the operating frequency band with fractional bandwidth (FBW) > 125% @10 dB with 0.088λL26. However, lump resistor-based CA absorbers have a number of limitations, such as needing more fabrication time, i.e., the soldering resistor process is quite lengthy. The fabrication is expensive and has reliability issues for the onset implementation in space technology.

The alternate methodology implements a resistive ink-based conductive pattern compounded with a lossy interlayer and metal back. In this, Kundu et al. proposed a unit cell design of an ultra-wideband absorber using a printed lossy capacitive surface which possesses a 10 dB reflection reduction @120% FBW by multi-resonance process with thickness 0.080λL, over 03–12 GHz27 and similar type of approach has been adopted by Olszewska-Placha et al.28.

This communication aims to present a novel 2 × 2 supercell design comprised of a network of bullets and diamonds with a superior IFSMS parameter over the reported ones. The proposed absorber is sleek (0.027λL), has a larger polarization insensitivity up to 90°, and offers angular stability till 60° compounded with 150% of FBW and > 90% of absorption losses over 4–28 GHz. The analysis of EM properties, both theoretical and experimental together with their structure–property relationship of the fabricated meta- absorber has been discussed.

Materials and methods

Design and simulations: computational EM

The current study deals with EM simulations based on the finite element analysis technique. The unit cell was simulated using a frequency domain full-wave EM simulator of computer simulation technology (CST) Microwave Studio. For periodicity, floquet boundary conditions were used along x and y direction. Along − Z direction open space condition were applied and from + Z direction the EM signal has been incident. This configuration is kept invariant throughout the simulation. The IFSMS cell architecture, consisted of: (1) a resistive top layer, (2) a dielectric interlayer, and (3) conducting ground.

FSMS: overlayer sheet resistance

The simulations were performed by imposing a periodic floquet boundary condition along the xy-plane where the FSMS unit cell (dimension: ~ 12.0 mm × 12.0 mm) was placed. The incident wave vector was set along the positive z-axis. The computation for reflection loss (RC in dB) was carried out at a constant over layer thickness, ts, 0.02 mm (200 μm), by varying the surface resistance from 200 to 350 Ω/sq. The performance was obtained to achieve an optimum mid-band resonance compounded with a symmetrical RC pattern over 4–30 GHz.

Lossy dielectric laminate and conducting ground: intermediate layer thickness optimization

In order to achieve an Eigen, RC, response, simulations were performed on the Lossy intermediate layers by varying the thickness of a glass fabric-reinforced plastic (GFRP) dielectric from 1 to 3 mm at an interval of 0.5 mm. The conducting ground acts as a physical barrier for microwaves to block the transmission across the IFSMS. The grounding back has been chosen to be copper with electrical conductivity σ, 5–6 × 107 S/m having a thickness of ~ 0.035 mm.

Production sequence of the IFSMS absorber

Inclusion manufacturing: overlayer design

Simulation results provided an input to choose a resistive ink for an optimized Rc value having a sheet resistance of ~ 290 Ω/sq. The designed inclusion pattern was directly imprinted onto a GFRP of 2 mm thick interlayer. The components of absorber such as resistive ink, was customizely fabricated, whereas, items like GFRP coated copper ground were readily available, including their bonding material (epoxy/resin/hardener, make LY 5052, M/s Huntsman, Germany).

Mask lithography and printing

In mask lithography process, the FSMS overlayer was screen-printed onto the front side of a GFRP tile (30 × 30 cm2 having 23 × 23-unit cells) whose rear side was coated with a copper ground. For screen printing, the mask fabric of appropriate mesh/pores and area (preferably tile dimensions) had been attached to a wooden frame in order to hold and make the action of printing easy onset. The screen area through which the printing has been carried out was kept open, and the other portion had been blocked. Subsequently, the prepared screen was positioned onto the tile, and attached squeegee was dragged across the screen. In order to achieve better quality, homogeneous, and uniform imprints, the entire assembly was positioned onto a firm, stationary, and hard platform.

Characterizations of resistive ink material

In order to correlate the response of the resistive ink to the incident microwave field, its structure–property relationship has been investigated at microstructure, micro-crystal, molecular, and electronic levels. The morphological studies have been performed using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, Model: Merlin Compact) compounded with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The vibrational spectroscopic measurements were performed using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, make: Tensor, Model: 27) over a 4000–500 cm−1. The powder X-ray diffraction technique (XRD, make: PANalytical, Model: X’pert) was used to investigate the crystallographic phases formed in the ink admixed with a binder. The X-ray analysis was done with the help of a Cu Kα X-ray source of wavelength 1.54 Å with 2θ variations from 0° to 90° at a step of 0.05°/s. UV absorption spectroscopic (UV–Vis., Biochrome Libra S60PC) measurements were carried out in order to identify the electronic transitions present in the ink matrix.

Anechoic chamber and free space measurements

The absorber was mounted on a sample holder placed in an anechoic chamber; the EM measurement system consisted of two horn-shaped antennae. These antennae were attached to the two ports of the vector network analyzer (Model: VNA E8364B), with one port acting as a transmitter and the other as a receiver. Before measurements, the sample holder was positioned away from both the antennae to avoid a far-field illumination effect. In this configuration, free-space calibration was performed. Initially, the reflection coefficient data from a metallic plate having the same dimensions as those of the absorber were recorded. Subsequently, the reflectivity from the absorber side was taken. The difference in the reflection data from both specimens provided the actual reflectivity of the absorber. The interlayer dielectric laminates were characterized for their dielectric and magnetic properties subjecting to the free-space measurement. The system is a non-destructive tool. Results and discussion

Designing parameters for FSMS over layer: simulation studies

Architectural details

The topological shapes were equispaced. Image (a) shows that triangles had bases, v, and s, each of dimensions 2 mm, with adjoining hypotenuses, u, and w, respectively, of length 3.64 and 2.24 mm. The separation of the square from the cell corner is q = 1.85 mm. The cell has an overall size of (p × l) ~ 12 × 12 mm2.

Figure 1b shows orientation of cell in yz plane. As discussed, the FSMS cell has a layered design. It comprised of ink imprint of thickness, ts, ~ 0.02 mm, a dielectric (GFRP) substrate, t, ~ 2.00 mm sandwiched between FSMS over layer and conducting copper ground (tg = 0.035 mm). Figure 1c shows, correspondingly, 3D structure of IFSMS unit cell. The geometry of the unit cell is a bit complex to envisage the overall architectural functioning. Hence, in Fig. 1d, the structure of a typical 2 × 2 adjoining unit cell, termed as the supercell, has been displayed.

The augmentation of 2 × 2 cells generated a four anti-clock wisely oriented bullet-shaped pattern at the center, surrounded with the four diamond-like structures, orthogonal to each other. The topology of the supercell has a fourfold symmetry, i.e., rotation by the π2c will keep the geometry invariant. Figure 1e shows a zoomed portion of a digi-cam photographic image recorded for the fabricated IFSMS tile of dimension ~ 30 × 30 cm2 (Fig. 1f). The supercell produces multiple numbers of impedances (R), inductances (L), and capacitances (C) owing to the bullet-diamond topology on interaction with the microwaves, resulting in an equivalent electronic circuit.

Sheet resistance, dielectric thickness, and equivalent circuit model

Figure 2a shows the simulated Rc vs frequency response of the IFSMS overlayer of resistive ink with different sheet resistance. For 200 Ω/sq, a shallow absorption peak has been observed at ~ 17.2 GHz with a magnitude of Rc ~ − 18.62 dB and respective bandwidth of ~ 12.44 GHz. With subsequent increase in the sheet resistance, a gradual enhancement in both loss and bandwidth has been observed. For 290 Ω/sq, it was reported to be, respectively, − 36.93 dB and 22.8 GHz, whereas, for 320 Ω/sq, a corresponding value of − 37.42 dB and 25.27 GHz have been attained. In the current study, a sheet resistance of 290 Ω/sq has been chosen due to optimal bandwidth performance by keeping in mind about the ease of fabrication. Subsequently, thickness optimization has been carried out on the intermediate dielectric layers, as shown in Fig. 2b. From the Figure, one can see that, at 1.5 mm, a prominent lossy response has been emerged. The resonance has appeared in the high-frequency region ~ 22 GHz compounded with loss ~ − 32 dB. For 2 mm, the resonance emerged at 17.2 GHz, i.e., at the mid-frequency range, achieving a loss of ~ − 37 dB, has relatively higher performance over others and chosen for the current study. At higher thicknesses, resonance was observed to be shifted down to the low-frequency region (10–11 GHz) with reduced losses of up to − 25 dB. Additionally, a double resonance feature has been observed at a thickness of 3 mm. The emergence of resonances at 11.5 and 35.7 GHz is indicative of binary LC response over the simulated frequency region. Since the current study involves a spectral region of 4–28 GHz, the appearance of a second resonance is of little interest. Further, to make the absorber suitable for a realistic stealth application, it is important to design a thinner absorber. Thus, the proposed design has a geometric limitation, hence, a reduced thickness has been implemented. As a result, a single resonance has been observed with the optimized parameters. One can obtain a double resonance structure by designing the shape of FSS that can offer resonance well before the grating lobes, for example, crosses or rings as reported in Ref.29. The circuit has been simulated using a transmission line equivalent model and displayed in Fig. 3a. In such circuitry, the overlayer FSMS imprints represent a serial R–L–C circuit in which resistance shows losses within the resistive area, and inductance is attributed to the current path induced within the nearest neighbor cells and gap within the cell results in capacitive action. Thus, for the proposed absorber, the computed equivalent values are R = 371 Ω, L = 0.3155 nH, and C = 0.196 pF. The GFRP dielectric interlayer is represented by a transmission line of length t, and Zt indicates the corresponding characteristic impedance.

(a) Equivalent FSMS circuitry modeled via transmission line theory, (b) simulated absorptivity and reflectivity comparison using models of the equivalent circuit and full-wave theory for the designed absorber.

From the theory of transmission-line, the short circuit input impedance, Zin, is given by: Zin=jZttanβt, where, β, is a phase constant. The input impedance, ZFSMS, exhibited by the over layer FSMS can be written as: ZFSMS=R+jωL-1ωC. Thus, the input impedance on the top of the over layer pattern is: =ZinZt||ZFSMS=ZtZFSMSZt+ZFSMS. Connecting return losses, Г(ω), associated with the designed absorber to the impedances : Г(ω) = |S11|2 = Z0-ZinZ0+Zin, where, Z0 is the impedance of the free space ≅ 377 Ω. One can compute absorptivity, A(ω), and reflectivity, Г(ω), in the form of S-parameters. Figure 3b displays the computed performance of the designed absorber in the form of absorptivity and reflectivity over the frequency region using a transmission line circuit and full wave model. For both models, the dispersion characteristic is exhibited to be quite stable and found to be varied marginally from 0.9 to 1.0 for the computed absorptivity. The respective reflectivity profile showed marginal variations from 0 to 0.1. The resonance frequency of the absorber was found to be at ~ 17.2 GHz, where absorptivity is ~ 1 and reflectivity = 0. By and large, both models are in good agreement; however, a small variation in two responses has been observed. Moreover, it has been reported that the emergence of a resonance peak for a given frequency band depends on the number of architectural factors, such as design and symmetry of the pattern, surface resistivity, coupling modes, thickness of the interlayer dielectrics, overall width, etc. One may obtain a number of resonances, thereby modulating the original resonance due to the change in admittance and LC response of the absorber30,31. The corresponding study has been provided in supporting information as Fig. S6.

Behavior of TE and TM modes: normal to oblique incidence

The absorber has been illuminated by mode TE and TM at an incident wave vector, K→, with variable angles of incidence ranging from 0° to 60°. Figure 4a,b shows patterns of Rc losses over 4–30 GHz. Inset shows plane of the oscillatory electric and magnetic vector components of the field with reference to the incident, K→. Figure 4c shows a typical trend of losses i.e., Rc @ TE and TM at 17.2 GHz (resonance frequency) of the absorber. For TE mode (Image 4(a)), at Θ = 0° i.e., normal incidence, the absorber has registered a loss peak of magnitude ~ − 37.0 dB (> 99.9% absorption).

Simulated patterns for the absorber at an angle of incidence from 0° to 60° for (a) mode TE, (b) TM, and (c) corresponding data of RC losses with angle of incidence at peak resonance frequency (~ 17.2 GHz) for both the modes.

With subsequent increase in the value of Θ, the dB levels were, correspondingly, found to be decreased monotonically. At Θ = 60°, the designed absorber has attained a loss value of − 8.0 dB. Physically, the absorption of a mode is associated with the generation of the displacement currents in the absorber. The main resonance peak, which appeared at ~ 17.2 GHz, indicates that there is a coupling of the incident TE mode with the displacement currents generated within the supercells of the absorber. For Θ = 60°, the shoulder has appeared at ~ 19 GHz and is comparable in intensity with the main resonance peak. This indicates that, with the increase in angle, more than one source of displacement current exists in the supercells comparable in its strength with respect to the main loss peak.

The overall response of the absorber for TM mode is significantly different than TE. A 10°–20° offset in the losses with respect to Θ values has been observed for TM, as seen in Fig. 4b. At Θ = 0° (normal incidence), losses were reported to be ~ − 36 to − 37 dB (TM/TE). Thereafter, an increase up to ~ − 40 dB has been seen at Θ = 10°–20°, followed by a gradual decay to a − 10 dB level at Θ = 60°. Notably, except at Θ = 0°, where losses were comparable with TE, mode TM dB levels remained higher than TE for other angles of incidences. This indicates, that the coupling of TM with the absorber is stronger than TE over the simulated 4–30 GHz. Unlike TE, no splitting in the resonance peak is seen. This indicates a strong, singular displacement current source in the absorber supercell of magnetic origin. The data for TE and TM is displayed vis-à-vis in Fig. 4c.

Analysis of the structure–property relationship of the inclusion material

Figure 8a–d shows the recorded surface morphology of the ink. The elemental composition revealed that, the printed ink material mainly comprised of the carbon. The morphology recorded in images (a)–(d), broadly, shows that, carbon has a tendency to get deposited in the form of layers. Image (d) prominently shows the nature of the scaffolds that might have thickness in the range of 20–50 nm. The carbonaceous material, due to their covalent binding environment, offers electrical resistivity, however, low in magnitude. Thus, it could be an advantageous host surface to generate the displacement currents and arrest the scattering field on their exposure to the incident microwaves. The printing of the carbonaceous ink may get self-assembled layer-by-layer to offer the irrotational displacement current (indicated by red, yellow arrow) that could effectively diminish the field once entered into the absorber. At some places, the morphology is seen to be completely ruptured (image (a)) disintegrating larger layers into smaller flakes or thereby forming the amorphous carbon.

At the molecular level, the investigations have been carried out using the FTIR technique in order to analyze the surface functional groups present in the host ink. The emergence of a broad absorption band in the range of 4000–3500 cm−1 is assigned to the presence of free and intermolecular carbonyl group present in the graphitic carbon network. There are two regions where one can discuss the variations in experimentally obtained data over simulated one. These two regions are separated by the resonance frequency. Prior to resonance frequency, i.e. C band Rc data obtained experimentally is in good agreement with the theory. In the X band region, relatively larger variations in Rc were observed for about + 2 to 3% with respect to the simulated profile. Whereas, in Ku band, some portions up to 14 GHz seems to have a relatively smaller deviation of about + 0.5 to 1%. Thereafter, theory and experiment agrees well. Near the resonance frequency, the experimental curve is observed to be shifted towards a low frequency region down to 16.82 GHz when compared with the theoretically resonance point. Further, for the region beyond the resonance frequency experimental RC value is deviated by − 4 to 5% with respect to the simulated profile. Thus, before resonance frequency, measured RC is positive; at resonance, the positivity is maintained. Thereafter, RC turned to be negative. This has an impact on overall frequency downshift beyond resonance point. Thus the + and − variations in RC over the spanned band are attributed to the geometrical factors associated with the fabricated IFSMS. Analysis revealed that, the proposed design has a thickness of 2 mm, whereas, the fabricated absorber has a thickness 2.021 ± 0.027 mm. The thickness variation could be responsible for shifting of RC that has been obtained practically. Further, the inter-sub cell spacing is 0.027λ0, which is ~ λ0/33. Measurement revealed that the experimental pattern has a value greater than λ0/33. This has implications for a shift in the resonance frequency peak towards a lower frequency region. Thus, the practical structure seems to be sensitive to the geometrical factors to commensurate ideal/simulated RC response.

Further, for TE @ Θ = 10°, the default loss was noted to be > − 10 dB at low frequency regime, and found to reduce to − 5 dB. For larger angles, the departure from the agreement is noted. This shows that at the larger angles, the structure is going to generate a feeble shadow effect. The change in the RC response by rotation of the structure also provides the information about the stealthy behavior of the designed architecture. Conclusion

In this study, a unit cell of 12 × 12 mm2 comprised of a square and two triangles were topologically spanned to generate a 2 × 2 supercell of bullet-diamond pattern. The pattern was designed as an overlayer element integrated with a GFRP interlayer coupled with conducting copper ground to form an absorber. The absorber was optimized for its overlayer sheet resistance (290 Ω/sq) and interlayer thickness of 2 mm. The absorber surface equivalently acted as a circuit with surface impedance R = 371Ω, inductance L = 0.3155nH, and capacitance C = 0.196pF. Correspondingly, linear transmission line and full wave circuit analysis is presented. The absorber showed symmetric and mid-band resonance (RC = − 35 dB @ 17.2 GHz) compounded with an accessible bandwidth > 20 GHz. The angular variations in TE and TM modes and planner clockwise rotation of the absorber were found to be largely stable and insensitive against such transformations. The displacement current and power density distribution analysis identified the role of the frequency selective modes. By simulating surface impedance functions and phase reversal, S11, Fabry–Perot modes, LC behaviour of meta-circuitry has been characterized. The surface imprinted ink was analyzed for its structure–property relationship using SEM, XRD, FTIR, and UV–visible spectroscopy to reveal the surface property of the overlayer pattern. The meta-pattern was manufactured using mask lithography. The tile (30 × 30 cm2) comprised of an array of supercells was tested in an anechoic chamber spanning ¾ of the theoretical frequency band region. The simulated and experimental data were found to be in excellent agreement. The smaller variations in RC values are attributed to the geometrical factors associated with the fabricated absorber. Their details have been discussed. It could be of the interest to see the performance of fabricated absorber in the C band. The geometrical imperfection could be improve to realize a high performance IFSMS absorber practically to commensurate the simulated counterpart.
