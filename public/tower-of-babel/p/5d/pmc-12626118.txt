Introduction

Superior vena cava (SVC) syndrome is caused by a partial or total obstruction of the SVC. It can be caused by tumour infiltration, thrombus formation, or stenosis of the vein due to pacemaker leads1. Depending on the clinical situation, current treatment options are percutaneous interventional (lead extraction and stenting) or surgical extraction and reconstruction2,3. While first-line treatment is endovascular stenting with high success rates, surgical reconstruction is an uncommon yet safe procedure.4,5

Case presentation

We present the case of a 41-year-old male patient with congenital long QT syndrome Type 2 and congenital complete atrioventricular block. In the early eighties, the patient had received an abdominal single chamber pacemaker with an epicardial lead at the age of 5 months because of survived sudden cardiac death after ventricular fibrillation that was thought to be due to bradycardia-induced torsades. At age 21, the patient had received an upgrade to a right pectoral dual chamber device with endovascular leads. These procedures had been performed at outside institutions in different countries. At age 41, the patient presented with exercise intolerance and swelling of the hands and head with exertion and after sleeping in supine position (Figure 1A). Left ventricular ejection fraction declined from 50% in the previous years to 36% at this point. The patient had a junctional escape rhythm at 35 beats per minute. Computed tomography showed a stenosis of the SVC in the region of the junction to the right atrium. There was a subtotal occlusion of the caudal part of the SVC and a large mass (likely fibrous tissue) around the pacing leads within the right atrium (connected to the right atrial wall) resulting in an upper and lower compartment of the right atrium (Figure 1B). Since a percutaneous interventional approach was deemed unsuitable, the heart team recommended a hybrid surgical approach with lead extraction and reconstruction of the right atrium and SVC using a bovine pericardial patch. Informed consent was obtained from the patient.

An interdisciplinary team of cardiac surgeons and cardiologists performed the procedure. First, preparation of the pacemaker pocket was performed. Second, the chest was opened via median sternotomy. Cardiopulmonary bypass was performed with arterial cannulation of the aorta and venous cannulation of the right femoral vein well as direct cannulation of the brachiocephalic vein for total cardiopulmonary bypass. Transvenous lead extraction was performed to the level of brachiocephalic vein using a standard powered sheath (13 F TightRail™ Powered Sheath, Spectranetics Corporation, Colorado Springs, CO, USA). Next, the aorta was clamped and cardioplegia induced. The right atrium was opened to the SVC showing massive trabeculations dividing the atrium into multiple compartments (Figure 1C). The trabeculations were resected, and the pacemaker leads were manually extracted with the tip of the right ventricular lead remaining in the apex of the right ventricle. The right atrium and the SVC were then reconstructed with a bovine pericardial patch (Supple Peri-Guard, Synovis Life Technologies, Inc., St. Paul, MN, USA, Figure 1D and E). A new dual chamber pacemaker with epicardial leads [Azure S DR MRI and 4968 Capsure Epi, both Medtronic (Schweiz) AG, Tolochenaz, Switzerland] was implanted, and the patient could be weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass. After sternal closure, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit under stable haemodynamic conditions.

The intensive care unit course was uneventful, and the patient was transferred to the cardiac surgery ward on postoperative Day 2. The patient quickly experienced relief of his symptoms with disappearance of facial, neck, and upper extremity swelling (Figure 1F). Compared to preoperative DDD pacing, postoperative showed stable epicardial VVI pacing (70 b.p.m.) during the pacemaker interrogation (Figure 2).

On postoperative Day 6, the patient suffered from ventricular tachycardia (VT), which was successfully treated by increasing the beta-blocker dosage to the regular preoperative regimen. The VT was non-sustained, most likely bradycardia induced, and not tolerated haemodynamically well. Left ventricular ejection fraction has improved to 45% at the time of discharge to an inpatient rehabilitation centre on postoperative Day 13. Histology showed myocardial hypertrophy with distinct interstitial oedema and fibrosis.

Discussion

We present a hybrid heart team approach to treat a rare type of SVC syndrome caused by obstruction within the SVC and the right atrium due to pacemaker leads. Fibrous tissue can form in patients with implanted endovascular leads and was particularly pronounced in this case, most likely due to the long dwell-time of the leads in a patient receiving the pacemaker leads at a young age.

To our knowledge, only one comparable case of a 56-year-old female patient has been reported in 2002. The patient presented with severe stenosis of both the superior and inferior vena cava. During surgery, fibrotic tissue was excised via lead extraction on cardiopulmonary bypass, without necessitating reconstruction of the right atrium using bovine pericardium. However, residual stenosis of the SVC was noted postoperatively, located cranially to the cannulation site.6 In contrast, our approach, namely, primary cannulation of the brachiocephalic vein, successfully avoided this complication by enabling complete removal of fibrotic tissue within the SVC.

To date, no definite mechanisms have been identified to explain the extensive development of fibrotic tissue. Nonetheless, dual-coil leads and passive fixation have emerged as independent risk factors for tissue adherence.7 A comprehensive post-mortem study of 62 patients with transvenous leads revealed encapsulating fibrotic tissue binding to vessel walls in all cases. Interestingly, the prevalence of wall-binding appears to decline with increasing vessel diameter: 90.2% in the brachiocephalic vein, 74.6% in the SVC, and 57.1% at the cavo-atrial junction.8 Thus, prolonged lead dwell-time likely represents the most plausible explanation for the extensive fibrosis observed in our patient.

Although SVC syndrome remains relatively rare and lead extraction techniques have advanced considerably over the past decades, the presence of skilled cardiac surgeons on-site remains essential for delivering a comprehensive lead extraction programme.9 In summary, the close collaboration of cardiologists and cardiac surgeons was essential to provide optimal treatment and symptom relief for this patient. As previously described by Azarrafiy and Carrillo10, such complex cases demand detailed preparation within a multidisciplinary team and a surgical environment with options for surgical and interventional procedures.
