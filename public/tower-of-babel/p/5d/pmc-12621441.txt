Using the American heart association's new 'cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome' metrics to assess life expectancy in us adults: A life table analysis

Published: 2025

Twitter summary

We examined how cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome affects life expectancy in U.S. adults. Our results show that as CKM syndrome progresses, life expectancy decreases, with those in the early stages living significantly longer than those in advanced stages.

Introduction

Obesity, diabetes mellitus, and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are strongly associated with cardiovascular disease (CVD) and all-cause mortality [[1], [2], [3]]. From 1999 to 2020, the proportion of United States adults with multiple cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic syndromes disease increased significantly (from 5.3 % to 8.0 %) and these diseases became the leading causes of death in 2021 [4,5]. Consequently, increasing attention is being paid to these diseases.

The American Heart Association (AHA) formally proposed a novel concept of the cardiovascular-kidney and metabolic (CKM) syndrome in 2023. This syndrome focuses on the pathophysiological interactions between obesity, diabetes mellitus, metabolic risk factors, CKD, and the cardiovascular system [6,7]. Nearly 90 % of United States adults are estimated to meet the criteria for CKM syndrome (stage 1 or higher), and 15 % meet the requirements for the advanced stage [8]. CKM syndrome may lead to high healthcare costs and even premature mortality, which were largely attributed to the increase in CVD [7,9,10].

Life expectancy is one of the most commonly used composite indicators of the overall health status of a population [11], more meaningful than mortality, and of great value to both policymakers and the public. The effect of a single CKM syndrome risk factor on life expectancy has been assessed in several previous studies [[12], [13], [14]]. The Framingham study found that obese and overweight adults were associated with substantially shorter life expectancy and increased early mortality [14]. Chi Wen et al. similarly found that diabetes with early kidney involvement may shorten life expectancy by 16 years [15].However, the relationship between CKM syndrome and life expectancy remains unclear. Exploring the impact of CKM syndrome on life expectancy is essential and can provide valuable insights to the public, healthcare providers, and policy makers. In this study, the association between CKM syndrome and life expectancy among United States adults was assessed using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).

Methods

Study population

NHANES is a program conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics to assess the health and nutritional status of Americans. NHANES used a complex multi-stage probability sampling design to select a representative sample of non-institutionalized populations in the United States. All participants provided written informed consent. The Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention16 approved the survey protocol. All data and analytical guidelines were available free of charge and publicly accessible from the National Center for Health Statistics of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and could be accessed at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm.

In this study, we used six cycles of NHANES from 2007 to 2018, and a total of 14,252 participants aged ≥ 20 years and older who underwent fasting laboratory measurements were included. Excluding missing CKM syndrome data (n = 1108), pregnant women (n = 125), and failure to follow up (n = 25), 12,994 participants were enrolled in the sensitivity analysis (Supplemental Fig. S1). After removing 3951 participants with missing covariates (Supplemental Figure S2) 9043 participants were included in the main model analysis.

Assessment of CKM syndrome stage

CKM syndrome is defined as a health disorder attributable to connections among obesity, diabetes, CKD, and CVD [7,16]. Referring to previous study [4,8], the definition of CKM syndrome stage in this analysis was applied to the data available in NHANES. Age, gender, race/ethnicity, and history of CVD (heart failure, coronary heart disease, and stroke) were determined by self-report. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 80 mmHg, or taking antihypertensive medications. Diabetes was defined as fasting blood glucose ≥126 mg/dL, glycosylated hemoglobin≥6.5 %, or use of lucose lowering medications. BMI (body mass index, kg/m2) was calculated as weight divided by the square of height. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) rate was calculated using the CKD-EPI 2021 creatinine equation. CKM syndrome was divided into 5 stages (0, 1, 2, 3 and 4). Details of the CKM syndrome stage were presented in Table text 1 and Supplementary Table 1. CKM syndrome stages were defined based on baseline measurements from NHANES. Since NHANES is a cross-sectional survey, the CKM syndrome was only assessed at the time of enrollment, and changes in CKM status over time were not captured.

Mortality ascertainment

The outcomes of this study include all-cause and CVD mortality, which were determined by linking to the National Death Index as of 31 December 2019. The document outlines procedures for linking NHANES to the National Death Index through rigorous chance matching and death certification. All-cause mortality was defined as death from any cause. CVD mortality was defined using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes for CVD (ICD-10 code 053–075) [17].

Population all-cause mortality rates (by single age (50 to 100 years)) and cardiovascular disease mortality rates (50 to 84 years) for 2019 were obtained from the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) and the WONDER (Wide-Online Data for Epidemiological Research) databases from the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, respectively [18,19]. As year-by-year CVD mortality rates were not available in the WONDER database for those aged greater than or equal to 85 years, we estimated single-year CVD mortality rates for those aged 85 to 100 years based on extrapolation from a Poisson regression model and a quadratic term at the midpoint of the one-year age group minus 49.5 years [20,21] (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Covariates

Covariates were collected by standardized questionnaire, including age, gender, race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican American, and other races), education levels (less than high school and more high school or than), household income and poverty rate (<1.3, ≥1.3, and <3.5, ≥3.5), smoking status, alcohol consumption, and comorbidities [stroke, coronary heart disease, and hypertension], marital status, physical activity and sedentary time. Smoking status was categorized as never smoker, former smoker, or current smoker. Alcohol consumption was classified as never, former, or now drinker. High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and total cholesterol were assessed according to standardized procedures and protocols. The Healthy Eating Index 2015 was calculated based on dietary components.

Statistical analysis

To ensure that the estimates were nationally representative, sampling weights were considered in all analyses to account for oversampling of certain subgroups and complex sample designs [22]. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute) and R version 4.3.1. All statistical tests were two-sided, and we considered P < 0.05 to be statistically significant.

Categorical variables were presented as unweighted numbers and weighted percentages, while continuous variables were presented as weighted means (95 % CI). The Cox model was used to assess the association between CKM syndrome with mortality. The proportional risk hypothesis was tested using the Schoenfeld residual method and no violations were found. Models were adjusted for the following covariates: age, gender, race/ethnicity, education levels, marital status, family poverty, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, sedentary time, and Healthy Eating Index 2015. We combined the CKM syndrome 0 stage and CKM syndrome 1 stage because there were too few deaths events in CKM syndrome 0 stage. In addition, we conducted stratified analyses by gender or racial and ethnic groups (white, black, and Mexican). Multiplicative interactions were calculated to assess the association between different CKM syndrome and all-cause mortality. In sensitivity analyses, first, we performed analyses after excluding the sample of participants who died within 2 years of follow-up. Second, to mitigate sample size reduction caused by missing covariates, we employed multiple interpolations of chain equations using 5 iterations (utilizing the SAS procedures SURVEYIMPUTE and MIANALYZE with fully conditional specification methods) for imputing missing values [23,24].

A life table approach was used to calculate the life expectancy of participants [25,26]. We created life tables starting at age 50 and ending at age 100 to calculate three estimates of cumulative survival after age 50, as follows: (1) access to the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) 2019 all-cause mortality rates for populations of all ages [18]; (2) all-cause mortality-adjusted HRs for each exposure group compared to the NHANES reference values; and (3) age-specific population-based prevalence rates for each exposure group in NHANES. The methods used to estimate differences in life expectancy have been described in Supplementary Text 1. To determine the effect of CVD mortality on life expectancy, we also estimated the contribution of CVD mortality to the difference in life expectancy between different groups of participants using the Arriaga decomposition [27,28]. The analysis process consisted of 2 steps: (1) decomposition by individual age groups and (2) decomposition by cause of death within individual age groups. Monte Carlo simulations (parametric bootstrapping) using 1000 runs were used to estimate the 95 % CI of life expectancy.

Association of the CKM syndrome and risk of mortality

During a mean 6.8 years of follow-up, a total of 842 deaths occurred, of which 218 individuals died of CVD. CKM syndrome was significantly associated with all-cause mortality and CVD mortality (Table 2). HRs for stages 2, 3, and 4 CKM syndrome with all-cause mortality were 1.67 (95% CI, 1.13-2.47), 2.97 (95% CI,1.99-4.42), and 3.67 (95% CI, 2.44-5.53) compared to participants with stages 0-1 CKM syndrome, respectively. Similarly, stages 2, 3, and 4 CKM syndrome were at increased risk of CVD mortality compared with participants in stages 0-1 CKM syndrome, with HRs of 2.88 (95% CI, 1.1-7.56), 6.35(95% CI, 2.37-17.03), and 9.34 (95% CI, 3.4-25.68), respectively. In addition, we rebuilt the model using stage 0 CKM syndrome as a reference, and the results for all-cause mortality and CVD mortality were consistent with the main analysis (Supplementary Table S2). We did not find a significant interaction between gender race/ethnicity, and CKM syndrome stage on the risk of all-cause mortality (Supplementary Table S3-S4).

Association of the CKM syndrome with estimated life expectancy

Estimated life expectancy at age 50 years was 37.5 (95 % CI, 34.7–40.4), 32.6 (95 % CI, 32–33.6), 27.1 (95 % CI, 26.4–28.1), and 25.1 (95 % CI, 24.2–26.1) for participants in stages 0–1, 2, 3, and 4 CKM syndrome, respectively (Fig. 2A). Life expectancy at age 50 was reduced by an average of 12.4 years (95 % CI, 8.6–16.2) in stage 4 CKM syndrome participants compared with stages 0–1 CKM syndrome participants (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Table S7). In a cause-specific decomposition analysis of the difference in life expectancy, we found that compared with stages 0–1 participants, an average of 26.7 % of the reduction in life expectancy at age 50 among stage 4 participants was attributable to increased CVD mortality (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, recalculating life expectancy for each stage using stage 0 of the cardio-renal metabolic syndrome as the reference group yielded results consistent with the primary analysis (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Subgroup analysis of life expectancy

We stratified the analysis by gender and race/ethnicity. We observed that higher stages of the CKM syndrome, the shorter the life expectancy in both men and women. Among men, participants with CKM syndrome stage 4 had a life expectancy at age 50 that was 10.2 years (95 % CI, 5.1–15 years) shorter than those with CKM syndrome stages 0–1. The corresponding difference in women was 14.4 years (95 % CI, 8.9–19.6 years) (Supplementary Table S7). Compared with participants in CKM syndrome stages 0–1, among men, participants with CKM syndrome stage 4 had a 29.4 % descend in life expectancy at age 50, attributable to a reduction in cardiovascular disease mortality; for women, the corresponding percentage was 22.5 % (Supplementary Fig. S5). Compared with CKM syndrome stages 0–1, the shortened life expectancy among white, black, and Mexican American participants in stages 4 CKM syndrome was attributed to increased CVD mortality, by 24.6 %, 31.0 %, and 28.7 %, respectively (Supplementary Table S8 and Fig. S6). Since the population mortality rate for the Other Race was not available, we did not analyze the life expectancy of the Other Race.

Discussion

In a nationally representative cohort of United States adults, CKM stage was inversely and stepwise associated with life expectancy, with each higher stage corresponding to shorter expected years of life, after multivariable adjustment. We observed a 12.4-year shorter life expectancy at age 50 for participants with CKM syndrome stage 4 compared to those with CKM syndrome stages 0–1. In addition, we found that >26 % of the short in life expectancy was attributable to a increase in CVD mortality. In 2019, life expectancy for adults aged 50 years in the United States was 31.8 years. In this study, we observed a life expectancy of 37.5 years for participants with stages 0–1 CKM syndrome, while the corresponding life expectancy for participants with 4 stage CKM syndrome was only 25.1 years. Our results suggest that advanced stages of cardiorenal metabolic syndrome are strongly associated with reduced life expectancy.

The results showed that the life expectancy of participants gradually declined with the increase in CKM syndrome stages. This phenomenon may be closely related to the significantly increased mortality risk in individuals with the higher the stage of CKM syndrome. Previous studies have confirmed that the higher the stage of CKM syndrome, the greater the gradient increase in all-cause mortality risk [29]. A study by Na Li et al. also found that compared with participants with CKM syndrome stage 0, those with stage 4 had a significantly higher all-cause mortality hazard ratio of 3.73 (95 % CI: 3.19–4.37) [30]. This association was more pronounced in young adults, indicating that early progression of CKM syndrome stages may more easily accelerate the accumulation of lethal risks [30]. Notably, the core pathological feature of advanced CKM syndrome stages (stages 3–4) is the overt or progressive state of clinical or subclinical cardiovascular disease (CVD) [31], which largely explains why patients in advanced CKM syndrome stages have shorter life expectancies and face higher CVD mortality risks. According to the CKM syndrome staging criteria, stage 3 is defined as subclinical CVD (e.g., atherosclerosis), and stage 4 as clinical CVD (e.g., myocardial infarction or heart failure). These pathological conditions lead to patient mortality by directly increasing the risk of cardiac events [[32], [33], [34]]. In this study, >26 % of the increase in life expectancy among participants with CKM syndrome stages 0–1 could be attributed to the reduction in CVD mortality, highlighting the importance of CVD management for the prognosis of CKM syndrome patients. Meanwhile, metabolic disorders play a key role in advanced CKM syndrome, further exacerbating cardiovascular mortality risk and shortening life expectancy. A study based on NHANES data showed that an elevated triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index was associated with increased mortality risk in individuals with CKM syndrome stages 0–3 [35]. Additionally, patients with CKM syndrome stages 3–4 had more severe conditions compared to those with stages 0–1. For example, among patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI), those with CKM syndrome stages 3–4 had a relatively lower proportion of males, as well as lower high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratios, but higher proportions of hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and higher levels of triglycerides, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and creatinine [[36], [37], [38], [39]]. These abnormal indices reflect more severe metabolic disorders and organ dysfunction in patients, significantly increasing the risk of cardiovascular events and thus having a negative impact on life expectancy.

Strengths of this study included the use of nationally representative data to assess the association of CKM syndrome with mortality and life expectancy, as well as stratified analyses by gender and racial/ethnic group providing important clinical guidance. Some limitations also exist in this study. Firstly, diagnosed CVD as well as lifestyle factors (diet and physical activity) were based on questionnaires, and measurement error was inevitable. Second, some of the data used to define advanced stages (including cardiac biomarkers, echocardiography, coronary angiography, cardiac computed tomography, atrial fibrillation, and peripheral arterial disease) were not available, which may have led to an underestimation of stages 3 and 4 CKM syndrome. Third, although we made full use of the large sample size of the NHANES database, there were small sample sizes in the early (0–1 stage) and late (3–4 stage) subgroups of CKM syndrome. When exploring differences between different subgroups, the small sample size may also mask the true effect of CKM syndrome on life expectancy in specific populations. Fourth, One important limitation of our study is that CKM syndrome stages were measured at a single time point at baseline. Therefore, we were unable to assess how changes in CKM stages over time or the effect of treatment interventions may influence life expectancy or CVD mortality. Future longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the dynamic nature of CKM syndrome and its temporal impact on health outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates a significant negative correlation between CKM syndrome staging and population life expectancy: the higher the CKM stage, the shorter the patients' life expectancy. Given the severe harm of high-grade CKM syndrome to health, the field of public health should regard it as a priority and explore more effective prevention and treatment strategies.
