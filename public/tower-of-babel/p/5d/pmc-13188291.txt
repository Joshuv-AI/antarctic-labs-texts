Clinical predictors of enzyme immunoassay accuracy in Clostridioides difficile infection: the impact of immune status and infection predisposing factors
Authors: Simiao Duan, Tao Lv, Lisi Zheng, Danhua Zhu, Jianqin He, Yun-bo Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is a major cause of healthcare-associated diarrhea, with increasing prevalence in China and the Asia-Pacific region. Despite its limited sensitivity, the enzyme immunoassay (EIA) is widely used for CDI diagnosis in clinical practice due to its cost-effectiveness and rapid results. However, false-negative results can lead to underdiagnosis and poor patient outcomes. Limited research has examined the clinical characteristics of EIA false negative (FN) cases in the Chinese healthcare setting.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, we analyzed 673 stool samples from suspected patients with CDI. Patients were classified as false-negative (FN; GDH-positive/EIA-negative, cytotoxicity culture-positive) or true positive (P; GDH-positive/EIA-positive, cytotoxicity culture-positive). Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of FN results and their association with clinical outcomes.ResultsIn the multivariable analysis, immunocompromised status was the strongest factor associated with a lower likelihood of a false-negative result (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] for FN: 0.12, 95% CI: 0.06–0.24). Patients in the FN group presented with a significantly milder degree of clinical severity at diagnosis, as measured by the Zar CDI severity score. Critically, after adjusting for baseline disease severity, an FN result was independently associated with a 2.83-fold increased risk of developing shock (aOR = 2.83, 95% CI: 1.23–6.52).ConclusionHost immune status is a key factor associated with EIA accuracy, while the influence of other traditional risk factors like PPI use appears to be confounded. False-negative EIA results are not benign; they are independently associated with a substantially higher risk of shock, suggesting that reliance on EIA alone in high-risk patients may carry significant prognostic implications. These findings highlight the limitations of EIA in immunocompetent patients with milder symptoms and underscore the critical need for adjunctive testing to improve patient outcomes.Keywords: Clostridioides difficile, Enzyme immunoassay, Underdiagnosis, Clinical characteristics, Retrospective study

Introduction

Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) is recognized as a leading cause of healthcare-associated diarrhea worldwide and is becoming increasingly prevalent in Southeast Asia and the Asia-Pacific region [1, 2]. CDI is associated with notable morbidity and mortality, particularly in elderly and immunocompromised populations [3]. Diagnosis and management of CDI are critical for infection control practices, with timely and accurate detection being essential to prevent complications and reduce transmission.

In clinical settings, CDI detection methods include enzyme immunoassays (EIAs), nucleic acid amplification tests, stool cultures, toxigenic culture, and cell culture cytotoxicity assays. Recently, innovative methods such as CRISPR-based detection have emerged as promising tools for rapid and precise diagnostics. Importantly, multistep testing algorithms are often recommended in practice to optimize diagnostic accuracy and reliability [4]. Among these methods, EIA is widely used because it is cost- and time-efficient. However, the EIA method is associated with poor detection of specimens with low toxin levels, which is a major contributor to false-negative (FN) results [5]. The toxin levels in stool samples are highly associated with host immune response, as well as other clinical characteristics and manifestations [6], thereby suggesting a link between EIA accuracy and patient characteristics.

Incidence of CDI has increased over the past decade due to heightened awareness, better diagnostics, and changing healthcare practices [7]. The main CDI detection methods in China include a diagnostic algorithm combining a glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) and an EIA test for C. difficile toxin A&B (CDAB) [8]. CDI diagnosis relies highly on EIA-positive results; therefore, the low accuracy associated with the EIA likely increases the risk of CDI underdiagnosis, particularly in patients who exhibit mild symptoms [9]. Further, previous studies suggest that patients who present with FN results tend to exhibit milder laboratory results [10]. Furthermore, FN reports can result in undertreatment and worsened prognoses [11].

Considering the importance of the EIA in CDI diagnosis, there has been limited research into the clinical characteristics of EIA FN cases or studies aimed at addressing the implications of these diagnostic discrepancies in the Chinese healthcare setting. The primary hypothesis of this study was that specific clinical factors, such as immune status, may influence the accuracy of toxin EIAs. To address this, we sought to analyze the clinical profiles and outcomes associated with EIA FN results, offering new insights to improve diagnostic accuracy and to inform clinical decision-making.

Materials and methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, a 2,200-bed tertiary hospital located in Hangzhou, China, analyzing GDH-positive stool specimens from 1,743 patients hospitalized with suspected CDI between April 1, 2018, and March 31, 2024. A two-step algorithm was employed: samples that were GDH-positive but EIA-negative or -positive were tested by a cytotoxicity culture assay (CCA) as the reference standard.

Patients were divided into two groups based on the results:

True-Positive (P) Group: Patients with stool samples testing positive for GDH, EIA, and confirmed by CCA.

True-Positive (P) Group: Patients with stool samples testing positive for GDH, EIA, and confirmed by CCA.

False-Negative (FN) Group: Patients with stool samples testing positive for GDH, negative for EIA, but positive by CCA.

False-Negative (FN) Group: Patients with stool samples testing positive for GDH, negative for EIA, but positive by CCA.

Diagnostic tests and definitions

Diagnostic tests

Our laboratory established exclusion criteria for stool specimens, excluding samples that were either fully formed or did not conform to the shape of the container upon inversion. Stool samples were tested for GDH and toxins using the VIDAS®C. difficile GDH or Toxins A&B kits (bioMérieux, France), as previously described [12]. VIDAS toxin testing was conducted only when the GDH test yielded a positive result. Additionally, the QCC tests (Techlab Inc., Blacksburg, VA, USA) for GDH (QCC-GDH) and toxins A/B (QCC-Toxins A&B) within a single cartridge were performed following the manufacturer’s instructions [13]. Further, previous studies have shown that there are no differences in sensitivity and specificity between these two methods [14].

Reference method: unformed stool samples were either processed immediately or stored at 4 °C for up to 24 h when immediate processing was not feasible. All samples underwent anaerobic inoculation on cycloserine-cefoxitin fructose agar (Oxford, UK) after being subjected to alcohol shock following VIDAS® or QCC testing. C. difficile-positive colonies were confirmed using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization-time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI/TOF MS, Bruker Corporation, USA). The presence of the tcdA, tcdB, and cdtA/B genes in the isolated C. difficile strains was determined via PCR, following previously published protocols [15]. Specimens with C. difficile isolates testing positive for any of these genes were classified as CCA-positive. While Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests (NAAT) are increasingly used for screening due to high sensitivity, the Cell Cytotoxicity Assay (CCA) was selected as the reference standard for this study to strictly define active C. difficile infection rather than colonization. NAAT detects the presence of the toxin gene (tcdB), which cannot distinguish between active infection and asymptomatic carriage—a phenomenon observed in up to 20% of hospitalized patients [16]. Previous large-scale validation studies have demonstrated that CCA-positive patients exhibit significantly worse clinical outcomes compared to NAAT-positive/Toxin-negative patients, whose outcomes closely mirror those of uninfected controls [17]. Therefore, using CCA ensures that the clinical predictors identified in this study are associated with biologically active toxin-mediated disease.

Definition

CDI episode: An episode of CDI was defined according to IDSA/SHEA guidelines as the presence of ≥ 3 unformed stools in 24 h with a positive stool test for C. difficile toxins. Recurrent CDI was defined as the reappearance of symptoms within 2–8 weeks following symptom resolution, with a subsequent positive diagnostic test result. Recurrent CDI subjects were excluded in this study [9]. Recurrent cases were excluded to eliminate confounding introduced by the adaptive immune response. Patients with recurrent infection often possess distinct serum antibody profiles (specifically IgG and IgA against Toxin A and B) acquired from prior exposure [18]. Since the primary objective of this study was to evaluate how baseline host immune status impacts EIA diagnostic accuracy, including patients with primed adaptive immunity could obscure the relationship between the initial immune state and toxin detection.

Immunocompromised status: Special medical conditions impairing immune system function like solid organ transplantation, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, hematological malignancy, active chemotherapy for a solid tumor, high-dose steroid therapy (> 0.5 mg/kg/day of prednisone or equivalent for more than 4 weeks), or other forms of immunosuppressive therapy (e.g., TNF-alpha inhibitors or other biologic immune modulators) [19].

Risk of contributing to the incidence or progression of CDI (high-, medium-, and low-risk antibiotics), as previously described: high-risk antibiotics: carbapenem, 2nd-, 3rd-, or 4th-generation cephalosporin, fluoroquinolone, lincosamide, pivampicillin, or temocillin; medium-risk antibiotics: penicillin, penicillin combination, 1st-generation cephalosporin, macrolide, monobactam, or streptogramin; low-risk antibiotics: other antibiotics in systemic administration [20, 21].

Standard anti-CDI treatment: oral administration of vancomycin at 125 mg four times daily for more than one week, or metronidazole at 500 mg three times daily for 10 to 14 days. This definition aligns with the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis, Treatment, and Prevention of Clostridioides difficile Infection [8]. While recent IDSA guidelines recommend fidaxomicin as first-line therapy, metronidazole and vancomycin remain the primary standard of care in the Chinese healthcare setting during the study period due to drug availability and cost-effectiveness considerations.

Data collection

In this retrospective case-control study conducted between April 2018 and March 2024, clinical and laboratory data were collected from electronic medical records for recruiting patients.

Assessed variables included comorbidities such as hypertension, coronary artery disease, diabetes mellitus (DM), renal disease, malignancy, leukemia, colitis and transplantation. Hospitalization and treatment history were reviewed, including recent hospital stays (within 30 days), administration of immunosuppressive or antineoplastic drugs, antibiotics use within 30 days, proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use, gastrointestinal operations and immunosuppressive status.

Vital signs, including ear-measured body temperature, were recorded. Laboratory examinations, performed within three days of CDI diagnosis, included white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet count (PLT), serum creatinine (SCr) and triglycerides (TG).

To provide an objective measure of disease severity at the time of diagnosis, the Zar CDI severity score was calculated for each patient based on the criteria originally described by Zar et al. [22]. Points were assigned for the following clinical and laboratory parameters: age > 60 years (1 point), temperature > 38.3 °C (1 point), serum albumin < 25 g/L (1 point), white blood cell (WBC) count > 15 × 10⁹/L (1 point), admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) (2 points), and the presence of pseudomembranous colitis (2 points). The total score was the sum of these points, with a score of ≥ 2 indicating severe disease.

Prognosis-related outcomes, documented only if they occurred after CDI diagnosis, included hypotension, exposure to antibiotics with different risk levels, 30-day mortality, ICU admission, gastrointestinal obstruction, perforation, blood infection and shock. Shock was defined as a systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg or a mean arterial pressure < 65 mmHg requiring vasopressor support.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1. The significance level was set at α = 0.05. Continuous variables, including mean and standard deviation, were calculated for each group as defined by the grouping of P and FN. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check the normality of data. The differences between groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test for the continuous non-normally distributed variables. The differences in proportions across groups were evaluated using the Chi-Square test.

Initially, a univariate logistic regression was performed for each potential clinical predictor to assess its individual association with the binary outcome (FN vs. P). The results of these analyses were visualized using a volcano plot to identify variables with both strong statistical significance and large effect sizes.

To identify independent predictors of false-negative (FN) EIA results and to control for potential confounders, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed. The binary outcome was group allocation (FN vs. P). Variables considered for inclusion were based on clinical importance and the results of the univariate analysis.

Furthermore, to investigate whether FN results were independently associated with adverse outcomes, a second multivariable logistic regression model was built. The development of shock was the binary outcome. The primary predictor was group allocation (FN vs. P), and the model was adjusted for key potential confounders: age, immunocompromised status, and the calculated Zar severity score to account for baseline disease severity.

For all regression models, results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) or adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Data completeness was high for the primary clinical variables included in the multivariable models. Missing data rates were 0% for immunocompromised status, surgical history, and medication exposure (antibiotics, PPIs), and < 5% for age and key laboratory markers (WBC, platelets). Consequently, a complete-case analysis was performed, as the risk of bias from this low level of missingness is minimal. For exploratory metabolic biomarkers not routinely ordered for acute diarrhea (e.g., triglycerides), data availability was lower (~ 73%); these variables were analysed only within the available subset and were not included in the primary multivariable model to preserve statistical power. Results

Risk factors for FN results from patient clinical characteristics

From April 2018 to March 2024, 1,743 stool specimens tested positive for GDH. After excluding patients with repetitive submissions, incomplete electronic medical records, insufficient follow-up data, and recurrent CDI, 673 patients were enrolled in the analysis (Fig. 1). Subsequently, patients were classified into the FN (n = 166) or the Positive group (P, n = 507) according to their EIA and CCA results.

Flow diagram of study cohort selection and specimen classification. A total of 1,743 stool specimens that tested positive for glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) between April 1, 2018, and March 31, 2024, were enrolled for further evaluation. These specimens were initially stratified by enzyme immunoassay (EIA) and subsequently underwent a cytotoxicity culture assay (CCA) for verification. After this two-step diagnostic process, 673 eligible specimens were included in the final study cohort, which comprised 507 true positives (EIA+/CCA+) and 166 false negatives (EIA-/CCA+). A total of 1,070 specimens were excluded, consisting of 428 true negatives (EIA-/CCA-) confirmed by CCA, in addition to specimens excluded due to repetitive submissions (n = 321), loss to follow-up (n = 176), or classification as recurrent infections (n = 145)

Among the 673 patients, the age distribution ranged broadly, with a mean age of 59.7 ± 19.8 years. The gender distribution was uneven, with a higher proportion of males compared to females (63.6%), which agrees with previous studies [23]. However, no significant differences in sex distribution were found between the FN group and P group. (FN group: age 58 ± 21 years, male proportion 67.5%. P group: age 60 ± 19 years, male proportion 62.3%)

The two groups were comparable in terms of demographic and most clinical characteristics, including key CDI treatment parameters and comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes, though a significant difference was observed in immune status, which is exactly our variable of interest (Table 1). Demographic and clinical history data were nearly complete across the cohort. While standard inflammatory markers like WBC were available for > 95% of patients, lipid profiles (TG) were available for 491 patients (73.0%), as this test is not part of the standard emergency workup for CDI.

Several variables exhibited significant differences between the FN and P groups (Fig. 2). Immunocompromised status was significantly less frequent in the FN group compared to the P group (10.2% vs. 35.5%, P < 0.001). Similarly, PPI use was lower in the FN group than in the P group (58.4% vs. 68.8%, P = 0.048). Gastrointestinal operations were also less common in the FN group compared to the P group (34.9% vs. 49.3%, P = 0.006). The significantly lower prevalence of these key factors in the FN group suggests that immunocompromised status, PPI use, and gastrointestinal operations can impact the result of EIA test.

Comparison of key clinical factors between False-Negative (FN) and True-Positive (P) groups. The frequencies of immunocompromised status, proton pump inhibitor (PPI) use, and gastrointestinal (GI) operations were significantly lower in the FN group

However, the comorbidities such as hypertension, coronary disease, DM, respiratory disease, kidney disease, cancer, leukemia and colitis showed no statistically significant differences between the two groups.

It was observed that the FN group presented with markedly milder clinical signs of infection. The prevalence of febrile status (12.0% vs. 23.5%, P = 0.002) and hyperleukocytosis (WBC count > 10 × 10⁹/L) (16.9% vs. 25.0%, P = 0.039) were both significantly lower in the FN group. To provide a more comprehensive and objective assessment of baseline disease severity, the Zar CDI severity score was calculated. Although the median score was identical between the groups (1 [IQR 1] for both), the mean score was lower in the FN group (0.77 ± 0.86) compared to the P group (0.95 ± 1.00). A comparison of the overall Zar score distributions via the Wilcoxon rank-sum test confirmed a trend towards lower disease severity in the FN group (P = 0.0508). Furthermore, when applying the clinical definition of severe CDI (Zar score ≥ 2), the proportion of patients with severe disease was substantially lower in the FN group (21/166, 12.7%) than in the P group (99/507, 19.5%), a difference that approached statistical significance (P = 0.0585). While the absolute difference in mean Zar scores was modest (0.18 points), when combined with the lower rates of fever and leukocytosis, these data suggest that patients in the FN group presented with a trend toward lower Zar severity scores.

Multivariable analysis of predictors for FN results

To identify independent predictors while controlling for confounding factors, we constructed a multivariable logistic regression model. The results are presented in Table 2. After adjustment, immunocompromised status remained the most powerful independent factor associated with diagnostic accuracy, with such patients having an 88% lower likelihood of a false-negative result (aOR = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.06–0.24, P < 0.001). Other factors independently associated with a lower likelihood of a false-negative result included a history of gastrointestinal operation, the presence of fever, higher WBC count, and older age. Notably, several factors that were significant in univariate analyses, such as recent hospitalization and PPI use, were no longer independent predictors in the final multivariable model.

It is noteworthy that several factors showing a significant association in the univariate analysis (Table 1, and visualized in the volcano plot, Fig. 3), such as pre-diagnosis exposure to medium- and high-risk antibiotics, did not remain significant in the final multivariable model (Table 2). This suggests that their initial association with FN results was likely confounded by more dominant clinical predictors, particularly immunocompromised status. In other words, while immunocompromised patients were more likely to receive broad-spectrum antibiotics, it is their immune status itself, rather than the antibiotic exposure alone, that serves as the strong, independent predictor of a positive EIA result.

Impact of underdiagnosis on treatment and prognosis

Treatment decisions typically rely on a valid diagnosis in our study, as the FN group showed significantly lower proportion of patients who have received recommended standard anti-CDI treatment compared to the P group (4.8% vs. 20.3%, P < 0.001), suggesting that FN results often lead to insufficient, or a complete lack of treatment in patients with CDI. The interquartile range (IQR) of regimen duration in the P group was 3 to 11 days, indicating that the middle 50% of diagnosed patients received a treatment regimen within this duration. These data suggest a tendency toward shorter, insufficient treatment regimens, due to mismanagement and patient adherence.

Regarding clinical outcomes, a higher incidence of shock was observed in the FN group (9.6% vs. 4.9%, P = 0.044). To determine if this association was independent of baseline disease severity, we performed a multivariable logistic regression analysis. After adjusting for age, immunocompromised status, and the Zar severity score, an FN result remained an independent risk factor for the development of shock (aOR = 2.83, 95% CI: 1.23–6.52, P = 0.014). This finding indicates that, for patients with the same initial level of disease severity, those with an FN result had 2.83 times the odds of developing shock. Other adverse outcomes such as 30-day mortality and blood infection were comparable between the groups. (Table 3).

Discussion

This retrospective study investigated the clinical characteristics influencing the accuracy of the EIA test. Through multivariable analysis, we identified several independent factors associated with FN results, with immunocompetence (lack of immune suppression) emerging as the primary risk factor for a false-negative outcome. Furthermore, we demonstrated that FN results were independently associated with a worsened prognosis, including a significantly higher incidence of shock, even after adjusting for baseline disease severity, and poorer post-diagnostic antibiotic management.

An intact immune system more effectively suppresses C. difficile, reducing toxin production and enhancing toxin clearance. Specifically, innate lymphoid cells (ILC3s) have been shown to orchestrate early neutrophil defense via GM-CSF secretion, which is critical for controlling bacterial load [24]. Conversely, immune senescence and the accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) impair the protective Th17 response, increasing susceptibility to infection and toxin-mediated damage [25]. This relationship is particularly important because diagnostic tests like EIA rely on a minimum toxin threshold for detection, which varies across different methods [26]. Host clinical characteristics, including immune status and other physiological factors, can influence toxin production and clearance [27], potentially lowering toxin levels below the detection threshold of certain tests. Consequently, patients with reduced toxin levels are at a higher risk of yielding FN results in laboratory tests [28, 29]. Recent studies have shown that immunocompromised patients have lower concentrations of serum toxin A- and B-specific IgA during the early course of CDI therapy [28]. However, antibodies such as IgM, IgG, and IgA against C. difficile toxins (TcdA and TcdB) and surface proteins are critical for toxin neutralization and clearance [29]. These findings suggest that, in immunocompromised patients, reduced immune-mediated toxin neutralization may lead to higher detectable toxin levels. Consistent with this, our study observed a significantly lower prevalence of immunocompromised status in the FN group, suggesting that patients with an intact immune system may more effectively resist CDI, resulting in lower toxin levels and an increased likelihood of FN EIA results.

Further supporting the hypothesis of a distinct host profile in the FN group, we observed elevated triglyceride (TG) levels, a finding that aligns with the established “inflammation-metabolism crosstalk” [30]. Recent mechanistic studies demonstrate that inflammatory cytokines, particularly IL-1β and TNF-α, can directly disrupt lipid homeostasis by sequestering AMPK and suppressing PPARα-mediated fatty acid oxidation, leading to serum TG accumulation [30, 31]. Furthermore, acute inflammatory challenges (e.g., LPS exposure) have been causally linked to elevated TG levels via the NF-κB pathway [32]. Although we consider this an exploratory finding in our cohort, the elevated TG levels in the FN group may reflect an underlying dysregulated inflammatory state that contributes to the unique clinical presentation of these patients.

This finding that immunocompetent patients are at higher risk for false-negative EIA results adds a crucial layer of nuance to the role of other well-established CDI risk factors. Our univariate analysis, consistent with existing literature [33–38], initially showed that factors like PPI use, gastrointestinal operations, and prior exposure to medium- or high-risk antibiotics were all significantly associated with a higher likelihood of a positive test. However, these associations vanished in our multivariable model. This strongly suggests that their effects are not independent but are largely confounded by the patient’s underlying immune status. For instance, immunocompromised patients are precisely those who are more likely to undergo GI operations or receive broad-spectrum antibiotics. Our analysis clarifies that it is the host’s immune capacity, the central component of the host-pathogen interaction, rather than these secondary exposures, that serves as a strong independent predictor of detectable toxin levels.

The lower proportions of hyperleukocytosis and febrile status in the FN group compared to the P group in this study align with previous research indicating that patients with FN EIA results for CDI tend to exhibit milder symptoms, lower WBCs, and less severe clinical manifestations [10, 39]. Our study substantiates this finding objectively, as the FN group not only had lower rates of hyperleukocytosis and fever but also demonstrated a clear trend towards lower disease severity as measured by the Zar CDI severity score. Additionally, they experience fewer complications and shorter hospital stays [10]. However, studies have shown that patients with EIA-negative carry similar risks for transmitting C. difficile spores [40], emphasizing the importance of accurate and timely diagnosis to prevent underdiagnosis and to improve infection control.

CDI underdiagnosis can lead to mismanagement of antibiotic administration. Post-CDI diagnosis, the FN group showed greater exposure to low- and medium-risk antibiotics, while high-risk antibiotic exposure remained comparably low in both groups. This difference reflects clinical decision-making influenced by the absence of a valid CDI diagnosis. Prior antibiotic exposure is a known risk factor for CDI [20, 21], and research has shown that concomitant antibiotic use during anti-CDI treatment significantly increases the risk of treatment failure and recurrent CDI [37]. In the P group, efforts to limit antibiotic use during treatment likely mitigated these risks. However, in the FN group, the lack of a CDI diagnosis may have prevented physicians from appropriately adjusting or reducing antibiotic use, inadvertently increasing the risk of further CDI onset. Our heatmap analysis clarifies the nature of this mismanagement. While pre-diagnostic exposure was statistically identical between groups, the FN group failed to de-escalate therapy after the negative test result. Specifically, these patients were significantly more likely to remain on β-lactamase inhibitors, quinolones, and penicillins compared to the P group. This persistent exposure to broad-spectrum agents likely compounded the gut dysbiosis, potentially contributing to the worse clinical outcomes observed.

The clinical consequences of such underdiagnosis were significant. A key finding of this study is that an FN result was an independent risk factor for the development of shock, even after adjusting for baseline disease severity with the Zar score. This strongly suggests that the worsened prognosis is not merely because these patients were inherently different. We hypothesize that this association may be linked to unmeasured factors such as delays in initiating effective anti-CDI therapy, allowing for uncontrolled toxin production and disease progression, culminating in severe outcomes like shock. This underscores that a false-negative EIA is not a benign event but a critical clinical marker associated with severe adverse outcomes.

Laboratory test-based decision-making for CDI diagnosis should account for factors such as immunocompromised status, PPI use, gastrointestinal operation history, and prior exposure to medium- or high-risk antibiotics. For patients with positive GDH but negative EIA results, the presence of these factors may increase the likelihood of ruling out a CDI diagnosis. However, CDI diagnosis is inherently complex and requires a comprehensive, multifactorial evaluation. The relative contribution of these factors to the diagnostic process remains unclear, highlighting the need for further research to refine diagnostic protocols and enhance accuracy.

This study provides valuable insights into the clinical characteristics and outcomes associated with FN EIA results in CDI diagnosis. However, its retrospective design has certain limitations, including potential information loss due to incomplete medical records, particularly for patients who were transferred or discharged, resulting in underreporting of follow-up data. While our multivariable analysis successfully quantified the independent contributions of several key predictors, this study has other limitations. Its retrospective design is subject to potential information bias. Additionally, confounding factors not captured in our models, such as host inflammatory cytokine profiles, co-infections, and gut microbiota composition, were not evaluated and could offer further insights into the differences between the FN and P groups. Moreover, interpretations of the role of TG in chronic inflammation and the higher shock incidence in the FN group remain speculative, requiring further validation through direct immunological or systemic studies. Furthermore, conducting the study in a single tertiary hospital may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Future prospective research with comprehensive data collection and advanced diagnostic methods is essential to confirm these findings and improve the accuracy and management of CDI diagnosis. Finally, as a retrospective analysis, this study was not prospectively powered for all secondary outcomes, and some subgroup analyses (e.g., specific comorbidities) may be limited by sample size.

Conclusions

In this study, we identified several independent factors associated with enzyme immunoassay (EIA) accuracy for CDI diagnosis through multivariable analysis. Host immune status emerged as the most powerful independent factor, with immunocompromised status significantly lowering the likelihood of a false-negative result. Conversely, factors like gastrointestinal operations and more severe clinical signs (e.g., fever) were associated with a higher likelihood of a true-positive result. Critically, we demonstrated that an FN result is not a benign event; it is independently associated with a nearly threefold increased risk of developing shock, even after accounting for baseline disease severity. These findings underscore the limitations of relying solely on EIA, particularly in immunocompetent patients presenting with milder symptoms. Our results strongly advocate for the use of adjunctive, more sensitive diagnostic methods in these specific patient populations to prevent diagnostic delays and mitigate the risk of severe adverse outcomes.
