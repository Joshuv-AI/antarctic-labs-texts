A High-Resolution Multifocal RGB Pollen Grain Image Dataset for Deep Learning Computer Vision Tasks from Biobío Region, Chile
Authors: I. Sanhueza, P. Coelho, L. Viafora, R. Jofre-Cerda, V. Salamanca-Levi, B. Muñoz-Cepeda, A. M. Obregón-Rivas, J. Cofré, M. Rondanelli-Reyes, I. Lamas, J. Troncoso, C. Toro, J. Cariñe, J. Staforelli-Vivanco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PollenBB16 is an RGB pollen image dataset of Chilean flora with pixel-accurate instance segmentation masks, whose annotation was fully verified by an expert palynologist to guarantee the taxonomic reliability of every published instance. The dataset is designed to close a concrete gap in existing palynological datasets, which typically combine low taxonomic diversity, few samples per class, and low-resolution crops restricted to bounding boxes. PollenBB16 contains 16,198 brightfield optical microscopy images at the native resolution of 3088 × 2064 pixels and 36,383 pixel-accurate polygons across 16 species from the Biobío Region, spanning endemic, native and exotic species of high ecological and melliferous value such as Eucryphia glutinosa and Quillaja saponaria (endemic), Gevuina avellana and Aristotelia chilensis (native), and Medicago sativa and Brassica rapa (introduced). Each spatial position is recorded at three focal planes. The displacement along the z axis reveals features of the exine together with information on the internal structure of the grain that remain inaccessible on a single plane. From this multifocal information, more robust convolutional networks can be trained with more accurate classification. The operational quality of the dataset is backed by a leakage-safe partition that keeps the three focal planes of the same position in the same subset to avoid metric inflation, complemented by a YOLO11n-seg baseline trained for 50 epochs that reaches 0.985 mask mAP@50 on the validation set, establishing a reproducible reference point. Beyond deep learning, PollenBB16 enables interdisciplinary applications in aerobiology, biodiversity monitoring under climate change, ecological restoration of the South American temperate forest, and botanical-origin authentication of Chilean monofloral honeys.

Background & Summary

Pollen grain identification is fundamental in melissopalynology for certifying the botanical origin of premium honeys1, in aerobiology for monitoring atmospheric pollen concentrations2,3, and in ecology for floristic diversity studies4,5. However, traditional identification through optical microscopy requires specialized palynologists and represents a laborious process that limits the scalability of these analyses6.

Deep learning has demonstrated outstanding capabilities for automating pollen recognition, achieving accuracies exceeding 95%7–9. YOLOv8-based systems have achieved mAP of 97.6% in Chilean monofloral honey certification10, and multifocal approaches have reached macro-F1 scores of 0.95611. Nevertheless, the development of robust models faces a critical bottleneck: the scarcity of publicly available annotated pollen datasets.

Available public pollen datasets typically present one of two complementary limitations: either low taxonomic diversity (few species) or a small image budget per species. POLEN23E provides only 805 images spread across 23 Brazilian species12, an average of approximately 35 images per class, a volume that in practice forces heavy reliance on data augmentation and transfer learning when training modern deep architectures from scratch. POLLEN73S extends taxonomic coverage to 73 species from the Brazilian Cerrado but distributes only 2,523 images in total13, again an average of about 35 images per class, so its bottleneck is not diversity but the small number of samples per species. Pollen13K, in contrast, contains more than 13,000 images but covers only 5 European species9 and is distributed as pre-cropped low-resolution patches (84 × 84 pixels) designed for classification in aerobiology applications, not for instance segmentation in full-resolution microscopy scenes required by melissopalynological analysis14. This combination—few datasets simultaneously offering sufficient taxonomic diversity and sufficient samples per class and full-resolution images with pixel-level annotations—limits the training of robust models and hinders cross-validation between studies.

A critical limitation of existing pollen datasets is the predominant use of simple bounding boxes for annotation instead of pixel-level instance segmentation masks. Bounding boxes only provide rectangular regions containing pollen grains, including significant background area and failing to capture the precise grain morphology—a fundamental aspect for palynological identification based on shape, aperture configuration, and exine ornamentation. Instance segmentation masks, by contrast, provide pixel-accurate delineation of each individual grain, enabling models to learn fine morphological features, facilitating precise grain counting in overlapping scenarios, supporting advanced tasks such as morphometric analysis and shape-based classification, and reducing background noise during feature extraction15. This distinction is particularly relevant for pollen analysis, where morphological details at the pixel level constitute diagnostic taxonomic characters.

To the best of our knowledge, no previously published pollen dataset simultaneously combines the following four elements: (i) brightfield RGB microscopy at the native resolution of 3088 × 2064 pixels, (ii) a multifocal acquisition protocol with three focal planes per spatial position, (iii) pixel-level instance segmentation masks (not classification labels nor bounding boxes), and (iv) species relevant to the South American temperate forest of the Biobío Region, Chile. This combination defines the gap that the present dataset is intended to close.

The situation is particularly critical for South American flora. Despite Chile harbouring 4,655 native vascular plant species with 46% endemism16, virtually no public pollen datasets exist for austral flora. The present dataset is focused on south-central Chile (Biobío Region), a biogeographic transition zone between the Mediterranean matorral and the temperate rainforest, and spans all three biogeographic categories of the Chilean flora: endemic species of high ecological and melliferous value such as Eucryphia glutinosa (guindo santo) and Quillaja saponaria (quillay), native species such as Gevuina avellana (Chilean hazelnut) and Aristotelia chilensis (maqui), and introduced species of apicultural and agronomic relevance such as Galega officinalis, Medicago sativa and Brassica rapa16,17. These species are not an arbitrary selection: they simultaneously concentrate value as (i) bioindicators of the temperate ecosystems of south-central Chile, (ii) sources of allergenic pollen of aerobiological interest, and (iii) nectar–pollen resources that sustain local beekeeping. In particular, guindo santo (Eucryphia glutinosa) is a recently characterised species whose honey is closely tied to the Mapuche-Pehuenche communities of the Alto Biobío and exhibits remarkable antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties10,18, while ulmo honey (Eucryphia cordifolia) has demonstrated antimicrobial activity superior to that of New Zealand Manuka honey19. Authentication of the botanical origin of Chilean monofloral honeys still faces the absence of standardised mechanisms equivalent to the Manuka UMF system, which has motivated the development of combined solutions based on physico-chemical analysis20, hyperspectral microscopy for detecting syrup adulterations21, and deep-learning-assisted classification over pollen counts10. The taxonomic coverage of PollenBB16 is directly applicable to (i) biodiversity monitoring in the Biobío biogeographic transition zone, (ii) aerobiological forecasting of allergenic pollen, (iii) studies of the impact of climate change on native flowerings, (iv) support of ecological restoration programmes for the South American temperate forest, and (v) botanical-origin certification of monofloral honeys of high commercial and cultural value.

To address this gap, we present the first high-resolution RGB pollen grain image dataset from Chilean flora with multifocal acquisition and pixel-accurate instance segmentation masks, designed for deep learning computer vision tasks. The dataset comprises 16,198 images with 36,383 instances across 16 classes of native and introduced species from the Biobío Region, acquired under a multifocal protocol that records three focal planes per spatial position—a strategy supported by recent evidence showing that z-multifocal information raises classification accuracy on pollen, reaching a macro-F1 of 0.956 with three planes11. Annotations were verified under expert palynologist supervision and are distributed in the YOLO segmentation format22,23, directly consumable by instance-segmentation frameworks without preprocessing.

Anthers were extracted from flowers using forceps and dissection scalpel, placing them inside centrifuge tubes. Acetolysis of plant samples was performed following Erdtman’s classic method24, consisting of subjecting pollen grains to boiling in a mixture of concentrated sulfuric acid and pure acetic anhydride in a 1:9 ratio. This chemical treatment destroys cellular content and intine, preserving the exine with its diagnostic morphological characteristics (ornamentation, apertures, shape) that allow taxonomic identification.

The resulting material was suspended in drops of a glycerin and water mixture (1:1), maintaining the suspension for at least 10 minutes to adequately hydrate the grains. Finally, samples were mounted and fixed on slides with coverslips for preservation and observation under optical microscope. This preparation protocol ensures that pollen grains present optimal morphological characteristics for identification and photography, with clearly visible diagnostic structures.

To ensure sample representativeness and reduce potential bias from individual preparations, at least two replicate slides were prepared for each species and genus combination. These replicates were obtained from different flower specimens when possible, ensuring morphological variability within each taxonomic class. The complete slide collection constitutes a physical pollen reference library (palinoteca) organized by region and species (Fig. 2), which serves as permanent reference material for taxonomic validation.Fig. 2Physical pollen reference library (palinoteca) from the Biobío Region. Image Acquisition System

The image acquisition system (Fig. 3) integrates optical, mechanical, electronic, and computational components in a modular architecture designed for automated microscopy. The optical core consists of a Nikon ECLIPSE Ci-L plus brightfield microscope equipped with a stabilized LED intensity light source and an optical condenser that, through Köhler illumination technique, eliminates image artifacts. The optical system employs a Plan Fluor 60× objective with numerical aperture of 0.85, maximizing spatial resolution for visualization of diagnostic morphological structures such as exine ornamentation, colpi, and pollen pores.Fig. 3Integrated architecture of the automated microscopy system for pollen image acquisition. The system comprises: a digital camera (Camera) mounted on the microscope’s upper optical port, a high-magnification objective lens (Objective Lens), a motorized positioning stage (Platform XY) with micrometric vertical focus adjustment (Manual Z Stage Control), a stabilized illumination system (Light Source), and a computational station running an ad-hoc developed control interface (Computer and GUI). Communication between peripherals is performed through USB buses (Camera USB Bus and Platform USB Bus) enabling synchronized control of image capture and platform movements.

Integrated architecture of the automated microscopy system for pollen image acquisition. The system comprises: a digital camera (Camera) mounted on the microscope’s upper optical port, a high-magnification objective lens (Objective Lens), a motorized positioning stage (Platform XY) with micrometric vertical focus adjustment (Manual Z Stage Control), a stabilized illumination system (Light Source), and a computational station running an ad-hoc developed control interface (Computer and GUI). Communication between peripherals is performed through USB buses (Camera USB Bus and Platform USB Bus) enabling synchronized control of image capture and platform movements.

The Thorlabs MLS203 motorized XY positioning platform provides bidimensional displacement with submicrometric resolution through stepper motor micro-actuators with microstepping, enabling both automated raster scanning of complete preparations and point positioning for directed capture of individual grains. Vertical focus control is performed through manual adjustment of the microscope Z control, with micrometric graduation allowing precise and reproducible vertical displacements between focal planes for z-stacking protocols.

Image acquisition is performed using a Basler acA3088-57ucMED digital camera, with native resolution of 3088 × 2064 pixels (6.37 megapixels), pixel size of 2.4 μm × 2.4 μm, color depth of 8 bits per RGB channel in sRGB color space, and transfer speed up to 59 fps at full resolution via USB 3.0 interface. The camera is configured to maintain consistent image quality across all acquisitions: color temperature is calibrated to 6000 K to match the LED microscope illumination spectrum, ensuring faithful reproduction of pollen exine pigmentation and surface characteristics; manual white balance and gain control guarantee color consistency and minimize electronic noise across the dataset; and gamma correction set to 1.0 preserves linear intensity response for accurate morphological representation without artificial contrast enhancement.

The computational system integrates a workstation with solid-state storage for fast writing of uncompressed image files. Control software was developed ad-hoc implementing bidirectional communication with peripherals through USB protocols, enabling synchronized control of image capture and platform movements for fully automated operation.

The peripheral connection module manages initialization and status monitoring of the Basler camera and Thorlabs motorized platform, with visual connection status indicators and exception handling for automatic reconnection upon transient disconnections. The taxonomic management module allows the operator to select pollen species and genera from a preloaded database with Linnaean binomial nomenclature (genus + species), or create new classes through editable fields, ensuring consistency in labeling of captured images.

The movement control panel provides directional buttons for fine manual displacement of the XY platform, allowing systematic exploration of microscopic preparations and precise positioning over regions of interest. The scanning module defines a capture protocol by specifying the analysis area through absolute coordinates, the number of steps in the X and Y axes, the trajectory geometry (linear raster or optimised serpentine), and the separation between capture points. Acquisition is semi-automatic: the operator advances the stage step by step along the programmed trajectory while monitoring the real-time RGB stream in the central visualisation window and, when a pollen grain is detected, pauses the scan to acquire the three focal planes (fp0, fp1, fp2) at that position. All captures are recorded with position metadata and timestamps in a chronological event log, ensuring experimental traceability and enabling subsequent spatial reconstruction.

Multifocal Operation and Acquisition Protocol

The image acquisition process follows a standardized protocol that ensures taxonomic traceability from the moment of capture. Each slide was scanned until between 950 and 1,050 images containing pollen grains had been acquired, with the exact number adjusted by the operator according to the observed grain density on the preparation: slides of species with sparser grain distribution were scanned over a larger area until the target range was reached, whereas slides with denser preparations reached the same range within a smaller scanned area. Before initiating an acquisition session, the operator programs a scanning routine in the GUI defining the analysis area through absolute coordinates (X, Y) delimiting the preparation region to be scanned, the number of steps that will form the capture grid on each axis, trajectory geometry (normally in raster mode to maximize systematic coverage), and taxonomic information corresponding to the pollen species and genus present in the preparation.

During routine execution, when pollen grains are identified in the field of view, the z-stacking protocol is executed, capturing three focal planes per spatial position through controlled displacement of the microscope Z control (Fig. 5). The central plane fp0 is defined as the optimal focus plane selected by the operator for each grain: the plane in which the external grain contour appears sharpest and the exine ornamentation is resolved most clearly under the numerical aperture of the Plan Fluor 60× objective. The two flanking planes fp1 and fp2 are then captured by symmetric displacements of ±5 μm along the optical axis. This ±5 μm offset corresponds to the minimum reproducible step allowed by the microscope’s fine Z-axis control (1 μm increments) and is sufficient to reveal the depth-varying diagnostic structures (apertures, exine ornamentation, internal content) without the redundancy and storage cost of a denser z-stack. The three-plane convention is consistent with previous multifocal pollen classification work11 and provides systematic volumetric sampling of each pollen grain at a storage and acquisition cost compatible with large-scale scanning campaigns.Fig. 5Z-stacking protocol with three focal planes. The system automatically stores each image with a standardized nomenclature encoding taxonomic, spatial and focal-plane information:

{Species}_{Genus}_{Region}_{Country}_{Rxx}_{Xnnnn}_{Ynnnn}_fp0,1,2.jpg

For example: Schinus_Polygamus_Biobio_Chile_R06_X6750_Y3250_fp1.jpg

This naming convention encodes the species, genus, geographic region, country, replicate slide (Rxx), the absolute stage position of the capture in micrometres along the X and Y axes (Xnnnn, Ynnnn) and the focal-plane index (fp0, fp1, fp2) corresponding to the three z-stack planes acquired at each spatial position. The three focal-plane files that share the same {Species}_{Genus}_{Rxx}_{Xnnnn}_{Ynnnn} stem therefore correspond to the same physical pollen grain imaged at −5, 0 and +5 μm relative to the optimal focus, providing a direct link between each file and its spatial and taxonomic origin. The expert palynologist periodically supervises captures to verify the taxonomic identification performed by the operator during acquisition.

Grains in the preparations are randomly oriented, so the acquired images include polar, equatorial and intermediate views for every species.

Labeling and Annotation Protocol

The annotation process follows the hierarchical role structure defined in Table 1 and produces pixel-level instance segmentation masks that delineate each pollen grain with its diagnostic features (shape, aperture configuration, exine ornamentation), information that rectangular boxes cannot resolve. Taxonomic labels (species and genus) are derived directly from the image filename established during acquisition, so no manual classification is needed at the annotation stage. Annotation is performed on the Roboflow web platform, whose Smart Polygon tool wraps a SAM2 backbone25: the annotator places positive click points inside the grain, accepts the candidate mask returned by SAM2, or refines it vertex by vertex when the automatic mask fails (typically on low-contrast borders or strongly overlapping grains). When two or more grains overlap, each is annotated as a separate instance: the upper, fully visible grain is traced along its complete contour, while the occluded lower grain is traced only along its visible portion and stops where the upper grain covers it, with no extrapolation of the hidden section. Grains that cross the image border follow the same rule, bounded by the field-of-view edge. Out-of-focus debris, tissue fragments and non-pollen particles are not annotated. All polygons are finally verified by the expert palynologist, who validates both the species label and the contour and acts as the final adjudicator; every annotation distributed in the public archive has passed this verification step. Because fp0 is selected by the acquisition operator as the plane where the external contour and exine ornamentation of the grain reach maximum contrast, the segmentation polygon is traced once on fp0 and propagated identically to fp1 and fp2, which share the same external silhouette within the ±5 μm z-stack range and differ only in internal diagnostic content (apertures, internal material). As a result, the labels/*.txt files of the three siblings at a given position are byte-identical by construction.

Data Overview

The archive contains 16,198 RGB microscopy images with 36,383 annotated pollen grain instances (average of 2.25 instances per image, median of 1, maximum of 19; mean of 16.1 vertices per polygon), covering 16 plant species organized in 13 botanical families (Table 3). Images were acquired across three focal planes per field of view at the native camera resolution of 3088 × 2064 pixels (6.37 megapixels, 8 bits per RGB channel, sRGB), and capture pollen grains in diverse spatial orientations including polar, equatorial, and intermediate views. The archive also includes images without pollen grains (null examples) that can be used as negative examples during training.

Data Records

The dataset is available at Zenodo26 under 10.5281/zenodo.19830051, released under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license. The release is a single flat archive whose top-level directories describe every file an end user needs. There are no auxiliary per-split image folders, no duplicated copies of the data, and no framework-specific wrappers: the archive contains the full-resolution images, the polygon labels in YOLO segmentation format, a convenience folder with derived bounding-box labels in YOLO detection format, the class map, the plain-text split definitions, and the script that regenerates the bounding-box labels from the polygons.pollenbb16/images/# 16,198 JPEG files, 3088 x 2064 pxlabels/# 16,198 .txt files (YOLO polygon format)labels_bb/# 16,198 .txt files (YOLO bounding-box format)data.yaml# class names (0..15) and pathssplits/train.txt# 11,333 image pathsval.txt# 2,429 image pathstest.txt# 2,436 image pathsscripts/polygons_to_bboxes.py# regenerates labels_bb/ from labels/

Images

Each file in images/ is a JPEG at 3088 × 2064 pixels (6.37 megapixels, 8 bits per RGB channel, sRGB).

Filename Convention

Image filenames encode acquisition metadata as

{Species}_{Genus}_{Region}_{Country}_{Rxx}_{Xnnnn}_{Ynnnn}_fp0,1,2.jpg

where Species and Genus are the capitalised binomial tokens (e.g. Anthemis_Cotula), Region and Country identify the sampling locality (e.g. Biobio_Chile), Rxx is the biological replica index with two-digit zero padding (e.g. R03), Xnnnn and Ynnnn are the absolute stage coordinates in micrometres, and fp0, fp1, fp2 are the three focal planes of the z-stack. A valid example is Anthemis_Cotula_Biobio_Chile_R03_X6750_Y3250_fp0.jpg. The same basename is used in labels/ and labels_bb/ with the .txt extension.

Labels

For every image there is a corresponding .txt file in labels/ with the same basename. Each line encodes one annotated pollen grain as

<class_id> <x1> <y1> <x2> <y2> ... <xn> <yn>

where class_id is an integer in {0, …, 15} and each (xi, yi) pair is a polygon vertex with coordinates normalized to [0, 1] relative to the image width and height. Vertices are ordered sequentially along the grain contour. The pixel-level polygons in labels/ are the primary annotation resource of the dataset.

Bounding-Box Labels

The labels_bb/ directory ships one YOLO detection-format .txt file per image, with the same basename as the image and the polygon label. Each line encodes one annotated grain as

<class_id> <cx> <cy> <w> <h>

where (cx, cy) is the centre of the axis-aligned bounding box and (w, h) its width and height, all normalised to [0, 1]. These boxes are not an independent annotation layer: they are the axis-aligned envelope of the matching polygon in labels/, generated by scripts/polygons_to_bboxes.py. The folder is provided as a convenience for users consuming detection-only pipelines; box and polygon labels share basename, class_id and instance ordering, so the two directories can be used interchangeably by framework-specific loaders.

Class Map

The data.yaml file follows the YOLO convention23 and lists the number of classes (nc: 16) together with the mapping between numerical indices and taxonomic binomial names in lowercase with underscores (e.g. conium_maculatum, eucryphia_glutinosa).

Splits

The splits/ directory contains three plain-text files, one per subset, with one image path per line. These files are the authoritative definition of the training, validation and test partitions; the split construction protocol and its leakage-safety rationale are described in the Technical Validation section.

Technical Validation

The reliability of the dataset is established through a multi-stage validation framework that guarantees taxonomic accuracy, optical quality, and annotation precision at all levels of the generation process. This technical robustness is achieved through standardized protocols, comprehensive expert supervision, and a quantitative deep-learning baseline.

Taxonomic Traceability and Sample Integrity

Taxonomic assignment is ensured from the sample origin: botanical identification of source plants guarantees that each slide contains exclusively pollen from a single species. Anthers dissected from botanically identified specimens are processed using Erdtman’s acetolysis standardized protocol24, which removes cytoplasmic content while preserving diagnostic exine structures. Taxonomic information is encoded in image filenames during acquisition, establishing full traceability from botanical source to digital record.

Imaging and Optical Quality Control

The optical system ensures consistent image quality across the entire dataset. Köhler illumination eliminates artifacts and provides uniform field illumination, while the Plan Fluor 60× objective (NA 0.85) combined with 2.4 μm pixel size provides sufficient spatial resolution to resolve the finest exine ornamentation details critical for taxonomic identification. Acquisition of three focal planes per field at standardized ±5 μm intervals (achieved through fine Z-axis control with 1 μm steps) captures the optical volume necessary to represent depth-varying morphological features, ensuring consistent volumetric sampling across the entire dataset.

Annotation Accuracy

The annotation workflow implements a hierarchical Assistant–Expert protocol that guarantees accuracy while maintaining efficiency. The palynology assistant generated initial segmentation polygons on the Roboflow platform using its SAM2-backed Smart Polygon tool25, with taxonomic class assignment derived directly from image filenames. Critically, a senior expert palynologist reviewed 100% of the 36,383 annotated instances, validating both taxonomic assignment based on diagnostic morphological characteristics (aperture type, shape, exine ornamentation) and boundary definition accuracy of segmentation masks. The published polygons contain on average 16.1 ± 2.7 vertices (range 6–33), a resolution sufficient to represent exine ornamentation without overfitting contour sampling noise. During this expert review, annotations were manually refined, corrected, or discarded according to palynological criteria, consulting reference literature and physical pollen collection specimens when necessary.

The dataset leader performed a final comprehensive review including statistical validation of instance distribution, verification of technical specifications, and confirmation of correspondence between image and annotation files before publication.

Inter-Annotator Agreement

To quantify inter-annotator agreement, a study was carried out on a subset of 160 images (10 per species), annotated independently by the annotators and by the expert palynologist. Both sets of polygons were compared instance by instance via greedy Intersection over Union (IoU)27 matching.

Table 4 summarises the resulting agreement. Geometric overlap is high: the mean instance-level IoU is 0.938 ± 0.066 with a median of 0.960. The mean absolute relative error in polygon area is 5.35%, and taxonomic class agreement is 100%: whenever the two annotators segmented the same grain, they also agreed on its species.

Agreement is uniformly high across species: the mean IoU stays well above 0.9, the relative area error remains near 5%, and the two annotators never disagree on the taxonomic identity of a matched grain. The small residual dispersion is consistent with the intrinsic subjectivity of deciding where the visible contour ends on partially occluded grains, and in the published corpus such cases are adjudicated by the expert palynologist before release.

Leakage-Safe Split Protocol

The splits distributed in splits/ are constructed by grouping all images that share the same spatial position on a slide so that the three focal planes of a single field are always assigned to the same subset (Table 5). This prevents the near-identical frames of a z-stack from being split between training and evaluation, which would otherwise inflate validation and test metrics. Deep Learning Baseline

A quantitative deep-learning baseline was trained on the train split and evaluated on the val split as defined in Table 5. The model is a YOLO11n-seg instance segmentation network23 (nano configuration, ≈ 2.8 M parameters) trained for 50 epochs from the official COCO-pretrained weights, using the native image resolution. No architectural or loss-function modifications were introduced; the baseline is deliberately kept as the stock configuration so that the reported numbers are reproducible from the shipped data.yaml and splits/ files alone.

The dataset ships only the pixel-level polygon masks. Bounding boxes are derived at load time by the YOLO loader as the axis-aligned envelope of each polygon, so a single YOLO11n-seg training run yields both box and mask metrics from the same weights. The box–mask comparison reported here is therefore intrinsically controlled.

On the validation set the model reaches 0.984 box mAP@50 and 0.907 box mAP@50:95, with closely matched mask values of 0.985 and 0.859, confirming that the pixel-level masks are recovered at the same precision as the bounding boxes. Mean precision and recall exceed 0.95 and 0.96 respectively for both detection and segmentation heads. Per-class metrics are reported in Table 7. Usage Notes

Recommended Applications

The dataset is designed to support various computer vision tasks. It can be used for training instance detection and segmentation models (YOLO v11-seg, YOLO v12-seg, Mask R-CNN, or other instance segmentation architectures) for simultaneous pollen grain detection and pixel-accurate segmentation, for training CNN classifiers (ResNet, EfficientNet, Vision Transformers) using individual grain crops extracted through segmentation masks to eliminate background noise, for developing automated counting pipelines for quantitative palynological analysis with precise grain delineation, and for morphometric studies leveraging pixel-level masks to extract shape descriptors and morphological parameters.

Using the Shipped Splits

End users are encouraged to consume the dataset through the split files provided in splits/ and the class map in data.yaml. Because those splits are leakage-safe with respect to the multifocal z-stack structure (Table 5), models trained and evaluated against them inherit a meaningful train/validation/test separation; reshuffling the data without respecting the spatial grouping of the three focal planes will systematically leak information between subsets and over-estimate performance.

Format Conversion and Reproducible Analyses

The project repository ships reusable analysis notebooks (analysis/) that (i) regenerate the dataset statistics and the morphometric figure, (ii) compare image sharpness across the three focal planes inside the masked grain region, and (iii) convert the annotations between the YOLO segmentation, YOLO bounding-box and COCO instance-segmentation formats. The notebooks consume only the files distributed under pollenbb16/ and produce reproducible tables and figures with a single shared graphical style, so that downstream users can rerun the analyses or retarget the dataset to alternative training frameworks (e.g. Mask R-CNN via the COCO format) without ad-hoc preprocessing.

Class Imbalance

The dataset presents a natural class imbalance (3.75:1 ratio between extreme classes) that reflects the actual conditions of the generation process. This imbalance originates from two main factors: the complexity of the manual labeling process, which requires expert supervision and considerable time per annotated instance, and the variable availability of pollen extracted from each species. In some cases, the slide contains scarce pollen material, either because the acetolysis process partially destroys more fragile grains, or due to the intrinsically low density of the sample prepared from available anthers.

It is recommended to use data augmentation techniques28–30 to balance classes during model training, applying specific transformations to minority classes.

Known Limitations

The dataset presents some limitations that users should consider. It represents only 16 of the more than 500 melliferous species present in the Biobío Region. Images come from acetolyzed preparations, which may differ morphologically from fresh pollen.
