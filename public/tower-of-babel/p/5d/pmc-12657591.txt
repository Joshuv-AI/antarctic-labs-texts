Epidemiology of fungal urinary tract infections: A retrospective case series
Authors: Aiah M Khateb, Lina S Alamri, Ward A Alseyoufi, Yara H Almusaiferi, Osama Y Safdar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFungal urinary tract infections (FUTIs) are a growing concern among hospitalized patients, with Candida albicans being the most common causative agent. The aim of this case series was to describe the clinical and microbiological characteristics of FUTIs in patients admitted to a key hospital in the Madinah region.Materials and methodsThis retrospective observational study used data from all hospitalized patients with positive urine cultures for fungal infections accompanied by clinical symptoms at King Fahd Hospital between June 2016 and October 2019. Patient demographics, clinical comorbidities, and microbiological data were collected for analysis.ResultsDuring the study period, 46 patients with confirmed FUTIs were identified from 87,447 admissions. There was a dramatic increase in the number of cases every year. C. albicans was the most frequently isolated organism, accounting for 86.9 % cases. Renal disease was the most prevalent comorbidity.ConclusionThis case series highlights the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with confirmed FUTIs, providing valuable insights into the increasing burden of these infections in hospital settings. The findings underscore the importance of understanding risk factors and the need for continuous surveillance to inform clinical practice and guide prompt and effective intervention.Keywords: Fungal urinary tract infections, Candiduria, Candida albicans, Epidemiology, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) occur when microbes enter the urethra and infect the urinary tract. These microorganisms often originate from the skin or rectum. Although UTIs can affect different parts of the urinary tract, bladder infections (cystitis) are the most common type [1]. The incidence of cystitis in women is 50–80 cases per 100,000 people, whereas the incidence in men of the same age is 5–7 cases per 100,000 people. Symptoms of UTI include painful or burning urination, frequent urges to urinate with minimal output, pressure in the lower abdomen, and cloudy or foul-smelling urine. Blood in the urine is more common among younger female individuals, whereas older women may feel weak, disoriented, tired, and/or unsteady. The presence of fever may indicate infection spread to the kidneys [2].

Fungi are rare but important causes of UTI [3]. The most common fungal species causing UTI is Candida albicans (30 %), while Nakaseomyces glabratus is the most frequently isolated non-Candida species (28.8 %). Candida kefyr is the least common species, accounting for 2.5 % cases [4]. C. albicans, which exists naturally in the human body, is essential for maintaining a healthy microbiome. It typically coexists harmlessly in healthy skin and organs, including the mouth, throat, gut, and vagina [5]. However, when the balance between normal flora and yeast is disrupted, Candida can overgrow and cause an infection known as candiduria. Candiduria is commonly observed in hospitalized patients and associated with conditions such as diabetes mellitus, broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, indwelling urinary devices, and structural abnormalities in the genitourinary tract [6].

The pathogenesis of C. albicans infection is aided by various virulence factors, including adhesion molecules, morphogenesis (reversible transition from unicellular yeast cells to filamentous forms), and secreted aspartyl proteases. These proteases can induce macrophage chemotaxis, modify the host complement cascade, and damage epithelial cells. Phospholipases are also involved, helping in host identification, nutrient uptake, and tissue penetration. In addition, “phenotypic switching” in C. albicans and other species is associated with changes in tissue affinity, colony morphology, and antigen production. This switching may provide the flexibility required for the organism to adapt to the harsh conditions in the host environment. According to previous data, the most common diagnosis among affected patients was chronic kidney disease (CKD), with 46 observations [7]. CKD involves prolonged inflammation, which diminishes the body's ability to fight infections, thereby increasing the infection risk [7].

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the risk factors, clinical manifestations, and appropriate management of fungal urinary tract infections (FUTIs) to help ensure prompt and effective intervention. We hypothesized that the prevalence of FUTIs is increasing in the Madinah region of Saudi Arabia. In this study, the aim is to determine temporal trends in the prevalence of FUTIs by systematically reviewing urine culture data and corresponding clinical notes from hospitalized patients at King Fahd Hospital in Madinah, Saudi Arabia. Recent evidence suggests a notable rise in FUTI cases in the region, particularly those linked to Candida species, but there is a lack of comprehensive epidemiological data from official sources, such as the Ministry of Health. This research addresses the gap by testing a precise and meaningful hypothesis about the local FUTI burden, providing clarity for future surveillance and management strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study design and population

In this retrospective case series, data were extracted for all hospitalized patients who showed positive urine cultures for fungi across all departments at King Fahad General Hospital in Madinah, Saudi Arabia from June 2016 to October 2019. Collected data comprised the initial diagnosis, age, sex, comorbidities, detected species, and resistance profiles for all FUTI episodes. For patients admitted more than once, a new episode was counted each time. A colony count of ≥104–105 colony-forming units/mL in urine culture was considered significant for existing FUTIs.

Urine samples were collected when there were clinical indications of infection. In high-risk cases, Myco/F Lytic culture vials (Becton, Dickinson and Company, New Jersey, in the United States) were used for fungal cultures. Cultures were grown on specific media optimized for fungal growth, allowing for the observation of colony morphology and identification. Relevant staining techniques were used to highlight fungal elements. Positive urine cultures were processed for further identification using potassium hydroxide. Yeasts were identified by conventional methods and subjected to sensitivity testing using the VITEK 2 system (bioMérieux, Marcy-L'Étoile, France), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. In addition, the new Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute recommendations served as the basis for interpretation of the antifungal susceptibility reporting criterion.

Statistical analysis

SPSS for Windows Version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). was used for data analysis. Static assets were used to examined the precise relationship between variables, and basic descriptors such as frequencies, medians, and incidence rates per 1000 individuals were also calculated for each year. Prevalence analysis was performed according to the total number of hospital admissions. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to determine the disease distribution by sex, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. In addition, linear regression analysis was performed using Microsoft® Excel® for Microsoft 365 MSO (Version 2403 Build 16.0.17425.20124) 64-bit.

Results

Demographic and epidemiological characteristics

The total number of hospital admissions over the 4-year period was 87,447, and the prevalence of FUTIs in the 46 patients (81 episodes) was 0.052 %. There was a notable increase in prevalence over the years: 2.1 % (n = 1) in 2016, 6.5 % (n = 3) in 2017, 8.6 % (n = 4) in 2018, and 82.6 % (n = 38) in 2019. The incidence rates per 1000 individuals were as follows: 5.4 in 2016, 16.3 in 2017, 21 in 2018, and 206 in 2019. These data indicated a significant increase (approximately 40-fold increase) from 2016 to 2019 (Table 1).

Candida albicans is the most frequently isolated organism, accounting for 50 % women (n = 23) and 37 % men (n = 17). This indicates a higher overall prevalence among women in the study cohort. Other identified species included Candida glabrata, Candida parapsilosis, and Trichosporon mucoides.

In the context of comorbidities, urinary system diseases were the most prevalent, observed in 24.53 % (n = 13) patients. Gastrointestinal (GI) disorders were present in 13.21 % (n = 7) patients. Respiratory and cardiac system disorders were each identified in 11.32 % (n = 6) patients. Central nervous system (CNS) disorders and cancer were each observed in 5.66 % (n = 3) patients. Stroke was the least common, observed in 3.77 % (n = 2) patients. The “Others” category included 21.43 % (n = 12) patients; within this category, sepsis accounted for 6.5 % (n = 3) patients while diabetes mellitus accounted for 4.35 % (n = 2) patients. Furthermore, specific conditions such as shock, anemia, congenital malformation of the peripheral vascular system, systemic lupus erythematosus, Guillain–Barre syndrome, cutaneous leishmaniasis, and unspecified disorientation were observed in one patient each, contributing to a total percentage of 2.17 %.

Regression analysis revealed valuable insights into the relationship between risk factors and outcomes. The R2 value of 0.370 suggested that approximately 37 % variance in the outcome was explained by the factors in our model. Age and sex were significant predictors in our analysis, with P-values of 0.001 and 0.0167, respectively; this indicated that they influenced the outcomes by varying degrees. Among the comorbidities, urinary system and GI disorders showed nonsignificant impacts, with P-values of 0.483 and 0.856, respectively. Respiratory and cardiac disorders also lacked significant associations, with P-values of 0.4331 and 0.449, respectively. Cancer and stroke displayed potential associations, with lower but statistically insignificant P-values of 0.908 and 0.373, respectively. In addition, conditions in the “Others” category did not show a significant association with the outcome, as reflected by an overall P-value of 0.432 (Table 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective observational study on the prevalence and characteristics of FUTIs in 46 patients, C. albicans was identified in 85 % fungus-positive samples, consistent with the findings in previous studies where C. albicans was the most commonly isolated fungal species. However, non-C. albicans spp. Were also isolated (Candida tropicalis and C. glabrata) [8]. These results support our theory that Madinah has a high frequency of FUTIs. As expected, the frequency of FUTI-positive samples was slightly higher for female patients (52 %) than for male patients (48 %). This observation was consistent with that in a previous study showing positivity rates of 53 % and 47 % for female and male patients, respectively [9], and it can be attributed to the short urethra and proximity to the rectum in female individuals.

In the present study, all 46 patients showed different comorbidities with FUTIs, including cardiovascular diseases (e.g., acute endocarditis), renal diseases (e.g., acute kidney failure), GI diseases (e.g., chronic gastritis), sepsis, and shock. This highlights the importance of understanding their associations with and effects on FUTIs in order to provide comprehensive management and minimize the associated risks. Urinary system diseases were the most common (24.53 %) comorbidities. Chronic renal conditions or kidney transplantation affect the immune system, thus making the patient more susceptible to infections with Candida species. In particular, C. albicans was found to colonize the GI tract of immunocompromised patients with a prevalence of 13.21 % [10,11]. Both cardiovascular and respiratory system disorders were observed at a frequency of 11.32 %; this might indicate shared risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, immune diseases, chronic inflammation, and hospitalization. Meanwhile, both CNS disorders and cancer were observed at a rate of 5.66 % in infected patients; this may indicate the progression of the fungus into the bloodstream, leading to fungal dissemination and CNS involvement. Patients with cancer are at an increased risk of developing FUTIs because of chemotherapy-induced immunosuppression. A Brazilian retrospective observational study showed consistent results, as renal system diseases were the most observed (47 %) among patients with FUTIs, followed by patients with immunosuppression (34 %; probably because of chemotherapy and transplantation [12].

Another study from Turkey [13] showed that 37.8 % patients with FUTIs had comorbidities with different frequencies from those observed in our study. Diabetes mellitus was the most common (7.81 %), followed by respiratory system disorders (7.30 %), cancer (4.41 %), cardiovascular disorders (4.16 %), and urinary system diseases (3.99 %). The differences in results may be attributed to various factors such as genetic predisposition, behavioral habits, structural abnormalities in the urinary system, diabetic state, weak or compromised immune system, pregnancy, high blood pressure, kidney stone formation, other infections, and medical procedures like catheterization [14].

Analysis of the positive sample distribution revealed that a considerable number of episodes occurred between June 2016 and October 2019. The prevalence increased from 2.1 % (n = 1) in 2016 to 82.6 % (n = 38) in 2019. This indicated a gradual increase over the years, specifically in 2019. The increasing incidence of FUTIs, which were mostly associated with urinary system diseases in our study, might be due to the invasion of Candida spp., particularly in hospitalized patients. Thus, it may reflect a real epidemiological trend driven by a high-risk patient population with severe comorbidities, or it could be a consequence of increased clinical awareness. The growing body of literature on fungal–bacterial co-infections and a lower diagnostic threshold among physicians may have led to more frequent fungal cultures for at-risk patients, which increased the detection rate, rather than a true change in prevalence. In contrast, in a previous study, the prevalence ranged from 8.1 % in 2017 to 5.4 % in 2021. This difference in findings could be associated with the sample size, which was smaller in our study than in the previous study. In addition, it could be affected by the geographical location [15]. Antifungal drugs should be appropriately prescribed to patients with FUTIs and other underlying medical conditions. Importantly, the patient's clinical state determines the best treatment for FUTIs. The treatment of FUTIs depends on various factors such as symptoms, type of fungus causing the infection, and effective patient management. To ensure the correct course of treatment and effective patient management, it is imperative to identify the specific species of Candida that is causing the disease and any underlying conditions or risk factors. Some fungal species may be more resistant to treatment or cause more severe complications. The severity of the infection is also considered when determining the treatment plan [16].

In our study, risk factors and comorbidities were analyzed using regression analysis of all factors that could contribute to FUTIs. Only two factors were statistically significant: sex (0.016) and age (0.0015). The ages were distributed into groups as follows: teens group 2.17 % (n = 1), adults’ group 39.1 % (n = 18), and the largest group was the seniors, representing 58.69 % (n = 27) of all patients' total. In addition, the female predominance could be due to the anatomical structure of the female urinary system and its vulnerability to vaginal infections because of its proximity to the anus [17]. In a previous study, significant risk factors for FUTIs were urinary catheterization (43.5 %), diabetes mellitus (33.6 %), surgical procedures (22.7 %), use of broad-spectrum antibiotics (22.7 %), pregnancy (21.7 %), use of systemic corticosteroids (6.9 %), and cancer (0.9 %). Our results were probably different from these previous results because of differences in the sample size (our study had a smaller sample size), the studied risk factors, and the geographic location [18].

Candida overgrowth can cause multiple medical conditions such as oral thrush, diaper rash in newborns, and vaginal yeast infection [18]. This emphasizes the importance of maintaining healthy microbial flora to prevent this kind of overgrowth and the accompanying health issues. In the present study, C. albicans was the most common causative fungus because of its pathogenicity, propensity to produce biofilms, and presence in the human microbiome. Although other species of Candida may have similar virulence characteristics, C. albicans is more capable of infecting and colonizing host tissues. Its importance in UTIs is attributed to its interaction with the host immune system, biofilm formation, and possible antibiotic resistance [19,20]. Nevertheless, other studies have revealed contrasting findings, with non-Candida species being the most isolated fungi (n = 297; 86.3 %) and C. albicans accounting for only 13.7 % (n = 47) cases [12]. Numerous factors, including local epidemiology, patient characteristics, patterns of antibiotic usage, diagnostic techniques, and possible sampling bias, may be responsible for this difference in results.

In our study, yeasts (6.5 %, n = 3), T. mucoides (2.2 %, n = 1), C. glabrata (2.2 %, n = 1), and C. parapsilosis (2.2 %, n = 1) were isolated in addition to C. albicans. There are numerous reasons why these species are less common than C. albicans, including the status of the host's immune system, the ability to adhere and colonize, ecological habitat, and antifungal susceptibility. The subungual region is the most common site of C. parapsilosis isolation in humans [20]. Despite being a common oral, skin, and nail flora, Trichosporon causes both superficial and profound infections in humans [21]. C. glabrata, on the other hand, is a commensal in human mucosa, specifically the vaginal, gastrointestinal, and oral epithelia. However, it lacks the harsh characteristics of other species of Candida, namely the ability to create filaments that cause tissue damage and immune cell lysis [22].

The retrospective nature of this study and its reliance on the hospital records may have introduced bias, and the applicability of the findings to other populations or regions may be limited. Our results may not be generalizable to less severe FUTI cases that occur in outpatient or nonhospital settings [23,24]. Considering the current findings, further studies on FUTI should focus on delineating optimal management strategies while effectively addressing existing limitations. This entails further investigation into novel therapeutic approaches, refinement of diagnostic methods for early detection, use of more recent data to track this ongoing epidemiological shift, exploration of antimicrobial stewardship programs, and enhancement of our understanding of host–pathogen interactions to develop targeted interventions. Additionally, efforts should be directed towards overcoming challenges such as limited antifungal drug options, increasing rates of drug resistance, and the need for standardized guidelines to streamline clinical decision-making. By prioritizing these avenues, future research endeavors aim to significantly advance our ability to effectively manage candiduria, ultimately improving patient outcomes and reducing the healthcare burden.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the analysis of urine samples collected over a 4-year period revealed a significant increase in FUTIs, particularly candiduria, in the Madinah region of Saudi Arabia. Our findings revealed that C. albicans was the most common species responsible for the majority of FUTI cases, consistent with previous findings highlighting its pathogenicity. Non-C. albicans species such as C. glabrata and C. parapsilosis were also identified. In addition, we identified various comorbidities associated with FUTIs, highlighting the causes and associations between underlying health conditions and the susceptibility to FUTIs. Furthermore, our analysis highlights the importance of sex and age as risk factors for FUTIs, with female individuals and seniors being more susceptible to infection.

Glossary

Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs): Infections that occur when microbes, often originating from the skin or rectum, enter the urethra and infect the urinary tract.

Cystitis: The most common type of UTI, which is an infection of the bladder.

Fungi: A rare but important cause of UTIs.

Candida albicans: The most common fungal species causing UTI, which is a natural part of the human body's healthy microbiome.

Nakaseomyces glabratus: The most frequently isolated non-Candida species causing UTI.

Candiduria: An infection caused by the overgrowth of Candida in the urinary tract.

Virulence Factors: Elements that help C. albicans cause infection, including adhesion molecules, morphogenesis, secreted aspartyl proteases, and phospholipases.

Morphogenesis: The reversible transition from unicellular yeast cells to filamentous forms, a virulence factor of C. albicans.

Secreted Aspartyl Proteases: A type of virulence factor that can induce macrophage chemotaxis and damage host cells.

Phenotypic Switching: Changes in a fungus's tissue affinity, colony morphology, and antigen production, which helps it adapt to the host environment.

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD): A common diagnosis among affected patients that involves prolonged inflammation, increasing the risk of infection.

Fungal Urinary Tract Infections (FUTIs): An infection of the urinary tract caused by fungi.

Comorbidities: Other diseases or conditions present in patients with FUTIs, such as urinary system diseases, gastrointestinal disorders, and cancer.

Colony-Forming Units/mL (CFU/mL): A measurement used in urine culture, where a count of ≥104–105 is considered significant for FUTIs.

Retrospective Case Series: A type of study design where data are extracted for all patients who meet certain criteria over a past period.

Myco/F Lytic Culture Vials: A specific type of vial used for fungal cultures in high-risk cases.

VITEK 2 System: A system used for identifying yeasts and conducting sensitivity testing.

Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI): An organization whose recommendations are used for interpreting antifungal suscepticbility results.

SPSS: Statistical software used for data analysis.

Mann–Whitney U Test: A statistical test used to determine the distribution of disease by sex.

Regression Analysis: A statistical method used to analyze the relationship between risk factors and outcomes.

R2 value: A statistical value indicating the percentage of variance in the outcome that can be explained by the factors in the study's model.

P-value: A statistical value used to determine significance, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.
