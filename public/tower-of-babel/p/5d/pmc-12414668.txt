Endovascular Salvage of Iliac Limb Maldeployment during EVAR Using a CODA Balloon and Bridging Stent: A Case Report
Authors: Dipankar Mukherjee, Bibhas Amatya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Iliac limb maldeployment during endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) is an uncommon but technically challenging complication. In this study, we present a case involving a patient with multiple comorbidities, including hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and coronary artery disease, who underwent EVAR for a progressively enlarging abdominal aortic aneurysm using the ALTO endograft. During the procedure, the right iliac limb was inadvertently deployed outside the contralateral gate into the aneurysm sac, resulting in maldeployment. This complication was successfully managed with endovascular salvage using a CODA balloon and a bridging stent graft. This case illustrates a variation of endovascular correction strategy adapted to the ALTO platform.

INTRODUCTION

Endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) has become the preferred treatment for abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAAs) in anatomically suitable patients [1]. While generally safe and effective, complications such as iliac extension graft maldeployment may occur and present significant technical challenges. If left uncorrected, maldeployment can lead to type III endoleak, graft compression or kinking, or persistent aneurysm sac pressurization, ultimately compromising procedural success and patient outcomes [2,3].

The ALTO endograft (Endologix) is a newer-generation device designed to treat complex aortic anatomies, including short infrarenal necks (≥7 mm), severe aortic angulation (up to 60°), and narrow aortic bifurcations [4]. It uses polymer-based sealing rings and suprarenal nitinol anchors, offering fixation and sealing mechanisms that differ from traditional friction-based grafts [4,5]. While these features expand treatment eligibility, ALTO’s workflow—where the contralateral gate is not fully visualized until polymer fill—and its use in very narrow bifurcations may increase the risk of cannulation difficulties and maldeployment during iliac limb placement.

In this report, we describe a case of iliac extension graft maldeployment during EVAR using the ALTO endograft. We highlight the successful use of a CODA balloon (Cook Medical) and a bridging stent to achieve realignment and seal restoration. This case illustrates a variation of endovascular salvage strategy adapted to the ALTO platform, providing a practical solution for managing a challenging intraoperative complication. Informed consent was obtained from the patient, and their confidentiality and rights were fully respected. Institutional review board was not needed since this is a single-patient case report.

CASE

A 64-year-old male with a history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease status post-percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease requiring 2 L/min of supplemental oxygen presented with a progressively enlarging AAA. The aneurysm measured over 5 cm in diameter, with an increase of more than 1 cm during the past year. Preoperative computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed a short, angulated neck (length: 7 mm; angulation: approximately 55°), and a narrow aortic bifurcation, which guided the selection of the ALTO endograft (Fig. 1). The selected main body had an inner diameter of 16 mm, expanding to no more than 30 mm at a point 7 mm below the inferior renal artery, consistent with previous studies [3].

Under general anesthesia, bilateral common femoral artery access was obtained. The main body of the ALTO endograft was introduced via the left femoral artery in accordance with standard technique. The contralateral iliac extension graft was advanced through the right femoral artery; however, it failed to enter the contralateral gate and was inadvertently deployed into the aneurysm sac, resulting in maldeployment (Fig. 2).

This complication was likely caused by inadvertent over-withdrawal of the Lunderquist wire (Cook Medical) during polymer ring molding, which eliminated proximal support for the main body. Simultaneously, the guidewire advanced from the right femoral access, tracked outside the intended gate due to absence of fluoroscopic rotational angiography and inadequate confirmation of true gate cannulation. Retrospective review confirmed that neither pigtail catheter rotation nor rotational angiography had been performed prior to extension graft delivery, contributing to the maldeployment.

To correct the maldeployment, a CODA balloon was advanced into the proximal portion of the misplaced iliac extension graft, just above the intended overlap zone. The balloon was inflated to apply gentle downward traction, realigning the extension graft with the contralateral gate of the main body (Fig. 3A, B). A new guidewire was then advanced through the repositioned extension graft into the true lumen of the ALTO device.

A bridging stent graft (16×100 mm) was subsequently deployed from the flow divider of the main body into the realigned iliac extension graft to restore endograft continuity and prevent endoleak. The left iliac extension graft had a distal diameter of 14 mm, and the corrected right iliac extension graft had a distal diameter of 16 mm. All connections and sealing zones were reinforced with balloon molding (Fig. 3C, D).

Final angiography demonstrated successful exclusion of the aneurysm without endoleak. The femoral access sites were closed using two ProGlide sutures (Abbott Vascular) per side. The patient recovered without complications and was discharged uneventfully. At the 6-month and 1-year follow-ups, CTA confirmed a well-positioned graft without evidence of endoleak, extension graft kinking, or graft migration.

DISCUSSION

EVAR has become the preferred treatment for AAA. It is estimated that up to 80% of AAA repairs in the United States are currently performed by EVAR, with only a minority undergoing open repair [6].

In this case, the primary reason for iliac extension graft maldeployment was the failure to re-advance the Lunderquist wire to the original position after it was intentionally pulled back to the soft portion of the wire to allow molding of the polymer rings against the aortic neck. This led to a loss of stability of the main body of the endograft. At this point, the main body of the endograft was unsupported; therefore, the contralateral iliac extension graft failed to enter the intended gate and instead followed the main body. This incident shows the importance of constant awareness of the wire position, especially during periods of reduced stiff wire support, such as polymer infusion. This is particularly critical for ALTO devices, which require precise proximal sealing under minimal radial force conditions.

Furthermore, standard gate verification steps were not fully utilized in this case. Specifically, rotational angiography and pigtail catheter manipulation to confirm true contralateral gate orientation were omitted. These safeguards are critical to preventing maldeployment of the extension graft. Real-time fluoroscopic confirmation of guidewire trajectory and the use of preloaded contralateral wire systems with snare-assisted cannulation may further improve precision in anatomically complex cases. Routine incorporation of these techniques is strongly advised during ALTO deployment, particularly when operating near the anatomical limits of the device’s indications.

Maldeployment of the iliac extension grafts in modular devices is uncommon [5]. Nevertheless, when it occurs, it poses serious complications for the patient and a technically challenging situation for the operator. Although similar correction techniques using balloons, such as the RELIANT (Medtronic), have been reported [7], this case demonstrates a feasible and practical endovascular salvage strategy using a CODA balloon for realignment of a maldeployed iliac extension graft in the setting of an ALTO endograft.

The ALTO endograft is designed for patients with challenging aortic anatomies, including short infrarenal neck (as short as 7 mm), severe angulation (up to 60°), and narrow aortic bifurcation diameters, making it a viable option where standard endografts are unsuitable. These anatomical features, however, may increase the technical difficulty of extension graft cannulation and deployment, thereby elevating the risk of maldeployment. In this case, we employed a CODA balloon for repositioning the maldeployed iliac extension graft, realigning it with the main body of the endograft. A bridging stent, deployed from the flow divider of the main body to the lower extent of the iliac extension graft, provided structural integrity for a durable repair. Thus, our case report illustrates a variation of endovascular salvage strategy adapted to the ALTO platform, using both a CODA balloon and bridging stent to address the unique anatomical challenges.

The CODA balloon catheter is a compliant balloon that increases in volume while maintaining constant pressure during inflation [8,9]. This property enables it to be a safer option for use in large vessels such as the abdominal aorta. Compared to other balloon catheters, the CODA balloon requires smaller inflation volumes to achieve stable occlusion [8]. Consequently, it may provide a safer option for controlling non-compressible hemorrhage by significantly lowering the risk of vessel rupture. While effective, the CODA balloon’s success in repositioning depends heavily on the patient’s vascular anatomy. In cases with limited space or tortuosity, maneuvering the balloon to achieve precise repositioning can be challenging [6]. When using the CODA balloon for realignment, operators should position it just proximal to the overlap zone to apply traction without exerting excessive force on the graft fabric. Overinflation should be avoided, especially in tortuous vessels, to reduce the risk of graft dislodgement or aortic trauma.

Several endovascular salvage methods have been reported for maldeployed iliac extension grafts. Successful application of a RELIANT balloon for retracting a mispositioned iliac extension graft for re-cannulation and redeployment through the contralateral gate was detailed by Schulte et al. [7]. Another technique includes leaving the maldeployed extension graft in the aneurysm sac and deploying a new iliac extension graft at the proper location [7]. Although technically simpler, this approach may raise the risk of type I endoleak, sac pressurization, or graft component compression. Other methods include snare-assisted repositioning or contralateral crossover sheath access to recapture or redirect the misplaced iliac extension graft, but these require more advanced tools and operator expertise [10]. Compared with these options, our use of a CODA balloon for realignment combined with bridging stent placement provides a practical and structurally valid solution, particularly when rapid salvage is indicated and gate re-access is possible.

Lastly, it is important to mention that maldeployment of the iliac extension graft is not a complication limited to the ALTO endograft. Other modular endograft systems such as the AFX2 endograft (Endologix), are also susceptible to maldeployment due to challenging anatomy, including vessel tortuosity or short proximal landing zones [11]. Recognizing that this complication is not device-specific enables clinicians to better anticipate risks during EVAR and apply appropriate preventative measures regardless of the chosen endograft system.

In summary, ALTO endograft is a safe and effective treatment option for patients with suboptimal candidates for open repair. However, maldeployment of an iliac extension graft during EVAR may complicate the procedure. A maldeployed contralateral stent graft, as described in our case report, can be safely retracted with a compliant balloon such as CODA balloon, and a bridging stent can be placed to complete a durable repair.
