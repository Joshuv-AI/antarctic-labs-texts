Prevalence, Clinical Profile, and Factors Associated With Congenital Heart Disease in Children Diagnosed at National Referral Hospital, Bhutan: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Nima Phuntsho, Yeshey Dorjey, Kuenley Pedon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCongenital heart disease is a structural or functional abnormality of the heart. The prevalence of congenital heart disease varies between countries, and mortality and morbidities are high in children with congenital heart diseases. This study evaluated the prevalence, clinical profile, and the factors associated with congenital heart diseases in children in Bhutan.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted at the Department of Pediatrics at the Jigme Dorji Wangchuck National Referral Hospital from January 1 to December 31, 2021. All pediatric patients aged 0 days to 12 years diagnosed with congenital heart disease (CHD) by echocardiography during the study period were included. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.ResultsIn 2021, a total of 41,145 pediatric patients attended the Department of Pediatrics at the Jigme Dorji Wangchuck (JDW) National Referral Hospital, among whom 221 were diagnosed with congenital heart disease (CHD), corresponding to a hospital prevalence of 0.54% (5.4 per 1000 pediatric patients). CHD was observed slightly more frequently in female than male children (50.2% vs. 49.8%, p > 0.05). More than two‐thirds of CHD cases (68.3%) were diagnosed during the neonatal period, while over one‐quarter were diagnosed between 1 month and 1 year of age. The median age at diagnosis was 30 days (IQR: 7–60 days). The most common presenting symptoms were breathlessness (26.7%), poor weight gain (26.2%), and cough (21.7%), and 78.3% of the children had audible heart murmurs on examination. The most frequently identified CHD was atrial septal defect (ASD) (74.2%), followed by ventricular septal defect (VSD) (29.9%) and patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) (20.4%). On multivariable logistic regression, female children showed a lower likelihood of having VSD compared with male children (aOR 0.5, 95% CI 0.28–0.95, p = 0.036), though this did not reach the Bonferroni‐corrected significance threshold (p < 0.017). Female children were 1.5 times more likely to have ASD than male children, though this was not statistically significant (aOR 1.5, 95% CI 0.77–2.74, p = 0.245).ConclusionThe prevalence of congenital heart disease was 5.4 per 1000 pediatric patients, with most cases diagnosed postnatally, predominantly during the neonatal period. Atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, and patent ductus arteriosus were the most common types. These findings highlight the need to implement prenatal fetal cardiac screening to enable early detection and timely intervention, thereby improving outcomes for affected newborns.Keywords: congenital heart defect, heart abnormalities, heart septal defects, prevalence, risk factors

Introduction

Congenital heart disease (CHD) includes the structural or functional abnormalities of the heart present before delivery [1]. CHD is one of the most common birth defects, and the prevalence of congenital heart disease is 2.7 per 1000 births [2] and the incidence rate of 9.3 per 1000 live births [3].

Multiple factors play a role in the development of CHD in the unborn fetus, which include uncontrolled chronic medical disorders of the mothers, genetic predisposition, exposure to teratogens, environmental factors, and chromosomal abnormalities [4, 5, 6, 7].

There are different categories of congenital heart disease. Commonly, congenital heart disease is classified into acyanotic and cyanotic heart diseases based on the deoxygenated blood circulating in the body.

Congenital heart disease is often asymptomatic at birth, and sometimes, it is not detected during the postnatal examination. However, depending on the type of congenital heart disease, the baby presents with a wide range of signs and symptoms, which include shortness of breath, excessive sweating, chest pain, palpitation, fatigue, poor feeding, cyanosis, tachycardia, and clubbed fingernails [8]. At times, congenital heart diseases are detected during childhood when the child presents with recurrent chest infections [9].

Prenatal cardiac scanning and fetal echocardiography will diagnose congenital heart diseases in most cases. The diagnosis of congenital heart disease is further confirmed with evaluation with chest x‐rays and echocardiography during the postnatal period. Prenatal diagnosis of congenital heart disease helps in couple counseling and planning for further management [10].

Currently, in Bhutan, there is no data on prenatal diagnosis of congenital heart disease since fetal echocardiography is not routinely performed in pregnant mothers. Mostly, congenital heart disease is diagnosed during the postnatal period through clinical examination and echocardiography [11]. The prevalence and clinical profile of congenital heart disease in Bhutan remain unknown since no prior study has been performed.

This study aimed to generate baseline data on the prevalence, clinical profile, and the factors associated with congenital heart diseases in children at the National Referral Hospital, Bhutan.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

A hospital‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted at the Jigme Dorji Wangchuck (JDW) National Referral Hospital between January 1 and December 31, 2021.

Study Setting

JDW National Referral Hospital is the leading hospital in Bhutan, providing tertiary care services to its population. The Department of Pediatrics includes a general Outpatient Department (OPD), a chronic disease clinic, an Inpatient Department (IPD), a Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU), a Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU), and private cabins. Additionally, there is an echocardiography unit staffed by echocardiographers and cardiologists.

Children suspected of having CHDs are evaluated by pediatricians, with diagnoses confirmed through echocardiography. Those with severe CHD are referred to Durgapur Hospital in India for further evaluation and cardiothoracic intervention. Other cases of CHD are managed and followed up at the National Referral Hospital.

Study Population

During the study period, there were 41,145 pediatric patients who attended the Department of Pediatrics at the National Referral Hospital (denominator). Pediatric patients suspected of congenital heart diseases—cardiac murmurs on auscultation, cyanosis, poor weight gain, recurrent chest infections, dysmorphic features, or tachypnoea/tachycardia without another identifiable cause were referred for echocardiography. A total of 221 children aged 0–12 years were confirmed to have CHD by echocardiography (numerator = 221), yielding a hospital‐based prevalence of 5.4 per 1000 pediatric attendees (221/41,145).

Inclusion Criteria

All pediatric patients aged 0 days to 12 years diagnosed with congenital heart disease who attended the Department of Pediatrics at the JDW National Referral Hospital were included in the study.

Exclusion Criteria

Children older than 12 years and those with acquired heart disease were excluded from the study.

Sample Size Calculation

There were 221 confirmed cases of CHD during the period, and all of them were included for the study, and a formal sample size calculation is not performed.

Sampling Method

All eligible pediatric patients presenting to the Department of Pediatrics during the study period who met the inclusion criteria were consecutively enrolled.

Study Procedure

All pediatric patients aged 0 days to 12 years attending the Department of Pediatrics were approached for participation in the study. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of the children included in the study.

Following enrollment, demographic details (age at diagnosis, ethnicity, and maternal education) and clinical features (poor weight gain, breathlessness, cough, bluish discoloration of lips, poor feeding, murmurs, tachypnea, tachycardia, cyanosis, and clubbing) were collected from medical records provided by the parents. Children suspected of having congenital heart disease (CHD)‐based on clinical features such as poor weight gain, recurrent chest infections, dysmorphic features, and the presence of murmurs on auscultation‐were evaluated using echocardiography for confirmation.

The types of CHD (atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, atrioventricular septal defect, patent foramen ovale, tetralogy of Fallot, transposition of the great arteries, total anomalous pulmonary venous connection, double outlet right ventricle, aortic stenosis, pulmonary stenosis, pulmonary atresia, dextrocardia, partial anomalous pulmonary venous return, tricuspid atresia) were classified based on echocardiography findings.

Children with severe CHD requiring immediate surgical intervention were referred to India, while the remaining cases were managed at the National Referral Hospital, Bhutan. Some children received medical management with furosemide, spironolactone, sildenafil, enalapril, and propranolol.

All data were collected using a standardized pro forma developed for the study (Supporting Information S1).

Operational Definition

CHDs were diagnosed using echocardiography findings and categorized into different types according to the classification described in the Nelson Textbook of Pediatrics [12].

Data Analysis

All analyses were conducted on the complete cohort of 221 confirmed CHD cases. No post‐enrollment exclusions were made.

Data were double‐entered and validated using EpiData (Version 3.1 for entry and version 2.2.2.183 for analysis, EpiData Association, Odense, Denmark). Data were exported and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was performed to test for the normality of the quantitative data. Age at diagnosis of congenital heart diseases, and the age of the children were not normally distributed and compared using a Mann–Whitney U test between genders (male and female children), and differences were expressed as median (Interquartile range).

Categorical variables of demographic and clinical characteristics were compared using χ 2 and or Fisher's exact test, wherever appropriate, and presented in frequencies and percentages.

Spearman's rank correlation (rs) was used to assess the association between age at diagnosis and length of hospital stay, given the non‐normal distribution of both variables. For multivariable logistic regression, all candidate variables (age at diagnosis, sex, ethnicity, maternal education) were entered simultaneously using a forced‐entry approach, selected a priori on clinical and literature grounds. Given that three regression models were run on overlapping predictors, a Bonferroni‐corrected significance threshold of p < 0.017 (0.05/3) was applied. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test.

Results

During the year 2021, a total of 41,145 pediatric patients visited the Pediatric Department at the National Referral Hospital. Among them, 221 cases of congenital heart diseases were identified, resulting in a prevalence of 0.54% (5.4 per 1000 pediatric patients).

Of the 221 children diagnosed with CHD, 18 (8.1%) were referred to Durgapur Mission Hospital, India, for cardiac surgical intervention. Seven children (3.2%) died during the study period (5 neonatal deaths, 2 post‐neonatal deaths). All 221 cases, including those referred or deceased, are included in all analyses (Figure 1).

Demographic Characteristics

CHD was slightly more common in female than male children (50.2% vs. 49.8%, p > 0.05).

Most CHD cases (68.3%, 151/221) were diagnosed during the neonatal period (day 0 to 1 month of age), and over one‐fourth of the cases were diagnosed after 1 month to 1 year of age. The median age at diagnosis was 30 days (IQR: 7–60 days). The largest proportion of CHD cases (38.5%, 85/221) occurred in children from families in the eastern region of Bhutan (Sharchop). Furthermore, 44.3% of these children were born to mothers with secondary‐level education (Table 1).

Clinical Features

The majority of children with CHD presented with breathlessness (26.7%), poor weight gain (26.2%), and cough (21.7%). On clinical examination, 78.3% of cases had audible heart murmurs. The mean heart rate was 119.6 ± 18.4 beats per minute, the mean respiratory rate was 33.9 ± 8.2 breaths per minute, and the mean oxygen saturation (SpO2) was 91.3 ± 7.8%. These vital signs were comparable between males and females, with no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). Detailed findings are shown in Table 2.

Medications

Of the 221 children with CHD, 95 (43.0%) received medical management, and 18 (8.1%) were referred to India for cardiac surgical intervention. Furosemide (45.3%) and spironolactone (44.2%) were the most commonly prescribed medications, used primarily for children with haemodynamically significant left‐to‐right shunts presenting with congestive heart failure. Propranolol was prescribed in 4 children for heart rate management, enalapril in 5 for afterload reduction, and sildenafil in 1 child for pulmonary arterial hypertension (Table 4).

Factor Associated With the Congenital Heart Diseases

Individual congenital heart diseases—ASD, VSD, and PDA were considered as dependent variables. The independent variables included the age at diagnosis, gender, ethnicity, and the mother's education. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with these CHD.

ASD are more likely to be diagnosed at birth or during the neonatal period, however it is not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Female children had a 50% lower likelihood of having VSD compared to male children (aOR 0.5, 95% CI 0.28–0.95, p = 0.036); however, this did not meet the Bonferroni‐corrected significance threshold of p < 0.017 and should be interpreted with caution. In contrast, female children were 1.5 times more likely to have ASD than male children, though this was not statistically significant (aOR 1.5, 95% CI 0.77–2.74, p = 0.245). Discussion

The prevalence of CHD is 5.4 per 1000 pediatric patients at the National Referral Hospital, Bhutan. A prospective cohort study conducted in the eastern part of Bhutan showed a slightly higher prevalence rate of 6.8 per 1000 pediatric patients [13]. A systematic review and meta‐analysis by Zhao et al. in the Chinese population reported a similar prevalence rate of 4.9 per 1000 births [14]. However, another systematic review and a meta‐analysis by Liu et al. reported a higher global prevalence rate of 9.4/1000 births [15]. The variations in prevalence of congenital heart disease indicate that heart disease is partly dependent on the prior genetic predisposition, and other risk factors associated with the people dwelling in a particular geographical location.

In the current study, CHD were slightly more prevalent in female children compared to male children. On the contrary, Zhao et al. and Salari et al. studies showed CHD were more prevalent among males compared to female child (4.1 per 1000 for male vs. 3.5 per 1000 for females, respectively) [2, 14]. The differences in prevalence between the genders are thought to occur due to combination of developmental and specific gene expression during the cardiac development in utero.

In the present study, the majority of CHD cases (68.3%) were diagnosed during the neonatal period. Ideally, all the pregnant mothers should be offered prenatal echocardiography between 18 and 22 weeks of gestation to screen for possible congenital heart diseases in the fetus. A study by van Velzen et al. demonstrated a prenatal detection rate of CHD of approximately 45%. The detection rate varied by type of heart defect, with univentricular defects and heterotaxy showing the highest detection rate at 85% during the prenatal period [16]. In Bhutan, prenatal cardiac screening is not done, and almost all the congenital heart diseases are detected at birth or after birth during the postnatal period [11]. There is an urgent need to initiate and offer prenatal cardiac screening services to all pregnant mothers during the second trimester in Bhutan.

The most common presentations of CHD were breathlessness, poor weight gain, and cough. Almost all cases had audible murmurs, along with cyanosis, edema, tachycardia, and tachypnea. A study by Verma et al. also reported similar findings, including difficulty in breathing, recurrent chest infections, and malnutrition [17].

In the current study, around 6% of the newborns with CHD had extra‐cardiac dysmorphic features, and a few had features of Down syndrome detected after delivery (5%).

Numerous genetic syndromes are associated with congenital heart diseases, including Down syndrome, Turner syndrome, 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, Williams syndrome, Noonan syndrome, Kabuki syndrome, and Alagille syndrome, among others [18].

Many of these genetic syndromes can be identified during the prenatal period, enabling appropriate counseling of the couple and facilitating planning for postnatal management and treatment. In cases involving severe or lethal cardiac anomalies, options such as medical termination of pregnancy may also be discussed. However, in Bhutan, prenatal screening and diagnostic testing for chromosomal or genetic disorders are currently not available. Furthermore, fetal echocardiography is not routinely performed during pregnancy.

Therefore, it is important to establish prenatal screening programs for chromosomal and genetic disorders and to introduce routine fetal echocardiography services in Bhutan to improve early detection and management of congenital heart diseases.

In the present study, the most common CHD detected was ASD, followed by VSD and PDA. A systematic review and meta‐analysis reported similar findings regarding the subtypes of CHD, identifying VSD as the most prevalent, followed by ASD, PDA, TOF, and tricuspid atresia or stenosis [14].

Since there is no facility to perform cardiac intervention in Bhutan, all those children with CHD who required immediate cardiac intervention were referred to India, and a few cases were treated conservatively with medicine in Bhutan. Among the CHD patients treated with medicines, most of them were treated with furosemide (45.3%) and spironolactone (44.2%). The mainstay of treatment of CHD is the correction of the cardiac defect, and the medical treatment is supportive care to stabilize the patient until a definitive corrective surgery can be performed [8].

In the literature, it is reported that consanguinity is the most important risk factor for CHD, and other risk factors are advancing maternal age, low birth weight, maternal co‐morbidities, and family history of CHD [19, 20, 21]. In the current study, maternal factors were not studied, and their association with CHD remains unknown.

This study has several limitations. This study captures only children who survived to present at the hospital; those who died before presentation, and fetuses with CHD lost to spontaneous abortion or stillbirth, are not reflected in our figures. Furthermore, although second‐trimester anomaly scans are performed in Bhutan, dedicated fetal cardiac screening is not routine, meaning CHD‐affected pregnancies may be lost before postnatal diagnosis is possible. The reported hospital‐based prevalence of 5.4 per 1000 is therefore likely an underestimate of the true birth prevalence. Future population‐based studies incorporating vital registration data and routine prenatal cardiac screening are needed to establish more accurate figures for Bhutan.

Conclusion

The prevalence of congenital heart disease was 5.4 per 1000 pediatric patients. Most cases were diagnosed after birth, particularly during the neonatal period, with atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, and patent ductus arteriosus being the most common types. Female children showed a lower likelihood of having VSD compared to male children.

Recommendation

Congenital heart diseases in Bhutan are predominantly diagnosed during the postnatal period due to the lack of routine fetal cardiac screening. Capacity building through training and retraining of sonographers and obstetricians on fetal cardiac screening during the second‐trimester anomaly scan (18–22 weeks of gestation) is recommended. Strengthening prenatal detection will enable appropriate counselling, delivery planning, and timely postnatal management.

Genetic syndromes and chromosomal abnormalities are commonly associated with congenital heart diseases. In Bhutan, the absence of prenatal screening services results in most syndromic infants being identified only at birth or during the postnatal period. Establishing comprehensive prenatal screening and diagnostic services is essential to enable early detection, provide timely genetic counselling, and support informed decision‐making. This would help reduce the clinical, emotional, and socioeconomic burden on affected families and the healthcare system.
