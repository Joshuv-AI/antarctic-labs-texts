Reorganization of H3K9me2-modified chromatin regions during mouse embryonic development
Authors: Diana Fulmer, Emily J Shields, Andrey Poleshko, Jonathan A Epstein, Cheryl L Smith
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe three-dimensional organization of chromatin in the nucleus is critical in regulating gene expression. There are two classes of large genomic regions demarcated by the repressive chromatin modification histone H3 lysine 9 dimethyl (H3K9me2) and enriched at the nuclear periphery: lamina-associated domains (LADs); and H3K9me2-only domains (KODs) which have minimal lamina contact and are highly enriched for transcriptional enhancers. LADs have been studied in multiple cell types. In contrast, KODs have been characterized only in pluripotent cells and it remains to be determined whether they are fixed or rearrange according to cell type. Analysis of KODs from various embryonic mouse tissues revealed that KODs adopt cell type-specific configurations that correlate with changes in lineage-specific enhancer activity. Within KODs, local cell type-specific depletion of H3K9me2 was enriched for H3K27ac peaks at active lineage-specific enhancers. KODs were also enriched across ultra-long regulatory regions suggesting a role for KODs in long-range gene regulation. These results suggest that KODs are cell-type specific and maintain cell type-specific enhancers in a repressed state to allow for tissue- and stage-specific gene activation.Keywords: Enhancer, Gene regulation, H3K9me2, Lamina, Nuclear architecture

Introduction

The arrangement of the genome in the eukaryotic nucleus follows distinct organizational principles and contributes to the coordinated regulation of gene expression (Luperchio et al., 2014; van Steensel and Belmont, 2017; Guerreiro and Kind, 2019; Klemm et al., 2019; Misteli, 2020). The genome can be divided into compartments of transcriptionally active euchromatin, enriched in the nuclear interior (Cremer et al., 2015; Egecioglu and Brickner, 2011; Shevelyov, 2023), and transcriptionally silent, condensed heterochromatin (Hildebrand and Dekker, 2020; Oji et al., 2024) which is relegated to the nuclear periphery and chromocenters (Amendola and van Steensel, 2014; Yanez-Cuna and van Steensel, 2017; Shevelyov and Ulianov, 2019; Briand and Collas, 2020). Peripheral heterochromatin has been shown to interact with the nuclear lamina and these genomic regions have been aptly termed lamina-associated domains (LADs) (Pickersgill et al., 2006; Guelen et al., 2008). LADs cover a large portion of mostly gene-poor areas of the genome, but many genes in LADs are developmentally regulated and lineage-specific (Poleshko et al., 2017; Shah et al., 2023; Madsen-Osterbye et al., 2022). Consistent with these observations, subsets of LADs reorganize during cell differentiation to adopt cell-type specific conformations concordant with changes in gene expression (Guelen et al., 2008; Poleshko et al., 2017; Peric-Hupkes et al., 2010; Shah et al., 2021). Mutations in genes encoding nuclear lamina or chromatin-tethering proteins result in an inability of the cell to maintain LAD structure and are associated with differentiation and developmental defects in multiple human diseases (Poleshko et al., 2017; Shah et al., 2021; Gonzalez-Sandoval et al., 2015; Coffinier et al., 2011; Cheedipudi et al., 2019). These findings demonstrate a crucial role for peripheral heterochromatin in gene regulation as well as the establishment and maintenance of cell identity.

LADs are enriched for the histone modification histone H3 Lys 9 dimethyl (H3K9me2) which is an evolutionarily conserved mark of peripheral heterochromatin (Shah et al., 2023; Kind et al., 2013; Poleshko et al., 2019). H3K9me2 is distinctly associated with peripheral heterochromatin and is distributed broadly across LADs (Shah et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2021), while other repressive epigenetic marks, including H3K9me3 and H3K27me3, are found on both peripheral and pericentric heterochromatin (Poleshko et al., 2017). While LADs have been shown to encompass H3K9me2-modified chromatin (Poleshko et al., 2017; Lienert et al., 2011), our group demonstrated that, in pluripotent mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), not all H3K9me2-modified chromatin overlaps LADs. In genome-wide assays, an additional fraction of H3K9me2-marked chromatin was separate from Lamin B1-isolated chromatin, suggesting that these regions are less tightly associated with the lamina. These large H3K9me2-modified chromatin regions that were found to be distinct from LADs, “H3K9me2-Only Domains” (KODs), average nearly 100 kb in size, and cover 10–15 % of the genome in mESCs (Smith et al., 2021). In mESCs, KODs, unlike LADs, were not marked by H3K9me3, and KODs had higher levels of chromatin accessibility than LADs (Smith et al., 2021). Another notable feature of KODs, and one that further distinguished them from LADs, was their enrichment for distal transcriptional enhancer elements (Smith et al., 2021).

Enhancers are non-coding genomic elements that contain transcription factor binding sites with dynamically regulated accessibility determined, at least in part, by the epigenetic state of the enhancer. Enhancers may be identified by monomethylation at H3 lysine 4 (H3K4me1), and transcriptionally active enhancers by high levels of histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation (H3K27ac) as well as increased chromatin accessibility (Heintzman et al., 2007; Long et al., 2016). These features mediate recruitment of transcriptional co-activators, and both differential expression of transcription factors and 3D nuclear organization determine cell type-specific enhancer function (Ernst et al., 2011; Visel et al., 2009). While enhancers may be located tens or hundreds of kilobases away from the gene they regulate, they function through higher-order chromatin organization and phase-separated transcriptional condensates to bring transcription factors into proximity with the promoter and transcriptional machinery associated with the transcription start site (Bulger and Groudine, 2010; Kim and Shendure, 2019; Wagh et al., 2021). Regulatory elements are critical for cell fate specification and lineage determination but a complete understanding of how they are controlled remains to be elucidated. The frequent occurrence of enhancers within the H3K9me2-modified chromatin of KODs suggests that these domains may play a role in maintaining lineage-specific enhancers in a repressed or poised state at the nuclear periphery.

While LADs have been extensively studied and shown to vary across numerous cell types and stages of cell fate commitment (Peric-Hupkes et al., 2010; Robson et al., 2016; Zheng et al., 2018; Pal et al., 2023; Ronningen et al., 2015), KODs have, to date, been characterized only in pluripotent mESCs (Smith et al., 2021) and it remains unknown if they are similar in all cell types or if they rearrange during cell-state transitions. In order to identify and evaluate KODs in lineage-restricted cells, we surveyed these H3K9me2-modified chromatin domains in tissues from the developing mouse embryo at mid-gestation and included analyses of LADs in these tissues for comparison. Here we report the genome-wide organization of H3K9me2-modified LADs and KODs in lineage-restricted cells isolated from mouse embryonic day 11.5 (E11.5) heart and forebrain tissues. We chose this developmental timepoint because, at E11.5, both the heart and forebrain are largely comprised of homogeneous cell populations. The majority of cells from E11.5 heart are cardiomyocytes (Feng et al., 2022), and most cells from E11.5 forebrain are neuroepithelial cells (He et al., 2001).

Our results show that KODs are cell type-specific in embryonic heart- and forebrain-derived cells. While both LADs and KODs rearrange during development to adopt cell type-specific conformations, KODs are even more variable than LADs among the samples in our study. KODs in heart and brain are enriched for distal enhancers and display higher chromatin accessibility than LADs, consistent with observations in mESCs. These and other features confirm that KODs are a distinct class of H3K9me2-modified domains, separate from LADs, in multiple cell types. In addition, we observe cell type-specific local depletion of H3K9me2 at enhancers within KODs that correlates with cell type-specific increases in the active enhancer modification H3K27ac and chromatin accessibility. Local H3K9me2 depletion at enhancers also correlates with cell type-specific expression of genes associated with these enhancers. Finally, we show that KODs are particularly enriched for ultra-long gene regulatory regions that have distal enhancers distributed across hundreds of kilobases. Together, our findings indicate that H3K9me2-modified KODs maintain lineage-specific genes and transcriptional enhancers in a repressed state until the appropriate developmental stage for activation is reached and thereby contribute to gene regulatory networks that determine the spatial and temporal expression patterns of genes.

Materials and Methods

Animals

All experiments on mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the University of Pennsylvania and mice were housed in facilities accredited by the American Association for Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC). Pregnant C57BL/6J mice (cat# 000664) were obtained from Jackson Laboratory and delivered to our animal facilities at embryonic day 10.5 (E10.5) to be dissected for embryos the following day at E11.5.

Cell lines and culture

Murine C57BL/6 embryonic pluripotent stem cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, cat# SCRC-1002). These cells were thawed and initially plated on 0.1 % bovine gelatin coated plates with mitotically inactivated murine embryonic fibroblasts (ATCC, cat# SCRC-1040.2a) before transitioning them to LIF + 2i media. LIF + 2i media consists of DMEM with 15 % Hyclone FBS (Fisher Scientific, cat# SH30070.03), 1 % NEAA (Gibco, cat#11140-050), 1 % Glutamax (Gibco, cat# 35050-061), 1 % Pen/Strep (Gibco, cat# 15140-122), 1 % Sodium Pyruvate (Gibco, cat# 11360), and 0.0007 % B-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, cat# 21985-023), supplemented with LIF (1:10,000, Millipore-Sigma, cat# ESG1106), 1uM PD0325901 (Millipore-Sigma cat# PZ0162), and 3uM CHIR99021 (Millipore-Sigma, cat# SML1046).

Dissections and CUT&RUN

Wildtype pregnant C57BL6/J dames were dissected, and embryos were staged to confirm they were E11.5. Any embryos not matching the exact stage were discarded. Next, whole hearts and right forebrain lobes were collected and pooled by tissue type from the remaining embryos. Due to limitations of material, embryos were not separated by sex. Each tissue or cell type consisted of 3 biological replicates (tissues from 3 separate pooled litters or 3 separate culture vessels). Mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) or freshly isolated tissues were dissociated into single cell suspensions (via TrypLE (Gibco, cat# 12605-010) for mESCs or papain (Worthington, cat# LK003150) for tissues). CUT&RUN was then performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions in the CUTANA ChIC/CUT&RUN Kit (EpiCypher, cat# 14–1048, v1.0-v3.0). In brief, cells were washed and then allowed to bind to activated ConA beads. Samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C on a nutator with the appropriate antibody. The following day samples were washed again and then incubated with pAG-MNase for 10 min at room temperature. Samples were washed again, and targeted chromatin digestion was initiated with 100 mM calcium chloride and allowed to proceed for 2 h at 4 ° C on a nutator. The reactions were then stopped, and DNA fragments were collected via incubation in a thermocycler set to 37 °C for 10 min. Finally, DNA fragments were purified via the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up kit (Takara Bio USA, Inc, cat# 740609). Primary anti-bodies used: H3K9me2 (Active Motif, cat# 39239, lot# 34718003), Lamin B1 (Abcam, cat# ab16048, lot# GR195923), and Rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling, cat#2729S, lot# 10).

Library prep and sequencing

DNA libraries for Next-Generation sequencing were prepared utilizing the KAPA HyperPrep Kit (Kapa Biosystems, cat# KK8502 07962347001) and NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (New England Biolabs, cat# E6440S). To begin, samples were end repaired & A-tailed via incubation in a thermocycler for 30 min at 20 °C followed by a 58 °C incubation for 60 min. Next adapters were ligated per kit instructions with a 15-min incubation at 20 °C with ligation buffer & DNA ligase, followed by a 37 °C incubation for 15 min with NEB USER enzyme for an additional 15 min. Post-ligation cleanup was performed using 1.1x AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter, cat# A63880). Next, the libraries were amplified per kit instructions with the following thermocycler conditions: 1 cycle of initial denaturation at 98 °C for 45 s, 14 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 15 s and annealing and extension at 60 °C for 10 s, and 1 final extension cycle at 72 °C for 1 min. Amplification was followed by an additional cleanup step with the 1.1x AMPure XP Beads and library quantification using the NEBNext Library Quantification Kit (cat# E7630) as per kit instructions. Library quality and fragment size was then determined via Agilent Technologies TapeStation using the High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape (cat# 5067–5584) and reagents (cat# 5067–5585). Libraries were then pooled and denatured via the standard Illumina protocol. Finally, samples were paired-end sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 with a high-throughput flow cell (cat# 20022408).

Genome alignment and QC pipeline

For CUT&RUN samples, adapters were trimmed from fastq files using TrimGalore with default parameters (ver. 0.6.4 using Cutadapt 4.2 (Krueger, 2015; Kechin et al., 2017)). The resulting reads were mapped with bowtie2 (ver. 2.5.0 (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012)) with the parameters -X 2000 -N 1 –local –dovetail to the mm10 assembly. Only reads mapping to the 19 autosomal chromosomes were retained, and any reads mapping to the mm10 CUT&RUN blacklist (Nordin et al., 2023) or the first 3 Mb of each chromosome (repetitive sequences near centromeres (Cox et al., 2009) were removed. Duplicates were marked using Picard (RRID:SCR_006525) Normalized bw files were produced for each Lamin B1 or H3K9me2 sample, normalizing against a merged bam file of all IgG samples from the corresponding tissue, using deeptools (Ramirez et al., 2016) bamCoverage with the parameters –operation log2 –extendReads –binSize 50. To minimize the impact of LTR/LINE regions on window-based scores, any regions overlapping LTR or LINE elements detected by RepeatMasker (Smit, AFA, Hubley, R & Green, P. RepeatMasker Open-4.0. 2013–2015 <http://www.repeatmasker.org>) and downloaded from the UCSC Table Browser (Raney et al., 2024) were set to a minimum of 0. From these bw files, counts in tiled bins of 1 kb or 10 kb were calculated using deeptools multiBigwigSummary.

Domain calling

Chromatin domains were called similarly to a previous study (Smith et al., 2021). Any window where there were 0 counts in any LaminB1, H3K9me2, or IgG sample was excluded from the analysis. Counts (average of three replicates) in 10 kb bins were used to call domains. For each sample, z-scores for each bin were calculated, then smoothed using a rolling mean incorporating the 5 windows flanking each side (Zeileis and Grothendieck, 2005). KODs were defined as any bins with smoothed Lamin B1 z-score below the genome-wide median, and the difference between H3K9me2 and Lamin B1 smoothed z-scores in the top 80th percentile of the genome. LADs were defined as any region with both Lamin B1 and H3K9me2 smoothed z-scores above their respective genome-wide medians. Non-KOD, non-LAD regions (“open”) were regions with both Lamin B1 and H3K9me2 smoothed z-scores below their respective genome-wide medians. Any regions that did not fall into these categories were considered ambiguous and excluded from any downstream analysis. Adjacent bins that were classified as the same domain type were merged.

Enrichment across annotated genomic features

Feature enrichment analysis was performed using Genomic Association Tester (GAT) (Heger et al., 2013). The significance of overlap between sets of genomic intervals was calculated based on simulation using a permutation-based approach and accounting for genome organization regions of low mapability. All enrichment analyses were subjected to 1000 simulations. The fold enrichment is expressed as the ratio of observed/expected (by chance). P values reflect an estimate of the probability to obtain an observed (or larger) overlap between two segment sets by chance and are reported as “significant” only if p < 0.01. All fold enrichment values and individual p values for each comparison are reported in Supplemental Table S1.

Depleted regions

Cell type-specific H3K9me2-depleted regions were defined as 1 kb regions with H3K9me2 z-scores lower in one tissue compared to the other two tissues. To find depleted regions for tissue A, the three tissue A replicates were compared to both the three tissue B and the three tissue C replicates using a Student’s t-test with the alternative hypothesis “less,” and regions with p-values in the lowest 5 % were retained as depleted regions. Adjacent bins defined as depleted were merged.

Gene Ontology analyses

GO analysis was performed using PANTHER (Thomas et al., 2022) statistical overrepresentation tests for the biological process gene complete ontology, using appropriate background for each analysis (Fig. 2E background: all genes in Mus musculus genome; Fig. 3F background: all genes with at least one linked enhancer).

Miscellaneous data analysis

Transcription by domain (Fig. 1H) represents the amount of RNA-seq signal per base pair in each domain, calculated using multiBamSummary from merged, aligned bam files downloaded from ENCODE (mES: ENCFF018WFD and ENCFF374OXM, heart: ENCFF748XUF and ENCFF077ZPD, forebrain: ENCFF362XNO and ENCFF962IWY) with a bin size of 10 kb. Two replicates were averaged for each tissue.

Results

KODs and LADs are found in both pluripotent and lineage-restricted cells

To identify and annotate cell type-specific H3K9me2-modified chromatin domains, we harvested hearts and forebrains from embryonic day 11.5 (E11.5) mouse embryos and generated H3K9me2 and Lamin B1 CUT&RUN datasets (biological triplicates). To compare the chromatin organization in a proxy pre-cursor cell type, we generated H3K9me2 and Lamin B1 CUT&RUN datasets (biological triplicates) from pluripotent mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) derived from the same mouse strain. Representative genome tracks of normalized H3K9me2 and Lamin B1 signals from the average of three replicates for each cell type are shown (Fig. 1A). We defined two classes of H3K9me2-modified chromatin domains – LADs and KODs – using a combination of H3K9me2 and Lamin B1 signals observed in bins across the genome for each sample (see Methods). Both LADs and KODs have high H3K9me2 signal, but they can be differentiated by the Lamin B1 signal (Fig. 1A–B, Supplemental Fig. S1A–B). Genomic regions with markedly higher H3K9me2 signal than Lamin B1 (>80th percentile in difference between the two signals) were defined as KODs (Fig. 1C, Fig. S1B). In contrast, LADs were defined as regions where H3K9me2 and Lamin B1 signals were both high (Fig. 1C, Fig. S1B). We refer to regions that are depleted for both Lamin B1 and H3K9me2 as “open” chromatin regions, previously also called inter-LADs (Pickersgill et al., 2006; Guelen et al., 2008; Peric-Hupkes et al., 2010; Manzo et al., 2024). Notably, analyses of these H3K9me2 and Lamin B1 datasets from E11.5 heart and E11.5 forebrain allowed us to identify KODs in lineage-restricted cell types and thus confirms that KODs are not exclusive to pluripotent mESCs where they were originally defined (Smith et al., 2021).

Defining features of KODs differentiate them from LADs

Using the outlined domain annotations, we sought to determine whether the characteristics of KODs were the same in differentiated cell types as those observed in pluripotent cells. We first compared the fraction of the genome covered by H3K9me2-modified domains – LADs and KODs – in developing embryonic tissues with mESCs and found that the proportion was nearly identical in all three cell types. LADs make up 40–44 % of the genome in the three cell types; KODs contribute 17–18 %; and non-H3K9me2-modified open chromatin represents 30–32 % (Fig. 1D). The remainder of the genome could not be unambiguously assigned and was excluded from further analyses (see Methods). Consistent with the initial identification of KODs in mESCs (Smith et al., 2021), KODs were found to span tens to hundreds of kilobases, while LADs were slightly larger on average (Supplemental Fig. 1C–D). For both LADs and KODs, the distributions of sizes as well as the number of individual domains were highly consistent among all three cell types (Supplemental Fig. 1C–D).

To gain insights into the biological function of KODs, we evaluated the likelihood of genomic features, such as genes and regulatory elements, to be located in KODs, LADs or open chromatin. For each comparison, we performed 1000 random permutations to estimate the probability of a given observation occurring by chance, given the sizes of the features, the domains, and the genome. The observation was noted as “significantly enriched” only if it occurred much more likely than chance with a probability p < 0.01 or “significantly depleted” only if it occurred much less likely than chance with a probability p < 0.01 (see Methods; Supplemental Table S1). These enrichment analyses revealed that KODs in developmentally restricted cells were no more likely than chance to contain protein-coding genes or promoters but were significantly enriched (p < 0.001) for distal transcriptional enhancers (defined by ENCODE as cis-regulatory regions lacking typical promoter modifications and located more than 2 kb from a transcriptional start site) (Fig. 1E). This was also observed in pluripotent mESCs in this study, consistent with our previous report (Smith et al., 2021). For comparison, open chromatin regions were significantly enriched (p < 0.001) and LADs were significantly depleted (p < 0.001) for genes, promoters, and enhancers (Fig. 1E) (Smith et al., 2021). Promoter and enhancer density metrics across LADs, KODs, and open chromatin regions reflect these patterns: enhancer densities in KODs were nearly as high as in open regions, but promoter densities were much lower in KODs (Supplemental Fig. S1E–F). Each of these characteristics distinguishing KODs from LADs were observed in pluripotent and lineage-restricted cell types.

In addition to protein-coding genes and annotated promoters and enhancers, we compared the repetitive element and non-coding RNA content of each domain type. KODs were significantly enriched (p < 0.001) for SINEs (short interspersed nuclear elements) in both embryonic tissues and significantly depleted (p < 0.001) for LINEs (long interspersed nuclear elements) in all three cell types (Fig. 1F). These features of KODs were similar to open chromatin, and starkly different from LADs where LINEs were significantly enriched (p < 0.001), and SINEs were significantly depleted (p < 0.001) in all three cell types (Fig. 1F). We also compared different classes of non-coding RNAs present in KODs and LADs and found that KODs were uniquely enriched for both lincRNAs (long intergenic RNAs) and antisense RNAs (Fig. S1G). Altogether, the distinctive genomic content found in KODs – especially the relative abundance of known and putative regulatory elements such as enhancers, SINEs, and non-coding RNAs – sets KODs apart from LADs and suggests that KODs may be involved in gene regulation through mechanisms different from those of LADs.

KODs feature chromatin with intermediate levels of accessibility

H3K9me2-modified chromatin has been studied primarily in the context of LADs (Poleshko et al., 2017; Shah et al., 2023), which are comprised of highly compact heterochromatin (van Steensel and Belmont, 2017). To evaluate the relative chromatin accessibility of KODs and LADs, we overlayed these domains with publicly available ATACseq (Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin with Sequencing) peaks identified in mESCs, E11.5 hearts, and E11.5 forebrains (Wu et al., 2016; Consortium, 2012). As expected, LADs were significantly depleted for ATAC peaks and open euchromatin regions were significantly enriched for ATAC peaks (Fig. 1G). Notably, KODs were found to overlap with ATAC peaks approximately as often as expected by chance (Fig. 1G), indicating that the chromatin accessibility in KODs is an intermediate level between the highly compact heterochromatin of LADs and the highly accessible chromatin of open euchromatin regions. This moderate degree of accessibility was observed for KODs in the lineage-restricted cells of embryonic heart and forebrain as well as for pluripotent mESCs (Smith et al., 2021).

The current understanding in the field is that H3K9me2 is a repressive histone modification (Poleshko et al., 2017; Snowden et al., 2002) and it has been well documented that the nuclear peripheral heterochromatin of LADs forms an environment of transcriptional repression (van Steensel and Belmont, 2017; Shevelyov and Ulianov, 2019; Buchwalter et al., 2019). Given the shared H3K9me2 modification defining KODs, we used publicly available RNAseq data from ENCODE to assess whether KODs also held transcriptionally silent regions despite their intermediate level of chromatin accessibility. Transcription emanating from KODs was limited, only slightly more than that of LADs and in sharp contrast with the high level of transcription evident in open chromatin regions (Fig. 1H). Based on our characterizations in mESCs, and E11.5 heart and forebrain tissues, we can conclude that both LADs and KODs are present in pluripotent and lineage-committed cells and represent mainly transcriptionally repressed, gene-poor chromatin domains. KODs, however, are uniquely enriched in distal regulatory elements and have an intermediate level of chromatin accessibility, which distinguishes them from LADs.

LADs adopt cell type-specific conformations and maintain cell type-specific genes in a repressed state

After defining LADs and KODs in each of the three cell types, we compared their lineage-specific conformations to identify differences between the representative precursor cell type (mESC) and the developmentally restricted cells from embryonic heart or forebrain. Alluvial plots show the global reorganization of chromatin domains (in Mb) between mESCs and E11.5 heart or forebrain (Fig. 2A, Fig. S2A). LADs remain largely unchanged in the differentiated cell types compared to mESCs with 91.8 % of the mESC LADs remaining LADs in E11.5 hearts, and 92.8 % remaining LADs in E11.5 forebrains. Nevertheless, a small subset – up to 7 to 8 percent of mESC LADs or more than 75–85 Mb – is reorganized to KODs or open regions (Fig. 2A, Fig. S2A–B). These regions are reminiscent of so-called variable or facultative LADs described previously (Meuleman et al., 2013). Comparing domains across the three cell types, 76 % of LADs are common to all three, i.e. the same regions designated as LADs in one cell type are also LADs in both of the other two cell types (Fig. 2B). To determine whether there are any differences in lineage-specific features between the common LADs and LADs that switch only in one cell type, we examined the gene and regulatory content of these regions. The common LADs cover 811 Mb or 34.6 % of the genome and include almost two thousand protein-coding genes (Fig. 2C, Fig. S2B). Variable LADs that are open only in a single cell type cover from 5 to 18 Mb and contain fewer than two hundred genes each (Fig. 2C). We found that gene density was not uniform across common and variable LADs. While promoters and enhancers were significantly depleted across all LAD categories (Supplemental Fig. S2B–C), regions categorized as LADs in mESCs that transitioned to open chromatin in either E11.5 heart or E11.5 forebrain contained gene bodies at a higher density than common LADs, resulting in a significant enrichment of genes in these cell-type specific open regions (Fig. 2C). Together, our data show that cell type-specific LADs contain some gene bodies, but both common and variable LADs are depleted for promoter and distal regulatory element content.

Although genes are relatively rare in LADs, we observed cell type-specific gene expression that correlated with cell type-specific configurations of LADs. In cases where a gene locus was in an open chromatin region in one cell type, but in a LAD in the other two cell types, the expression of that gene was generally higher in the tissue in which the gene was in open chromatin (Fig. 2D). Notably, many of these genes were found to be lineage specific. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the genes found in LADs in mESCs but in open regions only in E11.5 heart or only in E11.5 forebrain were significantly enriched for genes in categories specific to that tissue, such as cardiac muscle differentiation or synaptic signaling, respectively (Fig. 2E). This is consistent with prior studies that documented lineage-specific genes being “released” from LADs and expressed in the appropriate cell type (Peric-Hupkes et al., 2010; See et al., 2019). Taken together, these data demonstrate that LADs in the developing embryo are mostly stable and relatively promoter- and enhancer-poor, but they do contain tissue-specific genes associated with cell identity.

KODs differ between cell types and are enriched for lineage-specific enhancers

We next compared the cell type-specific configurations of KODs in mESCs, E11.5 heart and E11.5 forebrain and found that a moderate fraction of regions designated as KODs in mESCs remained KODs in heart (66.4 %) or forebrain (57.4 %) (Fig. 3A, Fig. S3A). Only 45 % of KODs were common to all three tissues, compared to 76 % of LADs that were common to all three tissues, indicating more cell type variability for KODs than LADs (Fig. 3B). The common KODs in our datasets make up approximately 200 Mb or roughly 8.4 % of the genome, and KODs that switch to open chromatin in a single cell type vary from 13 to 84 Mb (Supplemental Fig. S3A–B). While these domains represent a small fraction of the genome, analysis of their gene and regulatory element content revealed that common KODs overlap tens of thousands of distal transcriptional enhancers, and both common and variable KODs were significantly enriched for enhancers (Fig. 3C). Notably, KODs of all categories (common as well as switching KODs) were enriched for distal enhancers to nearly the same extent as that observed for open chromatin regions (Supplemental Fig. S3C). The enrichment of enhancers in KODs that become cell type-specific open chromatin is even more striking when compared to promoters which were, in most cases, not enriched in KODs (Fig. S3C). The main exception was seen for regions that were open exclusively in mESCs, which did show promoter enrichment (Supplemental Fig. S3B–C).

Cell type-specific KOD configurations maintain cell type-specific enhancers in a repressed state

When we compared the H3K9me2 signal in KODs from various cell types, the signal differences were sometimes broad enough to result in full domain-scale changes. But we also observed smaller scale, local “valleys” of H3K9me2 depletion within the broader KOD (Fig. 3D). By leveraging multiple biological replicates of H3K9me2 in each cell type, we annotated statistically significant, tissue-specific H3K9me2-depleted valleys in KODs (see Methods). To identify cell type-specific enhancers, we analyzed chromatin accessibility using publicly available ATAC-seq data from mESCs, and E11.5 heart and forebrain. ATAC peaks, a reliable indicator of enhancer activity (Daugherty et al., 2017), were identified for each cell type and enhancers accessible only in a single cell type were defined as tissue-specific enhancers. We found that these tissue-specific enhancers were significantly enriched in the tissue-specific H3K9me2-depleted regions within KODs (Fig. 3E). Consistent with a lineage-specific regulatory role for KODs, the top GO categories for genes shown to be associated with enhancers (“linked enhancers”) from heart-specific H3K9me2-depleted regions include terms related to cardiac development and morphogenesis, and genes with linked enhancers from forebrain-specific H3K9me2-depleted regions include terms related to neurogenesis and brain development (Fig. 3F). These results reveal that KODs differ between cell types and that local H3K9me2 depletion occurs in regions containing lineage-specific enhancers.

H3K9me2 is depleted at active enhancers

Based on the enrichment of tissue-specific enhancers in H3K9me2-depleted regions within KODs, we predicted that these H3K9me2 valleys might reflect tissue-specific transcriptional activation. As an example, the KOD that spans the myocardin (Myocd) regulatory region (gene body, promoter, and all distal enhancers) includes local H3K9me2 signal depletion specifically in E11.5 heart where Myocd is expressed (Fig. 4A). Notably, these heart-specific H3K9me2 valleys correlate with E11.5 heart-specific peaks of H3K27ac, a histone modification associated with enhancer activation (Fig. 4A). We evaluated the genome-wide relationship between H3K9me2 and H3K27ac by separating all enhancers within KODs into groups of active and inactive according to the H3K27ac signal in each tissue. At active enhancers marked by H3K27ac, H3K9me2 was locally depleted at the H3K27ac peak, but not in the neighboring 5 kilobases upstream and downstream of the enhancer (Fig. 4B–C, Fig. S4). This indicates that, even in KODs that are shared among cell types, H3K9me2 can vary locally and in accordance with other markers of cell type-specific enhancers. Peaks of chromatin accessibility, another feature of active enhancers, were also observed at local H3K9me2 valleys (Fig. 4D, Fig. S4). The finding that two markers of tissue-specific enhancer activation, H3K27ac and ATACseq peaks, correlate with local H3K9me2 depletion in KODs suggests that KODs may contribute to the regulation of tissue-specific enhancers. Predicting that local changes in KODs might reflect changes in the activity of developmentally relevant regulatory elements, we next asked whether cell type-specific H3K9me2 depletion correlated with cell type-specific gene expression. In heart and forebrain, transcript levels of the genes linked to enhancers found in H3K9me2-depleted regions of KODs were higher in the cell type with the H3K9me2 depletion (Fig. 4E). Our results are consistent with a model in which KODs maintain enhancers in an inactive state that can be released through stage- or tissue-specific local removal of the repressive H3K9me2 modification corresponding with increases in chromatin accessibility and enhancer activation.

Gene deserts overlapping KODs are rich in regulatory elements

Protein-coding genes make up roughly 1–2 % of the mouse genome, but the genes are not evenly distributed across the genome. Instead, there are regions of high gene density as well as so-called gene deserts – long stretches of hundreds of kilobases that lack protein-coding genes (Ovcharenko et al., 2005). Numerous well-studied gene deserts, such as those adjacent to the Shh, Runx1, Bcl11b, and Sox2 loci, have been shown to contain distal long-range enhancers critical for precise spatio-temporal gene regulation (Lettice et al., 2002; Li et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2014; Owens et al., 2022), and we observed KODs flanking several of these regions (Fig. 5A; Fig. S5A). We verified that, for all three cell types in our study, gene deserts (defined as 100 kb or more without a protein-coding gene) were significantly enriched for LADs; KODs were found in gene deserts about as often as would be expected by chance, while open chromatin was significantly depleted from gene deserts (Fig. S5B). In total, gene deserts were comprised of an average of 65 % LADs and 16 % KODs (with the remainder being open chromatin or unassigned; Fig. S5C). Consistent with previous reports finding H3K9me2 to be a common feature of gene deserts in mammalian genomes (Rosenfeld et al., 2009), we observed an average of 70 % of LADs and 40–45 % of KODs overlapped gene deserts in the three cell types (Fig. 5B). To determine whether there was any difference between gene deserts overlapping LADs and KODs, we compared the regulatory content of these regions and found that those within KODs had approximately 5-fold higher enhancer density than those overlapping LADs (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, the density of lincRNAs in KOD-overlapping gene deserts was more than 3-fold higher than that found in deserts overlapping LADs (Fig. S5D). This echoes the general features of LADs and KODs throughout the genome (Fig. 1). The presence of numerous putative regulatory elements within KOD-marked gene deserts adjacent to critical developmental genes (Fig. 5A, Fig. S5A) supports a model in which KODs denote genomic regions that participate in complex gene regulation.

KODs are enriched for regulatory regions that span large linear distances

Even outside of gene deserts, regulatory regions can encompass distal enhancers located hundreds of kilobases away from their cognate promoter (Fig. 6A). We postulated that KODs might be enriched for ultra-long regulatory regions. For each set of enhancers shown to be regulating or “linked” to a given gene, we calculated the total length of the annotated regulatory region (defined as the number of kilobases separating the two most distal elements – promoter or enhancer). We then split the regulatory regions into quartiles by size, with the smallest regions assigned to quartile 1 (q1; ranging from 3.5 to 101 kb) and the largest assigned to quartile 4 (q4; 666 to 27,650 kb) (Fig. 6A–B). The distributions of sizes of regulatory regions in each quartile and the number of enhancers associated with an individual gene are shown (Fig. 6B–C). We calculated the probability of regions in each quartile to overlap LADs, KODs, or open euchromatin. We found that the largest, q4 regulatory regions were significantly enriched in KODs in all three cell types (Fig. 6D). In contrast, the smallest, q1 regulatory regions overlapped KODs only about as often as expected by chance (Fig. 6D). The opposite trend was observed for open chromatin: q4 regulatory regions were not enriched in open chromatin, while the smallest, q1 regulatory regions were significantly enriched in open chromatin domains for all three tissues. All regulatory regions, regardless of size, were significantly depleted from LADs in all three cell types (Fig. 6D). The overrepresentation of KODs in parts of the genome with the longest spanning regulatory regions and their infrequent occurrence in short regulatory regions suggests that KODs play a role in the 3D organization of regulatory regions with distal enhancers located hundreds of kilobases away from their promoter in linear space.

Discussion

Here we report evidence of cell type-specific, genome-wide H3K9me2-modified chromatin domains that change over the course of mouse embryonic development. Prior to this study, KODs had been described in pluripotent mouse cells, but it was not known whether they existed in other cell types and, if so, whether they remain static across different cell types or adopt cell type-specific configurations. We identified KODs in embryonic mouse heart and forebrain tissues and found that they display tissue-specific patterns, suggesting that they reorganize during differentiation. Further, cell type-specific differences in KODs were associated with differences in lineage-specific gene expression programs. Regions of the genome that are organized as KODs in mESCs, but are open chromatin in heart or forebrain, contain lineage-specific enhancers linked to cardiac- or neuronal-related genes, respectively (Fig. 7). Further, we found that H3K9me2 is often locally depleted over enhancers in KODs, and these small reductions in H3K9me2 correspond to peaks of H3K27ac that designate active enhancers. Promoter and enhancer states and their impact on gene expression have been well characterized in terms of associated epigenetic modifications such as H3K4me3 at active promoters, H3K27me3 and H3K4me1 at inactive poised enhancers, and H3K27ac together with H3K4me1 at active enhancers (Creyghton et al., 2010; Heintzman et al., 2009; Rada-Iglesias et al., 2011). The results presented here suggest that loss of H3K9me2 may make an enhancer available for acquisition of other enhancer marks or coincide with the full activation of a distal transcriptional enhancer. In this context, it is interesting that local depletion of H3K9me2 in mESC KODs does not always correspond to higher expression of the associated genes in mESCs (Fig. 4E). This may reflect the pluripotent nature of mESCs and would be consistent with a model in which depletion of H3K9me2 in KODs makes the enhancer available for activation, while subsequent steps are necessary for gene expression. Taken together, the characterization and comparison of enhancer-rich KODs across multiple cell types revealed that KODs adopt cell type-specific configurations that reflect differential enhancer usage required for the regulation of tissue-specific gene programs.

(A) The model illustrates the correlation between chromatin reorganization and activation of lineage-specific genes (in LADs) and lineage-specific enhancers (in KODs) that occurs during cell differentiation. (B) A schematic illustration of lineage-specific transcriptional enhancer organization in a KOD. Enhancers linked to a given promoter are indicated. In precursor cells (top), enhancers in a KOD are in an inactive, H3K9me2-modified state. During cell differentiation, enhancers undergo local loss of H3K9me2 and acetylation of H3K27 to allow for gene expression.

LADs have been well studied, especially in human cells, and are the better characterized H3K9me2 domain at the nuclear periphery but they have yet to be mapped in tissues from mid-gestation mouse embryos; we include them here for comparison with KODs. As in other cell types, LADs in heart and forebrain tissues from mouse E11.5 embryos are relatively gene-poor, but they do harbor some tissue-specific genes in regions that vary by cell type. Regions of the genome that are organized into LADs in mESCs and transition to open chromatin in heart or forebrain contain lineage-specific genes related to cardiac or neuronal function, respectively (Fig. 7). While we acknowledge that cultured mESCs are imperfect representatives of the undifferentiated cell types from which the embryonic mouse heart and forebrain cells have developed, this study includes findings of cell type-specific differences in LAD configurations that correlate with cell type-specific gene expression patterns, in agreement with previous reports (Shah et al., 2023; Peric-Hupkes et al., 2010). The identification of tissue-specific KOD configurations is novel and suggests that KODs impact tissue-specific organization of transcriptional enhancers. Together, the tissue-specific configurations of LADs and KODs demonstrate that the distribution of H3K9me2-marked domains are not static but vary by cell type in concordance with lineage-specific gene expression.

LADs and KODs are both characterized by the H3K9me2 histone modification associated with transcriptionally repressed peripheral heterochromatin, but these two types of domains differ in critical ways that we predict are relevant to their roles in gene regulation. LADs have been shown through both biochemical assays of Lamin B1 interaction and oligo-FISH imaging to be closely associated with the lamina at the nuclear periphery, while KODs are much less likely to interact with the nuclear lamina (Smith et al., 2021). Additionally, LADs encompass highly condensed heterochromatin and are generally depleted for genes, promoters, and enhancers, and enriched for LINE transposable elements (Fig. 1). In contrast, KODs contain moderately accessible chromatin and are enriched for enhancers, lincRNAs, antisense RNAs, and SINEs – all elements associated with regulatory competence (Fig. 1, Fig. S1). The intermediate chromatin accessibility of KODs leads us to speculate that KOD-localized enhancers might be somewhat permissive for binding of transcription factors or chromatin remodeling factors necessary for gene activation. Consistent with comparatively higher chromatin accessibility, we observe more transcription occurring in KODs than LADs (Fig. 1). As previously reported, KODs in mESCs are found mainly in transcriptionally active A compartment where they are more likely to interact with other A compartment regions of the genome that include open, transcriptionally active, gene-rich euchromatin (van Steensel and Belmont, 2017; Oji et al., 2024). Chromatin accessibility and transcriptional activity in KODs might result in reduced interactions with the nuclear lamina; alternately, reduced lamina interactions might produce increased accessibility and transcription. Though we cannot presently draw conclusions about cause and effect, it will be an interesting focus of future investigation.

Identification of KODs in multiple cell types allowed us to uncover a previously unappreciated correlation between H3K9me2 and epigenetic marks of active enhancers. Within KODs, we observe valleys of H3K9me2 where peaks of both chromatin accessibility and marks associated with enhancer activity such as H3K27ac overlap tissue-specific enhancers, particularly in the tissues in which the enhancer-linked genes are expressed. This suggests a model in which KODs play a role in maintaining transcriptional enhancers in a repressed state and the removal of H3K9me2 is part of the molecular mechanisms that lead to enhancer and gene activation. A study of histone H3.3K27R mutant-expressing mESCs demonstrated that H3K27ac might not be required for aspects of gene activation such as increased chromatin accessibility and acetylation of other histone residues occur in the absence of H3K27 acetylation (Zhang et al., 2020). It is tempting to speculate that removal of a repressive histone modification, achieved through local depletion of H3K9me2 at enhancers, might be an essential step for H3K9me2-modified enhancers to transition to an active state.

The levels of 3D genome organization range from chromosome territories and A/B compartments to nucleosome compaction and enhancer-promoter loops. Changes in histone modifications are thought to reflect, if not dictate, rearrangement of the genome at all of these levels. Numerous studies have documented the variation of LADs across cell types (Poleshko et al., 2017; Peric-Hupkes et al., 2010; Robson et al., 2016; Zheng et al., 2018; Pal et al., 2023). These differences not only alter which genomic regions are found in the B compartment, localized to the nuclear periphery and in contact with the nuclear lamina in a given cell type, but also change the DNA accessibility and proximity to other genomic regions. In this way, changes in LADs alter the actual or potential intra- and inter-chromosomal interactions. Similarly, changes in histone marks such as increased H3K27ac at an active enhancer have been shown to correlate with the “rewiring” of enhancer-promoter interactions during development (Siersbaek et al., 2017). While this study did not include specific investigation of the impacts of cell type-specific KOD configurations on nuclear organization, we would predict that 3D genome rearrangements, including changes in accessibility, enhancer-promoter interactions, and/or proximity to the nuclear periphery, are likely to occur alongside changes in H3K9me2 domain identity.

Results of this study suggest that KODs contribute to the molecular mechanisms underlying long-range control of gene expression. We found that KODs are enriched for sets of distal enhancers that form exceptionally long gene regulatory regions. Three-dimensional chromatin organization ensures the proper spatial arrangement of regulatory regions such that distal and proximal elements are appropriately available to interact with each other and with the genes whose transcription they modulate (Czapiewski and Schirmer, 2024). One of the most well-studied ultra-long regulatory regions, that of the Shh gene, is notably found in a KOD (Fig. 5) and its broadly distributed regulatory elements are arranged in a compact configuration (Harke et al., 2024). Based on our findings that KODs are significantly enriched for ultra-long regulatory regions spanning hundreds of kilobases, we posit that KODs play a role in arranging these regions in 3D space, likely bringing far-flung elements closer together to potentiate gene activation.

We posit that KODs consolidate broadly distributed regulatory elements into an intermediate staging area that is governed by transcriptional repression and intermediate chromatin accessibility. In this model, such an arrangement both facilitates and constrains the interactions of these elements with each other and with their cognate promoters to allow for complex and potentially cell-type specific regulation of gene expression. As we continue to probe the relationship between genome structure and function, the contributions of H3K9me2-marked domains will likely factor into the understanding of higher order chromatin organization and its role in the intricate spatiotemporal patterns of gene expression required for mammalian development.

Conclusions

Analyses of H3K9me2-modified genomic domains identified in tissues from mid-gestation mouse embryos reveal cell type-specific differences that correlate with changes in cell identity.
