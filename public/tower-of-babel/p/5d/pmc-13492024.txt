Prognostic value of X-box binding protein 1 and phospholipase A2 in myocardial injury after ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction: a prospective observational study
Authors: Fei Wang, Meng Ning, Yingwu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study explored the role of X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1), phospholipase A2 (PLA2), and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) as biomarkers for diagnosing and predicting myocardial injury in patients with ST-segment elevation acute myocardial infarction (STEMI).MethodsThis prospective cohort study enrolled 283 adult patients with Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) admitted to Tianjin Third Central Hospital between October 2021 and October 2022. Patient selection followed strict criteria: inclusion required ACS diagnosis or high ACS risk with informed consent; exclusion criteria were history of myocardial infarction, ejection fraction < 30%, or comorbidities (e.g., cardiomyopathy, renal disease). Patients were divided into STEMI and non-STEMI/unstable angina groups based on electrocardiogram findings. Serum levels of XBP1, PLA2, and other related indicators were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits. The relationships between these biomarkers and myocardial injury were analyzed.ResultsXBP1 and PLA2 levels were significantly elevated in patients with myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI), myocardial injury, and those undergoing intervention. Recombinant human BNP (rhBNP) treatment was associated with lower XBP1 and PLA2 levels 24 h post-surgery. Logistic regression analysis identified smoking history, neutrophil percentage, sICAM-1, and PLA2 as independent risk factors for myocardial injury. XBP1, PLA2, and BNP levels were independently related to myocardial injury and showed significant positive correlations with each other. ROC curve analysis indicated that XBP1 and PLA2 levels had significant diagnostic efficacy for postoperative myocardial injury, with the combined diagnostic approach yielding the best performance.ConclusionPre-treatment serum PLA2 and XBP1 levels, alone or in combination, may serve as significant predictors of myocardial injury in ACS patients undergoing PCI. rhBNP may protect the myocardium and improve cardiac function by reducing XBP1 and PLA2 levels. These findings suggest that pre-treatment serum PLA2 and XBP1 levels could be used as practical biomarkers for risk stratification in ACS patients undergoing PCI, aiding clinicians in decision-making regarding rhBNP therapy. Further studies are needed to validate these results and integrate them into clinical guidelines.Keywords: XBP1, PLA2, rhBNP, Acute coronary syndrome, Myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, Myocardial injury

Introduction

ACS encompasses a spectrum of coronary artery pathologies, including unstable angina, ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), and non-STEMI. Patients with ACS commonly present with chest pain or a sensation of chest pressure, although atypical symptoms may also occur. ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), a severe cardiovascular condition, is characterized by myocardial cell necrosis secondary to prolonged ischemia [1]. Primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is the preferred therapeutic strategy for STEMI. By promptly revascularizing the culprit vessel, pPCI safeguards left ventricular systolic function, curtails the extent of irreversible myocardial damage, and enhances post-infarction survival [2, 3]. Despite the efficacy of pPCI, a significant proportion of STEMI patients still experience adverse left ventricular remodeling (ALVR), characterized by detrimental structural and functional changes in the left ventricle. Left ventricular remodeling is a pivotal factor in the progression, prognosis, and survival quality of cardiac diseases [4]. The optimal diagnostic characteristics and therapeutic regimens for STEMI patients are crucial for predicting and preventing clinical complications. During hospitalization, various parameters in STEMI patients, including cardiac structural indices, cardiac functional parameters, coronary lesion characteristics, and biomarker levels, serve as significant predictors of myocardial injury [5]. However, there is currently a paucity of evidence regarding effective tools that can assist clinicians in forecasting the occurrence of myocardial injury in STEMI patients.

The emergence of biomarkers for STEMI patients has been proven to enhance the prediction of mortality and complication risks [6, 7]. Accurate and reliable detection of biomarkers is crucial for the correct diagnosis of STEMI and the examination of evidence of myocardial necrosis. XBP1, a major transcription factor associated with the unfolded protein response (UPR), regulates the expression levels of brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) in cardiomyocytes [8]. Meanwhile, XBP1 also activates gene transcription involved in immune cell differentiation, cancer cell survival, autophagy responses, as well as glucose homeostasis and lipid metabolism. Several articles have revealed the potential regulatory role of XBP1 in various cardiovascular diseases, including diabetes, atherosclerosis, and myocardial ischemia. PLA2, a member of the lipolytic enzyme family, plays a functional role in multiple cellular reactions, such as phospholipid digestion and metabolism, host defense, and signal transduction [9, 10]. Evidence suggests that PLA2 is highly expressed in various diseases, including inflammation, sepsis, Alzheimer’s disease, and different types of cancer. BNP is a circulating hormone produced by ventricular muscle, which regulates osmotic balance, blood pressure, and cardiovascular function [11, 12]. Recombinant human brain natriuretic peptide (rhBNP) has a structure and biological properties similar to endogenous BNP. It has been reported that rhBNP can shorten hospital stays, enhance myocardial function and clinical efficacy without increasing the incidence of adverse reactions, which is helpful for diagnosis and prognostic evaluation and also has predictive value for STEM patients [13–15]. The occurrence of myocardial injury in STEM patients usually precedes clinically evident manifestations and eventually develops into severe myocardial injury, which remains a clinical challenge [16, 17]. The purpose of this study is to evaluate whether the concentration of STEM biomarkers is elevated compared to other clinical characteristics, and secondly, to analyze whether these biomarkers may be helpful in predicting the occurrence of myocardial injury in STEM patients.

Subjects and methods of study

Research sample

A total of 362 patients with chest pain or suspected ACS admitted to the Emergency Department or Cardiology Department of the Third Central Hospital of Tianjin were screened for eligibility. Of these, 283 patients met the inclusion criteria and were finally enrolled in this study, while 79 patients were excluded due to a history of myocardial infarction, ejection fraction < 30%, or significant comorbidities. The sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome of predicting myocardial injury. Preliminary data and previous literature indicated an expected area under the ROC curve of approximately 0.67 for XBP1 and PLA2. Assuming a two-sided alpha level of 0.05 and a statistical power (1-β) of 0.80, a minimum sample size of 186 participants was required to detect diagnostic significance. Considering a potential 10% dropout or incomplete data rate, the final enrollment of 283 patients was deemed more than sufficient to provide robust statistical power for the multivariable logistic regression and subgroup analyses.

This was a single-center, prospective observational cohort study. Consecutive adult patients admitted to the Department of Heart Center with a suspected or confirmed diagnosis of ACS between October 2021 and October 2022 were screened for eligibility at the Third Central Hospital of Tianjin. The study followed a strict longitudinal design where exposure variables (biomarkers) were measured at baseline, and clinical outcomes were observed and adjudicated during the subsequent hospital course. To ensure the prospective nature of the data, patients were enrolled in real-time upon presentation before any intervention or outcome assessment. A total of 283 adult patients diagnosed with ACS were enrolled based on predefined selection criteria: inclusion required ACS diagnosis or high ACS risk with informed consent, while exclusion criteria included history of myocardial infarction, ejection fraction < 30%, or significant comorbidities.

Upon enrollment (Time 0), baseline blood samples were collected for biomarker analysis. Based on initial electrocardiogram findings, patients were initially categorized into two main groups: ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI, n = 137) and non-STEMI/unstable angina (n = 146). The follow-up period extended through the duration of the hospitalization to observe the occurrence of pre-specified endpoints. For specific analyses, distinct subgroupings were applied based on the subsequent clinical progression:: (1) Within the STEMI cohort, patients were evaluated for MIRI based on post-intervention clinical manifestations (e.g., arrhythmias, no-reflow phenomenon), resulting in MIRI (n = 61) and non-MIRI (n = 76) groups; (2) STEMI patients were also classified by the physician’s decision regarding rhBNP treatment into rhBNP (n = 58) and control (n = 79) groups; (3) Non-STEMI/unstable angina patients were divided into myocardial injury (n = 76) and non-myocardial injury (n = 70) groups using biomarker criteria assessed at follow-up; and (4) All ACS patients were grouped by PCI intervention status into intervention (n = 148) and non-intervention (n = 135) groups. These subgroupings were used for separate comparative analyses to avoid overlap, and statistical methods included multivariate adjustments to control for potential confounding. The screening and grouping workflow is summarized in Fig. 1. Importantly, laboratory personnel performing the ELISA assays were blinded to the clinical characteristics and future outcomes of the patients to minimize bias. All patients were managed based on scientific clinical assessment, including (i) serum cardiac troponin I (Trop-I) measurement at initial visit, (ii) ECG examination, and (iii) echocardiography. If no evidence of myocardial ischemic injury or necrosis was found in all three tests, the diagnosis was confirmed. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Central Hospital of Tianjin, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inclusion criteria

Patients with ACS or at risk of ACS who were willing to sign the informed consent form were eligible for inclusion in the study.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with a history of myocardial infarction, an ejection fraction less than 30%, or comorbidities such as cardiomyopathy, renal disease, liver disease, malignancy, or active infection/inflammatory conditions were excluded.

Standard clinical management

All enrolled patients received guideline-directed management for ACS during their hospitalization, which formed the standard care background for this observational study. This management included, but was not limited to: (1) Pharmacological Therapy: Dual antiplatelet therapy (e.g., aspirin combined with a P2Y12 inhibitor), statins, beta-blockers, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers were administered according to contemporary clinical guidelines and individual patient tolerance. (2) Revascularization Decision: The decision to perform PCI, its timing, and procedural strategy were made by the attending cardiologist based on the patient’s clinical condition, coronary angiography findings, and guideline recommendations. (3) Monitoring and Supportive Care: Continuous electrocardiographic monitoring, serial assessments of cardiac biomarkers (troponin, CK-MB), and management of hemodynamics and symptoms were provided as standard.

Therapeutic intervention

For STEMI patients in the rhBNP group, recombinant human brain natriuretic peptide (rhBNP) was administered intravenously. The treatment regimen consisted of an initial bolus of 1.5 µg/kg intravenously over 1 min, followed by a continuous intravenous infusion at a rate of 0.0075 µg/kg/min for 72 h. This regimen was initiated upon admission and prior to PCI, in accordance with standard clinical protocols for patients with acute heart failure complicating STEMI.

Diagnostic criteria for myocardial injury

According to the 2018 Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction, post-PCI cardiac marker levels are a key indicator of myocardial injury, post-PCI cardiac marker levels are a key indicator of myocardial injury. Major myocardial injury biomarkers include cTnI, CK-MB, and CRP. Post-PCI cTn elevation ≥ 20%, along with any of the following, indicates myocardial injury: (i) Ischemic symptoms like chest pain lasting over 2 min. (ii) New ischemic ECG changes. (iii) Imaging findings consistent with post-PCI complications, such as side-branch occlusion or slow/no reflow. (iiii) Nuclear myocardial perfusion imaging showing new viable myocardial defects or new wall motion abnormalities.

Definition and distinction: myocardial infarction vs. injury

The diagnosis of AMI was strictly based on the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction, requiring biochemical evidence of myocardial injury (defined as a dynamic change of cardiac troponin with at least one value above the 99th percentile upper reference limit) accompanied by clinical evidence of acute myocardial ischemia. Patients were classified as having ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) if the ECG showed new ST-segment elevation at the J-point in two contiguous leads, or as non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) if other ischemic ECG changes or evidence were present. In contrast, myocardial injury was biochemically defined as an elevated cardiac troponin value (> 99th percentile URL), with the specific cohort for this study operationally defined by a post-procedural increase in troponin of > 20% from a pre-procedural elevated baseline. The critical distinction is that AMI requires objective evidence of acute myocardial ischemia as the underlying cause of the injury, whereas myocardial injury is a broader biochemical entity that does not necessarily fulfill the complete clinical criteria for AMI, allowing for separate analysis of biomarker patterns in our study.Collection of clinical data.

The clinical data collected from patients encompasses multiple facets. Basic information such as name, gender, and age is recorded. Medical history, including conditions like hypertension and diabetes, is documented. Cardiac function -related indicators are assessed, involving parameters such as atrial and ventricular dimensions, ejection fraction values, and cardiac function class. The clinical parameters include the number of diseased vessels, onset time, and D-B time. Blood routine indicators measured and recorded consist of ALT, AST, urea, creatinine, TG, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein, low-density lipoprotein, atherosclerosis index, white blood cell percentage (%), neutrophil percentage (%), and neutrophil count percentage (%).

Crucially, peripheral blood for the experimental biomarkers (XBP1, PLA2) was collected in the morning following an overnight fast immediately after admission and prior to the development of MIRI or post-PCI myocardial injury. Peripheral blood was collected from patients in the morning following an overnight fast. For patients undergoing emergency PCI, baseline blood samples were drawn immediately upon admission to the catheterization laboratory prior to the procedure. For patients scheduled for elective PCI or those in the non-intervention group, blood samples were collected in the morning following an overnight fast. The time interval from symptom onset to blood collection was strictly recorded for each patient, with a median interval of 3.5 h (interquartile range, 1.5–6.0 h). To ensure biomarker stability and comparability, all baseline blood samples were obtained within 12 h of the last episode of chest pain. To assess biomarker dynamics and potential treatment effects, samples were obtained at specific time points relative to the intervention: baseline (pre-PCI) samples were collected immediately after admission and prior to the procedure, while follow-up samples were obtained at 24 h post-PCI. This 24-hour time point was selected to capture significant biomarker fluctuations post-reperfusion, a protocol also strictly applied to the rhBNP treatment subgroup analysis. Blood samples (3 mL) were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min, and the serum was separated and stored at -80 °C for subsequent analysis. The concentrations of CK-MB, cTnI, MYO, sICAM-1, PLA2, XBP1, BNP, and other related indicators in the serum samples were measured using ELISA kits (mlbio, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The analysis of these biomarkers was conducted in batches after the completion of patient enrollment, ensuring that the biomarker data could not influence clinical decision-making or endpoint adjudication. The absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA), and the concentrations of the analytes were determined based on standard curves.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).Data analysis was performed using SPSS. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using t-tests, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons. Categorical variables are presented as n (%) and were compared using χ2 tests. To address potential confounding from multiple groupings and the potential influence of symptom duration on biomarker levels, the time from onset to admission (onset time) was included as a covariate in all multivariable models,, multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to identify independent predictors, with covariates selected based on univariate significance. Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to assess correlations between biomarkers. Multivariable linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between clinical and laboratory variables. Covariates included in the models were myocardial injury and variables significant in univariate analysis. Regression analyses (logistic and linear) were employed to identify relationships and predictors, with diagnostics including checks for multicollinearity (VIF < 10) and goodness-of-fit. ROC curve analysis was used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of key biomarkers. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for non-multiple comparison analyses.Multivariable logistic regression was also used to identify risk factors for postoperative MIRI and myocardial injury. To account for multiple hypothesis testing, we applied the Bonferroni correction by adjusting the significance level to P < 0.05 divided by the number of comparisons.

Results of logistic regression analysis of factors influencing the occurrence of MIRI after surgery

Multivariate analysis of myocardial injury in each group of patients

In this study, a Cox proportional hazards regression model was employed to evaluate the association between various variables and the risk of myocardial injury, with adjustments made for potential confounding factors including gender and history of cerebrovascular disease. The analysis revealed that several factors, such as gender (male vs. female, P = 0.255), history of cerebrovascular disease (present vs. absent, P = 0.344), hypertension (present vs. absent, P = 0.813), diabetes (present vs. absent, P = 0.133), neutrophil count (P = 0.465), CK-MB (P = 0.812), cTnI (P = 0.369), BNP (P = 0.29), EF value (P = 0.055), and XBP1 (P = 0.889), showed no significant association with the risk of myocardial injury (P > 0.05, Table 6), with their corresponding hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) all including 1. Conversely, smoking history (present vs. absent, P = 0.016, HR = 1.808, 95% CI: 1.115–2.935), neutrophil percentage (P = 0.001, HR = 1.044, 95% CI: 1.008–1.081), sICAM-1 (P = 0.021, HR = 1.001, 95% CI: 1.000–1.003), and PLA2 (P = 0.009, HR = 0.994, 95% CI: 0.999–0.999) significantly impacted the risk of myocardial injury (P < 0.05, Table 6)). Smoking history, neutrophil percentage, and sICAM-1 were identified as independent risk factors, with HRs > 1 and P-values < 0.05, indicating increased risk with each one-unit rise. PLA2 served as an independent protective factor, with an HR < 1 and P-value < 0.05, suggesting a reduced risk of myocardial injury with each one-unit increase in PLA2 levels. In summary, after adjusting for confounders, only smoking history, neutrophil percentage, sICAM-1, and PLA2 showed independent predictive value for myocardial injury, while other variables did not demonstrate independent effects. However, the P-value for EF value was near 0.05, indicating some uncertainty, and the potential effects of non-significant variables warrant further investigation in future studies.

In clinical practice, BNP, a natriuretic peptide initially identified in the brain but primarily released from the ventricles of the heart, plays a crucial role in regulating blood pressure and cardiovascular function. It is upregulated in response to myocardial stress and volume overload, and elevated BNP levels are associated with various cardiac diseases, including heart failure and acute myocardial infarction. BNP levels are also influenced by factors such as age and sex, and their measurement can aid in the diagnosis and prognosis of cardiac diseases. However, the specific relationship between BNP and EF may vary depending on the individual patient’s condition and other clinical factors.

Discussion

ACS is a life-threatening disease with a substantial global health burden, affecting millions of individuals annually [3]. Recent years have witnessed significant advancements in the diagnosis, prevention, and management of ACS [18]. However, the considerable burden of recurrent ischemic events and the persistently high mortality rate underscore the pressing need for further research to enhance the prognosis of ACS patients. In this study, we aim to delve into the correlations between XBP1 and PLA2 as well as MIRI in ACS patients, and to explore the impact of rhBNP on myocardial injury in patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI).

Our study preliminarily demonstrated that, compared with the non-MIRI group, non-myocardial injury group, and non-intervention group, the levels of XBP1 and PLA2 were significantly elevated in the MIRI group, myocardial injury group, and intervention group. It has been reported that XBP1 can mediate endothelial angiogenesis and proliferation in ischemic tissues via growth factor signaling pathways. Zeng et al. confirmed that in isoproterenol-induced and pressure overload - induced hypertrophic and failing myocardium, myocardial XBP1s protein levels were elevated [19]. XBP1 contributes to the progression of adaptive hypertrophy and provides a new target for the prevention and treatment of heart failure. In addition, some studies have indicated that spliced XBP1 exerts a cardioprotective effect against enhanced cardiac stress by promoting vascular endothelial growth factor-mediated cardiac angiogenesis [20, 21]. These findings have established the role of XBP1 in cardiac-related diseases. PLA2, due to its elevated activity, has been considered for the treatment of various diseases. According to the order of discovery, the PLA2 superfamily is divided into 16 groups, 13 of which have been found to function in cardiac-related diseases. Studies have found that ACS patients have higher Lp-PLA2 activity, and Lp-PLA2 is closely associated with major cardiac adverse events [22]. Moreover, a study disclosed that plasma concentrations of Lp-PLA2 are elevated in cardiovascular diseases, and Lp-PLA2 may serve as a predictor of long-term outcomes in ACS patients. These facts have confirmed the role of PLA2 and its subgroups in cardiac-related diseases.

Our study found that elevated XBP1 and PLA2 levels are influencing factors for MIRI occurrence and post - surgery myocardial injury through logistic regression analysis. Moreover, the levels of XBP1 and PLA2 in the rhBNP group were lower than those in the control group at 24 h after surgery. In addition, rhBNP can achieve myocardial protection and improved cardiac function by lowering XBP1 and PLA2 levels. RhBNP possesses multiple functions like increasing cardiac output, promoting natriuresis and diuresis, and improving cardiac expansion function [23]. So far, the application of rhBNP has been found to have therapeutic effects on a variety of cardiac - related diseases. For example, it can reduce inflammation and renal impairments after heart surgery [24]. One study also disclosed that rhBNP application is beneficial to AMI patients undergoing PCI treatment, which may help to select effective therapeutic options. Zhang et al. pointed out that in a rat model of cardiac arrest, rhBNP can protect against post - resuscitation cardiac dysfunction [25]. Ning et al. also found that rhBNP can reduce the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events and maximize patient benefits, providing new evidence for treating patients with acute carbon monoxide poisoning and high cardiac event risk [26]. However, the effects of rhBNP on XBP1 and PLA2 levels need further study.

Our study indicates that serum XBP1, PLA2, and BNP levels in ACS patients are independently linked to myocardial injury. Notably, certain clinical parameters don’t swiftly predict myocardial injury. Our density distribution analysis of XBP1, PLA2, BNP levels and the commonly used clinical parameter EF reveals they differently affect EF (Figs. 2, 3, 4 and 5). Preventing myocardial injury remains a clinical challenge in ACS patients. EF is a key indicator of cardiac systolic function, and its decrease is typically associated with myocardial injury [27, 28]. CMR studies show that patients with abnormal cardiac systolic function have a about 20% higher risk of myocardial damage than those with normal function [28]. In STEMI patients, some exhibit hypercontractile EF (EF ≥ 50%) post - PCI but have higher in - hospital mortality than those with normal EF. Research shows that an EF threshold of 67.5% exists [29]. Patients above this threshold have an 18.8% in - hospital mortality, versus 0.4% below it, a difference with statistical significance. This implies that patients with normal or elevated EF may still have myocardial injury and poor prognosis. Serum biomarker concentrations (XBP1, PLA2, and BNP) offer early and rapid detection advantages. Thus, our discussion on the relationship between serum XBP1, PLA2, BNP concentrations and EF may hold great clinical significance. BNP, a hormone released in response to myocardial stress, is influenced by factors such as age and sex and is widely used in the diagnosis and prognosis of cardiac diseases. The elevated levels of XBP1 and PLA2 in patients with myocardial injury highlight their potential as clinical tools for early detection and risk assessment. In practice, measuring these biomarkers upon admission could help identify high-risk STEMI patients who may benefit from more aggressive monitoring or adjunctive therapies like rhBNP. This approach could improve personalized treatment and ultimately enhance patient outcomes.

This comprehensive focus on novel biomarkers (XBP1 and PLA2) and the therapeutic agent rhBNP aligns with a broader research trend toward enhanced risk stratification models in cardiovascular disease, particularly within the ACS patient population, which has been the focus of our group’s prior work. For instance, the prognostic utility of new risk factors has been consistently explored, as seen in the independent association of higher serum total bilirubin levels with increased severity of coronary lesions (SYNTAX score) and poor long-term prognosis in patients with new-onset non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) [30]. Furthermore, our studies have successfully integrated multiple data streams, including traditional clinical variables and novel physiological markers, to improve predictive accuracy for long-term prognosis following ACS. Specifically, the inclusion of Deceleration Capacity (DC), an indicator of cardiac autonomic function, significantly enhances the predictive power of established tools like the GRACE score for major adverse cardiac events (MACEs) after ACS [31–32]. In a similar vein of multimodal risk assessment, a data-driven prognostic model was developed that integrated clinical features, biomarkers, and echocardiography data to accurately predict long-term outcomes in patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction after CABG [33]. While the association between fibrinogen and coronary plaque vulnerability was not established in one of our cohort studies using intravascular optical coherence tomography (OCT) [34], the cumulative evidence from these studies underscores the crucial role of identifying and integrating multiple independent predictors—ranging from novel biochemical markers to non-invasive functional assessments—to refine risk stratification, a challenge our current study addresses by focusing on XBP1, PLA2, and rhBNP.

We conducted this study on a small but well - characterized patient sample. In addition to routine blood tests such as alanine aminotransferase, AST, urea, creatinine, TG, TC, HDL, LDL, atherogenesis index, white blood cell percentage (%), neutrophil percentage (%), neutrophil count percentage (%), CK - MB, cTnI, MYO, slCAM − 1, we also carried out biochemical measurements. These measurements, including CK - MB, cTnI, MYO, slCAM − 1, are helpful for evaluating our targets in daily clinical practice. Although evidence supports the diagnostic value of CK - MB, cTnI, MYO, slCAM − 1, we did not find it in this trial. Also, neutrophil percentage (%) and neutrophil count percentage (%) have certain assessment value, but the laboratory tests were mainly based on ELISA. Moreover, assessing myocardial injury added to the study’s complexity. We were able to include patients and conduct follow - up based on our estimated minimum sample size, so we believe the results may be reliable. However, there are several limitations to our study that should be considered. First, it is a single - center study with a relatively small sample size. Second, more indicators need to be measured through echocardiography, which may be less accurate than CMR, although it is more accessible and clinically representative. Third, as mentioned above, there may be many uncontrolled or unmeasured variables that can explain some of the results. Finally, although we previously validated the reliability of blood samples collected within the first 24–48 h after admission [35], the smaller sample size in this study may induce some confounding factors that are difficult to control and may also be affected by post - admission treatment. Clinical implications: Our findings suggest that measuring serum XBP1 and PLA2 levels in ACS patients undergoing PCI could help identify those at high risk for myocardial injury. This may allow for earlier intervention with rhBNP or other protective strategies to improve outcomes. Additionally, the combination of XBP1 and PLA2 may serve as a useful diagnostic tool for risk stratification in clinical practice [9, 12, 20, 36]. Our study did not adjust for multiple hypothesis testing, which may increase the likelihood of false-positive results. Future studies should validate these findings with appropriate statistical corrections.

Conclusion

This study identifies serum PLA2 and XBP1 as promising, novel biomarkers for predicting the risk of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) in ACS patients undergoing PCI. More importantly, it provides the first clinical evidence that the cardioprotective effect of recombinant human brain natriuretic peptide (rhBNP) may be mediated, in part, through the modulation of these biomarkers. This mechanistic insight strengthens the rationale for using rhBNP in high-risk PCI patients and positions PLA2/XBP1 not only as prognostic tools but also as potential therapeutic targets. Further large-scale, multicenter studies are warranted to translate these findings into definitive clinical practice.
