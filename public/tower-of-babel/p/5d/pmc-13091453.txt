Artificial intelligence (AI) for early identification of radiotherapy related toxicities from the electronic health records of patients with head and neck cancer
Authors: Anne Liu, Faisal Alfadli, John Mohan Mathew, Samantha Parmelee, John Waldron, Jennifer Y.Y. Kwan, Steven Aviv, Michelle ML Nguyen, Viktoriia Mokriak, Christopher Pettengell, Philip Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Artificial intelligence model analyzed 207 patient records for chronic radiation toxicities.•Optimized model performance achieved 87% accuracy (F1 = 0.92) across four pre-determined toxicities.•Model processed 759 notes with 20,385 sentences in four seconds.•Artificial intelligence shows potential in detecting chronic radiation toxicities in routine follow-up.

Introduction

Radiation therapy (RT) is a fundamental component of curative treatment for approximately 75% of patients with head and neck cancer (HNC) [1]. While effective, RT is associated with radiation-related late toxicities (RLTs) that can significantly impair quality of life and potentially compromise treatment outcomes [2], [3], [4]. As advancements in treatments improve cancer patient survival rates, a growing population of survivors is experiencing RLTs [5], [6]. However, the true incidence of RLTs is difficult to determine, and reported rates are widely variable, ranging from 4 to 40% [7].

Early identification of RLTs is crucial, as it can lead to earlier interventions and better patient outcomes [8]. However, researching RLTs is challenging because they often appear years after RT when patients have been discharged or have less frequent follow-up appointments [9]. Symptoms can progress slowly and remain unnoticed until multiple toxicities culminate in a severe event requiring hospitalization or re-referral to oncologists [2], [4]. Manual chart review demands considerable time and is prone to inconsistency due to its subjective nature [10]. Furthermore, RLTs are often described in unstructured formats within electronic health records (EHRs), such as multi-disciplinary clinical notes, making them difficult to extract using traditional methods and be recognized for early treatment [11].

Artificial intelligence (AI), particularly natural language processing (NLP), offers a promising solution for analyzing free text in records, identifying patterns, and converting unstructured text into organized, quantitative data derived from routinely documented clinical assessments [12]. Improved recognition of RLTs can help healthcare providers gain a more realistic understanding of the prevalence of RLTs and respond appropriately to address them. Among RLTs associated with the treatment of HNC, osteoradionecrosis (ORN), fibrosis, trismus, and dysphagia are common and clinically important [13]. These conditions often lead to chronic pain, malnutrition, and a significant decline in quality of life, underscoring the need for early and accurate detection [2], [3].

The overarching goal of this research is to leverage AI to better characterize RLTs. Specifically, we aimed to assess the feasibility and accuracy of DARWEN, a previously validated AI engine developed by Pentavere that uses NLP to extract clinical features from unstructured text in electronic health records. In this study, DARWEN was used to identify 4 key RLTs—ORN, fibrosis, trismus, and dysphagia—from the unstructured EHRs of HNC patients.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study analyzed de-identified clinical notes from 207 consecutive HNC patients referred to the Adult Radiation Late Effects Clinic (ARLEC) at the Princess Margaret Cancer Centre from 2000 to 2016. As eligibility for ARLEC requires physician referral specifically for radiation-related toxicities, all included cases had previously undergone clinical assessment by the referring medical practitioner and confirmation by ARLEC physicians. The final attribution of late effects to radiation were done based on the reason for referral by the referring oncologist, ARLEC physician assessment, and a secondary verification by a radiation oncology reviewer.

Patients were included in the analysis based on the availability of complete electronic health record documentation during this period. Patients with documented or suspected cancer recurrence at the time of referral to ARLEC were excluded [2]. As ARLEC referrals typically occur after prolonged surveillance, the likelihood that the late effects documented represented early recurrence rather than radiation-related late effects were reduced. All patients had one or more RLTs of interest, and patients were randomly split into a training cohort (n = 167) and an unseen validation/testing cohort (n = 40). This study received institutional research ethics board (IRB/REB) approval (20–5969).

All notes were extracted from unstructured EHRs that were anonymized to remove identifiable patient information and toxicity labels. A radiation oncologist created a structured rulebook that defined the clinical criteria and documentation patterns for each of the four RLTs. A trained RLT reviewer manually annotated the patient records to create the “ground truth” labels based on physician documentation within the clinical notes. The reviewer underwent training using a standardized rulebook developed by a radiation oncologist practicing within ARLEC, which defined the terminology and clinical phrasing for each toxicity. These were then validated by a second reviewer. The ground truth labels were assigned on a binary basis, indicating either the presence or absence of one or more clinically documented RLTs corresponding to Grade 2 or higher toxicities, as reflected in the ARLEC clinician’s assessments, normalized symptoms, and contextual language patterns within the EHR. Toxicity grading was based on Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) criteria, using symptom descriptions and functional impairments documented within clinical notes [2]. Cases where only mild symptoms were present were not labelled as positive. All reviewer discrepancies within the training cohort were adjudicated by consensus to refine the AI model, prior to final testing on the independent hidden cohort.

DARWENTM AI platform

Created by Pentavere, DARWEN TM is a commercially developed clinical NLP platform whose pretraining methodology and architecture have been described previously [14], [15]. This AI platform was used for RLT detection, though, in the present study, only the query creation and rulebook fine-tuning were performed locally, while all foundational model training was conducted by the developers at Pentavere. The rulebook was converted into queries to fine-tune the NLP on the training dataset to detect four pre-specified RLTs: ORN, fibrosis, trismus, and dysphagia. For each patient, the NLP examined the entirety of their EHR notes to determine the four RLT toxicity statuses.

The AI employs a sequence of four core language models: the relevance model, which filters out irrelevant sentences and retains all clinically significant content; subject detection model, which identifies who the sentence refers to; assertion status model, which classifies the presence of the condition; and the query model, which searches for each of the four predefined toxicities given their context and unique terminology. The four models are coordinated to sequentially refine unstructured clinical notes into accurate toxicity classifications.

An adjudication process was then conducted for discrepancies between the initial ground truth labels and AI predictions by manually re-examining the underlying EHR documentation, with the goals of resolving documentation inconsistencies and labeling errors (Table 1). After the adjustments on the AI were complete, the final model was tested on the unseen cohort to assess its generalizability. Performance was evaluated both before and after adjudication to determine the impact of expert corrections on model refinement. It was evaluated using the following metrics: accuracy, which is the proportion of correct classifications across all types of toxicities; precision, or positive predictive value, which refers to the fraction of predicted positives that were true positives; recall, which is the rate of false negatives; and F1 score, which is the harmonic mean of precision and recall. The computational efficiency was measured by recording the mean time required to process the notes in the validation cohort.

Results

EHR data from 207 patients were included in this study, with 167 used for training and 40 for validation. EHR clinical notes ranged from 58 to 5,416 days between the first and last note for an individual patient. Within the full cohort, documented toxicities included dysphagia (n = 64), trismus (n = 77), fibrosis (n = 96), and osteoradionecrosis (n = 125), with patients frequently exhibiting more than one RLT.

Running the AI model on the training dataset before adjudication and refinement initially returned an accuracy of 53% (F1 = 0.66) for all RLTs. The precision was 42% for dysphagia (F1 = 0.33), 70% for fibrosis (F1 = 0.74), 86% for ORN (F1 = 0.88), and 53% for trismus (F1 = 0.50). The discrepancies between the initial report on the test group and the ground truth were adjudicated by revisiting the training dataset EHRs and confirming the toxicity status. The model was then further fine-tuned by adjusting the model parameters.

After adjudication and fine-tuning, the AI achieved an overall accuracy of 87% (F1 = 0.92) across all toxicities in the validation cohort. Discussion

This study demonstrates the ability of an AI tool to detect indications of RLTs within unstructured clinical notes. After adjudication and tuning, the AI model achieved an overall post-adjudication accuracy of 87% (F1 = 0.92). Its ability to accurately detect these RLTs shows potential for identifying subtle, overlooked toxicities in follow-up visits after RT. It also highlighted discrepancies in toxicity documentation across longitudinal clinical notes, demonstrating potential to surface RLTs that may be inconsistently or incompletely documented during manual chart review. Given its efficiency in processing more than one million characters in approximately four seconds per toxicity, the model shows promise for clinical scaling within healthcare settings. This tool could support longitudinal recognition and surveillance of clinically documented RLTs within routine follow-up care.

The initial baseline results showed higher precision for ORN and fibrosis, likely because these conditions are often described with more distinct and specific terminology in clinical notes based on clear staging criteria [16]. In contrast, dysphagia had the lowest initial precision, which may be due to its symptoms being documented with less consistent language and often being inferred from less specific indicators like dietary changes [17]. The significant improvement in performance post-adjudication underscores the importance of a human-in-the-loop approach to refining AI models and correcting for inconsistencies in manually labeled data.

A significant challenge in managing RLTs is the poor concordance between patient-reported symptoms and what is formally documented in medical records, pointing to a broader issue where many symptoms are never officially recorded despite being discussed. This documentation gap is worsened by diagnostic hurdles for clinicians, who are often better trained to diagnose diseases than to identify nuanced treatment-related toxicities, as well as by the difficulty of tracking the subtle, chronic progression of ungraded symptoms over time [18]. Furthermore, care is frequently fragmented across multiple providers [19], such as oncologists, nurses, and speech-language pathologists, making it challenging for any single clinician to recognize a developing toxicity longitudinally. To address these challenges, AI-assisted software integrated within the EHR may present a potential solution. Such a tool could act as a safety net by identifying and flagging important, but inconsistently documented, toxicities across disciplines that might otherwise be overlooked, allowing healthcare providers to become more aware of potential RLTs and enabling earlier interventions, improved continuity of care, and better patient outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, the validation cohort was relatively small (n = 40), and the data was sourced exclusively from a specialized late-effects clinic (ARLEC), where clinical notes may be more detailed and specific than those in general oncology practice. Consequently, the performance of the model may not be representative of real-world oncology settings where prevalence of severe RLTs is lower and documentation styles vary. A second limitation is the inherent subjectivity of the human-created “ground truth” labels and rulebook, especially for more ambiguous toxicities. Although the subjectivity of the small group of reviewers is a manageable problem for a small cohort of well-defined RLTs, the model may become overfitted to the interpretations of the initial reviewers if applied to larger datasets. Therefore, external validation on larger, more diverse datasets from various clinical settings is necessary to ensure the generalizability of the model and test its real-world performance on different and non-speciality clinics. Future iterations of the model could also be trained to distinguish between higher- and lower-grade toxicities by integrating structured treatment severity outcomes and descriptors.

In conclusion, AI-based RLT detection from unstructured notes is efficient and accurate following optimizations. By automating the unbiased collection of toxicity data, this approach can provide a more realistic understanding of RLT incidence and aid in the development of research and clinical trials aimed at preventing and treating the late effects of RT.
