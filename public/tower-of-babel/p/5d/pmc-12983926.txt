Knowledge, attitudes and practices towards urinary calculi among patients: a cross-sectional study in Jiangsu Province, China
Authors: Nan Zhang, Min Shu, Yingying Jiang, Zhimei Chen, Hu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) of patients with urinary calculi in China, where prior KAP research on this condition is lacking.DesignA cross-sectional study was conducted.SettingThe study was carried out in Jiangsu Province between August and September 2024.ParticipantsPatients diagnosed with urinary calculi were included, with 440 valid questionnaires analysed (65.9% male).Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcomes were KAP scores (knowledge: 0–30, attitude: 6–30, practice: 7–35). Secondary outcomes included structural equation modelling (SEM) analysing relationships between KAP domains.ResultsMean scores were 10.63±3.73 (knowledge), 22.37±4.16 (attitude) and 22.40±5.36 (practice). SEM revealed direct effects of knowledge on attitude (β=0.652, p<0.001) and practice (β=0.375, p<0.001), and attitude on practice (β=0.432, p<0.001). Knowledge also indirectly influenced practice via attitude (β=0.281, p<0.001).ConclusionsPatients with urinary calculi tended to demonstrate limited knowledge but moderate attitudes and practices towards their condition. These findings suggest potential gaps in patient understanding and indicate that educational approaches may play a role in supporting urinary calculi management.Keywords: knowledge, attitude, practice, patient, urinary calculi, cross-sectional study

Introduction

Urinary calculi is a prevalent disease of the urinary system, affecting individuals worldwide. A previous study estimated that urinary calculi affect 12% of men and 5% of women during their lifetime, with a recurrence rate in two-thirds of patients within 20 years.1 The global prevalence of this condition has risen significantly, from approximately 2.2% 50 years ago to about 8.8% today, marking a nearly fourfold increase.2 3 The formation of urinary calculi is a complex process influenced by multiple factors. These factors include an elevated urinary concentration of stone-forming ions, variations in urinary pH and certain anatomical and metabolic factors that inhibit urinary flow and promote crystallisation of stones.4 Symptoms of urinary calculi can vary widely depending on the stone’s position, size and shape but typically include flank pain, haematuria, nausea and vomiting.5 Patient’s factors such as compliance of dietary also play an essential role in the management and prognosis of the disease.

The Knowledge Attitude, and Practice (KAP) theory plays a crucial role in influencing human health behaviours and is frequently used in the healthcare sector to evaluate the knowledge, attitudes and practices of targeted populations.6 This model is essential for enhancing health literacy, as it is based on the idea that increased knowledge positively affects attitudes, which in turn shape behaviours.7 By employing the KAP questionnaire, researchers can measure not only the knowledge and attitudes but also the practices of individuals, thereby assessing their demand for and acceptance of pertinent information.8 Given the notable recurrence rates and the growing prevalence of urinary calculi, understanding the KAP of patients affected by this condition becomes particularly significant. By examining patients’ knowledge and attitudes, gaps in comprehension and prevailing misconceptions that could obstruct effective management of the disease are identified. Moreover, analysing their practices allows healthcare providers to customise interventions that align more closely with the actual needs and behaviours of these patients9

While previous studies have explored the KAP towards urinary calculi in different populations,10 11 evidence from China remains relatively limited. Recently, several KAP studies on urinary calculi have been conducted in China, providing important information on patients’ levels of knowledge, attitudes and practices, as well as associated demographic and clinical factors.6 9 However, these studies were carried out in different regions and populations, and regional variations in lifestyle, healthcare access and health education may influence patients’ KAP patterns. Jiangsu Province is an economically developed region with a high population density and distinct occupational and lifestyle characteristics, yet region-specific evidence on patients’ KAP towards urinary calculi is still scarce. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the KAP of patients with urinary calculi in Jiangsu Province, China, and to provide region-specific evidence to inform locally tailored patient education and management strategies.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between August 2024 and September 2024 in two cities in Jiangsu Province, and included were patients with urinary calculi as participants by convenience sampling. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling approach from urology outpatient clinics and inpatient wards of the participating hospitals. During the study period, eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria were consecutively approached by trained researchers and informed about the purpose and procedures of the study. Patients who agreed to participate provided written informed consent and were then invited to complete the questionnaire either on-site or online. No financial or other incentives were provided for participation.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosed with urinary calculi according to the ‘Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Genitourinary Surgery Diseases in China (2014 Edition)’; (2) aged >18 years old; (3) voluntary participation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) combined with severe organic diseases such as heart, brain, liver and kidney; (2) consciousness disorders or a history of mental illness; (3) anatomical abnormalities of the urinary system.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using Cochran’s sample size estimation equation: n = z2pq/e2. In this formula, n represents the required sample size, z is the z-score corresponding to a 95% CI (1.96), p is the expected proportion, q = 1 − p and e denotes the margin of error set at 5%. As previous studies reported varying prevalence estimates for urinary calculi-related knowledge, attitudes and practices, an expected proportion of 50% was adopted to obtain the maximum required sample size. Based on this calculation, the estimated minimum sample size for the present study was 384 participants.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was initially designed based on pertinent literature.12,14 A pilot study involving 40 participants yielded an overall Cronbach’s α of 0.722, indicating acceptable internal consistency. The Cronbach’s α coefficients for the knowledge, attitude and practice dimensions in the pilot study were 0.783, 0.739 and 0.871, respectively. Content validity was assessed through independent review by three experts in urology and nursing, who evaluated the accuracy, relevance and appropriateness of the items. Face validity was examined during the pilot study by collecting participant feedback on item clarity, comprehensibility and response feasibility, with no major difficulties reported. To enhance clarity, specific examples were provided in the questionnaire (eg, foods rich in citric acid in Knowledge item 15) based on pilot test, to ensure participants were able to understand and respond to these items appropriately. The final questionnaire (online supplemental material) included four dimensions: demographic characteristics, knowledge, attitude and practice. The knowledge dimension comprises 16 questions, with the eighth question serving as a duplicate to gauge questionnaire validity. Participants receive 2 points for each correct response and 0 points for incorrect or ambiguous answers, resulting in a score range of 0–30 points. The attitude dimension consists of eight questions using a 5-point Likert scale. Among the eight questions, items 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6 are positively formulated; item 2 is negatively phrased and items 7 and 8 are not scored, yielding a score range of 6–30 points. Likewise, the practice dimension comprises seven questions employing a 5-point Likert scale, with a score range of 7–35 points. According to the Bloom’s cut-off, participants scoring above 80% of the total are categorised as possessing adequate knowledge, positive attitude and proactive practice; those falling within the range of 60–80% of the total are deemed to have moderate knowledge, attitude and practice; and those scoring below 60% of the total indicate inadequate knowledge, negative attitude and inactive practice.15

The questionnaires were distributed to participants by parper-based (on-site) and online methods. The online questionnaire was distributed among both outpatients and inpatients. Outpatients fill the questionnaire after completing their medical consultations, while inpatients fill it out after routine treatments. Patients fill out the questionnaire themselves, and if they have any questions during the process, they complete it with guidance from the researchers. For patients with difficulty in completing the online questionnaire, the paper-based (on-site) questionnaire was distributed. For ease of data analysis, two researchers double-check the paper questionnaires and input the data into Questionnaire Star.

The online questionnaire is generated using the Questionnaire Star platform, and the (quick response) QR code was printed for patients to scan and fill out. Participants need to log in via WeChat.

Statistical analysis

SPSS V.26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD. Counting data were presented as n (%). The continuous variables confirmed to normal distribution were compared by t-tests or analysis of variance. The continuous variables confirmed to skewed distribution were compared by Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test or Kruskal-Wallis test. The KAP scores were converted to binary variables, and univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with higher KAP scores. The cut-off value was the median of the scores (11, 23 and 27 for knowledge, attitude and practice, respectively). In multivariate analyses, variables with a p<0.05 in univariate analysis were included. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to evaluate the correlations between knowledge, attitude and practice scores. Structural equation modelling (SEM) was used to explore the interaction among KAP. A two-sided p<0.05 was deemed statistically significant in this study. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and all variables included in the regression models showed VIF values below 2.0, indicating no significant multicollinearity. Model performance was further evaluated using Nagelkerke R2, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), demonstrating acceptable discriminative ability of the regression models.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

Initially, a total of 521 patients with urinary calculi participated in this study. Among them, 66 questionnaires had logical conflicts, 15 questionnaires had the same responses in all items and the remaining 440 valid questionnaires were considered valid, with a validity rate of 84.45%. Among them, 290 (65.9%) were male, 203 (46.1%) had a body mass index of 18.5–24 kg/m2, 214 (48.6%) had an education of middle/high/technical school, 369 (83.9%) had received treatment for urinary calculi, 189 (43.0%) were treated with medication to remove the calculi, 324 (73.6%) had been ill for less than a year and 142 (32.3%) experienced recurrence (table 1). Additionally, 422 (95.9%) had social medical insurance, 151 (34.3%) had no underlying disease, but 140 (31.8%) had high blood pressure, followed by 126 (28.6%) with other underlying diseases. Reliability was further evaluated using Cronbach’s α, and construct validity was examined using confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) based on the data from the formal survey. In the formal survey, the overall Cronbach’s α was 0.883, with values of 0.856 for knowledge, 0.777 for attitude and 0.789 for practice, indicating good internal consistency across all dimensions. The CFA showed a borderline model fit (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation [RMSEA]=0.083, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual [SRMR]=0.076, Tucker-Lewis Index [TLI]=0.803, Comparative Fit Index [CFI]=0.826).

Knowledge, attitudes and practices

The knowledge, attitude and practice scores were 10.63±3.73 (possible range: 0–30), 22.37±4.16 (possible range: 6–30) and 22.40±5.36 (possible range: 7–35), respectively. The knowledge score varied from patients with different gender (p=0.004), education (p<0.001), ethnicity (p=0.049), monthly income (p=0.039), family history (p=0.017), whether ever received treatment (p<0.001) and treatment method (p<0.001). As for the attitude score, there were differences among patients with different age (p=0.047), education (p=0.048), whether ever received treatment (p=0.001) and treatment method (p=0.006). The difference in practice score was found among patients with different age (p<0.001), education (p=0.006), occupation (p=0.023), monthly income (p=0.030), family history (p=0.028) and disease duration (p=0.018) (table 1).

The distribution of knowledge dimension revealed that the three questions with the highest number of participants choosing the ‘Unclear’ option were ‘Consuming fewer foods rich in oxalic acid can aid in the treatment and prevention of recurrence of calculi’. (K9), with 43.4%; ‘Consuming fewer foods high in purines can aid in the treatment and prevention of recurrence of urinary system calculi’. (K11), with 33.6%; and ‘Consuming fewer foods rich in citric acid can aid in the treatment and prevention of recurrence of urinary system calculi’. (K15), with 33.2% (online supplemental table 1).

Responses to items on the attitude dimension showed that 33% of patients strongly disagreed that the knowledge they acquired could meet the treatment needs for calculi (A1). Moreover, 10.7% were very worried and 18.4% were worried about the possible symptoms of urinary system calculi (A2). Strikingly, 22.7% strongly agreed and 35.2% agreed that changing their lifestyle and dietary habits due to urinary system calculi makes them feel distressed (A8) (online supplemental table 2).

The practice of the patients was not committed; specifically, 25.9% did not follow the medical advice to improve their diet (P1), 23.2% did not ensure enough exercise in their life (P6) and 21.6% did not go to the hospital for regular check-ups (P7), which showed that there is still a lot of room for improvement for a considerable number of patients’ practice (online supplemental table 3).

Correlation among knowledge, attitudes and practices

The fit indices of the SEM model reached the desired range, indicating good model fit, with CFI=0.962, TLI=0.951, RMSEA=0.045 and SRMR=0.038 (online supplemental table 4), and the analysis showed the direct effects of knowledge on attitude (β=0.652, p<0.001) and practice (β=0.375, p<0.001) as well as of attitude on practice (β=0.432, p<0.001). Moreover, knowledge also has an indirect effect on practice through attitude (β=0.281, p<0.001) (figure 1 and table 2).

Correlation analysis indicated significant positive correlations between knowledge and attitude (r=0.508, p<0.001), as well as practice (r=0.541, p<0.001). Meanwhile, there was also a correlation between attitude and practice (r=0.513, p<0.001) (online supplemental table 5).

Factors associated with knowledge, attitudes and practices

Multivariate logistic regression showed that having education of college or above (OR=2.75, 95% CI 1.37 to 5.55, p=0.005), having monthly income of more than 20 000 Chinese Yuan (CNY) (OR=0.32, 95% CI 0.14 to 0.73, p=0.007) and being treated with medication extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy, surgical calculi removal or other methods (OR >1 for all) were independently associated with knowledge (online supplemental table 6). Concurrently, knowledge score (OR=1.28, 95% CI 1.19 to 1.38, p<0.001) and being treated with extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (OR=0.28, 95% CI 0.09 to 0.88, p=0.029) were independently associated with attitude (online supplemental table 7). Furthermore, attitude score (OR=1.47, 95% CI 1.27 to 1.71, p<0.001), occupation not related to medicine (OR=0.11, 95% CI 0.02 to 0.55, p=0.007), having monthly income of 5000–10 000 CNY (OR=2.99, 95% CI 1.11 to 8.09, p=0.031), no family history (OR=16.45, 95% CI 1.88 to 143.70, p=0.011) and unclear of family history (OR=14.05, 95% CI 1.12 to 176.09, p=0.040) were independently associated with practice (online supplemental table 8). The overall performance of the multivariate logistic regression models was acceptable, with AUC values of 0.822 for knowledge, 0.651 for attitude and 0.848 for practice. Detailed model performance metrics, including Nagelkerke R2, sensitivity, specificity and F1-score (online supplemental table 9). There was no significant multicollinearity (all VIF <2, online supplemental tables 10–12).

Discussion

While patients with urinary calculi demonstrated moderate attitudes and practices, their knowledge about the condition was found to be inadequate. These findings suggest that improving patient education programmes, tailored to different education levels and socioeconomic statuses, could enhance knowledge, attitudes and practices regarding the management of urinary calculi. In the context of China, several sociocultural factors may influence patient behaviour and understanding. For instance, culturally influenced health beliefs may affect patients’ perceptions of urinary disorders and contribute to underestimation of evidence-based medical guidance.9 16 17 However, the present study did not directly assess health literacy or cultural beliefs, and therefore, these factors should be interpreted as potential contextual considerations rather than evidence-based determinants of KAP in this population. Furthermore, the rapid pace of urbanisation and occupational pressures, particularly among lower-education or migrant workers, often lead to irregular lifestyles and poor hydration habits, which can exacerbate the risk of urinary calculi. These contextual elements highlight the importance of culturally adapted education strategies that bridge cultural beliefs and evidence-based medical practices.

This study’s findings provide critical insights into the KAP of patients with urinary calculi, revealing a notable gap in understanding alongside moderately positive attitudes and practices. Gender disparities were evident, with male participants scoring significantly higher in knowledge compared with females, a pattern aligning with prior research highlighting gender differences in health literacy and healthcare information engagement.18 Age also emerged as a significant factor, with younger individuals exhibiting more favourable attitudes and practices towards urinary calculi management, reflecting research that identifies younger age groups as more receptive to health-related information and proactive in adopting preventive behaviours.19

Educational attainment consistently predicted both knowledge and practice, emphasising the role of education in promoting health literacy and fostering healthier behaviours among these patients. Moreover, results from multivariate logistic regression underscoring the multifaceted interplay of socioeconomic factors and healthcare access in shaping patient behaviour. Notably, the finding that patients with monthly incomes above 20 000 CNY were associated with lower knowledge scores appears counterintuitive. This observation may be partly attributable to the relatively small proportion of high-income participants in the study, which could have introduced statistical instability. Beyond sample size considerations, differences in health information-seeking behaviours and disease perceptions may also play a role. Higher-income individuals may rely more on previous clinical advice or self-perceived health status, and sociodemographic disparities have been shown to influence health literacy and health information-seeking behaviours, with income-related inequality affecting individuals’ access to and use of health information from healthcare professionals and other sources.20 In addition, greater work intensity and time constraints among higher-income groups may limit engagement in structured health education activities, potentially contributing to knowledge gaps despite better access to healthcare resources. This interpretation aligns with broader evidence that socioeconomic characteristics, including income and education, shape health literacy and information-seeking behaviour.21 Those with higher education levels showed greater knowledge and adherence to practices. Treatment modality also emerged as significant, with patients undergoing procedures like extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy exhibiting higher knowledge and more positive attitudes due to comprehensive preprocedural education, which may include information on urinary calculi, rationale for treatment and post-treatment care. However, in the case of attitude scores, patients treated with extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy showed unexpectedly lower scores, which may again reflect the small sample size and potential heterogeneity within this subgroup. Experiencing such procedures can prompt patients to follow healthier lifestyle practices and adhere to management strategies.22 23 For example, individuals who undergo extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy may become more motivated to follow dietary modifications or adhere to medication regimens to prevent recurrence of urinary calculi.24 25

Furthermore, correlation analysis and SEM provided additional insights into the interactions among knowledge, attitudes and practices. Positive correlations between knowledge and attitudes/practices, as well as between attitudes and practices, suggest a synergistic relationship wherein greater understanding contributes to more positive attitudes and, subsequently, to healthier practices. This finding reinforces the importance of comprehensive patient education strategies in promoting informed decision-making and empowering patients to actively participate in their care, consistent with the principles of patient-centred care and shared decision-making.26 27

Among the items assessing knowledge about urinary calculi, it was observed that while many participants demonstrated adequate knowledge about the types and consequences of urinary calculi, significant gaps were present in understanding symptoms, risk factors and preventive measures. Notably, awareness was particularly low regarding the absence of symptoms before an attack and the risk of recurrence post-treatment. To address these weaknesses, healthcare professionals should provide specific educational resources focusing on symptom recognition and preventive practices. This could include visual aids illustrating early symptoms, personalised risk factor assessments to guide lifestyle changes, and accessible brochures or videos demonstrating long-term management strategies. Information campaigns could target symptom awareness, while interactive workshops might emphasise practical measures to mitigate recurrence.28 29

Regarding attitudes, although the importance of dietary and lifestyle modifications was widely recognised, a significant number of participants expressed concerns about the lifestyle changes required for managing urinary calculi. Healthcare providers should adopt a patient-centred approach to address these concerns by offering emotional support and personalised advice. This could involve helping patients set manageable dietary goals, providing recipes and tips for gradual lifestyle changes and using motivational interviewing techniques to bolster confidence. Additionally, establishing a supportive network through group sessions or online communities could offer patients a platform to share their challenges and successes, thereby improving their adherence.

In terms of practice, we noted variable adherence to recommended behaviours such as dietary modifications and adequate hydration, with many participants showing suboptimal practices like irregular water intake and infrequent follow-ups. Particularly concerning was the high frequency of holding urine, a known risk factor for urinary calculi. Practical strategies may include personalised goal-setting, behaviour tracking tools and regular feedback from healthcare providers to reinforce positive habits and address barriers to adherence.30 31 Based on the identified knowledge gaps and behavioural deficiencies, targeted interventions should be implemented. Non-adherence to dietary recommendations and follow-up has long been recognised in stone disease management, and adherence to key preventive recommendations (eg, increasing fluid intake) remains low despite usual-care education and encouragement in routine practice.32 Recent advances highlight the potential role of artificial intelligence (AI)-based systems in providing personalised education, risk stratification and real-time behavioural feedback for patients with urolithiasis. AI-driven digital platforms may support individualised dietary guidance, hydration monitoring and follow-up reminders, thereby addressing long-term adherence challenges more effectively than traditional approaches. Importantly, the successful implementation of such technologies requires careful consideration of human factors, including usability, patient engagement and trust, to ensure meaningful integration into routine care.33 34 These may include distributing simplified dietary education brochures in both Mandarin and local dialects, offering community-based health talks focusing on calculi prevention, and using WeChat public accounts or mini-programmes for follow-up reminders and lifestyle tracking. Additionally, urology clinics could provide hydration tracking cards or urine colour charts to promote better compliance with daily water intake recommendations. Additionally, leveraging digital health technologies, such as mobile applications and wearable devices, could facilitate self-monitoring and promote ongoing engagement in urinary calculi management. By addressing the specific challenges identified in patients’ practices, healthcare providers can optimise outcomes and enhance the quality of care for individuals with urinary calculi.35 36

An important clinical reality is that many patients with urolithiasis remain lifelong ‘stone formers’ and may harbour residual stone fragments after treatment. Residual stones are common following different interventions and are associated with long-term risks of stone growth, recurrence and reintervention, highlighting the need for sustained patient education and follow-up, which is consistent with our findings of suboptimal knowledge and practices.37 Stone disease risk is also strongly influenced by dietary and lifestyle habits established during childhood and adolescence. Long-term exposure to unfavourable behaviours early in life may partly explain the current prevalence of urolithiasis observed in adulthood, further emphasising the importance of early and sustained preventive education across the lifespan.38

This study has several strengths, including the use of a well-structured and internally consistent KAP questionnaire and the application of SEM to explore complex relationships among knowledge, attitudes and practices. The inclusion of diverse participants from both urban and non-urban areas within Jiangsu Province enhances the representativeness of the findings. However, limitations must also be noted. The use of self-reported data may introduce social desirability or recall bias. In addition, the CFA indicated a borderline fit for the proposed questionnaire structure, which may have introduced measurement error and could affect the precision of the KAP scores and the estimated associations among KAP domains. The cross-sectional design precludes any causal inference. Moreover, the relatively small sample sizes within certain subgroups, such as high-income individuals or those receiving specific treatments, may have led to unstable estimates in multivariate analyses. Future longitudinal or multicentre studies would help validate and expand upon these results.

While this study provides valuable insights, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. First, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causality between variables, thus hindering definitive conclusions regarding observed temporal relationships. Second, reliance on self-reported data via questionnaires may introduce response bias, potentially affecting the accuracy and reliability of results. Third, the study’s sample was drawn from specific geographic locations, which may limit the generalisability of findings to broader populations with differing demographic characteristics or healthcare access. Additionally, this study used convenience sampling and was conducted in only two cities in Jiangsu Province, which may have introduced selection bias and substantially limited the representativeness and generalisability of the findings to other regions or populations. Furthermore, participants were recruited from both outpatient clinics and inpatient wards, which may have resulted in differential representation of disease severity and healthcare-seeking behaviours. Although both urban and non-urban residents were included, the study was not designed to ensure proportional representation across residential settings, and rural populations may therefore be under-represented. Future research using longitudinal designs, objective measures and diverse participant samples could address these limitations and offer a more comprehensive understanding of the KAP among patients with urinary calculi.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study suggests that patients with urinary calculi tend to have relatively limited knowledge but moderate attitudes and practices regarding their condition. These findings highlight potential gaps in patient understanding and indicate that educational approaches may play a role in supporting disease management, particularly among subgroups defined by educational attainment and income level. However, given the cross-sectional design and sampling characteristics, further studies with larger and more representative populations are needed to confirm these observations.
