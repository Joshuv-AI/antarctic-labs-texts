Out-of-pocket insulin spending before and after the Inflation Reduction Act across demographic subgroups
Authors: Caroline G Borden, Renhao Wang, Alexander O Everhart, Pinar Karaca-Mandic, Stacie B Dusetzina, Rozalina G McCoy, Kasia J Lipska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) capped out-of-pocket (OOP) insulin spending at $35 for Medicare Part D beneficiaries in 2023. Studies have shown lower per-prescription insulin spending after the IRA but less is known about annual OOP insulin spending. We examined whether beneficiaries' annual insulin spending fell below $420 (ie, $35/month) and whether savings differed across demographic subgroups.MethodsUsing nationally representative data from the Medical Expenditures Panel Survey, we estimated survey-weighted two-part models comparing annual per-person OOP insulin spending in 2022 and 2023 overall, and by sex, race and ethnicity, income, education, insurance type, and number and type of insulin prescriptions.ResultsAnnual per-person OOP spending on insulin decreased from $359.95 in 2022 to $214.11 in 2023 (P = 0.016). However, 13.2% of Part D beneficiaries spent more than $420 in 2023, and rates of cost-related medication nonadherence were unchanged. Reductions in spending were consistent across demographic subgroups.ConclusionThe IRA's universal cap on insulin for Medicare beneficiaries was associated with reductions in annual OOP spending across demographic subgroups, highlighting its potential to improve equitable access to insulin. However, persistent spending above the annualized cap for 1 in 7 patients suggests gaps in protection and the need for additional solutions.

Introduction

Out-of-pocket (OOP) spending on insulin has posed significant financial burdens for many Americans with diabetes,1 leading 1 in 6 to ration this lifesaving medication.2 Prior to 2023, the Medicare Senior Savings Model (SSM) capped insulin spending at $35 per month, but only for a subset of Medicare beneficiaries who voluntarily enrolled in participating Part D plans. The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) expanded access to $35 per month insulin through a monthly cap, mandatory for all Medicare Part D beneficiaries as of January 1, 2023.3 Recent evidence suggests significant decreases in OOP spending per 30-day insulin supply4 and total quarterly OOP spending on insulin5 among Part D enrollees with the SSM and later the IRA. However, existing estimates of the proportion of Part D enrollees continuing to pay over $35 per month following the IRA vary widely.4,5

A key feature of the IRA lies in its automatic inclusion of all Part D beneficiaries, ensuring that cost protections reach those with the greatest barriers to navigation, literacy, and administrative burden. In contrast, opt-in programs such as the SSM risk widening disparities, as uptake has been shown to vary by demographic characteristics including income, education level, and race.6 However, the potential differential savings across demographic groups following the IRA remain unexplored, as existing studies rely on pharmacy claims data with limited to no demographic information.4,5,7 To quantify changes in the overall burden of spending following the implementation of the IRA across demographic subgroups, we compared annual per-person OOP spending on insulin in the year before and after the IRA using nationally representative data.

Data and methods

We used the 2022 and 2023 Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS), a national survey conducted by the U.S. Department of Health & Human Services containing information reported by U.S. households and verified by pharmacy records, medical providers, and employers. The MEPS supplies weights to generate nationally representative estimates of healthcare expenditures8 to characterize national trends in the use of and expenditures on various classes of medications,9 including insulin.1 The Yale University Institutional Review Board determined that this study qualified under the exempt category.

We included respondents 65 years and older reporting Medicare Part D coverage and filling at least 1 insulin prescription in their respective survey year. Part D coverage was determined using a composite variable validated by Hill et al.10 that reduces under-reporting by accounting for the different ways that respondents may obtain Part D coverage, including self-reported Medicaid coverage in addition to Medicare, Medicare Prescription Medication Benefit, or Medicare Managed Care/Medicare Advantage coverage.

MEPS data do not include exact prescription fill dates but can be used to capture total annual OOP spending, a measure that reflects patients' overall financial burden. Accordingly, for each participant, we calculated total annual OOP spending on insulin, non-insulin diabetes medications, and all prescription medications. We also classified participants by their total annual OOP spending on insulin being at or below the IRA's annualized cap ($420, equivalent to twelve fills of $35) or above the annualized cap.

For each participant, we reported age, sex, self-identified race and ethnicity, family income, education level, and insurance coverage (Medicare with or without public insurance). We also reported the total number of prescription fills for insulin, for non-insulin diabetes medications, and for all medications filled in the survey year for each participant. Insulin prescriptions were identified by Multum Lexicon Medicine Name and cross-checked against the National Drug Code Directory maintained by the U.S. Food & Drug Administration.11 We report total days of insulin supplied for the subset of participants (68%) with data available for all insulin prescriptions in the survey year. Insulin prescriptions were further categorized as long-acting (degludec, detemir, glargine), rapid-acting (aspart, lispro), pre-mixed (insulin isophane-insulin regular), or unable to classify type (prescriptions reported as “insulin” without further detail) by comparing the medication name and NDC code to the CMS 2022 Part D SSM NDC List.12 Participants were categorized as having prescriptions for a single type of insulin (only long-acting, only rapid-acting, or only pre-mixed), multiple types (ie, 2 or more known types of insulin), or unable to classify type (ie, prescriptions of an unknown type, with or without additional prescriptions of a single known type). Prescriptions for non-insulin diabetes medications included all medications classified in the Multum Lexicon Therapeutic Sub-Class “Antidiabetic Agents,” excluding all insulin prescriptions identified above. We defined cost-related medication nonadherence as self-reported delaying getting a prescription medication due to cost or being unable to afford a prescription medication.

We used survey-weighted two-part models, with a logistic regression for the presence of spending and a generalized linear model with gamma family and log link for positive spending,13,14 to compare annual per-person OOP spending on insulin, on non-insulin diabetes medications, and on all prescription medications between 2022 and 2023. We ran additional two-part models to compare annual per-person OOP spending on insulin as a function of year, selected characteristics, and their interaction, running separate models for each of the following characteristics: sex, race and ethnicity, family income, education level, type of insurance coverage, type(s) of insulin prescriptions, and number of insulin prescriptions in a survey year. We compared the proportion of insulin users with OOP spending on insulin over the annualized cap between the 2022 and 2023 cohorts using a survey-weighted logistic regression model.

For all two-part models, unconditional mean OOP spending was derived as the product of the predicted probability of any spending and the predicted conditional mean among individuals with positive spending. Unconditional means were estimated separately for 2022 and 2023, and year-to-year changes (Δ2023-2022) were computed as differences between these unconditional means, overall and within a subgroup of interest. Standard errors and confidence intervals for unconditional means and year-to-year changes were obtained using the delta method based on the joint variance–covariance matrix from both parts of the model. Differences in year-to-year changes across subgroups were evaluated using joint Wald tests of subgroup-specific difference-in-differences contrasts. All analyses used weights supplied by MEPS to generate nationally representative estimates and were conducted in R Version 4.3.0 and STATA Version 19.

Discussion

In this nationally representative sample of Medicare Part D beneficiaries, the total annual burden of OOP spending on insulin declined by 40% following the IRA's $35 monthly insulin spending cap. In contrast to a recent study showing concurrent increases in spending on other diabetes agents,5 we found no change in spending on non-insulin diabetes medications, suggesting that savings on insulin were not offset by greater spending on alternative agents for glycemic control. Instead, Part D beneficiaries experienced a concurrent decrease in annual per-person OOP spending on all prescription medications during this time; this may potentially reflect changes in Part D benefit design, generic substitution, or differences in medication mix in the population sampled by the MEPS. Despite this decrease in OOP spending, cost-related prescription medication nonadherence was unchanged.

The reductions in OOP insulin spending following the IRA were consistent across demographic subgroups defined by sex, race and ethnicity, income, education, insurance type, and number and type of insulin prescriptions. Although the reduction in OOP spending on insulin was similar across insurance types, adults without other public insurance (such as Medicaid) spent approximately $300 more on insulin than those with other public insurance both before and after the IRA, highlighting the impact of programs aimed at healthcare affordability for low-income adults.

Of note, in the year following the IRA, nearly 1 in 7 Part D beneficiaries continued to spend over the annualized insulin cap. This can be partially explained by the structure of the IRA, which applies the $35 cap separately to each monthly insulin prescription. Thus, patients needing more than 1 insulin simultaneously (eg, both long-acting and short-acting) still face costs of $35 for each, increasing their overall expenditures. Even among those taking a single insulin, the packaging and dispensing of insulin may require patients to purchase more than 1 pre-packaged box of insulin pens to ensure their monthly insulin needs are met. In these cases, the IRA's $35 cap may be applied twice, rather than prorated, resulting in OOP costs that exceed $35 to cover a single monthly fill. Fang et al.4 who found that 25% of Part D beneficiaries still spent over $35 per 30-day supply of insulin in 2023, observed a similar phenomenon: nearly all participants spending over the cap had at least 1 insulin prescription for a day's supply that was not a multiple of 30 and was not appropriately prorated under the IRA. In contrast, Hong et al.5 found that less than 1% of their study population spent more than $35 for a 30-day supply of insulin in the fourth quarter of 2023; however, Hong's study excluded adults with type 1 diabetes, a population that is more likely to fill multiple types of insulin prescriptions and therefore to incur higher OOP costs. In addition, their methods applied the $35 cap to all prescriptions of a 1–30 day supply.

Notable strengths of this study include the nationally representative data and inclusion of demographic information, which is directly reported at the individual level, rather than inferred or imputed. However, the MEPS dataset, while rich in demographic covariates, offered a small sample size, limiting the power of our analyses and leading to unstable OOP spending estimates for a small subset of demographic subgroups. Other limitations of this study include the lack of prescription fill dates and incomplete data on days of insulin supplied, which precludes assessment of monthly spending patterns and confirmation that respondents filled insulin prescriptions throughout the entire survey year. While MEPS relies on self-reported data and has been shown to underestimate OOP spending,15 the risk of recall or misclassification bias is mitigated by verification of self-reported data with pharmacy records.

Our findings should be interpreted descriptively rather than as isolating the effect of a single policy. Because the IRA's universal monthly insulin cap followed the Part D SSM, some beneficiaries may already have had access to capped insulin costs before the IRA's implementation. We therefore could not disentangle the incremental effect of the IRA's cap from the earlier opt-in model.

Conclusion

The total burden of OOP spending on insulin among Part D enrollers declined following the IRA's insulin spending cap, which strengthens the case for future policies capping spending in the non-Medicare population. Similar reductions were seen across subgroups defined by sex, race and ethnicity, education, income, insurance type, and number and type of insulin prescriptions, highlighting the reach of this universally applied policy as compared to the opt-in SSM that preceded the IRA. Still, nearly 1 in 7 Part D beneficiaries continued to spend over the annualized cap, and rates of cost-related medication nonadherence remained unchanged, highlighting the need for additional policies to improve prescription drug affordability.
