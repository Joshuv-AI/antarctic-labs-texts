A qualitative review of Fitzpatrick skin types featured on the front covers of the British Association of Dermatologists journals
Authors: Brian Nolan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The British Association of Dermatology (BAD) has gone to notable efforts to ensure adequate representation of darker skin tones within its journal. The Association has frequently highlighted the lack of awareness of presentations of hair, skin and nail pathologies in minority groups, and the contents of its journals – British Journal of Dermatology, Clinical and Experimental Dermatology and Skin Health and Disease – are representative of the significant work it has done to enhance physician awareness of darker skin tone pathologies. We explored the role of a front page of a medical journal. Our qualitative analysis of journal covers suggests front cover representation to be an ample opportunity for the BAD to continue to promote darker skin tone pathology awareness.

The British Association of Dermatologists (BAD) publishes three widely read and cited dermatology medical journals: British Journal of Dermatology (BJD), Clinical and Experimental Dermatology (CED) and Skin Health and Disease (SHD). It has been well established that skin, hair and nail pathologies in people with skin of colour can be under-recognized by physicians due to a lack of knowledge regarding varying presentations.1 The BAD has highlighted the importance of integrating minority ethnic group skin types into all BAD workstreams as a matter of priority. Through the development of inclusive initiatives such as the Skin Diversity Sub-Committee, the BAD has provided educational resources easily accessible via their website for healthcare providers to improve knowledge of skin presentations among minority ethnic group skin types.2 The Editors-in-Chief of the BAD journals have made notable efforts to ensure the journals’ contents feature minority ethnic group skin types and have encouraged Black, Asian and ethnic minority group dermatology submissions to combat disproportionate physician knowledge.

The front page of a medical journal is the most widely seen component of a publication. Papers featured on a journal’s cover have previously been shown to gain more citations and Altmetric scores than non-cover-featured papers.3 Our study endeavoured to review the representation of minority ethnic group skin types on the front covers of the BAD journals. This study acknowledges and supports the significant efforts the BAD has taken to have adequate representation within its journal contents, and aim to support the work of the BAD. Through this study, the aim is to assess the potential for front cover illustrations to be a further novel opportunity to raise awareness of under-recognized pathologies.

A qualitative analysis was conducted on the published covers of BJD, CED and SHD journals over 5 years (January 2020 to November 2024) archived on the BAD website. Covers with patient images were categorized based on Fitzpatrick skin phototype (I–VI). Results were ­cross-tabulated to identify the proportion of representation of patients with Fitzpatrick skin phototypes V and VI.

A total of 104 published journal front covers over 5 years were reviewed; during this period the BJD had 59 covers, CED 51 covers and SHD 4 covers. Some 57% (n = 59) of journals featured human participants on the cover, including 34% (n = 20) of BJD covers, 71% (n = 36) of CED covers and 75% (n = 3) of SHD covers (Table S1; see Supporting Information). One in three SHD front cover illustrations featuring human participants published to date represented minority skin types, demonstrating the effort being made by BAD journals to represent minority ethnic group skin types. Covers with human participants were reviewed and categorized based on the Fitzpatrick skin phototype of the participant, the results were as follows: I: 31% (n = 18); II: 41% (n = 24); III: 14% (n = 8); IV: 3% (n = 2); V: 5% (n = 3); VI: 7% (n = 4) (Figure S1; see Supporting Information). Sixty-nine per cent of patients featured on the front cover had Fitzpatrick skin phototype I or II vs. 12% of cover participants with Fitzpatrick skin phototype V/VI. The Fitzpatrick skin phototype scale was selected due to its practicality for our study design and its widespread use in clinical dermatology.

A limitation of this study was the inability to account for participants’ exposure to ultraviolet. It must also be acknowledged that front cover illustrations are often selected from a journal’s submitted manuscripts and thus decisions regarding front covers often do not lie only in skin phototype, but also the calibre of each manuscript submitted and the population studied.

Front cover illustrations of skin conditions offer a novel opportunity to raise awareness and highlight to dermatologists and general practitioners the presentations in darker skin tones. According to the 2021 UK census, the total population of England and Wales was 59.6 million, with 81.7% of residents identifying as White. People from Asian ethnic groups constituted 9.3% of the population, followed by Black (4.0%), Mixed (2.9%) and Other (2.1%) ethnic groups.4 While these ethnicity categories do not directly correspond to Fitzpatrick skin phototypes, they provide a useful proxy for understanding population diversity. These data demonstrate that the population is becoming increasingly ethnically diverse, and it is imperative for dermatologists to recognize this evolving demographic landscape. This diversity should be accurately and consistently reflected in the medical literature to ensure inclusive education and improved clinical care. Given that approximately 18% of the population of the UK belongs to ethnic groups more likely to have Fitzpatrick skin phototypes V and VI, the 12% representation of these skin types on journal covers may indicate a missed opportunity to better reflect the UK’s ethnic diversity for educational purposes.

This study acknowledges the significant strides taken by the BAD towards diversity and inclusion in its publications, resources and clinical imagery to date. This effort for inclusion is also reflected in one of three SHD covers to date with human participants featuring a patient with a darker skin tone. Front covers should be considered a potential opportunity to effectively augment physician awareness and tackle disproportionate knowledge of minority ethnic group presentations. The implementation of such measures could enhance the noteworthy work that the BAD journals are carrying out in enhancing disproportionate physician awareness of pathologies among people with darker skin tones.
