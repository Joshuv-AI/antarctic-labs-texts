The role of 5-hydroxytryptamine receptor 2A (HTR2A) gene polymorphisms in treatment-resistant obsessive–compulsive disorder: a comparative study with other treatment-resistant mental disorders
Authors: Antonio Del Casale, Giovanna Gentile, Jan Francesco Arena, Martina Nicole Modesti, Clarissa Zocchi, Serena Mancino, Ottavia De Luca, Gloria Angeletti, Stefano Ferracuti, Robert Preissner, Maurizio Pompili, Maurizio Simmaco, Marina Borro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigates the role of genetic variations in the 5-Hydroxytryptamine Receptor 2A (HTR2A) gene in subjects with treatment-resistant obsessive–compulsive disorder (TR-OCD), compared to individuals with other treatment-resistant mental disorders (TRMDs). The goal is to explore whether specific HTR2A polymorphisms contribute to distinguishing TR-OCD from other TRMDs, thereby advancing our understanding of the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted with 210 individuals affected by TRMDs (72 with major depressive disorder, 62 with bipolar disorder, 37 with schizophrenia, 30 with OCD, and 9 with other diagnoses). Genetic analyses focused on three HTR2A single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (rs6314, rs7997012, and rs6311), using next-generation sequencing from blood samples. Chi-square testing and single- and multiple-SNP analyses were employed to study the association between these SNPs and the TR-OCD diagnosis.ResultsThe analysis revealed that the HTR2A rs7997012 A|A vs. G|G genotype was significantly associated with a higher likelihood of belonging to the TR-OCD group compared to other TRMDs, with an odds ratio of 6.85 (95% CI = 2.44–19.26; p < 0.001). The same genotype, compared to the combined G|G and A|G genotypes, showed a significant association with TR-OCD (OR = 7.68; 95% CI = 2.90–20.34; p < 0.001). Haplotype analyses demonstrated a significant global association between the combined rs6314-rs7997012-rs6311 variants and TR-OCD (p = 0.028). Specifically, the G-G-C haplotype was significantly associated with a reduced likelihood of TR-OCD (OR = 0.32; 95% CI = 0.11–0.93; p = 0.038) compared to the reference haplotype G-G-T.ConclusionsThis preliminary study identifies the rs7997012 polymorphism in the HTR2A gene as a potential genetic marker for TR-OCD, distinguishing it from other TRMDs. The study of rs7997012 and other genetic variants within the serotonergic system may enhance our understandings of the biological mechanisms underlying OCD and contribute to the development of precision psychiatry approaches.Keywords: Obsessive–compulsive disorder, Depressive disorder, Treatment-resistant, Schizophrenia, Treatment-resistant, Mental disorders, Genomics, Serotonin 5-HT2 receptors, Precision medicine

Introduction

Obsessive–Compulsive Disorder (OCD) is a mental disorder classified under the Obsessive–Compulsive Spectrum Disorders in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR). Its core symptoms include intrusive thoughts (obsessions) and ritualistic behaviors (compulsions) [1]. This disorder has a worldwide prevalence higher than 1% among the population [2, 3], and a lifetime prevalence of 2–3% with no differences in gender distribution [4].

Ample evidence supports that the most effective OCD treatment is a combined approach with selective serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and cognitive-behavioral therapies. In fact, in clinical practice, most patients obtain an improvement of symptoms with these treatments, alone or in combination [5, 6]. A major clinical issue, however, is that even when appropriate treatments are followed, about half of OCD patients show disabling persistent symptoms, which indicates the need for innovative treatments and further research to better comprehend the neuropathophysiological correlates, especially in treatment-resistant conditions [5, 6].

Studies concentrating on candidate genes and genome-wide associations (GWAS) demonstrate that various genetic susceptibilities may be associated with specific OCD subtypes, varying based on factors such as sex, onset age, family history, comorbidities with other mental disorders, and symptom clustering [5].

Dysregulation of the serotonergic system has been identified as a contributing factor to the neuropathophysiology of OCD. Existing literature indicates that the serotonin receptor 2 A gene (HTR2A) is a principal candidate in this context. Located on chromosome 13q14-21, the HTR2A gene encodes a G-protein coupled receptor, encompassing approximately 66 kb and featuring 7 exons, with some of the latter being recently described [7]. This gene is characterized by two alternative promoters, a silencer element, and four sites for transcription initiation [8, 9].

Different HTR2A polymorphisms were studied in OCD patients, including rs6313 (T102C), rs6311 (G-1438A), and rs6305 (C516T). The T102C variant is a synonymous or silent single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) identified within the initial exon, while the G-1438A variant is situated upstream of the promoter region of HTR2A. The functional implications of these polymorphisms remain uncertain [10, 11]. A meta-analysis of 18 studies using case–control or family-based designs underlined that the HTR2A rs6311 A allele and/or the HTR2A rs6313 T allele were associated with the OCD diagnosis. This finding provides support for the participation of genes encoding proteins crucial in serotonergic neural transmission in the neuropathophysiology of OCD, suggesting that dysfunction of the 5-HT2A receptor may contribute to the development of this disorder [12].

There is a lack of studies in literature focused on other HTR2A SNPs that may be involved in the neuropathophysiology of OCD. For example, to our knowledge, the rs6314 (C1354T or His452Tyr/H452Y) and rs7997012 were never studied in OCD, although the first has been involved in signaling conformation [13], and the second was involved in 5-HTT binding in the thalamus and insula [14], although with inconsistent evidence [15]. In general, rs6314, rs6311, and rs7997012 may influence risk for psychiatric disorders, mediate response to drug treatments and underly treatment resistance [15, 16].

Although controlled trials have demonstrated the selective efficacy of serotonergic agents in the treatment of OCD, approximately 40–60% of patients fail to achieve a satisfactory clinical response [17]. Treatment-resistant OCD (TR-OCD) has been defined as a less than 25% reduction in Y-BOCS scores following at least 12 weeks of treatment with SSRIs or clomipramine at the highest tolerated dose, combined with a minimum of 30 h of cognitive-behavioral therapy [18, 19]. Refractory OCD is characterized by non-response after 3 to 6 months of treatment with at least three antidepressants (including clomipramine) and two augmentation trials with atypical antipsychotics, although universally accepted criteria for these classifications are lacking [18, 19]. Increasing attention has been directed toward the concept of “staging of response,” which proposes a structured framework to define chronological treatment milestones and guide clinical decisions regarding dose escalation, switching between serotonin reuptake inhibitors, medication class changes, or augmentation strategies [17, 20].

Given the substantial clinical burden posed by TRMDs [21, 22], and the lack of clear biological markers distinguishing TR-OCD from other disorders, we specifically selected a treatment-resistant patient group to investigate whether specific HTR2A gene polymorphisms are associated with treatment resistance and whether they may contribute to differentiating TR-OCD from other TRMDs.

In this retrospective study, we analyzed data available from a previously tested pharmacogenomic panel, including the rs6314, rs6311, and rs7997012 polymorphisms in the HTR2A gene, hypothesizing that their genotype frequencies might be different between patients with TR-OCD and other TRMDs.

We hypothesized that specific HTR2A gene polymorphisms are involved in differentiating TR-OCD from other TRMDs, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of the genetic architecture underlying OCD. This research aimed to shed light on whether these specific genetic markers could serve as valuable indicators or potential contributors to the development and differentiation of OCD and other mental disorders, ultimately contributing to our understanding of the underlying genetic factors involved in these conditions.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective observational study at the Centre of Precision Medicine, Sant'Andrea University Hospital, Sapienza University of Rome, covering the years 2017 to 2023. The study adhered to the Principles of Human Rights established by the World Medical Association (WMA) at the 18th WMA General Assembly in Helsinki, Finland, in June 1964, revised at the 64th WMA General Assembly in Fortaleza, Brazil, in October 2013. The local ethical committee approved this study (protocol N. 6279/2021), and all participants provided written consent after receiving detailed information about the nature and objectives of the study.

The inclusion criterion involved the manifestation of a treatment-resistant condition during a mental disorder. We defined treatment-resistant depression as an illness in which patients with major depressive and bipolar disorders experienced failure in two or more antidepressant trials (adequate in doses and duration) [23]. We established the definition of treatment-resistant schizophrenia based on the lack of efficacy observed in two or more antipsychotic trials, ensuring adequacy in both doses and duration [24]. We established the definition of other treatment-resistant conditions using similar criteria, specifically, the non-response observed in two or more psychotropic drug trials for OCD, autism spectrum disorders, attention deficit and hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and insomnia disorder. All diagnoses were made in accordance with the DSM-5-TR criteria [1].

Exclusion criteria encompassed individuals below the age of 18, patients with concurrent substance use disorders (excluding nicotine dependence), patients with neurological illnesses such as major neurocognitive disorders, Parkinson’s disease, and Huntington’s chorea, as well as those with severe acute organic conditions including major cardiovascular pathologies, uncontrolled diabetes, severe toxic, infectious and metabolic disorders, malignancy, liver failure, and renal failure, as well as patients who had not received pharmacological treatments at adequate dosages and durations according to established clinical guidelines.

Genetic analyses were conducted following the manufacturer's guidelines. DNA extraction from 200 μL of EDTA-anticoagulated peripheral blood samples was performed using an automated nucleic acid extraction system (Qiasymphony, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). A pharmacogenomic panel encompassing genomic variants, namely rs6314, rs7997012, and rs6311 (HTR2A gene), was subjected to analysis through Next-Generation Sequencing utilizing the IonS5 platform (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The Ion AmpliSeq™ Library Kit 2.0 reaction chemistry was employed per the supplier's instructions (ThermoFisher Scientific). In summary, DNA underwent amplification with a set of oligonucleotide primer pairs designed for specific genomic regions. Subsequently, barcoded DNA libraries were prepared, and clonal amplification, as well as pooled library sequencing, were executed on the IonChef/IonS5 system, employing the Ion 510™, Ion 520™, and Ion 530™ Kit–Chef (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Statistical analysis

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis to assess co-inheritance of the three analyzed SNPs with other known functional polymorphisms in the HTR2A gene, listed in the PharmGKB knowledgebase (a curated database reporting clinically actionable gene–drug and genotype–phenotype associations available at https://www.pharmgkb.org/). As shown in Fig. 1, rs6311—one of the SNPs included in this study—is in high LD with rs6313 and rs1928040.

We conducted a power analysis with the software G*Power [25, 26], considering effect size w = 0.04, power = 80%, and α = 0.05 for goodness-of-fit tests (contingency tables) analysis on the three considered polymorphisms, which indicated a total sample size of 75 participants for a comparison between two groups (λ = 12; critical χ2 = 9.488; actual power = 0.802). Descriptive analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28 [27], while Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) analysis, single- and multiple-SNP analyses were conducted with the SNPStats software (https://www.snpstats.net/). HWE reflects the expected distribution of genotype frequencies in a population unaffected by evolutionary pressures. Testing for HWE helps validate the reliability of genetic data and rule out biases such as genotyping errors or population stratification.

Since that age and illness year variables did not follow a normal distribution, we employed the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. The Pearson's chi-square test was used for categorical variables in our descriptive analyses.

Genotype and allele frequency comparisons between the groups (TR-OCD vs. TRMDs) were conducted using the Pearson's chi-square test. We set the cut-off for statistical significance at (two-tailed) p < 0.05.

Results

The sample comprised 210 Caucasian, Italian subjects (111 females and 99 males; mean age = 43.27 years, SD = 17.09) affected by TRMDs, of which 72 with major depressive disorder (mean age = 51.54 years; SD = 15.91), 62 with bipolar disorder (mean age = 45.03 years SD = 15.04), 37 with schizophrenia (mean age = 35.48 years; SD = 16.18), 30 with OCD (mean age = 35.97 years; SD = 14.82), 3 with autism spectrum disorder (mean age = 22 years; SD = 6.93), 2 with ADHD (mean age = 21.5 years; SD = 4.95) and 4 with Tourette's disorder (mean age = 20.75 years; SD = 4.86).

The diagnostic groups differed significantly by age (W = 1945.5; p = 0.014).

The single-SNP analyses showed that the rs7997012 A|A vs. G|G genotype was significantly associated with TR-OCD vs. other TRMDs (OR = 6.85; 95% CI = 2.44—19.26; p < 0.001). The log-additive model further supported a frequency-dependent effect of the A allele (OR = 2.21; 95% CI = 1.27–3.84; p = 0.005). The results are summarized in Table 4. No significant associations were observed for the other polymorphisms analyzed.

Discussion

This preliminary study is the first to demonstrate that the HTR2A rs7997012 SNP significantly contributes to the differential diagnosis of TR-OCD and may play a role in the neurophysiopathology of OCD. Specifically, A|A homozygosity was more frequently associated with this diagnosis than other TRMDs.

The findings of this study raise two key questions: whether the rs7997012 polymorphism is generally associated with TR-OCD as compared to other TRMDs, and whether its association is specifically linked to the pathophysiology of OCD.

Focusing on the first issue, the rs7997012 polymorphism has been broadly associated with treatment refractoriness across various mental disorders, with the A|A genotype consistently linked to poorer response to serotonergic agents and greater treatment resistance [28–30]. Other research has shown a significant association between the rs7997012 G allele and more favorable treatment outcomes across various mental disorders. In children and adolescent patients affected by major depressive disorder (MDD), G|G homozygotes exhibited the highest rates of remission, substantial symptom reduction, and better overall clinical improvement [31]. Moreover, the presence of the G allele has been linked to enhanced response to antidepressants, especially those that are not serotonergic or mixed antidepressants [29, 30]. Although some studies have reported weaker associations between the G allele and antidepressant response [32–34], other evidence indicates that G|G homozygous patients with depression exhibit better social adjustment in work and family domains [35]. Additionally, G|G carriers have been shown to display lower treatment resistance to second-generation antipsychotics compared to A|G patients with TRMDs [16]. The presence of the same allele correlated with a better treatment response to venlafaxine over time also in patients with generalized anxiety disorder [36, 37]. Taken together, these findings suggest a potential protective role of the G allele in reducing treatment refractoriness across different mental disorders, with our results indicating that this protective effect may be more pronounced in TR-OCD compared to other TRMDs. The protective effect of the rs7997012 G allele may be more pronounced in TR-OCD, which is potentially consistent with the central role of serotonergic agents such as SSRIs and clomipramine in its treatment. In contrast, other TRMDs involve more heterogeneous pharmacological approaches, which may dilute genotype-specific effects.

Focusing on the second question—the involvement of rs7997012 in OCD pathophysiology—our findings suggest that this SNP could be involved in broader serotonergic dysfunction underlying the disorder. Previous evidence linking rs7997012 to serotonergic drug response [28–30] further supports its relevance in OCD pathophysiology and highlights the need for future studies including larger, prospective samples, including treatment-responsive OCD cohorts and broader genetic and environmental assessments to clarify its specific role.

Although no studies to date have demonstrated a direct association between the rs7997012 genotype and OCD diagnosis, our finding of its involvement in the differential diagnosis of OCD may reflect variations that affect 5-HT neurotransmission, potentially contributing to symptom expression. A PET study indicated that rs7997012 is associated with 5-HTT binding potential in the thalamus and insula, suggesting that this SNP can be involved in 5-HTT expression, trafficking, and binding [38]. Such variations may be related with differences in synaptic 5-HT availability, potentially affecting SSRI efficacy and tolerability. Thalamic dysfunction is one of the most consistent findings in OCD neuropathophysiology and is closely linked to serotonergic dysregulation. A functional neuroimaging study has shown significant genotype-diagnosis interaction involving serotonergic polymorphisms, particularly in the orbitofrontal cortex and thalamus of OCD patients [39]. Additionally, increased thalamic phospholipid concentrations and high-energy phosphate metabolism have been reported in OCD patients, pointing to impaired serotonin metabolism [40]. Further supporting this, 5-HTT availability in the thalamus and midbrain has been associated with OCD severity, particularly in drug-free patients [41], and serotonergic transporter reduction in the thalamus-hypothalamus correlates with clinical improvement following clomipramine treatment [42]. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential role of rs7997012 in modulating thalamic serotonergic dysfunction and contributing to OCD pathophysiology.

The results of this study may also suggest the presence of distinct neurobiological subgroups within TR-OCD, wherein the rs7997012 A|A genotype is associated with reduced SSRI responsiveness, potentially reflecting non-serotonergic mechanisms, while the G|G genotype may correspond to a serotonergic-driven pathology more responsive to SSRI treatment. Further research is needed to clarify these interpretations. Furthermore, the presence of the A allele appears to increase the likelihood of belonging to the TR-OCD group, suggesting its potential utility as a biomarker for differential diagnosis.

In our study, we did not observe a significant direct association between the rs6311 polymorphism and TR-OCD compared to other TRMDs. However, we found that rs6311 contributes to differential diagnosis within the context of the rs6314–rs7997012–rs6311 G-G-C haplotype, which was associated with a significantly reduced risk of TR-OCD. This result is partially consistent with previous studies reporting an association between rs6311 and OCD diagnosis in general populations [43, 44]. One possible interpretation is that rs6311 may influence overall susceptibility to OCD, with a potential role in distinguishing OCD from other TRMDs, and that its effect may be modulated by interactions with other genetic factors. In this context, the involvement of rs6311 may also reflect its strong linkage disequilibrium with rs6313 and rs1928040, which are implicated in serotonergic modulation, OCD pathophysiology [43], and depressive symptoms [45]—aspects that warrants further investigation in future studies, supporting the notion that haplotype structures may better capture the polygenic modulation of serotonergic mechanisms than single SNP analyses.

Separately, several studies have investigated the involvement of rs7997012 in suicidal behavior. Carriers of the A allele with a history of child abuse displayed a significantly higher rate of suicide attempts compared to G|G carriers with similar histories [46]. Additional research supports the potential involvement of this SNP in suicidal behavior among subjects with histories of sexual and physical abuse [47], though evidence remains inconsistent [48]. Further studies are needed to clarify whether rs7997012 may also play a role in the comorbidity of suicidality and TR-OCD.

In conclusion, the study of rs7997012 and other genetic variants within the serotonergic system may enhance our understandings of the biological mechanisms underlying OCD and contribute to the development of precision psychiatry approaches.

Although preliminary, this study offers several strengths. It features a well-characterized clinical sample, with diagnoses based on standardized DSM-5 criteria and clearly defined treatment-resistant conditions, ensuring the reliability of group classifications. The use of next-generation sequencing provides high accuracy in genotyping, and adherence to guideline-recommended dosages and treatment durations minimizes variability in prior treatment exposure. Additionally, the focus on previously understudied HTR2A polymorphisms adds novelty to the research. Nonetheless, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design and relatively small sample size of the OCD subgroup may limit generalizability. The absence of a treatment-responsive OCD group prevents determination of whether the identified associations are specific to treatment resistance. Furthermore, other unmeasured environmental or genetic factors may have influenced TR-OCD group membership. Among other potential limitations, the inclusion of diverse diagnoses within the TRMDs group may introduce heterogeneity, possibly obscuring disorder-specific genetic effects. However, the primary objective of our study was to differentiate TR-OCD from other TRMDs regardless of diagnosis. Furthermore, the younger age of OCD subjects compared to the TRMDs group could influence genetic associations through age-related factors. Nonetheless, this age difference may indicate an earlier onset of treatment resistance in OCD, highlighting the need for further research to investigate this aspect and inform potential preventive strategies.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates differences in HTR2A rs7997012 genotype frequencies among patients with TR-OCD and other TRMDs. The primary finding indicates that A|A homozygosity was more frequently associated with a diagnosis of TR-OCD than with other TRMDs. Further investigations are warranted to explore the role of the HTR2A rs7997012 and other genes encoding for proteins involved in the serotonergic system. This research could contribute to a deeper understanding of OCD pathophysiology, paving the way for developing improved tailored treatments and clinical applications.
