Integrating computational simulation and high-throughput screening for the development of robust ultra-high concentration formulation of IgG1 antibody
Authors: Chen Lyu, Shuyan Lu, Yiqiu Yin, Hongling Li, Dahui Xing, Yanjie Lu, Shujing Zheng, Qiuyu Zhang, Lingyu Gao, Shuxian Xu, Qian Zou, Feng Liu, Jinliang Zhang, Yun Kang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigh-viscosity monoclonal antibody (mAb) formulations impede injection, complicate manufacturing, and may affect drug stability and patient outcomes. Nevertheless, these issues can be effectively mitigated through the judicious selection of appropriate excipients and meticulous formulation optimization. In this study, we present an innovative approach that integrates computational simulation with high-throughput screening to enhance the efficiency of high-concentration mAb formulation development. Specifically, we introduce in silico modeling to predict mAb developability based on structural characteristics and reveal moderate risks in stability, solubility, viscosity, as well as hotspots in the complementarity-determining regions. Furthermore, by predicting protein–protein interactions and protein–excipient interactions and leveraging UNCLE, high-throughput protein stability analyzer, we successfully selected appropriate viscosity reducers, stabilizers, and buffer systems for drug formulation. The formulation confirmation studies demonstrated that the optimal formulation exhibited outstanding stability under various conditions, including high-temperature, accelerated conditions, long-term storage, light exposure, photosensitivity, oscillation, and freeze–thaw cycles, which can ensure the storage and transportation of the product. This approach not only addresses the current challenges associated with high-viscosity mAb formulations, but also offers a framework for future formulation optimization efforts in the biopharmaceutical industry.

Introduction

The rational development of protein therapeutics, particularly monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), often necessitates highly concentrated formulations to achieve therapeutic efficacy, yet these formulations are prone to self-association and elevated viscosity due to deleterious protein–protein interactions (PPIs), which compromise stability, bioavailability, and manufacturability.1,2 While excipients are routinely incorporated to mitigate these issues by disrupting nonspecific PPIs and enhancing protein solubility and stability, their selection remains predominantly empirical and trial-and-error-driven, lacking a mechanistic understanding of excipient–protein interactions at the molecular level.3 This knowledge gap hinders the ability to predictably formulate proteins with optimal buffer systems, viscosity profiles, and minimal aggregation, a critical bottleneck in protein-based drug delivery.

Computational modeling has recently emerged as a transformative approach to address the challenges of developing suitable protein formulations.4–6 In silico studies predict protein druggability, serving as a risk assessment and mitigation strategy. Mechanistically, in silico analysis uses molecular informatics to forecast various properties of candidate molecules, including their physicochemical attributes, structural characteristics, aggregation tendencies, potential post-translational modifications, stability profiles, and immunogenicity. By leveraging theoretical computational results, in silico analysis can efficiently rank, screen, and directionally optimize candidate drug molecules.7,8 To mitigate these in silico-predicted risks, appropriate excipients can be added to the formulation. Furthermore, when selecting different excipients within the same category, SILCS (Site Identification by Ligand Competitive Saturation), a computational approach, has proven valuable for mapping ligand-binding hotspots and residues susceptible to PPI on protein surfaces. This is achieved via extensive molecular dynamics simulations that generate functional group interaction maps (FragMaps), which encode the energetic propensity of hydrogen-bond donors/acceptors, aliphatic, and aromatic groups to interact with the protein while accounting for desolvation penalties and conformational flexibility.9 Building on this foundation, SILCS-Biologics extends the SILCS framework to protein formulations by modeling the 3D structure and dynamics of proteins, excipients, and solvents as a unified system, enabling two key analyses: (1) SILCS-PPI, which uses FragMaps for antibody–antibody PPI docking to identify self-association hotspots and guide excipient design to disrupt deleterious PPIs, and (2) SILCS-Hotspots, which performs global excipient docking to predict excipients that stabilize the protein via favorable interactions (e.g., hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic packing).10 However, in vitro validation of these predictions demands high-throughput, multi-parameter screening platforms capable of correlating computational predictions with biophysical stability metrics (e.g., thermal unfolding, colloidal stability, and aggregation propensity).

A critical enabler of this integration is UNCLE, a high-throughput protein stability analyzer that synergizes dynamic light scattering (DLS), static light scattering (SLS), full-wavelength fluorescence (FLD), and differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) to assess protein conformational stability (Tm), colloidal stability (Tagg), intermolecular interaction (G22), and polydispersity (PDI) across diverse buffer conditions with unprecedented efficiency.11 By leveraging its ultra-high-throughput capabilities (simultaneous analysis of 48 samples within 2 hours, using ≤9 μL per assay) and microscale precision, UNCLE allows researchers to screen several excipient-buffer combinations in a fraction of the time and sample volume required by conventional approaches.12 For example, UNCLE’s high-throughput platform rapidly identifies optimal excipient conditions (e.g., polysorbate 80, trehalose, arginine) for antibody–drug conjugate/mAb thermal stability via Tm/Tagg measurements13 and optimizes pH (e.g., pH 5.0 for monomeric lysozyme) to prevent aggregation.14 This strategy notably enhances both the stability of the drugs and the efficiency of the experimental workflow, consequently boosting drug safety and therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, by integrating SILCS-Biologics-predicted protein–excipient interaction with UNCLE-derived experimental metrics, researchers can now rapidly identify optimal excipient-buffer combinations that minimize aggregation and enhance long-term stability.

In this study, we present a computational-guided, high-throughput screening-demonstrated strategy that elucidates excipient–protein interaction mechanisms at atomic resolution while establishing a predictive-experimental workflow (Figure S1) to accelerate therapeutic protein formulation development.

Materials and methods

Computational modeling

In silico modeling

Drug developability was assessed via in silico modeling, a computational platform that integrates physicochemical, structural, and immunological parameters. TAP (Therapeutic Antibody Profiler) scores were used for rapid antibody scoring based on variable-region structure, including complementarity-determining region (CDR) hydrophobicity, charge distributions, and heavy-light chain charge imbalance. The CamSol algorithm predicted protein solubility scores considering amino acid physicochemical properties. Viscosity was predicted using the formula: η, cP (180 mg/ml, 25°C) = 10^[0.15 + 1.26(0.60)*ϕ − 0.043(0.047)*q − 0.020(0.015)*qsym] (ϕ: Fv hydrophobicity index, q: Fv charge, qsym: VH-VL charge asymmetry).

SILCS-Biologics analysis

SILCS-Biologics for mAbs starts with the Fab crystal structure (or homology model). It then performs SILCS simulations to generate FragMaps, which are created by binning solute atoms into grid voxels to calculate grid-based free energy, along with an exclusion map. Subsequently, SILCS-Biologics conducts PPI preference (PPIP) analysis for Fab–Fab self-interactions. For excipient binding site identification, it employs SILCS-Hotspots, sampling ligand conformations within FragMaps, evaluating their energy using Ligand Grid Free Energy (LGFE), and utilizing the exclusion map to prevent sampling of the protein interior. Finally, SILCS-Biologics combines PPI preference and excipient hotspot maps to pinpoint overlapping regions.

Solution property measurement

The stability of the protein solutions under different buffer systems was evaluated using a high-throughput protein stability analysis and formulation development system (UNCLE) (Unchained Labs, USA). The protein concentration of the buffer-exchanged solutions was measured using a UV spectrophotometer (Unchained Labs, USA) and adjusted to 1 mg/ml by respective corresponding buffers. Parameters including melting temperature (Tm), aggregation temperature (Tagg), polydispersity index (PDI), and average particle size (Z-Ave. Dia., nm) were measured according to the instructions from UNCLE. Then, the various buffer systems were concentrated to 100 mg/ml, and G22 detection – a method for evaluating intermolecular forces – was performed using the UNCLE system according to the protocol.

Oxidation and deamination measurement

To measure oxidative deamination, each 200 μg sample was added to a 1.5 ml tube, then diluted to 1 mg/ml with 8 mol/L Guanidine HCl − 200 mM histidine buffer (pH 6.0). After mixing with 2 μl 1 mol/L dithiothreitol (final 20 mmol/L), samples were incubated at 56°C for 1 h. After buffer-exchange with 20 mM histidine-HCl buffer (pH 6.0) by centrifuging at 12,000 rpm for around 10 min, which was repeated till the final concentration to 1 mg/ml, 4 μl of 0.5 mg/ml trypsin (protein: enzyme = 100:1) was added and samples were incubated at 37°C overnight. The reaction was terminated with 6 μl of 20% trifluoroacetic acid (final 0.6%), centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min, and injected supernatant for detection. The supernatant was separated on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC Peptide BEH C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm, 300 Å) (Waters, USA) at 40°C. A gradient of 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (mobile phase B) was used at 0.25 ml/min in a volume of 30 μl. Samples were then analyzed by Triple-TOF 6600 mass spectrometry (AB Sciex, USA) in positive ion mode for 80 min. Raw data were deconvoluted with BiopharmaView 2.0 software (AB Sciex, USA) to identify oxidation – modified peptides.

Viscosity measurement

To account for potential interference from high protein concentrations on viscosity readings, protein concentration was simultaneously quantified using the SoloVPE System (Repligen, USA), with a sample volume of 50 μL per assay. Then, the viscosity measurements were performed using a DV2T viscometer (Brookfield, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Chromatography

Size exclusion chromatography

The reference sample for size exclusion chromatography (SEC) was prepared in-house at 4 mg/ml, with 200 μL diluted to 1 mg/ml by mixing with 600 μL of the diluent. The mobile phase consisted of 0.15 M phosphate buffer-0.2 M NaCl (pH 7.0) with 3% isopropanol. The analysis was performed using a Waters e2695 HPLC system (Waters, USA) coupled with an Agilent 1260II system and a NanoChrom BioCore SEC-150 column (5 μm, 7.8 × 300 mm) (NanoChrom, China). The test sample, initially prepared at a concentration of 200 mg/ml, was diluted to a final concentration of 1 mg/ml. The subsequent testing steps were carried out by the procedures used for the reference sample.

Strong cation exchange chromatography

A volume of 250 μL of the sample was transferred into a 10 kDa ultrafiltration tube, and the volume was adjusted to 500 μL with 6 mM imidazole, 6 mM piperazine, and 6 mM Tris (pH 6.50) (mobile phase A). The sample was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 2–8°C to reduce membrane residue to ≤50 μL. Mobile phase A was added to adjust the volume to 500 μL, and the centrifugation was repeated three times. The final solution was prepared with mobile phase A to reach a concentration of 1.0 mg/ml IgG1 (Immunoglobulin G1). Separation by strong cation exchange chromatography (SCX) was conducted using a Waters e2695 HPLC system (Waters, USA) equipped with a Nano BioCore SCX column (4.6 × 100 mm, 5 μm) (NanoChrom, China). A gradient system comprising mobile phase A and mobile phase B (mobile phase A containing 50 mM NaCl) was used, with UV detection at 280 nm, a flow rate of 0.8 ml/min, and a 100 μL injection volume over a 35 minutes run. The acidic, basic, and main peak contents were quantified via area normalization.

Cation exchange chromatography

Cation exchange chromatography (CEX) was used to characterize the acidic, basic, and main peak variants of the sample. A volume of 20 μL of the sample was transferred into a 10 kDa ultrafiltration concentration tube, diluted to 500 μL with the 21.9 mM MES (pH 5.82), and then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 minutes at 8°C. This buffer exchange process was repeated three times, and the final solution was diluted to achieve a concentration of 1 mg/ml. The analysis was performed using a Waters e2695 HPLC system (Waters, USA) with a ProPacTM WCX-10 column (4 × 250 mm) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Capillary electrophoresis-sodium dodecyl sulfate

Capillary electrophoresis-sodium dodecyl sulfate (CE-SDS) analysis was executed under non-reduced and reduced conditions to assess mAb integrity. Non-reduced CE-SDS samples were prepared by combining 5 μL of 100 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) with 2 μL of 10 kDa internal standard, followed by thermal denaturation at 60°C for 5 minutes. Reduced CE-SDS utilized 2-mercaptoethanol as the reducing agent, substituting NEM, with an extended denaturation step at 60°C for 10 minutes. Post-treatment, all samples were subjected to centrifugation (6000×g, 1 minute) to remove particulates prior to injection. Separation was performed on a bare-fused silica capillary (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA) using a PA 800 Plus CE system (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA). Electrophoretic runs were conducted at 15 kV and 22°C for 40 minutes, with UV detection at 220 nm to capture peptide migration profiles.

SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis

For sample preparation, the test sample was diluted to 2 mg/ml with water, mixed with either 2X reducing or non-reducing sample buffer in a 1:1 ratio, and heated at 60°C for 5 minutes depending on whether reduced or non-reduced SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) analysis was required. Then, the electrophoresis was conducted using a Power PacTM Basic apparatus (Bio-Rad, USA) with an initial voltage of 80 V for 30 minutes followed by 130 V for approximately 40 minutes, and the gel was stained using an eStainTM L1 rapid protein staining instrument (GenScrip, China) and scanned with a Gel DocTM XR+system (Bio-Rad, USA) equipped with an 11,000 XL white-light scanner for electrophoretic pattern analysis.

Subvisible particles measurement

Subvisible particle counts were determined using an AccuSizer A2000USP (Particle Sizing Systems, USA). A volume of 10 ml of the settled sample was transferred into the analyzer’s sterile vial, avoiding air bubbles during transfer. Then, a sensitivity threshold of ≥10 μm and ≥25 μm was applied to discriminate particles based on their equivalent spherical diameter. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate (n = 3), with the analyzer calibrated using NIST-traceable polystyrene latex microspheres (10 μm or 25 μm) prior to measurements.

Results

Drug developability

In silico modeling analyses predicted a high developability profile for the IgG1 antibody, as evidenced by favorable structural metrics. These include an isoelectric point (pI) of 8.756, charge patch distribution metrics (Patches of Positive Charge: 1.5361, Patches of Negative Charge: 0.056), and a hydrophobicity score of 169.785, all of which align with recommended thresholds for antibody developability. However, moderate risks were identified for stability, solubility, and viscosity, with viscosity-related concerns particularly emphasizing the need for formulation optimization to mitigate potential challenges during downstream processing and therapeutic application (Table S1).

Buffer system screening

SILCS-Biologics analysis was used to predict excipient–protein interactions for the selection of a potential buffer system. FragMaps were generated and PPI simulations were performed to visualize functional group interactions (an indicator of excipient–protein interactions), identify high-potential protein–protein binding sites (Figure 1A, B). Based on the pI value of the product, we established different buffer system types, pH value and ionic strength for screening. Subsequently, four excipients (Histidine, Acetate, Citrate, and Succinate at 10/20 mM) were assessed for their capacity to mitigate PPI. Among these excipients, Histidine, Acetate, and Citrate demonstrated a greater number of LGFE binding sites with values < −1 (62 for Histidine, 49 for Acetate, and 38 for Citrate). Moreover, Histidine and Citrate exhibited a higher prevalence of overlapping regions characterized by LGFE < −2.0 and PPIP > 0.10 (4 for Histidine and 3 for Citrate). The distribution of the part of binding sites of Histidine, Acetate, and Citrate is shown in Figure 1C, and individual binding sites are presented in Figure S2. In addition, the binding site positions of Histidine were consistent with the high-potential PPI sites, as shown in Figure 1D, indicating Histidine’s potential to prevent PPI. The results of “Tm & Tagg with optional DLS” mode of UNCLE revealed that Histidine and Acetate buffer formulations exhibited superior performance compared to the Citrate buffer system (Figure 1E). However, the melting temperatures (Tm) and denaturation curves of these formulations show a close resemblance, indicating a comparable level of conformational stability (Figure S3A and D). Among them, five specific formulations exhibit superior stability, including four Histidine-based preparations (10 mM Histidine at pH 5.0, 10 mM Histidine at pH 5.5, 20 mM Histidine at pH 5.0, and 20 mM Histidine at pH 5.5) and one Acetate-based formulation (10 mM Acetate at pH 5.0). This superior stability is evidenced by their more favorable protein aggregation curves (Figure 1F), which suggest a reduced tendency for protein aggregation. Furthermore, G22 test results revealed that the 10 mM Histidine buffer system at pH 5.0 and pH 5.5 exhibited strong repulsive intermolecular forces (Figure 1G), which were more favorable compared to other formulations.

Antioxidant screening

In silico analysis predicted two oxidation sites at heavy chain (HC63 and HC106) within the CDR region, indicating a potential oxidation risk. To mitigate the oxidation risk of these sites, antioxidants, specifically methionine and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), were selected for inclusion in the formulation. For the HC63 site, the NC group (without antioxidant) exhibited a continuous increase in the oxidation proportion. However, when 0.5 mg/ml or 1.0 mg/ml of methionine was added, there was a notable trend toward decreased oxidation modification ratio. Conversely, the inclusion of EDTA showed an opposite effect, with an increase in oxidation. As for the HC106 site, when compared to the NC group, treatments with 0.5 mg/ml or 1.0 mg/ml of methionine also resulted in a lower modification ratio. Therefore, methionine was selected as the antioxidant in the formulation (Figure S4).

Viscosity reducers screening

Initiated by the prediction result of moderate risk in viscosity, the viscosity–concentration curve was depicted and shown in Figure 2C, with an inflection point at 180 mg/ml (R2 = 0.992). The initial viscosity of the 200 mg/ml solution (48 cP) exceeded the target threshold (≤25 cP), necessitating a systematic investigation into viscosity-reducing agents. Using SILCS-Biologics analysis, four viscosity reducers (Proline, Arginine, Glycine, and Lysine) demonstrated superior potential for reducing protein viscosity, as evidenced by their higher number of binding sites of LGFE < −2.0 and PPIP > 0.10 (5 for Arginine, 4 for Proline, 4 for Lysine, and 2 for Glycine) (Table S3). Similar to the prediction outcomes in the number of binding sites, preliminary screening results revealed that both Arginine and Proline exhibited superior performance compared to Lysine and Glycine, as shown in Figure 2D. Finally, the potential formulation (200 mg/ml IgG1, 300 mM Proline, 10 mM Histidine at pH 5.5, 1 mg/ml Polysorbate 80 (PS80), and 0.5 mg/ml Methionine) achieved a viscosity of 23.78cP, along with balanced rheological properties (initial/sustained force: 10.03/9.44) (Table S4).

For the formulation’s pH value, we plan to adjust it from 5.5 to 5.2 in the upcoming study. As shown in Figure 1, in the 10 mM histidine buffer system, samples at pH 5.0 and 5.5 have similar stability. The histidine group at pH 5.5 offers better protection (Figure 1G), indicating the 5.0–5.5 pH range does not severely limit formulation stability. A lower pH like 5.0 reduces solution viscosity (Table S5), aiding subcutaneous injection of large-volume, high-concentration proteins by enabling smoother injection. But, when administered to patients, an excessively low pH formulation causes local irritation and pain. Thus, pH 5.2 was selected as the optimal formulation condition, demonstrating superior viscosity reduction compared to pH 5.5, while simultaneously mitigating injection-site pain risk relative to pH 5.0. These combined advantages are expected to enhance syringeability and improve patient adherence to subcutaneous delivery.

Stabilizers and surfactants screening

Stabilizers and surfactants play pivotal roles by ensuring protein stability and mitigating processing stresses in biopharmaceutical formulations, so we proceeded with further screening of these essential components. Given Proline’s dual role as both a viscosity reducer and a stabilizer, we formulated various combinations to evaluate the stability. Initially, based on SILCS-Biologics analysis, we explored the combinations of different stabilizers (Sucrose, Mannitol, and Sorbitol) with Proline in a 10 mM Histidine buffer system. The findings indicated a comparable number of binding sites among these stabilizers, as detailed in Table S5. The results suggest significant similarities in how these stabilizers interact with the protein, and the distribution of their binding sites is visually represented in Figure 3A.

Subsequently, the stability of various formulations was assessed using the UNCLE method to evaluate the potential of different stabilizers and surfactants, as listed in Table S6. The formulated product exhibited consistent physicochemical stability across all tested formulations, as evidenced by the protein aggregation curves and denaturation profiles (Figure S5A and B). Moreover, the formulations maintained stability under different stress conditions. Under high-temperature conditions (40°C/75%RH for 4 weeks), oscillation (200 rpm at 5°C ±3°C for 2 weeks), and freeze–thaw cycles (−20°C ↔ 25°C for three cycles), the ratio of main peak in CEX was preserved (> 95%) and the purity revealed by non-reduced SDS-PAGE (Figure 3B-D) as well as reduced SDS-PAGE (Figure S6B) remained stable. These results indicate the great stability of the formulations under these stress conditions. Furthermore, under high-temperature stress, increasing the concentration of PS20 or 80 from 0.6 mg/ml to 1.0 mg/ml reduced opalescence. Notably, in freeze–thaw cycle analysis, formulations containing PS80 demonstrated superior performance to those with PS20, with a more significant reduction in opalescence observed when PS80 reached a concentration of 1.0 mg/ml. However, under oscillation stress, visible particles were observed in all PS80 groups; no particles were formed in the PS20 groups (Figure S6D). Based on the results, we have elected to exclude the use of a stabilizer (Sucrose, Mannitol, and Sorbitol) and have instead chosen PS20 as the surfactant for our subsequent formulation confirmation experiments.

Confirmation of the formulation

Generally, for biological products, the specification for pH is set as the “target value ±0.5.” To further investigate product stability within the pH control range, samples with pH of 4.7, 5.0, 5.2, 5.4, and 5.7 were subjected to stability assessments under diverse conditions. These encompassed high-temperature exposure (40°C±2°C/75% relative humidity (RH)±5% RH for 30 days), accelerated condition (25°C ± 2°C/60% RH ± 5% RH for 6 months), and long-term storage (5°C ± 3°C for 6 months). Stability was evaluated by analyzing purity parameters such as SEC, SCX, CE-SDS/NR (Figure 4A-C), CE-SDS/R, and subvisible particles (Figure S7B). No significant changes in quality attributes were observed within the pH range. The final formulation exhibited robust physicochemical stability under a comprehensive set of conditions. These included high-temperature conditions (40°C ± 2°C/75% RH ± 5% RH for 30 days), accelerated condition (25°C ± 2°C/60% RH ± 5% RH for 6 months), long-term storage (5°C ± 3°C for 6 months), photosensitivity testing (5000 ± 500 Lx at 5°C ± 3°C for 7 days), light exposure testing (1000 Lx at 25°C ± 2°C for 10 days), and oscillation (200 rpm at 5°C ± 3°C for 15 days) (Figure 5A). Slight changes in quality attributes were observed under light exposure conditions (ratio of monomer in SEC decreased by 1.5%; ratio of main peak in SCX decreased by 2.3%), suggesting the need for photo-protection measures during manufacturing, storage, and transportation (Figure 5B, C). The formulation demonstrated resilience under oscillation conditions, as evidenced by the maintenance of consistent quality attributes, suggesting that it ensures stability during manufacturing and transport. Although certain quality attributes were altered under high-temperature stress testing (e.g., ratio of main peak decreased by 14.3%; purity revealed by CE-SDS/NR decreased by 3.4% compared to the Day 0 baseline), the product remained relatively stable under long-term storage and accelerated conditions for 6 months (Figure 5C, E). Furthermore, stability prediction analysis, incorporating long-term， accelerated, and high-temperature condition data, indicated that the product could maintain stability for up to 2 years at the intended storage temperature (Figures S9). These findings collectively demonstrate that the formulation effectively ensures product stability. Discussion

The development of high-concentration mAb formulations for subcutaneous delivery, which requires a delicate balance between stability, viscosity, and manufacturability, has been a long-standing challenge in the biopharmaceutical field.15 Our study presents an integrated computational-experimental approach that combines molecular dynamics-driven ligand docking (SILCS-Biologics) with high-throughput stability screening (UNCLE) to address these challenges, and the results offer valuable insights into the rational design of mAb formulations.

Based on our experience with the platform, the antibody formulation development process systematically progresses through three key steps: (1) buffer/pH screening to optimize pH, buffer type and ionic strength; (2) excipient screening (e.g., viscosity reducers, stabilizers, and surfactants) to enhance stability and processability; and (3) formulation confirmation via stability studies to ensure compatibility with storage and transportation goals. For the initial buffer selection, the pI value (8.756) of the mAb was the key determinant. In line with the “pI-1” buffering principle, which recommends buffers with a pH approximately one unit below the pI to minimize conformational stress and enhance stability, we selected Histidine, Acetate, Citrate, and Succinate buffers suitable for this pH range.16–18 The integration of computational modeling with experimental data was central to a mechanistic understanding of buffer performance. SILCS-Biologics identified Histidine as an exceptional buffer solution for protein stabilization, exhibiting 62 energetically favorable-binding sites (LGFE < −1.0) and four potential overlapping binding sites (LGFE < −2.0, PPIP > 0.10). This prediction was confirmed by UNCLE-based stability assays, where histidine-based buffers delayed aggregation onset (elevated Tagg) and suppressed intermolecular attraction (enhanced G22), solidifying its selection as the primary buffer component. The synergy between SILCS-Bio simulations and UNCLE data provides a multiscale understanding of histidine’s stabilizing role, directly linking molecular interaction patterns to the stability metrics. SILCS-Bio simulations revealed that histidine molecules form specific, high-affinity interactions with aggregation-prone regions on the protein surface (Figure 1D), leveraging its intrinsic molecular features: the protonated imidazole ring facilitates electrostatic shielding of charged residues, while the aromatic ring enables hydrophobic masking via π-π stacking and hydration-mediated surface protection, thereby reducing aggregation propensity.19–22

Viscosity reduction is a critical aspect of mAb formulation for subcutaneous delivery, as high viscosity ( > 30 cP) can impede administration.23 SILCS-Biologics predicted Proline, Arginine, Glycine, and Lysine as potential viscosity-reducing agents, with Proline and Arginine showing the highest binding site density (LGFE < −2.0, PPIP > 0.10). The experimental confirmation showed that 300 mM Proline reduced viscosity to 23.78 cP (pH 5.5), meeting the target threshold (≤25 cP) and suitable rheological properties for syringeability (Table S5). Proline’s advantage over Arginine is likely due to its ability to balance steric hindrance and hydrogen bonding, effectively disrupting β-sheet aggregation without causing excessive electrostatic repulsion.24 Mechanistically, Proline’s cyclic pyrrolidine side chain, with the Cα atom covalently bonded, restricts the main-chain φ angle, preventing it from fitting into the regular β-sheet hydrogen-bonding network and disrupting β-sheet formation.25 Previous research indicates that while both inhibit Aβ1–42 peptide aggregation, Arginine is more effective at equimolar concentrations. However, Proline’s physical-blockade mechanism (steric hindrance + hydrogen bonding interference) is safer for preserving protein native conformations.26 Its dual mechanism disrupts β-sheet hydrogen-bonding without strong electrostatic perturbations, making it a secure option for aggregation inhibition in charge-sensitive protein design.

Moreover, in the context of surfactant performance under different stress conditions, PS80 demonstrated superiority over PS20 in suppressing opalescence during freeze–thaw stress, whereas PS20 formulations exhibited lower opalescence compared to PS80 formulations under oscillation stress. The underlying mechanisms for these distinct behaviors can be attributed to the unique properties of each surfactant. During freeze–thaw stress, PS80’s longer hydrophobic chain (17-carbon unsaturated, compared to PS20’s 12-carbon saturated chain) likely enables more effective coverage of the ice-water interface.27 This coverage helps to shield proteins from direct exposure to the harsh interface conditions, thereby inhibiting protein aggregation and the subsequent development of opalescence.28 In contrast, under oscillation stress, PS80‘s advantageous properties turn detrimental. The high hydrophobicity of PS80, combined with its susceptibility to oxidation and a tendency for self-aggregation at dynamic interfaces, may promote the formation of turbid and visible particles.29 On the other hand, PS20, with its smaller molecular size enabling more even distribution in the solution and higher oxidative stability, is better suited to interact with proteins and counteract oscillation-induced aggregation under dynamic conditions.30 Previous studies have revealed that in oscillation experiments involving interferon β-1b, 0.01% PS80 did not show a significant advantage over PS20. Moreover, the hydrophobic chain of PS80 might even promote aggregation under such conditions.31 However, it is noteworthy that predicting protective effects in advance through current computational simulation methods like SILCS-Bio is extremely challenging, owing to two inherent problems with polysorbates. Commercial polysorbates are complex mixtures composed of heterogeneous species that vary in fatty acid chain lengths and esterification degrees, making it unfeasible to define a single representative structure for simulation. Moreover, even simplified single polysorbate molecules possess high molecular weights, resulting in a computationally unmanageable number of atoms when simulated alongside a protein in a solvated system. Thus, while simulations perform well in studying small molecules, they currently lack the ability to accurately clarify the precise binding modes and relative protective efficacies of polydisperse surfactants like PS20 and PS80.

The robustness of the final formulation was thoroughly studied under a comprehensive set of conditions. The formulation exhibited exceptional stability across accelerated conditions, long-term storage, and stress conditions (high temperature, oscillation, light test, and photosensitivity). In terms of accelerated condition and long-term storage, SEC monomer retention (> 95%) and minimal SCX main peak shifts (Δ10%) confirmed its resistance to aggregation and charge variants. The minimal changes in critical quality attributes demonstrated the formulation’s adaptability to manufacturing variability. Notably, the optimized formulation was rapidly developed through a computationally guided rational prioritization strategy. This approach eliminated the need for upfront procurement of numerous excipients, significantly reducing material costs. Unlike traditional screening of ~20 conditions over ≥2 months, our two-stage workflow first used simulation (2–3 days) to identify top excipient candidates, followed by focused high-throughput validation. This reduced wet-lab workload by 60–70%, cut material consumption by >70%, and condensed screening to 3–6 weeks. The accelerated process enabled rapid candidate selection and initiation of stability studies within <6 months, delivering a pivotal trial-ready formulation efficiently.

Additionally, this study investigated a computationally guided formulation for an IgG1 mAb. We also extended its applicability to other therapeutic modalities, such as IgG4 and fusion protein. For example, when applied to an IgG4 molecule, the platform demonstrated strong concordance between computational predictions and experimental results, specifically identifying histidine buffer and sucrose as optimal stabilizers, which were identified by the UNCLE and several stability tests. This suggests that the method may be capable of capturing fundamental excipient–protein interaction mechanisms that are relevant across IgG subclasses. Furthermore, for high-concentration formulation development, future research directions will involve deepening the understanding of molecular mechanisms governing high-concentration protein behavior (e.g., intermolecular interactions and conformational stability), developing dynamic predictive models that integrate environmental stress parameters (e.g., temperature, pH, and shear forces), and establishing seamless integrated platforms for real-time data-experiment feedback, ultimately aiming to accelerate formulation development timelines for next-generation biotherapeutic drugs.32

In conclusion, our study presents a comprehensive strategy for the development of high-concentration mAb formulations suitable for subcutaneous delivery. By leveraging computational insights and experimental confirmation, we identified a formulation that meets the stringent requirements of stability, viscosity, and manufacturability. This approach not only accelerates the development process but also ensures the production of high-quality biologics for patient-centric delivery.
