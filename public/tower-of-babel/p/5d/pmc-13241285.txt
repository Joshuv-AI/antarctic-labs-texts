CT Hounsfield Unit Measurements Around the Hip and Their Utility for Opportunistic Screening: A Systematic Review
Authors: Quinn Steiner, Eric Lee, Paul Anderson, Andrea Spiker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Computed tomography scans (CTs) are routinely obtained before hip preservation surgery or to evaluate pelvic and/or hip fractures. Analyzing bone mineral density (BMD) using CT Hounsfield units (HU) before surgical intervention may improve surgical management and outcomes. This systematic review summarizes the current literature on reliability and placement of regions of interest (ROIs) within the hip, pelvis, and sacrum, and their correlation with local and systemic BMD.Methods:A systematic review in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science was performed. Twenty-four studies reported intraclass correlation coefficients of HU measurements and/or compared the HU value obtained from CT and t-score of DXA for predicting regional BMD within the hip, pelvis, and sacrum.Results:Twenty-four studies were included, enrolling 7,035 patients. The HU measurement was most frequently made at the femoral neck (n = 10). Femur and pelvis intrarater and interrater reliability was moderate to excellent. Moderate to strong correlations between femoral head and femoral neck CT HU and DXA, and poor to moderate correlations between the pelvis CT HU and DXA were found.Conclusion:A variety of ROIs have been described around the pelvis and proximal femur all with reasonable reliability for the measurement of CT HU. The strongest correlations between CT HU and DXA were reported between HU of the femoral neck and DXA of the hip/femur suggesting this ROI may be used for opportunistic BMD screening using preoperative hip/pelvis CT scans.Level of Evidence:III.

Computed tomography (CT) Hounsfield units (HUs) are defined as the normalized linear radiograph attenuation coefficients of tissue, with water having a value of zero, air as −1000, and tissues denser than water having positive values at standard temperature and pressure.1,2 HUs have been demonstrated to be an effective estimate of bone mineral density (BMD), and CT HU may be a reliable way to screen patients who may otherwise not have screening for changes in BMD.3 Multiple investigations confirm moderate to strong correlation of DXA BMD and CT HU, and suggest CT HU may be used to identify those at risk for osteoporosis.2,4 Given that hip/pelvis CTs are obtained preoperatively for hip and pelvic fractures as well as hip preservation surgery, the opportunistic use of CT may be helpful in evaluating BMD in this patient population and help guide surgical management.

Multiple regions of interest (ROIs) for measuring CT HU within the hip, pelvis, and sacrum have been described, all of which would be measurable on a preoperative hip/pelvis CT. However, no study has directly compared these different ROIs. Before using preoperative hip/pelvis CT scans to opportunistically evaluate BMD, it is important to determine which ROI is most reliable and best correlates with DXA. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to map the current literature studying the reliability and placement of ROIs within the hip, pelvis, and sacrum, and their correlation with local and systemic BMD.

Methods

Study Design

The design was a systematic review of studies involving the application of HU around the proximal femur, pelvis, and sacrum. The study was registered with PROSPERO, and used PRISMA (Supplemental Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A519) and QUADAS-2 (Supplemental Table 1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A520) methodology. A medical librarian assisted in the development of a comprehensive literature search of MEDLINE using PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Two independent reviewers evaluated the studies of inclusion. References of included studies were also reviewed for applicable studies. Searches were done from Medical Subject Headings used by the National Library of Medicine. The specific search parameters are provided in Supplemental 1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A518.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Investigations comparing CT HU of the proximal femur, pelvis, and sacrum with DXA-based BMD were reviewed. Studies that referenced quantitative CT of the proximal femur, pelvis, or sacrum and report HU were included. In addition, studies were required to report comparisons with DXA measurements of bone mineral density and/or intraclass correlation coefficients, and be written in English with living, human subjects. ROIs that included cortical bone measurements were excluded because the borders are difficult to define, are rarely used clinically, and this exclusion allows for consistent comparisons of measured HU. Two studies recorded measurements of preteriparatide and postteriparatide treatment, and only pretreatment values were included for analysis. In addition, Amin reported values for contrasted and noncontrasted CTs, and only noncontrasted values were reported.5 Two reviewers independently reviewed the titles, abstracts, and full text of articles to determine eligibility based on these criteria. If a consensus could not be reached, then a third reviewer was used to resolve the disagreement.

Statistics

For correlation between CT HU and DXA, Pearson correlation coefficients were defined as very strong (r > 0.8), moderate (0.6 < r ≤ 0.8), fair (0.3 < r ≤ 0.6), and poor (r ≤ 0.3).6 Interrater and intrarater agreement was reported using intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). ICC was graded as excellent (ICC >0.9), good (0.75 < ICC ≤ 0.9), moderate (0.5 ≤ ICC ≤ 0.75), or poor (ICC < 0.5).7 ICC was recorded with 95% CI when reported by the authors. We abstracted area under the curve (AUC) and optimized HU thresholds based on ROC analysis for DXA-based osteoporosis diagnosis. We considered an AUC of <0.7 as nondiagnostic. We elected not to perform a meta-analysis. Although this would be statistically feasible, the heterogeneity of the populations studied (n = 20 to 430, age 26 to 77 years, % female 0 to 100), the variability in what was selected as benchmark ROI based on DXA (femoral head, neck, intertrochanter, pelvis, and sacrum), and the lack of CT method standardization (contrast use, CT kilo voltage, and CT to DXA interval) would make any conclusions misleading.

Quality Assessment

Our previously defined inclusion criteria were used to develop the QUADAS-2 tool to assess potential bias and applicability for each included manuscript, Supplemental 1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A518.8 In addition, this tool was used to develop criteria on pertinent information to include when reporting CT HU ROIs, Supplemental 1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A518. Two reviewers independently reviewed and assessed each included manuscript according to the signaling questions of the QUADAS-2 tool for our specific review. Summarized bias assessment results are reported in Table 1, and responses to each individual question are reported in Supplemental Table 1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A520.

Results

Literature Search

Our search strategy identified a total of 1,046 individual studies. Inclusion/exclusion criteria were applied to titles and abstracts yielding 25 articles that underwent full-text analysis. Two studies were removed after full text analysis, and one study was added through a reference review of added studies, Supplemental Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A519. A total of 24 articles are included in the current review. Twenty-two studies were retrospective, one was prospective and the other was cross-sectional.

Study Demographics

A total of 7035 patients were analyzed across all studies. Mean age ranged from 26.4 to 77.8 years. The proportion of females ranged from 0% to 100%. Varying patient populations were studied (although all CT scans included the area of interest for this review) including hip fractures, virtual colonoscopy, pelvic ring injuries, preoperative arthroplasty, and spine. The percent of patients with osteoporosis among the studies that reported osteoporosis alone ranged from 6.6% to 100%. CT kilo voltage was reported in 16 (66%) of the included studies and ranged from 100 to 140Kv, while contrast was specified in 14 (58%) of studies with the use of contrast reported in 4 (16%). Of the 18 studies that compared CT with DXA, 13 (72%) reported the time interval between the two studies with a range from <10 days to >2 years, Supplemental Table 2, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A521.

Study Quality

Overall, our studies had low risk of bias. Within the patient selection domain, most studies did not have a random sample of patients enrolled. However, a study was only given a potential risk of bias score if they had more than one bias risk within a domain. Within the index test domain, most studies had a low risk of bias. All studies marked unclear did not indicate whether the CT HU results were interpreted without knowledge of the results of DXA. Within the reference standard domain, all studies marked unclear did not indicate their reference standard to determine whether it would correctly classify the target condition. Within the flow and timing domain, the most common risk of bias was a prolonged interval between the CT scan and comparative DXA.

Reliability

Femur

The intrarater and interrater reliability of HU in the femoral head was good to excellent (0.81 to 0.99, 0.78 to 0.99). Seven studies examined the reliability of the femoral neck. Intrarater- and interrater reliability was moderate to excellent (0.69 to 0.97, 0.51 to 0.99). The interrater reliability of the greater trochanter, lesser trochanter, and intertrochanter was good (0.76), poor (0.49), and excellent (0.96). The intrarater reliability of the intertrochanter and greater trochanter was both excellent (0.94, 0.90), Supplemental Table 3, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A522.

Pelvis

The sacral interrater reliability was good to excellent (0.76-0.99), and one study demonstrated excellent intrarater reliability (0.98 to 0.99). The acetabulum was less reliable with the average intrarater and interrater reliability ranging from moderate to excellent (0.60 to 0.99, 0.61 to 0.99), although Nishi19 reported CI with values in the poor range. Mikula24 reported poor interrater reliability in the ilium (0.49), Supplemental Table 3, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A522.

Correlation With DXA

The reviewed studies demonstrated heterogeneity regarding the DXA site that was selected to perform correlation analysis. However, all were shown to be notable, but varied in the strength of correlation.

Proximal Femur-DXA Correlation

Kilinc and Erhart18,30 found moderate Pearson correlations (0.74 to 0.71) of CT HU of the femoral head with femoral DXA, while Gruber15 reported a strong correlation (0.84). Lee et al31 reported fair correlations between the femoral head and DXA of other anatomic regions within the proximal femur (0.41 to 0.43). Femoral neck CT HU to femoral neck DXA had a moderate to strong correlation (0.60 to 0.84), with the exception of one study (Lee et al31) demonstrating fair correlation (0.52). Proximal femur CT HU had poor to fair correlation with the lumbar spine DXA (0.20 to 0.47). Kim et al16 demonstrated moderate correlation of the head neck junction and intertrochanter with total hip DXA (0.61, 0.66), Table 2.

Pelvis DXA Correlation

There were no studies that compared acetabular or ilium measurements with DXA. The sacrum had a larger variability in correlation ranging from poor to moderate (0.22 to 0.61). Flanigan et al23 compared an average of ilium, sacral ala, and sacral body measurements with lumbar, femoral neck, and total hip DXA and reported fair to moderate correlations (0.38, 0.55, and 0.66), Table 2.

Osteoporosis Thresholds and Sensitivity/Specificity

Using ROC analysis, three studies reported a HU osteoporosis threshold within the femoral head and proximal femur ranging from 180 to 201 with sensitivity ranging from 0.82 to 0.89 and specificity ranging from 0.71 to 0.90. An additional three studies reported a lower HU threshold within the femoral head and proximal femur ranging from 54 to 108 with sensitivity ranging from 0.83 to 1.0 and specificity ranging from 0.90 to 1.0. Zou et al22 reported a threshold HU threshold within the sacrum of 185 and 202 with a sensitivity of 0.76 and specificity of 0.80, Supplemental Table 4, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A523. In one study, Christensen et al14 correlated femoral neck ROI with proximal femur DXA BMD to set a threshold for fragility fractures of 112 HU with a sensitivity of 0.80 and a specificity of 0.39. This was excluded from the table because it did not include specific T-score thresholds for osteoporosis or normal BMD.

Normal BMD Thresholds and Sensitivity/Specificity

Two studies reported a HU threshold for normal bone in the proximal femur ranging from 113 to 151. The sensitivity ranged from 0.73 to 1.0 and specificity ranging from 0.76 to 1.0. Berger-Groch et al25 reported a HU threshold for normal bone in the sacrum of 163 with a sensitivity of 0.50 and specificity of 0.79. Donohue et al12 reported a HU threshold of 345 and was the only study to report a threshold for a T-score of ≤ 0, Supplemental Table 4, http://links.lww.com/JG9/A523.

Discussion

We sought to identify the optimal ROI for the measurement of HU around the proximal femur, pelvis, and sacrum, based on correlation with local and systemic BMD and the reliability of their measurements. The main findings of our study were that CT HU measurements of the proximal femur ROI had moderate to strong correlation with DXA of the hip/femur. Femoral head CT HU had moderate strength of correlations with DXA of the femoral head. Pelvic CT HUs were described by fewer studies and had weak to moderate correlation with hip/femur DXA. Therefore, CT HU of the proximal femur may be the most optimal ROI for measuring BMD. When examining reliability, we found moderate to excellent reliability reported across all ROIs in our reviewed studies. This suggests almost any ROI around the proximal femur, pelvis, and sacrum can be used and be expected to have acceptable reliability; however, correlation with BMD was much more variable based on ROI.

The use of opportunistic CT to measure BMD within the spine has been well established. A number of studies have shown good >0.8 and excellent >0.9 ICC for CT HU measurements of the lumbar and thoracic spine.32,33 This is comparable with the reliability of CT HU measurements of the femoral head and neck. In addition, similar correlations between HU and DXA with the results in our study have also been established within the spine, with r values ranging from 0.70 to >0.85.25,34-36 Furthermore, reported osteoporosis HU thresholds in the spine range from 112 to 160.37-40 This suggests that CT HU using ROIs around the hip and pelvis may be used in a clinical setting to reliably predict BMD.

Although there is commercially available software using quantitative CT to measure BMD, this is primarily used for research purposes and not easily accessed clinically. Using CT HU does not require additional learning of new techniques, or the download of additional software and imaging packages. These tools are easily accessible in PACS and useable on routine CT scans allowing for quick BMD measurements during image review as a part of standard preoperative planning. Thus, it could serve as a screening tool for low BMD in patients who are undergoing surgical procedures of the hip and/or pelvis. Because of this, measuring ROIs around the hip is feasible for orthopaedic surgeons using clinical hip/pelvis CT scans with high intrarater and interrater reliability allowing for opportunistic measures of BMD on preoperative CT scans.

One example of the clinical utilization of this process is in hip preservation patients. In our institution, any patient undergoing a hip preservation surgery (hip arthroscopy, periacetabular osteotomy, femoral derotational osteotomy, and surgical hip dislocation) has a preoperative CT scan with 3D reconstructions. Hip preservation surgeons can thus perform BMD screening with these CT studies, allowing for early diagnosis and appropriate treatment of overall bone health and potential underlying osseous pathology. Multiple studies have demonstrated the importance of adequate bone quality in hip preservation surgery. Morris et al41 identified 15% of patients who underwent periacetabular osteotomy (PAO) surgery developed pelvic stress fractures. Furthermore, Selberg et al42 demonstrated an 8% risk of nonunion 1 year after PAO surgery. Beyer et al43 found that low HU at the femoral neck was associated with an increased rate of PAO nonunion. Biomechanical studies have reported femoral neck weakening after CAM osteoplasty, which may predispose patients to femoral neck fractures.44 Furthermore, studies have demonstrated changes in strain distribution across the hip and femur hip after acetabular resurfacing.45,46 These changes may also occur after the anatomic changes associated with hip preservation patients stressing previously unstressed bone increasing risk for bony pathology.

In the setting of femoral fractures requiring fixation, preoperative CTs and HU calculation can inform expected outcomes and surgical planning. Lund et al9 demonstrated that patients with lower femoral neck CT HU had increased screw penetration into the subchondral bone of the femoral head and increased femoral neck shortening postoperatively. Di Monaco et al47 found that increased femoral BMD was independently associated with improved Barthel Index scores after a hip fracture in elderly women. Kim et al34 found that lower preoperative femoral neck HU values were associated with increased risk of intraoperative fracture during noncemented bipolar hemiarthroplasty surgery. Furthermore, osteoporosis has been shown to correlate highly with osteosynthetic failure48 and that obtaining preoperative DXA to evaluate this in patients is helpful in guiding fixation selection.49

Low BMD is also associated with multiple complications after arthroplasty at the hip and knee. Finilla et al50 found that women with low BMD had statistically significant acetabular cup migration between 3 and 12 months compared with normal BMD and that these differences in cup migration persisted at 24 months. In a study by Lee et al51 comparing patients with and without osteoporosis, patients with osteoporosis have greater valgus positioning of the tibial implant postoperatively. Despite these findings, the rates of diagnosis and treatment of osteoporosis are low.52 Measuring CT HU as a part of preoperative imaging review would provide the arthroplasty surgeon information to improve diagnostic rates and guide additional preoperative optimization and surgical planning.

From the current review, we recommend the ROI set by Christensen et al14 to assess preoperative bone health. This measurements is done in the axial plane when the image displayed shows the femoral neck connecting to the greater trochanter. A rectangle is then drawn within the femoral neck cancellous bone (Figure 2D). This has been used in a number of studies11,13,14 with good intrarater and excellent interrater reliability, a strong correlation with central DXA, and consistent thresholds across multiple populations. It was also used as a predictor of fracture using the FRAX calculator which is the cornerstone for osteoporosis measurements to determine treatment.13

Limitations

There are several limitations to our study. First, the retrospective nature of these studies has inherent limitations including recall bias, missing data, and potential confounders. Second, the reporting of CT technique was variable especially with tube voltage and contrast use limiting generalizability. In addition, DXA ROI used for correlation and thresholds to CT HU were variable or omitted entirely, with limited numbers in some ROIs making comparisons more difficult. Notable heterogeneity was observed within populations. Future research should focus on the significance of the CT protocols used and their effect on HU measurements. This could then be used to develop an automated CT protocol associated with a preoperative planning tool to measure BMD consistently in all patients. Additional research should focus on continuing to apply these correlation and threshold values to FRAX scores, osteoporosis treatment, and clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

A variety of ROIs have been described around the pelvis and proximal femur all with reasonable reliability for the measurement of CT HU. The strongest correlations between CT HU and DXA were reported between HU of the femoral neck and DXA of the hip/femur suggesting this ROI may be used for opportunistic BMD screening using preoperative hip/pelvis CT scans.
