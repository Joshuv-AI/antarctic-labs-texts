Long-term impact of blood pressure in women and men in their early 40s on left atrial strain: data from the Akershus Cardiac Examination (ACE) 1950 Study
Authors: Magnar Gangås Solberg, Trygve Berge, Peter Selmer Rønningen, Steve Enger, Mohammad Osman Pervez, Eivind Bjørkan Orstad, Brede Kvisvik, Erika Nerdrum Aagaard, Magnus Nakrem Lyngbakken, Inger Ariansen, Helge Rosjo, Kjetil Steine, Arnljot Tveit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe life-long exposure of the left heart chambers to systemic blood pressures may be important to changes in left atrial (LA) function with age, but long-term follow-up studies are scarce. We aimed to assess the impact of blood pressure in mid-life on LA function assessed by echocardiographic reservoir (LASr) and contractile (LASct) strain two decades later, in men and women.MethodsEchocardiography was performed at ages 62–65 in 3706 participants born in 1950 of the prospective observational Akershus Cardiac Examination (ACE) 1950 Study. Data was linked with blood pressure measurements from the Age 40 Programme, a national health survey performed when the participants were 40–43 years of age. Participants were categorised into three groups representing normal blood pressure, elevated blood pressure and hypertension, based on measurements at ages 40–43. Linear regression models were used to assess associations between blood pressure and echocardiographic LA strain analysis.ResultsA total of 2399 participants (51.6% women) had available LA strain analysis from the ACE 1950 Study (mean age 63.9±0.6 years) and blood pressure data from the Age 40 Programme (mean age 40.1±0.3 years). At ages 62–65, mean LASr was 35.1±9.2% and LASct was 17.7±5.6%. Adjusted regression models showed a significant association between blood pressure category increase at ages 40–43 and LASct (adjusted β 1.03% (95% CI 0.37% to 1.69%), p=0.002) at ages 62–65, but not with LASr. In women, no associations were evident between blood pressure at ages 40–43 and LA strain two decades later.ConclusionsIncreased blood pressure in the early 40s was associated with higher LA contractile strain two decades later in men, but not in women.Keywords: Echocardiography, Hypertension, EPIDEMIOLOGY

Introduction

Hypertension is the leading risk factor attributable to cardiovascular death worldwide.1 However, the risk of negative impact from blood pressure on the cardiovascular system occurs along a continuum even at levels below the diagnostic thresholds which guide physicians in diagnosis and treatment to modify risk of cardiovascular disease.2

Left atrial (LA) size is a well-established marker of cardiovascular risk,3 4 and elevated blood pressure has been associated with an increase in LA volumes.5 6 Assessment of LA reservoir (LASr) and contractile (LASct) function by echocardiographic strain analysis has emerged as a newer method to assess LA remodelling and these parameters have been proven to be prognostic in several heart diseases,7 with incremental value over LA volumes and Doppler-based markers of diastolic function.8 9 LA strain parameters seem to be sensitive markers of left ventricular (LV) diastolic function and can detect subtle cardiac dysfunction occurring prior to overt structural changes related to sustained elevation of blood pressure, such as LV hypertrophy and LA dilation.10 11

With healthy ageing, LASr typically declines, while LASct shows a compensatory increase; however, with different timing in men and women.12 As the blood pressure, even within normal limits, exerts a continuous impact on the cardiovascular system, it may be an important determinant of LA functional decline with age. In hypertensive adults, reduced LASr and an inhibition of the expected age-related LASct increase have been demonstrated,13 and similar results have been demonstrated in another study population exposed to cumulative systolic blood pressure over 24 years.14 Women and men are subject to different hormonal influences through life and differ in their timing and type of cardiovascular disease manifestation.15 There is a lack of sex-specific long-term observational data on the effects of blood pressure from mid-life to early elderliness. To address this gap, we investigated the relationship between midlife blood pressure and LA function in early old age by linking echocardiographic data from a large cardiovascular observational study of 62–65 year-olds with blood pressure measurements collected two decades earlier in a national health survey. We hypothesised that elevated blood pressure in midlife would impair LA function in early old age and that the effects would differ between women and men.

Methods

Study population

The Akershus Cardiac Examination (ACE) 1950 Study is an observational cohort of individuals born in 1950 and residing in Akershus County, Norway as of 2011. Of 5827 invited individuals, 3706 (64%) consented to participate. Examinations were performed at two study centres (Bærum Hospital and Akershus University Hospital) between September 2012 and May 2015, when the participants were aged 62–65 years and included medical history, clinical examination, blood and urine sampling, electrocardiographic recordings and echocardiography. An extensive elaboration of this has been published previously.16 Blood pressure was measured three times while seated and registered as the average of the second and third recordings. Data from the ACE 1950 Study was linked to the Age 40 Programme, a nationwide cardiovascular health screening examination conducted in Norway during the 1980s and 1990s by the National Health Screening Service.17 Participants in the ACE 1950 Study that had data from both studies were 40–43 years old at the time of participation in the Age 40 Programme.

Patient and public involvement

During the ACE 1950 examinations, a random subset of study participants was invited to share their experience and to come with suggestions to improve the conduct. Study results are semi-regularly disseminated to participants and the general population through media, seminars, newsletters and www.ace1950.no.

Variables from the Age 40 Programme

Prior to the study visit, Age 40 Programme participants filled out a questionnaire including medical history, medication use and smoking status. Height and weight were measured at the visit, and blood pressure and heart rate were recorded using an automatic device (DINAMAP; Criticon, Tampa, Florida, USA). To ensure correct blood pressure measurements, right arm circumference was measured 10 cm above the cubital fossa. A blood pressure cuff of the right size (small, medium or large) was attached to the arm at chest height, and participants were instructed to sit on a chair with legs uncrossed and feet flat on the floor. After 2 min of quiet rest, blood pressure and heart rate were measured three times at 1 min intervals, and the average of the second and third readings was registered.

Echocardiography in the ACE 1950 Study

Echocardiographic image acquisition and measurements of cardiac dimensions and function were performed following the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) and European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging (EACVI) recommendations for chamber quantification,18 19 using commercially available equipment and software. Echocardiographic methods in the ACE 1950 Study have previously been described in detail20 and are specified further in the online supplemental file 1.

LA strain analyses were performed in semiautomated two-dimensional speckle tracking software (Q-analysis, EchoPAC V.203, GE HealthCare, Horten, Norway), in accordance with EACVI and ASE recommendations.21 Criteria for analysis of LA strain were an apical four-chamber image without LA foreshortening, with visualisation of at least 2/3 of its wall and an image frame rate of ≥40/s. If these criteria were met, a representative heart cycle was selected, and the LA endocardial border was manually tracked to obtain a region of interest consisting of six segments. If needed, the region of interest was adjusted to fit the wall thickness and to realistically track the wall motion. Tracings with ≥4 acceptable segments were approved. Echocardiographic recordings with insufficient image quality, image frame rate <40/second or with any ongoing arrhythmia such as atrial fibrillation during examination were excluded from LA strain analysis. The values reported are averages of approved segmental strain values at the time of ventricular end-systole (LASr) and before atrial contraction (LASct), as shown in figure 1. LA stiffness index was calculated as E/e’ divided by LASr, E/e’ being the ratio of transmitral early diastolic flow velocity (E) over the average of septal and lateral mitral annular early diastolic velocities (e’). The method and reproducibility of LA strain analysis in the ACE 1950 Study has been described in a previous paper.22

Statistics

Categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages and were compared by the χ2 test. Continuous variables are presented with mean and SD and were compared by the Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance as appropriate. Non-normally distributed variables are expressed with median and IQR and compared by Mann-Whitney U-test or Kruskal-Wallis test depending on number of categories.

Corresponding with the 2024 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines on elevated blood pressure and hypertension,2 participants were categorised into three blood pressure groups at ages 40–43. Normal blood pressure was defined as <120/<70 mm Hg, elevated blood pressure as systolic 120–139 mm Hg or diastolic 70–89 mm Hg and hypertension as systolic ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic ≥90 mm Hg or any use of antihypertensive medication.

Using blood pressure category at ages 40–43 as the independent variable, linear regression analyses were performed to assess associations with LASr and LASct at ages 62–65. Analyses using systolic and diastolic blood pressures separately as continuous independent variables were also performed to further characterise the impact of blood pressure on LA strain. Unadjusted regression analyses are presented in Model 1, and in Model 2, we adjusted for available confounders at ages 40–43 that were regarded clinically meaningful and present the adjusted β with 95% CI. The confounders were body mass index, resting heart rate, current smoking and the use of antihypertensive medication. As a sensitivity analysis, participants using antihypertensive medication at ages 62–65 were excluded from the regression analyses. To assess whether the associations were better described as non-linear, regression analyses using restricted cubic splines with knots at the 5th, 27.5th, 50th, 72.5th and 95th percentiles were performed and tested against the linear models. P values<0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using STATA V.17 (StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

Study population characteristics

Of the 3706 participants included in the ACE 1950 Study, 2733 had clinical and demographic data available from the Age 40 Programme. Out of these, 328 (12%) were excluded from the analysis because of insufficient image quality for LA strain assessment and 6 had missing blood pressure measurements from the Age 40 Programme. Thus, a total of 2399 participants (51.6% women) constituted the final study population (figure 2).

Left atrial strain measures

In the entire population, mean LASr was 35.1±9.2% and LASct was 17.7±5.6%. Men had lower LASr and higher LASct than women, as shown in online supplemental table S1. Those with higher blood pressure at ages 40–43 had non-significantly lower LASr, but a significantly and incrementally increasing LASct, and the difference in LASct was driven by the male participants (table 2 and online supplemental figure S1). The results of linear regression analyses of the overall study population were in line with these findings (online supplemental table S2). Moreover, the LA stiffness index was increasing from the lower to the higher blood pressure groups in both men and women (online supplemental table S3 and online supplemental figure S2).

Unadjusted sex-stratified linear regression analyses (table 3) demonstrated a significant association between increase in blood pressure category at 40–43 years and higher LASct at 62–65 years in men (Model 1). Adjustment for confounders (Model 2) attenuated these results, showing a LASct percentage point increase of 1.03 (95% CI 0.37% to 1.69%, p=0.002) per blood pressure category increase in men, but no significant association was found for LASr. The corresponding adjusted values of LASr and LASct for each blood pressure category are visualised in figure 3. Using systolic and diastolic blood pressures separately as continuous independent variables in Model 2, a significant association between systolic, but not diastolic, blood pressure and LASr was found in men (online supplemental table S4 and online supplemental figure S1). Here, both systolic and diastolic continuous blood pressures were associated with increases in LASct in men. In women, no associations were found between blood pressure categories or continuous blood pressures at ages 40–43 and LA strain at ages 62–65.

Reproducibility

Reproducibility assessments of LA strain analyses in the ACE 1950 population have been published previously.22 The intraobserver intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.97 (95% CI 0.89 to 0.99; p<0.001) for LASr, and 0.97 (95% CI 0.88 to 0.99; p<0.001) for LASct. The interobserver ICC for LASr was 0.97 (95% CI 0.87 to 0.99; p<0.001) and 0.87 (95% CI 0.43 to 0.97; p<0.01) for LASct.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest study exploring the effects of both systolic and diastolic blood pressures in midlife on LA function two decades later, with sex-stratified analyses. Higher blood pressures at ages 40–43 were associated with increased LASct at ages 62–65 in male participants, both in unadjusted analyses and after adjustment for confounders. The adjusted analyses also detected that higher systolic blood pressure was associated with an increase of LASr in men. In women, no significant associations were found between blood pressures at ages 40–43 and LA strain two decades later.

Previous studies have shown that elevated systolic blood pressure and hypertension are associated with reduced LASr and impaired age-related compensatory increases in LASct. The Atherosclerosis Risk In Communities (ARIC) Study, with a similar observation time, included 3859 participants from a community cohort in whom cumulative systolic blood pressures were calculated over a time period of 24 years until LA function assessment was performed at a mean age of 75 years.14 In analyses adjusted for demographics, cardiovascular risk factors and disease as well as echocardiographic determinants of LA function, they found that a higher cumulative systolic blood pressure was associated with lower LASr, an association that was related to LV function and mass. No significant association was found in relation to LASct. While sex-separated results for the associations between systolic blood pressure and LA strain were not presented in the ARIC Study, no interaction for sex was found in the linear regression analyses. Women and men typically develop cardiac disease at different ages,15 and the contrast between our findings and those of ARIC may be explained by the older age of the ARIC population at the time of echocardiography. At the age of 75, a higher burden of comorbidities and more pronounced LA and LV remodelling is to be expected in both sexes, and the differences we found at a younger age may therefore have evened out.

Reduced LASr and LASct have been associated with elevated LV filling pressures, and in validation by invasive measurements, they showed better accuracy in patients with reduced LV ejection fraction <50%.10 However, values of LASct >14% were indicative of normal filling pressures in those with normal LV function. In the present study, mean LV EF and GLS were within normal limits, both in the study population as a whole and at the level of each blood pressure group. Thus, the finding of increased LASct by higher blood pressure, but no impact on LASr seems less likely to be linked with elevated LV filling pressures. Further, diastolic indices such as transmitral E/A ratio, E/e’ or LA volumes showed only small and clinically insignificant differences between the blood pressure groups in our population, which is also consistent with the lack of impact of blood pressure on LASr.

A compensatory increase in LASct related to the reduction of LASr with age has previously been demonstrated by Stefani and colleagues.12 Another study from the same group where 242 participants with hypertension were compared with 157 non-hypertensive controls found lower values of LASr and a lack of compensatory increase in LASct in the hypertensive patients.13 The expected age-related increase in LASct was observed in non-hypertensive controls and the difference in LASct between these groups increased with age and started separating at an age of approximately 60 years. Possibly, the present study’s finding of a blood pressure-associated increase in LASct in male participants could be explained by age at the time of echocardiography and may indicate early cardiac remodelling. The lack of impact on LASr may indicate that any significant LA remodelling may still not be present in these participants. However, the sensitivity analysis, in which we excluded participants with antihypertensive treatment at ages 62–65, revealed that elevated blood pressure in untreated patients was associated with increased LASr. This highlights the complexity of LA functional adaptation to elevated blood pressure and hypertension and may suggest that participants with antihypertensive treatment have more pronounced atrial remodelling, possibly due to more severe or longer-standing elevation in blood pressure compared with those without treatment.

Women typically develop cardiovascular disease later in life compared with men, and more often present with heart failure with preserved EF or cerebrovascular disease, while men are more likely to experience acute coronary syndromes.15 Up until menopause, endogenous oestrogen production in women promotes availability of nitric oxide in vascular endothelium which results in arterial vasodilation and lower blood pressures than in men of the same age.23 Lower systemic blood pressures in premenopausal women favourably influence LV loading conditions and promote myocardial relaxation, which may explain our finding of increased LASct in men, but not in women. Women with hypertension did not express the same change in LA function as the hypertensive men, but had a similar increase in LA stiffness with blood pressure, which may indicate that the differences in LA functional adaptation to blood pressure between men and women are not related to LA stiffness.24 Further, the attenuation of hypertension and cardiac remodelling in women after menopause has also been attributed to increased sympathetic tonus compared with male peers, and a higher prevalence of states associated with inflammation such as obesity or rheumatic disease.25 Thus, men seem to have less overall neurohormonal regulation counter-acting cardiac remodelling through life. These hormonal, metabolic and tonic regulative differences in combination may influence the timing of blood pressure-related cardiac remodelling, which could account for the sex differences we observed in the association between blood pressure and LA strain.

Because high blood pressure remains the most prevalent and important preventable risk factor for cardiovascular disease,1 it is of importance to further study the mechanisms behind and how they affect individuals differently. The present study provides evidence of subtle sex differences in the LA functional response to increases in blood pressure two decades earlier in a large observational cohort with nearly equal participation between men and women. Although the differences are relatively small and may not affect day-to-day clinical management, our findings provide new and possibly hypothesis-generating perspectives on sex differences in long-term effects on cardiac adaptation to hypertension and the development of hypertensive heart disease.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of the present study include long-term follow-up data in a large cohort of men and women of similar age, which gives the opportunity to examine differences between large samples of age-matched individuals from the general population. Further, reproducibility of LA strain measures was good. Despite having a high participation rate, selection bias may be present as not all participants of the ACE 1950 Study had participated in the Age 40 Programme. Individuals with more severe and disabling cardiovascular illness might also have declined participation in the ACE 1950 Study, although eligible, and this could also introduce bias towards weaker associations between blood pressure and LA strain if these individuals had elevated blood pressure at ages 40–43. Blood pressure measurements at ages 40–43 were performed in one sitting, and measurement errors may have occurred despite using a standardised procedure. Further, blood pressure data from the two decades between the studies were not available, limiting the resolution of our data and measurement bias towards higher blood pressure values may be present. The Age 40 Programme did not include questions on heart failure and atrial fibrillation, but the rates of these conditions were probably minor at ages 40–43 and are therefore less likely to have impacted our results.

Conclusions

In the present study on individuals born in 1950 from the general population, a higher blood pressure in the early 40s was associated with increased LA contractile strain two decades later in men, but not in women. This may reflect blood pressure-related subtle cardiac remodelling with age, affecting men and women differently.
