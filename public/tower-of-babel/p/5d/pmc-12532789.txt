A novel approach for phyto-synthesis of silver nanoparticles using floral extract of Punica granatum for potent anti-cancerous and antibacterial capabilities
Authors: Sumit K. Shrivastava, Asita Kulshreshtha, Rajesh K. Gangwar, Shikha Srivastava, Monisha Banerjee, Saurabh Kumar, Shama Parveen, Dhirendra K. Chaudhary, Dimple Kumari, Abhishek P. Tiwari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This article explores the phyto-synthesis of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) derived from the floral extract of Punica granatum and their potential applications in antibacterial and anticancer treatments. In the synthesis process, silver nitrate (AgNO3) has been utilized as both a reducing and stabilizing agent, leading to the successful formation of stable silver nanoparticles (PG-Ag NPs). The nanoparticles were characterized using various analytical techniques, revealing their spherical shape and uniform dispersion, with an average size of ~ 27 (± 2) nm. The antibacterial and anticancer properties of the PG-AgNPs have been investigated, demonstrating significant anticancer activity with an IC50 value of ~ 13 µg/mL. Furthermore, the nanoparticles exhibited potent antimicrobial efficacy against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. These findings indicate that PG-Ag NPs, synthesized from Punica granatum flower extract, present a promising and cost-effective solution for antibacterial and anticancer applications.

Introduction

Nanotechnology is increasingly focused on the eco-friendly synthesis of metal nanoparticles, with silver and gold nanoparticles gaining prominence for their broad applicability [1–10]. In recent years, researchers have developed various green synthesis methods for creating affordable, non-toxic, and environmentally friendly nanoparticles, with phyto-synthesis being a widely adopted approach [11–20]. This method leverages plants, fruits, and herbs, which act as reducing and stabilizing agents to produce metal nanoparticles [21–30]. The phyto-synthesis process is simple, cost-effective, and offers versatile nanoparticle production without the use of harmful chemicals. The unique physicochemical properties of silver and gold nanoparticles, combined with their promising antibacterial and anticancer potential, continue to attract significant attention [31–34].

Phyto-synthesis offers a practical solution, as plant-based reducing and stabilizing agents can yield stable nanoparticles with significant biological activity. Ethno medicinal plants rich in antioxidants, in particular, have demonstrated enhanced capabilities in synthesizing metallic nanoparticles with strong antibacterial and anticancer potential. Given the growing demand for safer alternatives to conventional cancer treatments like chemotherapy, phyto-synthesized metallic nanoparticles have emerged as a promising solution, offering effective yet less harmful therapeutic options. Gangwar et al. developed a green technique for improving bioavailability using curcumin-conjugated silica nanoparticles [35]. According to Shrivastava et al., Plumeria Pudica Plant sections can synthesise silver nanoparticles. They phytosynthesized silver nanoparticles from Plumeria Pudica flowers [36]. They also phytosynthesised silver nanoparticles from Plumeria Pudica leaves and found anti-cancerous action [37]. Huang et al. used the aqueous extract of Fumaria officinalis for the growth of nickel nanoparticles, which were subsequently identified as a groundbreaking chemotherapeutic agent for the treatment of ovarian cancer [38]. Zhang et al. used Leucus aspera to phytosynthesize Ag NPs and observed significant efficacy in inhibiting alveolar carcinoma, demonstrating promising anticancer action [39].

The synthesis of metallic nanoparticles has advanced significantly with the use of plant-based methods. Recent research has highlighted the successful use of various plant extracts for nanoparticle synthesis, demonstrating not only biocompatibility but also enhanced therapeutic properties. For instance, studies have shown that silver nanoparticles synthesized using plants like Plumeria pudica and Fumaria officinalis demonstrated remarkable anticancer properties. These findings underline the potential of green-synthesized nanoparticles to revolutionize cancer therapy by offering cost-effective, non-toxic, sustainable alternatives to conventional treatments. Pomegranate (Punica granatum), a plant known for its medicinal properties, has gained attention as an effective source for synthesizing silver nanoparticles. Traditionally used in treating a wide array of conditions, from infections to cardiovascular diseases, pomegranate’s bioactive compounds are now being explored for their potential in cancer therapy. Various parts of the plant-such as the peel, leaves, and seeds-contain potent phytochemicals that can reduce metal ions and stabilize nanoparticles, making them valuable tools in green nanotechnology [40–44].

In this research, a green synthesis method was employed to synthesize silver nanoparticles using an aqueous extract of Punica granatum. The resulting nanoparticles, named as PG-Ag NPs, were thoroughly characterized and demonstrated a uniform, spherical structure with an average size of ~ 27 (± 2) nm. The biological properties of these nanoparticles were also evaluated, and it demonstrated significant antibacterial and anticancer activity. The findings indicate that PG-Ag NPs, synthesized through an eco-friendly process, have the potential to be developed further for cost effective biomedical applications, particularly in antibacterial and anticancer treatments.

Methods

Experimental protocol/design

Materials

Trypan blue, 0.25% Trypsin–EDTA solution, Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), Fetal bovine serum (FBS), Antibiotic–antimycotic (Ab/Am) solution, Fetal bovine serum (FBS), PBS: Phosphate Buffered Saline, 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5- diphenyltetrazolium bromide tetrazolium salt (MTT), PBS: Phosphate Buffered Saline, Silver Nitrate (AgNO3, 99.99%) and other chemicals required for the experiment, Fresh Punica Granatum flowers, commonly known as Anar or pomegranate.

Phyto-synthesis of silver nano particles

The blooms of Punica granatum was collected from Department of Zoology, Babasaheb Bhimrao Ambedkar University (Lucknow, India). The genus of the collected blooms was identified and confirmed by comparing it with herbarium specimen existing in the Department of Horticulture, Babasaheb Bhimrao Ambedkar University (Lucknow, India). The flowers of Punica granatum were thoroughly washed under running water to remove dust and impurities. The petals were rinsed six times with double-distilled water, allowed to air dry at room temperature, and then chopped into small pieces. 60 g of the flower petals were boiled in a beaker containing 300 ml of double-distilled water for 30 min. The resulting extract was filtered six times using Sartorius filter discs (Grade 292) and allowed to cool. The extract was then concentrated to 90 ml and stored at 4 °C until further use.

To prepare a 10 mM aqueous solution of silver ions, 1.6 g of silver nitrate (AgNO3) salt was dissolved in 1000 ml of deionized water. Subsequently, 10 ml of the Punica granatum flower extract (PG-ex) was mixed with 100 mL of the 10 mM AgNO3 solution. The reaction was conducted under constant stirring on a hot plate at 65 °C for 75 min. The formation of silver nanoparticles (Ag-NPs) was indicated by a color change of the solution from bright yellow to dark brown within the first 25 min. To ensure the reaction was complete, the mixture was stirred at the same temperature for an additional 50 min, after which no further color change was observed, confirming the end of the reaction. The phytosynthesized silver nanoparticles (PG-AgNPs) remained stable and uniformly distributed for several months. Characterization and biomedical applications

The absorption spectra of the samples were measured with an Evolution 201 double beam UV–Vis. spectrometer. The morphology and elemental composition of the Ag nanoparticles (Ag NPs) were analyzed using a TESCAN MAIA3 scanning electron microscope equipped with an Oxford EDX system. FTIR spectra were collected in transmittance mode using a Bruker FTIR spectrometer. A diluted solution of PG-AgNPs was drop-cast onto a pre-cleaned silica wafer and vacuum dried. The structural characteristics of the Ag NPs were determined using a 9 kW PANalytical X'Pert X-ray diffractometer with a Cu-Kα radiation source. XRD samples were prepared by spin coating the sample onto a glass substrate.

The National Centre for Cell Sciences (NCCS), Pune, India, was kind enough to provide us with our SiHa cell line, which was produced from cervical squamous cell carcinoma obtained from human patients. The procedure of doing research ultimately resulted in the acquisition of this cell line. Supporting the cells in DMEM was accomplished by the use of a medium that included 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic–antimycotic solution. This action was taken in order to achieve the goal of maintaining the cells in a state of excellent repair. After that, they were put into a CO2 incubator that was established at 37℃ and contained 5% (v/v) CO2. There were further exposure experiments in which the cells were reused after being cultured for 24 h.

In order to determine whether or not the PG-Ag NPs had a cytotoxic effect, the researchers used the SiHa cancer cell line. The reduction test that was developed by Mosmann (1983) was used in order to assess the viability of the cells that received treatment as well as those that served as the control [45]. For this particular objective, the tetrazolium dye known as 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) (Himedia, Pennsylvania,USA) was used. The yellow 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide salt (MTT) turns into the purple formazan crystals that are employed in the MTT experiment. This transformation is caused by the mitochondrial translocation protein (MTT), which is responsible for the transformation.

According to Parveen et al., the function and health of the mitochondria are considered to be a measure of the vitality of the cell [46]. Inoculation was carried out for a period of 24 h after the seeding of cells in 96-well plates at a density of one million cells per well. After that, the cells were subjected to different concentrations of PG-ex and PG-Ag NPs, ranging from 5 to 30 µg, and then they were permitted to incubate at a temperature of 37ºC in an incubator with a CO2 content of 5% for a period of 24 h. After being exposed for 24 h, 10 µl of MTT solution with a concentration of 5 mg/mL was added to each well. The wells were then incubated in a CO2 incubator at 370C for 4 h. Following the incubation process the culture medium was discarded and then 100 µl of DMSO was added in order to dissolve the formazan crystals for 15 min. The optical density (OD) was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, Epoch 2, USA). This was done in order to evaluate the viability of the cells. The statistical analysis was carried out with the assistance of GraphPad Prism. Two harmful microorganisms, namely E. coli and S. aureus, were subjected to antimicrobial activity testing on silver nanoparticles in order to assess the efficiency of these particles against these bacteria. It is well recognised that silver nanoparticles have the ability to function as antimicrobial agents against bacteria that cause an infection. The traditional agar well diffusion technique was used in order to validate the effectiveness of silver nanoparticles as antibacterial agents. These nanoparticles were produced by using the flower of Punica Granatum, which was a green synthesis process. To ascertain the zones of inhibition (ZoI) against every kind of bacteria, the cultures were put in an incubator and heated to 37 ºC for a period of 24 h. This was done in order to discover the ZoI.

Each experiment was conducted with a maximum of three times. Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA and Tukey’s multiple comparison test, with P < 0.05 considered to be significant.

Measurement of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

The induction of intracellular ROS was analyzed using DCFDA [47]. Cells, cultured in freshly prepared medium in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 per well, were allowed to proliferate for 24 h. Afterward, the adherent cells were exposed to varying concentrations of PG-ex (10 and 25 μg/mL) and PG-AgNPs (5 and 13 μg/mL) for an additional 24 h. The medium was then carefully removed, and the wells were rinsed with 1 × PBS. Subsequently, 40 μL of DCFDA (10 μM) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 20–30 min. After incubation, the cells were washed with 1 × PBS and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti2, Japan) at wavelengths of 394–490 nm and a magnification of 200×.

Detection of Apoptosis by acridine orange/propidium iodide (AO/PI) dual staining

SiHa cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates and incubated overnight. Subsequently, the cells were treated with varying concentrations of PG-ex (10 and 25 μg/ml) and PG-Ag NPs (5 and 13 μg/ml) for 24 h. To assess cell viability, double staining with AO (500–526 nm) and PI (535–615 nm) was performed, following the method described by Parveen et al. [46]. After removing the treatment medium, the cells were washed with 1 × PBS, and 300 μL of an AO and PI solution (100 μM each in a 1:1 ratio) was added. The cells were incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Post-incubation, the dye was discarded, the cells were rinsed twice with 1 × PBS, and fluorescence microscopy (Nikon Eclipse Ti2, Japan) was used to capture images at 200 × magnification.

Result and discussions

Figure 2a presents the UV–Vis. spectra of PG-extract, exhibiting a significant absorption peak at approximately 312 nm, likely attributed to the functional groups present in the extract. In Fig. 2b, the UV–Vis spectra of PG-Ag NPs display a distinct absorption peak around 453 nm, which is associated with surface plasmon resonance (SPR) of silver nanoparticles. The progression of the synthesis was visually evident through a color shift in the solution, transitioning from a translucent appearance to golden and finally dark brown. This change results from the excitation of the SPR band of free electrons in the Ag NPs. The specific position of the plasmon peak depends on the size and shape of the synthesized nanoparticles.Fig. 2a UV–Vis. spectra of PG-ex. b UV–Vis. spectra of PG-Ag NPs. c FTIR spectra and d XRD diffractogram of PG-Ag NPs

Figure 2c illustrates the FTIR spectra of the samples of PG-Ag NPs recorded in transmittance mode, showing key peaks. The prominent band at 3449.43 cm−1 corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations from intermolecular hydrogen bonding in secondary amines, while the peak at 2086.54 cm−1 is attributed to C ≡ C stretching. The band at 1640.82 cm−1 likely arises from C = O stretching in tertiary amides. Additionally, a peak at 689.68 cm−1 indicates C–Cl bond stretching and halide group vibrations. Many Studies revalidated that the samples of Punica Granatum flower extract have an abundance of the major polyphenolic classes [48], including, flavonoids, tannins, and anthocyanin pigments. Anthocyanins [49] such as Pelargonidin, Cyanidin, Delphinidin, as well as Ellagitannins [50, 51] such as Punicalin, Ellagic acid, Punicalagin and Flavonoids [51] such as Luteolin (flavones), Kaempferol (flavonols), Catechin (flavan-3-ols) are some of the possible most important groups and subgroups of compounds present in extract.

Figure 2d shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of the phyto-synthesized Ag NPs. The XRD results reveal sharp peaks at 2θ angles of 38.43°, 44.64°, 64.84°, and 77.75°, corresponding to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of the face-centered cubic (FCC) structure of silver nanoparticles, in agreement with JCPDS File No. 04–0783. These findings confirm the polycrystalline nature of the Ag NPs synthesized using PG flower extract. The lattice parameter was calculated as 4.06 Å, closely matching the standard value of 4.08 Å (JCPDS File No. 04–0783). The average crystallite size, determined using the Debye–Scherrer equation, was approximately 17.81 nm [45, 52].

The high-resolution FE-SEM image, shown in Fig. 3a, clearly reveals the spherical shape of the synthesized silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs). The particle size was measured using ImageJ software, and the average size was found to be approximately 27.33 nm, which aligns closely with the results obtained from XRD analysis. The EDX spectra, as seen in Fig. 3b, provide insight into the elemental composition of the nanoparticles. A strong signal for silver (Ag) is observed, along with some minor peaks for gold (Au), which are present due to the Au conducting thin film used during imaging. The antibacterial properties of the substance were examined in bacterial cultures using the disc diffusion method. In order to determine the efficacy of the PG-AgNPs as an antibacterial agent, they were subjected to testing against both Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus) and Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli). Evaluating Cytotoxic Activity (MTT Assay) revealed that compounds considerably reduced cell viability of SiHa cells from cervical cancer. Various quantities of silver nanoparticles were used to test the cytotoxicity of substances by calculating the % cell viability. As the dosage concentrations increased, the findings showed that cancer cell viability dropped dramatically compared to untreated cells (control cells). The IC50 values for PG-ex and PG-Ag NPs were determined to be around 25 µg/mL and 13 µg/mL, respectively, as shown in Fig. 5. In order to suppress cells by 50% in vitro, this concentration is required. The MTT result suggested that PG-Ag NPs may be more effective than PG-ex in inhibiting cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis in cervical cancer cells. Conclusions

The study successfully demonstrates that the floral extract of Punica granatum can act as an efficient reducing agent for the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles (AgNPs). This environmentally friendly and cost-effective approach offers a sustainable method for nanoparticle production. The synthesized AgNPs were found to be spherical, with an average size of ~ 27 ± 2 nm, as confirmed by FE-SEM analysis. Additionally, the phytosynthesized PG-AgNPs exhibited strong antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, and significant anticancer properties, particularly in reducing the viability of SiHa cervical cancer cells. A comparison with existing studies further highlights the promise of this synthesis approach for broader applications. These findings suggest that PG-AgNPs could be valuable cost effective solution for future biomedical applications.
