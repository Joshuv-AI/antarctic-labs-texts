Hemoglobin-to-red blood cell distribution width ratio is inversely associated with prevalent gastrointestinal cancers in NHANES and serves as an informative feature in machine-learning models
Authors: Shiming Chen, Kexin Zhao, Jingxia Zhang, Yingxue Niu, Ran Zou, Min Wang, Yifang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe hemoglobin-to-red blood cell distribution width ratio (HRR) is an inexpensive index derived from routine blood tests, but its relationship with gastrointestinal (GI) cancers in the general population remains unclear.MethodsWe pooled NHANES 2001–2018 data (adults ≥ 20 years). GI cancer history was screened using MCQ220 and site-confirmed using site-specific follow-up items for colon, rectal, stomach, esophageal, liver, and pancreatic cancers. HRR was calculated as hemoglobin (g/dL) divided by RDW (%). Survey-weighted logistic regression estimated odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs for prevalent GI cancer across HRR (per 0.1-unit increase and tertiles), with restricted cubic splines and subgroup interaction tests. In parallel, eight machine-learning (ML) algorithms were trained and evaluated (70/30 split) to assess predictive performance (AUC, calibration, and decision-curve analysis), with SHAP for interpretability.ResultsAmong 39,663 participants, 344 had site-confirmed GI cancer. Higher HRR was inversely associated with GI cancers in the fully adjusted model (per 0.1-unit increase: OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77–0.95; P = 0.003). Participants in the highest HRR tertile had lower odds than the lowest tertile (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.35–0.93; P = 0.026; P for trend = 0.028). Sex modified the association (P for interaction = 0.002), with an inverse association in men (OR 0.74) but not women. ML models showed similar discrimination (top AUC ≈ 0.82), and SHAP highlighted HRR as an important contributor alongside age.ConclusionsHigher HRR was inversely associated with prevalent, site-confirmed GI cancers in NHANES. In machine-learning analyses, HRR functioned as an informative classification feature.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16500-6.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal cancers (GI cancers) encompass malignancies of the esophagus, stomach, liver, pancreas, and colorectum [1] and remain among the most common cancers worldwide with substantial incidence and mortality [1–3]. In 2018, approximately 4.8 million new GI cancer cases and 3.4 million deaths occurred globally [1]. GI cancers accounted for about 26% of all cancer cases and 35% of cancer-related deaths, and the global numbers are projected to increase by 58% (incidence) and 73% (mortality) by 2040 [1, 2]. Given this burden, there is a continuing need to clarify disease-related biological processes and to identify accessible indicators that may aid classification of prevalent GI cancer and population-level surveillance.

Among hematological indicators, red blood cell distribution width (RDW) and hemoglobin (HGB) are routinely measured indices that are inexpensive, standardized, and widely available [4]. RDW reflects the variability in red blood cell volume [4]. GI cancers—particularly gastric cancer (GC) and colorectal cancer (CRC), as well as liver cancer—may be associated with alterations in RDW and HGB through multiple pathways [5–8]. For example, tumor-related increases in hepcidin can contribute to functional iron deficiency by reducing intestinal absorption and macrophage iron release [5, 6], thereby promoting microcytosis and greater heterogeneity of red blood cell size. Such heterogeneity in erythrocyte morphology can lead to higher RDW values [4].

Anemia in patients with malignant tumors is commonly categorized as tumor-associated anemia and treatment-related anemia [7–9]. Tumor-associated anemia can arise from chronic inflammation, disordered iron metabolism, and nutritional deficiency, which impair erythropoiesis [7–9]. In addition, malignancies may cause blood loss, hemolysis, or bone marrow infiltration that reduces red cell production [7–9]. Treatment-related anemia can occur secondary to surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or systemic therapies, which may suppress bone marrow function and alter red cell turnover [7–9]. Inflammatory cytokines and tumor microenvironmental factors can further disrupt erythropoietin signaling and iron utilization [6–9]. These processes may also be accompanied by oxidative stress and systemic metabolic disturbances that influence erythrocyte maturation and survival. Collectively, the chronic inflammatory milieu and altered hematopoietic microenvironment may contribute to hematopoietic dysfunction in cancer.

However, using RDW or HGB alone as biomarkers may be limited because each index is influenced by multiple non-cancer conditions and by intercurrent inflammation. The hemoglobin-to-RDW ratio (HRR) is a derived index calculated from HGB and RDW [10] and may provide a convenient composite summary of anemia- and inflammation-related information. Nevertheless, the existing evidence is largely based on observational or computational analyses [10–13]. It remains unclear whether HRR provides incremental information beyond HGB or RDW alone. Therefore, in addition to evaluating the association between HRR and prevalent GI cancers, we compared HRR, HGB, and RDW separately. Prior studies have reported associations between HRR and outcomes in several settings, including stroke [11], chronic kidney disease [12], and population-based mortality outcomes, including cardiovascular mortality [13], suggesting its potential clinical value [10–13]. As an index derived from routine blood tests, HRR requires no additional cost and can be implemented with standard laboratory data in large-scale population settings where low-cost, scalable measurements are needed.

Although HRR has shown associations with several disease-related outcomes, evidence regarding its association with site-confirmed GI cancers remains limited. Using NHANES, we aimed to evaluate the association between HRR and prevalent site-confirmed GI cancers; compare HRR, HGB, and RDW separately; evaluate HRR as an informative feature in machine-learning classification models; and compare HRR-only, HGB-only, and RDW-only classification performance.

Study design

Dataset origin

As shown in Fig. 1, data were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2001–2018 cycles and combined following NHANES analytic guidelines [14–16]. The number of participants available in the public-use demographic files for each NHANES cycle was as follows: 11,039 in 2001–2002, 10,122 in 2003–2004, 10,348 in 2005–2006, 10,149 in 2007–2008, 10,537 in 2009–2010, 9,756 in 2011–2012, 10,175 in 2013–2014, 9,971 in 2015–2016, and 9,254 in 2017–2018, yielding 91,351 participants before eligibility screening. Participants aged ≥ 20 years with available hemoglobin and RDW measurements were eligible; individuals who were pregnant, had missing GI cancer outcome information based on MCQ220 screening with site confirmation from MCQ230A–MCQ230C, or had missing key covariates were excluded, yielding 39,663 participants for the primary analysis. MCQ230D was additionally reviewed, where available, to identify participants reporting more than three cancer types. NHANES is conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics using a complex, multistage probability sampling design [14, 16]; all participants provided written informed consent and the protocol was approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board [15].

HRR measurement

Hemoglobin-to-red blood cell distribution width ratio (HRR) was calculated as hemoglobin (HGB, g/dL) divided by red blood cell distribution width (RDW, %) using laboratory complete blood count measurements from NHANES. HRR was treated as a derived ratio, with higher values indicating higher hemoglobin relative to RDW. Detailed variable definitions and laboratory procedures were obtained from the cycle-specific NHANES documentation [17].

Outcome definition (GI cancers)

Cancer history was first screened using MCQ220 (“Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had cancer or a malignancy of any kind?”). Among participants answering “Yes” to MCQ220, cancer site was identified using the site-specific follow-up items MCQ230A–MCQ230C (“What kind of cancer was it?”), which record up to three reported cancer types. MCQ230D was additionally reviewed, where available, to identify participants reporting more than three cancer types [18].

GI cancers were defined by identifying digestive-organ sites reported in the site-specific follow-up items, including colon, rectum, stomach, esophagus, liver, and pancreas. Participants with a reported cancer history but missing, unknown, or non-specific site information were coded as having missing outcome data and excluded from outcome modeling to minimize misclassification. Participants reporting more than one GI cancer site were counted once in the combined GI cancer endpoint. Because case counts for several individual GI cancer sites were limited, the primary analyses used a combined GI cancer endpoint. Site-specific case counts and the exact site mapping are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Covariate definitions & protocols

Studies and clinical experience informed the a priori selection of covariates, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, poverty income ratio (PIR), body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). Self-reported kidney disease was assessed using KIQ022 (“Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had weak or failing kidneys?” [19]). Responses were categorized as Yes, No, or Unknown; the Unknown category included “Don’t know,” “Refused,” and missing responses. In sensitivity analyses, self-reported kidney disease was additionally included as a covariate on top of Model 3. We also repeated the fully adjusted analysis after excluding participants with self-reported kidney disease. Age was recorded in years, and sex was categorized as male or female. Race/ethnicity, education, marital status, and PIR were derived from NHANES demographic items and categorized as shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²) and was analyzed as both a continuous variable and categorized groups where appropriate. Smoking status was classified as never, former, or current based on NHANES smoking questionnaire responses (e.g., SMQ020/SMQ040). Alcohol consumption was categorized into three groups: drinker (at least 12 drinks in any one year), non-drinker, and unknown (reflecting missing questionnaire data). This simplified categorization was used to maximize the sample size while accounting for missingness as a distinct group. Hypertension was defined using self-reported physician diagnosis and/or antihypertensive medication use, with measured blood pressure used as supportive information when applicable. Diabetes was defined using self-reported physician diagnosis and/or use of glucose-lowering medication/insulin, with laboratory criteria used as supportive information when applicable. Hyperlipidemia was defined based on laboratory-confirmed lipid thresholds and medication history. Participants were classified as having hyperlipidemia if they met any of the following: total cholesterol ≥ 200 mg/dL, triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, LDL-C ≥ 130 mg/dL, low HDL-C (< 40 mg/dL for men, < 50 mg/dL for women), or the use of lipid-lowering medications. Given that the primary exposure (HRR) and outcome (GI cancers) were assessed in the full MEC sample, standard 2-year MEC examination weights were used for all primary models. Fasting lipid thresholds were utilized alongside non-fasting lipids (TC and HDL-C) and medication history to maximize case identification without restricting the overall analysis to the fasting subsample. Triglyceride and LDL-C measurements were obtained from the fasting subsample. eGFR was calculated from serum creatinine using standard NHANES-recommended equations and treated as a continuous covariate in multivariable models. C-reactive protein (CRP) was included in sensitivity analyses for the 2001–2010 cycles, where data were available. For this specific subset, examination weights were rescaled by a factor of 5 to ensure valid population estimates (Table S4).

Data preprocessing

All analyses followed NHANES analytic guidelines [16] and accounted for the complex, multistage sampling design using strata, primary sampling units, and examination weights. For pooled 2001–2018 cycles, the 2-year MEC examination weights were divided by the number of included cycles to create the combined survey weight [16, 20]. Baseline characteristics were summarized overall and by GI cancer status using survey-weighted procedures [21]. Survey-weighted logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between HRR and prevalent GI cancers. HRR was analyzed both as a continuous variable (per 0.1-unit increase) and as tertiles, with a test for linear trend across tertiles. Three progressively adjusted models were fitted: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for sex, age, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, and PIR), and Model 3 (further adjusted for BMI, smoking, alcohol use, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, CVD, and eGFR). Sensitivity analyses were conducted in an expanded sample and additionally adjusted for CRP where available. Pre-specified subgroup analyses were performed, and effect modification was evaluated by including multiplicative interaction terms in Model 3. In parallel, eight machine-learning algorithms were trained to evaluate predictive performance for prevalent GI cancer, and discrimination was quantified by area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% CIs. AUCs were compared using DeLong’s test [22], and additional metrics including PR-AUC [23] and Brier score were reported in Table S5. Threshold-based metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, and Cohen’s kappa, were reported in Table S6. Model hyperparameters, search grids, cross-validation performance, and test-set AUCs are provided in Table S8. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) [24] were used to evaluate calibration and clinical net benefit for the top-performing models. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3), and a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Analytical procedures

All analyses accounted for the NHANES complex, multistage sampling design by incorporating the appropriate examination sample weights, strata, and primary sampling units (PSUs). Continuous variables were summarized as survey-weighted means with standard errors (SEs). Categorical variables were presented as unweighted counts (n) together with survey-weighted percentages (%), in accordance with standard NHANES reporting guidelines [16, 21]. Group differences in continuous variables (e.g., age and HRR) were assessed using design-based t tests, and differences in categorical variables were assessed using Rao–Scott chi-square tests.

The association between HRR and prevalent GI cancers was evaluated using survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression, reporting odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). To improve interpretability and robustness, HRR was analyzed in two parallel forms across all adjustment models: (i) as a continuous variable (per 0.1-unit increase), and (ii) as a categorical variable (tertiles), with tertile 1 as the reference group.

Potential non-linear associations between HRR and GI cancers were explored using restricted cubic spline (RCS) functions [25] in the fully adjusted design-based model. Because the test for non-linearity was not statistically significant, the linear term (per 0.1-unit increase in HRR) was treated as the primary effect estimate, and the spline plot was presented in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Figure S1). Effect modification was examined by adding multiplicative interaction terms (e.g., HRR × sex) into the survey-weighted model, with stratum-specific effect sizes reported when appropriate.

For component-wise analyses, HRR was modeled per 0.1-unit increase, HGB per 1-g/dL increase, and RDW per 1-percentage-point increase using the same adjustment framework. To facilitate comparison across indices with different units, per-1-SD standardized estimates were also calculated. HGB and RDW were additionally entered jointly into one fully adjusted model. HRR was not entered simultaneously with HGB and RDW because HRR is mathematically derived from these two indices.

For predictive modeling, we developed and compared eight distinct machine learning algorithms (XGBoost, logistic regression, neural network, random forest, ranger, LDA, QDA, and bagging) rather than combining them into a single ensemble system. The dataset was split into training (70%) and held-out testing (30%) sets, and the test set was kept completely isolated from model training and hyperparameter tuning. To prevent data leakage, all preprocessing steps (including imputation, scaling/standardization, and encoding of categorical variables) were performed within the resampling process of the training set using the caret workflow [26], so that preprocessing parameters were learned only from training folds and then applied to the corresponding validation folds. Hyperparameters were optimized using cross-validation combined with grid search [26]. Model performance was evaluated on the independent test set using ROC-AUC together with additional metrics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, and Cohen’s kappa), and extended metrics (e.g., PR-AUC and Brier score) were reported where applicable. A fixed random seed was used to ensure reproducibility of data splitting and model training procedures. To enhance interpretability, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were calculated (particularly for tree-based models such as XGBoost) to quantify feature contributions and visualize the direction and magnitude of each predictor’s contribution to model output for classification of prevalent GI cancer [27]. HRR tertiles were defined based on its distribution in the analytical sample: Tertile 1 (HRR ≤ 1.023), Tertile 2 (1.023 < HRR ≤ 1.160), and Tertile 3 (HRR > 1.160).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents survey-weighted baseline characteristics of the study population. A total of 39,663 participants were included, of whom 39,319 were controls without GI cancers and 344 had GI cancers. The overall and control-group HRR values appeared identical when rounded to two decimal places in the previous version; after increasing the precision to three decimal places, the corresponding survey-weighted means were 1.106 (SE = 0.002) and 1.107 (SE = 0.002), respectively. Compared with controls, participants with GI cancers had lower HGB levels (13.69 vs. 14.30 g/dL, P < 0.001), higher RDW values (13.65% vs. 13.06%, P < 0.001), and consequently lower HRR values (1.019 vs. 1.107, P < 0.001). Self-reported kidney disease was also more common among participants with GI cancers than among controls (6.8% vs. 2.3%, P < 0.001). Significant group differences were additionally observed for age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, smoking status, alcohol use, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, CVD, and eGFR (all P < 0.05). In contrast, education level, PIR, BMI, and hyperlipidemia did not differ significantly between groups (all P ≥ 0.05). GI cancer cases were site-confirmed using MCQ230A–MCQ230C, with MCQ230D additionally reviewed where available; the distribution by digestive-organ site is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

The inverse association between HRR and GI cancers

As shown in Table 2, higher HRR was inversely associated with GI cancer prevalence in survey-weighted logistic regression. After adjustment for potential confounders (Model 3), each 0.1-unit increase in HRR was associated with lower odds of GI cancers (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77–0.95; P = 0.003). When HRR was categorized into tertiles, participants in tertile 3 had significantly lower odds of GI cancers compared with tertile 1 (OR 0.57, 95% CI 0.35–0.93; P = 0.026; P for trend = 0.028), whereas the association for tertile 2 was not statistically significant.

Considering the potential heterogeneity among different gastrointestinal cancer sites, we conducted a secondary analysis specifically focusing on colorectal cancer (CRC). As shown in Supplementary Table S3, the direction of the CRC results was generally consistent with the primary findings. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), each 0.1-unit increase in HRR was associated with lower odds of prevalent CRC (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.79–0.99, P = 0.033). Compared with the lowest HRR tertile, participants in the highest tertile had lower odds of CRC (OR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.33–0.98, P = 0.044), with a marginal linear trend (P for trend = 0.064). These secondary findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the reduced number of CRC cases.

Component-wise associations of HRR, HGB, and RDW with prevalent GI cancers

To further clarify the contributions of the individual components of HRR, we separately evaluated the associations of HGB and RDW with prevalent GI cancers. In fully adjusted survey-weighted models, higher HGB was associated with lower odds of prevalent GI cancers (OR 0.835, 95% CI 0.751–0.929; P = 0.001 per 1-g/dL increase), whereas higher RDW was associated with higher odds (OR 1.112, 95% CI 1.008–1.226; P = 0.036 per 1-percentage-point increase). Standardized estimates were broadly comparable for HRR and HGB (OR 0.766 and 0.755 per 1-SD increase, respectively). When HGB and RDW were entered simultaneously into the fully adjusted model, the association for HGB remained significant (OR 0.855, 95% CI 0.762–0.960; P = 0.009), whereas the association for RDW was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (OR 1.056, 95% CI 0.943–1.183; P = 0.348). These findings suggest that the association observed for HRR is directionally consistent with its component indices, with HGB contributing substantially to the observed pattern (Supplementary Table S10).

Subgroup analysis

In survey-weighted subgroup analyses based on the fully adjusted model (Model 3), evidence of effect modification was observed by sex (P for interaction = 0.002). Specifically, higher HRR was inversely associated with prevalent GI cancers among men (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.64–0.85; P < 0.001), whereas no association was observed among women (OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.87–1.15; P = 0.995). No statistically significant interactions were identified across the remaining prespecified subgroup variables. The detailed subgroup results are presented in Table 3.

Comparative assessment of multiple ML algorithm performance

As shown in Fig. 3, the ROC curves of the eight machine-learning algorithms largely overlapped, indicating broadly comparable discrimination across models. On the independent test set, the best-performing models achieved similar AUCs, including neural network (AUC = 0.823, 95% CI: 0.790–0.857), logistic regression (AUC = 0.822, 95% CI: 0.788–0.856), and XGBoost (AUC = 0.820, 95% CI: 0.783–0.856) (Table S8). Given the close performance between XGBoost and logistic regression, DeLong’s test showed no statistically significant difference between their ROC curves (P = 0.694). To better evaluate models under the highly imbalanced outcome, extended metrics were additionally reported (Table S5). Threshold-based performance at the cutoff selected by Youden’s index is summarized in Table S6. This operating point was used as a standardized statistical threshold for model comparison rather than as a clinically prescriptive cutoff. Given the rare-outcome setting and the low precision values observed across models, any future clinical use would likely require context-specific validation and threshold selection. Model calibration and decision-curve behavior are further illustrated using calibration plots (Fig. 4A) and decision curve analysis (Fig. 4B). In an additional component-wise comparison, the HRR-only model showed AUC-based classification performance similar to HGB-only and RDW-only models, indicating that HRR did not show clear discrimination advantages over either individual component.

Model interpretability and feature contributions

To enhance interpretability of the classification models, we performed SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analyses for the tree-based model (XGBoost) to quantify feature contributions and visualize how predictors influenced the model output for prevalent GI cancer classification (Fig. 5). The SHAP summary plot indicated that age was the most influential feature, followed by HRR and several cardiometabolic and lifestyle variables (e.g., BMI, smoking status, and PIR). Higher HRR values generally contributed to lower model-estimated probability of prevalent GI cancer, which was directionally consistent with the regression results. The SHAP dependence plots further suggested a monotonic pattern whereby higher HRR tended to shift model output toward lower probability values, although individual classifications were also influenced by interactions with other features (e.g., age and comorbidities). These findings support the role of HRR as an informative classification feature within the ML framework, while emphasizing that model explanations reflect predictive structure within the observed dataset rather than causal importance.

Sensitivity analysis

In the CRP sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S4), the association between HRR and prevalent GI cancers remained directionally consistent but was attenuated and no longer statistically significant after additional adjustment for CRP (OR = 0.94, 95% CI 0.84–1.05; P = 0.245 per 0.1-unit increase in HRR).

In the multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) [28] sensitivity analysis, an expanded eligible sample (N = 45,566) was analyzed to address missing covariates. The pooled imputed analysis showed an inverse association between HRR and prevalent GI cancers (OR = 0.83, 95% CI 0.74–0.92; P = 0.001 per 0.1-unit increase in HRR; Supplementary Table S7).

In the kidney disease sensitivity analysis, Model 4 additionally adjusted for self-reported kidney disease on top of Model 3 (Model 3 + self-reported kidney disease). The association remained significant (OR = 0.858, 95% CI 0.776–0.950; P = 0.004 per 0.1-unit increase in HRR). In the restricted sample excluding participants with self-reported kidney disease, the association was also significant (OR = 0.875, 95% CI 0.788–0.971; P = 0.013; Supplementary Table S9). Overall, the sensitivity analyses generally supported the robustness of the primary findings, although the association was attenuated after additional adjustment for CRP.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to evaluate the association between the hemoglobin-to-RDW ratio (HRR) and site-confirmed gastrointestinal (GI) cancers in a nationally representative NHANES sample while also comparing multiple machine-learning algorithms for prevalent GI cancer classification. In survey-weighted logistic regression, higher HRR was inversely associated with prevalent GI cancers. In the fully adjusted model, each 0.1-unit increase in HRR was associated with lower odds of GI cancers (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.77–0.95; P = 0.003), and participants in the highest HRR tertile (HRR > 1.160) had lower odds compared with the lowest tertile (HRR ≤ 1.023) (OR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.35–0.93; P = 0.026; P for trend = 0.028). Restricted cubic spline analyses suggested an approximately linear pattern (non-linear P = 0.731), supporting the use of a linear term in the primary models. The secondary CRC analysis was directionally consistent with the primary analysis, but it should be interpreted cautiously because of the smaller site-specific case count. These findings reflect associations with prevalent, self-reported cancer history rather than temporal or causal relationships.

The component-wise analyses help interpret what the HRR signal may represent. Participants with GI cancer had lower HGB and higher RDW than controls. In separate fully adjusted models, HRR was associated with lower odds of prevalent GI cancers (OR 0.855, 95% CI 0.773–0.947; P = 0.003 per 0.1-unit increase), HGB was associated with lower odds (OR 0.835, 95% CI 0.751–0.929; P = 0.001 per 1-g/dL increase), and RDW was associated with higher odds (OR 1.112, 95% CI 1.008–1.226; P = 0.036 per 1-percentage-point increase). Standardized estimates for HRR and HGB were broadly comparable. In the joint HGB/RDW model, HGB remained significant (OR 0.855, 95% CI 0.762–0.960; P = 0.009), whereas RDW was attenuated and was not statistically significant (OR 1.056, 95% CI 0.943–1.183; P = 0.348). Thus, HRR is a convenient composite index, but the present data do not demonstrate clear superiority over HGB or RDW alone.

This finding suggests that the inverse association observed for HRR may be driven substantially by its hemoglobin component, while RDW may provide complementary information related to red-cell heterogeneity. Clinically, a lower HRR can result from reduced HGB, increased RDW, or both. In the context of GI cancers, lower HGB may reflect chronic blood loss, iron deficiency, inflammation-related impairment of erythropoiesis, nutritional deficiencies, or cancer- and treatment-related marrow suppression [5, 7–9]. Higher RDW may reflect anisocytosis associated with disordered erythropoiesis, iron-restricted red-cell production, and systemic inflammation [4–6]. Therefore, HRR should be interpreted as an integrated hematologic signal rather than as a cancer-specific biomarker. Its practical value may lie in its simplicity and ability to summarize routinely available blood-count information, rather than in superior discriminatory performance.

Subgroup analyses suggested effect modification by sex, with a statistically significant inverse association in men (OR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.64–0.85; P < 0.001) but not in women (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.87–1.15; P = 0.995), and a significant interaction by sex (P for interaction = 0.002). These sex-specific findings are exploratory and may reflect differences in baseline hematologic profiles and GI cancer epidemiology [3, 29]. Dedicated prospective studies are required to validate and mechanistically evaluate these observations. Importantly, the present cross-sectional analysis does not support fixed HRR thresholds for endoscopy or other prescriptive clinical decisions.

From a machine-learning perspective, ROC curves across the evaluated algorithms largely overlapped, and the top models showed broadly similar discrimination on the held-out test set, with no statistically significant difference between XGBoost and logistic regression by DeLong’s test [22]. HRR was an informative classification feature, and SHAP analyses helped visualize the predictive structure in the observed dataset. However, SHAP reflects model-based prediction patterns rather than causal importance [27]. HRR-only, HGB-only, and RDW-only models showed similar AUC-based discrimination, which further supports a cautious interpretation of HRR as a derived feature rather than a clearly stronger standalone marker. Because GI cancer was rare in this sample, the low PR-AUC and low precision values require cautious interpretation [23], and no prescriptive cutoff should be proposed from these exploratory classification analyses.

This study has several strengths. We leveraged population-representative NHANES data and applied survey-weighted regression to enhance generalizability. We operationalized GI cancers using site-specific follow-up variables (MCQ230A–MCQ230C), with MCQ230D additionally reviewed where available, and provided transparent site mapping and counts. We also compared HRR with its component indices and incorporated machine-learning interpretability analyses to provide complementary perspectives on the association and classification relevance of an index derived from routine blood tests.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes assessment of temporality and causality. Second, cancer history and cancer site were self-reported and may therefore be subject to recall bias and misclassification. Third, individual GI cancer sites could not be analyzed separately because of limited case counts, and heterogeneity across GI tumor types may exist. Fourth, detailed clinical information, including tumor stage and treatment status, was unavailable, and longitudinal HRR trajectories could not be assessed. Fifth, CRP was available only for selected survey cycles, and the association was attenuated and no longer statistically significant after additional CRP adjustment, limiting direct mechanistic interpretation. Sixth, kidney disease may affect both HGB and RDW and therefore represents a potential source of confounding. Although eGFR was already included in Model 3 and additional adjustment for KIQ022 and restricted analysis excluding self-reported kidney disease did not materially alter the association, residual confounding related to renal function cannot be completely excluded. Seventh, medication use, including iron supplementation and erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, may influence hematologic indices and was not fully accounted for. Finally, to maximize the identification of hyperlipidemia cases, our composite definition incorporated both non-fasting measurements (total cholesterol and HDL-C) and fasting measurements (triglycerides and LDL-C). Because the primary exposure and outcome were evaluated in the full MEC sample, we applied the standard MEC examination weights rather than fasting-subsample weights. Although this approach preserved the sample size and statistical power, minor weighting variability may remain for fasting-derived lipid components.

Conclusion

In conclusion, higher HRR was inversely associated with prevalent site-confirmed GI cancers in NHANES. HRR is an accessible derived index and functioned as an informative classification feature in machine-learning analyses, but it has not been shown to outperform HGB or RDW alone. Prospective studies and external validation are needed before clinical implementation can be considered.
