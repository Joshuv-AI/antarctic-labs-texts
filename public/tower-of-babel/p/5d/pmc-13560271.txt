3D printed nano-hydroxyapatite scaffold combined with BMSCs cell sheet containing concentrated growth factor (CGF) for enhancing bone regeneration
Authors: Qi Shao, Jianhong Shi, Jin Lu, Yuanye Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCraniofacial bone defects represent significant clinical challenges resulting from diverse etiological factors. Despite advancements in biomaterials science, autologous tissue transplantation remains the gold standard for craniofacial reconstruction due to the limitations of existing materials in replicating the complexity and functionality of native tissues.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the efficacy of combining 3D-printed nano-hydroxyapatite (n-HA) scaffolds with concentrated growth factor-enriched cell sheets (CGF-BMSCs-CS) for bone defect repair, exploring their synergistic effects on osteogenesis and tissue regeneration.MethodsBone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were utilized to fabricate CGF-enriched cell sheets. 3D-printed porous n-HA scaffolds (5 mm diameter, 1 mm thickness) were prepared and wrapped with CGF-BMSCs-CS to form composite scaffolds. Four experimental groups were established: control, n-HA alone, CGF-BMSCs-CS alone, and the composite n-HA/CGF-BMSCs-CS. Osteogenic potential was evaluated through alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity assays and quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis of osteogenesis-related gene expression. Sprague-Dawley rats were randomly assigned to these groups for a cranial defect model. Micro-computed tomography (µCT) was performed at 6 and 12 weeks post-surgery to assess bone regeneration metrics, including bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th).ResultsScanning electron microscopy (SEM) confirmed the n-HA scaffold’s uniform porosity and structural integrity. The CGF-BMSCs-CS exhibited optimal thickness and viscosity for tissue engineering applications. The composite group demonstrated significantly elevated ALP activity and upregulated expression of osteogenesis-related genes, indicating enhanced osteogenic capacity. µCT analysis revealed superior bone formation and osseointegration in the composite scaffold group compared to other groups. This was further supported by significantly higher bone mineral density (BMD) values at both time points (p < 0.05). Histological evaluation via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed increased osteocyte activity, neovascularization, and trabecular bone formation in the composite group.ConclusionsWe innovatively integrated 3D printed n-HA scaffolds with CGF BMSCs CS, which combines mechanical stability with enhanced osteogenic potential for craniofacial bone defect repair, promoting bone healing. This innovative strategy provides promising prospects for advancing bone tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Bone tissue engineering, Cell sheet, CGF, 3D printed scaffold, Osteogenesis

Introduction

Craniofacial bone defects, which frequently result from congenital malformations, trauma, tumors, or inflammatory disorders (such as cleft lip and palate, traumatic injuries, oral cancers, and periodontal diseases) [1, 2], significantly impair both the physiological functions of craniofacial bones and the aesthetic appearance of affected individuals. These defects consequently lead to a marked reduction in the quality of life for those affected. Currently, the primary approaches for bone defect repair include autologous bone transplantation, allogeneic bone transplantation, and artificial bone transplantation [3, 4]. However, the clinical application of autogenous bone grafts is still associated with several limitations and drawbacks. Additionally, both allogeneic bone transplants and artificial bone implants often induce significant immune rejection reactions in recipients. Tissue-engineered bone, which consists of porous scaffolds, seed cells, and cytokines, offers distinct advantages over other treatment modalities for managing bone defects [3, 5]. Ideal scaffolds facilitate cell adhesion, growth, and proliferation while inducing cells to secrete extracellular matrix (ECM). Over time, the ECM replaces the degraded biomaterials, ultimately forming new bone tissue to repair defective areas [4, 5]. Furthermore, scaffolds with appropriate mechanical strength provide effective structural support for bone defects while withstanding specific levels of stress. This applied stress also stimulates osteogenesis, further promoting the overall repair and regeneration of bone tissue [6, 7, 8].

Hydroxyapatite (HA), a calcium phosphate ceramic that closely resembles the composition of human bones and teeth, exhibits excellent integration with normal bone tissue in vivo [9]. Its exceptional biocompatibility and osteoconductivity enable it to induce angiogenesis, promote stem cell proliferation, and facilitate bone regeneration without triggering local or systemic toxic reactions [10]. In recent years, nanotechnology has been applied in the design and fabrication of bone scaffolds to achieve precise control over their properties [11]. Nano-hydroxyapatite (n-HA) materials are commonly used for this purpose. With particle diameters ranging from 1 to 100 nm, n-HA offers several advantages over conventional HA, including a larger surface area, enhanced stability, and the activation of bone biomarkers. Additionally, optimal porosity in n-HA scaffolds can enhance the adsorption of bioactive factors, thereby improving cell-material interactions [12]. Although synthetic bone substitutes, such as calcium phosphate, nano-hydroxyapatite (n-HA), composite bioceramics, and metallic materials, generally exhibit favorable mechanical properties, clinical trials have shown that these materials often possess limited osteoinductive capabilities. In the field of regenerative medicine, bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) have emerged as a promising cell source for tissue engineering due to their strong proliferative capacity and multilineage differentiation potential [13, 14]. The processes of cell attachment, proliferation, and differentiation, along with the formation of cellular junctions, are critical for tissue growth and repair. The cell sheet (CS) technique offers a significant advantage by eliminating the adverse effects associated with trypsin digestion while preserving the extracellular matrix (ECM), cell-cell junctions, ECM-cell interactions, and various function-related proteins such as fibronectin, integrin alpha-5, and stromal cell-derived factor 1 [15, 16]. This preservation creates a stable microenvironment that supports cell survival and maintains normal physiological functions. Fibronectin and integrin alpha-5 play pivotal roles in cell adhesion by interacting with other ECM components, enabling the CS to establish a strong bond with the surface of damaged tissue. This robust binding enhances the stability of CS at the defect site, minimizes cell loss following injection of cell suspensions, and improves therapeutic efficacy. However, this approach also has limitations, particularly regarding insufficient cell differentiation and osteogenic capacity. Moreover, the lack of reliable vascular supply poses a significant challenge in addressing large-scale bone defects [17]. Concentrated growth factor (CGF), classified as an advanced platelet-derived product, is prepared through a simplified centrifugation process without the addition of any external components [18]. CGF extracts (CGFe) are rich in various growth factors, including platelet-derived growth factors (PDGF), transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1), epidermal growth factor (EGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and fibroblast growth factor (FGF) [19, 20]. These endogenous growth factors exert synergistic and cascading effects, playing a crucial role in promoting cell proliferation, matrix remodeling, and angiogenesis, all of which are essential for effective wound healing and tissue repair [21].

In this study, we utilized CGF-enriched BMSC-CS while employing digital light processing (DLP) 3D printing technology to fabricate n-HA scaffolds which combines mechanical stability with enhanced osteogenic potential for craniofacial bone defect repair, promoting bone healing. The scaffolds were then wrapped with cell sheets, integrating the benefits of biological factors and CS technology with scaffold materials. This innovative approach provides a novel composite material for bone defect repair, leveraging the unique advantages of both biological and engineering strategies to enhance tissue regeneration outcomes.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

Male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats were purchased from the Animal Experiment Centre of the Basic Medical Sciences College of Jilin University. All experiments involving animals were approved by the Ethics Committee of Basic Medical Sciences School of Jilin University (No.2022450). This work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Preparation of CGFe

3-week-old male SD rats (50–100 g) were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital. Blood samples (5 mL) were then collected via the abdominal aorta using disposable blood collection tubes, after which the animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation to allow for the subsequent extraction of BMSCs. Immediately after collection, the blood samples were centrifuged (Medifuge™, Silfradent, Sofia, Italy) according to a specific variable-speed program: 30s of acceleration, 2 min at 2700 rpm, 4 min at 2400 rpm, 4 min at 2700 rpm, 3 min at 3000 rpm, 36s of deceleration, and then stopping. Following centrifugation, the blood separated into three distinct layers: the upper platelet-poor plasma (PPP), the middle CGF, and the lower red blood cells (RBC). Notably, the CGF layer and its junction with the RBC were carefully preserved, as previous reports have indicated that platelets, active factors, and cells are predominantly located in this region [21].

The collected CGF was rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), then frozen at 80 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, it was thawed and centrifuged at 4 °C (230 g for 5 min). Next, 5 mL of α-Modified Eagle Medium (α-MEM) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The CGF was centrifuged once more (400 g for 5 min), and 5 mL of the supernatant was collected. This supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-µm filter to obtain concentrated growth factor extracts (CGFe). Previous studies have established that a 10% concentration of CGFe is optimal for promoting the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) [22]. Consequently, a 10% CGFe concentration was selected for the subsequent experiments.

Isolation and identification of BMSCs

BMSCs were isolated following previously described protocols [14]. Following the above procedures, the rats were immersed in 75% ethanol for 10 min. Under aseptic conditions, bilateral femurs and tibias were harvested, and all muscle tissues were meticulously removed. Subsequently, the bone marrow was flushed out using Alpha Modified Eagle Medium (α-MEM, Gibco, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Biological Industries, Israel) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The resulting mixture of bone marrow and the complete medium was collected and pipetted repeatedly to ensure uniform distribution of the bone marrow tissue.

The cell suspension was then transferred to a 10-cm-diameter culture dish and incubated at 37 °C with 5%CO₂ in a humidified cell incubator to facilitate BMSC adhesion. Non-adherent cells were subsequently removed. Once the adherent cells reached a confluence of ≥ 80%, they were detached using trypsin. Typically, the third passage (P3) of stem cells was utilized for subsequent experiments and identified by provided a BMSC surface markers (positive for CD73, CD90, CD105). For further analysis, BMSCs were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6-well plates and cultured with complete medium. Osteogenic differentiation was performed over 21 days with a specific medium containing 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 10 nM dexamethasone, and 50 mg/mL ascorbic acid. Adipogenic differentiation was induced over a 3-week period with a cyclic protocol alternating between two media: Induction Medium A (200 µM indomethacin, 0.5 mM IBMX, 10 µg/mL insulin, 1 µM dexamethasone) and Induction Medium B (10 µg/mL insulin). Each cycle defined as 3 days in Medium A, 1 day in Medium B, and a return to Medium A. Calcium nodules were quantified via alizarin red staining, while intracellular lipid droplets were quantified by means of Oil red O staining and adipogenic.

Fabrication and characterization of CGF-BMSCs-CS

Fabrication

The third-passage BMSCs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 106/well. Once the cell confluence reached 70–80%, the culture medium was replaced with the cell sheet induction medium. This induction medium consisted of 10%CGFe, 50 µg/mL vitamin C (VC), and complete medium. The medium was refreshed every 2 to 3 days, and the cells were cultured continuously for 14 days. Subsequently, a white, translucent cell sheet emerged at the bottom of the well plate. The edges of the cell sheet were curled and wrinkled. The entire CGF-BMSCs-cell sheet (CGF-BMSCs-CS) could be harvested using a cell scraper.

Characterization

Once the CGF-BMSCs-CS was formed, the culture medium was carefully removed. The cell sheet was then rinsed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 4 h. After another round of rinsing with PBS, the general morphology of the cell sheet was documented through scanning. Finally, routine H&E staining was carried out to observe and record the morphological features under a microscope.

Printing of n-HA scaffold

Based on our group’s previous research, a commercial 3D printer (SU-100 A, CeraEPI, China) operating on the DLP principle was employed. The computer-Aided Design (CAD) models were crafted using SolidWorks™ software. These models were then saved as Stereolithography (STL) files, which were subsequently sliced into layers with a thickness of 20 μm, converted into a printer-compatible format, and uploaded to the printing system. Production was carried out with the following parameters: a light intensity of 2.5 mW/cm², an exposure time of 10 s and a layer thickness of 20 μm. Nano-hydroxyapatite (n-HA) powder with a purity of 97% and a particle size of D50 < 100 nm, procured from Aladdin (Shanghai, China), was combined with light-curing resin using a ceramic three-roll mill. The formulation for the 3D-printable light-curing resin, adapted from prior research, consisted of polyethylene glycol (600) dimethacrylate (PEG600DMA, 80%), γ-(Methylallyl) propyl trimethoxysilane (KH-570,15%),2,4,6-Trimethylbenzoyl-diphenyl oxide phosphine (TPO-L, 2%), and Sudan red (3%) [23]. The resultant ceramic paste was poured into the printer’s tank, and the printing process was then completed. Following a series of post-processing steps, including washing, drying, debinding, and sintering, the n-HA scaffolds were successfully obtained [24, 25]. Sterilization by ultraviolet (UV) radiation (30 min per side).

Preparation of n-HA/CGF-BMSCs-CS scaffold

The sterilized n-HA scaffolds, with a diameter of 5 mm and a thickness of 1 mm, were pre-soaked in the culture medium of a 6-well plate for 12 h. To construct the composite scaffold in vitro, microforceps were used to carefully lift the CGF-BMSC-cell sheet (CGF-BMSC-CS) and wrap it around the n-HA scaffold. Subsequently, 10 mL of α-Modified Eagle Medium (α-MEM) was added to ensure complete coverage of the composite scaffold. The assembly was then incubated in a cell incubator for an additional 12 h, in preparation for the subsequent experiments.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM) Scans

Scanning electron microscopy (TM-4000, Tianmei, China) was employed to examine the surface and internal structure of the scaffolds. The operating procedure was as follows: Initially, the surface of the scaffold was coated with gold/palladium. Subsequently, the gold-sprayed scaffolds were positioned on the workbench, and the surface of the scaffolds as well as the internal cell conditions were observed at appropriate magnifications.

Reagents

Osteogenic differentiation medium was purchased from shenggong, China; L-DMEM (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) QuantiChrom™ Alkaline Phosphatase Assay Kit, BioAssay Systems, USA.

Biosafety

To assess the cytotoxic effect of 3D n-HA scaffolds, the scaffolds were immersed in 10 mL of complete culture medium and incubated at 37 °C for 1 day, 4 days, and 7 days respectively. Following the incubation period, the scaffolds were removed, and the mixture solutions containing the scaffold eluates were collected. A total of 1 × 104/ well BMSCs were placed in 96-well plates. These cells were then cultured with complete culture medium and the three mixtures. The culture conditions were maintained consistent with those previously described. After 48 h, a cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) was utilized to measure the overall cell viability. The results obtained from the CCK-8 assay were employed to evaluate the cytotoxic effect of the scaffolds.

ALP activity

The third generation of BMSCs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 104/cells per well. The complete culture medium was replaced with various conditioned induction media: normal complete medium, n-HA scaffold eluates, cell sheet induction medium containing CGFe with or without n-HA scaffold eluates. The medium was renewed every 3 days. After 7-day, 14-day, and 21-day culturing periods, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Osteogenesis was then examined using alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and alizarin red staining. Specifically, on days 7 and 14, ALP staining was conducted using the BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase color-development kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The staining process lasted for 30 min, after which the plates were rinsed several times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For alizarin red staining, on day 21, the reagent (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added for 10 min to stain the mineralized nodules formed during osteogenic differentiation. Following this, the plates were rinsed several times with PBS to remove any residual dye. Finally, the staining results were documented through microscopic observation.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

A real-time quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) system was utilized to measure and assess the expression of osteogenesis-related genes in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). The BMSCs suspension was gently seeded drop-by-drop onto the scaffolds and incubated at 37 °C. The culture medium was refreshed every 2–3 days. After 7 days, trypsin was used to detach the cells from the scaffolds. Subsequently, TRIzol reagent (HaiGene, Harbin, China) was employed to extract total RNA. The relative expression levels of osteogenic genes, such as Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), collagen type I (COL1), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and osteocalcin (BGLAP), were evaluated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with the housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as a reference. In vivo bone regeneration in a rat skull defect model

Grouping and surgical procedures

A total of 30 SD rats (7–8 weeks old, weighing 250–300 g), were randomly assigned to five groups by using the online service https://www.random.org/ (n = 6 per group): Control, CGF-BMSCs-cell sheet (CGF-BMSCs-CS), nano-hydroxyapatite (n-HA), n-HA/CGF, and n-HA/CGF-BMSCs-CS. The sample size was determined based on reference with similar in vivo bone regeneration experiments [26–27]. All surgical procedures were meticulously executed by the same experienced surgeon within a strictly sterile operating room environment. The rats were anesthetized via intravenous administration of 3% pentobarbital sodium at a dosage of 30 mg/kg. Post-disinfection, the muscle tissues and periosteum were carefully dissected to expose the cranial surface. Utilizing a surgical hollow trephine drill, a precisely measured full-layer circular bone defect, with a diameter of 5 mm and a thickness of 1 mm, was created bilaterally along the middle cranial suture. Throughout the operative procedure, normal saline was continuously infused to irrigate the surgical site and maintain appropriate thermoregulation. The corresponding materials specific to each experimental group were carefully implanted into the bone defects, followed by meticulous suturing of the dissected tissues. In the post-operative period, the rats were administered subcutaneous injections of penicillin (160,000 units/kg) for a consecutive three-day duration to prevent potential infections. At the 6-week time point, 15 rats were humanely sacrificed for evaluation (3 rats from each of the 5 groups), and at the 12-week time point, the remaining 15 rats humanely sacrificed for evaluation (3 rats from each of the 5 groups). The skull specimens were then promptly retrieved and immersed in 4% neutral polyformaldehyde for subsequent histological, morphological, and biochemical analyses, as part of the comprehensive assessment of bone regeneration and tissue repair processes.

µCT measurement and analysis

Fixed skull specimens were scanned using micro-computed tomography (µCT, PerkinElmer Quantum GX2, USA) at 90 kV source voltage, 88 µA beam current, and a resolution of 25 μm. Three-dimensional reconstruction was achieved using CTvol software (Bruker Micro-CT, Germany). The area of the scaffold (D: 5 mm, H: 1.5 mm) was identified as the region of interest (ROI). Then a quantitative analysis of new bone was conducted, and the bone volume/total volume (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) and trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) were calculated. Specifically, Micro-CT scanning was performed on cell-free blank n-HA scaffolds identical to those used in the experimental groups (same composition, porosity, and dimensions) under identical scanning parameters. Using CTAn software, voxel-wise background subtraction was applied by subtracting the blank scaffold’s grayscale value distribution from experimental group datasets, enabling the accurate isolation and quantification of pure bone tissue independently of scaffold radiopacity.

Histological analysis

The skull specimens fixed in 4% neutral paraformaldehyde were soaked and embedded in paraffin after decalcification in 10% EDTA, dehydration in gradient ethanol and made transparent in xylene. And then they were sliced into 4 μm thick sections for histological analysis. The slices were stained with H&E staining to observe the microstructure and analyze the osteogenic effect in vivo. All the staining above was observed with an optical microscope.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was repeated at least three times. All quantitative data obtained were expressed as mean ± SD and GraphPad Prism software (9.0) was used for statistical analysis. An independent-samples t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to calculate significant differences. p < 0.05 was assumed to be statistically significant.

Morphology and biosafety of n-HA scaffold

The macropores of n-HA scaffold observed by SEM were interconnected and have a diameter of approximate 500 μm, which is believed to be conducive to bone regeneration, as pore diameter above 100 μm allows cell adhesion and proliferation, and nutrient transport. The cells in n-HA/CGF-BMSCs-CS scaffold were observed to extend and adhere effectively on the scaffold surface and grow well. At the same time, a large number of ECM was synthesized, closely connected cells and almost covered the whole surface of the scaffolds (Fig. 3A). In CCK-8 experiment (Fig. 3B), the OD values of n-HA group are increasing over time compared with control group, indicating that the n-HA scaffold was biocompatible and had no toxic effect on cells.

In Fig. 4B, Alizarin Red staining—which reflects extracellular matrix mineralization during osteogenic differentiation rather than gene-level expression—provides qualitative evidence that the n-HA/CGF-BMSCs-CS group may promote mineralized nodule formation.

Discussion

Bone defects caused by congenital malformations, trauma, tumors, or inflammation pose significant challenges for craniofacial bone repair due to their impact on both biological function and aesthetic appearance. While autologous bone transplantation remains the gold standard for treating bone defects due to its superior osteogenic properties, it is hampered by limitations such as donor site morbidity and insufficient graft supply. To address these challenges, we developed a novel composite scaffold combining CS (collagen scaffold), BMSCs (bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells), and n-HA (nano-hydroxyapatite), which integrates the advantages of these components to provide a comprehensive solution for bone regeneration.

Our experimental results demonstrated that the nHA/CGF-BMSCs-CS composite scaffold exhibited excellent biocompatibility, as evidenced by SEM imaging showing uniform pore architecture and good interconnectivity. This structure facilitated stem cell distribution and nutrient exchange, promoting cell growth and extracellular matrix (ECM) synthesis. Furthermore, in vitro toxicity tests confirmed that n-HA scaffolds were non-cytotoxic, aligning with previous studies [28–30]. The ECM secreted by cells on the scaffold surface formed a cohesive structure, further enhancing osteogenic potential [31, 32].

The osteogenic capacity of the composite scaffold was comprehensively evaluated through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. ALP activity, an early marker of cell mineralization, was significantly higher in the composite scaffold group compared to other groups, indicating enhanced osteogenic differentiation. Alizarin red staining revealed a increase in calcium nodules, suggesting that nHA/CGF-BMSCs-CS scaffolds possess strong osteogenic induction capability. However, Alizarin Red staining performed in the presence of a calcium-rich scaffold is controversial, as it cannot fully represent or quantify the actual mineral deposition within the extracellular matrix. Accordingly, this qualitative approach alone is insufficient for accurate and reliable quantification of osteogenic mineralization. Additionally, the expression of osteoblastic marker mRNAs, including Col I, ALP, OCN, and Runx-2, was significantly upregulated, confirming the scaffold’s ability to promote bone regeneration across all stages of osteogenesis [33–35].

In vivo experiments further validated the efficacy of the composite scaffold. H&E staining and 3D reconstruction demonstrated superior bone-scaffold integration, with new bone formation observed on and around the scaffolds. Micro-CT analysis revealed significant improvements in bone volume (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), thickness (Tb.Th), and spacing (Tb.Sp) compared to other groups, further confirming the scaffold’s effectiveness in promoting bone regeneration.

The integration of CS, BMSCs, and n-HA offers several advantages over traditional approaches [36]. Unlike enzymatic digestion methods that disrupt the ECM and intercellular connections, CS preserves these critical components while enhancing cell survival rates [37, 38]. The use of 3D printing technology ensures precise control over scaffold architecture, mimicking natural bone morphology and providing enhanced surface area for cell attachment and proliferation. Additionally, the high concentration of bioactive factors in CGF significantly promotes ECM synthesis and osteogenesis.

However, this study is not without limitations. Adult BMSCs, which are more relevant for clinical translation, if our research findings are beneficial for juveniles, we will continue to study adult or elderly mice in subsequent research. While the composite scaffold demonstrates excellent biocompatibility and osteogenic potential, its mechanical properties remain a concern, restricting its use to filler materials rather than load-bearing applications. Especially when compared to metal materials such as titanium mesh that have already been applied in clinical treatment [39]. Future studies should focus on improving the mechanical strength of the scaffold to expand its clinical applications. Notably, the current composite system lacks a well-designed controlled-release mechanism for bioactive factors, which represents a significant limitation of the present study. The release of bioactive components in this work is mainly achieved via passive diffusion, without specific structural or chemical design to achieve programmable, sustained, or stimuli-responsive release. Therefore, the local concentration and duration of bioactive factor delivery cannot be precisely regulated. This limitation should be taken into consideration when interpreting the therapeutic outcomes, and the present strategy does not aim to achieve precise controlled-release functions. Future investigations will focus on the optimization of scaffold composition and microstructure to establish a more reliable and tunable controlled-release system, thereby further improving the efficacy of bone regeneration.

Our novel nHA/CGF-BMSCs-CS composite scaffold represents a significant advancement in bone tissue engineering, offering improved biocompatibility, osteogenic potential, and architectural precision compared to traditional methods. While further refinements are needed to address its mechanical limitations, this approach provides a promising solution for addressing the challenges of bone defect repair.

Conclusions

In summary, CGF-BMSCs-CS is a material with strong osteogenic ability but poor mechanical properties. The combination application of CGF-BMSCs-CS and 3D-n-HA scaffold combines the advantages of their, and a large number of growth factors released by CGF also play an important role in the osteogenic microenvironment. Therefore, the overall osteogenic performance is good and has great application potential, which provides a new method and idea for bone defect repair.
