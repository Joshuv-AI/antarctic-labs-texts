Exploring the role of IGHV3-30 in the immune microenvironment and prognosis of uterine endometrial cancer
Authors: Ling Shi, Yaoguo Mei, Ping Jiang, Rui Chen, Yifei Wang, Daosheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveUterine endometrial cancer (UCEC) is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in women, with a complex immune microenvironment influencing tumor progression and prognosis. This study aims to evaluate the prognostic significance of immune scores in UCEC, characterize immune cell infiltration patterns, and investigate the role of IGHV3-30 in the immune microenvironment and its association with clinical outcomes.MethodsWe performed bioinformatics analyses using TCGA data from 541 UCEC patients. Patients were stratified into high- and low-immune score groups based on optimal cutoffs determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Immune microenvironment profiling was conducted using CIBERSORT and ESTIMATE algorithms. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified and analyzed for their association with immune cell infiltration, tumor stage, and prognosis. Somatic mutation analysis was also conducted to examine the relationship between IGHV3-30 expression and genetic alterations.ResultsHigh immune scores were significantly associated with favorable overall survival in UCEC patients, and decreased with advancing tumor stage.High expression of IGHV3-30 was significantly associated with favorable overall survival (OS) in UCEC patients. IGHV3-30 was identified at the intersection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially mutated genes (DMGs) between immune groups.Additionally, IGHV3-30 expression correlated positively with the infiltration of immune cells such as M1 macrophages and plasma cells, and negatively with memory B cells and activated dendritic cells. Mutation analysis revealed that IGHV3-30 expression was linked to somatic mutations in key tumor suppressor genes.ConclusionImmune scores serve as valuable prognostic indicators in UCEC. IGHV3-30, predominantly expressed in B-cell lineages, demonstrates potential as a prognostic biomarker associated with favorable outcomes and distinct immune infiltration patterns. These findings provide insights for future immunotherapy strategies, though experimental validation is warranted.Keywords: Uterine endometrial cancer, Immune microenvironment, IGHV3-30, Immune infiltration, Prognostic biomarker, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Uterine endometrial cancer (UCEC) is one of the most common malignant tumors of the female reproductive system, with its incidence steadily increasing in recent years, particularly among women over 50 years old [29]. According to global cancer statistics, both the incidence and mortality rates of UCEC continue to rise, making it one of the leading causes of death among reproductive system cancers [3]. Despite advances in early diagnosis and treatment, the overall prognosis for UCEC patients remains highly variable, particularly in advanced stages, where survival is typically short and treatment responses are poor [8, 22]. Therefore, early screening, precision therapy, and prognosis assessment for UCEC are critical.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a key factor in tumorigenesis, progression, metastasis, and immune therapy responses. It consists of immune cells, stromal cells, blood vessels, and extracellular matrix, all of which play essential roles in tumor growth and metastasis [37]. Immune cells in the TME contribute not only to tumor immune evasion but also to tumor progression by altering the metabolic environment [10]. Recent studies have shown that immune cell infiltration characteristics in the TME are closely linked to tumor malignancy, metastasis risk, and patient survival prognosis [42]. The immune microenvironment of UCEC is complex, and the infiltration patterns and quantities of immune cells have significant implications for tumor immune evasion and treatment response [9]. Notably, immune scoring systems such as ESTIMATE have emerged as valuable tools for quantifying immune cell infiltration and predicting clinical outcomes in various malignancies. However, the prognostic significance of immune scores in UCEC and their relationship with specific immune cell populations remain to be systematically investigated.

Advancements in understanding immune cells' roles in the TME have led to the discovery of several immune-related genes that are closely associated with tumor development and prognosis [2]. Abnormal expression of these genes in UCEC's immune microenvironment is associated with tumor cell proliferation, metastasis, and immune evasion [6]. Notably, immune-related genes such as PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4 have garnered significant attention for their roles in regulating immune responses and immune evasion in UCEC [26, 32]. However, how immune-related genes modulate immune responses in the TME, influence UCEC progression, and affect patient prognosis still requires further research. Thus, exploring novel immune-related genes and understanding their roles in UCEC's immune microenvironment is a key objective of this study.

Among the numerous immune-related genes, IGHV3-30, a member of the immunoglobulin superfamily, has shown potential in immune responses in other cancers. Elevated expression of IGHV3-30 in multiple myeloma and other malignancies is linked to immune cell infiltration and immune evasion [24]. In multiple myeloma, IGHV3-30 gene usage has been associated with distinct clinical phenotypes and serves as a marker for B-cell clonal evolution [24]. The immunoglobulin heavy chain variable region encoded by IGHV3-30 participates in antigen recognition and antibody diversification through somatic hypermutation, a process critical for adaptive immune responses. Furthermore, IGHV3-30-expressing B cells demonstrate preferential expansion in certain autoimmune conditions, suggesting its involvement in immune regulation beyond conventional antibody production. Given the established role of B-cell infiltration in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment, we hypothesized that IGHV3-30 expression may similarly influence immune cell composition and clinical outcomes in UCEC [4, 18, 19]. While CD163, a well-established M2 macrophage marker, was also identified in our preliminary screening, we focused on IGHV3-30 due to its relatively unexplored role in solid tumors and its potential to reveal novel insights into B-cell-mediated immune regulation in UCEC. However, the role of IGHV3-30 in the immune microenvironment of UCEC remains unclear. It should be noted that IGHV3-30, as an immunoglobulin gene, is predominantly expressed by B-cell populations rather than tumor cells themselves. Therefore, its expression levels in bulk tumor samples likely reflect the extent of B-cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment. Investigating the expression of IGHV3-30 and its relationship with immune cell infiltration in UCEC will provide insights into its potential function and offer new targets for immunotherapy.

This study aims to: (1) evaluate the prognostic significance of immune scores derived from ESTIMATE algorithm in UCEC patients; (2) characterize the immune cell infiltration patterns associated with different immune score groups using CIBERSORT deconvolution; and (3) investigate the expression and prognostic value of IGHV3-30, a B-cell lineage marker identified at the intersection of differentially expressed and differentially mutated genes. Through comprehensive bioinformatics analyses of TCGA data, we seek to elucidate the relationship between immune microenvironment features and clinical outcomes, thereby providing potential biomarkers for prognosis assessment in UCEC.

Methods

Data source and database link

We obtained the transcriptomic data (RNA-seq), clinical data, and somatic mutation data of 541 UCEC patients from the TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA-UCEC). Clinical variables included age, tumor stage (FIGO staging system), histological subtype, and overall survival status. These data are publicly available and do not require additional ethics approval as per TCGA data access policies. For immune scoring, we used the ESTIMATE algorithm, which calculates immune, stromal, and ESTIMATE scores based on transcriptomic data. These scores reflect the degree of immune cell infiltration, stromal component content, and the combined level of immune and stromal components in the tumor microenvironment. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0), with a significance threshold set at P < 0.05. The ESTIMATE algorithm was applied to normalized RNA-seq data (log2-transformed TPM values) using the 'estimate' R package (version 1.0.13). This algorithm generates three scores: Immune Score (reflecting immune cell infiltration), Stromal Score (reflecting stromal cell presence), and ESTIMATE Score (combining both components). Higher scores indicate greater proportions of the respective cell types in the tumor microenvironment.

Immune microenvironment analysis

We used the CIBERSORT algorithm to analyze the immune microenvironment of UCEC transcriptomic data from the TCGA database. CIBERSORT is a deconvolution algorithm based on transcriptomic data that quantifies the relative abundance of 22 immune cell subtypes in samples. We used this algorithm to evaluate immune cell infiltration in UCEC samples and performed correlation analysis with clinical pathological features, such as tumor stage and immune score. Patients were stratified into high- and low-immune score groups based on the optimal cutoff value determined by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, using overall survival as the outcome variable. The cutoff value that maximized the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity—1) was selected for group stratification. All analyses were conducted using R 4.2.0, and the `estimate` and `CIBERSORT` packages were used for data processing and calculation. CIBERSORT analysis was performed with 1000 permutations. Samples with CIBERSORT p-value > 0.05 were excluded from subsequent immune cell infiltration analyses to ensure result reliability. The LM22 signature matrix, which contains 547 genes distinguishing 22 human immune cell phenotypes, was used as the reference.

Gene expression analysis

Gene expression data were analyzed for differential expression using the `limma` package. The TCGA UCEC transcriptomic data were first normalized using the DESeq2 package. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the `limma` package with a significance threshold set at |log2FC|> 1 and a corrected P-value < 0.05. These DEGs were further subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses to reveal their potential roles in the immune microenvironment.

Immune cell infiltration analysis and correlation assessment

We used the CIBERSORT algorithm to quantify immune cell infiltration in UCEC samples and performed correlation analysis with clinical pathological features. We also analyzed the correlation between immune cell subtypes and IGHV3-30 gene expression. Immune cell subtypes, including but not limited to T cells, B cells, and macrophages, were analyzed using Spearman rank correlation to assess the relationship between various immune cells and IGHV3-30 expression.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 and R 4.2.0. For normally distributed data, independent t-tests were used for group comparisons. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis H tests were performed. Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, with differences between groups evaluated using the Log-rank test. Follow-up time was censored at 10 years (3650 days) due to the limited number of patients at risk beyond this timepoint, which could lead to unreliable survival estimates. Univariate Cox regression analysis was first performed to identify potential prognostic factors. Variables with p < 0.1 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate Cox regression to evaluate the independent prognostic value of IGHV3-30 expression and immune scores, adjusting for clinicopathological confounders including age, tumor stage (FIGO), and histological subtype. The correlation between immune cell infiltration and clinical features was assessed using Spearman rank or Pearson correlation coefficients, with all statistical tests set to a significance level of P < 0.05.

Results

Correlation between high immune score and clinical features of UCEC

We first analyzed the relationship between immune scores, stromal scores, ESTIMATE scores, and overall survival (OS) in UCEC patients. The results showed that high immune score groups were significantly associated with longer OS. Specifically, patients with higher ESTIMATE scores demonstrated better survival outcomes (Figs. 1A–C). Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis further confirmed that the survival curve of the high immune score group was significantly higher than that of the low immune score group. These findings suggest that immune scores provide valuable prognostic information for UCEC patients, with high immune scores indicating longer survival. We also analyzed the correlation between immune scores, stromal scores, ESTIMATE scores, and UCEC tumor stage. We observed a trend of decreasing scores with advancing tumor stage. Specifically, the Immune Score was significantly lower in Stage II compared to Stage I (p < 0.05). However, the differences between other stage comparisons (Stage I vs. III, Stage I vs. IV, Stage II vs. III, etc.) did not reach statistical significance for all three scores. These findings suggest that immune microenvironment alterations may occur early in UCEC progression. The high immune score group showed better prognosis in clinical stages I and II, while patients with low immune scores had poorer outcomes (Figs. 1D–F).

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis of immune scores, stromal scores, and ESTIMATE scores in UCEC patients, and correlation analysis with clinical staging A–C: Kaplan–Meier survival analysis of immune scores, stromal scores, and ESTIMATE scores. D–F: Correlation analysis of scores with clinical staging

It should be noted that advanced-stage UCEC (Stage III-IV) includes a higher proportion of serous and high-grade tumors, which are known to have lower immune infiltration and distinct molecular profiles compared to endometrioid subtypes. Therefore, the observed score differences may partially reflect tumor subtype heterogeneity rather than stage-specific immune changes alone.

Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Using the optimal threshold for immune scores based on ROC analysis, we identified 363 DEGs, of which 356 were upregulated in the high immune score group, and 7 were downregulated in the low immune score group (Figs. 2A and 2B). GO analysis revealed that upregulated genes in the high-immune group were primarily involved in leukocyte-mediated immunity, immunoglobulin complexes, and antigen binding (Fig. 2C), consistent with enhanced adaptive immune responses. KEGG enrichment analysis showed significant enrichment in cell adhesion molecules (facilitating immune cell trafficking into the tumor microenvironment), hematopoietic cell lineage (reflecting immune cell diversity), and Th17 cell differentiation (Fig. 2D). These pathway enrichments suggest that high immune scores in UCEC are associated with active immune surveillance mechanisms, which may contribute to the favorable prognosis observed in this group.

Immune cell infiltration in UCEC

To investigate the immune cell infiltration in UCEC patients, we divided the samples into high-immune and low-immune groups based on immune scores. The immune cell proportions in the two groups are shown in Fig. 3A, with boxplots in Figs. 3B and 3C. Interestingly, in the low-immune group, the relative proportions of CD8 + T cells, activated memory CD4 + T cells, follicular helper T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) were significantly higher than those in the high-immune group (Figs. 3C and 3D). The heatmap visualization (Fig. 3D) illustrates the distinct immune cell composition patterns between the two groups, with hierarchical clustering revealing that samples within each group share similar immune profiles, supporting the biological relevance of our immune score-based stratification. This seemingly paradoxical finding can be explained by CIBERSORT's output representing relative proportions (summing to 100%) rather than absolute cell counts. In the low-immune group, reduced overall immune infiltration results in a smaller denominator, thereby inflating the relative proportions of certain cell types. Additionally, the elevated Treg proportion suggests an immunosuppressive microenvironment that may functionally impair the anti-tumor activity of infiltrating T cells. Conversely, in the high immune group, the proportions of memory B cells, naïve CD4 + T cells, resting NK cells, monocytes, M2 macrophages, activated dendritic cells, resting mast cells, and neutrophils were significantly higher than those in the low immune group.

Analysis of somatic mutations in high and low immune groups

To further investigate the relationship between immune microenvironment and somatic mutations in UCEC, we performed somatic mutation analysis on TCGA UCEC samples. We found that PTEN, PIK3CA, ARID1A, TTN, PIK3R1, TP53, and KMT2D exhibited the highest mutation frequencies in both groups (Figs. 4A and 4B). We also analyzed the intersection of differentially mutated genes (DMGs) and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups, and identified two genes: IGHV3-30 and CD163 (Fig. 4C). The mutation frequency of IGHV3-30 differed significantly between the two groups (Figs. 4D and 4E). It is important to note that IGHV3-30, as an immunoglobulin gene, undergoes somatic hypermutation (SHM) as part of normal B-cell affinity maturation. These frequently mutated genes have established roles in UCEC pathogenesis and immune modulation. PTEN loss activates the PI3K/AKT pathway, which has been associated with reduced T-cell infiltration and immunotherapy resistance. PIK3CA mutations may alter cytokine production and immune cell recruitment. TP53 mutations, characteristic of serous UCEC subtypes, are associated with increased genomic instability and higher tumor mutational burden, potentially influencing neoantigen generation and immune recognition. Therefore, the "mutations" detected in IGHV3-30 likely represent physiological SHM events in tumor-infiltrating B cells rather than oncogenic driver mutations. The higher IGHV3-30 mutation frequency in the low-immune group may reflect altered B-cell dynamics or clonal expansion patterns in these tumors.

Correlation between IGHV3-30 expression and UCEC patient survival

We further analyzed the relationship between IGHV3-30 expression and the survival of UCEC patients. We found that high expression of IGHV3-30 was significantly associated with better prognosis. Specifically, patients with high expression of IGHV3-30 had a significantly longer survival compared to those with low expression (Fig. 5A). Additionally, IGHV3-30 expression was higher in the normal group than in the tumor group (Fig. 5B). This finding is consistent with the B-cell origin of IGHV3-30 expression. Normal endometrial tissue contains resident immune cells including B cells, while tumor development may disrupt normal tissue architecture and reduce B-cell infiltration. The lower IGHV3-30 expression in tumors likely reflects reduced B-cell presence rather than tumor cell-intrinsic downregulation. IGHV3-30 expression showed a trend of decrease with advancing tumor stage, with the most significant reduction observed between Stage I and Stage II (Fig. 5C). These results suggest that IGHV3-30 expression, as a surrogate marker for B-cell infiltration, may serve as a prognostic indicator in UCEC. The association between high IGHV3-30 expression and favorable outcomes likely reflects the beneficial role of B-cell-mediated immune responses in tumor surveillance.

Correlation between IGHV3-30 expression and immune cell infiltration

Using the CIBERSORT algorithm, we analyzed the relationship between IGHV3-30 expression and immune cell infiltration. We found that high IGHV3-30 expression was positively correlated with the infiltration of Macrophages M1 (r = 0.336), Plasma cells (r = 0.447), CD4 + memory T cells (r = 0.373), and CD8 + T cells (r = 0.344), while it was negatively correlated with memory B cells (r = − 0.203), activated dendritic cells (r = − 0.335), and activated NK cells (r = − 0.218) (Figs. 5D and 5E). These results suggest that IGHV3-30 expression may influence UCEC’s immune microenvironment by modulating the activity and infiltration of specific immune cells, further highlighting its potential in immunotherapy. We also analyzed single-cell datasets of UCEC patients, where high IGHV3-30 expression was observed in B cells, T cells, and endothelial cells. Additionally, in another UCEC single-cell dataset, IGHV3-30 expression was found to be associated with monocytes, macrophages, and plasma cell-like dendritic cells (Fig. 5F). The negative correlation between IGHV3-30 expression and memory B cells warrants explanation. While IGHV3-30 is a B-cell marker, its expression is most prominent in actively differentiating B cells and plasma cells rather than memory B cells. The positive correlation with plasma cells (r = 0.447) suggests that high IGHV3-30 expression reflects active humoral immune responses with B-cell differentiation toward antibody-secreting cells, rather than the maintenance of memory B-cell populations.

Discussion

This study represents the first investigation of the significant correlation between immune scores and the overall survival (OS) of UCEC patients. High immune scores were found to be significantly associated with longer OS, confirming that immune scores could serve as an important prognostic marker for UCEC. Similar findings have been observed in other cancers, where high immune scores correlate with better clinical outcomes [13, 33]. However, the immune microenvironment of UCEC may differ from other cancers, particularly regarding immune tolerance. This study indicates that immune scores not only serve as prognostic markers for UCEC but also provide a new perspective for immunotherapy.In our analysis of the correlation between immune scores, stromal scores, ESTIMATE scores, and clinical pathological features, we observed a trend of decreasing scores with advancing tumor stage. Notably, the Immune Score showed a significant decrease between Stage I and Stage II (p = 0.046), although differences between other stage comparisons did not reach statistical significance. Patients with higher immune scores generally had better outcomes. It should be noted that advanced-stage UCEC (Stage III-IV) includes a higher proportion of serous and high-grade tumors with distinct molecular profiles, which may partially account for the observed score variations.These results are consistent with findings in other cancer types, where high immune scores correlate with lower disease stages [5, 27]. These observations suggest that alterations in the immune microenvironment are closely related to UCEC progression, and immune scores may be a useful tool for predicting patient prognosis.

The identification of 363 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the high-immune score group revealed their involvement in immune responses, immunoglobulin complexes, and antigen binding, consistent with previous studies showing the critical role of immune receptor pathways in tumor development [41]. KEGG pathway enrichment further highlighted the importance of these pathways in the UCEC immune microenvironment. The enrichment of cell adhesion molecules and hematopoietic cell lineage pathways suggests active immune cell trafficking and diversity in high-immune tumors, which may contribute to enhanced tumor surveillance and the favorable prognosis observed in this group.

The results of our study reveal distinct immune cell infiltration profiles in UCEC patients, classified into high-immune and low-immune groups based on immune scores. In the low-immune group, we observed significantly higher proportions of CD8 + T cells, activated memory CD4 + T cells, follicular helper T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) compared to the high-immune group. This finding contrasts with studies in other cancers, where CD8 + T cells and activated CD4 + T cells are often associated with better clinical outcomes [11, 28]. The high levels of Tregs in the low-immune group might suggest a potential immunosuppressive microenvironment in UCEC, a phenomenon that has been well documented in various malignancies [1, 35, 36]. This seemingly paradoxical finding can be explained by the nature of CIBERSORT output, which represents relative proportions (summing to 100%) rather than absolute cell counts. In the low-immune group, reduced overall immune infiltration results in a smaller denominator, thereby inflating the relative proportions of certain cell types even when their absolute numbers may be lower. Tregs are known to promote immune evasion by suppressing anti-tumor immune responses, and their increased presence in the low-immune group might explain the poorer prognosis observed in this subgroup [20, 30].

Conversely, in the high-immune group, there was a higher proportion of memory B cells, naïve CD4 + T cells, resting NK cells, monocytes, M2 macrophages, activated dendritic cells, resting mast cells, and neutrophils. Previous studies have shown that the infiltration of B cells and NK cells in the tumor microenvironment often correlates with improved prognosis in various cancers, including colorectal cancer and neuroblastoma [12, 31, 38]. The higher proportions of memory B cells in the high-immune group could suggest an enhanced humoral immune response, which is known to be crucial for tumor surveillance [16]. Similarly, NK cells, which play a key role in the innate immune response against tumors, are often implicated in preventing metastasis and promoting better survival outcomes [17]. Their higher levels in the high-immune group might therefore contribute to the improved prognosis in these patients.

Furthermore, the increased proportions of monocytes, M2 macrophages, activated dendritic cells, and neutrophils in the high-immune group provide further evidence of a dynamic immune landscape in the high-immune microenvironment. M2 macrophages are typically associated with immune suppression, tissue repair, and cancer progression [21, 23, 35, 36, 39]. However, the presence of activated dendritic cells and neutrophils, which are involved in antigen presentation and immune surveillance, suggests a complex interplay between different immune cell types that could support tumor recognition and elimination [7, 21, 23]. The recruitment of these cells into the high-immune group may reflect an effective anti-tumor immune response, potentially influencing the overall clinical outcome.

The contrasting immune profiles in the high-immune and low-immune groups provide valuable insights into the immune mechanisms underlying UCEC progression. Our findings highlight the importance of the immune microenvironment in determining tumor behavior and prognosis. Several studies have shown that a high degree of immune cell infiltration, especially by cytotoxic T cells, NK cells, and dendritic cells, is linked to favorable outcomes in cancers such as breast and ovarian cancers [40]. On the other hand, the prevalence of immunosuppressive cells, including Tregs and M2 macrophages, is often associated with poor prognosis, as seen in UCEC and other solid tumors [15, 34].

A key finding of this study is the identification of IGHV3-30 as a potential prognostic marker in UCEC. It is important to acknowledge that IGHV3-30, as an immunoglobulin heavy chain variable region gene, is predominantly expressed by B-cell lineages rather than tumor cells themselves. Therefore, IGHV3-30 expression in bulk tumor samples serves as a surrogate marker for B-cell infiltration. We observed that high expression of IGHV3-30 was positively correlated with the infiltration of plasma cells (r = 0.447) and M1 macrophages (r = 0.336), and was significantly associated with better prognosis in UCEC patients. This association likely reflects the beneficial role of B-cell-mediated humoral immunity and antigen presentation in anti-tumor responses. Previous studies have also indicated the importance of immunoglobulin genes in immune responses, especially in immune cell infiltration and tumor immune evasion [14, 25]. Our results provide new evidence of IGHV3-30's role in UCEC and suggest that it may modulate immune cell functions to influence the tumor immune microenvironment.

Moreover, we observed that IGHV3-30 expression showed a trend of decrease with advancing tumor stage, with the most significant reduction between Stage I and Stage II. As IGHV3-30 expression reflects B-cell infiltration, this finding suggests that B-cell-mediated immunity may be progressively attenuated during UCEC progression. The reduced B-cell presence in advanced tumors could result from multiple factors, including altered chemokine gradients, physical barriers within the tumor stroma, or immunosuppressive signals from the tumor microenvironment.This discovery provides a theoretical basis for future targeted therapies focused on IGHV3-30.

The molecular mechanisms underlying the association between IGHV3-30 expression and immune cell infiltration warrant further investigation. B cells expressing IGHV3-30 may contribute to the tumor microenvironment through multiple pathways: (1) antigen presentation to T cells via MHC class II molecules, facilitating adaptive immune responses; (2) production of tumor-specific antibodies that enable antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) by NK cells and macrophages; and (3) secretion of cytokines such as IL-10 and TNF-alpha that modulate the local immune milieu. The positive correlation between IGHV3-30 and M1 macrophages suggests potential crosstalk between B cells and macrophage polarization, possibly through IFN-gamma signaling or immune complex-mediated activation. Future studies employing single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics would be valuable to delineate these interactions at higher resolution.

In conclusion, our findings present new targets and biomarkers for immunotherapy, particularly for immune score and IGHV3-30 expression, which have significant clinical potential. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, all data were derived from the TCGA database, and external validation using independent cohorts (such as GEO datasets or institutional data) would strengthen the generalizability of our findings. Second, the lack of direct experimental validation with cell models and animal studies limits our understanding of the causal relationships between IGHV3-30 expression and immune cell infiltration. Third, IGHV3-30 expression in bulk RNA-seq data reflects B-cell content rather than tumor cell-intrinsic expression, and single-cell analyses would be needed to further characterize its cellular origin and function. Fourth, while we identified associations between immune scores and clinical outcomes, the retrospective nature of TCGA data precludes assessment of treatment effects on these relationships. Future research should involve prospective clinical validation, functional experiments to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of B-cell-mediated immunity in UCEC, and investigation of IGHV3-30 as a potential biomarker for immunotherapy response.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the prognostic significance of immune scores in UCEC, highlighting their potential as biomarkers for patient survival. High immune scores, as determined by the ESTIMATE algorithm, are significantly associated with improved overall survival, underscoring the critical role of the immune microenvironment in UCEC outcomes. Our findings reveal distinct immune cell infiltration profiles between high- and low-immune groups, with CIBERSORT analysis demonstrating contrasting immune landscapes that correlate with clinical outcomes. Notably, we identify IGHV3-30, a B-cell lineage marker, as a potential prognostic indicator in UCEC. High IGHV3-30 expression correlates with favorable prognosis and increased infiltration of plasma cells and M1 macrophages, suggesting that B-cell-mediated immunity may play a beneficial role in tumor surveillance. However, these findings are based on computational analyses of TCGA data, and experimental validation is warranted to establish causal relationships and explore the therapeutic potential of targeting the immune microenvironment in UCEC.
