Functional crosstalk between the vanilloid and endocannabinoid systems in modulating vascular tone: implications for (neuro)vascular disorder therapy
Authors: Eduardo Rivera-Mancilla, Antoon van den Bogaerdt, AH Jan Danser, Antoinette MaassenVanDenBrink
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe endocannabinoid system (ECS) exerts its effects through cannabinoid (CB) receptors and/or transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channels. Additionally, capsaicin, a TRPV1 agonist, can also activate CB receptors to modulate vascular tone and pain pathways. As capsaicin-induced vasodilation appears to be mediated by TRPV1-independent mechanisms, we investigated whether capsaicin exerts vascular modulation through the activation of CB receptors, and the possible role of TRPV1 as a modulator of the ECS.MethodsHuman coronary arteries (HCAs; males, n = 16; 53 ± 4 years; females, n = 14; 56 ± 2 years) were used to evaluate: (i) the responses to capsaicin in the absence or presence of the antagonists capsazepine (TRPV1), AM6545 (CB1 receptor), AM630 (CB2 receptor), O-1918 (putative endothelial CB receptor) or cannabidiol (GPR55 receptor); and (ii) the effect of capsazepine on responses to anandamide (AEA, CB1/CB2 receptor agonist), arachidonyl-2’-chloroethylamide (ACEA, CB1 receptor agonist), and WIN 55,212-2 (CB2 receptor agonist). In a different set of experiments, the effect of AM6545 on responses to AEA or ACEA was investigated in HCAs with or without capsaicin pretreatment.ResultsIn HCAs from both males and females, capsaicin induced concentration-dependent vasorelaxation. This response was inhibited only by AM6545 and O-1918. Moreover, the vasorelaxation induced by AEA and ACEA, but not by WIN 55,212-2, was inhibited by capsazepine and AM6545. Finally, TRPV1 desensitisation induced a significant decrease in the maximum responses to AEA or ACEA in HCAs from females, but not in HCAs from males.ConclusionsCapsaicin-induced vasodilation is mediated by CB1 and the putative endothelial CB receptors, without the involvement of CB2 receptors. Moreover, TRPV1 channels may play a role in modulating AEA- and ACEA-induced vasodilation as capsazepine inhibited the induced responses. Additionally, the role of TRPV1 channels in modulating ECS differs between males and females, suggesting an important role for sex hormones. Therefore, the interaction between the ECS and vanilloid system could offer new treatment targets for (neuro)vascular disorders, including migraine, by sharing a signalling pathway to modulate vascular tone.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Endocannabinoid system, Migraine, Neurovascular disorders, TRPV1 channels, Vascular tone

Background

The modulation of vascular tone is essential for maintaining cardiovascular and cerebral homeostasis (i.e., blood flow and pressure). Dysregulation of vascular tone is a central feature of neurovascular disorders, including migraine [1], where abnormal vascular responses, such as altered vasodilation and vasoconstriction, may contribute to its pathophysiology [1, 2].

Beyond the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), a key vasodilator neuropeptide implicated in the pathophysiology of migraine, both the endocannabinoid system (ECS) [3–5] and the transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channels [6, 7] have been associated with vascular tone modulation and the pathophysiology of migraine. These systems have also been proposed as an alternative, cardiovascularly safe therapeutic target for the treatment of migraine and cardiovascular disorders [3, 8].

Along with its role in specific brain functions, such as control of movement, memory, neuroendocrine modulation, and chronic pain, the ECS is involved in the modulation of (cardio)vascular functions [3] via the activation mainly of the cannabinoid (CB) receptors (i.e., CB1 and CB2 receptors) [9, 10]. These receptors, expressed in endothelial and vascular smooth muscle cells of larger arteries [10], modulate vascular tone via an increase in Ca2+ influx [11, 12] or the activation of the nitric oxide pathway [13, 14]. In this respect, anandamide (AEA), an endogenous and non-selective CB1/CB2 receptor agonist, induces endothelium-dependent or–independent relaxation responses in various vascular beds—including sheep [15] and rat [16] coronary arteries, rabbit aorta [17], cat cerebral arteries [18], and rat mesenteric arteries [11, 19, 20], as well as in human vascular endothelial cells [21]—via CB1 receptors. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that AEA also activates non-CB receptors, namely, the orphan G-protein-coupled receptor 55 (GPR55), or the putative endothelial CB receptor (CBe receptor) [22].

In addition, recent evidence suggests that the ECS and TRPV1 may interact functionally. In the trigeminovascular system, AEA can also activate TRPV1 channels, which in turn induce CGRP release and, consequently, vasodilation through a CB1-independent mechanism [23]. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that CB1 activation by AEA inhibits KCl-evoked CGRP release, but only when TRPV1 channels are blocked, suggesting that TRPV1 activity may counteract or mask CB1-mediated inhibition [24]. In the vasculature, capsaicin-induced relaxation responses have been shown to be mediated via CB1 receptor activation, but not TRPV1 channels [25], while TRPV1 desensitisation reduces vascular responses to AEA [11]. These findings highlight a dual effect of the ECS and TRPV1 systems, as both are involved in vascular tone modulation and may work in concert to influence neurovascular function, suggesting a novel therapeutic target for treating (neuro)vascular complications. However, to our knowledge, the precise mechanisms and vascular effects of ‘more selective’ CB1 or CB2 receptor agonists (e.g., arachidonyl-2’-chloroethylamide, ACEA; or WIN 55,212-2; respectively) on human vasculature remain unclear.

This study therefore used human isolated coronary arteries (HCAs) to investigate the functional interaction between the ECS and TRPV1 channels in vascular tone modulation. The use of HCAs provides a clinically relevant and reliable model for understanding vascular responses in humans, which is essential for translating findings to a clinical context. Although anatomically distinct from the cranial vasculature and other vasculature associated with the trigeminovascular system (e.g., structural differences in their vascular walls that influence blood flow regulation), HCAs share key neurovascular features, including expression of TRPV1 and the canonical CGRP receptor complex, and demonstrate functional responses to CGRP [26–28]. These similarities support their use as a model for studying migraine-related mechanisms and assessing the potential cardiovascular safety of antimigraine drugs [29, 30]. Given the safety concerns associated with direct CB1 receptor agonism at both central and peripheral levels [31], investigating the interplay between the ECS and TRPV1 channels using HCAs may help develop targeted therapies aimed at minimising cardiovascular side effects. Moreover, by analysing how capsaicin influences CB receptor activation—or vice versa—, and investigating how TRPV1 channels modulate ECS vascular responses, we provide new insights into the mechanisms underlying vascular modulation relevant to (neuro)vascular disorders, including migraine, where vascular dysregulation is a key feature. Furthermore, we investigated whether the interaction between the ECS and the TRPV1 channel differs between males and females, considering that (i) the involvement of TRPV1 channels in regulating vascular responses within the ECS has been reported to be sex-dependent [32], and (ii) potential sex differences in vascular responses could have implications for targeted therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Human tissues

After donor screening and acceptance by the Dutch Transplant Foundation (Leiden, The Netherlands), 30 human hearts (n = 16 males, 53 ± 4 years; and n = 14 females, 56 ± 2 years) were obtained from Dutch post-mortem donors who died of non-cardiac disorders and had consented to research purposes. The hearts were provided by the Hearth Valve Department (ETB BISLIFE; Haarlem, The Netherlands) after the dissection of the aortic and pulmonary valves for homograft valve transplantation. Following circulatory arrest, the hearts were collected and preserved in a sterile solution and stored at 4 °C and brought to the laboratory within the first 24 h after death. Distal segments of the left anterior descending coronary artery (internal diameter 0.3–1.0 mm) were then isolated and maintained in carbogenated (95% O2, 5% CO2) Krebs buffer solution (pH 7.4) containing: 118 mM NaCl, 4.7 mM KCl, 2.5 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM MgSO4, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 25 mM NaHCO3, and 8.3 mM glucose, and were stored at 4 °C until the ex vivo wire myography experiments [26, 28, 33].

Wire-myograph experiments

The HCAs were dissected into segments of approximately 1.5–2 mm in length, carefully cleaned of surrounding connective and perivascular tissues, including visible nerves, and suspended between two parallel stainless-steel wires (40 μm calibre). The segments were then mounted in 6-ml organ baths in Mulvany myographs (ADinstruments, Danish Myograph Technology, Aarhus, Denmark). Given the ex vivo nature of this model, autonomic and perivascular sensory innervation is unlikely to be preserved, allowing the study of intrinsic vascular responses [34, 35]. Therefore, although we cannot categorically exclude the presence of non-functional nerve remnants, the observed responses reflect vascular-specific mechanisms rather than neurogenic regulation [34]. The organ baths were then filled with carbogenated Krebs buffer solution maintained at 37 °C to assess vascular function. Following an equilibrium period of at least 30 min, during which the Krebs buffer solution was exchanged twice at 15-min intervals, the HCAs segments were gradually stretched to a tension normalized to 90% of l100, corresponding to a transmural pressure of 100 mmHg [36]. The blood vessels were subsequently exposed to 30 mM KCl, and the maximum contractile response was determined using 100 mM KCl as an internal standard for measuring the contractile force generated by the segments [7, 26, 28, 37]. Data were recorded using LabChart data acquisition system (AD Instruments Ltd, Oxford, UK).

After washout and stabilization of the artery segments, a single concentration-response curve was constructed on precontracted segments using 30 mM KCl in a parallel set-up model under the following conditions (see Table 1 for details): (i) exposure to increasing concentrations (0.1–100 µM) of capsaicin (TRPV1 agonist) [7, 26, 37], in the absence or presence of the antagonists capsazepine (TRPV1 channels, 5 µM) [7, 26, 37]; AM6545 (CB1 receptor, 1 µM) [38]; AM630 (CB2 receptor, 1 µM) [16, 39]; CID16020046 (GPR55 receptor, 2.5 µM) [40]; cannabidiol (CB1/GPR55 receptor, 1 µM) [41]; or O-1918 (CBe receptor, 10 µM) [40, 42]; (ii) exposure to increasing concentrations of AEA (CB1/CB2 receptor agonist; 0.001-100 µM) [11], ACEA (CB1 receptor agonist, 0.001-10 µM) [39], and WIN 55,212-2 (CB2 receptor agonist, 0.001-10 µM) [41] in the absence or presence of 5 µM capsazepine; and (iii) exposure to AEA (0.001-100 µM) or ACEA (0.001-10 µM) in the absence or presence of 1 µM AM6545. Additionally, to investigate the role of TRPV1 channels in ECS-induced vascular effects, artery segments were pretreated with 10 µM capsaicin during 30 min [11, 43] before starting a single concentration-response curve to AEA or ACEA, in the absence or presence of 1 µM AM6545.

Upon completion of the concentration response curves, endothelial function of the artery segments was evaluated by adding a single concentration of substance P (10 nM) following precontraction with the stable TxA2 analogue U46619 (10 nM) [7, 26, 28].

Data and statistical analysis

The number of artery segments used in the functional experiments included at least n = 6 segments per group, based on previous studies from our group on sex differences in vascular responses (i.e., a power analysis using standard deviation, a significance level of α = 0.05, and a statistical power of 80%) [26, 28]. All data presented in the text and figures are presented as means ± SEM. The relaxant responses to the agonists are shown as the percentage relaxation of the tone induced by KCl 30 mM. Concentration-response curves were analysed using nonlinear regression. The maximum effect (Emax) was defined as the relaxation obtained at the highest concentration of the agonists, and the differences between groups were evaluated by one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test. In addition, statistical comparisons between concentration-response curves to the agonists, both in the absence or presence of antagonists, were evaluated with Dunnett’s post-hoc test, once a mixed-effects model revealed that the samples represented different populations. Moreover, the potency of the agonist was expressed as pEC50. Since a plateau was not reached in the concentration-response curves, the response observed at the highest concentration of the agonists used was considered as Emax. Hence, pEC50 values derived from the concentration-response curves may be an overestimation of the real pEC50 values. Differences in pEC50 values were analysed using Tukey’s post-hoc test, once an one-way ANOVA data indicated that the samples represented different populations. Finally, comparisons between two groups were analysed using Student’s t-test [7, 26, 37]. Statistical significance was considered when P < 0.05. All statistical analysis and graphics were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Materials

The compounds used in this study were (see Table 1 for details): capsaicin (8-Methyl-N-vanillyl-trans-6-nonenamide) and capsazepine (9 N-[2-(4-Chlorophenyl)ethyl]-1,3,4,5-tetrahydro-7,8-dihydroxy-2 H-2- (from Sigma-Aldrich, Schnelldorf, Germany); anandamide ((5Z,8Z,11Z,14Z)-N-(2-hydroxyethyl)icosa-5,8,11,14-tetraenamide); arachidonyl-2’-chloroethylamide ((5Z,8Z,11Z,14Z)-N-(2-chloroethyl)icosa-5,8,11,14-tetraenamide); WIN 55,212-2 mesylate ((11R)-2-methyl-11-(morpholin-4-ylmethyl)-3-[(naphthalen-1-yl)carbonyl]-9-oxa-1-azatricyclo[6.3.1.0^[1]dodeca-2,4,6,8(12)-tetraene); AM6545 (5-[4-(4-cyanobut-1-ynyl)phenyl]-1-(2,4-dichlorophenyl)-N-(1,1-dioxo-1,4-thiazinan-4-yl)-4-methylpyrazole-3-carboxamide); AM630 ([6-iodo-2-methyl-1-(2-morpholin-4-ylethyl)indol-3-yl]-(4-methoxyphenyl)methanone); CID16020046 ( 4-[4-(3-hydroxyphenyl)-3-(4-methylphenyl)-6-oxo-1,4-dihydropyrrolo[3,4-d]pyrazol-5-yl]benzoic acid); (-)cannabidiol (2-[(1R,6R)-3-Methyl-6-(1-methylethenyl)-2-cyclohexen-1-yl]-5-pentyl-1,3-benzenediol); and O-1918 (1,3-Dimethoxy-5-methyl-2-[(1R,6R)-3-methyl-6-(1-methylethenyl)-2-cyclohexen-1-yl]benzene) (from Tocris-Bioscience, Abingdon, UK).

Results

Capsaicin-induced vasodilation is mediated by CB1 and CBe receptors

While it has been demonstrated that capsaicin induces relaxation responses in human arteries, the mechanisms of vasodilation appear to involve pathways independent of TRPV1 channel activation. In this context, we have investigated additional mechanisms contributing to capsaicin-induced vasodilation in HCAs, focusing on the role of the ECS.

As depicted in Fig. 1, capsaicin induced concentration-dependent vasorelaxation (Fig. 1A). The maximum response observed at the highest concentration of capsaicin (Emax, achieved at 100 µM) was 107 ± 4% (Fig. 1B), with an estimated potency (expressed as pEC50 value) of 4.75 ± 0.12. Moreover, capsaicin-induced vasorelaxation was unaffected in the presence of the antagonists capsazepine (Emax: 105 ± 7%, pEC50: 4.77 ± 0.10) or AM630 (Emax: 99 ± 3%, pEC50: 4.81 ± 0.12), suggesting that neither TRPV1 channels nor CB2 receptors, respectively, are involved in mediating the vasoactive effects of capsaicin (Fig. 1A, B). In contrast, in the presence of the selective CB1 receptor antagonist, AM6545, the relaxation response induced by capsaicin was inhibited (Emax: 82 ± 4%, pEC50: 4.67 ± 0.11; Fig. 1A, B), supporting the involvement of CB1 receptors. In addition, the role of non-CB receptors (i.e., GPR55 or the CBe receptors) was evaluated using the antagonists cannabidiol (non-selective for GPR55 receptors), CID16020046 (selective for GPR55 receptors), and O-1918 (selective for the putative CBe receptors). Capsaicin-induced vasorelaxation (Emax: 105 ± 4%, pEC50: 4.75 ± 0.12) was resistant to blockade by cannabidiol (Emax: 92 ± 10%, pEC50: 4.86 ± 0.15) or CID16020046 (Emax: 98 ± 3%, pEC50: 4.91 ± 0.10) (Fig. 1C, D), but was inhibited in the presence of O-1918 (Emax: 88 ± 6%, pEC50: 4.68 ± 0.10), revealing a role for the putative CBe receptors but not for the orphan GPR55 receptor in modulating vascular responses to capsaicin (Fig. 1C and D).

It is important to mention that no differences were observed in the vasorelaxation induced by capsaicin in HCAs obtained from male and female donors (males Emax: 107 ± 4%, pEC50: 4.81 ± 0.19 versus females Emax: 107 ± 5%, pEC50: 4.78 ± 0.12), nor in the pharmacological profile of the (non-)CB receptors mediating these responses. Specifically, in both male and female donor tissue, only AM6545 and O-1918 inhibited capsaicin-induced relaxation responses (Fig. 2).

Finally, the analysis of endothelial function after capsaicin-induced relaxation responses resulted in a mean vasorelaxation of 78 ± 5% (induced by 10 nM substance P) of the precontraction induced by 10 nM U46619. Moreover, a moderate positive correlation was observed between the vascular responses to 100 µM capsaicin and endothelial integrity (ρ = 0.57, P = 0.03, Spearman’s rank correlation test).

Vascular responses to ACEA and AEA involve both CB₁ receptors and TRPV1 channels

To further explore the involvement of the vanilloid system in the modulation of the vascular responses induced by CB receptor agonists, we analysed the vasodilation to AEA (a non-selective agonists of CB1, CB2 and GPR55 receptors and the TRPV1 channels), ACEA (a selective CB1 receptor agonist), and WIN 55,212-2 (a selective CB2 receptor agonist) in the presence or absence of capsazepine.

Concentration-dependent vasorelaxation was observed in KCl-precontracted HCA segments of male and females donors after administration of AEA and ACEA (Fig. 3). The maximum responses to AEA in both males (Fig. 3A) and females (Fig. 3B) (males Emax: 43 ± 3%, pEC50: 6.16 ± 0.35; females Emax: 62 ± 2%, pEC50: 6.56 ± 0.17) were significantly inhibited in the presence of AM6545 (males Emax: 18 ± 5%; females Emax: 22 ± 5%) and in the presence of capsazepine (males Emax: 30 ± 5%; females Emax: 32 ± 7%). Likewise, the responses induced by ACEA (Fig. 3B and D) in HCAs from males (Emax: 29 ± 4%, pEC50: 6.19 ± 0.35) and females (Emax: 49 ± 3%, pEC50: 6.64 ± 0.19) were inhibited by AM6545 (males Emax: 10 ± 2%; females Emax: 18 ± 4%) and by capsazepine (males Emax: 16 ± 2%; females Emax: 25 ± 3%). Moreover, both AEA (males Emax: 43 ± 3% versus females Emax: 62 ± 2%) and ACEA (males Emax: 29 ± 4% versus females Emax: 49 ± 3%) induced enhanced vasorelaxation in HCA from female donors compared to that induced in males (Fig. 3). Moreover, assessment of endothelial function after AEA- and ACEA-induced relaxation responses resulted in a mean vasorelaxation values of 85 ± 5% and 89 ± 4%, respectively (induced by 10 nM substance P) of the precontraction induced by 10 nM U46619. Spearman’s rank correlation test indicated weak correlation between AEA-induced relaxation and endothelial integrity in both males (ρ = 0.15, P = 0.78) and females (ρ = 0.25, P = 0.36). Similarly, ACEA-induced relaxation showed moderate correlations in males (ρ = 0.46, P = 0.36) and females (ρ = 0.36, P = 0.78), none of which reached statistical significance. In contrast, WIN 55,212-2-induced vasorelaxation (Emax: 54 ± 7%, pEC50: 6.05 ± 0.25) remained unaffected by capsazepine (Emax: 53 ± 7%, pEC50: 6.04 ± 0.40; Fig. 3C). The fact that AEA- and ACEA-induced vasodilation involve TRPV1 channel activation, whereas the vasodilation induced by WIN 55,212-2 does not, might suggest a functional link between CB1 receptors and the vanilloid system in mediating these effects, with no apparent role for CB2 receptors.

TRPV1 desensitisation modulates vasorelaxation to AEA and ACEA

It has been demonstrated that prolonged pretreatment of isolated arteries with capsaicin induces functional desensitisation of TRPV1 channels [11, 43]. To confirm the functional link between the ECS and TRPV1 channels, we incubated HCA segments from male and female donors with 10 µM capsaicin for 30 min to construct concentration-response curves to AEA or ACEA in the absence or presence of 1 µM AM6545.

Desensitisation of TRPV1 channels significantly inhibited the magnitude of vasorelaxation to AEA (control Emax: 53 ± 4% versus after capsaicin Emax: 37 ± 2%, Fig. 4A, B). No significant differences were observed in the potency of AEA to induce vasorelaxation in the absence (pEC50: 6.30 ± 0.26) or presence (pEC50: 5.80 ± 0.22) of capsaicin. Significant inhibition of AEA-induced vasorelaxation was also observed in the presence of 1 µM AM6545 in capsaicin-pretreated HCA segments (Emax: 14 ± 3%, pEC50: 5.20 ± 0.40) and in HCAs without capsaicin (Emax: 20 ± 3%, pEC50: 5.58 ± 0.42) (Fig. 4A, B).

Sex-dependent effect of TRPV1 desensitisation on CB1-mediated vasodilation

Given that the role of TRPV1 channels in modulating vascular responses within the ECS has been described to be sex-dependent [32], we analysed potential sex differences in the modulation of CB1mediated vasorelaxation by TRPV1 in HCAs from male and female donors.

When artery segments were pretreated with capsaicin, AEA-induced vasorelaxation was inhibited, with a significant rightward shift in the concentration-response curve, in HCAs from females (Emax: 40 ± 4%, pEC50: 5.55 ± 0.35; Fig. 4D, E), but not in HCAs from males (Emax: 33 ± 3%, pEC50: 6.15 ± 0.26; Fig. 4C, E). Moreover, the addition of AM6545 to capsaicin-pretreated arteries resulted in a more pronounced inhibition of vasorelaxation in HCAs from females (without capsaicin: Emax: 22 ± 5%, pEC50: 5.38 ± 0.38; with capsaicin: Emax: 14 ± 4%, pEC50: 5.10 ± 0.24) (Fig. 4D, E), but not in males (without capsaicin: Emax: 18 ± 5%, pEC50: 5.95 ± 0.0.65; with capsaicin: Emax: 13 ± 4%, pEC50: 5.47 ± 0.32) (Fig. 4C, E).

Similar results were observed when analysing the vasorelaxation induced by ACEA. A significant inhibition of ACEA-induced vasorelaxation after pretreatment with capsaicin was observed only in HCAs from females (Emax: 27 ± 3%, pEC50: 5.97 ± 0.30; Fig. 5D, E), but not in HCAs from males (Emax: 23 ± 5%, pEC50: 6.03 ± 0.28; Fig. 5C, E). Further inhibition by AM6545 in HCAs pretreated with capsaicin was also observed only in female donors (without capsaicin: Emax: 18 ± 4%, pEC50: 5.90 ± 0.0.50 versus with capsaicin: Emax: 7 ± 3%, pEC50: 6.67 ± 0.23; Fig. 4D, E).

Discussion

The present study offer new insights into the complex interaction between the ECS and TRPV1 channels in modulating vascular responses. Using isolated HCAs, a clinically relevant model to directly assess vascular function, we demonstrated that capsaicin-induced vasodilation is mediated via CB1 receptors and the putative CBe receptors, without direct involvement of TRPV1 channels or CB2 receptors. Moreover, our data highlight a modulatory role for TRPV1 channels in CB1-mediated vasorelaxation, which appears to be sex-dependent.

Capsaicin, a well characterised TRPV1 agonist, has been linked to vascular effects trough the release of vasodilator neuropeptides (e.g. CGRP and substance P) from sensory nerves [44–47]. Nevertheless, evidence suggests that capsaicin-induced vasodilation in different vascular beds involves TRPV1- and CGRP-independent mechanisms [7, 25, 26, 37, 48]. In this study, the observation that capsaicin-induced vasorelaxation was unaffected by the TRPV1 antagonist capsazepine (Fig. 1A, B) supports previously reported findings, suggesting that capsaicin elicits vasorelaxation through alternative pathways. It is worth mentioning that a factor to consider in this study is the use of 5 µM capsazepine, as it is a competitive antagonist. Therefore, the concentration used may be insufficient to fully antagonise the effects of capsaicin [37, 49]. This could partly explain the lack of effect observed on capsaicin-induced vasodilation without excluding potential TRPV1 involvement. However, previous studies have reported that higher capsazepine concentrations impair precontraction and induce non-specific vasodilatory effects, which may confound TRPV1-specific mechanisms [37]. Additionally, the concentrations of capsaicin required to induce vasodilation in vascular tissue are much higher than those effective for neuronal TRPV1 activation [49], suggesting that effects of capsaicin may involve alternative or non-canonical mechanisms in the vasculature. Nevertheless, lower concentrations of capsaicin (i.e., 0.1 nM–1 µM) were not blocked by capsazepine either [37]. Future studies using “more selective” TRPV1 antagonists, such as AMG-9810 [24] may help clarify the role of TRPV1 channels in capsaicin-induced vasorelaxation. Interestingly, the fact that the vasorelaxant response was significantly inhibited by the CB1 receptor antagonist AM6545, but not by the CB2 receptor antagonist AM630, suggests that CB1 receptors mediate capsaicin’s vascular effects, without an apparent role of CB2 receptors (Fig. 1A, B). In agreement with our findings, Andrade et al. [25] demonstrated that capsaicin-induced vasorelaxation in rat aorta is attributed to CB1 receptor activation rather than TRPV1 or CB2 receptors. Moreover, the modulation of relaxant responses to capsaicin through the activation of CBe receptors may suggest that capsaicin-induced vasorelaxation also involves an endothelium-dependent mechanism independent of classical CB receptor activation (Fig. 1C, D). In fact, some studies have reported that CBe receptors are involved in vasodilation induced upon the activation of TRPV1 channels [42].

The mechanistic pathway between TRPV1 and CB1 receptors could stem from the structural resemblance between capsaicin and the endocannabinoids. In fact, it has been suggested that capsaicin and AEA share structural similarities, alongside their shared binding affinities at TRPV1 [50]. Therefore, it might be plausible that capsaicin binds to CB1 receptors to activate downstream signalling pathways that induce vasodilation. This highlights the interaction between TRPV1 and the ECS, revealing an alternative mechanism for the vascular effects of capsaicin that operates independently of TRPV1 activation

In addition, AEA is also known to be a non-selective endogenous agonist of CB1, CB2 and GPR55 receptors with a similar pEC50 values (6.3–7.5, 5.7–7.5, and 7.7, respectively) [51–53]. Moreover, it has been described that AEA is also an activator of TRPV1 channels (pEC50 value: 5.9) [54], inducing some capsaicin-like effects by inducing vasodilatation through the activation of TRPV1 channels [55]. In agreement with this, our findings show that AEA-induced vasorelaxation is mediated by TRPV1 as these responses were inhibited in the presence of capsazepine (Fig. 3A). The contribution of TRPV1 to CB1-mediated vasorelaxation was demonstrated using a more selective agonists for CB1 receptors (ACEA) and for CB2 receptors (WIN 55,212-2). In this regard, the responses induced by ACEA (Fig. 3C and D), but not by WIN 55,212-2, were inhibited in the presence of capsazepine. This suggests that ACEA-induced vasorelaxation involves TRPV1 activation, which may occur through both direct interaction of ACEA with TRPV1 channels and/or CB1 receptor-mediated signalling, indicating a dual effect. Certainly, capsazepine does not inhibit the responses to capsaicin (Figs. 1 and 2) but it can inhibits the responses to AEA and ACEA (Fig. 3), which may suggest that (endo)cannabinoid agonists mediate their effects through CB1 receptors, with capsazepine inhibiting responses to these agonists, likely because CB1 receptor activation involves mechanisms that interact with or influence TRPV1 activity. Therefore, the relaxation responses to AEA and ACEA seem to involve both TRPV1-dependent and TRPV1-independent pathways, whereas capsaicin-induced relaxation appears to occur through mechanisms that do not involve TRPV1.

To further elucidate the role of TRPV1 in ECS modulation, we examined the effects of capsaicin pretreatment, which is known to induce the functional desensitisation of TRPV1 channels. This approach revealed significant inhibition of AEA- and ACEA-induced vasorelaxation following TRPV1 desensitisation (Figs. 4 and 5), supporting the hypothesis that TRPV1 channels modulate CB1 receptor activity. These findings are consistent with prior observations, where TRPV1 desensitisation attenuated AEA-induced relaxation responses in different vascular beds [11, 16, 39, 55]. A key finding in this study is the sex-dependent modulation of CB1-mediated vasorelaxation by TRPV1 channels. While CB1 receptor blockade with AM6545 inhibited AEA- (Fig. 4A, B) and ACEA-induced vasorelaxation (Fig. 5A, B) in both males and females, the impact of TRPV1 desensitisation was observed exclusively in HCAs from females. Specifically, capsaicin pretreatment resulted in a significant rightward shift in the concentration-response curve for both AEA (Fig. 4D, E) and ACEA (Fig. 5D, E) in females, but not in males (Figs. 4C and 5C). Moreover, the addition of AM6545 to capsaicinpretreated HCAs led to a more pronounced inhibition of vasorelaxation in females, suggesting an enhanced interaction between CB1 and TRPV1 pathways in female HCAs.

It is well known that TRPV1 is primarily expressed in sensory nerves, where its activation by capsaicin leads to Ca²⁺ influx, triggering neurotransmitter release (e.g., CGRP) and pain signalling and/or vascular modulation [55, 56]. Repeated stimulation induces TRPV1 desensitisation (tachyphylaxis) through phosphorylation, internalisation, and downstream inhibitory mechanisms, resulting in a reduced response to painful stimuli and/or reduced neurogenic vasodilation [55–57]. However, TRPV1 is also expressed in vascular smooth muscle cells, where it modulates vascular tone. In fact, some studies have shown that capsaicin can induce varying patterns of desensitisation in vascular tissues compared to sensory neurones, suggesting a unique regulatory mechanism of TRPV1 in vascular smooth muscle cells that influences vascular tone and reactivity [56, 58]. ​Moreover, TRPV1 channels exert physiological effects in vivo, such as regulating body temperature and cardiovascular responses, by modulating sympathetic nervous system [59]. Nevertheless, in our model, using HCAs in an ex vivo setup, where autonomic and perivascular sensory innervation is unlikely to be preserved [34, 35], we cannot assume the presence of functional autonomic or sensory nerve remnants. Therefore, any functional response and/or any observed TRPV1 desensitisation reflects vascular-specific mechanisms rather than neurogenic regulation. Furthermore, although capsaicin is generally considered a selective TRPV1 agonist, the possibility of CB1 receptor desensitisation by capsaicin cannot be entirely ruled out. However, current evidence does not support direct CB1 activation by capsaicin. Instead, CB1 modulation may occur indirectly via ECS or through alternative signalling pathways influenced by TRP channel activity [25, 60, 61]. In addition, the enhanced inhibition observed with AM6545 after capsaicin pretreatment suggests that CB1 receptors remain functionally active, reinforcing TRPV1’s role in modulating CB1-dependent vasorelaxation. In general, our findings suggest that prolonged TRPV1 activation in vascular tissue leads to desensitization, potentially through receptor trafficking, Ca²⁺-dependent inactivation, or crosstalk with CB1 receptors.

The sex-specific differences observed may be attributed to the modulatory effects of sex hormones (e.g. estrogen and prolactin) on TRPV1 expression and function. Previous studies have shown that estrogens can upregulate TRPV1 expression and sensitise TRPV1-mediated vascular responses [32], as well as enhance nociceptive responses to capsaicin in a sex-dependent way, with females showing increased sensitivity to acute pain compared to males [62]. Additionally, prolactin can also induce sensitisation of TRPV1 channels, leading to a significantly potentiated response to capsaicin in females, with no effect in males [63]. Therefore, the modulatory role of sex hormones may explain the heightened sensitivity of female HCAs to TRPV1 modulation of ECS responses observed in our study. Certainly, in the present study, the (post)menopausal status of the female donor was unknown. Nevertheless, residual estrogen may still influence TRPV1 function, albeit to a lesser extent than in premenopausal women. It has been demonstrated that even in the (post)menopausal state, sex hormones can exert both genomic and non-genomic effects to modulate vascular function [64, 65]. Notably, even low concentrations of estrogen have been shown to affect TRPV1-mediated pain perception [65]. Hence, although some mechanisms underlying sex-related vascular differences—such as CGRP-induced vasodilation in human arteries—are clearly age-dependent and influenced by hormonal status [28], others, including the differential responses to TRP agonists, seem to be age-independent and therefore likely unaffected by (post)menopausal status [26]. In contrast, male arteries appear to exhibit a reduced dependence on TRPV1 channels for CB1-mediated vasorelaxation, highlighting potential differences in receptor cross-talk between sexes. Paradoxically, no sex differences were observed when comparing capsaicin-induced relaxation responses between males and females (Fig. 2). In fact, we have recently published that there are no sex differences in the vasoactive responses to capsaicin nor in the expression of TRPV1 channels in human isolated arteries from male and female donors [26]. We suggest that the absence of sex differences in capsaicin-induced relaxation is unlikely to result from direct activation of TRPV1 and is instead mediated by TRPV1-independent mechanisms, which are not significantly influenced by sex hormones. However, further studies are needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which capsaicin induces vasorelaxation and to determine the potential contribution of TRPV1 channels to this responses. In contrast, relaxation responses to AEA and ACEA seem to be enhanced in female donors compared to those in male donors (Figs. 4E, F and 5E and F). The sex differences in TRPV1 modulation of CB1-mediated relaxation arise from estrogen-induced changes in TRPV1 sensitivity and its interaction with CB1 receptors, leading to more pronounced relaxation in females, which could be mediated by (non)genomic mechanisms [32].

Certainly, the use of cannabis or its derivatives has increased in recent years due to their therapeutic potential, especially in treating neurological conditions such as schizophrenia, epilepsy, Alzheimer’s, and Parkinson’s diseases [66], as well as migraine headaches [4, 5, 67]. However, the exact mechanisms of action of cannabinoids are still not fully understood, and there are concerns regarding the possible cardiovascular events associated with cannabis use [68]. Evidently, the interaction between the ECS and TRPV1 channels has significant implications for the understanding of vascular tone modulation and the pathophysiology of neurovascular disorders. The sex-dependent modulation of CB1-mediated vasorelaxation by TRPV1 suggests that therapies targeting these pathways may need to be tailored based on sex-specific differences to achieve optimal efficacy. Furthermore, the ability of capsaicin to modulate CB1 receptor activity, potentially involving TRPV1 desensitisation, introduces new therapeutic possibilities for targeting CB1 receptors in cardiovascular and neurovascular disorders. Given the safety concerns surrounding direct CB1 receptor agonism, understanding the interplay between CB1 and TRPV1 channels may aid in the development of targeted therapies that exploit this functional interaction while minimising adverse effects (e.g., cardiovascular side effects). Finally, additional experiments, beyond the scope of this study, should investigate the mechanisms underlying the differential effects of the ECS, such as the potential increased expression of receptors or the modulatory role of CGRP in these responses. This would provide a better understanding of the complex interplay between TRPV1 channels and endocannabinoids in the regulation of vascular tone.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that the vasodilation induced by capsaicin in HCAs occurs independently of TRPV1 channel or CB2 receptor activation and involves the activation of CB1 and CBe receptors. Interestingly, TRPV1 channels contribute to the vasodilatory effects of AEA and ACEA, supporting a modulatory role for TRPV1 in CB1-mediated vasorelaxation. Notably, this modulatory effect appears to be sex-dependent, potentially reflecting sex hormone-dependent functional coupling between TRPV1 and CB1 receptors. The interaction between the ECS and vanilloid system may therefore represent promising therapeutic avenues for (neuro)vascular disorders, including migraine, by targeting shared signalling pathways that modulate vascular tone. These findings may offer new insights into the development of sex-specific treatment strategies.
