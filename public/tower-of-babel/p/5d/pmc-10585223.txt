Assessment of ship speed, operational carbon intensity indicator penalty and charterer profit of time charter ships
Authors: Ling Sun, Xinghe Wang, Youshui Lu, Zijiang Hu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe operational carbon intensity indicator (CII) proposed by the International Maritime Organization (IMO) has been officially implemented on January 1, 2023. As an important way of ship operation, applicable time charter ships are subject to the CII regulation. How to properly deal with the CII regulation is a challenge for the shipowner and charterer of time charter ships. Speed reduction is an effective measure to reduce carbon emissions and carbon intensity of ships. This study establishes a speed model including CII penalty for time charter ships. Results show that speed reductions of a time charter ship of 10 %, 20 % and 30 % reduce carbon emissions by 27.1 %, 48.8 % and 65.7 % and carbon intensity by 19 %, 36 % and 51 %, respectively. Speed reduction leads to reductions of carbon emissions, carbon intensity and CII penalty, with greater reductions for larger ships. Under the optimal charterer profit, the speed of a time charter ship increases with the rise of freight rate and reduces with the decrease of freight rate. When the fluctuation range of freight rate is the same, the larger the ship type is, the smaller the speed adjustment range is. For the same ship type, when its freight rate decreases, it is suggested that the charterer reduces speed; otherwise, it is suggested that the charterer increases speed. For different ship types, if the shipping market is booming, the charterer should charter more large ships; otherwise, the charterer can choose more small ships.Keywords: Operational carbon intensity indicator (CII), CII penalty, Speed reduction, Time charter ship, Carbon emissions, Carbon intensity

Introduction

Global warming has become one of the largest challenges we are encountering today [1]. As the most economical way to transport large quantities of goods over long distances [2], maritime transport undertakes approximately 90 % of the global trade volume [[3], [4], [5]]. With the rapid development of economic globalization and trade integration, the demand for shipping goes up year by year [6,7]. While completing cargo transportation tasks, ships consume a large amount of energy and generate a high quantity of emissions [7,8]. An increasing number of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions from ships have accelerated the process of global warming [9]. According to a report released by the International Maritime Organization (IMO), from 2012 to 2018, the GHG emissions of total shipping industry have increased from 977 million tons to 1076 million tons, and the share of shipping emissions has increased from 2.76 % to 2.89 % [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Total shipping carbon dioxide (CO2), as the main GHG, increased from 962 million tons in 2012 to 1056 million tons in 2018 [[12], [13], [14]]. The carbon emissions of the shipping industry continuously increase with the development of the global economy and international trade [15]. It is expected that by 2050 emissions from shipping industry will increase 90–130 % of 2008 emissions [12]. It is projected that by 2060 carbon emissions of maritime transport will reach the level of road transport [16]. Therefore, reducing carbon emissions is critical for the shipping industry to deal with global warming [17].

As the United Nations specialized agency responsible for the prevention of marine pollution by ships [18], IMO attaches great importance to the issue of global warming and devotes to reducing carbon emissions of the shipping industry. In 1997, the Kyoto Protocol, the world’ s first legal document on GHG, was adopted at the conference of the United Nations and it authorized IMO to be responsible for reducing GHG emissions from international shipping. In the same year, IMO formally enshrined “Regulations for the Prevention of Air Pollution from Ships” as Annex VI of the International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships (MARPOL) [16,19]. In 2009, the 59th session of the Marine Environmental Protection Committee (MEPC) introduced the Energy Efficiency Operational Indicator (EEOI) and the Ship Energy Efficiency Management Plan (SEEMP), which are voluntary for applicable ships [20]. In 2011, MEPC 62 introduced the Energy Efficiency Design Index (EEDI), which is mandatory for applicable ships, and changed the SEEMP from voluntary use to mandatory application [21]. In 2016, MEPC 69 proposed the Data Collection System (DCS) of ship fuel consumption [22]. In 2018, MEPC 72 adopted the Initial IMO Strategy on Reduction of GHG Emissions from Ships (hereinafter the Initial Strategy) [23]. Levels of ambition directing the Initial Strategy mainly includes: (1) reducing the carbon intensity of international shipping by at least 40 % by 2030, pursuing efforts towards 70 % by 2050, compared to 2008; (2) reducing the total annual GHG emissions from international shipping by at least 50 % by 2050 compared to 2008 [10,[24], [25], [26], [27], [28], [29]]. In MEPC 76, the Operational Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII) was proposed as an operational measure to achieve the carbon emission reduction target of the shipping industry [15]. CII is an indicator that reflects the carbon intensity of ships. The CII regulation has been officially implemented on January 1, 2023 [30]. It requires that carbon intensity of the ship is reduced by 2 % annually from 2023 to 2026 [28]. In 2021, MEPC 77 discussed the revision of the Initial Strategy adopted in 2018, or to achieve zero carbon emissions by the middle of this century [31]. Therefore, it is of great significance to study how the shipping industry can effectively reduce carbon emissions and carbon intensity to cope with global warming and achieve shipping decarbonization target [28].

There are three basic modes of operation in the shipping industry: industrial shipping, liner shipping and tramp shipping [[32], [33], [34]]. In industrial shipping, ship operators own the cargoes and aim to minimize the cost of carrying cargoes [34,35]. In liner shipping, ships have regular schedules, fixed sailing routes, fixed ports of call and relatively stable freight rates [34,36]. Contrary to liner shipping, tramp ships have no fixed schedules or sailing routes [33,37]. Tramp shipping is oriented by cargo transportation demand and has greater flexibility. Compared with liner shipping, tramp shipping does not consider service frequency [38]. Tramp ship operators often follow available cargoes and transport contracted cargoes to maximize the profit of transporting cargoes [32,34]. The report from the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) shows that tramp ships account for over 70 % of the total maritime transportation scale [39,40]. Tramp shipping is an important source of carbon emissions in the shipping industry. Carbon emission reduction in tramp shipping plays an important role in decarbonization for the whole shipping industry [35]. Time charter is an important mode of operation in tramp shipping. Time charter parties make up the majority of contracts in the shipping industry [41]. Time charter ships that meet the requirements of ship tonnage and ship type are subject to the CII regulation. Although the CII regulation adds new vitality to shipping carbon emission reduction, it brings more challenges to time charter ships and their related parties.

Sailing speed is the key factor and variable in maritime transportation [18,42]. On one hand, speed affects fuel consumption of the ships [43,44], and indirectly affects CO2 emissions [45]. Fuel consumption of a ship is very sensitive to its sailing speed [43,46], mainly because daily fuel consumption is approximately proportional to the cube of sailing speed [18,47], also named as the cubic law [48,49]. A ship’ s carbon emissions are directly proportional to its fuel consumption [50,51], hence carbon emissions from a ship are closely related to its sailing speed [52]. Thus, a small speed reduction can lead to a significant improvement in the ship’ s fuel consumption and carbon emissions [53]. On the other hand, speed is a key determinant of a ship's fuel costs [47]. Fuel costs, which account for more than 50 % of the total operational costs [39,54], have become the dominant factor of shipping costs that cannot be ignored [49]. Thus, a small speed reduction can lead to a large saving in fuel costs, that is, a large saving in operational costs of ships [55]. Therefore, speed reduction could reduce both carbon emissions and fuel costs at the same time, and may achieve a win-win situation from both environmental and economic perspectives [56]. The target of the CII regulation is to reduce carbon emissions and carbon intensity of ships. The objective of time charter ships, a type of tramp ships, is to maximize the profit by transporting cargoes. Speed reduction can not only achieve the target of the CII regulation, but also achieve the objective of time charter ships, finally achieve shipping decarbonization. That is to say, it is both environmentally friendly and cost-efficient [57]. Furthermore, speed reduction is easy to implement on ships and costs almost nothing [58]. This paper takes the CII regulation as the background and chooses the time charter ships that apply the CII regulation as the object. The goal of this paper is to deal with the implementation of CII regulation proposed by IMO and reduce carbon emissions and carbon intensity of the shipping industry. Furthermore, we hope to accelerate the process of carbon emission reduction in shipping and achieve the goal of shipping decarbonization as soon as possible.

Under the background of the CII regulation, according to the agreement of the time charter party, when the CII rating of a time charter ship during the charter period is D or E, the charterer of this time charter ship should pay the CII penalty to the shipowner. The attained CII of a time charter ship determines its CII rating, and the CII rating determines the charterer's CII penalty. Considering different ship types, freight rate fluctuations and other situations, a speed model of a time charter ship including the CII penalty is proposed. Carbon emissions, carbon intensities, CII penalties and charterer profits of time charter ships at different speeds are obtained. The impacts of speed reduction on carbon emissions and carbon intensity of time charter ships are studied. The impacts of freight rate fluctuation in the shipping market on the speed of time charter ships under the optimal charterer profit are investigated.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews the relevant literature; the speed model of a time charter ship including CII penalty is established in Section 3; Section 4 takes four main types of bulk carriers as a case study to validate the model; and the conclusions and policy implications of this study are given in Section 5.

Literature review

The research on tramp ships has mainly focuses on ship routing and scheduling problems. Most scholars investigate and solve the practical problems by constructing mathematical models, then use examples to verify the effectiveness of the models. Among them, Lin and Liu [33] studied the tramp ship routing problem by proposing a combined mathematical model for ship allocation, routing and freight assignment aiming at maximizing the total profit. There are several stochastic factors that may affect tramp shipping, such as the uncertainty of cargo demand, seasonal fluctuations in cargo demand and weather factors [59]. Considering the above factors, Yu et al. [59] established a mixed integer programming model with the goal of maximizing the profit to study the tramp ship scheduling problem. Li et al. [32] built a mixed integer non-linear programming model targeting minimal ship fuel consumption. The results indicate that this model can lead to an effective reduction in fuel consumption of tramp ships [32].

Some scholars combine tramp ship routing and scheduling problems with speed optimization. Wen et al. [56] studied a multiple tramp ship routing and speed optimization problem with the objectives of time, cost and environment to seek a balance between economic and environmental performance of the shipping industry. Ma et al. [60] developed a multi-objective optimization model of ship routing and speed to lower total costs and ship emissions. Considering ship scheduling optimization and speed reduction, Xia et al. [61] proposed a new emission reduction method, and pointed out that the negative impact of speed reduction can be compensated by scheduling optimization. Fan et al. [45] built a tramp ship routing, scheduling and speed optimization model to minimize the total costs, and confirmed that the joint optimization of ship routing, scheduling and speed can lead to the reduction of cost and carbon emissions. Wen et al. [38] chose full-shipload tramp shipping for research with the goal of maximizing the total profit and determined the optimal route and the optimal speed. It is found that speed optimization can increase the total profit by an average of 16 %.

In order to reduce carbon emissions of the shipping industry, speed optimization and speed reduction are discussed by IMO as candidate short-term measures [62]. There have been many studies on speed optimization [[63], [64], [65], [66]]. Regarding speed reduction, Psaraftis and Kontovas [41] proposed that reducing speed is an important operational measure to reduce ship emissions and it has important environmental benefits. As for bulk carriers, it is reasonable to assume that “daily fuel consumption is a cubic function of speed” [41]. And the GHG emissions from ships are directly proportional to fuel consumption [41,52,67], therefore, slower ships generate much less emissions than faster ships [18,41]. Chang et al. [68] discussed the changes in fuel consumption and CO2 emissions when the speed of main type of bulk carriers was reduced by 10 %, 20 %, and 30 %, and put forward that speed reduction reduces emissions and larger ships reduce more emissions. Xia et al. [61] considered that appropriately reducing the speed of ships sailing in the port can reduce carbon emissions. Speed reduction is also called “slow steaming” in the shipping parlance [3,69]. The method and practice of slow steaming reduces fuel consumption, thereby reducing CO2 emissions [3,44,70,71]. Finnsgård et al. [44] revealed that slow steaming may be the result of certain market conditions, such as freight rate, fuel price, transportation capacity and demand.

By reviewing relevant literature, it can be found that there are many researches on tramp ship routing and scheduling problems, and some researches combine them with speed optimization. However, few scholars have conducted research on the impact of the CII regulation on tramp ships or time charter ships. With the implementation of the CII regulation, speed reduction may be one of the most effective operational measures to reduce carbon emissions and carbon intensity of the shipping industry and achieve the decarbonization target of the shipping industry.

Methodology

In this section, the CII regulation, mainly CII calculation methods and CII rating mechanism, are interpreted in detail first. Based on relevant policies, the speed model of time charter ships including CII penalty is established.

CII regulation

In IMO MEPC 76 (June 2021), CII regulation is introduced into 2021 Revised MARPOL Annex Ⅵ [30]. It is a mandatory operational measure of carbon emission reduction [27]. It is applicable to international ships of 5000 gross tonnage and above [30]. The CII regulation entered into force on November 1, 2022, and it has been officially implemented on January 1, 2023 [30].

Problem description and basic assumptions

Under the control of the CII regulation, for time charter ships, the shipowner owns the ships, while the charterer operates the ships. The shipowner need to calculate the attained CII value of each ship every year, report it to administration and take responsibility for noncompliant ships. Therefore, the shipowner hope that each time charter ship can comply with the CII regulation during the charter period. However, the charterer hopes that the ships can convey more goods to obtain more profits.

Considering different ship types, freight rate fluctuations and other situations, this paper establishes a speed model of time charter ships including the CII penalty. In this case, (1) this paper investigates carbon emissions, carbon intensities, CII penalties and charterer profits of time charter ships at different speeds; (2) this paper studies the impacts of speed reduction on carbon emissions and carbon intensity of time charter ships; (3) this paper explores the impacts of freight rate fluctuation in the shipping market on the speed of time charter ships under the optimal charterer profit.

To solve the above problems, on the basis of considering different ship types, freight rate fluctuations and other situations, this paper establishes a speed model of time charter ships including CII penalty and hopes to provide a theoretical basis for time charter ships to scientifically respond to the CII regulation and properly implement carbon emission reduction measures.

The basic assumptions are given as below.

Under the background of CII, the shipowner enters into a time charter party with the charterer. The charter period is exactly a year from January 1 to December 31. In the time charter party, the shipowner and the charterer make an agreement that during the charter period, if the time charter ship violates the CII regulation, that is, the CII rating of the ship is D or E, that is, the attained CII of the ship exceeds the upper boundary value, the charterer should pay the penalty to the shipowner as agreed in the charter party.

The CII penalty is added in the speed model of time charter ships to reduce carbon emissions and carbon intensity. According to the agreement of both parties, the time charter party with CII penalty will take effect in 2028 [30].

The charterer charters the ship for cargo transportation. Each time charter ship has sufficient sources of goods. The hull of each time charter ship is in good condition. The influences of special weather, wind waves, and currents are not considered here [77,78].

The fuel consumption of each time charter ship mainly comes from the main engine of the ship. The main engine mainly uses heavy fuel oil during the charter period.

Case study

In this section, some applicable bulk carriers are applied in a case study to validate the effectiveness and feasibility of the speed model of time charter ships.

Ship selection

Tramp shipping is the main mode of transportation for dry bulk cargoes [35,59]. Dry bulk shipping is one of the most important forms of seaborne trade [82], accounting for over 70 % of the global shipping market [83]. Dry bulk commodity trade is mainly undertaken by bulk carriers. As an important part of time charter ships, bulk carriers release many carbon emissions while completing cargo transportation tasks.

According to the size of deadweight, bulk carriers are mainly divided into four categories, namely, Capesize (above 100,000 dwt), Panamax (70,000–99,999 dwt), Handymax (40,000–69,999 dwt) and Handysize (10,000–39,999 dwt) [84]. Each type of bulk carrier accounts for approximately 40 %, 25 %, 23 % and 12 %, respectively [84].

Parameter setting

A company charters the above four types of bulk carriers in the form of time charter for cargo transportation, with a charter period of 1 year. Referring to the annual reports of shipping companies, we assume that the operating time of each ship type during the charter period is 340 days. The ratio of the ship's time at sea to time in port is approximately 7/3 to 8/2 [68,85]. In this paper, the average value (7.5/2.5) is used to estimate the time at sea [68].

Four ship types of bulk carriers use heavy fuel oil (HFO). According to Clarkson Research, the average price of HFO is 398USD/tFuel [84]. The carbon conversion factor of HFO is 3.114tCO2/tFuel [86].

The CII penalty takes the upper boundary as the boundary value. When the attained CII value exceeds the upper boundary value, the CII penalty is generated. A government white paper proposes to increase the CO2 tax level from about €60 to €200. Taking the recent prices of the ETS-permits as a reference, it is assumed that the CII penalty standard is 90.5USD/tCO2 [87,88].

Conclusions and policy implications

Based on the target and requirement of the CII regulation, the CII penalty is proposed. The speed model of time charter ships including the CII penalty is established. Four main types of bulk carriers are used in the case study. As for time charter ships, the CII regulation binds the shipowner by rating the carbon intensity of the ships, and the shipowner binds the charterer by entering into the terms of the time charter party with the charterer. In this study, according to the agreement of the time charter party, the attained CII of the time charter ship determines its CII rating, and the CII rating determines the CII penalty of the charterer.

Carbon emissions, carbon intensities and CII penalties of time charter ships at different speeds are determined. The speed of a time charter ship is positively correlated with its carbon emissions, carbon intensity and CII penalties. As time charter ships increase speed, their carbon emissions, carbon intensity and CII penalty all increase, and their charterer profit first increases and then decreases.

This paper investigates the impacts of speed reduction on carbon emissions and carbon intensity of time charter ships. The results show that speed reductions of 10 %, 20 %, and 30 % can reduce carbon emissions by 27.1 %, 48.8 % and 65.7 %, respectively and reduce carbon intensity by 19 %, 36 %, and 51 %, respectively. Reducing speed can reduce carbon emissions, carbon intensity and CII penalty of time charter ships. Speed reduction can help time charter ships comply with the CII regulation and cope with global warming. However, it should be noted that speed reduction brings some problems, such as extending roundtrip time, reducing capacity and increasing fixed cost.

This paper studies the impacts of freight rate fluctuation on the speed of time charter ships under the optimal charterer profit. The results indicate that the speed of time charter ships increases with the increase of freight rate and decreases with the decrease of freight rate. When the freight rate decreases, it is suggested that the charterer reduce speed; when the freight rate rises, it is suggested that the charterer chooses to increase speed. When the fluctuation range of the freight rate is the same, the charterer can adjust the speed of larger ships less and adjust the speed of smaller ships more. The speed adjustment range of the Capesize bulk carrier is smaller, while the speed adjustment range of the Handysize bulk carrier is larger. For different ship types, when the shipping market is prosperous and the freight rate is high, the charterer should charter more large ships; when the shipping market is sluggish and the freight rate is low, the charterer should choose more small ships.

Under the CII regulation, our findings emphasize the importance of speed reduction. It is an effective short-term measure to reduce carbon emissions and carbon intensity of the shipping industry. It not only has environmental benefits, but also has economic benefits. However, speed reduction cannot maximize the charterer profit at all times. For instance, when the freight rate of the time charter ship rises, the charterer should instead increase the speed.

The method suggested in this paper has some limitations. Firstly, this method is only applicable to the 12 types of ships stipulated in the CII regulation. It might be not common to all ship types. Secondly, this method is proposed based on a reasonable analysis and prediction of the CII regulation, and there are still certain uncertainties in the future. Thirdly, this method is also related to the agreed terms in the time charter party signed by the shipowner and the charterer. The agreement on the terms of the charter party is actually a game of strength between the shipowner and the charterer of time charter ships, which is related to the negotiation strength of the two parties and their grasp of the term. Finally, this method is based on certain assumptions. In fact, in addition to the freight rate fluctuations discussed above, there are multiple environmental factors, such as the velocity and direction of wind, waves, and water flow, which will affect the speed model. The navigation environment data should be added in the model. The speed of the ship can be adjusted in real time according to the above factors.

In the future, other technical, operational and market-based measures can be further explored, and the optimal carbon emission reduction measure can be provided according to the different conditions of the shipping market.
