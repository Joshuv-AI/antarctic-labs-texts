Response to Letter Regarding Article, Association Between Nut Consumption and Metabolic Syndrome in Korean Adults
Authors: Sunhye Shin
Published: 2025

I would like to thank the authors of the letter regarding my article previously published in the Journal of Lipid and Atherosclerosis1 for their constructive comments and suggestions for future research.

It would indeed be interesting to examine the association between the consumption of specific nut types and the risk of metabolic syndrome (MetS). However, according to Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES) VII,2 only 24.6% of Koreans reported consuming nuts, and the proportion for each nut type was relatively low. Approximately 13.0% of Koreans consumed peanuts, followed by 9.8% for almonds, 9.7% for walnuts, and 6.4% for chestnuts; fewer than 2.0% consumed other types of nuts. These low prevalence rates make subgroup analyses difficult, and even when statistical significance is achieved, the findings may lack clinical relevance in the Korean population. Such analyses may be more meaningful in populations with higher nut consumption. For populations with lower intake, such as Koreans, it may be more appropriate to group nuts according to nutrient composition—for example, low-fat nuts, nuts high in monounsaturated fatty acids, and nuts high in polyunsaturated fatty acids—and then examine the effects of these categories.

As the authors noted, dietary assessments beyond a single 24-hour recall, such as food frequency questionnaires (FFQs) or dietary records, may more accurately capture regular dietary habits. Preparation methods (e.g., raw, roasted, steamed, baked, salted) could also influence outcomes since they alter nutrient density and salt content. Previous studies using semi-quantitative FFQs have reported inverse associations between nut consumption and the risk of MetS in both Western and Asian populations.3,4 However, little is known about whether differently prepared nuts exert divergent effects on MetS risk, and KNHANES VII2 provides insufficient data on preparation methods. Future longitudinal or intervention studies employing multiple dietary assessments that include preparation methods will help clarify the association more precisely, and may even establish causality.

Contrary to the authors’ expectations, higher nut consumption was not more effective in my study. When participants’ nut intake was categorized into 3 groups—no consumption, intake below the median, and intake equal to or above the median—the difference between the under-median (odds ratio [OR], 0.71; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.51–0.99) and over-median groups (OR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.46–0.95) among middle-aged men (40–64 years) was not large, and the association disappeared among older adults (≥65 years). This result may have been influenced by the reduced sample size after age- and sex-stratification. In another study without stratification, higher nut consumption was associated with a lower OR of MetS in Korean adults.4

Although adjustments were made for lifestyle-related confounders, including alcohol intake, smoking, and physical activity, the association patterns varied by age and sex, suggesting a role for metabolic or hormonal changes. For example, the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance has been shown to be inversely associated with appendicular skeletal muscle mass in older Korean adults,5 and premenopausal Korean women had a lower risk of MetS than postmenopausal women.6 Skeletal muscle is a major tissue for insulin-mediated glucose uptake,7 and declining estrogen levels are associated with features of MetS, including increased central adiposity and altered lipid and glucose profiles.8 Therefore, it would be valuable to examine associations after adjusting for skeletal muscle mass or to compare outcomes between pre- and postmenopausal women.

I am grateful for the interest shown by the authors and look forward to future studies that will address their thoughtful comments and suggestions.
