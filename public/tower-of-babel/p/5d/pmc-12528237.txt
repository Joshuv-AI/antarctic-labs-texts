Effect of neoadjuvant targeted therapy on the tumor microenvironment in resectable lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Ling Yi, Heng Yao, Zhexin Bai, Ziwei Xu, Huimin Li, Yuting Cheng, Chong Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeoadjuvant targeted therapy has emerged as a promising strategy for resectable non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). The analysis of the immune status of tumor microenvironment (TME) after targeted therapies is crucial for understanding the impact of targeted therapy on the TME and providing a basis for synergistic therapeutic approaches.MethodsForty-two patients with resectable lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) were enrolled in this study, and multiplex immunofluorescence technology was used to explore the immune status of the TME after neoadjuvant targeted therapies.ResultsAmong the 42 patients, 9 (21.4%) and 33 (78.6%) had ALK and EGFR mutations, respectively, and TKIs were their first-line treatment. All patients received R0 resection, and thoracoscopic minimally invasive surgery were the predominant method. Seven (16.7%) patients reached pathological complete response (pCR), 4 (9.5%) get major pathological response (MPR), and these 11 patients were classified into the MPR group. The remaining 31 (73.8%) patients were non-MPR. The densities of CD8 + T cells (P < 0.001, P = 0.001), GZMB + CD8 + T cells (P = 0.004, P = 0.008), PD-1 + CD8 + T cells (P = 0.019, P = 0.036), macrophages (P = 0.020, P = 0.007), M1 macrophages (P = 0.010, P = 0.007), and ratios of CD8 + T/Treg (P < 0.001, P = 0.026) in TME were significantly higher in the MPR group and ALK mutation group compared with non-MPR and EGFR group. There were positive correlations between CD8 + T cells, PD-1 + CD8 + T cells (r = 0.397, P = 0.009) and GZMB + CD8 + T cells (r = 0.351, P = 0.023); CD8 + T cells and macrophages (r = 0.343, P = 0.026), and M1 macrophages (r = 0.412, P = 0.007). Additionally, eleven patients experienced disease progress during the follow-up period, and the Log-Rank test revealed that MPR patients tended to get longer PFS compared with non-MPR patients (P = 0.063), especially patients with higher densities of macrophages in the TME had significantly longer PFS (P = 0.042).ConclusionTKI-targeted therapy could reduce tumor burden, facilitating complete surgical resection. Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality and incidence [1]. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is a major type of lung cancer that contributes to approximately 85% of lung cancer [2]. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the predominant histological phenotype of NSCLC, evolving from epithelial cells located in mucosal glands, and represents about 50% of NSCLC [1, 3]. With the rapidly evolving treatment, such as immunotherapy and targeted therapy, the overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) of patients with metastatic NSCLC have been markedly prolonged [4]. However, over the past 20 years, few major advances have been reported in the treatment for resectable NSCLC. Checkmate-816 (NCT02998528) evaluated neoadjuvant nivolumab plus chemotherapy in patients with resectable lung cancer. It significantly improved PFS and pCR and has significantly advanced the field of neoadjuvant treatment. However, this strategy excluded patients with EGFR and ALK mutations [5, 6].

Recently, the application of targeted therapies in the neoadjuvant setting has gained attention as a promising strategy for resectable LUAD [7, 8]. Neoadjuvant EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), such as osimertinib, and ALK inhibitors, such as alectinib, have demonstrated potential in reducing tumor burden, facilitating complete surgical resection, reducing local and distant relapses without increasing adverse events [9–11]. Clinical studies, including EMERGING-CTONG 1103, have highlighted the efficacy of TKIs in improving PFS compared to neoadjuvant chemotherapy [12]. Similarly, alectinib has shown significant tumor shrinkage in ALK-positive LUAD, reducing postoperative recurrence [13, 14]. However, the precise effects of these therapies on the TME during the neoadjuvant phase remain underexplored.

The TME, comprising tumor cells, immune cells, stromal cells, various matrix components and associated cytokines, is a dynamic entity that significantly influences therapeutic responses and patients prognosis [15]. Post-neoadjuvant therapy, the TME undergoes significant immunological and molecular remodeling. EGFR and ALK mutations in LUADs are associated with a cold TME, which recruits immunosuppressive immune cells, such as tumor associated macrophages (TAMs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), secretes inhibitory cytokines and metabolites, and exhibits low response rates to PD-1 pathway blockade [16–18]. Although targeted therapies are recommended as first-line treatment for LUADs with EGFR and ALK mutations, drug resistance may be an inevitable challenge [19]. A comprehensive analysis of the TME after targeted therapies is crucial for understanding its effects, elucidating resistance mechanisms, and establishing a foundation for rational combination strategies [20].

Forty-two patients with LUAD who underwent neoadjuvant targeted therapy and subsequent surgery were included for this study. We summarized these patients’ clinical characteristics, surgical details, and pathological response. Multiplex immunofluorescence technology was employed to explore the effects of TKI neoadjuvant therapies on the TME of LUAD patients. This study preliminarily unveils the correlation between TME status and patients’ efficacy and prognosis after targeted therapy. It provides a foundation for optimizing combination treatment strategies and offers novel insights to enhance the therapeutic outcomes for LUAD.

Methods

Patients and study design

Patients who were histologically confirmed with NSCLC and had ALK or EGFR mutation through next-generation sequencing (NGS) at Beijing Chest Hospital from January 14, 2021 to October 31, 2024 were enrolled. Inclusion criteria: aged between 18 and 75 years; stage IIA to IVA according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification; adequate cardiac and pulmonary function; TKIs was first line treatment. Patients were excluded if they had another type of cancer at the same time; were not suitable for subsequent surgery or had no pathological evaluation, or had incomplete information. All enrolled patients received subsequent surgery.

Demographic and clinical information, including age, gender, TNM staging, details of neoadjuvant treatment, duration of treatment, surgical records, imaging data, and pathological evaluation results were collected. The final follow-up date was set for Jul 3, 2025. This research was approved by the ethics committee of Beijing Chest Hospital (YJS-2023-24), and all patients signed an informed consent form.

Response evaluation

To assess tumor response during TKIs, radiological assessments were performed at baseline and at each return visit. According to the “Solid Tumor Response Evaluation Criteria” (RECIST v1.1), the treatment response was divided into complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD) and progressive disease (PD), and all treated patients were continued to be monitored. The pathological responses were evaluated by professional pathologists in Beijing Chest Hospital. Pathologic complete response (pCR) was defined as 0% of residual tumor in living cells, 0%~10% of living tumor cells are the major pathological response (MPR), > 10% of living tumor cells are non-MPR, in hematoxylin and eosin staining view.

Tissue collection and multiplex Immunofluorescence analysis

Forty-two LUAD patients’ tumor tissues were obtained during surgical resection, all tissues were fixed in formalin and sectioned into 4 μm slices for subsequent analysis. TME were assessed through multiplexed quantitative immunofluorescence (QIF) in situ using the PANO 6-Plex IHC Kit (Panovue, Beijing, China, 0003100100) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the histological sections of the patients were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval; then tissue sections were stained with antibodies as follow, panel 1: pan-CKs (ab27988, clone AE1/AE3, 1:500 dilution), CD8 (ab17147, clone C8/144B, 1:200), GZMB (ab208586, EPR20129-217, 1:500), CD86(CST91882S, E2G8P, 1:200), CD68 (ab955, clone KP1, 1:5000); panel 2: pan-CKs, CD8, Foxp3 (ab20034, clone 236 A/E7, 1:200), and PD-1 (ab52587, NAT105, 1:50). The stained slides were scanned using a Vectra multispectral microscope (Akoya Biosciences). Monochromatic images of the stained tissue sections were acquired, and the spectra of each fluorophore were extracted to create the required spectral library for multiplex immunohistochemical staining, which was performed in InForm (v2.6.0, Akoya Biosciences). The data represented as the percentage of positive cells out of the total number of cells. The stained slides were visually inspected by a pathologist. The numbers of neighboring target (single or double positive) cells within a 20 μm radius around specific immune cells (single or double positive) was phenotyped by inForm 2.6.0 and calculated by the phenoptr package in R (v3.1.0).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software) and IBM SPSS Statistics 27 (International Business Machines Corporation). Given that the data from the two groups did not satisfy the assumption of normal distribution, a non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was employed for inter-group comparisons. For categorical variables, Fisher’s exact test or chi-square test was applied as appropriate for the specific conditions. To evaluate the degree of association between the two sets of data, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used. In survival analysis, Log-rank tests were performed to compare survival curves across different groups, and Cox proportional hazards regression models were utilized to identify factors influencing patient survival times. In all statistical tests, a P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient clinical characteristics

A total of 42 LUAD patients were recruited in this study, 21.4% (9/42) and 78.6% (33/42) patient were ALK and EGFR mutations, respectively. EGFR and ALK-TKI were administered as the first-line treatment, and all patients subsequently underwent surgical resection. The study schema was illustrated in Fig. 1A. The median age of the 42 patients was 59 years (range:37–74 years), 54.8% (23/42) were female and 45.2% (19/42) were male. Regarding smoking status, 31.0% (13/42) were smokers and 69.0% (29/42) were non-smokers. Clinical T and N staging showed that 38.1% (16/42) of patients were in stage IA-IIB and 61.9% (26/42) were in stage IIIA-IVA. Fifty-seven point two percent of patients (24/42) were in T1-T2 stage, and 42.8% (18/42) were in T3-T4; 69.0% (29/42) and 31.0% (13/42) of patients were categorized as clinical N0-N1 and N2-N3. The treatment and clinical evaluation details were as follows: second-generation ALK-TKIs were the predominant agents (alectinib in 6 cases and ensartinib in 2 cases). Icotinib (39.4%, 13/33) and osimertinib (24.2%, 8/33) were the primary EGFR TKIs. The median neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) duration for the 42 patients was 77 days (IQR: 63–110), 85.7% (36/42) patients’ treatment time was less than 6 months and 14.3% (6/42) was over 6 months. Based on patients’ CT dynamic changes, the tumor shrinkage ranged from 0 to 80% (median: 36.5%). According to the RECIST criteria, 66.7% (28/42) of patients achieved PR, 33.3% (14/42) showed SD, and none had PD. According to the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer multidisciplinary recommendation for the assessment of response in surgically resected lung carcinomas after neoadjuvant therapy, 7 patients had no viable tumor cells in the lesions, and 4 had ≤ 10% viable tumor cells. These 11 patients were classified into the MPR group, while the remaining patients with > 10% viable tumor cells were classified as the non-MPR group in the statistical analysis. The clinical information of patients, including sex, TNM staging, radiologic evaluation, pathological response, surgical scope, tumor shrinkage, and target agent, duration of NAT are summarized in Fig. 1B and Supplementary Table 1. Changes in tumor size and the composition of resection tissue from representative MPR and non-MPR cases are shown in Fig. 2. Correlations between patients’ clinical characteristics and their pathological response

After targeted therapy, all patients received R0 resection, and minimally invasive methods were the predominant method, such as 88.1% (37/42) of patients underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), while 11.9% (5/42) underwent thoracotomy. Differences in the density of cytotoxic CD8 + T cells and macrophages in TME among patients with different pathological response after targeted therapy

Cytotoxic CD8 + T cells are key effector cells that can kill tumor cells, while M1 macrophages are the essential antigen-presenting cells and attract more CD8 + T cells into the TME through CXCL9. Therefore, we analyzed the densities of CD8 + T, GZMB + CD8 + T cell, macrophage and M1 macrophage in the TME after targeted therapy. CD68 + cells represent macrophages, and CD86 + CD68 + cells represent M1 macrophages. The multiplex immunofluorescence results showed the density and distribution of CD8 + T cells, GZMB + CD8 + T cells, macrophages and M1 macrophages in the resection tissues from MPR and non-MPR patients (Fig. 3A-B). The densities of CD8 + T cells (MPR, 15.83%, 9.46–17.63% versus Non-MPR, 3.39%, 2.44–4.80%; P < 0.001), GZMB + CD8 + T cells (MPR, 0.218%, 0.065–0.465% versus Non-MPR, 0.108%, 0.034–0.166%; P = 0.004), macrophages (MPR, 7.71%, 5.11–8.78% versus Non-MPR, 4.70%, 3.71–8.82%; P = 0.020) and M1 macrophages (MPR, 3.57%, 1.67–4.68% versus Non-MPR, 1.78, 0.81–2.58%; P = 0.010) were significantly higher in the MPR group (Fig. 3C-F).

Next, we evaluated the densities of CD8 + T cells, PD-1 + CD8 + T cells, Tregs, PD-1 + Tregs, and ratios of CD8 + T cell/Treg and PD-1 + CD8 + T/ PD-1 + Treg in the resection tissue to evaluate whether anti-PD-1 treatment could be used after targeted therapy. The immune cell density and distribution in the resection tissues from MPR and non-MPR patients were showed in Fig. 5A, B. The densities of PD-1 + CD8 + T cells (MPR, 0.600%, 0.343–1.108% versus Non-MPR, 0.270%, 0.047–0.438%; P = 0.019) in the TME after therapy were significantly higher in the MPR group than in the non-MPR group. The densities of Tregs and PD-1 + Tregs showed no significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 5C-G).

ALK-TKI demonstrates stronger immune activation compared to EGFR-TKI

In addition, we compared the immune status of the TME between the ALK-TKI and EGFR-TKI subgroups. The results showed that the densities of CD8 + T cell (ALK, 15.83%, 8.02–17.63% versus EGFR, 6.30%, 2.44–9.46%; P = 0.001), PD-1 + CD8 + T cell (ALK, 0.54%, 0.27–0.91% versus EGFR, 0.33%, 0.05–0.55%; P = 0.036), macrophage(ALK, 7.71%, 6.74–11.14% versus EGFR, 4.52%, 4.12–6.99%; P = 0.007), M1 macrophage (ALK, 3.57%, 1.86–4.84% versus EGFR, 1.78%, 1.25–2.56%; P = 0.007), and the ratio of CD8 + T cell/Treg (ALK, 13.03, 5.52–17.17 versus EGFR, 6.02, 1.94–8.20; P = 0.026) were significantly higher in the ALK-TKI group than in the EGFR-TKI group. These results suggest that ALK-TKIs may induce stronger anti-tumor immune response.

Correlations between the density of infiltration immune cells and patients’ PFS

Among 42 patients, the median follow-up time was 642 days (range: 73–1641 days) as of July 3, 2025 and 11 patients (1MPR, 10 non-MPR) experienced disease progression, including 6 patients with intrapulmonary recurrence or metastasis, 3 patients with brain metastases, 1 with adrenal metastasis, and 1 with transformed into small cell lung cancer. Furthermore, we analyzed the correlations between patients pathological response, gene mutation type, immune cell densities and patients’ PFS. The results showed that patients in MPR group tended to obtain longer PFS than non-MPR group (Fig. 7A, P = 0.063). In addition, patients with higher densities of macrophage in the TME had significantly longer PFS (Fig. 7E, P = 0.042). Neither gene mutation type nor other immune densities showed correlations with prognosis (Fig. 7).

Discussion

In this study, we first evaluated the feasibility of neoadjuvant targeted therapy and the subsequent surgery. Our results showed that a total of 42 patients with LUAD, 16.7% (7/42) of patients achieved a pCR, 9.5% (4/42) achieved a MPR. The median PFS was 642 days (21 months), Eleven patients experienced progression, with intrapulmonary and brain metastasis are the most common progressive events. In a phase II RCT, Yi-Long Wu’s team compared erlotinib with gemcitabine plus cisplatin (GC) as neoadjuvant therapy in NSCLC patients with EGFR mutations. The MPR rates were 9.7% and 0%, respectively, and the erlotinib arm (21.5 months) showed significantly longer median PFS than the GC arm (11.4 months) [12]. NeoADAURA trial (NCT04351555), reported an MPR rate of 25% in osimertinib monotherapy group and also demonstrated significantly higher MPR rate versus placebo plus chemotherapy [21]. LungMate 007 trial (NCT04685070) reported the MPR and pCR rates were 21.7% and 13.0%, respectively [22]. Other clinical trials of third-generation EGFR-TKIs achieved MPR rates of 10.7–14.8% and median DFS of 40.9 months in neoadjuvant settings [11, 23]. Our MPR rate was consistent with the clinical trials mentioned above. Collectively, these positive findings support the potential inclusion of targeted neoadjuvant therapy in clinical guidelines.

Regarding the surgical outcomes after neoadjuvant targeted therapy, clinical trials, such as NEOS, NCT03433469 etc., indicate that most patients achieve R0 resection after neoadjuvant targeted therapy [11, 23, 24] suggesting an acceptable surgical feasibility after targeted therapy. Hao Chen et al. also reported that neoadjuvant TKI therapy did not increase surgical difficulty or complications [25]. Other studies have highlighted challenges such as tissue adhesion, leading to omitted lymph node dissection [26]. In this study, R0 resection was achieved in 100% of cases, and 88.1% of patients underwent with thoracoscopic minimally invasive surgery. The median operative time was 155 min, and median blood loss was 50 mL. These findings suggest that neoadjuvant targeted therapy does not significantly increase surgical difficulty.

Understanding the dynamics and differences in TME of patients with different responses is gradually recognized as the key to optimizing cancer treatments, as TME plays key roles in both therapy efficacy and resistance [20, 27, 28]. In neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, T cells are crucial effector cells influencing treatment efficacy [29–33]. In this study, we found that the densities of CD8 + T cells, PD-1 + CD8 + T cells and GZMB + CD8 + T cells in the TME of MPR group were significantly higher than those in non-MPR group, indicating that patients with better response to TKIs exhibited stronger anti-tumor immune activation in the TME. In addition to T cell subset, we also analyzed macrophage infiltration in the TME. Although macrophages were less than CD8 + T cells, their presence, including macrophage and M1 macrophage, was associated with pathological response. In particular, macrophage infiltration was positively correlated with patients’ PFS. The TME profiles of MPR and non-MPR patients after targeted therapy are illustrated in Fig. 8. Caicun Zhou’ team reported increased myeloid cell infiltration and antigen presentation were increased in EGFR19DEL/T790M-driven lung tumors treated with osimertinib [34]. Moreover, CD8 + T cell density was positively correlated with both macrophage and M1 macrophage densities and spatial analysis further revealed the clinical significance in localization patterns among CD8 + T subsets and macrophage subsets. Because signal molecules can be directly transmitted when the distance between two cells is less than 20 μm [35, 36]. Our result demonstrated that patients’ pathological response correlated with the numbers of macrophages, M1 macrophages within 20 μm around CD8 + T cells and CD8 + T cell activation closely related to the numbers of M1 macrophages around them. Thus, the activation of innate immune responses may be the earlier and more critical issue of response toTKI treatment [37].

Targeted therapies generally exert anti-tumor effects by inhibiting rather than eradicating tumor cells, making acquired resistance almost inevitable [38–41]. This is a significant limitation of targeted therapy. Although newly developed targeted drugs are in clinical trials, multimodal therapeutic strategies are needed to improve treatment efficacy [42]. Several clinical trials have evaluated the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant TKIs in combination with chemotherapy, or sequential TKI and chemotherapy, in resectable NSCLC [43–45]. Combining targeted therapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) is also a promising approach: targeted therapy may prime the TME, while ICIs offer durable responses. This provides a rationale for combination therapy [34, 46]. PD-1 pathway blockade reinvigorates not only PD-1 + CD8 + T cells but also PD-1 + Tregs [47]. Accordingly, we analyzed PD-1 + T cell subset densities in the resected tissues. We believed that MPR patients would benefit from PD-1 mAbs because they had higher densities of PD-1 + CD8 + T cell and higher ratios of PD-1 + CD8 + T cell/ PD-1 + Treg in the TME. Li Li et al. found that an immunologically active TME plays a pivotal role improved response to immunotherapy [48]. TKI-refractory patients received limited benefit from the treatment of immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy [49]. In addition, there may be an optimal window prior to immune suppression occurs for immunotherapy to be effective [20]. Overall, therapeutic combinations and biomarker identification require further investigation [18, 50].

In clinical, compared with EGFR-positive NSCLC, ALK-positive cases appear to achieve higher MPR rates in targeted neoadjuvant therapy [20, 27, 51]. In this study, the MPR rate in ALK and EGFR groups was 66.7% (6/9, 5 pCR, 1 MPR) and 15.2% (5/33, 2 pCR, 3MPR), respectively. There was a significant difference in pathological response between two groups (supplementary Figure 1, Chi-Square Test, P = 0.002). The EGFR-mutant NSCLC cohort also showed limited pathological regression after TKI thearpy [12, 52]. Higher pCR rates in ALK-driven tumors have been similarly observed in phase II trials of next-generation TKIs [24, 53]. Regarding immune cell infiltration into TME, ALK positive samples exhibited a higher density of CD8 + T cells, PD-1 + CD8 + T cells, GZMB + CD8 + T cells, macrophages, and M1 macrophages in TME, as well as a significantly higher CD8 + T/Treg ratio compared with EGFR group, indicating ALK and EGFR TKIs had distinct regulatory effects on the TME, ALK may induce stronger anti-tumor immune response.

There are several limitations in this study. First, the sample size was small, especially in subgroups, restricted statistical power. Second, this was a retrospective study, and prospective clinical trials of neoadjuvant targeted therapy are needed to validate our findings. Third, the follow-up period was relatively short. Long-term follow-up (e.g., at three and five years) is required to evaluate patient survival and the sustained impact of TME immune status on prognosis. Lastly, advanced technologies such as single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptomics and mass cytometry should be applied for a more comprehensive characterization of the TME. Future studies will aim to address these limitations.

Conclusion

Our research reveals that after TKI therapy, the TME differed significantly between patients with varying pathological responses. Those with better responses exhibited greater infiltration of CD8 + T cell and macrophage subsets. Especially more macrophage infiltration indicated of longer PFS. In addition, ALK and EGFR TKIs exhibited distinct effects on the TME and ALK-TKI treatment inducing a robust anti-tumor immune response.
