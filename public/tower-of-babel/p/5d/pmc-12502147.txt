Osteoblast dysfunction associated with mitophagy suppression under simulated microgravity
Authors: Jindong Xue, Min Wang, Songsong Liu, Congncong Xu, Haoyang Yu, Yong Guo, Biao Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone loss is a significant health concern during spaceflight and mechanical unloading. Simulated microgravity (SMG) disrupts bone homeostasis by inhibiting osteoblast proliferation and differentiation while promoting apoptosis. Although these functional effects have been reported, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Mitochondrial quality control, particularly mitophagy involving the PINK1/Parkin pathway, may play a key role. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between osteogenic dysfunction and mitochondrial damage under SMG conditions and preliminarily validate the potential link using the small molecule probe icariin (ICA).MethodsAn SMG model was established using a rotary cell culture system. Cell proliferation was assessed by CCK-8 assay, apoptosis was analyzed via flow cytometry, and osteogenic differentiation was evaluated by alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and Alizarin Red staining. Expression levels of relevant genes and proteins were measured by qPCR and Western blot. Mitochondrial function was assessed through ATP content, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, JC-1 staining for mitochondrial membrane potential, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for ultrastructural observation. Additionally, cells were treated with the mitochondrial function-related small molecule icariin (ICA) to observe its regulatory effects on mitophagy markers (PINK1, Parkin, p62, LC3B) expression and osteogenic function.ResultsSMG significantly inhibited osteoblast proliferation and differentiation and induced apoptosis. These changes were accompanied by impaired mitochondrial function and downregulated expression of mitophagy-related genes. TEM revealed mitochondrial swelling and disrupted cristae structure. Treatment with ICA partially restored mitochondrial function and mitophagy marker expression, along with improved expression of osteogenic markers and cell viability.ConclusionsSMG induces osteogenic dysfunction, mitochondrial damage, and downregulation of mitophagy-related gene expression. The results suggest that impaired mitophagy may be a key mechanism underlying unloading-induced bone loss, and ICA, as a small molecule modulator, holds potential as a therapeutic intervention.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12938-025-01454-w.Keywords: Microgravity, Osteoblast, Mitophagy, Icariin

Introduction

Bone homeostasis is a dynamic process regulated by the balance between bone formation by osteoblasts and bone resorption by osteoclasts [1]. Mechanical stimulation, such as the load provided by Earth’s gravity, plays a pivotal role in maintaining this balance [2]. Under normal conditions, mechanical cues are transduced into biochemical signals within osteoblasts, promoting their proliferation, differentiation, and bone matrix production [3]. However, when the mechanical load is reduced—as in long-duration spaceflight, prolonged bed rest, or orthopedic immobilization—this balance is disrupted, leading to rapid and progressive bone loss [4, 5].

Studies have shown that bone mineral density can decrease by up to 1.5% per month during microgravity exposure, primarily due to suppressed osteoblast activity and enhanced osteoclast-mediated bone resorption [6]. In the microgravity environment, osteoblasts exhibit reduced proliferation, increased apoptosis, impaired mineralization, and downregulation of key osteogenic markers such as RUNX2, COL1, and OCN [7–9]. Despite growing evidence, the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying osteoblast dysfunction under microgravity remain incompletely understood [10].

One of the emerging hypotheses involves mitochondrial dysfunction. Mitochondria not only serve as the powerhouse of the cell, supplying ATP to support osteoblast function, but also regulate cellular redox balance, calcium homeostasis, and apoptosis. Disruption of mitochondrial integrity under microgravity has been associated with elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), and energy depletion—all of which contribute to cellular damage and impaired osteogenesis [11–13].

Mitophagy, a specialized form of autophagy that selectively removes damaged mitochondria, is essential for maintaining mitochondrial quality and cellular homeostasis. The PINK1/Parkin signaling pathway orchestrates mitophagy by identifying and labeling dysfunctional mitochondria for degradation. When mitophagy is inhibited, damaged mitochondria accumulate, exacerbating oxidative stress and triggering cell death [14]. Recent studies have implicated impaired mitophagy in various diseases, including Parkinson’s disease, metabolic disorders, and more recently, bone pathologies such as osteoporosis and disuse-induced bone loss [15–17].

Despite these insights, few studies have directly examined the link between microgravity-induced mitochondrial stress and mitophagy in osteoblasts. Whether suppression of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy contributes to osteoblast dysfunction under microgravity is still poorly understood [18]. Addressing this gap is critical for developing novel interventions to counteract skeletal degeneration in space environments or in clinical settings involving mechanical unloading.

In our previous studies, Icariin, a flavonoid compound derived from Epimedium, has emerged as a promising pharmacological agent due to its well-established osteoprotective properties. We demonstrated that Icariin enhances osteoblast proliferation and differentiation, promotes osteogenic marker expression, and improves bone formation under conditions of mechanical unloading. However, deeper investigations suggested that its effects may extend beyond classical osteogenic signaling pathways and involve mitochondrial regulation. To explore this possibility, we performed literature-based semantic analysis using the Coremine Medical database. Notably, Icariin showed a relatively strong co-occurrence with the term “mitophagy” under the “Drug” category, with a relevance score of 0.010458 (see Attachment 1). This observation led us to hypothesize that osteoblast dysfunction under microgravity may be closely linked to impaired mitophagy, and Icariin might exert its protective effect by modulating this process.

In this study, we established a simulated microgravity (SMG) model using a Rotary Cell Culture System (RCCS) and examined its effects on osteoblast proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, mitochondrial function, and mitophagy [19]. We aimed to elucidate the role of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy in osteoblast response to microgravity, thereby providing mechanistic insight into how mechanical unloading impairs bone formation and suggesting potential therapeutic targets to prevent microgravity-induced osteoporosis [20].

Result

Simulated microgravity inhibits osteoblast proliferation, induces apoptosis, and suppresses osteogenic differentiation

The effects of simulated microgravity (SMG) on osteoblast function were comprehensively evaluated by assessing cell viability, apoptosis, and osteogenic differentiation. CCK-8 assay results (Fig. 1a) demonstrated that MC3T3-E1 cells cultured under SMG conditions exhibited a significant decrease in proliferation compared to the control group (p < 0.01), indicating impaired metabolic activity and growth potential. Flow cytometry analysis following Annexin V-FITC/PI staining (Fig. 1b) revealed a marked increase in early and late apoptotic cells in the SMG group relative to controls (p < 0.01), suggesting that apoptosis contributes to the anti-proliferative effects induced by microgravity.

Simulated microgravity inhibits osteoblast proliferation, induces apoptosis, and suppresses osteogenic differentiation. A CCK-8 assay demonstrated a significant reduction in cell viability in the SMG group compared to the control group (n = 6). B Flow cytometry analysis following Annexin V-FITC/PI staining showed an increased percentage of apoptotic cells under SMG conditions (n = 3). C ALP staining and quantification revealed decreased enzyme activity in the SMG group, indicating impaired early osteogenic differentiation (n = 6). D Alizarin Red staining illustrated reduced mineralized nodule formation after osteogenic induction (n = 3). E Relative mRNA expression levels of osteogenic markers RUNX2, COL-1, and OCN were significantly downregulated under SMG, as determined by qPCR (n = 3). F Western blot analysis confirmed corresponding decreases in protein expression levels of these markers (n = 3). ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001

In parallel, osteogenic differentiation was examined through ALP activity assays (Fig. 1c) and Alizarin Red staining (Fig. 1d) SMG exposure significantly reduced ALP activity, indicating suppression of early-stage differentiation. After 21 days of osteogenic induction, the SMG group displayed notably fewer calcium nodules, reflecting impaired matrix mineralization. Molecular analyses by qPCR (Fig. 1e) and Western blot (Fig. 1f) further confirmed a significant downregulation of key osteogenic markers RUNX2 and COL-1 under SMG conditions.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that simulated microgravity significantly compromises osteoblast viability by inhibiting proliferation and promoting apoptosis, while concurrently impairing differentiation at both functional and transcriptional levels. These detrimental effects may underlie the bone loss observed in microgravity environments.

Simulated microgravity induces mitochondrial dysfunction

To assess mitochondrial function under simulated microgravity, several indicators were analyzed. Flow cytometry results (Fig. 2a) showed that intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were significantly elevated in the SMG group, suggesting increased oxidative stress. Furthermore, JC-1 staining (Fig. 2b) revealed a pronounced loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), as evidenced by a lower red/green fluorescence ratio. ATP content measurements (Fig. 2c) also indicated reduced mitochondrial energy production in the SMG group.

Simulated microgravity induces mitochondrial dysfunction. A ROS levels were significantly elevated under SMG (n = 3). B JC-1 assay showing loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) (n = 3). C ATP production was significantly reduced (n = 5). D Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of CON and SMG groups showing mitochondrial swelling, cristae disruption, and reduced autolysosomes under simulated microgravity conditions (n = 3). ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Fig. 2d) revealed marked ultrastructural differences between osteoblasts in the CON and SMG groups. In the CON group, osteoblasts showed no obvious swelling, with evenly distributed cytoplasm and abundant organelles, most of which appeared intact. The plasma membrane was well preserved. The nuclei (N) were large, mostly round, and eccentrically located, with the nuclear envelope displaying two invaginations. Three distinct nucleoli (Nu) with dense structures were observed within the nuclei, where chromatin was predominantly euchromatin and the nuclear envelope remained intact without evident perinuclear space widening. Mitochondria (M) were abundant, mostly round or oval, and appeared morphologically normal with intact cristae, though a few displayed mild swelling, uneven matrix distribution, partial cristae reduction, or occasional membrane blurring. The rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) was abundant with moderate dilation. Several lipid droplets (LD) and multiple autolysosomes (ASS, six in total) were present in the cytoplasm.

In contrast, osteoblasts in the SMG group exhibited mild overall swelling, uneven cytoplasmic matrix distribution, and slightly reduced electron density. Organelles were fewer in number, and some showed marked swelling. Localized plasma membrane disruptions were evident. The nuclei (N) were enlarged, irregularly oval, and centrally located, containing abundant euchromatin with scattered regions of increased heterochromatin; the perinuclear space was locally widened. Mitochondria (M) were predominantly oval, with most showing mild to moderate swelling, pale and uneven matrix, and frequent cristae breakage or loss. The RER was mildly dilated, with partial ribosome detachment on its surface and occasional blurred cisternal membranes. A few lipid droplets (LD) and two autolysosomes (ASS) were observed in the cytoplasm.

Simulated microgravity reduces the expression of mitophagy-related genes and proteins

To investigate the potential involvement of mitophagy in SMG-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, we assessed the expression levels of several mitophagy-related markers. qPCR analyses (Fig. 3a) and Western blot analyses (Fig. 3b) revealed decreased expression of PINK1, Parkin, and LC3B in the SMG group, while P62 expression was increased. These findings suggest a possible alteration in mitophagy-related pathways under simulated microgravity, potentially affecting mitochondrial homeostasis in osteoblasts.

Simulated microgravity reduces the expression of mitophagy-related markers. A Relative mRNA expression levels of mitophagy-associated genes (PINK1, Parkin, P62) (n = 3). B Western blot analysis showing protein levels of mitophagy-related markers (n = 3). ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001

Relationship between mitophagy restoration and osteogenic dysfunction rescue under simulated microgravity

To further investigate the relationship between mitophagy and osteogenic dysfunction, we performed both gene expression and molecular docking analyses in icariin (ICA)-treated cells. Molecular docking results (Fig. 4a) revealed that ICA exhibited strong binding affinities with several key mitophagy-related proteins, including PINK1 (− 9.3 kcal/mol), Parkin (− 6.5 kcal/mol), p62 (− 7.3 kcal/mol), and LC3B (− 8.6 kcal/mol). These results suggest that ICA may directly interact with these targets to modulate mitophagy. Consistently, qPCR analysis (Fig. 4b) showed that ICA treatment significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of these genes, which were otherwise suppressed under simulated microgravity (SMG) conditions.

Icariin (ICA) Mediates Mitophagy Enhancement and Osteogenic Dysfunction Alleviation under Simulated Microgravity (SMG). A Molecular docking images showing the binding of ICA to mitophagy-related proteins PINK1, Parkin, p62, and LC3B. B qPCR analysis showing mRNA expression levels of mitophagy-related genes (PINK1, Parkin, p62, LC3B) across Control, SMG, and SMG + ICA groups (n = 4). C Intracellular ATP levels significantly increased following ICA treatment compared to SMG (n = 5). D Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of SMG and SMG + ICA groups showing autophagosome-like structures and mitochondrial morphology improvements after ICA treatment (n = 3). E CCK-8 assay quantification of cell viability indicating partial recovery in the ICA-treated group (n = 9). F qPCR analysis of osteogenic markers (OCN, RUNX2, COL-1) showing increased expression in the ICA group compared to SMG (n = 3). G ALP staining images and quantification demonstrating enhanced ALP activity in ICA-treated cells compared to SMG (n = 4). H Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry plots showing reduced apoptotic cell proportions following ICA treatment (n = 3). ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, **** p < 0.0001

ATP assay results (Fig. 4c) showed a significant increase in intracellular ATP content in the ICA-treated group compared to the SMG group, indicating improved mitochondrial function. Consistently, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis (Fig. 4d) revealed ultrastructural differences between osteoblasts in the SMG and SMG+ICA groups. In the SMG group, cells showed no obvious overall swelling, with evenly distributed cytoplasm and abundant organelles, some of which exhibited mild swelling. The plasma membrane was relatively intact. The nuclei (N) appeared slightly irregular with multiple mild invaginations on the nuclear envelope. Prominent and dense nucleoli (Nu) were eccentrically located. A small amount of heterochromatin was scattered within the nucleus. The nuclear envelope was intact but partially blurred, with no evident perinuclear space widening. Mitochondria (M) were abundant, round or oval, mildly swollen with locally pale and uneven matrix, reduced cristae, and preserved membrane structure. The rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) was abundant, mostly mildly dilated with ribosome attachment. Few lipid droplets (LD) and four autolysosomes (ASS) were observed.

In contrast, the SMG+ICA group exhibited cells without obvious edema and a more uniform cytoplasmic matrix. Organelles were abundant, with some showing marked swelling. The plasma membrane was intact with no visible damage, and occasional pseudopodia (PS) were noted. The nuclei (Nu) were irregularly shaped with abundant euchromatin; the nuclear envelope was intact and the perinuclear space normal. Mitochondria (M) were oval, mostly mildly swollen with pale and slightly uneven matrix and minor cristae breakage. The rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER) showed mild to moderate dilation with partial ribosome detachment and occasional blurred membranes. Numerous autolysosomes (ASS, 17) were present in the cytoplasm.

In addition to these ultrastructural improvements, functional assays further demonstrated the beneficial effects of ICA treatment. CCK-8 assay quantification (Fig. 4e) indicated a partial recovery of cell viability in the ICA-treated group compared with the SMG group. qPCR analysis of osteogenic markers (Fig. 4f) showed significantly increased expression levels of OCN, RUNX2, and COL-1 in ICA-treated cells relative to SMG. ALP staining and quantification (Fig. 4g) demonstrated enhanced ALP activity in ICA-treated cells, indicating improved early osteogenic differentiation. Annexin V-FITC/PI flow cytometry plots (Fig. 4h) further confirmed a reduced proportion of apoptotic cells following ICA treatment.

Together, these results demonstrate that icariin not only restores mitophagy-related gene expression and mitochondrial function, but also effectively improves osteoblast proliferation and differentiation suppressed by simulated microgravity. The integration of molecular docking, ATP quantification, ultrastructural analysis, and functional assays provides compelling evidence supporting ICA as a potential therapeutic agent for microgravity-induced osteogenic dysfunction via modulation of mitophagy.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that simulated microgravity (SMG) significantly impairs osteoblast function, accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction and suppression of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy. SMG exposure led to reduced osteogenic differentiation, diminished ALP activity and matrix mineralization, decreased mitochondrial membrane potential, and downregulation of mitophagy-related markers. These findings suggest that mitochondrial homeostasis and autophagy regulation may be involved in the impaired osteogenic activity induced by mechanical unloading.

While previous studies have primarily focused on cytoskeletal disruption, calcium signaling disturbances, and the inhibition of classical osteogenic pathways (such as Wnt/β-catenin and BMP/Smad) under SMG [21], our data highlight mitochondrial quality control—particularly PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy—as a potential regulatory mechanism. Unlike earlier reports showing mild reductions in mitophagic activity, we observed more pronounced suppression, possibly reflecting an unresolved mitochondrial stress state. Furthermore, our findings support a close association between mitochondrial damage and impaired osteoblast maturation, reinforcing the emerging view that mitochondrial dynamics are essential to bone cell mechanosensitivity.

To further investigate this connection, we introduced a small-molecule compound known to affect mitochondrial processes, not as a therapeutic intervention, but as a tool molecule to explore the relationship between mitophagy and osteogenic dysfunction under SMG. Its application partially restored mitochondrial function and gene expression and was accompanied by moderate improvements in osteogenic activity. These results suggest that disruption of mitochondrial quality control may underlie the functional deficits observed in osteoblasts exposed to microgravity-like conditions, providing a mechanistic link rather than a treatment outcome.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that icariin (ICA) can promote osteoblast proliferation, differentiation, and mineralization by activating the PI3K-AKT-eNOS-NO-cGMP-PKG signaling pathway, significantly increasing alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity and the expression of bone-related genes, thereby enhancing osteogenic function [22, 23]. Additionally, ICA protects osteoblast differentiation under hypoxic conditions by regulating HIF-1α gene expression [24]. Although there are currently no direct studies on ICA regulating mitophagy, its analog ICAII has been shown to modulate mitophagy via the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway, suggesting that ICA may participate in maintaining osteoblast function through mitophagy regulation [25]. Our results indicate that under simulated microgravity conditions, ICA activates PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, promoting the clearance of damaged mitochondria, restoring mitochondrial function, and alleviating mitochondrial stress and oxidative damage, thereby improving osteoblast proliferation, increasing ALP activity and mineralization, and protecting normal osteoblast function. In summary, this study elucidates the mechanism by which ICA regulates mitophagy to improve osteoblast function under simulated microgravity conditions, providing new insights for the prevention and treatment of bone loss associated with mechanical unloading.

Several limitations should be considered. First, this study was conducted using a single osteoblast-like cell line (MC3T3-E1), which does not fully reflect the multicellular complexity of bone tissue. Second, although the experiments were conducted with three independent replicates (n = 3) yielding consistent results, the relatively limited sample size may reduce the statistical power to detect more subtle changes in SMG’s effects on mitophagic flux and its downstream osteogenic impacts. Future studies should expand sample sizes and conduct multicenter validations. Third, although the rotary culture system effectively simulates aspects of SMG, it introduces shear stress that may independently influence mitochondrial function. Fourth, our mechanistic analysis focused mainly on the PINK1/Parkin pathway and did not include other regulators such as BNIP3/NIX, DRP1, or MFN2, nor broader stress response mechanisms like mitochondrial biogenesis or ER-mitochondria crosstalk [26, 27]. p62, as a selective autophagy receptor, plays a bridging role in mitophagy by recognizing and linking damaged mitochondria to autophagosomes, facilitating their degradation and clearance [28]. Our study found that under simulated microgravity conditions, mitophagy activity was suppressed while p62 expression was significantly increased, which may reflect a blockade in the autophagy pathway leading to p62 accumulation. This finding is consistent with related reports. However, due to experimental limitations, we were unable to further explore the specific regulatory mechanisms of p62 in mitophagy inhibition induced by microgravity. Future studies are needed to investigate this in more detail.

Future studies should consider using primary osteoblasts or mesenchymal stem cells in more physiologically relevant models, such as 3D cultures or bone-on-a-chip systems. More advanced SMG simulators (e.g., RPMs or clinostats) may help minimize mechanical confounding. Mechanistically, broader profiling of mitochondrial quality control—including fission/fusion dynamics, ROS, and ATP production—should be incorporated. In vivo models like hindlimb unloading in rodents could help determine whether enhancing mitophagy mitigates microgravity-induced bone loss.

Our study raises several important questions. Is mitophagy suppression under SMG reversible, and can modulation of PINK1/Parkin restore osteoblast function? Could other skeletal cell types, such as osteocytes or MSCs, exhibit similar mitochondrial responses? Might mitophagy markers serve as early indicators of bone loss risk under unloading conditions? Finally, while this study used a small molecule as a tool compound, future research may explore whether targeting mitochondrial homeostasis more broadly—through exercise mimetics or mitochondrial-targeted regulators—could support skeletal adaptation in altered gravity environments [29]. These questions warrant further investigation to better link fundamental mechanisms with translational applications in aerospace medicine and bone biology.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that simulated microgravity (SMG) leads to osteoblast dysfunction, characterized by reduced proliferation and differentiation, and increased apoptosis. These changes are closely associated with mitochondrial impairment and downregulation of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy. Our findings suggest that mitochondrial quality control, particularly mitophagy, plays a critical role in maintaining osteoblast function under mechanical unloading. Although a small-molecule compound was used to explore this relationship, further studies are needed to clarify whether modulating mitochondrial homeostasis can effectively mitigate SMG-induced skeletal deterioration. These insights may help advance the understanding of bone loss mechanisms in altered gravity environments.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and osteogenic differentiation

The MC3T3-E1 mouse pre-osteoblast cell line was obtained from the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Beijing, China). Cells were cultured in alpha minimum essential medium (α-MEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Hyclone, USA). Osteogenic differentiation was induced by supplementing the culture medium with 10% FBS, 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 5 mmol/L β-glycerophosphate, and 2 mmol/L glutamine. All cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, and the medium was refreshed every 2 days.

Simulated microgravity exposure

A rotary cell culture system (RCCS; Synthecon Inc., USA) was used to simulate microgravity. MC3T3-E1 cells were seeded onto Cytodex 3 microcarriers (GE Healthcare, USA) at a density of 1×105 cells/mL and cultured in rotating vessels at a constant speed of 20 rpm for 72 hours. Air bubbles were promptly removed during incubation to minimize fluid shear stress.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

Cell proliferation and viability were assessed by seeding cells from each group at a density of 1×105 cells per well in 96-well plates, followed by incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 hours. Subsequently, 100 μL of 10% CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for an additional 2 hours. Optical density (OD) was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader.

Annexin V-FITC/PI staining

Apoptotic and necrotic cells were detected and quantified after 72 hours of simulated microgravity exposure. Cells were harvested using trypsin without EDTA and washed with PBS. Approximately 1×10⁶ cells were resuspended in 500 μL of 1× binding buffer. Annexin V-FITC (5 μL) and propidium iodide (PI, 10 μL) were added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 5 minutes. Flow cytometry was performed (Ex = 488 nm, Em = 530 nm), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

JC-1 staining

Mitochondrial membrane potential changes were evaluated by resuspending approximately 1×10⁶ trypsinized and PBS-washed cells in 0.5 mL of culture medium and mixing with an equal volume of JC-1 staining solution. Cells were incubated at 37 °C for 20 minutes. After staining, the supernatant was discarded, cells were washed twice with 1× JC-1 buffer, and resuspended in 1 mL buffer. The red/green fluorescence ratio was measured by flow cytometry.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay

Intracellular reactive oxygen species levels, as indicators of oxidative stress, were measured using the DCFH-DA probe diluted in PBS to a final concentration of 10 μmol/L. After trypsinization and PBS washing, approximately 1×10⁶ cells were resuspended in the probe solution and incubated at 37 °C for 20 minutes, with gentle inversion every 3–5 minutes. Cells were washed three times with PBS prior to flow cytometry analysis.

ATPase activity assay

Cellular ATPase activity reflecting energy metabolism was determined by lysing cells on ice with 200 μL of lysis buffer per well in a 6-well plate. After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 5 minutes at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected. ATP standards and detection working solution (1:9 dilution) were prepared. Each well received 100 μL of detection reagent and 20 μL of sample or standard. Relative light units (RLU) were recorded using a chemiluminescence reader, and ATP levels were calculated based on the standard curve.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Ultrastructural mitochondrial changes were observed by fixing cell pellets in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 2 hours, followed by overnight storage at 4 °C. Samples were washed with PBS, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 1–2 hours, dehydrated through graded ethanol series, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (50–100 nm) were prepared and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate prior to examination by transmission electron microscopy.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) and reverse transcribed using the PrimeScript® RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II on the CFX96 system (Bio-Rad, USA). GAPDH was used as an internal control. The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher, USA) and quantified by the Bradford method. For each sample, 30 μg of protein was mixed with 4× loading buffer and boiled for 10 minutes. Proteins were separated on a 12% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 2 hours and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: RUNX2 (1:5000), COL-I (1:5000), PINK1 (1:2000), Parkin (1:2000), P62 (1:20000), and GAPDH (1:5000). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000) for 2 hours at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL detection reagent and analyzed with ImageJ software.

ALP activity assay

Mouse serum and cell culture supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 1200 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C. ALP activity was measured using an ALP assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was recorded at 520 nm using a microplate reader.

ALP staining

Cells were washed 2–3 times with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature. After washing with PBS, BCIP/NBT staining solution (prepared as per kit instructions) was added to cover the cells, and incubation was carried out at 37 °C for 30–60 minutes. After staining, cells were washed with PBS and observed under a microscope.

Alizarin red staining

After 21 days of osteogenic induction, cells in 6-well plates were washed twice with PBS and fixed with Olympus Brightfield fixative for 15 minutes, followed by three PBS washes. Alizarin Red solution was added and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 minutes. After staining, cells were washed three times with distilled water and examined microscopically.

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina software

The three-dimensional structure of the PINK1 protein was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Non-essential water molecules and cofactors were removed using PyMOL, and hydrogen atoms were added. The protein structure was then prepared and optimized using AutoDock Tools and saved in PDBQT format. The molecular structure of icariin (ICA) was downloaded from the PubChem database, optimized via Open Babel, and converted to PDBQT format. The docking grid box was defined in AutoDock Tools by specifying center coordinates (x, y, z) and box dimensions (length, width, height) to cover the potential binding site. Docking simulations were performed with AutoDock Vina, generating docking poses and binding affinity scores. The binding modes were visualized in PyMOL to analyze key interactions such as hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic contacts between ICA and PINK1. To ensure result reliability, docking was repeated multiple times with slight adjustments to the grid box parameters, confirming consistency in binding modes and energies.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.
