Complete resolution of recalcitrant scalp pruritus following greater occipital nerve ablation
Authors: Deena Fayyad, Shabnam Afzal, Perya Bhagchandani, Waleed Adawi, Shawn G. Kwatra
Published: 2026

Introduction

Neuropathic pruritus of the scalp can be debilitating and refractory to standard therapies, often requiring multimodal or experimental treatment approaches. Greater occipital nerve blocks and thermal ablation have demonstrated efficacy in refractory head and neck pain syndromes, including occipital neuralgia and migraines.1 Here, we report follow-up of a previously published case of recalcitrant bilateral scalp pruritus in a patient who experienced an excellent but time-limited response to bedside greater occipital nerve blocks with bupivacaine,2 followed by durable symptom control after bilateral greater occipital nerve radiofrequency ablation.

Case report

A 67-year-old man presented with a 3-year history of intractable bilateral occipital scalp pruritus in the setting of cervical spine disease, with magnetic resonance imaging demonstrating disc degeneration and multilevel cervical stenosis. Throughout the course of his care, he failed to respond to multiple treatment regimens including topical corticosteroids, topical tacrolimus, topical roflumilast, tapinarof, capsaicin cream, topical ruxolitinib, intralesional steroid injections, excimer laser, oral methotrexate, naltrexone, gabapentin, and dupilumab. Eventually, he underwent bedside bilateral greater occipital nerve block with 0.25% bupivacaine, which produced marked but temporary improvement lasting approximately 4 weeks per treatment. At the time, his WI-NRS score decreased from 7/10 to 2/10 with resolution of scalp lesions, and his case was reported in JAAD Case Reports.2

Over time, however, the efficacy of the bupivacaine injections declined. Beginning with his ninth injection, symptom relief lasted only 1 week and WI-NRS was at 3/10. The concentration was increased to 0.5% bupivacaine, but benefit again lasted only 1 week. Because of worsening symptoms, he was referred to interventional radiology for bilateral greater occipital nerve (GON) radiofrequency neurotomy with fluoroscopic guidance. The patient was positioned prone and a grounding pad was secured to the left calf, with the skin prepped in the usual sterile manner. The fluoroscope was positioned to provide a caudad slightly obliqued view. Local anesthesia was achieved with 1 mL of 1% lidocaine. Using fluoroscopic guidance, a sterile 20-gauge 100-mm radiofrequency cannula was positioned at the bilateral greater occipital nerves, with placement confirmed by fluoroscopy. A radiofrequency probe was then inserted into the cannula. With 0.5 volts of 1.0 ms duration and 2 Hz stimulation, multifidus muscle twitching was palpated in the neck only, with no muscle twitching in the arm. An additional 1 mL of 1% lidocaine was injected to provide anesthesia. A continuous lesion was then applied for 90 seconds at 80 °C, and the patient tolerated the procedure without complication.

At 2-month follow-up, the patient stated that the ablation had not provided additional relief beyond that achieved with the nerve blocks. Additionally, he described a flare of bumps and pruritus. Examination revealed new excoriated lesions with underlying erythema. Monthly 0.25% bupivacaine injections were continued, which provided significant relief, although the benefit now only lasted 3 to 4 days. He was subsequently referred for repeat interventional radiology evaluation and underwent a second bilateral GON ablation 8 months after the initial ablation using the same technique. One month after the second procedure, he reported a decrease in pruritus with a WI-NRS of 2/10. Three months later, he reported complete resolution of pruritus which has remained stable during 1 year follow-up (Fig 1). Discussion

Neuropathic pruritus presents as a challenging condition to treat, arising from damage or dysfunction within the somatosensory nervous systems.3 There are no current FDA-approved therapies for its management.3 Historically, nerve blocks and radiofrequency ablation have been used in the management of neuropathic pain syndromes, including cervicogenic headaches and occipital neuralgia.1 These techniques are increasingly being used as treatment strategies for neuropathic pruritus and have previously demonstrated efficacy in trigeminal neuralgia4 and post-herpetic itch.5 Herein, we present a novel case of fluoroscopically guided greater occipital nerve (GON) radiofrequency ablation for the treatment of refractory scalp pruritus, resulting in sustained itch reduction.

Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) uses radiofrequency current delivered through an electrode to interrupt sensory signal transmission.6 The resulting thermal energy creates a controlled lesion targeting specific sensory nerves.6 Common targets include the dorsal root ganglia and peripheral nerves at their origins along the spinal column.6 Newer types of RFA procedures have been described including pulsed RFA, water-cooled RFA, and cryoneurolysis.6 RFA may be repeated if symptoms do not improve initially or recur after a period of relief.7,8

In scalp pruritus, the target of RFA is the GON which supplies cutaneous innervation to the posterior scalp. The GON primarily arises from the dorsal ramus of the C2 spinal nerve.9 Rosenthal et al previously reported successful treatment of scalp pruritus with CT-guided GON ablation, with resolution of pruritus after a single ablation.10 In contrast, we describe RFA performed under fluoroscopic guidance, with pruritus resolution achieved only after a second ablation. Although both ablations in our patient were performed using the same documented technique, the GON may be challenging to consistently localize using image-guided approaches, and subtle variation in nerve course or cannula position may result in incomplete sensory interruption.7 Sensory nerve regeneration after RFA may also cause symptom recurrence months to years later.8 However, this typically follows an initial period of improvement, which was not observed in our patient. Additionally, given that monthly bupivacaine nerve blocks were still being performed prior to the second ablation, we cannot fully exclude a cumulative or sequential effect of ongoing nerve blocks contributing to the eventual resolution rather than the second ablation acting alone. This case further supports RFA as a potential treatment option for refractory scalp pruritus and suggests that repeat ablation may be beneficial for patients who do not achieve adequate symptom control after initial ablation.

This report of GON ablation for the management of intractable scalp pruritus highlights a potentially novel therapeutic approach for a debilitating condition with no FDA-approved therapies. Larger studies are needed to better define the efficacy and safety of nerve ablation for scalp pruritus and potentially other forms of neuropathic pruritus.
