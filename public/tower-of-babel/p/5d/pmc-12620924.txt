Barriers and Opportunities for Collaboration With Primary Care: Perspectives of Early Psychosis Intervention Programs in Ontario, Canada
Authors: Rebecca Rodrigues, Jordan Edwards, Candice Madakadze, Richard Booth, Suzanne Archie, Arlene G. MacDougall, Lena Palaniyappan, Chiachen Cheng, Sarah Chan, Kerri Nagy, Kelly K. Anderson, the project co‐investigators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCollaboration between early psychosis intervention (EPI) services and primary care is crucial for continuity of care for first‐episode psychosis. The aim of this study was to describe the perceptions of staff at EPI programs on barriers and opportunities for collaboration with primary care.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative descriptive study using focus groups with staff at six EPI programs across Ontario, Canada (2 rural, 4 urban) with 2–9 participants per focus group (total n = 39). Focus group sessions were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim. We used thematic analyses to code the transcripts and identify emergent themes.ResultsParticipants highlighted three points in the EPI treatment process where collaboration with primary care is important: during the referral/intake process, shared care throughout treatment, and at discharge from the EPI program. We identified four themes relating to broader structural issues impacting collaboration with primary care through the treatment process: client access to primary care, family physician knowledge of early psychosis, siloed communication structures, and time constraints for both EPI staff and family physicians.ConclusionsImproving primary care access in young people, increasing family physician knowledge of psychosis and EPI services, and allowing dedicated time for communication for both family physicians and EPI clinicians may improve collaboration and patient care through the processes of intake, ongoing care, and discharge from EPI services.

Introduction

Early psychosis intervention (EPI) services aim to increase early detection and provide effective treatment during the critical early stages of illness to improve symptomatic and functional outcomes in young people experiencing a first episode of psychosis (McGorry et al. 2007). One of the core principles is that EPI programs are linked to other services and supports in the community, particularly primary care (Ontario Ministry of Health and Long Term Care 2011). Connections between EPI services and primary care are important for early detection and referral to specialised care, co‐management or support of ongoing treatment plans, and continuity of care (Ontario Ministry of Health and Long Term Care 2011), as well as for meeting the physical health needs of clients in terms of metabolic monitoring, preventive care, and chronic disease management (Lester et al. 2012). Data on the current state of collaboration between EPI services and primary care, including barriers and opportunities for collaboration, are needed to inform health system planning and to facilitate linkages between EPI services and primary care to ensure continuity of care for young people with first‐episode psychosis.

However, there is limited information available on collaboration between primary care and EPI services. In a qualitative study from the United Kingdom (UK) focused on transitions out of EPI services, the EPI program staff reported low levels of communication and engagement with primary care (Woodward et al. 2019). However, most clients in this study were discharged to other services (e.g., community mental health teams), emphasizing the need for further evidence on this topic (Woodward et al. 2019). Perspectives from primary care physicians on collaborating with an EPI service in Ireland suggest there are opportunities for EPI services to support primary care through the assessment and referral process (Gavin et al. 2008). Primary care physicians indicated that they valued the rapid assessments provided by EPI services and appreciated the improved communication between mental health services and primary care that was offered by this EPI program (Gavin et al. 2008). Prior research by our team suggests that primary care providers would prefer a co‐management approach with EPI services to ensure continuity of care for their patients with early psychosis (Carter et al. 2024). However, there is a lack of data on EPI clinicians' perspectives on collaboration with primary care, and how primary care could support EPI services, as well as a lack of data on communication through care trajectories, from referral to intake, ongoing treatment, and discharge.

Young people with first‐episode psychosis and their families would benefit from strengthened collaborations between EPI services and primary care. It has been argued that EPI services need to more actively engage with the primary care sector to ensure success (Shiers and Lester 2004), and one of the barriers to successful implementation of EPI services is poor coordination with primary care (Csillag et al. 2018). Evidence to inform strategies to improve collaboration between EPI services and primary care is needed. The aim of this study was to explore the perceptions of EPI program staff on barriers and opportunities for collaboration with primary care throughout the care pathway for first‐episode psychosis.

Methods

Study Setting

EPI services are typically comprised of community education, low‐barrier access to services, multidisciplinary teams, small caseloads, case management, pharmacological treatment, psychosocial interventions, and assertive treatment (Nordentoft et al. 2014; McGorry 2015). In Ontario, Canada, implementation of EPI programs began in the early 2000s and grew to include more than 50 program sites as of 2015, with 11 programs located in rural and remote areas (Standards Implementation Steering Committee 2015; Durbin et al. 2016). As EPI programs expanded across the province, the provincial government released standards in 2011 to support the consistency and quality of EPI services (Ontario Ministry of Health and Long Term Care 2011). Adherence to these standards varies across the province, with a wide variation in capacity in terms of funding, staff size, and caseloads (Durbin et al. 2016).

Primary care is the cornerstone of the public healthcare system in Ontario, providing services for low‐acuity acute conditions, management of chronic health conditions, and preventive care. Access to specialist services typically requires a referral from a primary care provider; however most EPI services in Ontario do not. Approximately 1 in 10 young people with early psychosis do not have access to a regular primary care provider (Rodrigues et al. 2024), and this lack of access is particularly heightened in rural and remote regions of the province, where large geographic distances, low population density, and lack of infrastructure have led to a critical shortage of both family physicians and psychiatrists. EPI programs in rural and remote regions of Northern Ontario may, at times, have limited access to a psychiatrist, necessitating a greater involvement of primary care physicians.

Study Design

We used a qualitative descriptive approach to explore the perspectives of EPI staff on collaboration with primary care (Sandelowski 2000; Sullivan‐Bolyai et al. 2005). We conducted focus groups at six EPI programs across Ontario between 2019 and 2021. We received approval to conduct this study through the Western University Health Sciences Research Ethics Board (project #114507) and through site‐specific research ethics boards, as required.

Focus Groups

We conducted one focus group at each of the six participating EPI programs. Program staff were invited to participate via email and were eligible if they were involved in client intake, case management, or treatment. Participants provided written consent for participation and audio recording of the focus group. Focus groups were approximately 1 h in length and were facilitated by members of the study team (R.R., J.E.). One focus group was held in person and the remainder were conducted remotely via teleconferencing. We used a focus group guide which included questions on the following topics: the roles that family physicians play in each EPI program, what the ideal role would be, intake process for clients referred from primary care, discharge processes back to primary care, approaches for patients with no regular family physician, communication with family physicians, and barriers and opportunities for better collaboration with primary care. Focus groups were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim, and transcripts were anonymized and verified against the audio recording by a member of the study team. Saturation is often difficult to achieve in a qualitative descriptive design, as the objective is to describe participants' perceptions and experiences and to explore commonalities and differences across them (Milne and Oberle 2005). Therefore, we completed data collection when data on the primary themes had sufficient depth and breadth to enable a comprehensive description of the phenomenon (Sandelowski 1995).

Data Analysis

We used content analysis to summarize the focus group content using data‐derived codes or themes, which is the recommended approach for qualitative descriptive studies (Sullivan‐Bolyai et al. 2005). Two members of the study team (CM, RR) independently conducted initial coding using one transcript. Each coding scheme was reviewed and discussed to reach a consensus on the final coding scheme, after which one team member coded the remainder of the transcripts. We then reviewed the codes to generate emergent themes. We used NVivo 12 Pro to facilitate data management and analysis.

Communication structures and the flow of information were discussed in all focus groups. They seem to rely on reports being sent, so that sort of seems like that's the culture of how medicine works… [FG6]

Time Constraints

Participants discussed the fact that family physicians, as well as EPI staff, are “too busy” and as a result neither has the time to communicate and collaboration becomes a low priority. A structural barrier for EPI staff is that government standards do not allot time for communication with primary care providers, and instead require staff to meet a minimum number of minutes with clients. Participants also noted that the time constraints on family physicians also impact their engagement in outreach and education provided by EPI services.We have to prioritise on a day‐to‐day basis in our practice of what needs to be done, and although important as we've discussed today, I think for me personally it does fall at the bottom of the totem pole. [FG5]I think a significant barrier to that is the way … we are required to maintain stats. So, it's all about direct contact with clients rather than doing other, providing other roles which are probably more impactful, but don't meet the ministry standards in terms of, you know, number of minutes or hours or whatever with clients. Discussion

In our focus groups with EPI program staff, participants described family physicians as key stakeholders in the treatment of young people with first‐episode psychosis, but described current collaboration with family physicians as poor. We identified themes relating to the role of primary care throughout a client's treatment in EPI services, from referral/intake through to discharge. We further identified themes presenting barriers and opportunities for collaboration, including lack of access to primary care, primary care physician awareness of EPI services, siloed communication structures, and time constraints for both primary care physicians and EPI staff. We have previously explored the perspectives of primary care providers on early psychosis intervention, and their role in providing care for their patients with first‐episode psychosis, which has been described in detail in a separate report (Carter et al. 2024).

The themes we identified align with findings from a survey of EPI programs in Ontario, in which most programs indicated that their ability to deliver EPI services could be facilitated by early recognition and referral from primary care and by better access to primary care (Durbin et al. 2016). Our finding that discharge to primary care presents a critical opportunity for collaboration complements qualitative findings from the perspective of service users in the UK, who reported that good communication between the two sectors involved in transition helped clients feel in control and cared for, highlighting the importance of good communication through the discharge process (Lester et al. 2012). As well, Lester and colleagues underscore the importance of active communication and establishment of a relationship between primary care and EPI services well in advance of the discharge transition (Lester et al. 2012). Our findings echo this, and further highlight the importance of ongoing collaboration and communication throughout the course of treatment to ensure relationships are in place at the time of discharge—a preference also cited by primary care physicians (Carter et al. 2024). A lack of awareness of EPI services, and early psychosis more broadly, among primary care physicians is a theme that has been identified in prior studies from the perspectives of both family physicians (Carter et al. 2024; Strelchuk et al. 2020), and clients and caregivers (McCann et al. 2011; Rodrigues et al. 2025). Perspectives from EPI staff in our study further emphasise the need to support educational interventions to improve knowledge of primary care physicians regarding early psychosis and available resources.

Our findings highlight areas where linkages between EPI services and primary care could be improved. EPI clinicians identified several structural factors that impact collaboration with primary care, such as the lack of dedicated time to liaise with primary care providers. This issue was similarly noted in studies of fidelity to EPI standards, which concluded that providing EPI staff with time for family physician liaison may enhance collaboration (Durbin et al. 2016; Petrakis et al. 2011). Participants in our study also noted a lack of dedicated time to conduct community outreach and education to family physicians as a barrier to improving collaboration with primary care. The Ontario Ministry of Health has included outreach to primary care within the EPI program standards, indicating this opportunity for collaboration is well‐recognised (Ontario Ministry of Health and Long Term Care 2011). This lack of dedicated time for primary care collaboration has been attributed to high caseloads, with EPI staff in Ontario averaging 19 clients per clinical staff (Durbin et al. 2016)—higher than the recommended caseload of 10–15 clients (Nordentoft et al. 2014; Department of Health 2001; Bird et al. 2010). High caseloads may also discourage outreach, as EPI programs do not have the capacity to take on more referrals (Durbin et al. 2016)—a sentiment that was expressed in focus groups where EPI staff described feeling stretched. Focus group participants also noted that family physicians are themselves busy and lack time for collaboration, further compounding this issue. Our findings suggest that more resources are needed within EPI services and primary care to support the implementation of this standard. Additionally, structured communication processes may also be warranted, as we observed that EPI staff felt that current communication structures do not facilitate collaboration. Fuller and colleagues' review on building primary mental health care linkages suggests that the development of processes (e.g., care planning, guideline development, referral, and follow‐up protocols) enabled active communication and partnership formation (Fuller et al. 2011).

Limitations

One focus group was conducted in person, while the remainder were conducted via telephone or videoconferencing, which may have impacted participation and group dynamics. Although focus group participants represented a wide range of roles, no groups included a psychiatrist—these clinicians may have unique perspectives that were not captured in our data. Although we included two rural/northern sites, we lacked participation from a rural site in southern Ontario, and perspectives from rural EPI programs outside of the Northern Ontario context may differ.

Conclusions

EPI staff view family physicians as crucial stakeholders in the treatment of young people with first‐episode psychosis; however, communication between the two sectors needs improvement. Collaboration between EPI services and primary care may be improved through ensuring primary care access for young people with first‐episode psychosis, and by improving family physician knowledge of psychosis and EPI services. Importantly, structural issues such as one‐way communication, and lack of dedicated time for communication and outreach to primary care, are significant barriers. Mandating the allocation of time for such tasks, improving resource allocation to reduce caseloads, and developing communication protocols may improve collaboration with primary care, as well as patient care, throughout the processes of referral/intake, ongoing care, and discharge from EPI services.
