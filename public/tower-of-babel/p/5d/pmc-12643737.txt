Association of insulin resistance-related indexes with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease: A crosss-sectional study
Authors: Zheqin Zhu, Min Wang, Aoli Chen, Shenghua Lu, Sisi Dai, Rongzhen Liu, Jianhe Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Insulin resistance (IR) is linked to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease(ASCVD). This study analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey to reveal the association of IR-related indexes with ASCVD in adults, aiming to improve risk assessment and early intervention. The study included adults from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey between 2007 and 2016. We used multivariable logistic regression to assess the link between IR-related indexes and ASCVD. Nonlinear associations were identified using restricted cubic splines and threshold effect analyses. Subgroup analysis confirmed result stability, while receiver operating characteristic curves compared the predictive performance of the indexes. Among 11,687 participants, 9.40% had ASCVD. A linear association between the metabolic score for IR (METS-IR) and ASCVD. The triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), homeostasis model assessment of IR (HOMA-IR), and triglyceride glucose-waist height ratio (TyG-WHtR) had nonlinear correlations with ASCVD prevalence. When TyG > 9.03, it was positively correlated with ASCVD (OR: 1.31, 95% CI: 1.07–1.60). An increase in HOMA-IR < 6.79 correlated with ASCVD risk (OR: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.04–1.12), but when HOMA-IR > 6.79, the increasing trend slowed down. When TyG-WHtR > 5.82, ASCVD risk was increased markedly with increasing values (OR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.15–1.52), subgroup analysis showed a significant interaction between diabetes status and physical activity. Additionally, TyG-WHtR demonstrated superior predictive capacity for ASCVD (AUC = 0.644) compared to other indexes. Elevated IR levels are independently associated with increased ASCVD risk in American adults, with TyG-WHtR being the strongest predictor.

1. Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) persists as a leading global cause of morbidity and mortality, accounting for approximately 19.8 million deaths worldwide in 2022 alone.[1] This staggering burden underscores the urgent need for early risk stratification and targeted management of modifiable risk factors to mitigate societal and healthcare impacts. Emerging evidence highlights that ASCVD pathogenesis is intimately linked to metabolic dysregulation.[2] Insulin resistance (IR), as a core feature of metabolic syndrome, is considered a pivotal driver of ASCVD development by inducing chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and dyslipidemia.[3] With rising global rates of obesity and diabetes, exploring the association mechanism between IR-related indexes and ASCVD has become an important scientific proposition for early prevention.

The hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp is the gold standard for assessing IR; however, its invasiveness and high cost limit clinical applicability.[4] In contrast, noninvasive surrogate indices derived from routine biochemical measurements have emerged as practical alternatives for IR evaluation, including the metabolic score for IR (METS-IR),[5] the triglyceride-glucose index (TyG),[6] the homeostasis model assessment of IR (HOMA-IR),[7] and the triglyceride glucose-waist height ratio (TyG-WHtR).[8] METS-IR integrates multiple metabolic indicators and comprehensively reflects IR from various dimensions. The TyG index, which is calculated based on fasting blood glucose (FBG) and triglyceride (TG) levels, effectively captures IR caused by disturbances in glycolipid metabolism. HOMA-IR, a widely adopted method for assessing IR, calculates IR through measurements of FBG and insulin levels. TyG-WHtR innovatively combines body fat distribution parameters(specifically the WHtR) with glycolipid metabolic indicators, thereby not only reflecting metabolic dysregulation but also quantifying the contribution of central obesity to IR through the proportional relationship between waist circumference (WC) and height. This makes TyG-WHtR particularly valuable for the early identification of IR associated with abdominal obesity. While METS-IR and HOMA-IR primarily focus on the imbalance in insulin-glucose feedback regulation, TyG and TyG-WHtR place additional emphasis on the role of TG in the development of IR. Previous studies consistently associate these indexes with ASCVD progression, supporting their role in risk stratification and preventive interventions.[9–12] This study will conduct an in-depth exploration of it.

Due to the diversity of IR surrogate indexes, the predictive efficacy of different indexes for ASCVD risk has not been systematically compared in harmonized populations. Furthermore, most existing studies rely on single-center clinical cohorts with limited sample representativeness and insufficient adjustment for confounding factors (such as lifestyle and underlying comorbidities). Therefore, this study was based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database to investigate the associations between 4 IR-related indexes and ASCVD, as well as to compare their predictive values for ASCVD. From a public health perspective, revealing the deeper links between IR-related metabolic abnormalities and cardiovascular health will help to develop more targeted chronic disease prevention and control policies at the population level.

2. Methods

2.1. Study population

This study utilized data from 5 consecutive cycles (2007–2016) of NHANES, a nationally representative cross-sectional program administered by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention to monitor Americans’ health and nutritional status. The NHANES protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. No further ethical review approval is required for this study.

This study initially recruited 50,588 participants, as shown in Figure 1, with unclear ASCVD diagnosis, missing data on IR-related indexes (including FBG, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), TG, fasting insulin, body mass index (BMI), height, and WC, and participants younger than 20 years of age and pregnant women were excluded. A total of 11,687 participants were finally included.

The flow chart of study participants.

2.2. Calculation of IR-related indexes

In this study, 4 IR-related indexes were used as exposure variables. The formula was calculated as follows: METS-IR = ln [2 × FBG (mg/dL) + TG (mg/dL)] × BMI (kg/m2)/ln HDL-C (mg/dL]),[13] TyG = [TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2], HOMA-IR = FBG (mg/dL) × fasting insulin (uU/ml)/ 405, and TyG-WHtR = TyG × WC (cm)/height (cm). After fasting for 8.5 hours, FBG, TG, and HDL-C levels were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer. BMI (kg/m2) was calculated by dividing an individual’s weight (kg) by the square of their height (m).

2.3. Definition of ASCVD

The outcome of this study was ASCVD, which was defined as at least 1 coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack, and stroke according to the 2013 ACC/AHA Guideline on Managing Blood Cholesterol to Minimize Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Risk in Adults.[14]

2.4. Covariates

We selected a number of important covariates based on prior research and clinical expertise to assess the impact of potential confounders in our study analysis. The covariates included age, sex, race, education, marital status, family poverty income ratio , smoking status, drinking status, sleep disorders, physical activity, diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. Detailed measurement procedures can be found at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes. Table, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q738 provides a detailed description of covariates.

2.5. Statistical analysis

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the design recommended by NHANES, taking into account sample weights, clustering, and hierarchical analysis. Therefore, sample weights were taken into account in our subsequent analyses. According to the NHANES analysis guidelines (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/ nhanes/tutorials/default.aspx for details), we used SAF sample weight (WTSAF2YR/5) for weighted analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard error, and differences between groups were assessed using the T-test or one-way analysis of variance. The categorical variables were expressed as frequency (n) and percentage (%), and the Chi-square test was used for statistical analysis.

We used weighted logistic regression to analyze the association between IR-related indexes and ASCVD, and the results were shown as the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). We adjusted the covariates and established 4 models to address the effect of confounding factors and improve the robustness of our research conclusions. Model 1 was not adjusted for any covariates; model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, race, marital status, education, and poverty income ratio ; model 3 was further adjusted for smoking status, drinking status, sleep disorders, and physical activity; model 4 was adjusted for diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia on the basis of model 3.

In addition, we performed a restricted cubic spline (RCS) of the fully adjusted model to evaluate the nonlinear dose-response relationship between IR-related indexes and ASCVD. The likelihood ratio test determined the threshold effect between IR-related indexes and ASCVD under the premise of a nonlinear relationship. To test the predictive ability of different IR-related indexes on ASCVD, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Finally, subgroup analyses were used to classify the population into different levels, and the interaction test was used to test the potential heterogeneity among these subgroups, including gender, age, race, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and BMI. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2) and EmpowerStats (version 4.2). P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, as shown in Table 1, a total of 11,687 participants were included in this study, including 1098 ASCVD patients and 10,589 non-ASCVD individuals, of whom 5726 were males, accounting for 49.03% of the weighted sample, and 5961 were females, accounting for 50.97% of the weighted sample. The mean age was 47.2 ± 0.28 years, notably higher in the ASCVD group (64.61 ± 0.55) than the non-ASCVD group (45.63 ± 0.27). In addition, the ASCVD patients were more likely to be male, non-Hispanic White, smokers, drinkers, physically inactive, to have higher education levels, income, and BMI, and were potentially associated with diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. Among the IR-related indexes, METS-IR was 46.44 ± 0.66, TyG was 8.82 ± 0.03, HOMA-IR was 5.11 ± 0.30, and TyG-WHtR was 5.59 ± 0.05 in the ASCVD group, which were all significantly higher than those in the non-ASCVD group (P < .001), suggesting that there is a strong association between IR and ASCVD.

Weighted logistic regression analysis confirmed the relationship between IR-related indexes and ASCVD, as shown in Table 2. Continuous variable analysis showed that METS-IR, HOMA-IR, and TyG-WHtR were positively correlated with ASCVD in Model 4, while TyG had no significant linear correlation with ASCVD. After adjusting for all covariates in model 4, the prevalence of ASCVD increased by 45% for each 1 -unit increase in HOMA-IR in the highest quartile compared with the lowest quartile (OR: 1.45, 95% CI: 1.10–1.91). 3.3. Non-linearity and threshold effect analysis between IR-related indexes and ASCVD

We used RCS to analyze the possible nonlinear relationship between IR-related indexes and ASCVD, as shown in Figure 2. Through RCS, we can observe the nonlinear relationship between TyG, HOMA-IR, TyG-WHtR, and ASCVD (P-nonlinear was .001, .001, and .034, respectively). We next conducted threshold effect analysis on these 3 indexes, as shown in Table 3. The threshold analysis yielded a U-shaped relationship between TyG and ASCVD, and the inflection point value of TyG was 9.03. When TyG exceeded the threshold, the risk of ASCVD increased with increasing TyG values (OR: 1.31, 95% CI: 1.07–1.60). In contrast, ASCVD was negatively correlated with TyG when TyG was below the threshold (OR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.61–0.90). The inflection point value of HOMA-IR was 6.79, and ASCVD was positively correlated with HOMA-IR when HOMA-IR < 6.79 (OR: 1.08, 95% CI: 1.04–1.12); however, when HOMA-IR > 6.79, the risk of ASCVD increased slowly. The inflection point value of TyG-WHtR was 5.82, and the incidence of ASCVD increased by 32% (OR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.15–1.52) for every 1 -unit increase in the value of TyG-WHtR when TyG-WHtR > 5.82.

3.4. Subgroup analysis

To determine whether the association between IR-related indexes and ASCVD risk was robust in different demographic settings, we conducted subgroup analyses for sex, age, race, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and BMI. In Model 4, all covariates except those used to establish subgroups were considered. As shown in Figure 3, the correlation between the 4 IR-related indexes and the risk of ASCVD remained consistent in most subgroups (P for interaction > 0.05). However, there was a significant interaction between TyG, HOMA-IR, and TyG-WHtR with ASCVD prevalence in the diabetes subgroup (P for interaction <.05). In detail, TyG-WHtR was more strongly associated with the diabetic population, and TyG and HOMA-IR had higher predictive values in the nondiabetic population. It suggests that metabolic status is a central factor in the heterogeneity of IR indicator effects. In addition, the results show that physical activity is another potential regulator between HOMA-IR and ASCVD, and the risk of ASCVD increases with a higher HOMA-IR value in those who lack physical activity.

3.5. ROC analysis

ROC was used to analyze the diagnostic efficiency of 4 IR-related indexes for ASCVD, and AUC >0.5 was considered to have a diagnostic application, as shown in Figure 4. TyG-WHtR was the optimal predictive index (AUC: 0.644, 95% CI: 0.628–0.660), followed by HOMA-IR (AUC: 0.602, 95% CI: 0.584–0.62), TyG (AUC: 0.600, 95% CI: 0.583–0.617) and METS-IR (AUC: 0.586, 95% CI: 0.568–0.603).

4. Discussion

In this population-based cross-sectional study, we identified significant differences in gender distribution, socioeconomic status, and lifestyle habits between individuals with ASCVD and those without it. Notably, ASCVD patients exhibited higher rates of obesity and diabetes, suggesting distinct characteristics related to metabolic disorders. Through multi-dimensional analysis, we discovered that 4 IR-related indexes (METS-IR, TyG, HOMA-IR, and TyG-WHtR) were all associated with ASCVD. Specifically, continuous METS-IR showed a linear relationship with ASCVD, with risk values continuously accumulating as the indicator increased. HOMA-IR, TyG, and TyG-WHtR had significant nonlinear relationships with ASCVD and exhibited obvious threshold effects, with inflection point values of 6.79, 9.03, and 5.82, respectively. Subgroup analysis and interaction tests indicated that the associations between IR-related indexes and ASCVD risk varied mainly in the diabetes subgroup. Additionally, HOMA-IR was more strongly correlated with those who did not engage in regular exercise. The ROC results showed that all 4 indexes had specific predictive capabilities for ASCVD, with TyG-WHtR potentially being a more reliable predictor. Our results suggest that IR-related indexes have potential clinical applications in ASCVD risk prediction and contribute to primary prevention strategies.

HOMA-IR reflects insulin sensitivity in the liver and peripheral tissues through fasting insulin and blood glucose levels. A Framingham Offspring cohort study reported that individuals in the highest quartile of HOMA-IR had a significantly increased risk of 70% (Hazard Ratio [HR]: 1.7, 95% CI: 1.1–2.7).[15] The logistic regression analysis results in our study were consistent with previous studies, with a 45% increase in the risk of ASCVD for each unit increase in HOMA-IR in the highest quartile. Further RCS analysis revealed a mainly nonlinear trend between HOMA-IR and ASCVD risk (P-nonlinear <.05), and threshold effect analysis indicated an inflection point value of 6.79. This finding has significant clinical implications. When HOMA-IR is below 6.79, the risk of ASCVD increases as HOMA-IR rises, suggesting that HOMA-IR is a sensitive predictor of ASCVD risk within this range. It can be a vital criterion for early risk assessment and for enhancing the monitoring of blood glucose, blood pressure, and other related risk factors in individuals near this threshold. Timely lifestyle interventions can be implemented to slow the progression of IR. However, once HOMA-IR exceeds 6.79, the rate of risk increase diminishes. This may be attributed to the body’s compensatory mechanisms in cases of severe IR, which do not indicate a risk reduction. Individuals within this category still face a high risk for ASCVD and may require pharmacological interventions, such as insulin sensitizers, to manage glucose levels when necessary. Supporting our findings, a study based on NHANES revealed a nonlinear relationship between HOMA-IR and all-cause mortality in patients with coronary heart disease combined with hypertension.[16] This suggests that while HOMA-IR and ASCVD may show an approximately linear trend within a specific interval, the overall association exhibits a nonlinear relationship. Therefore, it is crucial to monitor changes in HOMA-IR dynamically in clinical practice and to develop individualized strategies for ASCVD prevention and management based on this threshold. Additionally, our study found that the link between elevated HOMA-IR and ASCVD risk is more pronounced and holds more excellent predictive value in nondiabetic individuals. A Chinese study demonstrated a strong positive correlation between HOMA-IR and both intracranial and extracranial atherosclerotic burden in nondiabetic patients.[17] Similar findings were reported by Mohamed Eid et al., who highlighted the clinical importance of HOMA-IR for risk stratification of coronary artery disease in a nondiabetic population.[18] The pathological mechanisms underlying diabetes are highly complex, particularly in patients with long disease duration or inadequate disease management, where numerous factors can interfere. In individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus, β cells are significantly destroyed due to autoimmune attacks, resulting in nearly complete insulin secretion, which necessitates the use of exogenous insulin to maintain blood glucose stability. In patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), as the disease progresses, the function of pancreatic β cells gradually fails, and insufficient insulin secretion and IR coexist, which interferes with the accuracy of insulin measurement and makes it difficult for HOMA-IR to precisely assess endogenous IR.[19] However, in nondiabetic individuals, pancreatic secretion and function are relatively normal, and fasting insulin levels are primarily influenced by the degree of their own IR, with less interference from exogenous factors, allowing HOMA-IR to more accurately reflect the association between IR and ASCVD. Furthermore, the risk associated with HOMA-IR was more significant in individuals who lacked physical activity. A meta-analysis by Sun et al showed that resistance exercise could significantly reduce HOMA-IR and effectively lower cardiovascular risk in adults with T2DM.[20]

The TyG index, which combines fasting TG and FBG to assess IR, has gained recognition for its relationship with ASCVD. The TyG index can serve as an independent predictor for primary prevention of ASCVD. A retrospective study based on the Turkish population demonstrated that the TyG index was independently associated with long-term major adverse cardiovascular events in patients at high cardiovascular risk.[21] Another cohort study based on the Korean NHIS-HEALS indicated that, especially in obese individuals with abnormal metabolism, the TyG index was significantly positively correlated with the risk of adverse cardiovascular diseases (CVD).[22] The TyG index is also closely related to the long-term prognosis of patients with ASCVD. Liu et al discovered that the TyG index independently predicted both 3-month and 12-month mortality in Chinese ischemic stroke patients under 65 years of age.[23] Similarly, a retrospective study reported that the TyG index has an independent predictive effect on the long-term mortality rate of patients with reduced ejection fraction heart failure who have received implantable cardiac defibrillator treatment.[24] Furthermore, Our study revealed a U-shaped relationship between the TyG index and ASCVD, with a threshold identified at 9.03. This indicates that both extremely low or extremely high levels of TyG will increase the risk of ASCVD. When the TyG index is below 9.03, the body may be in a state of metabolic stress related to hypoglycemia or low TG. Studies have shown that hypoglycemia can enhance the levels of counter-regulatory hormones (such as adrenaline, cortisol, etc) in the circulation, mediate vascular contraction and platelet aggregation, and thereby induce cardiovascular ischemic events.[25] Low TG levels have also been proven to be a predictive indicator for cardiogenic death in patients with heart failure.[26] Therefore, when the TyG index is below 9.03, clinical screening should focus on identifying reasons for excessive hypoglycemic treatment that may cause hypoglycemia. Adjustments to the hypoglycemic regimen or supplementation of nutrients should be made to avoid metabolic stress. When the TyG index exceeds 9.03, the risk of ASCVD dominated by glycolipid metabolism disorder significantly increases. This is consistent with the results of the NHANES study, where the cardiovascular mortality rate increased in the diabetes/diabetes-prone population with TyG > 9.05 (HR: 1.77, 95% CI: 1.08–2.91),[27] and the risk of adverse events accelerated in patients with acute coronary syndrome with TyG > 9.1 to 9.2 (HR: 1.77, 95% CI: 1.08–2.91).[28] At this time, clinical practice should adopt lifestyle intervention combined with drug treatment, regularly monitor lipid and blood glucose levels, and strengthen comprehensive treatment. It can be seen that maintaining an appropriate TyG level is very important. Both extremely low or extremely high levels may have adverse effects on health, and a precise management model based on thresholds can provide quantitative evidence for the early prevention and control of ASCVD.

TyG-WHtR, which combines the measurement of central obesity (waist-to-height ratio, WHtR) with abnormalities in glycolipid metabolism, is an important indicator derived from the TyG index for evaluating IR and is associated with ASCVD risk. A 15-year follow-up cohort study conducted in China demonstrated that the incidence of ASCVD significantly increased with higher levels of TyG-WHtR, and the RCS curve further demonstrated a nonlinear relationship between the 2.[29] Zhuang et al identified a nonlinear association through threshold analysis, revealing that CVD risk in individuals with impaired FBG progressively escalates when TyG-WHtR > 5.23.[30] A British cohort study established a TyG-WHtR threshold of 5.696, beyond which the index demonstrated a significant dose-response relationship with cardiovascular mortality.[31] Our research, through RCS analysis, obtained results similar to those of previous studies. TyG-WHtR showed a significant nonlinear relationship with ASCVD, with the inflection point threshold being 5.82. This suggests that the critical value of TyG-WHtR may be between 5.2 and 5.9. Due to the ethnic heterogeneity of the study population, there was a certain range of fluctuations. When TyG-WHtR exceeds this range, it indicates that abdominal obesity and abnormal glycolipid metabolism form a synergistic pathogenic effect, significantly accelerating the progression of atherosclerosis (AS). This result has crucial guiding value for clinical practice and can be used as a screening tool for ASCVD risk stratification, especially for “hidden obesity” populations with normal BMI but excessive visceral fat accumulation. At the same time, based on the threshold, comprehensive management strategies such as lifestyle intervention and drug treatment can be formulated. Importantly, TyG-WHtR has been shown to be an independent predictor of AS in individuals with T2DM, demonstrating improved predictive capacity for AS in patients over the age of 50.[32] Furthermore, a U.S. cohort study across different glucose tolerance categories revealed that TyG-WHtR is a strong predictor of CVD in the general population, with increased sensitivity to elevated TyG-WHtR levels observed in prediabetic and diabetic groups compared to those with normal glucose levels.[30] Existing evidence supports our findings, indicating that diabetic status enhances the predictive value of TyG-WHtR for ASCVD. The TyG reflects the body’s glycolipid metabolism. Elevated blood sugar levels and high TG levels are common among individuals with diabetes, and the interplay between these 2 factors exacerbates IR and promotes the progression of AS. The WHtR, which represents central obesity, is extremely prevalent among diabetic patients. In this context, visceral fat secretes a large number of fat factors, including leptin, adiponectin, and tumor necrosis factor-α.[33] These substances contribute to inflammation, damage the vascular endothelium, and accelerate the progression of ASCVD.[34] In diabetic individuals, the combined effects of glycolipid metabolism disorders and central obesity enable the TyG-WHtR index to more accurately reflect the relationship between metabolic abnormalities and cardiovascular risk, thus providing stronger predictive power for ASCVD in this population.

METS-IR is an evaluation tool based on BMI, HDL-C, and FBG levels used to assess IR related to metabolic syndrome. Recent studies have linked METS-IR with ASCVD risk factors. For instance, a cross-sectional study of the Japanese population found a significant positive correlation between METS-IR and brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity, suggesting that METS-IR could serve as a simple indicator for predicting arterial stiffness.[35] Additionally, Li et al identified a significant positive relationship between METS-IR, treated as a continuous variable, and CVD among middle-aged and older adults in both Chinese and Americans, which aligns with our findings.[36] However, in the fully adjusted model regression analysis, the highest quartile of METS-IR was not significantly linked to ASCVD risk. A recent cohort study in Korea found that METS-IR was not associated with cardiovascular mortality after adjusting for confounding variables.[37] Furthermore, a retrospective study with a median 29.8-month follow-up found that METS-IR was not correlated with adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events in the total population of patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention.[38] These findings collectively indicate that METS-IR’s constrained predictive validity for ASCVD may stem from its heightened sensitivity to established metabolic risk factors, whereas fully adjusted models (e.g., lipids, blood pressure, diabetes) attenuated its independent predictive capacity.

Previous studies have not compared the predictive effects of these 4 IR indexes on ASCVD in the same population. Our study addresses this gap through a multi-dimensional analysis. This study showed a notable attenuation of the effect of the TyG index after adjusting for lipids and diabetes, supporting its dependence on glycolipid metabolic pathways. The nonlinear threshold effect of the TyG was significant with an AUC = 0.600, which can be used for ASCVD risk stratification. In this study, TyG-WHtR was the best predictor of ASCVD with AUC = 0.644, which was considerably higher than the other indicators, and the association was stronger in the diabetic population, reflecting the dual pathomechanisms of glycolipid metabolism and central obesity in patients with ASCVD. Additionally, the ROC results from Deng's study indicated that TyG-WHtR has greater diagnostic efficacy for angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, and coronary heart disease in American adults when compared to TyG.[39] The predictive ability of the HOMA-IR ranked just below that of TyG-WHtR, with an AUC of 0.602. HOMA-IR demonstrates superior predictive utility in nondiabetic populations, reflecting its sensitivity to early-phase IR and independence from glucose-lowering medications. However, its clinical applicability in diabetic populations is limited due to multifactorial metabolic dysregulation and concurrent therapeutic interventions. Therefore, HOMA-IR appears to be more suitable for early screening of ASCVD risk in nondiabetic or prediabetic populations. One cohort study showed that HOMA-IR has a better predictive ability for carotid plaques than TyG, which is consistent with our findings.[40] However, some studies have shown opposite results, which may be partly related to metabolic differences among study populations. Therefore, more experimental and clinical studies are needed to elucidate the significance of IR in ASCVD prediction. As an emerging IR metric, METS-IR demonstrated the weakest predictive capacity for ASCVD prediction in our study (AUC = 0.586). Comparative analyses consistently show its inferior predictive performance relative to TyG and TyG-WHtR.[41–43] It is recommended that the METS-IR formula be optimized further in combination with clinical research.

IR is a major contributor to ASCVD and affects its development through various mechanisms. IR can directly lead to endothelial dysfunction. Chronic hyperinsulinemia can lead to oxidative stress in vascular endothelial cells and decrease the availability of nitric oxide, thereby inducing endothelial dysfunction and vasoconstriction and stimulating the expression of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1).[44,45] Elevated levels of PAI-1 are linked to an increased risk of coronary thrombosis. A recent study has demonstrated the presence of significant IR in prediabetes, accompanied by endothelial dysfunction and elevated PAI-1 levels, and further regression analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between HOMA-IR and PAI-1.[46] Under conditions of IR, the glucose-regulating and anti-inflammatory effects of insulin are diminished, leading to elevated blood glucose levels and a heightened inflammatory state. The persistent low-grade inflammation triggered by IR is considered a key factor in the development of ASCVD.[47] Cui et al reported the co-exposure effects and mutual mediation of the TyG index and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein in CVD.[48] The metabolic consequences of IR encompass a variety of diseases and pathologies, such as dyslipidemia and visceral obesity, with lipotoxicity being a significant driver of ASCVD. When hypertriglyceridemia occurs, free fatty acids overflow, activating inflammatory pathways, fostering the formation of oxidized low-density lipoprotein, and accelerating foam cell deposition and plaque progression.[49] Additionally, visceral adipose tissue releases pro-inflammatory factors and free fatty acids that promote IR, further disrupting lipid metabolism and contributing to visceral fat accumulation. This creates a vicious cycle.[50] Increased sympathetic nerve activity and impaired cardiac autonomic function related to IR may also hasten the progression of ASCVD.[51,52]

Our study has several advantages compared to previous research. Firstly, it utilizes the NHANES database and applies complex sampling weight adjustments, ensuring that the results are generalizable to the adult population of America. The multi-stratified sampling from NHANES significantly enhances the external validity compared to regional cohorts. In addition, we evaluated 4 IR indexes, METS-IR, TyG, HOMA-IR, and TyG-WHtR, simultaneously for the first time, systematically comparing their predictive efficacy and mechanisms of action. ROC curves demonstrated that the predictive ability of TyG-WHtR was markedly superior to the TyG, providing direct evidence of a clinical index. RCS and threshold effect analysis revealed the dose-response inflection points of TyG, HOMA-IR, and TyG-WHtR, which made up for the shortcomings of the traditional linear model. In addition, significant interactions were found in key subgroups such as diabetes and physical activity, suggesting the important regulatory role of metabolic status and lifestyle on ASCVD, providing a target for precise intervention.

The limitations of this study deserve attention. Firstly, the cross-sectional design did not allow us to determine a causal relationship between IR and ASCVD, which could also lead to secondary metabolic disorders. Further prospective cohort studies are needed to validate the temporal sequence of these relationships. Additionally, although we adjusted for demographic, lifestyle, and metabolic factors, we did not include dietary composition, genetic factors, or inflammatory markers. This omission could potentially exclude mediating or confounding variables, which may impact our results.

5. Conclusion

Our study identified a linear relationship between METS-IR and ASCVD risk. HOMA-IR, TyG, and TyG-WHtR showed significant nonlinear relationships with ASCVD and exhibited obvious threshold effects. TyG-WHtR demonstrated the best predictive efficacy for ASCVD. In summary, higher IR is associated with an increased risk of ASCVD.
