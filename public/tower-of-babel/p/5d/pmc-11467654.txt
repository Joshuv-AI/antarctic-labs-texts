Protocol for the processing, cryopreservation, and biobanking of patient-derived cells and tissues
Authors: Abhimanu Pandey, Shweta Venkataraman, Si Ming Man, Dipti Talaulikar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryBiobanking of patient-derived specimens offers unique opportunities for retrospective testing that could potentially contribute to diagnosing and evaluating clinical conditions, advancing personalized medicine and translational biomedical discovery. In this protocol, we detail the collection, processing, and cryopreservation of peripheral blood, bone marrow, and lymph nodes from patients with hematological malignancies. This protocol can be used for multiomics to gain cellular and molecular insights into blood cancers and to test the therapeutic potential of compounds for translational biomedical research.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Lim et al.1 and Rijal et al.2Subject areas: Cell isolation, Cancer, Health Sciences, Clinical Protocol

Before you begin

Our step-by-step protocol describes the collection, processing, and storage of patient-derived specimens in a hospital setting. We specifically outline the timing and procedures for processing peripheral blood, bone marrow and lymph nodes. In addition, we provide recommendations on the administrative and ethical requirements for the successful operation of a biobank.

Institutional permissions

Prior to working with patient-derived samples, approval from the local Human Ethics Committee and informed consent from the patient is required.

For this study, we obtained written informed consent from all participants for the research use of biological materials that were not required for diagnostic purposes. This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of The Canberra Hospital (ETH.4.04.193). All samples stored were de-identified of personal information and each patient was given a unique biobank code.

In BSC, transfer the tissue onto a sterile 100 mm tissue culture plate using sterile forceps (Figure 3B).

Expected outcomes

This protocol provides an inexpensive method to cryopreserve patient-derived samples including peripheral blood, bone marrow aspirate and lymph nodes. Upon the completion of this protocol and depending upon the health status of the donor, 1–3 × 106 PBMCs and 4–11 × 106 white blood cells per mL of whole blood, and 5–15 × 106 cells per mL of bone marrow aspirate are expected, as also reported previously.6,7 Biobanking of samples using this protocol provides the opportunity to analyze multiple samples from patients of different ages, disease stages, and treatment stages simultaneously, improving the consistency and robustness of experimental results. In addition, biobanking removes the time constraints of using samples at certain time points by preserving the cells/tissues for years.

Limitations

This protocol relies on coordination among multiple individuals including donors or patients, clinicians, research technicians and administrators. An adequate storage facility including liquid nitrogen storage and −80 freezers is required. A tissue bank committee with representation from relevant stakeholders, including clinicians, nurses, scientists, and patient representatives are required to ensure the proper functioning of the tissue bank, including reviewing applications for samples undergoing biobanking.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Low PBMC yield less than 1 × 106 per mL of whole blood. (During step 43).

Potential solution

Low PBMC yield can be addressed by optimizing various aspects of density gradient separation using Ficoll.

•Ficoll separation: For the optimal efficiency of Ficoll separation, the blood-HBSS and Ficoll phases must not mix prior to centrifugation. Care should be taken to underlay the Ficoll beneath the blood-HBSS mixture slowly. An alternative method is to first add the Ficoll to a new 50 mL tube with a serological pipette. Then the blood-HBSS is layered dropwise with a sterile transfer pipette or serological pipette.

Ficoll separation: For the optimal efficiency of Ficoll separation, the blood-HBSS and Ficoll phases must not mix prior to centrifugation. Care should be taken to underlay the Ficoll beneath the blood-HBSS mixture slowly. An alternative method is to first add the Ficoll to a new 50 mL tube with a serological pipette. Then the blood-HBSS is layered dropwise with a sterile transfer pipette or serological pipette.

•Storage of Ficoll and HBSS: The density of Ficoll is impacted by temperature, therefore, it is critical to ensure that the HBSS mixed with the PBMC sample is at room temperature. In addition, Ficoll is light-sensitive and improper storage may reduce the efficiency of PBMC separation.

Storage of Ficoll and HBSS: The density of Ficoll is impacted by temperature, therefore, it is critical to ensure that the HBSS mixed with the PBMC sample is at room temperature. In addition, Ficoll is light-sensitive and improper storage may reduce the efficiency of PBMC separation.

Problem 2

Red Blood Cell lysis. (During step 36).

Potential solution

Red pigment in the cell pellet indicates erythrocyte contamination in the sample. This may impact downstream measurement and analysis. We recommend repeating the cell lysis step twice if red pigmentation is observed after the first round of lysis.

Problem 3

Low cell viability before cryopreservation. (During step 43).

Potential solution

Low cell viability may be caused by dead cells in the cell suspension. Ensure prompt processing of samples once received. If samples including PB and BM cannot proceed immediately, they should be stored in the HBSS or RPMI at 4°C.

Problem 4

Low cell viability after reviving cells from the biobank. (During step 54).

Potential solution

Avoid freeze-thaw cycles. A detailed sample location (freezer, rack, box, and position) and type of samples (PBMC, plasma, lymph node and bone marrow) could avoid unnecessary delay in locating samples. Cryopreservation and thawing at controlled temperature rates could improve cell viability.8,9

Problem 5

The nucleic acid quantity and quality is not optimal for genetic sequencing. (During step 54).

Potential solution

Freezing more than 1 × 106 cells could provide better DNA yield. Prompt processing of samples could minimize RNA degradation. Patients who have undergone treatment may have poor nucleic acid quantity, a nucleic acid cleaning step, after extraction, could improve the quality.
