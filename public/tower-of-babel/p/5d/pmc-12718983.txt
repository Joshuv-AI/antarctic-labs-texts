Neoadjuvant therapy and locally advanced cancer diseases
Authors: Peng-Hui Wang, Szu-Ting Yang
Published: 2024

Neoadjuvant therapy (NAT) has become one of the very interesting approaches and also served a potential management in many kinds of cancers, particularly for those patients with locally or far-advanced cancers who need reversing their inoperatable clinical situations to suitability for surgery because these patients are not only at the lowest chance to be managed successfully by primary surgery but also at highest risk to be complicated by the surgery-related adverse events or even surgery-related mortality.1–4 To enhance the feasibility of subsequent surgical approach, there are many strategies available in the modern cancer treatment (called NAT by multimodality approach), including NAT by chemotherapy (CT) (NACT),1–4 and NAT by radiotherapy (RT) without (NART) or with simultaneous administration of CT (concurrent chemoradiotherapy: NACCRT or combination of CT and RT: NART+NACT),4–6 and all of them can be used alone or furthermore in combination of many potential therapeutic approaches or adding active agents, regardless of their availability in the market or under investigation, including immunotherapy (IO), targeted therapy, and many others.6–10 Moreover, besides aforementioned strategies, many modified protocols, such as sequential application of any one of aforementioned therapeutic approaches, such as NART+following consolidation CT (NART+CCT) have got very impressive and exciting results, according to the reports from many clinical observative studies and prospective randomized clinical trials (RCTs).6,11–14 The current article entitled “Short-term outcomes of short- and long-course chemoradiotherapy before total mesorectal excision for locally advanced rectal tumors: A single-center study in Taiwan utilizing propensity score matching” may be one of the best examples.14

Dr. Chang and his colleagues attempted to compare the short-term outcomes of patients with locally advanced rectal cancers (LARC) treated either with short-course RT+following at least four courses of CCT (SCRT+CCT) or with conventional long-course CCRT.14 To minimize the risk of selection biases, the authors did their efforts to make the study population appearing more homogeneously, including exclusion of SCRT without following CCT and SCRT+following immediate total mesorectal excision, or CCRT after December 2019.14 The results showed patients in the SCRT+CCT group had a statistically significantly higher 3-year disease-free survival (DFS) rate than in the CCRT group (81.8% vs 62.1%), although the above-mentioned survival benefit cannot be reproducible by both 3-year cancer-specific survival rate (CSS) and overall survival (OS) rate, which did not reach the statistically significant difference (93.9% vs 87.9% and 93.9% vs 86.4%, respectively).14 Why the discrepancy existed is uncertain? One reason may be explained by selection bias or some confounding factors were also associated with CSS and OS. Attempting to explore the potential biases, multivariate analyses were conducted and data showed carcinoembryonic antigen levels (≥5 ng/mL, hazard ratio [HR] 2.16; 95% CI, 1.13-4.10), lymphovascular space involvement (HR, 2.81; 95% CI, 1.38-5.73), and perineural invasion (HR, 3.01; 95% CI, 1.38-6.80) were all correlated with short DFS, meaning those patients with the aforementioned three prognostic factors had a higher recurrence rate than those patients without.14 Additionally, perineural invasion was independently associated with worse OS (HR, 3.79; 95% CI, 1.53-9.38).14 Furthermore, multivariate analyses also validated the benefits of using SCRT+CCT.14 Patients in the SCRT+CCT group had a longer DFS and a lower risk of recurrence (HR, 0.37; 95% CI, 0.19-0.74) compared to patients in the conventional CCRT group.14 Although the current study did offer little additional information for treating patients with LARC, the authors’ effort is worthy of applause.

NAT is a wait-to-see (or watch-to-wait) policy for cancer treatment. The delaying in performing the definite and potentially curable treatment, similar to management of locally advanced cervical cancer and advanced epithelial ovarian cancer may occur and this negative impact should be always in concern.3,4,7 The potential advantages of NAT-RS (NAT+radical surgery) are based on the following theoretical reasons, including (1) decreasing the tumor burden and shrinking large local lesions to make surgery less extensively and more precisely and easily, (2) increasing the successful rate to perform the definite and complete resection of originally unresectable diseases, (3) removing the residual tumor cells that are not or partially responsive to NAT because this approach continues to destroy resistant cells to minimize the risk of residual resistant strains deposition in the primary site and to avoid the subsequent dissemination, and (4) offering the possible chance to eradicate the concealed metastatic diseases during the NAT.11 If all of them can work well, it is believed to be beneficial for cosmetic purposes and provide a better chance to preserve the organs or organ-related function, secondary to fewer organ injuries and less-extent radical surgery needing adequate safe margin of tumor.11 Moreover, according to assess the patients’ response to NAT and examine the final pathological findings after NAT, healthy-providers may have a better chance to understand tumor behavior in precise and to offer the better accuracy to predict patients’ outcome, and additionally, it may serve the guideline to select the better agents or strategies for further rescue therapy.11 However, the theoretical benefits may not be always present because a higher regrowth rate has been reported and higher rates of complete clinical response with NAT did not improve DFS compared to historical controls.15 Furthermore, it is possible worse local control when surgery is delayed for radiochemo-resistant cancers.15 All question the safety of NAT in unselected patients.15

Another concern is that the optimal time interval between NAT and radical surgery is unknown. The shorter time interval may avoid the risk of “rebound” or “regrowth” of tumor and minimize the risk of dissemination of residual tumors which may be plentiful of resistant-strain tumors; however, the risk of surgery-related complications may be higher. After adequate time to recover from NAT, definite radical surgery can be performed in safety, contributing to the benefits of NAT apparently appeared in Dr. Chang’ study, which included more than half of patients having downstaging (59.1% vs 66.7%), more than 60% of patients having moderate to complete tumor regression (59.1% vs 66.7%).14 All hint NAT indeed works very well in the management of patients with LARC. However. Dr. Chang’s study showed the limited value of SCRT+CCT compared to conventional CCRT.14 In their study, SCRT+CCT only enhanced short-term DFS compared to conventional CCRT.14 This point may not be strong to convince the audience to accept SCRT+CCT can be totally in place of original conventional CCRT. As shown by authors, some experts and radiologists still recommended the use of CCRT as standard of care for their patients, even though evidence may support the better choice as SCRT+CCT after 2019.14 Therefore, extensive systemic review and meta-analysis may be required to meet clinical need.

The following systemic review and meta-analysis conducted by Linkou Chang gung Memorial Hospital may be responsible to this dilemma, and test the hypothesis that SCRT+CCT may be better than CCRT.6 The authors compared the outcome between two.6 Additionally, the subgroup analysis was also conducted, such as comparison between SCRT without CCT and conventional CCRT as well as comparison between SCRT+CCT and conventional CCRT.6 The results of the former showed SCRT without CCT was not statistically significantly different from CCRT in their evaluated items (DFS and OS), and even presented the inferiority of SCRT without CCT to CCRT, suggesting that pure SCRT alone may not successful in place of the conventional CCRT in the management of patients with LARC. By contrast, data limited to compare the difference between SCRT+CCT (n = 1452) and conventional CCRT (n = 1452) may test the findings of Dr. Chang’s current study14 and also be more appropriate to the recent trend or clinical need.

In this meta-analysis, pathological complete response was higher in using SCRT+CCT compared to CCRT with a better relative risk (RR) of 1.60 (95% CI, 1.35-1.91), and this finding was particularly for patients with ypN0 response, because SCRT+CCT was also significantly better than CCRT (relative risk [RR], 1.06; 95% CI, 1.01-1.12).6 By contrast, for investigating the ypT3-4 response or evaluating the rate of successful tumor downstaging status, there was no statistically significant difference between two groups (RR 1.04; 95% CI, 0.75-1.45 and RR 0.87; 95 CI, 0.74-1.01, respectively).6 However, these inconsistent results may not interfere with the trend to favoring the using SCRT+CCT for treating patients with LARC, based on their better outcomes.6 SCRT+CCT not only provided a superior DFS (RR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.04- 1.17) but also offered a statistically significantly prolonged OS (RR, 1.05; 95% CI, 1.00- 1.11) compared to CCRT.6 Moreover, besides the better outcomes of both DFS and OS in patients undergoing SCRT+CCT, sphincter preservation rates are also significantly higher (RR, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.01-1.11),6 which met the main rationale for using NAT to improve patients’ long-term quality of life (QoL) through organ preservation. Taken together, more and more doctors favored the use of SCRT+CCT in place of original CCRT as a better choice of NAT and also served the standard of care for patients with LARC if NAT is a planned watch-and-wait strategy in the management of this population patients.

However, does SCRT+CCT win this competition? Additionally, the audience may be interested in the difference of recurrence pattern between SCRT+CCT and CCRT. Dr. Chang’s study did not give an answer,14 but Dr. Liao’s meta-analysis provided the information.6 Compared to CCRT, the pooled results of recurrence patterns seem to be similar to SCRT+CCT, regardless whether local recurrence or distant metastasis was evaluated.6 The meta-analysis found the trend to increasing local recurrence and decreasing distant metastasis in the SCRT+CCT group.6

Organ preservation is currently considered a justifiable treatment strategy, as it is involved the long-term QoL, contributing to patients’ willing to accept NAT to avoid receiving a permanent stroma.15 However, the safety of NAT is seldom discussed, although the potentially worse oncologic outcomes may be really present. We congratulated Dr. Chang’s contribution to the current issue about the using NAT either by SCRT-CCT or by CCRT in the management of patients of LARC. Similar to some uncertainties of NAT in management of many kinds of cancers, such as cervical cancer and epithelial ovarian cancer,5–7,12–16 the well-conducted RCTs, increased number of real-world patients studies, homogeneity in the reported populations (such as biomarkers-guided patients an example), uniform but effective NAT strategy (after identification of a better combined strategy), standard dose escalation techniques of RT (to minimize the surrounding tissue damage and offer adequate dosage), effective tools for prediction and assessment of patients’ response before, the midpoint of or after treatment, and of most importance, long-term oncologic outcomes are urgently needed. Based on evidence, health-providers can provide the precise and standard of care therapy to cancer patients to achieve the better outcomes and maintain the better QoL. Of course, the current trend in the management for these highly complex and lethal diseases (cancers) is often based on evidence, and multimodality approach, such as an integration of NAT into radical surgery in Dr. Chang’s report, becomes more and more popular.
