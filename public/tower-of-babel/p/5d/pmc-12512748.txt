Viral insulin/IGF-like peptides inhibit IGF-1 receptor signaling to enhance viral replication
Authors: Aurelien Chuard, Kalaimagal Nesarajah, Khadija Danazumi, Kaitlin Reiners, Fa Zhang, Lev Levintov, Marta Lubos, Lenka Žáková, Rachel Ruggera, Sarah McMenamin, Harish Vashisth, Jiří Jiráček, Richard Dimarchi, Emrah Altindis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYThe insulin/insulin growth factor (IGF) system plays a central role in regulating metabolism and growth. We identified viral insulin/IGF1-like peptides (VILPs) in Iridoviridae and investigated their role in host-virus interactions. Using grouper iridovirus (GIV) on grouper and zebrafish cells, we show that VILPs are early viral genes and are secreted during infection. VILPs activate insulin receptor (IR) and IGF-1 receptor (IGF1R) phosphorylation and stimulate the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) pathway. GIV-VILP present in the supernatants of infected cells triggers dose- and time-dependent signaling through selective interaction with IGF1R. Functionally, IR inhibition suppresses GIV replication, whereas IGF1R inhibition enhances it, and IGF-1 stimulation reduces replication. During infection, GIV-VILP competes with IGF-1, attenuating IGF1R signaling and reducing proliferation. Transcriptome analysis confirms negative regulation of cell cycle pathways. Using a zebrafish infection model, we demonstrate VILP expression and IGF-1 signaling inhibition. Our findings reveal a viral mimicry mechanism that modulates host IGF-1 signaling to promote viral replication.

INTRODUCTION

Viruses have evolved a variety of viral mimicry strategies to imitate host proteins, enabling them to manipulate host metabolism, cell cycle, and cellular signaling cascades and evade the host immune response.1–3 We recently showed that nine viruses in the Iridoviridae family have genes encoding viral insulin/insulin-like growth factor 1-like peptides (VILPs).4 In our previous studies, we chemically synthetized five of these VILPs and showed that all VILPs can bind to human insulin receptor (IR) and insulin growth factor 1 (IGF-1) receptor (IGF1R) with varying potencies. While most of the VILPs, including all double chain (dc), resembling insulin VILPs act as agonists of IR and IGF-1,4,5 two VILPs act as antagonists of the IGF1R.6–8 Specifically, single chain (sc, resembling IGF-1 that contains a C-peptide) forms of lymphocystis disease virus-1 (LCDV-1) and Mandarin fish ranavirus (MFRV) VILPs have very high affinity for the IGF1R. However, these two scVILPs inhibit IGF1R signaling. These unique characteristics are most likely acquired as a result of the distinct evolution of the VILPs in the viruses to facilitate viral pathogenesis, unlike the evolution of the host insulin/IGFs.9,10

Insulin/IGF system plays a key role in orchestrating cellular machinery. Insulin primarily regulates cell metabolism, including the glucose, protein, and lipid metabolism.11,12 On the other hand, the IGF-1 regulates cell proliferation, survival, and differentiation.11 Because insulin and IGF-1 play key roles in human disease including diabetes and cancer, there has been extensive research over the last hundred years characterizing this system. However, our knowledge on the role of the insulin/IGF system is still relatively limited for most organisms, including fish. While the main functions of fish insulin13,14 (regulating metabolism, development, and feeding) and fish IGFs15,16 (proliferation, survival, differentiation, and growth) are similar to that in mammals, fish show some important differences. For example, compared to mammals, fish express less IR and more IGF1R.17–20 Our initial studies show that both forms of VILPs (sc and dc) have higher affinity for IGF1R compared to IR. Consequently, they are more potent on IGF1R.4,5,7 In fish, IGF-1 is also involved in osmoregulation, as higher doses of IGF-1 stabilize plasma osmolality and sodium levels.21 Furthermore, from a host-pathogen-interaction perspective, both insulin and specifically IGF-1 play key roles in regulating both adaptive and innate immune responses in fish.22

In this study, we used zebrafish and grouper infection models to determine the role of VILPs in host-pathogen interactions during grouper iridovirus (GIV) infection. GIV is one of the nine VILP carrying Iridoviridae family viruses with a large genome, ~140k bp in length and contains 120 open reading frames.23 This virus was isolated from spleen tissue of a diseased yellow grouper (Epinephelus awoara).24 GIV is a serious pathogen in mariculture causing high mortality rates in cultured grouper (Epinephelus spp.).25,26 GIV infection induces cytopathic effects including cytoplasmic vacuolation, fusion, formation of cell aggregations, and cell rounding in grouper cell lines.24,27 Our previous studies characterized chemically synthesized VILPs using mammalian systems. However, the role of VILPs on different fish models and their functional role in host-pathogen interactions remained unknown. Here, we address this gap by focusing on the effects of GIV infection and GIV-VILP on the host insulin/IGF system during the infection.

RESULTS

GIV-VILP exhibits significant homology to fish insulin and IGFs with a predicted insulin/IGF-like structure and distinct IGF1R binding

We previously showed that VILPs are 30%–50% identical to human insulin and IGF-1.4,7 The GIV genome encodes a single copy of the GIV-VILP gene, and alignment analysis of GIV-VILP compared to human, zebrafish, and grouper insulins and IGF-1s revealed that all six critical cysteine residues, essential for forming the intra- and interchain disulfide bonds required for insulin/IGF structures, are conserved among them (Figure 1A). Unlike insulin’s longer C-peptide, GIV-VILP possesses a short seven amino acid C-peptide similar to IGFs. GIV-VILP exhibits 35% identity to grouper IGF-1 and 38% identity to grouper insulin (Table S1). Furthermore, the GIV-VILP sequence contains a 184 amino acid (aa) long extension domain mainly composed of 18 repeated motifs (VAPST), which is potentially cleaved during processing of the ligand (Figure S3B).

We performed structural modeling of both single- and double-chain VILPs to their tertiary structures with zebrafish insulin and IGF-1. VILPs exhibited insulin/IGF-like structures, including two α helices in the A chain, an α helix in the B chain, and six conserved cysteines forming three disulfide bridges (Figures 1B and 1C). While GIV-dcVILP is predicted to have a similar configuration to zebrafish insulin (Figure 1B), GIV-scVILP closely resembles zebrafish IGF-1 (Figure 1C). The only difference in the GIV-scVILP was observed in the position of the C-peptide, which may contribute to distinct functions of the GIV-scVILP. We also completed structural analysis of the GIV-VILP binding to zebrafish IR (Figure 1D) and IGF1R (Figure 1E) compared to the native ligands, labeling residues important for receptor binding. First, we compared IR/IGF1R sequences from human, zebrafish, and grouper using UniProt and found ~65%–67% identity between fish and human receptors (Table S6). GIV-dcVILP and zebrafish insulin, as well as GIV-scVILP and zebrafish IGF1, have similar binding modes to the zebrafish IR (Figure 1D) and zebrafish IGF1R (Figure 1E), respectively. Specifically, residues that are critical to IR binding (GlyA1, TyrA19, AsnA21, GlyB8, and LeuB11; Figure 1D) and to IGF1R binding (Gly7, Glu9, Asp12, Leu14, Gly22, Tyr24, Gly42, and Tyr60; Figure 1E) are conserved and adopted similar positions upon receptor binding as in the native binders. Despite similarities between overall configurations of GIV-VILPs and native binders, there are also distinct features present in the binding modes of GIV-VILPs (see supplemental information II).

GIV-VILPs is localized in Golgi apparatus during infection and is an early viral product

To determine the cellular localization and potential secretion of GIV-VILP, we transfected GK cells with a pcDNA3.1+ plasmid expressing GIV-VILP fused to eGFP on its C terminus. After 24 h, cells were fixed, mounted, and analyzed by confocal microscopy. Using a Golgi-specific antibody (anti-58K Golgi protein), we observed that GIV-VILP-eGFP colocalized with the Golgi signals (Figure 1F). The correlation between the GFP and the 58K Golgi protein was quantified with a Pearson correlation factor of 0.76, indicating that GIV-VILPs are localized in the Golgi secretory pathway (Figure 1G). Consistently, SignalP 5.0 predicted that GIV-VILP (UniProt: Q5GAI4) possesses a signal peptide preceding the B chain that will direct the VILP to the secretory pathway (Figure S2). To understand the GIV-VILP kinetics, we first examined its transcription during viral replication. We infected GK cells at a multiplicity of infection of 1 (MOI 1) and collected samples at 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h post-infection (hpi). We extracted RNA and quantified viral transcripts using qRT-PCR. Based on prior kinetics experiments with Singapore grouper iridovirus (SGIV), we selected GIV90, GIV87, and GIV39 (major capsid protein) as immediate-early, early, and late gene controls, respectively. Our results confirmed this selection, and we found that GIV32, encoding GIV-VILP, was transcribed as early as 3 hpi, similar to GIV87 transcripts, classifying it as an early viral gene (Figure 1H).

GIV-VILP is secreted during infection and is not a structural protein

We purified GIV virions and analyzed the viral particle protein composition using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). We identified 20 structural proteins, including major capsid protein (GIV39), in all three independent experiments. Using BlastP, we determined the orthologs of these proteins and predicted their functions (Table S2). Notably, GIV01 (Rho_N domain-containing protein), GIV36 (surface protein), GIV17 (protein kinase domain-containing protein), GIV76 (methyl-accepting transducer domain-containing protein), and GIV44 (putative tyrosine protein kinase), and GIV39 (major capsid protein) were the top hits identified in all three experiments with the highest number of identified peptides. Intriguingly, GIV contained two structural protein kinases (GIV17 and GIV44) and an RNAse and three collagen-like proteins (GIV46, GIV20, GIV21, and GIV97). These results also indicate that GIV-VILP is not a structural protein of GIV virions.

Following the structural analysis, we analyzed the secretome of GIV-infected cells. Samples were collected 24 h post-infection and secreted proteins were concentrated and analyzed using LC-MS/MS. In total, we identified 10 secreted GIV proteins (Table S3). Notably, GIV-VILP (GIV32) was one of the most abundant, with peptides identified in both B (Gln19 to Gly40) and A domain (Arg51 to Lys78); C domain was not identified due to enzymatic limitation of prior LC-MS analysis (Table S2). In addition, ubiquitin-like domain-containing protein (GIV57), sema domain-containing protein (GIV87), and collagen-like protein (GIV63) were the other most abundant proteins (Table S3). Interestingly, two additional viral proteins have been identified in the secretome, mimicking fibroblast growth factors (FGFs): FGF-10 (GIV79) and FGF-22 (GIV78). Finally, we identified a second collagen-like protein (GIV62) in the secretome, in addition to the one identified in the structural proteins (GIV21). Ig-domain containing protein (GIV14) was another interesting protein, potentially involved in immune evasion. Overall, these results demonstrate active secretion of the VILPs during the GIV viral cycle.

Chemically synthesized GIV-VILPs are active ligands of grouper IGF1R and/or IR

In our previous studies, we characterized VILPs using murine and humanized cell models.4,7–9 However, the impact of VILPs on their native hosts, fish cells and fish IR/IGF1R, signaling has not been studied. To address this gap, we first chemically synthesized both sc (IGF-1 like, with a C peptide) and dc (insulin like, without a C peptide) forms of GIV-VILP as previously described.4,7 Using GK cells, we tested whether chemically synthesized GIV-VILPs can stimulate host IGF1R/IR autophosphorylation and activate post-receptor signaling. We initially conducted dose-response experiments for 15 min and measured Akt phosphorylation indicating activation of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt pathway and Erk phosphorylation signifying Ras/MAPK pathway activation.

Teleosts exhibit high IGF1R expression, likely due to the presence of two IGF1R genes, indeterminate growth patterns, and remarkable regenerative capacity.15–18 Consistent with the literature, GK cells were sensitive to IGF-1, and IGF1R receptor phosphorylation was strongly induced by IGF-1 compared to insulin (Figure 2A). On the other hand, GIV-scVILP and GIV-dcVILP were not as potent as IGF-1 and comparable to insulin in stimulating receptor phosphorylation (Figure 2A). The PI3K pathway was activated by all ligands, mirroring the receptor signaling pattern, with IGF-1 inducing the strongest activation (Figure 2A). However, we did not observe a similar dose response in the phosphorylation of Erk.

GIV infection induces receptor phosphorylation and selective pAkt signaling

To assess the VILP function during infection, we infected serum-starved GK cells with varying doses of GIV (MOI 0.01, 0.1, 1) and collected samples in 3 hpi, 24 hpi, and 48 hpi. Upon completion of the experiment, the supernatant from infected cells was transferred to serum-starved GK cells for signal stimulation analysis. At 3 hpi, GIV infection did not stimulate insulin/IGF signaling (Figure 2B). This is consistent with the GIV-VILP gene expression profile, confirming that it is not an immediate-early gene. Starting at 24 hpi, we observed strong receptor phosphorylation—in a viral load-dependent manner—as well as a biased post-receptor signaling phosphorylation of Akt. Interestingly, phosphorylation of Erk was not correlated with viral load (Figure 2B), and this is consistent with our results using chemically synthesized VILPs (Figure 2A). These results indicate that GIV-VILP produced during infection biases host insulin/IGF signaling specifically toward the PI3K/Akt pathway.

GIV-VILP secreted from infected GK cells selectively activates and phosphorylates the IGF1R receptor

While most teleost fish cells are known to express higher levels of IGF1R17,18 and our data indicate that GK and AB.9 (zebrafish caudal fin) fibroblast cell lines are more sensitive to IGF-1, these cells express both IR and IGF1R. To determine the receptor specificity of GIV-VILP, we used a unique mouse cell line model28,29 that overexpress either IGF1R or IR-B and tested the effect of GIV-VILP on each specific receptor. We first infected GK cells with GIV (MOI 1, 24 h), collected and filtered the supernatant, and used it to stimulate IGF1R-expressing cells. The supernatant activated both IGF1R autophosphorylation and Akt phosphorylation (Figure 2C). In contrast, IR-expressing cells showed no detectable phosphorylation after stimulation (Figure 2D). These results indicate that GIV-VILP produced during infection selectively interacts with IGF1R.

GIV VILP is IGF1R specific and the B domain of GIV-VILP is essential for binding

To examine the structure-function relationship and explore the contribution of each peptide chain to GIV-VILP’s selective binding and phosphorylation of IGF1R, we synthesized two hybrid double-chain peptides, GivB-InsA, combining the B domain of GIV-VILP with the A domain of insulin, and InsB-GivA, combining the B domain of insulin with the A domain of GIV-VILP. We first completed binding competition assays to assess the affinity of various ligands to the IGF1R. Human IGF-1 exhibited significantly higher affinity (Kd 0.2984 nM) to IGF1R than human insulin (Kd 293 nM) as expected (Table S4). The hybrid GivB-InsA exhibited affinity for IGF1R (Kd 71.35 nM) higher than insulin, while InsB-GivA did not bind to the receptor. These findings highlight the critical role of the GIV VILP B chain in mediating IGF1R binding. Consistent with our binding results, stimulation of IGF1R-overexpressing cells with the hybrid peptides revealed that GivB-InsA was almost comparable to GIV-dcVILP to induced autophosphorylation of IGF1R and Akt phosphorylation, whereas InsB-GivA failed to elicit any response (Figure 2E). This analysis demonstrates that the B domain of GIV-VILP is essential for its binding to IGF1R; however, this does not rule out a contributory role for the A domain. The lack of activity observed in A chain hybrid may result from impaired structural integrity or altered receptor interactions due to its non-native pairing with the human B chain, which could interfere with critical intrachain contacts necessary for proper folding and function.

Inhibiting IR or Akt activity decreases GIV replication, while IGF1R inhibition promotes it

The presence of a VILP in the GIV genome indicates the involvement of the insulin/IGF system in viral replication. To determine the roles of IR, IGF1R, and related post-receptor signaling pathways (PI3K/Akt) in GIV replication, we used specific inhibitors and competitive ligands of these receptors. We first used picropodophyllin (PPP), a tyrosine kinase inhibitor specific to the IGF1R. Surprisingly, inhibition of IGF1R by PPP (2 μM) resulted in a substantial increase in viral replication of 129% (Figure 2F). Subsequently, we tested the impact of an IR-specific peptide antagonist S961 and showed that S961 decreased GIV replication by 54% at the lowest dose tested (10 nM) (Figure 2G). Finally, BMS-754807, an inhibitor that targets both IR and IGF1R increased replication by 118% at concentration 0.1 nM (Figure 2H). Although the magnitude of these increases was lower than IGF1R inhibitor’s effect, the relatively higher expression of IGF1R in these cells suggests that BMS-754807 may exert an effect similar to IGF1R inhibitor. On the other hand, consistent with our signaling experiments, inhibition of the Akt pathway using MK-2206 led to a 38% decrease in GIV viral replication (Figure 2I). These findings demonstrate that while IR signaling promotes viral replication, IGF1R signaling does not have the same effect. In fact, activation of the IGF1R pathway appears to inhibit viral replication.

To determine whether the infection has an impact on IGF1R and IGF-1 expressions, we infected AB.9 and ZSSJ (zebrafish spleen) cells (MOI 0.1) and collected the samples at 24 h post-infection. We demonstrated that GIV infection downregulates IGF-1 transcript levels by 24% in AB.9 cells and 48% in ZSSJ cells, while IGF1R-A and IGF1R-B levels dropped by 56% and 41%, respectively, in AB.9 cells (Figure S6). In addition, there was a significant decrease in the total IGF1R protein levels after GIV infection (Figure S6).

IGF-1 inhibits GIV replication, while GIV-VILP and GIV supernatant counteracts this effect

Following the results of receptor and post-receptor pathway inhibition, we tested the direct impact of IGF-1, insulin, GIV-scVILP, or GIV-dcVILP on GIV replication. GK cells were inoculated with GIV (MOI 1) in serum-free media for 1 h, followed by repeated stimulation with each ligand (100 nM) every 3 h for 12 h. GIV replication was quantified at the end of the experiment. Consistent with our IGF1R inhibition experiments, IGF-1 stimulation reduced GIV replication by 32%, while stimulation with insulin, GIV-scVILP, and GIV-dcVILP had no effect on replication (Figure 2J).

Building on these results, we hypothesized that GIV-VILP will inhibit IGF-1 signaling to counteract IGF-1’s detrimental effect on viral replication. To this end, we first examined the competitive potential of GIV supernatant containing GIV-VILP on the IGF-1-mediated inhibition of GIV replication. Interestingly, co-incubation of IGF-1 and GIV supernatant restored GIV replication to initial levels, while GIV supernatant alone had no effect on replication (Figure 2K). GIV-scVILP solely also tended to restore the GIV replication.

To elucidate the underlying mechanism of this competitive effect, we performed a signaling competition experiment using IGF-1 and GIV-scVILP, as well as supernatant from GIV-infected cells (St) and IGF-1. Co-incubation of GIV-scVILP with IGF-1, as well as GIV supernatant with IGF-1, decreased phosphorylation of both IGF1R and Akt compared to the initial IGF-1 stimulation (Figure 2L). These results demonstrate that a key function of GIV-VILP is to mitigate the detrimental effects of IGF-1 on viral replication.

Finally, to determine whether this competitive effect is specific to the GIV-scVILP, we repeated the competition assay using GIV-dcVILP. Unlike GIV-scVILP, GIV-dcVILP failed to inhibit IGF-1 signaling, strongly supporting that the biologically active, secreted form of GIV-VILP during infection is a single-chain peptide (Figure S7).

Transcriptome analysis reveals GIV-VILP induced upregulation of negative cell cycle regulators

To evaluate the impact of GIV-scVILP on gene expression and associated metabolic pathways, we conducted a transcriptome analysis on GK cells stimulated either with IGF-1, GIV-scVILP (100 nM, 6 h), or control. Additionally, to assess the overall impact of GIV infection on host gene expression, we infected GK cells with GIV (MOI 1) and extracted RNA at 18 hpi. After stimulation with IGF-1, we identified 2,648 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) compared to control group, 2,463 DEGs with GIV-scVILP, and 5,128 DEGs following GIV infection (Figure 3A). Using the gene-set enrichment tool ShinyGO, we analyzed common pathways derived from the DEGs identified after stimulation with each ligand. This analysis showed that GIV infection primarily regulates autophagy processes, Golgi vesicle transport, and organelle structure organization (Figure 3A). Interestingly, stimulation with GIV-scVILP led to strong regulation of cell cycle processes (Figure 3B), while IGF-1 stimulation was mostly associated with organelle structure organization, regulation of cell cycle, and tRNA metabolic processes (Figure S5). We performed a direct comparative analysis of the GIV-scVILP and GIV infection transcriptomes, revealing 1,506 common genes with specific alterations (Figure 3C). These common transcripts suggest a potential mechanism underlying GIV-scVILP function during infection. Gene set enrichment analysis of these 1,506 common genes indicated that most pathways were related to cell cycle and mitosis regulation (Figure 3C).

When we categorized the common transcripts into upregulated (Figure 3D) and downregulated DEGs (Figure 3E), we found that 986 genes are upregulated, while 464 genes are downregulated. The top categories for both GIV infection and GIV-scVILP upregulated DEGs were related to the negative regulation of the mitotic cell cycle and the regulation of cell cycle phase transition (Figure 3D). On the other hand, downregulated DEGs were associated with rRNA processing and nuclear and mitochondrial transport genes (Figure 3E). Since the cell cycle is directly related to mitosis and cell proliferation, these results suggest that GIV may use GIV-scVILP to directly target and inhibit cellular proliferation.

GIV-VILP stimulation reduces cell proliferation and outcompetes the proliferative effects of IGF-1

Our transcriptome data indicate a strong negative impact of GIV-scVILP on cell proliferation and cell cycle regulation. To test this, we assessed the impact of IGF-1, GIV-scVILP, and the supernatant obtained from GIV-infected cells, as well as their combinatory effects on GK cell proliferation. Cells were stimulated for 4 h with each ligand or their combination (100 nM), followed by a 4-h incubation with EdU to monitor cell proliferation. While basal proliferation of GK cells was high (mock), IGF-1 incubation tended to increase the proliferation. Notably, the basal proliferation was significantly decreased when cells were incubated either with the supernatant (45%) or GIV-scVILP (33%) (Figure 3F). More interestingly, when cells were co-incubated with IGF-1 and either GIV-supernatant or GIV-scVILP, the proliferation was significantly decreased by 49% and 45%, respectively. These findings demonstrate that GIV uses GIV-VILPs as a potent inhibitor of GK cell proliferation, effectively counteracting the pro-proliferative effects of IGF-1 that is detrimental to GIV replication (Figure 2K).

GIV-VILP is expressed in vivo and stimulates signaling in zebrafish cells

Our findings on enhanced viral replication and its impact on cell proliferation suggest that VILP is a key pathogenesis factor actively expressed during in vivo infection, potentially driving disease progression. Due to the challenges of working with grouper in a laboratory setting, primarily because of its large size and aggressive nature, we developed a zebrafish infection model as an alternative. To this end, we orally infected adult zebrafish with GIV to characterize the kinetic of GIV infection in vivo and monitored viral progression over the first 4 days post-infection. Notably, MCP (major capsid protein) viral transcripts (GIV39) were first detected at 3 days post-infection (dpi), with no earlier detection (Figure 4A) and subsequently declined. In contrast, GIV-VILP transcripts were detected as early as 1 dpi and remained stable throughout the first 4 days of infection (Figure 4A), highlighting their potential role sustaining viral replication in vivo. To assess viral presence in different tissues, we pooled liver, kidney, and spleen samples and used the resulting tissue lysates to infect GK cells. At 4 dpi, we confirmed the presence of viable GIV (Figure 4B) providing the evidence that GIV can infect these tissues. These findings underscore the critical role of VILP during in vivo infection. To further investigate the function of GIV-VILP in zebrafish cells, we utilized the AB.9 cell line (CRL-2298). Consistent with our findings in GK cells, GIV-VILP was expressed as an early gene (Figure 4C). Chemically synthesized GIV-VILPs were also able to stimulate receptor and Akt phosphorylation (Figure 4D). Furthermore, supernatants from infected AB.9 cells induced receptor and Akt phosphorylation in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 4E).

To demonstrate the conserved inhibitory mechanism underlying GIV-VILP action on cell proliferation, we quantified AB.9 cell proliferation after ligand stimulation. IGF-1 increased AB.9 cell proliferation by 141%, but co-incubation with IGF-1 and either GIV-supernatant or GIV-scVILP reduced proliferation significantly, by 60% and 48%, respectively (Figure 4F). These results confirm that GIV utilizes GIV-VILP to competitively inhibit IGF-1 driven cell proliferation, a mechanism consistently observed in both zebrafish and grouper kidney cells. This functional conservation across models suggests a fundamental role for VILPs in GIV infection.

DISCUSSION

Over millions of years, viruses have co-evolved with their hosts, resulting in close interactions and adaptations with their associated host. Viral mimicry mechanism, wherein viruses mimic host proteins, arise from host-virus interactions, aiding viruses in subverting or manipulating cellular processes to their advantage.30 Most of these viral proteins mimic hosts’ growth factors. For example, poxviruses are known to encode epidermal growth factor (EGF) mimics.31 Likewise, the first growth factor identified for vaccinia virus was vaccina growth factor.32 Similarly, viruses are evolved to evade host immune response-associated proteins using mimics. For instance, Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV), cytomegalovirus (CMV), and Herpesvirus Saimiri gene 13 (HVS13) viruses carry cytokine mimicking interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-10, and IL-17, respectively.33–36 In addition, similarities between viral proteins and human proteins are linked to autoimmunity through molecular mimicry. For example, recent studies identified a potential link between Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) and the onset of multiple sclerosis (MS) via this mechanism.37 Epidemiological evidence further supports this connection between EBV infection and MS autoimmunity.38 Our findings extend our knowledge of viral mimicry mechanisms. Here, we demonstrate that GIV developed an insulin/IGF mimicry mechanism to directly manipulate the IGF1R signaling pathway and modulate cell proliferation to increase viral replication.

Our study demonstrates using grouper and zebrafish cell lines that GIV-VILP is transcribed as an early gene and translated during infection. Mass spectrometry analysis identified GIV-VILPs as nonstructural proteins, and secretome analysis found these peptides as secreted proteins during infection. GIV-VILPs have the capability to stimulate host IGF1R signaling and activation of PI3K pathway. Additionally, using specific cell lines expressing only IR or IGF1R, we demonstrated that GIV-VILPs contained in supernatant of infected cells lead to the specific binding of IGF1R rather than IR, showing the specificity of GIV-VILP for IGF1R. This IGF1R-specific effect is also observed for LCDV1-VILP and MFRV-VILP, where these VILPs display a unique inhibitory effect on IGF1R.6–8

Using inhibitors, we demonstrated that the PI3K/Akt pathway is indeed crucial for viral replication, as inhibition of the Akt pathway leads to a decrease in GIV replication. Previous research has shown that PI3K pathway is an important target of viruses.39 Indeed, HSV-1 entry relies on the PI3K activity as its inhibition decreases HSV-1 entry through an unknown mechanism.40 As previously mentioned, these regulations modulate key regulatory or adaptative proteins to regulate the PI3K/Akt pathway or glucose metabolism. However, specific VILPs that directly interact IGF1R receptors to stimulate PI3K/Akt pathway were not identified until this study.

Insulin and IGF-1 bind to and activate their respective receptors, the IR and IGF-1-R, stimulating a conformational change in the receptor structure that initiates receptor autophosphorylation.41,42 Our research demonstrates that the inhibition of IGF1R enhances GIV replication, IGF-1 stimulation reduces it, and GIV infection downregulated both IGF1R and IGF-1 gene expression. Conversely, IR inhibition decreases GIV replication. This highlights the importance of the IR signaling and IR-related downstream pathways for GIV replication and more importantly the negative implication of IGF1R signaling on efficient GIV viral replication. Indeed, IGF1R signaling compared to IR signaling is strongly linked to the regulation of cell proliferation.12,43 This difference in signaling patterns between the two receptors could explain the difference observed in our study. GIV may selectively stimulate PI3K/Akt phosphorylation to enhance nutrient uptake and activate cell metabolism, providing the necessary cellular resources for viral replication. By competing with IGF-1 and avoiding direct IGF1R stimulation, GIV-scVILP prevents the host cell from entering a proliferative state, thereby promoting optimal viral replication. This competitive effect is specific to the GIV-scVILP form, as GIV-dcVILP does not exhibit similar activity, suggesting that GIV-scVILP is the biologically active form produced during infection. GIV-VILP is abundantly and rapidly produced during infection, and we hypothesize that this enables the virus to saturate the IGF-1 system using a weak competitive agonist capable of modestly activating IGF1R alone but functioning as an antagonist under physiological IGF-1 levels by limiting the IGF-1 access to its receptor.

In addition, our work demonstrates that GIV-scVILP upregulates pathways associated with negative regulation of the mitotic cell cycle, and proliferation of GK cells was inhibited by GIV-scVILP and GIV-supernatant. These observations align with our previous work, which showed that MFRV-scVILP and LCDV1-scVILP, identified as the first specific peptide inhibitors of IGF1R signaling, exhibit similar effects.6,7 Interestingly another study showed that LCDV1-scVILP, another inhibitor of the IGF1R, also functioned as an inhibitor of ferroptosis, resulting in the prevention of cell death, which could be leveraged by LCDV-1 to increase its replication.44 Our findings indicate that IGF1R inhibition increases viral replication, and this may be a common virulence mechanism for some VILP-carrying viruses. Cell cycle regulation and viral replication are tightly associated, as several studies described the direct positive or negative implication of cell cycle progression on viral replication and immune evasion.45 Indeed, HSV-1 and HIV-1 reduces cell proliferation by inducing cell arrest in G1 or G2 phase, respectively.46,47 These events are mostly regulated by viral proteins interfering with the cell cycle regulators, such as CDK1, that is hyperphosphorylated by HIV viral protein R.

In this study, we established a zebrafish infection model for GIV to assess the expression of the GIV-VILP in vivo. Clinical signs in grouper include liver and spleen enlargement, fin damage, reduced kidney hematopoietic tissue, skin hemorrhages, inappetence, and high mortality.25,26,48 Grouper is not a suitable laboratory model for studying viral infections, which highlights the need for feasible in vivo models to study GIV-like viruses. In contrast, the zebrafish model has been effectively used to investigate host-virus interactions49 for both fish and human viruses.50–52 Zebrafish have also been used to characterize other Iridoviridae viruses, such as infectious spleen and kidney necrosis virus (ISKNV) and European sheatfish virus (ESV).53,54 Our results revealed the detection of GIV MCP and VILP viral transcripts in key organs after oral infection with GIV. Based on GIV tropism and infectivity, we used zebrafish caudal fin (AB.9) cells for in vitro characterization experiments. We demonstrate that GIV can effectively infect these cells and that GIV-VILP is expressed and secreted and stimulated insulin/IGF signaling in zebrafish cells. Our in vivo findings show early and sustained expression of VILP, highlighting its importance for viral replication. Additionally, we successfully reproduced the impact of GIV-VILPs on cell proliferation in this zebrafish cell model, indicating a conserved role for VILP in modulating host cell processes.

Our structural modeling indicates some potential directions to better understand GIV-VILP’s effect on IGF1R. For example, the differences between IGF-1 and GIV-scVILP for IGF1R binding could be attributed to differences in the length of the C domain, since the C domain in IGF-1 is five amino acids longer than the C domain in GIV-scVILP, respectively, thereby potentially providing IGF-1 additional residue contacts for interacting with the important receptor binding sites, L1 domain and the αCT peptide. Furthermore, IGF-1 contains more glycine/proline residues in the C chain than GIV-scVILP, which potentially assists IGF-1 residues to form tighter contacts with the zebrafish IGF1R primary binding site (Figure S1C). Thus, additional residue contacts coupled with extra glycine/proline residues in the C chain of IGF-1 potentially provide further flexibility to these peptides during receptor binding and potentially result in stronger binding affinities in comparison to the GIV-VILPs. This difference in C peptide and its unique interaction with the receptors may be related to the selective signaling leading to a positive impact on GIV replication.

This viral pathogenesis mechanism advances our understanding of viral infections and their impact on host physiology. Furthermore, it opens an avenue to define the concept of viral mimicry, encompassing these viral hormones. These findings may benefit therapeutic interventions targeting these pathways, particularly in the fish industry, by exploring the development of IR or Akt inhibitors to mitigate viral replication in GIV-infected fish. Overall, our findings define a mimicry mechanism in which GIV directly mimics a host hormone (insulin/IGF), targeting the receptor and inhibiting IGF-1 proliferative activity to enhance viral replication. These findings offer insights into their broader host-pathogen interactions and their broader implications for health and disease.

Limitations of the study

Although Epinephelus coioides (grouper) is the natural host of GIV, and thus the most biologically relevant model for studying host-pathogen interactions, we used zebrafish—the most widely used fish model in experimental virology—due to the significant logistical challenges associated with maintaining grouper in a laboratory setting. The lack of established protocols and experimental resources for grouper research currently limits its feasibility as a laboratory model, and this substitution represents a limitation of our study. We also acknowledge that generating a GIV mutant lacking the GIV-VILP gene would provide a more definitive tool to elucidate its function in viral pathogenesis. While we attempted to construct a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) clone of GIV to enable targeted gene deletions, the large genome size and the presence of extensive internal repeats hindered successful generation of a stable BAC clone by the time of submission. In our transcriptomic analyses, although we used grouper-derived cells to preserve natural host context, the limited quality of the E. coioides genome assembly posed constraints on the RNA-seq data interpretation. The de novo analysis was affected by incomplete annotation, and some transcripts remained unidentified or only partially characterized, potentially reducing the resolution and completeness of our transcript-level conclusions. In addition, while our structural modeling of the GIV-scVILP-IGF1R interaction has high confidence scores, a high-resolution cryo-EM or crystallographic structure would more definitively validate our predictions. Finally, the synthetic peptide used in our functional assays was designed based on our initial prediction based on insulin and IGF-1 alignments and may not fully recapitulate the entire native GIV-VILP sequence or its conformational dynamics in vivo.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Cells and viruses

Grouper Kidney (GK) cells were maintained in Leibovitz-15 medium (L-15) containing 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (ThermoFisher #A5256801), 1% L-Glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich #G3126), and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich #P4333) at 28°C without CO2.

AB.9 cells were maintained in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS, 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin and 1% L-Glutamine. Cells were maintained in 75 mL flasks and incubated at 28°C with 5% CO2.

ZSSJ cells were maintained in L-15 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin. Cells were maintained in75 mL flasks at 28°C without CO2.

Murine embryonic fibroblasts, obtained from IGF1R knockout mice and stably transfected with either IR-B (IR-B cells) or IGF1R (R+ cells), were generously provided by A. Belfiore (Catanzaro, Italy) and R. Baserga (Philadelphia, PA). These cells were cultured according to the previously established protocol.29

Grouper Iridovirus (GIV) viral stocks were prepared by infecting GK cells until the cellular monolayer was disrupted. Cells and viruses were collected by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C. The cell pellet containing the virus was then resuspended in culture medium and ultrasonicated on ice (5 rounds of 1-min intervals). Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 4,000 × g for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was layered onto a 35% sucrose cushion and centrifuged at 210,000 × g for 1 h at 4°C. The resulting pellet, resuspended in TN buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.4], 150 mM NaCl), was overlaid on 30%, 40%, 50%, and 60% (w/v) sucrose gradients and centrifuged at 210,000 × g for 1 h at 4°C. The lowest band (50% sucrose) was individually aspirated and spun down at 100,000 × g in PBS solution. Aliquots of pure GIV virions are then stored at −80°C.

Zebrafish animals

Male and female adult zebrafish of 3 months old were maintained and bred in the Boston College Animal Care Facility. Differences between sexes at this stage of development are complex, leading to a sex-heterogeneous population in this study. All the animal experiments were conducted following the regulations and ethics guidelines of the National Institute of Health and were approved by the IACUC of Boston College (Protocol No.#2022–005 and 2025–011). The fishes were maintained in a 12-h dark/light.

METHOD DETAILS

Peptide alignment and peptide structure prediction

Identified Viral Insulins-like Peptides used in this study (GIV-VILP) were aligned with corresponding Insulin and IGF-1 sequences from different organisms of interest, such as human (Homo sapiens), zebrafish (Danio rerio) and grouper (Epinephelus coioides) using ClustalW interface. We annotated the A and B chains by identifying conserved cysteine residues, which typically define disulfide-bonding domains. We expanded from these sites to generate domains of comparable length to human insulin, specifically 30 amino acids for the B-chain and 21 for the A-chain. The C-peptide region was inferred to lie between these domains, drawing on the vertebrate insulin structure as a reference.

Structural modeling

We used MODELLERv10.5 to generate the tertiary structures of GIV-dcVILP and Zebrafish insulin based on the template of the human Insulin structure (PDB: 6PXV), and GIV-scVILP and Zebrafish IGF-1 based on the template of the human IGF-1 structure (PDB: 6PYH). Additionally, we modeled truncated Zebrafish IR (μIR) and truncated Zebrafish IGF1R (μIGF1R) using the human IR (PDB: 6PXV) and the human IGF1R (PDB: 6PYH), respectively, as templates. Specifically, μIR was composed of the L1 domain (residues 1–155) and the αCT peptide (residues 691 to 717), and the μIGF1R was composed of the L1 domain (residues 1–155) and the αCT peptide (residues 691 to 705). We first modeled any missing residues (e.g., residues 38 through 40 in IGF-1) in the templates using the MODELLER software before constructing the tertiary structures of GIV-VILPs, Insulin, and IGF-1. We preserved the disulfide bonds across all peptide structures and maintained the double-chain configuration of peptides wherever necessary during model building. We generated 200 models of each peptide and the L1 domain, and selected the best model based on the lowest discrete optimized protein energy (DOPE) score. The peptide models were also scored according to the reliability score referred to as GA341 score ranging between 0 and 1. According to the MODELLER protocols, a model is considered reliable if the GA341 score is greater than 0.6. The GA341 score of each model peptide was as follows: GIV-scVILP had a score of 1.00, of GIV-dcVILP had a score of 0.74, of Zebrafish Ins had a score of 0.93, and of Zebrafish IGF1 had a score of 1.00 which were all greater than 0.6 providing confidence that the modeled structures were reliable. We superimposed the L1 domain and the αCT peptide from the Zebrafish IR and IGF1R on the human truncated IR (PDB: 6PXV) and IGF1R (PDB: 6PYH), respectively, and further superimposed peptide ligands (VILPs and Zebrafish Insulin/IGF-1) on the human Insulin/IGF-1 initially present in the human IR/IGF1R structures to obtain a complexed structure of each ligand using the PyMOL and VMD software tools.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

RNA from GK or AB.9 cells was purified using the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo #R2050) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using 2μg of RNA with the Maxima First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (ThermoFisher #K1641), following the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (ThermoFisher #4309155) and specific oligonucleotides for each targeted transcript (Table S5). Samples were amplified on the QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (ThermoFisher #A28567). Quality control and visualization were performed using the associated QuantStudio Design & Analysis Software. RT-qPCR quantifications were conducted using the 2−ΔΔCT method, employing the relative Ct abundance of cellular and viral transcripts normalized to basal level of infection.

Plasmids

All plasmid constructions were derived from original plasmid pcDNA3.1+. Gene cloning was accomplished through enzymatic restriction, followed by ligation and transformation processes using One Shot TOP10 Electrocomp E. coli (Invitrogen #C4040–50). Complete cloned plasmid sequences and accurate alignments were analyzed by Sanger sequencing performed by the Azenta Life Science platform. The sequences of oligonucleotides used for plasmid construction are listed in Table S5.

Transfection and immunostaining

Cells were seeded in P12 plates with coverslips previously placed at the bottom of each well. Plasmidic transfections with pcDNA3.1+-GIV32-eGFP were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (ThermoFisher #L3000001) following manufacturer’s instructions. After 24 h post-transfection, cells were fixed with 4% Paraformaldehyde (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, #sc-281692) and stored at 4°C until further processing. Immunostaining of the fixed samples was performed at room temperature. Firstly, samples were permeabilized and blocked in PBS-BSA (0.5%)-Triton (0.1%) for 30 min. After the blocking step, 58K Golgi Protein Antibody (Novus Biological #NB600–412SS) was diluted to 1/200 in PBS-BSA (0.5%)-Triton (0.1%) before applying to samples for 1 h. Samples were washed 3 times with PBS-Triton (0.1%). The secondary antibody Alexa Fluor 594 anti-rabbit IgG was diluted to 1/1000 in PBS-BSA (0.5%)-Triton (0.1%) before applying to samples for 1 h. Nuclear staining was performed using Hoechst (Cayman #16756) diluted to 1/2000 in PBS-BSA (0.5%)-Triton (0.1%) for 20 min. Samples were washed 3 times with PBS-Triton (0.1%). Coverslips were mounted on slides using Vectashield Antifade Mounting Medium (Vector #H-1000–10) and stored at −20°C until further acquisitions. Microscope observations were performed using a Zeiss Axio Imager 2.

Cellular colocalization analysis

Colocalization of GIV-VILP-eGFP with the Golgi apparatus was analyzed using ImageJ software following immunolabeling of the Golgi apparatus. Images were split into individual channels, and regions of interest (ROIs) were selected to isolate the cells under analysis. The Coloc2 plugin was then applied to the two channels to calculate the Pearson correlation coefficient that is a continuous scoring method. Data were collected from 30 images and plotted to illustrate the variation in Pearson’s correlation scores.

Structural and secreted GIV protein analysis

To determine the structural protein composition of GIV, GIV particles were purified as previously described55 and layered onto an additional sucrose gradient consisting of 30%, 40%, 50%, and 60% (w/v) sucrose at 210,000 × g to minimize interference from nonstructural proteins. The purified particles were loaded on a on a 15% SDS-PAGE for 10 min at 120V, followed by Coomassie blue staining to visualize the entire protein content. The whole gel was then treated by trypsin digestion followed by peptide identification by LC/MS at the Harvard Taplin Core Facility, resulting peptides were compared to the GIV proteome for identification. To determine secreted protein composition of GIV, GK cells were infected by GIV for 24h at MOI 0.1. Supernatant was harvested and filtrated by 0.1μM filter. Proteins were concentrated with Centrifugal Filter Unit 3KDa (Millipore # UFC5003) before analysis by peptide identification by LC/MS. Samples were subjected to a modified in-gel trypsin digestion procedure by rehydration of sample with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution containing 12.5 ng/μL modified sequencing-grade trypsin and incubated overnight at 37°C. Peptides were extracted by removing the ammonium bicarbonate solution, followed by a wash with 50% acetonitrile and 1% formic acid. Extracts were dried in a speed-vac for an hour and stored at 4°C until analysis. On the day of analysis, samples were reconstituted in HPLC solvent (2.5% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid). A nano-scale reverse-phase HPLC capillary column was prepared and equilibrated. Samples were loaded via an auto sampler, and peptides were eluted with increasing concentrations of solvent (97.5% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid). Eluted peptides underwent electrospray ionization and were analyzed using a Velos Orbitrap Pro ion-trap mass spectrometer. Peptide sequences and protein identities were determined using Sequest software, with data filtered to achieve a 1–2% peptide false discovery rate.

Peptide synthesis

Viral insulin-like peptides were synthesized via Fmoc solid phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) utilizing a commercial automated peptide synthesizer (Symphony X, Gyros Protein Technologies). All technical details related to VILPs synthesis were previously described.5

Receptor phosphorylation and downstream phosphorylation signaling

For receptor phosphorylation and downstream signaling experiments, GK cells were used to explore signaling properties of ligands via specific receptors. Cells were seeded into 12-well plates (100K cells per well) in 1mL of Complete Media (described above) and grown overnight. Cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and starved in serum-free media for 4 h. After the starvation, cells were washed with serum-free media and incubated with ligand diluted in serum-free media (0, 1, 10, 100 nM) at 28°C 15 min. The reaction was terminated by washing the cells with ice-cold PBS followed by snap freezing in liquid nitrogen.

Samples were stored at −80°C before further analysis.

Western blotting

Cellular samples analyzed by Western blot, were lysed using 50μL of RadioImmunoPrecipitation Assay (RIPA) buffer (Millipore #20–188) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Selleck #B15001 & B14001). Cells on plates were incubated in RIPA buffer on ice for 15 min, sonicated in a water bath sonicator for 1 min and then transferred to 1.5mL Eppendorf tubes. The lysates were centrifuged (13,000 g, 5 min, 4°C) and supernatant was transferred to 1.5mL Eppendorf tubes. Quantification of each sample’s protein concentration were performed using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFischer #23225).

Total of 6μg of protein is mixed with 6X Laemmli sample buffer (62.5 mM Tris, 2% SDS, 10% glycerol, 0.01% bromophenol blue, 0.1M DTT, pH = 6.8). Samples were loaded on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel. Proteins were transferred on PVDF Transfer Membranes, 0.45μm (ThermoFischer #88585) using the Trans-Blot SD Semi-Dry Transfer Cell (Bio-Rad #1703940). The membrane is then saturated in 5% BSA (GoldBio #A-420) in TBS-Tween and then probed overnight at 4°C with primaries antibodies. Membrane is then washed 3 times 5 min with TBS-Tween. Secondary detection was performed with the Horseradish peroxidase conjugated goat anti-rabbit (ABclonal #AS014) diluted at 1:10,000 in TBS-Tween-BSA 1%. Detection is made using the SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (ThermoFisher #34580). Acquisitions were made by exposition of autoradiography films (USA Scientific #19683810). Quantification of Western-Blot bands were made through the ImageLab software (BioRad).

Replication assay and plaque assay

Viral replication assays were performed at 12 or 24 h posts infection. Inhibitors were applied on cells after a 1h inoculation period to avoid interference with viral entry. Inhibitors used in this study are listed here: Picropodophyllin-PPP- (SelleckChem #S7668), IR-specific peptide antagonist S961 (Novo-Nordisk #NNC0069–0961), BMS-754807 (SelleckChem #S1124), MK-2206 (SelleckChem #S1078). Cells and supernatant were harvested followed by a snap freeze of the samples. Following the assay, viral loads were quantified by plaque assay. Briefly, samples were thawed and frozen 3 times to release all intra-cellular virions. Samples were then diluted in serial dilutions. The diluted samples were then inoculated to a confluent monolayer of GK cells placed in P12 plaques for 1 h. Following this incubation, L-15 Media supplemented with 10% Carboxymethylcellulose (Sigma #4888) was added to each well (1mL) to reduce viral dispersion during the replication. Plaques were quantified 5 days after incubation at 28°C.

Cell proliferation analysis

GK cells were seeded at a density of 100,000 cells per well in P24 plates. Following a 16-h incubation period, cells were synchronized by switching to serum-free media for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, cells were stimulated with the indicated ligands for 4 h, followed by a 4-h pulse with 10 nM EdU to label proliferating cells. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained for EdU according to the manufacturer’s instructions (BaseClick #BCK-EdU555IM100). Microscopic images were acquired using a Zeiss Axio Imager 2 and analyzed with ImageJ software.

RNA-sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA were extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen #74134) from GK cells that were serum-starved for 12 h, followed by 6 h of stimulation with respective ligands (100 nM) or infection with GIV (MOI 1) for 18 h. The RNA samples were submitted to the YCGA (Yale Center for Genome Analysis) for quality testing. Subsequently, cDNA libraries were prepared using the KAPA C HyperPrep Kit (Roche) and sequenced on the NovaSeq X (Illumina) with a read depth of 20 million reads. Transcriptomic datasets generated from the sequencing were trimmed for low quality reads and adaptor contamination was removed using TrimGalore.56 Trimmed reads were assembled using Trinity de novo assembly tool (version v2.15.1).57 Coding regions for the assembly were identified using TransDecoder.58 The identified 65,169 contigs were annotated to identify known genes using Blast2Go.59 Counts of reads mapping to the assembled cds in each sample were estimated using the Trinity tool (align_and_estimate_abundance.pl.) These read counts were used to estimate the differential transcript expression using DESeq2.60 All differentially expressed transcripts included in the following analysis were subject to a false discovery rate (FDR) of ≤0.01 and a fold change (FC) of ≥1.5. Gene Ontology networks were constructed by uploading the differentially expressed transcripts to the ShinyGO 0.80 software,61 utilizing the Danio rerio STRING.7955 reference database. Venn Diagrams where initially generated using Venny web base software.62

Zebrafish infection

Infections were performed by orally administered 107 pfu GIV virus through the mouth and gills following anesthesia with 150 mg/L of Tricane-S (Sigma-Aldrich #T0377). Post-inoculation recovery was monitored, and the fish were maintained in the tanks for the study duration. Monitoring of infection was performed on a 4 days period following inoculation of the animals. Organs were collected and RNA were extracted on GIV-potential susceptible organs, pooling the kidney, liver, and spleen using the Qiagen RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (#74134) and cDNA synthesized using Maxima First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (ThermoFisher #K1641). Viral transcript expression was assessed in infected animals using RT-qPCR was performed using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (ThermoFisher #4309155) and specific oligonucleotides for each targeted transcript (Table S5). RT-qPCR quantifications were conducted using the 2−ΔΔCT method, employing the relative Ct abundance of cellular and viral transcripts. The presence of infectious GIV particles was evaluated four days after post-infection by filtering at 0.2μM lysate tissues and applying them to GK cell monolayers. Cytopathic effects of GK cells were monitored for 72 h, and GIV presence was confirmed by PCR targeting the MCP gene.
