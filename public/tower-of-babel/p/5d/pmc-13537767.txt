Discovery of bioactive peptides from Arbacia lixula as dipeptidyl peptidase-IV inhibitors: An in silico approach
Authors: Zuhrah Taufiqa, Benny Alexander Maisa, Kevin Nathaniel Cuandra, Enrico Franditho Sihombing, Muhammad Fathu Ridho, Sheryl Pricilla Daniela Elizabeth Silalahi, Vina Sari Nugrahaning Widi, Afifah Rahma Adila, Aurellia Zahra Quinneala, Aryas Hakim Indrajaya, Reka Febriani, Zahra Roidah Amalia Hasna, Sari Rahmadani, Erra Kurniasari, Mazaya Mansu Lubis, Amira Puji Hastuti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDipeptidyl peptidase-IV (DPP-IV) inhibition is a validated therapeutic mechanism for type 2 diabetes mellitus. Marine-derived bioactive peptides offer a diverse array of potential inhibitors but often exhibit pharmacokinetic limitations that impede clinical translation. This study aimed to identify and evaluate candidate peptides from Arbacia lixula as potential DPP-IV inhibitors using an integrated in silico approach. A total of twenty peptides were evaluated in silico for absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) profiles using SwissADME, pkCSM, and ProTox. Molecular docking against DPP-IV was performed in MOE v2022.02 with linagliptin as the reference ligand; docking poses were evaluated by predicted binding energy and root mean square deviation (RMSD). Top-ranked peptide–DPP-IV complexes were subjected to 50 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations in YASARA Dynamics v4.3.13 to assess conformational stability. ADMET predictions showed low gastrointestinal absorption in most peptides, whereas Peptides 17, 18, and 20 showed high predicted absorption. No target-organ or endpoint-specific toxicity was predicted. Peptides 7, 15, and 18 were prioritized based on favorable docking scores, RMSD <2.0 Å, and relevant DPP-IV residue interactions. Docking scores were interpreted cautiously, not as direct quantitative comparisons with linagliptin. During 50 ns MD simulations, RMSD, root mean square fluctuation, radius of gyration, and solvent-accessible surface area profiles supported the stability, compactness, and dynamic consistency of selected peptide–DPP-IV complexes. Peptides 7, 15, and 18 showed favorable in silico predicting binding, relevant DPP-IV interactions, and acceptable preliminary safety profiles as potential DPP-IV inhibitory candidates. These findings should be interpreted as computational leads that require further in vitro and in vivo validation.

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a multifactorial polygenic metabolic disorder arising from the interplay between genetic predisposition and environmental factors, characterized by peripheral insulin resistance and progressive pancreatic β-cell dysfunction.[1] A substantial proportion of individuals with T2DM remain undiagnosed for prolonged periods due to the asymptomatic nature of the prediabetic phase.[2] Sustained hyperglycemia subsequently promotes the development of chronic multisystem complications, leading to functional impairment and reduced quality of life.[3] Consequently, the attainment of near-normoglycemic control constitutes the principal objective of diabetes management, most commonly achieved through pharmacological intervention with hypoglycemic agents.

Current antidiabetic therapies target multiple metabolic pathways, including stimulation of insulin secretion, inhibition of intestinal glucose absorption, improvement of insulin sensitivity, suppression of renal glucose reabsorption, and modulation of the incretin system.[4] Available drug classes encompass sulfonylureas, glinides, α-glucosidase inhibitors, biguanides, thiazolidinediones, sodium–glucose cotransporter inhibitors, and dipeptidyl peptidase IV (DPP-IV) inhibitors.[45] Among these, incretin-based therapies, particularly DPP-IV inhibitors, have gained prominence due to glucose-dependent enhancement of insulin secretion and suppression of glucagon release, thereby minimizing the risk of hypoglycemia.[6] Nevertheless, many antidiabetic agents are associated with adverse effects such as gastrointestinal disturbances, urinary tract infections, weight gain, and metabolic complications, which may limit long-term adherence and therapeutic outcomes.[6] These constraints emphasize the necessity for the development of safer and more efficacious incretin-oriented agents.

Peptide-based therapeutics have garnered increasing interest due to high target specificity, favorable safety profiles, and enhanced biological activity.[7] The growing number of peptide drugs approved by the United States Food and Drug Administration underscores clinical relevance.[8] Marine organisms represent a rich yet underexplored source of bioactive peptides, including those derived from the sea urchin Arbacia lixula, which exhibit potential antidiabetic properties.[9] Accordingly, this study aims to evaluate A. lixula-derived peptides as prospective DPP-IV inhibitors through comprehensive in silico analysis, thereby providing a rational computational basis for subsequent experimental and translational research.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Peptide and target protein preparation

Peptide sequences derived from A. lixula were obtained from a previously reported peptidomic dataset.[10] Three-dimensional peptide structures were generated using UCSF Chimera (v1.17.1). Nine approved incretin-based agents were employed as reference DPP-IV inhibitors. The crystallographic structure of DPP-IV (PDB ID: 3G0B) was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. All peptide, ligand, and protein structures were prepared in MOE 2022.02 through protonation state assignment, charge correction, artifact removal, and energy minimization using a uniform RMS gradient criterion (0.001 kcal/mol/Å2).[1112]

Peptide physicochemical property prediction

The physicochemical properties of A. lixula-derived peptides were predicted using the ToxinPred web server (accessed May 22, 2026). The peptide sequences were submitted in FASTA format, and the predicted parameters included molecular weight, net charge, hydrophobicity, and isoelectric point (pI).

Absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion prediction

In silico evaluation of absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) properties of A. lixula–derived peptides was performed using the pkCSM and SwissADME web server (accessed February 2, 2025).[13] Pharmacokinetic parameters included gastrointestinal absorption, P-glycoprotein substrate prediction, and blood–brain barrier permeability to characterize absorption and distribution. Potential metabolic interactions were assessed through inhibition predictions of major cytochrome P450 isoenzymes (CYP1A2, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4). Aqueous solubility (LogS) and plasma clearance were additionally analyzed to estimate solubility and systemic elimination.

Toxicity assessment

Toxicological profiles were predicted using ProTox-3.0 (accessed February 22, 2025). Acute oral toxicity was estimated based on predicted LD50 values and classified according to established toxicity categories.[14] Further safety endpoints included hepatotoxicity, carcinogenicity, immunotoxicity, mutagenicity, and cytotoxicity, enabling an integrated in silico toxicity risk evaluation.

Molecular docking

Docking simulations were performed in MOE 2022.02, and the DPP-IV active site was determined using the Site Finder feature. Docking results were evaluated based on predicted binding energy, interaction patterns, and root mean square deviation (RMSD) values. In the docking context, RMSD was used to assess the consistency and reliability of the predicted binding pose relative to the reference or best-ranked pose within the active site. An RMSD value below 2.0 Å was applied as a commonly accepted threshold indicating that the predicted ligand orientation remained within an acceptable positional deviation and could be considered a reproducible docking pose. Therefore, peptide prioritization was not based solely on predicting binding energy, but on a combined criterion consisting of favorable predicted docking score, RMSD <2.0 Å, and relevant interactions with DPP-IV active-site residues. Interaction patterns were further analyzed through two-and three-dimensional visualizations.[1112]

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using YASARA Dynamics v4.3.13 under physiological conditions (pH 7.4, 310 K) for 50 ns. The stability and dynamic behavior of the peptide–DPP-IV complexes were evaluated using several trajectory-based parameters, including RMSD, radius of gyration (Rg), solvent-accessible surface area (SASA), and root mean square fluctuation (RMSF).[1112]

Absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion profile

The ADME evaluation demonstrated that most peptides exhibited low gastrointestinal absorption, indicating limited oral bioavailability [Table 2]. None of the peptides were predicted to be blood–brain barrier permeant, suggesting minimal central nervous system exposure. Several peptides (Peptides 3, 10, 12, 16, and 19) were identified as P-glycoprotein substrates, while the remaining compounds were not. All peptides showed no inhibitory activity against major cytochrome P450 isoforms (CYP1A2, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, and CYP3A4). Predicted plasma clearance values varied across peptides, ranging from 1.353 to 4.656, indicating heterogeneity in systemic elimination profiles.

Molecular docking against dipeptidyl peptidase-IV

Molecular docking showed that all candidate peptides exhibited stronger predicted binding affinities than linagliptin (−6.96 kcal/mol), which was selected as the reference DPP-IV inhibitor based on its superior affinity among the control compounds (−5.87 to −6.96 kcal/mol). This cutoff was used to reduce the risk of selecting peptides with apparently strong predicted binding energies but unstable or inconsistent docking orientations. Thus, peptides with RMSD values above 2.0 Å were not prioritized, even when their predicted docking scores were favorable, because higher RMSD values may indicate greater positional deviation of the predicted pose within the binding pocket. Based on this criterion, only Peptides 7, 15, and 18 were selected for further evaluation. Although other peptides exhibited comparable or even higher predicted docking scores, they were not advanced because their RMSD values did not meet the selection cutoff. Peptide 7 exhibited a predicted docking score of − 10.71 kcal/mol with an RMSD of 1.69 Å. Among the selected candidates, Peptide 15 achieved the most favorable predicted binding energy (−11.31 kcal/mol; RMSD 1.57 Å), while Peptide 18 showed a comparable interaction profile with a predicted binding energy of −10.70 kcal/mol and an RMSD of 1.63 Å [Table 4].

Molecular dynamics simulation

MD simulation of DPP-IV complexes with linagliptin, Peptide 7, Peptide 15, and Peptide 18 was evaluated over 50 ns using RMSD, RMSF, Rg, and SASA analyses [Figure 2]. The RMSD profiles showed an initial increase in the early simulation phase, followed by relatively stable trajectories throughout the remainder of the simulation period [Figure 2a]. Linagliptin exhibited the lowest RMSD values compared with the peptide complexes, suggesting a more stable reference conformation. Among the peptide complexes, Peptide 7 showed relatively stable RMSD behavior, whereas Peptide 15 and Peptide 18 displayed slightly higher fluctuations, particularly after approximately 30 ns. Nevertheless, all peptide–DPP-IV complexes maintained RMSD values within an acceptable range, indicating preserved complex stability during the simulation.

Molecular dynamics profiles of Dipeptidyl peptidase-IV complexes with linagliptin and selected peptides over 50 ns, including (a) root mean square deviation, (b) root mean square fluctuation, (c) radius of gyration, and (d) solvent-accessible surface area analyses

The RMSF analysis revealed residue-level flexibility across the DPP-IV structure [Figure 2b]. Most residues showed relatively low fluctuations, whereas several localized regions exhibited higher RMSF values, particularly around the mid-residue region, suggesting flexible loop regions rather than global structural destabilization. The Rg profiles remained relatively constant throughout the 50 ns simulation, indicating that the overall compactness of the DPP-IV complexes was maintained [Figure 2c]. Similarly, SASA values fluctuated within a narrow range, suggesting no major changes in solvent exposure or unfolding tendency during simulation [Figure 2d].

DISCUSSION

Early-stage evaluation of pharmacokinetics and toxicity is critical for identifying viable drug candidates, particularly peptide-based compounds, which often exhibit limited oral bioavailability. The evaluated peptides showed variation in molecular weight, net charge, hydrophobicity, and pI, indicating diverse biochemical characteristics that may influence solubility, interaction behavior, and pharmacokinetic performance. In silico ADME analysis demonstrated that most A. lixula-derived peptides exhibited low predicted gastrointestinal absorption, except Peptides 17, 18, and 20, which showed high predicted absorption. All peptides were predicted to be nonpermeant across the blood–brain barrier, suggesting limited central nervous system exposure. In silico toxicity prediction did not indicate hepatotoxicity, carcinogenicity, immunotoxicity, mutagenicity, or cytotoxicity, suggesting an acceptable predicted safety profile that warrants further experimental validation.

The low predicted gastrointestinal absorption of the candidate peptides represents an important translational constraint and should be interpreted in the context of well-established barriers to oral peptide delivery. In the gastrointestinal tract, peptides are exposed to acidic conditions and a broad range of endo- and exopeptidases, including brush border peptidases, which can hydrolyze peptide bonds, reducing structural integrity before absorption. In parallel, unfavorable physicochemical properties, such as limited membrane permeability and high susceptibility to presystemic loss, further limit efficient intestinal uptake. Accordingly, low in silico oral absorption does not undermine the biological interest of the candidates, but it does indicate that oral administration is unlikely to be straightforward without additional delivery optimization.[15]

This limitation should be integrated into the overall interpretation, as the identified peptides are more appropriately considered computational leads rather than immediately developable oral drug candidates. Enhancement of systemic exposure typically requires formulation-based strategies, including chemical modification, enzyme inhibition, permeation enhancers, or encapsulation systems. Although oral peptide delivery has been achieved in selected cases using specialized technologies, alternative routes, such as parenteral or mucosal delivery, may offer more feasible translational approaches. Therefore, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and supportive of further experimental validation, rather than as directly applicable to therapy.[1516]

Molecular docking analysis enabled prioritization of peptides based on predicted binding energy and conformational stability within the DPP-IV catalytic site. However, peptide predicted docking scores should not be interpreted as directly equivalent to those of small-molecule inhibitors such as linagliptin, because peptides generally differ in molecular size, conformational flexibility, and interaction complexity. Therefore, linagliptin was used primarily as a reference inhibitor to contextualize binding-site orientation and key residue interactions rather than to establish a direct quantitative comparison of binding strength. Although all peptides exhibited more favorable predicted binding energies than linagliptin, only Peptides 7, 15, and 18 satisfied the RMSD criterion below 2.0 Å, indicating stable binding conformations.[111217] These peptides demonstrated strong predicted binding affinities ranging from −10.70 to −11.31 kcal/mol.

Interaction analysis revealed that Peptide 7 formed diverse acidic, basic, and hydrophobic contacts, whereas Peptide 15 predominantly engaged basic and polar residues. Peptide 18 shared key interactions with Arg125, Arg358, and Phe357, consistent with the binding mode of linagliptin. Overlapping interactions at conserved residues, such as Arg356 and Phe357, support functional similarity to known DPP-IV inhibitors and align with previous structure-based studies.[17]

MD simulations over 50 ns supported the structural stability of the peptide–DPP-IV complexes. RMSD analysis showed an initial conformational adjustment followed by relatively stable trajectories for all systems. Linagliptin, included as a reference inhibitor, exhibited lower fluctuation, while peptides 7, 15, and 18 maintained stable deviations throughout the simulation. RMSF analysis indicated that residue-level flexibility was mainly restricted to localized regions, suggesting that peptide binding did not cause broad structural destabilization. The relatively stable Rg values reflected the preservation of complex compactness, while consistent SASA profiles indicated no major alteration in solvent exposure during the simulation. Collectively, the RMSD, RMSF, Rg, and SASA results reinforce the docking findings and support MD simulation as a complementary approach for evaluating the dynamic stability of peptide–DPP-IV complexes.[1819]

Despite these promising findings, the inherent limitations of in silico approaches must be acknowledged. Simplified assumptions, rigid receptor models, and reliance on empirical scoring functions may not fully capture induced-fit effects, solvent interactions, or biological complexity.[2021] Accordingly, the present results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Overall, the integrated computational evidence provides a strong rationale for advancing Peptides 7, 15, and 18 to subsequent experimental stages in antidiabetic peptide development.

CONCLUSION

Peptides 7, 15, and 18 from A. lixula demonstrated favorable predicted binding affinities, interaction stability, and acceptable predicted toxicity profiles in computational analyses. Overall, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and provide a computational basis for future biochemical and cellular validation of DPP-IV inhibitory activity and selectivity.
