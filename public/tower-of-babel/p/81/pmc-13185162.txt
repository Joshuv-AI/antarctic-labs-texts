Self-stigma in individuals with substance use disorders and the relationship with meaning in life: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Jinjing Zhao, Rufang Wang, Yongqiu Li, Xiao Zang, Yufang Gao, Binbin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the latent profiles of self-stigma and their relationship with meaning in life among individuals with substance use disorders(SUDs).MethodsA total of 1001 participants were recruited from six drug rehabilitation centers in Sichuan Province between July and August 2025 and completed the self-stigma Scale for Drug Addicts (SSSDA) and the Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ). Latent profile analysis (LPA) was used to identify latent profiles of self-stigma. Multinomial logistic regression was employed to analyze influencing factors, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare differences in meaning in life across the different profiles.ResultsThe self-stigma of individuals with SUDs can be categorized into four latent profiles: the “stigma–resistant profile”(10.0%), “moderate stigma–concealment profile”(46.3%), “internalized stigma profile”(19.5%), and “low internalization–adaptation profile”(24.3%). Among these, the “moderate stigma–concealment profile”, “internalized stigma profile”, and “low internalization–adaptation profile” represent categories with higher levels of self-stigma. Risk factors associated with these profiles include male sex, low income, a history of being left-behind children, low social support, multiple rehabilitation attempts, as well as mental illness or HIV infection. Statistically significant differences were found among the four profiles in the total score of meaning in life and its sub-dimensions—presence of meaning and search for meaning (p < 0.001). The “stigma-resistant profile” presented the highest level of MIL, whereas the “internalized stigma profile” presented the lowest level.ConclusionSignificant heterogeneity exists in self-stigma among individuals with substance use disorders (SUDs), and the level of self-stigma is significantly negatively correlated with MIL.Keywords: Self-stigma, Meaning in life, Individuals with SUDs, Latent profile analysis

Introduction

The global spread of substances remains a significant challenge, with production in source regions continuing to rise and the scope of non-medical substance use expanding worldwide [1]. Concurrently, China's efforts to address substance use have achieved notable results, with a continuous decrease in the number of people using traditional and new synthetic substances. However, the types of substances used in China have undergone structural changes in recent years, with a notable increase in the use of pharmaceutical and non-scheduled addictive substances [2]. Thus, substance rehabilitation remains a crucial issue affecting social stability. Currently, compulsory isolated drug rehabilitation remains one of the special legal measures for addressing substance use in China [3]. While effective for addressing acute withdrawal symptoms [4], this model poses considerable challenges to psychological recovery. It effectively supports individuals with substance use disorders (SUDs) in avoiding substance use, yet it may also expose them to the risk of stigma-related labeling, thereby leading to social exclusion and discrimination [5]. Goffman (1963) introduced the concept of stigma, defining it as a social attribute that discredits an individual [6]. Corrigan further developed this concept, proposing self-stigma and establishing a corresponding theoretical framework. This theory posits that self-stigma constitutes a progressive process through which stigmatized individuals move from perceiving social discrimination to ultimately internalizing negative labels into their self-concept [7]. Relevant studies have found that during their reintegration into society, individuals with SUDs experience or perceive varying degrees of external discrimination and stigma, which they then internalize as self-stigma [8]. Stigmatized groups often perceive themselves as incompetent, shameful, and unworthy of respect. This self-negation significantly reduces self-esteem and self-efficacy, triggers emotional problems such as anxiety and depression, and prompts social avoidance and refusal to seek help [9], greatly increasing the risk of relapse [10]. However, according to Corrigan’s Situational Model of the Personal Response to Mental Illness Stigma, the response types of self-stigma are not unitary but jointly determined by situationally activated cognitions and individual perceptions, ultimately giving rise to three distinct response types of self-stigma: the self-devaluation type, the righteous anger-resistance type, and the relative indifference type [11]. This classification provides a core framework for understanding the heterogeneity of self-stigma and lays the theoretical foundation for the present study to investigate the types of self-stigma among individuals with SUDs. Meaning in life (MIL) is the subjective experience through which individuals perceive and discover the unique purpose, value, and significance of their lives [12], and it can be divided into two dimensions: the presence of meaning and the search for meaning [13]. It can alter an individual’s appraisal of adverse life events, mitigate the negative impacts of psychological distress and challenges, and help them establish a healthier psychological state [14]. In the field of substance addiction, MIL has been preliminarily confirmed as a key protective factor. For example, Li et al.(2023) reported that MIL negatively predicts depressive symptoms and alleviates negative emotions among individuals with SUDs [15]. Importantly, some studies have shown that MIL is closely related to perceived stigma [16], and that the disruption of MIL reduces an individual’s sense of self-worth, making them more susceptible to forming irrational self-stigmatizing cognitions [17]. Based on this, we hypothesize that there may be a potential association between the self-stigma profiles of individuals with SUDs and their level of MIL.

With respect to the self-stigma variable, previous research has focused on a variable-centered perspective, treating the sample as a homogeneous group and exploring the overall relationship between self-stigma and related variables [18, 19]. However, significant differences exist among individuals in terms of the degree and manifestation of stigma internalization. This heterogeneity may be obscured by traditional mean comparisons. Latent profile analysis (LPA), as a person-centered approach, can identify intrinsic, qualitative subtypes on the basis of individuals' response patterns across various dimensions of self-stigma, thereby providing a more refined depiction of the characteristics of different subgroups. A recent study by Li et al. (2025) has confirmed the efficacy of this approach in revealing psychological heterogeneity among individuals with SUDs; utilizing LPA, they identified distinct types of self-acceptance within this population and revealed a close association with mental health [20]. This provides methodological reference and support for the present study to employ a similar approach in exploring the latent structure of self-stigma. Currently, research on the association between MIL and self-stigma remains scarce both domestically and internationally, particularly within the field of SUDs. Therefore, building on Corrigan's theory of self-stigma, this study adopts latent profile analysis (LPA) as an exploratory, data-driven technique. It aims to: (a) reveal the latent profile structure of self-stigma among individuals with SUDs in Sichuan Province; (b) explore the influencing factors of different self-stigma profiles; and (c) examine differences in MIL across these profiles.

Methods

Design

This study was a cross-sectional study conducted from July to August 2025. Prior to data collection, all participants were informed of the research purposes and were assured of the confidentiality of their responses. After signing the informed consent form, the participants completed the Self-Stigma Scale for Drug Addicts (SSSDA) and the Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ). Upon completion, all the participants were thanked for their time and received souvenirs and gifts.

Participants

Cluster sampling was used to recruit participants. A total of 1,015 participants with SUDs were recruited for this survey from six compulsory isolated drug rehabilitation centers in Sichuan Province. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) completion of the physiological detoxification phase without severe withdrawal symptoms, (b) length of stay in the center ≥ 3 months, (c) age 16 years or older, (d) absence of severe cognitive impairment (e.g., dementia, severe intellectual disability), (e) education level of primary school or higher. A total of 1015 questionnaires were distributed and collected. After excluding 14 invalid questionnaires due to patterned responses, omissions, or errors, 1001 valid questionnaires remained, yielding an effective response rate of 98.62%.

The survey utilized anonymous surveys and provided detailed instructions to participants. This study strictly adhered to the principle of voluntariness; all participants were informed of the purpose of the survey and signed informed consent forms.

Measures

Sociodemographic questionnaire

The sociodemographic questionnaire includes gender, age, education level, occupation, marital status, place of residence, left-behind child history, monthly income, cumulative duration of substance use, number of rehabilitation attempts, type of substance used, history of mental illness, HIV status, and frequency of contact with family members. Among these, left-behind child history refers to the experience where an individual, under the age of 16, did not live with their parents because both or one of the parents migrated for long-term (6 consecutive months or more) work, business, or other purposes, and was mainly cared for by non-parental relatives (e.g., grandparents, other relatives) or self-cared. Types of substance use include traditional substances (heroin, cocaine, marijuana, etc.), new synthetic substances (methamphetamine, magu, ecstasy, ketamine, etc.), and psychoactive substances (etomidate, nitrous oxide, Rush-type inhalants, dextromethorphan, etc.).

Self-stigma Scale for Drug Addicts (SSSDA)

Developed by Li Jiaoyang et al. [21], this scale was developed to assess the self-stigma level of individuals with SUDs. It consists of 31 items across six dimensions: self-negation (8 items), stereotype agreement (8 items), secrecy (5 items), social avoidance (3 items), stigma experience (4 items), and rehabilitation stigma (3 items). All items use a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). Total scores range from 31 to 155, with higher scores indicating greater self-stigma. In this study (n = 1001), the scale showed robust reliability: Spearman-Brown corrected split-half reliability (odd–even split) = 0.884, Cronbach’s α = 0.957, with dimensional Cronbach’s α ranging from 0.813 to 0.913. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) confirmed good fit for the six-factor model: χ2/df = 4.368, CFI = 0.929, TLI = 0.922, RMSEA = 0.058 (90% CI [0.047–0.061]), SRMR = 0.041. Satisfactory convergent validity was evidenced by average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) for each dimension: self-negation (AVE = 0.527, CR = 0.898); stigma experience (AVE = 0.547, CR = 0.826); rehabilitation stigma (AVE = 0.592, CR = 0.813); stereotype agreement (AVE = 0.573, CR = 0.914); secrecy (AVE = 0.664, CR = 0.908); social avoidance (AVE = 0.694, CR = 0.872). All AVE > 0.5 and CR > 0.8.

Meaning in life Questionnaire (MLQ)

Originally developed by Steger et al. and revised into Chinese by Wang Xinqiang [22], this scale consists of 10 items across two dimensions: presence of meaning (5 items) and search for meaning (5 items). All items use a 7-point Likert scale (1 = Absolutely Untrue, 7 = Absolutely True). Total scores range from 10 to 70, with higher scores indicating a higher level of MIL. In this study (n = 1001), the scale demonstrated good reliability: Spearman-Brown corrected split-half reliability (odd–even split) = 0.861, overall Cronbach’s α = 0.882, with dimensional Cronbach’s α coefficients of 0.840 (presence of meaning) and 0.855 (search for meaning), indicating good internal consistency. CFA indicated a good fit for the two-factor model of the MLQ: χ2/df = 8.398, CFI = 0.952, TLI = 0.937, RMSEA = 0.086 (90% CI [0.077–0.095]), SRMR = 0.051. Given that the chi-square statistic is highly sensitive to sample size, the relatively high χ2/df value may be attributed to the large sample size (N > 1000) [23]. However, more robust fit indices (i.e., CFI, TLI, RMSEA, SRMR), which are less affected by sample size, all met statistical criteria, confirming adequate substantive fit between the measurement model and the data. Among them, search for meaning (AVE = 0.544, CR = 0.856); presence of meaning (AVE = 0.573, CR = 0.860), with both AVE > 0.5 and CR > 0.8.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected via uniformly distributed paper questionnaires. SPSS 26.0 was used for data entry, organization, and analysis. Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous data conforming to a normal distribution are presented as the mean (standard deviation), whereas non-normally distributed data are presented as the median (minimum, maximum). The analysis of the 1001 valid questionnaires indicated a very low rate (< 1%) of random missing data on the scale items, which was confirmed to be completely at random by Little's MCAR test (p > 0.05). Given this, missing values were imputed via the series mean method in SPSS, and all subsequent analyses utilized the imputed complete dataset. Using the six dimensions of the SSSDA as manifest indicators, latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted via Mplus 8.3. Multinomial logistic regression was used to explore the influencing factors of different self-stigma profiles. Differences in the total score of meaning in life and its two sub-dimension scores across the self-stigma profiles were compared using ANOVA.

Common method bias test

Harman's single-factor test was used to assess common method bias. The results revealed seven factors with eigenvalues greater than 1. The first factor accounted for 34.78% of the total variance before rotation, which is below the critical threshold of 40% [24], indicating that there was no severe common method bias in this study.

Results

Sample description

The sample consisted of 1001 individuals with SUDs (mean age = 40.73 ± 10.99 years), with 617 males (61.6%) and 384 females (38.4%).The majority were urban residents (73.8%), had generally low education levels, and a high proportion were divorced or widowed (40.8%). With respect to monthly income, the largest group had no income (29.8%), and the unemployment rate was as high as 41.4%. The vast majority of individuals with SUDs had a history of substance use for more than ten years. First-time rehabilitants accounted for 56.0%, those with 2–3 previous attempts accounted for 36.2%, and those with more than three attempts accounted for 7.8%. A total of 16.9% of the individuals with SUDs were HIV-positive, and 14.3% had a history of being left-behind. Among the types of substances used, new synthetic substances (e.g., methamphetamine) were the most common (61.6%), followed by traditional substances (e.g., heroin, opium) (36.7%) and other psychoactive substances (17.4%). The total percentage for substance types exceeded 100% as some individuals reported using multiple types of substances. The details are shown in Table 1.

Results of latent profile analysis on self-stigma

Using the six SSSDA dimensions as manifest variables, we performed LPA, testing models with one to six latent profiles. The model fit indices are presented in Table 2. As the number of profiles increased, the AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased consistently, indicating a gradual improvement in model fit. Furthermore, the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT) and the Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMRT) results were significant (p < 0.05) for models with two to six profiles, suggesting that each successive model fit the data significantly better than the previous one. Starting with the four-profile solution, the entropy value decreased from 0.836, indicating a slight decline in classification accuracy. Both the five- and six-profile models contained profiles with an estimated class proportion below 10%. In contrast, the four-profile model demonstrated a better balance across indices: entropy remained high at 0.836 (implying a classification accuracy above 90%), and the distribution of profiles was reasonable, with no extremely small classes. Although some information criteria (e.g., AIC, BIC) were slightly better for the five-profile model, Marsh et al. (2009) emphasized that fit indices should not be used mechanically as the sole criterion [25]. Equal importance should be given to model parsimony, theoretical interpretability, and entropy. While a small class (< 10%) in the five-profile model might be statistically acceptable by some standards, its theoretical meaning is ambiguous. Moreover, it increases model complexity without substantially enhancing explanatory power. Consequently, the four-profile model was selected as the final model due to its clearer and more parsimonious class structure, higher classification accuracy (entropy), and better alignment with the theoretical framework of self-stigma [11].

Naming of 4 types of potential profiles of self-stigma among individuals with SUDs

On the basis of the LPA of the six SSSDA dimensions, the four profiles were plotted (Fig. 1) and named according to their score characteristics.

Self-stigma scores for the 4 profiles

Profile 1 (C1): Individuals in this profile had the lowest scores across all dimensions, indicating low overall stigma internalization and a certain resistance to public stigma. Thus, it was named the “stigma-resistant profile”(n = 100, 10.0%).

Profile 2 (C2): This profile was characterized by moderately high scores for self-negation, stigma experience, rehabilitation stigma, stereotype agreement and social avoidance, coupled with a notably elevated score on the secrecy dimension. This profile was named the “moderate stigma–concealment profile”(n = 463, 46.3%).

Profile 3 (C3): This profile had the highest mean scores across all dimensions. Aligning with the core concept of Corrigan's self-stigma theory [7], it was named the “internalized stigma profile”(n = 195, 19.5%).

Profile 4 (C4): The mean scores across dimensions were generally low, but the score on the Secrecy dimension was significantly higher than those on the other five dimensions. As Goffman suggested that individuals tend to use “information control” as a shrewd, functional social adaptation strategy [6], this profile was named the “low internalization–adaptation profile”(n = 243, 24.3%).

Using the “stigma-resistant profile” as the reference group, odds ratios (OR) were calculated (Table 3), reflecting the likelihood of belonging to other self-stigma profiles on the basis of different demographics.

The results indicated that male participants, individuals with low or no income, and those with a history of being a left-behind child were at higher risk of belonging to the three higher self-stigma profiles (p < 0.05). For instance, the odds ratios (ORs) for males ranged from 1.946 to 3.136, while a left-behind history was associated with ORs between 4.955 and 6.000. In contrast, younger age (≤ 25 years) generally served as a protective factor against these profiles. Limited family social support emerged as a key factor: infrequent or no contact with family elevated the risk—particularly for the “internalized stigma profile”(OR = 6.122, p < 0.05, among those who never contacted their family)—while being married acted as a protective factor. Additionally, both a history of mental illness (OR = 4.896, p < 0.05) and HIV positivity (OR = 4.873, p < 0.01) significantly increased the likelihood of belonging to the “internalized stigma profile”. Notably, first-time rehabilitants exhibited a lower risk of belonging to the “internalized stigma profile” compared to those with three or more rehabilitation attempts.

Differences in self-stigma dimensions across profiles

One-way ANOVA was used to examine score differences in the six self-stigma dimensions across the four profiles. The results revealed significant statistical differences among the groups in all six dimensions (p < 0.001, effect sizes η2p between 0.415 and 0.740). Post hoc multiple comparisons via the LSD test revealed the specific patterns of these differences (with all pairwise comparisons significant at p < 0.001): The “stigma-resistant profile”(C1) scored significantly lower than the other three groups did in all dimensions; the “internalized stigma profile”(C3) scored significantly higher than all other groups; the “low internalization–adaptation profile”(C4), as an intermediate type, systematically had lower self-stigma levels than C2 and C3 but higher than C1. This indicates that the latent profile analysis effectively captured the heterogeneity in self-stigma and successfully differentiated individuals on the basis of their level of self-stigma (Table 4).

Relationship between self-stigma latent profiles and meaning in life

Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was used to compare differences in the total MIL score and its two sub-dimensions (presence of meaning and search for meaning) across the four self-stigma profiles. The results are shown in Table 5. The total MIL score and both sub-dimensions significantly differed across the self-stigma profiles (p < 0.001). Specifically, the “stigma-resistant profile”(C1) scored significantly higher than the other three groups did in terms of the total MIL score, search for meaning, and presence of meaning, indicating the highest level of MIL. In contrast, the “internalized stigma profile”(C3) had the lowest scores on all dimensions. Post hoc comparisons revealed a clear ordinal pattern for the total MIL score and the presence of meaning subscale: the “stigma-resistant profile”(C1) > the “low internalization–adaptation profile”(C4) > the “moderate stigma–concealment profile”(C2) > the “internalized stigma profile”(C3). However, for the search for meaning subscale, the differences among the moderate stigma–concealment, internalized stigma, and low internalization–adaptation profiles were not statistically significant.

Discussion

Self-stigma in individuals with SUDs exhibits heterogeneity, with four distinct latent profiles

This study identified four distinct latent profiles of self-stigma among individuals with SUDs via latent profile analysis: the “stigma-resistant profile”(10%), “moderate stigma–concealment profile”(46.3%), “internalized stigma profile”(19.5%), and “low internalization–adaptation profile”(24.3%). This result not only provides supportive evidence for the cross-population validation of Corrigan’s self-stigma theory but also identifies a potential transitional type not explicitly defined in the theory, based on the unique stigma experiences of the SUDs population, thereby offering a valuable supplement to enrich the heterogeneity framework of self-stigma. Corrigan’s core theoretical framework of self-stigma challenges the oversimplified view that “stigma inevitably leads to self-devaluation” and posits that self-stigma responses are dynamic outcomes resulting from the interaction of “situationally activated cognitions and individual perceptions”, with three core types [11]: (1) the “self-devaluation type”, characterized by internalizing negative stereotypes and forming self-negation; (2) the “righteous anger-resistance type”, involving rejecting stigma legitimacy and actively resisting internalization; and (3) the “relative indifference type”, which buffers stigma impact through strategies such as intragroup comparison, seemingly ignoring external stigma. His proposed progressive model of self-stigma further elaborates on the process of stigma internalization through the sequential stages of awareness, agreement, application, and harm [7]. The four-profile result of this study aligns with the aforementioned theoretical logic while presenting unique characteristics driven by data.

The “internalized stigma profile” scored significantly highest across all dimensions of self-stigma, which well aligns with the core characteristics of the self-devaluation type in Corrigan’s self-stigma theory: these individuals not only perceive external social prejudice and discrimination against the SUDs population but also endorse negative labels such as “individuals with SUDs are incompetent and shameful”, internalizing them into self-cognition and ultimately forming severe self-negation [26]. This result indicates that although the sources of stigma may differ between individuals with SUDs and those with mental illness, their forms of internalization are largely consistent with the predictions of Corrigan’s theory. The “stigma-resistant profile” had the lowest total self-stigma score and average scores across all dimensions. Its core logic is largely consistent with the perspective of “high self-esteem protection driven by low perceived stigma legitimacy” in Corrigan’s Situational Model of Self-Stigma [11]—while aware of negative stereotypes about the SUDs population, these individuals refuse to endorse the legitimacy of such stereotypes, and certain protective psychological factors may support them in resisting stigma internalization. The existence of this profile further validates the core hypothesis of Corrigan’s self-stigma theory: stigma does not inevitably lead to internalization, and variables such as individuals’ perceived stigma legitimacy and group identity jointly influence the types of self-stigma responses [11]. The “low internalization–adaptation profile” had an overall low level of self-stigma but a significantly higher score on the secrecy dimension. This profile may be considered a supplement and situational extension of Corrigan’s “relative indifference type”. Corrigan described “the relative indifference type” as “buffering the impact of stigma through mechanisms such as intragroup comparison and devaluing mainstream values, seemingly ignoring the influence of stigma” [11]. However, this study found that the “indifference” of the SUDs population is not complete “ignorance” but “active avoidance”—they do not endorse negative labels such as “individuals with SUDs are failures” but are acutely aware of the strong social exclusion faced by this group, thus engaging in defensive adaptation through the concealment of their substance use history [27, 28]. This difference in manifestations may stem from China’s unique social and legal context—substance use is an explicitly prohibited illegal act in China [3], making the stigma faced by the SUDs population more substantively harmful, while the stigma faced by individuals with mental illness is mostly manifested as social-psychological exclusion. As the core profile with the highest proportion (46.3%) in this study, the “moderate stigma–concealment profile” is a transitional type not explicitly defined in Corrigan’s core theoretical framework of self-stigma, and its formation is closely associated with the unique stigma experiences of the SUDs population. According to Corrigan’s progressive model of self-stigma, the internalization of self-stigma is a continuous process progressing through the sequential stages of awareness, agreement, application, and harm. Brehmer et al. (2024) further confirmed that this model is equally applicable to populations with a history of incarceration [29], providing strong corroboration for the principle of continuous internalization of self-stigma. Building on this, we speculate that individuals in the “moderate stigma–concealment profile” in this study may be in the intermediate transitional stage from awareness to agreement within this continuous process [7]: they have clearly perceived the social stigma directed at the SUDs population, exhibit vague endorsement of certain negative labels (e.g., “substance use is indeed dishonorable”), but have not yet reached the level of complete internalization that would lead to self-negation.

Influencing factors of different self-stigma profiles

The results indicate that compared with females, male individuals had a significantly greater risk of belonging to the moderate stigma–concealment, internalized stigma, and low internalization–adaptation profiles. This may be linked to gender roles, as men often face higher societal expectations of “toughness” and “self-reliance” [30]. Research has shown that traditional masculine socialization norms are significantly correlated with negative attitudes toward seeking help, where seeking help (especially psychological help) is perceived as dependent, weak, and a loss of control [31]. Consequently, male individuals with SUDs typically have a higher risk of depression [15] and may be more inclined to hide their distress rather than seek external support when facing stigma, potentially leading more easily to self-stigma.

Regarding age, individuals aged 25 years and younger were more likely to belong to the “stigma-resistant profile”, which is consistent with previous findings [32]. Older age was associated with relatively higher self-stigma levels. One reason might be that older individuals with SUDs have experienced societal discrimination for a longer duration, leading to more severe self-stigma. Furthermore, older individuals tend to have a more stable and coherent self-concept. A significant deviant act, such as substance use, is more likely to be perceived as a devastating blow to their entire life narrative and established identity [5], consequently leading to profound self-negation and stigma internalization.

Economically, having a monthly income ≤ 4000 RMB or no income significantly increases the risk of belonging to a stigma profile. Low income or unemployment often limits access to mainstream social participation, decent housing, high-quality healthcare, and social networks [33]. For individuals with SUDs, long-term unemployment or low-income work reinforces their marginalized identity as “social failures”, preventing them from proving their self-worth and leading to severe self-stigma.

Individuals with a history of being left-behind children had significantly elevated risks of belonging to all stigma profiles. According to attachment theory, establishing a secure, stable emotional bond with primary caregivers in childhood forms the foundation for developing healthy models of self and others [34]. The experience of being a left-behind child essentially constitutes long-term emotional neglect and attachment disruption, predisposing individuals to develop low self-worth early in life. Therefore, when individuals face social condemnation due to substance use, external negative evaluations intensify their internal self-negation, leading to severe self-stigma. This is also why individuals with a left-behind history had a significantly greater risk of belonging to the “low internalization–adaptation profile”. Early-formed insecurity and distrust of others drive them to adopt high levels of “secrecy” as a protective strategy.

Regarding social support, a low frequency of contact with family significantly increased the risk of belonging to high-stigma profiles, while being “married” was a protective factor. Relevant research confirmed that emotional, informational, and instrumental support from significant others (e.g., family, spouses) constitutes crucial psychological resources for coping with stress and adversity and is an important factor influencing mental health [35]. Stable, close social connections provide emotional comfort and affirmation of identity, acting as an important buffer against stigma internalization. Conversely, the lack of social support leaves individuals to bear the pressure of stigma alone, potentially accelerating the process of self-negation.

The number of rehabilitation attempts was also a significant factor influencing self-stigma profiles, with first-time rehabilitants having a lower risk of being classified into the “internalized stigma profile”. First-time rehabilitants may externalize the cause of their substance use (e.g., attributing it to “bad friends” or “high pressure”). This external attribution protects their core self-worth, reducing the degree of self-stigma. As the number of rehabilitation attempts increases, the cumulative experiences of social discrimination and interpersonal rejection reinforce the “addict” label, leading individuals to internalize this negative social identity into their self-concept [36], resulting in more severe self-stigma.

Individuals with mental illness or HIV infection had a greater risk of belonging to the internalized stigma profile. Individuals with SUDs often face intersectional stigma and discrimination due to their multiple social identities, factors which collectively exacerbate their social vulnerabilities and substance use risks [37]. For individuals with SUDs, the “substance user” itself is a heavily stigmatizing label. When this label overlaps with other stigmatized identities like “mental illness patient” or “HIV infected person”, the individual faces the dilemma of intersecting stigmas. Research shows that individuals with co-occurring mental health issues and substance use disorders experience double stigma, which exacerbates their social isolation and psychological distress and constitutes a unique treatment barrier [38]. Similarly, HIV-positive individuals with SUDs not only bear moral blame associated with substance use but also face social discrimination related to AIDS. This state of multiple stigmas profoundly impacts their self-identity, mental health, and help-seeking behavior [39]. As the results show, the oppression of multiple stigmatized identities directly damages the self-worth of individuals in compulsory rehabilitation, making them more prone to internalize self-stigma.

Significant negative correlation between self-stigma profiles and meaning in life

This study revealed significant differences in meaning in life (MIL) among the different self-stigma profiles, with a significant negative correlation. The “stigma-resistant profile” had the highest level of MIL, the “internalized stigma profile” had the lowest, with the “low internalization–adaptation” and “moderate stigma–concealment” profiles in between. This finding indicates that greater self-stigma is associated with lower MIL among individuals with SUDs. According to the Meaning Maintenance Model, MIL is not fixed but constantly fluctuates influenced by “individual needs”, “social expectations”, and “cultural values” [40].

From the perspective of individual needs, high self-stigma is often accompanied by severe negative self-cognitions and stereotype endorsement [38]. This negative self-schema impairs the individual's capacity for meaning-making—the ability to perceive goals and values—thereby hindering the generation and maintenance of MIL [41]. From the social expectations perspective, high self-stigma means that individuals perceive themselves as having severely violated core societal role expectations. They realize that they have brought shame to their families and face social exclusion. This rupture from mainstream social expectations prevents them from deriving a sense of value and purpose from important social roles [42], consequently losing the social sources needed to construct the MIL. Regarding cultural values, substance use behavior and the “substance addicts” identity itself are severely at odds with mainstream cultural values. Individuals with high self-stigma easily internalize this cultural critique, identifying themselves as “negative exemplars” within the cultural value system [43]. This cultural exclusion not only damages an individual's MIL but also persistently affects the stability of their meaning system. Numerous studies have confirmed that MIL is closely related to mental health [44–46]. Individuals with higher levels of MIL typically possess heightened self-awareness, enabling them to employ positive coping strategies when facing stress, which reduces associated distress and negative cognitions, thus fostering psychological well-being [47]. This underscores the need to foster a less stigmatizing and more supportive social environment, which can help individuals with SUDs rebuild self-worth and enhance their MIL, thereby facilitating their long-term recovery.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations, which future research could address and expand upon. First, six drug rehabilitation centers in Sichuan Province were selected for the survey. Caution is needed when generalizing the conclusions to other regions or populations under different rehabilitation models. Future studies could expand the sampling scope to include populations from different geographical areas, cultural backgrounds, and rehabilitation stages to test the universality of the findings. Second, the cross-sectional design, while revealing associations between variables, cannot establish causal relationships between self-stigma and MIL. Future research could employ longitudinal designs to examine the stability of self-stigma latent profiles and their dynamic interaction with MIL.

Conclusion

The self-stigma of individuals with SUDs can be categorized into four latent profiles: stigma-resistant, moderate stigma–concealment, internalized stigma, and low internalization–adaptation. Among these, the moderate stigma–concealment profile is the most common.
