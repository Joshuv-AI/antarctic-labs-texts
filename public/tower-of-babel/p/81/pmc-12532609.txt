Single-port laparoscopic versus open Hartmann’s reversal: a retrospective analysis on surgical and postoperative outcomes
Authors: A Akmercan, K D Batun, H İ Sevindi, T Akmercan, T K Uprak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aims to compare the intraoperative and postoperative outcomes of single-port laparoscopic Hartmann’s reversal (SPLHR) and open Hartmann’s reversal (OHR).Material and methodsConsecutive patients who underwent OHR and SPLHR between 2019 and 2021 were analyzed retrospectively from a prospectively maintained database.ResultsDuring the study period, 23 patients underwent SPLHR and 24 patients underwent OHR. The median age, gender, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, and presence of midline/parastomal hernias were similar across the groups. The median estimated blood loss was significantly lower (100 versus 175 ml, p = 0.011), and also the median operation time (92 versus 120 min, p = 0.016) was shorter in the SPLHR group. Inadvertent bowel injury was more frequently observed in OHR group (37.5% versus 8.7%, p = 0.02). Overall postoperative complications did not differ between groups, but wound infections were significantly more common in the OHR group (33.3% versus 4.3%, p = 0.023). The SPLHR group experienced a shorter time to first flatus (median 2 versus 3 days, p = 0.04), a shorter time to resuming a soft diet (median 2 versus 3 days, p = 0.002), and a shorter length of hospital stay (4 versus 4.5 days, p = 0.007).ConclusionThis study confirms that SPLHR is a reliable and efficient method. SPLHR has several advantages in terms of perioperative morbidity and postoperative outcomes compared with OHR in selected patients.Keywords: Hartmann’s procedure, Hartmann’s reversal, Single-port laparoscopy, Stoma site

Introduction

In 1921, Henri Albert Hartmann introduced Hartmann’s procedure as an alternative surgical approach to rectal cancer with low morbidity and mortality [1]. Hartmann’s procedure involves removing a portion of the colon or rectum, closing the remaining distal rectum, and creating an end colostomy with the proximal part [1]. Despite initially introducing the procedure for rectal cancer, its application has expanded to include perforation, diverticulitis with fecal contamination, and colorectal trauma. Surgeons prefer Hartmann's procedure in conditions with a risk of anastomosis leakage owing to its shortened operation time and lack of leakage risk [2]. However, living with the stoma is a major disadvantage owing to the decreased quality of life for patients [3]. With the improvement of surgical techniques, Hartmann’s reversal procedure became a viable option for favorable patients [4]. However, the history of previous surgery and changed anatomy make Hartmann’s reversal procedure challenging for many patients [5]. For many years, the open surgical approach has been the preferred method for restoring intestinal continuity after Hartmann’s procedure. The search for reducing mortality and morbidity in Hartmann’s reversal led to the development of new minimally invasive techniques. First, in 1993, Anderson et al. described laparoscopic Hartmann’s reversal using four ports. In 2011, Carus et al. introduced a single-port laparoscopic Hartmann’s reversal that was performed through the colostomy site [7]. De’Angelis et al. provided the initial description of a robotic reversal of an end colostomy [8]. Subsequently, many studies were published on the reversal of Hartmann’s procedure using minimally invasive techniques. Comparative studies between minimally invasive and open techniques of Hartmann’s reversal demonstrated the advantages of minimally invasive techniques, such as faster recovery time, less perioperative blood loss, and shorter hospitalization [9–13]. Our objective is to compare the intraoperative and postoperative outcomes of the single-port laparoscopic Hartmann’s reversal (SPLHR) and open Hartmann’s reversal (OHR) procedures.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted retrospectively, and data were obtained from a prospectively maintained database. The study enrolled patients who underwent Hartmann’s reversal at Marmara University between 2019 and 2022. Patients were divided into SPLHR and OHR groups. The study excluded patients who underwent intensive care in the postoperative period, those with incomplete data, and those who did not have long-term follow-up (at least 2 years). Patients unsuitable for laparoscopic surgery owing to medical reasons were also excluded to avoid selection bias.

Data were collected in three primary sections: preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative. Preoperative data covered baseline characteristics, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists performance status (ASA-PS), and comorbidities. The researchers also collected data on prior abdominal surgeries (excluding Hartmann’s procedure), the primary indications for Hartmann’s procedure, rectal stump length, the interval between procedures, and the presence of midline incisional or parastomal hernias. All patients underwent computed tomography (CT) and rectoscopy to assess potential pathologies, measure the rectal stump length, and evaluate the presence of midline incisional or parastomal hernias. Perioperative data covered estimated blood loss, duration of the surgery, intraoperative complications (including small intestinal injury, stump injury, and adjacent organ injury), and drain placement. Postoperative data covered functional recovery indicators (including time duration until liquid diet intake, time to first flatus passage, and time to resuming a soft diet), the length of hospital stay, readmissions, and postoperative complications, including anastomotic leakage, wound infection, delayed bowel movement, and bleeding. All complications were stratified according to the Clavien–Dindo complication grading system.

All surgeries were performed by experienced colorectal surgeons. All patients received bowel preparation before the operation. Antimicrobial prophylaxis was administered with cefazolin and metronidazole along with anesthesia induction. In patients who underwent OHR, access was achieved via a midline incision. In patients who underwent SPLHR, the colostomy was mobilized initially, local adhesions were divided, a circular stapler anvil was placed into the end colon, and either a single-port device (OCTO™ Port, DalimSurgNet, Seoul, Korea) or a customized handmade glove-port was inserted. Pneumoperitoneum was established, intraabdominal adhesions were divided as necessary to achieve a tension-free anastomosis, and the rectal stump was dissected. Colorectal anastomoses were performed end-to-end or end-to-side transanally with the EEA™ Circular Stapler (Medtronic, Minneapolis, USA) in all patients. Following colorectal anastomosis, a leak test was performed as a standard [14]. The fascia at the colostomy site was sutured with a USP 1 polydioxanone (PDS II) running suture, and the skin incision was sutured with a USP 3–0 poliglecaprone 25 purse-string subcuticular suture. In open surgeries, the fascia at the midline was usually closed according to the surgeon’s preference. Postoperatively, all patients were managed according to individualized treatment plans, although enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols were implemented in most patients.

Descriptive statistics indicated the study cohort’s demographics and perioperative data. The Shapiro–Wilk test was employed to evaluate the normality of numeric data. Subsequently, categorical data were expressed as frequency with percentage [n (%)] and numeric data were expressed as median (interquartile ratio, IQR). Categorical data were compared with the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test, and median values were compared with a Mann–Whitney U test. p-Value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using Jamovi (version 2.5.3.0).

Results

Forty-seven patients underwent restoring intestinal continuity surgery following Hartmann’s procedure, and patients were divided into SPLHR (n = 23) and OHR (n = 24) groups. Hartmann’s procedures assessed in this study had been performed with open surgery. The median age, gender distribution, and median BMI did not significantly differ between the two groups. ASA-PS and comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, heart disease, and lung disease were similar across both groups, with no significant differences. Preoperative midline incisional hernia and parastomal hernia rates were similar across both groups. A significant difference was observed in the primary indications for HP. The SPLHR group (60.9%) had a higher proportion of adenocarcinoma cases compared with the OHR group (25%) (p = 0.046). Additionally, the median rectal stump length was significantly longer in the SPLHR group (20 cm, IQR: 15–30 cm) compared with the OHR group (13.5 cm, IQR: 10–20 cm) (p = 0.025). The time between the initial Hartmann’s procedure and Hartmann’s reversal was consistent across both groups. Discussion

Restoration of intestinal continuity after Hartmann’s procedure has been associated with significant morbidity, and it can be considered a challenging procedure even for expert surgeons. The open surgical approach of Hartmann’s reversal is still considered the gold standard technique. However, minimally invasive techniques such as multiport and single-port laparoscopy have gained popularity in the past decades [15]. Many studies have aimed to compare open and minimally invasive techniques and have confirmed that the minimally invasive techniques result in a shorter hospital stay, less blood loss, and less surgical site infection [5, 16, 17]. A recent meta-analysis validated that laparoscopic reversal has fewer short-term complications, consisting of wound infection and postoperative ileus [18]. Consistent with previous studies, our results indicate that patients who underwent SPLHR had improved functional recovery, decreased intraoperative and postoperative morbidity, and a shorter hospital stay than those who underwent OHR.

There are considerable technical difficulties in Hartmann’s reversal, especially following open primary surgery in purulent or fecal peritonitis. Open surgery is generally preferred in Hartmann’s reversal compared with other colorectal procedures. However, the popularity of minimally invasive techniques has increased steadily over recent years. In most reports, patients who underwent minimally invasive techniques had more favorable baseline characteristics than patients who underwent open surgery. Those had significantly younger age, a lower fraction of oncological indications, and fewer comorbidities [9, 18]. On the other hand, a meta-analysis by Guerra et al. revealed no statistical differences in patient baseline characteristics between minimally invasive and open surgery [19]. In our study, baseline characteristics—including age, gender, BMI, ASA-PS score, history of abdominal surgery, and comorbidities—were comparable between the SPLHR and OHR groups. Unlike some previous reports, a higher proportion of patients in the SPLHR group underwent surgery for oncological indications. It is possible that patients with complicated diverticulitis, particularly those presenting with severe purulent or fecal peritonitis, developed more extensive intraabdominal adhesions, which may have influenced the preference for OHR in this subgroup. Within these limitations, our findings indicate that a single-port approach may be safely applied in carefully selected patients undergoing Hartmann’s procedure for oncological indications.

Common issues such as midline and parastomal hernias often go unreported after Hartmann’s procedure, with incidence rates ranging from 4% to 48% [20]. The incidences of midline (48.9%) and parastomal hernia (38.3%) observed in our study are consistent with previously reported values, and both hernias were distributed equally between the SPLHR and OHR groups. The median time interval between Hartmann’s procedure and its reversal is 11 months; this can be acceptable compared with previous studies and is long enough to observe midline and parastomal hernias. Midline and parastomal hernias can complicate Hartmann’s reversal procedure, especially in minimally invasive techniques, making it more challenging. Some studies have indicated that performing stoma reversal concomitant with hernia repair may result in unacceptably high postoperative morbidity rates [21, 22]. Even, in some studies, the existence of the incisional hernia has been considered an exclusion factor [23]. Conversely, some authors have suggested that, when conducted with the single-port laparoscopic approach, stoma reversal can be accomplished more easily without the need for midline dissection, thereby eliminating the need for concurrent hernia repair [24]. Our results support that SPLHR can be performed safely in the presence of midline or parastomal hernia. Moreover, avoiding adhesiolysis of the midline, particularly in patients with previous laparotomies, severe wound infection, or complex abdominal wall defects, may represent a potential advantage of SPLHR. This approach may also be considered in patients with a midline hernia, as restoration of intestinal continuity through the stoma site could allow subsequent abdominal wall repair to be performed with potentially lower morbidity. In addition, we evaluated the occurrence of stoma-site hernia after Hartmann’s reversal over a long-term follow-up period of at least 2 years. However, these observations should be interpreted with caution, as the number of patients was limited and further studies are required to confirm these findings. A meta-analysis designed to evaluate stoma-site hernia has reported incidence rates up to 30–35% [25]. We found an overall 14.9% stoma-site hernia rate, and there was no significant difference between SPLHR (17.4%) and OHR (12.5%).

We consider the length of the rectal stump to be an important factor in Hartmann’s reversal. A shorter rectal stump often necessitates more extensive peritoneal and splenic flexure dissection to achieve adequate mobilization of both ends for anastomosis, thereby increasing the technical complexity of the procedure. Previous studies have not adequately addressed the potential impact of this issue [9, 18, 19]. In our study, the median rectal stump length was significantly shorter in the SPLHR group compared with the OHR group. Conversely, a longer rectal stump may have facilitated shorter operation times, reduced blood loss, and lower rates of intraoperative morbidity observed in the SPLHR group. This factor, however, may also have introduced selection bias and should be considered when interpreting the results. Future prospective studies controlling for rectal stump length are warranted to better understand its impact on surgical outcomes.

Many studies have evaluated the effectiveness of minimally invasive techniques for Hartmann’s reversal. They concluded that minimally invasive techniques were superior to open surgery in terms of postoperative morbidity and functional recovery but highlighted a lack of high-level evidence. None of these studies focused on or separated SPLHR [16, 26, 27]. Recently, some studies have investigated the safety and feasibility of Hartmann’s reversal using single-port laparoscopy through the stoma site. These studies have indicated that SPLHR is not inferior to open surgery regarding intraoperative and postoperative morbidity. Moreover, SPLHR resulted in enhanced postoperative recovery and shorter hospitalization [23, 28, 29]. When evaluating the surgical aspects of SPLHR, we observed several advantages; the median operation time and blood loss in the SPLHR group were significantly shorter compared with OHR. No significant differences were observed between SPLHR and OHR in terms of overall intraoperative morbidity. Inadvertent bowel injuries were observed more frequently in the OHR group, contrary to the results of Thambi et al., which represents one of the largest series in the literature [28]. Midline adhesiolysis, often required in the open approach, can be cumbersome and carries the risk of inadvertent bowel injury. Using the colostomy site as a single incision and performing targeted left-sided and pelvic dissection may mitigate the aforementioned challenges. Our overall postoperative morbidity rate in all patients is consistent with the range reported in previous studies [16, 26]. When comparing SPLHR and OHR groups regarding overall postoperative morbidity, no significant differences were observed. However, wound infections occurred significantly more frequently in the OHR group. Additionally, patients who underwent SPLHR experienced a faster functional recovery, as evidenced by a shorter time to first flatus, a faster return to a soft diet, and a shorter length of hospital stay. These observations, however, should be interpreted cautiously given the potential influence of patient selection and other confounding factors.

This study is subject to several limitations. First, it was conducted in a nonrandomized and retrospective manner. Despite rigorous data collection and the application of well-defined inclusion criteria, the possibility of observation and selection bias remains. Second, the relatively small sample size may have increased the likelihood of a type II error. Finally, while many studies have demonstrated a reduction in postoperative pain and analgesic use following minimally invasive surgery compared with open surgery, these important data were not incorporated into our study, which emphasizes postoperative recovery.

Conclusions

Our study suggests potential advantages of SPLHR compared with OHR regarding perioperative morbidity and postoperative outcomes. While Hartmann’s procedure remains technically challenging, SPLHR may offer benefits in terms of reduced morbidity and faster recovery in selected patients. The comparable baseline characteristics between the SPLHR and OHR groups support the potential applicability of this approach in different clinical contexts. However, given the retrospective nature of our study and the limited sample size, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Further prospective, large-scale studies are warranted to clarify the role and establish the definitive noninferiority of the single-port approach in Hartmann’s reversal.
