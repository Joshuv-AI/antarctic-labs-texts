Single cell CRISPR screen identifies antagonism between Nsd1-H3K36me2 and Ezh2-H3K27me3 orchestrates pluripotency transition
Authors: Yuqing Zhu, Qingying Wang, Yanzi Cao, Linxiao Hou, Lin Yang, Chen Cheng, Shoudong Ye, Wei Li, Jin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe transcriptional and epigenetic landscape imposes constraints on the self-renewal capacity and lineage specification potential of both naive and primed mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs). CRISPR/Cas9-based functional screening coupled with single-cell RNA-seq (CROP-seq) establishes relationships between gRNA-mediated knockout genotype and transcriptome phenotype, providing a powerful tool to dissect gene regulatory networks. Here, we employed CROP-seq to investigate the epigenetic regulation governing the pluripotency network in mESCs. This highly sensitive method identified key genes essential for the acquisition and exit from pluripotency, and revealed a novel role for H3K36me2 in modulating DNA methylation through regulating the expression of Dnmt1 and Dnmt3a. Specifically, loss of Nsd1-mediated H3K36me2 delayed naive state exit, whereas Ezh2 deficiency accelerated primed entry. Collectively, our findings identify an epigenetic regulatory network critical for determining mESCs' pluripotent state transitions.Keywords: CROP-seq, CRISPR screen, epigenetic regulation, Nsd1, H3K36me2

Introduction

During murine embryonic development, pluripotent cells transition from a state of heightened self-renewal capability to one poised for restricted lineage specification (Kojima et al., 2014). Cells within the pre-implantation blastocyst exhibit a naive pluripotent state, characterized by a broad developmental potential, whereas those in the post-implantation embryo acquire a primed pluripotent state, which is more committed to differentiation (Nichols & Smith, 2009a). The transition between naive and primed pluripotency proceeds through a transient formative phase, during which cells acquire multi-lineage competence and responsiveness to specification cues (Kinoshita et al., 2021a). Although core transcription factors governing pluripotency have been extensively characterized (Marks et al., 2012; Martello & Smith, 2014), growing emphasis is being placed on the role of epigenetic modifications in regulating gene expression dynamics during this transition. For instance, global DNA methylation patterns and the repressive histone mark H3K27me3 at promoters of developmental genes undergo extensive reprogramming as cells transition from the naive to primed state (Jiang et al., 2024; Takahashi et al., 2018). H3K27me3, catalyzed by the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) is essential for early embryogenesis. PRC2 knockout in mice results in early embryonic lethality (Pasini et al., 2004), and loss of core PRC1 and PRC2 core subunits in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) impairs differentiation (Endoh et al., 2008; Wiles & Selker, 2017). Concurrently, genome-wide DNA methylation dynamics are profoundly altered during pluripotency progression(Clark et al., 2022; Shanak & Helms, 2020). The transition from naive to primed pluripotency, as well as the onset of differentiation, is accompanied by the activation of de novo DNA methylation, predominantly catalyzed by the methyltransferases DNMT3A and DNMT3B (Leitch et al., 2013). DNMT1 serves as a critical barrier preventing the spontaneous reversion of mESCs to a totipotent 2C-like state, and its loss leads to the activation of 2C-stage-specific genes (Fu et al., 2019). Notably, the deposition of H3K36me2 by Nsd1 in mESCs plays a crucial role in establishing the de novo DNA methylation pattern, primarily by serving as a binding platform for DNMT3A. The PWWP domain of DNMT3A specifically recognizes H3K36me2, directing DNMT3A to intergenic regions (Dukatz et al., 2019; Weinberg et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2020b). DNMT3A can also be recruited to genomic loci through an interaction between its N-terminal region and H2AK119ub-modified nucleosomes, establishing a PWWP-domain-independent targeting pathway (Weinberg et al., 2021). At the onset of trilineage differentiation in the embryo, lineage-determining signals upregulate Nsd1/Nsd2, which deposit H3K36me2 at lineage-specific intergenic and enhancer regions. This mark, in turn, directly recruits the DNMT3A/B complexes to catalyze the establishment of a lineage-specific DNA methylation landscape (Lu et al., 2025). And Nsd1 deficiency in the mouse male germline triggered a cascade where the loss of H3K36me2 and DNA methylation at target regions was followed by aberrant PRC2 and H3K27me3 accumulation, ultimately causing their erroneous and persistent silencing (Shirane et al., 2020). In line with this, Nsd1 knockout has been shown to lead to elevated H3K27me3 levels at specific downregulated gene regions, suggesting functional crosstalk between H3K36me2 and PRC-mediated repression (Fang et al., 2021b). Specifically, H3K36me2 acts to directly inhibit the chromatin binding or catalytic activity of the PRC2, thereby preventing the deposition of H3K27me3. For instance, biochemical studies have shown that the presence of H3K36me2/3 marks can directly suppress the methyltransferase activity of PRC2, providing molecular evidence for this mutual exclusion (Hoetker et al., 2023). Besides, it has been found that leukemia-associated mutations in Nsd1 alter its substrate preference from H3K36me2 to H3K36me1. This shift disrupts the balance of H3K36me2 and H3K27ac at enhancer regions, leading to the aberrant activation of oncogenic programs (Fang et al., 2021a).

To systematically identify epigenetic regulators governing the naive to primed transition, we performed a CRISPR knockout screen combined with single-cell transcriptomic readouts. Using the scMAGeCK pipeline, we recovered both established and novel factors, including the m6A methyltransferase Mettl3, the histone methyltransferases Ezh2 and Nsd1, that influence pluripotency. We further mechanistically unraveled how Nsd1-mediated H3K36me2 compromises pluripotency by coordinating DNA methylation via Dnmt3a and Dnmt1. Moreover, we demonstrate that H3K36me2 interacts with Ezh2-dependent H3K27me3 to co-regulate genomic regions critical for cell differentiation.

Results

Single-cell CRISPR screen for epigenetic regulators of naive to primed state transition

To clarify the role of epigenetic regulators in naive to primed state transition in mESC, we set out a pooled CRISPR screening method combined with single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) to utilize the transcriptome as a readout (Figure 1A). Given the transcriptional and methylation changes that occur during the naive to primed transition (Weinberger et al., 2016), we aimed to identify epigenetic factors that govern the long-term maintenance of naive pluripotency and its elimination. We generated a pooled CRISPR/Cas9 sgRNA library targeted to 60 genes of different classes of epigenetic regulators, primarily those involved in covalent domain modification (Figure S1A). We designed 3 sgRNAs per gene targeting coding sequences, along with 10 non-targeting and 10 positive control sgRNAs (Table S1). These sgRNAs were cloned into the CROP-seq vector, enabling a single-cell transcriptome as a readout, with the sgRNA sequence serving as a barcode (Figure S1B) (Datlinger et al., 2017a). NGS analysis confirmed that the gRNA sequences cloned into the CROP-seq vector followed a normal distribution, with a detection rate of 100% (Figure S1C). This pooled library was then transduced into the mES cell line constitutively expressing Cas9. Lentiviral transduction was performed at a low multiplicity of infection (MOI = 0.3) to ensure that most selected cells contained approximately one sgRNA per cell. Next, our scRNA-seq experiments were performed on mESCs transfected with sgRNA library and subsequently either maintained in naive conditions (LIF/2i) or subjected to a 48-h priming treatment (FGF/Activin A) to induce naive to primed transition. At the intended sequencing depth, 90% of all gRNAs in the library were successfully detected (Figure 1B). We also found that the sgRNA cassette assigned to cells was correlated with the gene detection threshold (Figure S1D). Applying a cutoff of 1000 detected genes per cell, we determined that 45.2% of the cells harbored a single unique sgRNA, while only 2.54% of cells were assigned two or more sgRNAs (Figure 1C), consistent with previous reported CROP-seq data (Hill et al., 2018). Overall, we obtained the transcriptome profiles of ∼8000 cells per sample. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) clustering demonstrated a clear separation of naive and primed state, not batches (Figure 1D). Known markers are selectively expressed in each state, including Nanog in the naive state, Dnmt3b in the primed state (Figure 1E).

scMAGeCK identifies Nsd1 knockout regulates naive maintenance

One particular advantage of single-cell CRISPR screen is that the full transcriptome serves as a readout, enabling simultaneous capture of a wide range of phenotypes without reliance on limited biomarkers (Cheng et al., 2023). Previous screens have relied on fluorescent reporters or colony formation to identify genes regulating state transitions (Hackett et al., 2018; Li et al., 2018b), which could only recognize candidates that rely on reporter gene expression and result in false positive. Therefore, genes whose knockout shows no significant effect based on the expression of only one or two genes are overlooked (Figure S2A). scMAGeCK is a newly developed computational framework for single-cell CRISPR screening analysis, including scMAGeCK-RRA and scMAGeCK-LR test, which systematically identify genes associated with multiple phenotypes at single-cell level (Figure 2A) (Yang et al., 2020). Applying scMAGeCK-RRA to our dataset, we identified that knockout of 41 out of 60 target genes led to a statistically significant reduction in expression. Based on this set of candidates, we next investigated the effect of individual gene knockout on pluripotent state transition using scMAGeCK-LR. To this end, we used a combined expression of naive markers Nanog and Zfp42 as a readout to evaluate gene knockout effects (Chambers et al., 2007). The combined expression of Nanog and Zfp42 in naive/primed state was defined as nscore/pcscore, respectively (Figure 2B). Among those, Mettl3, Kmt2b, Setd1a, and Nsd1 knockout significantly increases naive markers expression in primed state, which indicated these gene knockout play a protective role in naive state exit (p < 0.05) (Figures 2C and S2B). Kmt2b and Setd1a are both H3K4me3 methyltransferases, which display compensatory roles in H3K4 methylation (Ruthenburg et al., 2007). H3K4me3 is preferentially located at the promoters of lineage-specific genes in the primed state (Xiao et al., 2016), which indicates the knockout of H3K4me3 methyltransferases might contribute to the maintenance of naive pluripotency. A previously study demonstrated that Kmt2b-depleted ES cells could retain pluripotency and differentiation capacity (Barbagiovanni et al., 2018). Consistently, we found that Kmt2b depletion delayed Rex1-GFPd2 degradation (Figure S2C). Mettl3 is an N6-methyladenosine (m6A) transferase; its knockout is reported to fail to completely terminate naive state and restricted lineage priming at the post-implantation stage (Shay Geula, 2015). Nsd1 is a histone methyltransferase that catalyzes the deposition of H3K36me2. Nsd1-mediated H3K36me2 is required for the recruitment of DNMT3A and maintenance of DNA methylation at intergenic regions (Weinberg et al., 2019). Furthermore, when we use the combination of Nanog, Zfp42, Pou5f1, and Sox2 as selection markers, only Mettl3 and Nsd1 knockouts showed a significant upregulation of primed selection score (p < 0.05), which confirmed that the deficiency of Mettl3 and Nsd1 played an important role in naive to primed transition (Figures 2D and 2E). In contrast to Mettl3 and Nsd1, Ezh2 knockout significantly reduced naive marker expression in the naive state (Figure 2C), with Ezh2 gRNAs enriched clusters distinctly visualized by UMAP (Figure S2D). These results demonstrate a dominant role for Ezh2 in maintaining the naive state and preventing differentiation. Ezh2 is methyltransferases unit of the PRC2 complex, and it is responsible for H3K27me3 (Cao & Zhang, 2004). A previous study reported that PRC2 mediated H3K27me3 protects naive ESCs from lineage priming (van Mierlo et al., 2019). Using the combined expression of Nanog, Zfp42, and Tbx3 as nscore and Fgf2, Otx2, and Dnmt3a as the pscore, we found that Ezh2 knockout led to a significantly reduced nscore (Figure S2E), confirming that Ezh2-deficient mESCs exhibit diminished pluripotency. Collectively, we rudimentarily identified Mettl3, Nsd1, and Ezh2 as candidate genes for mESC pluripotency transition with various combinations of state-specific makers. Among these, Mettl3 and Ezh2 are known regulators of pluripotency transitions and therefore served as positive controls to validate the robustness of our screening approach.

Validation of the candidate genes that impede or accelerate pluripotency exit

To further validate individual candidate genes identified by the screen, we used an arrayed approach, which directly measured mutagenesis of the targeted genes. In separate transient transfections, sgRNAs targeting the candidate genes were introduced into naive mESCs harboring Rex1-GFPd2 reporter. Rex1 (Zfp42) is actively expressed in the naive state, but silent in the primed state (Nichols & Smith, 2009b). Single clones were then picked, the indel locus was determined by Sanger sequencing, and the target gene expression was assessed by western blot (Figures S3A, S3B and S3C). Each of the mutant and the original Rex1-GFPd2 cell lines (control) displayed similarly high levels of GFP fluorescence under naive conditions (Figures 3A and 3B). Upon transfer to primed medium for 48 h, Mettl3 and Nsd1 knockout mESCs showed a delayed loss of GFP fluorescence compared with control (Figures 3A and S3D), whereas Ezh2 mutation showed a slightly accelerated decline in GFP fluorescence relative to control (Figures 3B and S3E). Together, these data indicate that, unlike Ezh2, Mettl3 and Nsd1 mutations suppress naive state exit and preferentially support self-renewal, which aligns with the report that Mettl3 mediated m6A facilitates the resolution of naive pluripotency (Geula et al., 2015). Moreover, when the LIF/2i was withdrawn, and Fgf2/Activin A was added to the medium for 42 h, Ezh2 knockout mESCs showed an expedited GFP fluorescence decline in naive to primed transition, further demonstrating that Ezh2 loss promotes naive exit (Figures 3B and S3E). To further dissect the impact of candidate genes on the expression of core naive and primed transcriptional factors, we assessed the expression of naive and primed pluripotency markers on LIF/2i, FGF/activin, and withdrawal of LIF/2i conditions. Consistently, both Mettl3 and Nsd1 knockout mESCs exhibited elevated expression of naive marker Zfp42 and a significant downregulation of Dnmt3a under both primed and LIF/2i withdrawal conditions (Figures 3C, 3D, S3F and S3G). However, the marked upregulation of the formative marker Otx2 in Nsd1 KO mESCs maintained in primed condition suggested that Nsd1 deficiency impedes the transition by poising cells in a formative-like state between naive and primed pluripotency (Figure 3D) (Kinoshita et al., 2021b). Meanwhile, Ezh2 KO displayed a significant upregulated primed marker expression in both primed state and LIF/2i withdrawal medium (Figures 3E and S3H). Collectively, the expression profiles of candidate genes provided strong validation for the CROP-seq and scMAGeCK pipelines. As noted above, Mettl3 and Ezh2 have well-characterized roles in mediating pluripotency transition (Batista et al., 2014; Chamberlain et al., 2008; Shen et al., 2008) and were therefore used as positive controls. Consequently, we focused our investigation on the function of Nsd1. In line with this, Nsd1 KO mESCs exhibited enhanced alkaline phosphatase (AP) activity under both on LIF/serum and LIF withdrawal conditions (Figures 3F and 3G), as well as increased expression of naive markers and decreased expression of primed markers under LIF/serum condition (Figure S3I), suggesting a potential role for Nsd1 in facilitating the exit from naive pluripotency.

Nsd1 and Ezh2 exert opposing effects on pluripotency and differentiation

Given the known but functionally divergent roles of Nsd1 and Ezh2 in pluripotency regulation, we performed RNA-seq of Nsd1 KO and Ezh2 KO mESCs alongside wildtype controls under primed conditions to comprehensively assess their involvement in naive pluripotency defense or exit. In Nsd1 KO mESCs of the primed state, naive pluripotency genes such as Tbx3, Klf4 and Zfp42 were significantly upregulated, whereas primed pluripotency marker genes such as Dnmt3b and Dnmt3c were downregulated (Figure S4A). However, we observed a decrease of naive pluripotency genes Nanog and Zfp42 in Ezh2 KO mESCs under primed state, while Dnmt3b and Otx2 showed significantly increased (Figure S4B). The results indicated that these two epigenetic regulators showed opposing phenotypes in terms of self-renewal capability and gene expression for naive and primed pluripotency markers. Based on these findings regarding differentiation discrepancy and given that Nsd1 KO mESCs exhibited enhanced AP activity under both LIF/2i-containing and LIF/2i-free conditions (Figures S4C and S4D), we next investigated whether their differentiation potential differed from that of controls. After 5 days of culture in LIF/2i withdrawal medium (N2/B27), Nsd1 KO mESCs retained higher expression of pluripotency marker genes, along with significantly reduced levels of H3K36me2 compared to control (Figures 4A and 4B). Analysis of germ layer markers by qRT-PCR across multiple stages of embryonic body (EB) formation revealed that Nsd1 KO EBs consistently expressed higher levels of endoderm-associated genes but lower levels of mesoderm- and ectoderm-associated genes (Figures 4C and 4D). To further validate lineage commitment, we performed western blot. The results showed that although pluripotency gene expression was markedly downregulated following EB formation, the protein level of OCT4 and SOX2 remained comparable between Nsd1 KO and control EBs. In contrast, the protein levels of KLF4 were significantly reduced in Nsd1 KO EBs (Figures 4E and S4E). This finding suggests that Nsd1 may regulate developmental genes during EB-mediated differentiation, consistent with prior reports (Li et al., 2025). Therefore, we performed western blot analysis to assess the protein levels of key lineage markers. The results showed a pronounced upregulation of endoderm-specific proteins SOX17 and GATA6 in Nsd1 KO EBs relative to control (Figures 4F and S4F). Notably, protein levels of BRACHYURY, a marker associated with early mesendodermal commitment, were also elevated in the Nsd1 mutant EBs (Figures 4F and S4F). Since BRACHYURY upregulation is indicative of enhanced germ layer specification, its increase further supports the conclusion that Nsd1 depletion biases differentiation toward endodermal lineages (Song et al., 2016). Consistent with these findings, immunofluorescence analysis confirmed uniformly increased GATA6 expression across all individual Nsd1 KO EBs (Figures 4G and S4G). Collectively, these data indicate that Nsd1 loss not only delays pluripotency exit but also biases differentiation toward the endodermal lineage.

In contrast, Ezh2 KO mESCs under the LIF/2i withdrawal conditions exhibited markedly reduced expression of the core pluripotency factor SOX2 as shown by immunofluorescence (Figure S4H). qRT-PCR analysis further confirmed substantial downregulation of pluripotency genes, accompanied by upregulated expression of the endoderm marker Sox17 (Figure S4I). However, in EB differentiation assays, Ezh2 KO cells showed significant suppression of endoderm markers (Figure S4J), consistent with reported gastrulation defects in PRC2-depleted embryos (O'Carroll et al., 2001). These results suggest that Ezh2 ablation disrupts both naive to primed transition and pluripotency maintenance, ultimately impairing proper lineage specification.

Nsd1 depletion alters DNA methylation patterning and modulates DNMT expression

To elucidate the mechanism by which Nsd1 depletion sustains self-renewal, we explored its role in reorganizing the DNA methylation landscape. To assess whether Nsd1 loss influences DNA methylation levels genome-wide, we performed quantitative analysis of global DNA methylation under both naive and primed conditions. We found that reduced DNA input resulted in progressive loss of methylation signal across all samples, with Nsd1 KO cells exhibiting pronounced hypomethylation compared to controls, particularly under primed conditions (Figure 5A). Bisulfite sequencing of specific CpG sites within the Klf4 promoter revealed significant hypomethylation in Nsd1 KO cells compared to control under primed conditions (Figures 5B and S5B), consistent with the concomitant upregulation of KLF4 expression (Figure S5A). In contrast, analysis of the Nanog promoter showed no significant methylation differences between control and Nsd1 KO mESCs (Figures S5C and S5D). These results suggest that Nsd1 depletion leads to locus-specific dysregulation of DNA methylation at Klf4 promoter regions, consistent with previous findings that re-expression of Klf4 can regenerate the naive state from EpiSCs (Guo et al., 2009). Moreover, both H3K36me2 enrichment at the Klf4 promoter and its transcript levels were significantly increased upon Nsd1 knockout (Figure 5C), suggesting that H3K36me2 deposition coordinates with DNA methylation to regulate gene expression at pluripotency loci. Given the critical role of DNA methyltransferases in establishing methylation patterns, we next examined their expression. Western blot analysis revealed a marked downregulation of DNMT3A in Nsd1 KO mESCs compared with control under both naive and primed conditions (Figure 5D), supporting the proposed functional interaction between NSD1 and DNMT3A (Weinberg et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2020a), while the expression levels of DNMT3B and DNMT3L remained unchanged (Figures 5D and S5E–S5G). Surprisingly, the expression of the DNA maintenance methyltransferase DNMT1 was elevated in Nsd1 KO mESCs (Figures 5D and S5G). Given that DNMT1 deficiency is reported to destabilize the epigenome, causing defects in DNA methylation, histone modifications, and gene expression that hinder proliferation and differentiation (Elder et al., 2025), we speculate that its elevation in Nsd1 KO mESCs may act as a compensatory response. This could alleviate the loss of de novo methylation activity to support self-renewal. To directly test whether Dnmt1 is required for pluripotency maintenance, we performed RNAi-mediated knockdown. This led to significant downregulation of the core pluripotency factors Klf4, Sox2, and Nanog (Figure 5E), demonstrating that Dnmt1 function is essential for maintaining the naive transcriptional program. Furthermore, Dnmt1 knockdown significantly downregulated REX1 expression and impaired colony formation ability under both wildtype and Nsd1 KO conditions (Figures 5F, S5H and S5I), indicating that Dnmt1 is required for naive pluripotency maintenance. Given the unexpected upregulation of DNMT1 in Nsd1 KO cells and the established role of H3K36 methylation in promoting gene expression (Padilla et al., 2025), we hypothesized that Dnmt1 expression might be sustained through redistribution of H3K36me2. ChIP-seq of H3K36me2 in Nsd1 KO versus control mESCs revealed that Nsd1 depletion downregulates global H3K36me2 distribution (Figure S5J). We next performed ChIP-qPCR to assess H3K36me2 enrichment specifically at the Dnmt1 promoter region. Surprisingly, despite the global reduction of H3K36me2, our analysis revealed that H3K36me2 remained present at the Dnmt1 promoter in Nsd1 KO mESCs (Figure 5G). Consistently, our comparative ChIP-seq analysis of H3K36me2 between Nsd1 KO and wildtype mESCs further demonstrated increased H3K36me2 enrichment at Dnmt1 promoter region (Figure 5H), which is also supported by the published data from Weinberg et al. (2019) (Figure 5I). These observations suggest that other H3K36 methyltransferases, such as Nsd2, may compensate for the loss of Nsd1 function, consistent with previous reports of their overlapping roles (Hoetker et al., 2023; Wagner & Carpenter, 2012). This suggests a compensatory mechanism to sustain Dnmt1 expression and supports its critical role in pluripotency maintenance.

Nsd1 depletion promotes self-renewal through rearrangement of H3K27me3

Given the opposing effects of Nsd1 and Ezh2 depletion on pluripotency gene expression, coupled with the significant alteration of H3K27me3 levels upon Nsd1 loss (Figure 6A), we hypothesized a potential mechanistic interplay between these two epigenetic regulators. Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays confirmed that Nsd1 physically interacts with Ezh2 in mESCs, suggesting a direct functional relationship (Figure 6B). This finding is consistent with previous reports identifying Nsd1 as a critical modulator of PRC2 function, essential for the regulation of genome-wide H3K27me3 domains (Streubel et al., 2018a). Given this regulatory interplay, we next examined whether Nsd1 loss could counteract the phenotypic consequences of Ezh2 deficiency. In contrast to the impaired pluripotency observed in Ezh2 single-knockout cells, AP staining revealed that Nsd1/Ezh2 DKO mESCs exhibited significantly enhanced pluripotency (Figures S6A–S6C). Taken together, these results demonstrate that Nsd1 antagonizes Ezh2 to maintain the pluripotent state and that dual depletion partially rescues the accelerated differentiation phenotype caused by Ezh2 loss alone. Subsequently, ChIP-seq analysis revealed that Ezh2 KO mESCs exhibited globally reduced H3K27me3 levels and markedly increased H3K36me2 abundance (Figures 6C and 6D). These changes were further validated by western blot analysis (Figure S6D). Furthermore, the expression of DNA methyltransferases DNMT1 and DNMT3A displayed changes opposite to those seen in Nsd1 KO cells (Figure S6E). Consistent with this, ChIP-qPCR analysis revealed a significant reduction in H3K27me3 occupancy at the Dnmt1 promoter in Ezh2 KO cells, which correlated with the ChIP-seq results and directly corresponded to its transcriptional upregulation (Figures 6E and 6F). This finding aligns with the established role of EZH2-mediated H3K27me3 in transcriptional repression, as removal of this repressive mark typically leads to increased gene expression (Boyer et al., 2006). Given the established role of H3K36me2 in antagonizing PRC2 recruitment and activity (Xu et al., 2024; Yano et al., 2022), we found that Ezh2 depletion caused an enriched redistribution of H3K36me2 at the Dnmt1 promoter region (Figure 6G). Subsequently, we asked whether the loss of Nsd1 would conversely lead to a redistribution of H3K27me3 at a specific site. Accordingly, we found that Nsd1 depletion substantially rearranged the distribution of H3K27me3 at key developmental genes, including Fgf5, Sox17, and Otx2 (Figure 6H), potentially underpinning the altered lineage specification in Nsd1 KO mESCs. Moreover, integrated analysis of ChIP-seq and RNA-seq data revealed that most differentially expressed pluripotency-associated genes are direct targets of either H3K36me2 or H3K27me3 (Tables S3 and S4). Together, these results suggest a reciprocal antagonism between Nsd1 and Ezh2 in coordinating histone methylation and DNA methylation programs, which may underlie their opposing roles in pluripotency regulation.

Discussion

Despite research elucidating genetic and epigenetic mechanisms governing mESC self-renewal and pluripotency, predictive frameworks remain incomplete. Scalable genetic tools for comprehensively mapping genotype to phenotype relationships are lacking, as traditional approaches rely on either low-throughput-driven methods or inefficient genome-wide screens, both offering limited biological context. Diverging from conventional CRISPR-KO screens, which typically focus on one or two makers labeled fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) phenotypes in mESCs (Li et al., 2018a; Seruggia et al., 2019). Our platform leveraged the transcriptome as the phenotypic readout (Datlinger et al., 2017b). Meanwhile, utilizing the developed scMAGeCK pipeline provides simultaneous monitoring of single-gene perturbation effects on specific phenotypic outcomes at single-cell resolution. Our approach successfully validated known mediators, including Mettl3 and Ezh2, and uncovered previously underappreciated factors such as Nsd1, a histone methyltransferase responsible for H3K36me2 deposition. Subsequent mechanistic studies revealed that Nsd1 and Ezh2 function antagonistically to regulate the equilibrium between self-renewal and differentiation through coordinated epigenetic reprogramming.

This study unveils a central epigenetic regulatory axis in which the antagonistic interplay between Nsd1 and Ezh2 orchestrates DNA methylation dynamics and pluripotency transitions in mESCs. A key finding is that Nsd1 depletion reinforces naive pluripotency and biases lineage differentiation toward endoderm, hallmarked by sustained expression of core pluripotency factors and preferential activation of endodermal markers. Consistent with this, Sun et al. reported that NSD1 is dispensable for pluripotency exit but critical for lineage specification (Sun et al., 2023). However, they observed broad lineage defects (especially mesodermal deficiency) upon acute NSD1 degradation, whereas we identified a specific endodermal bias. This discrepancy may be explained by compensatory mechanisms in our constitutive knockout model (Shipman et al., 2024), which are less likely to occur upon acute protein degradation. Importantly, this phenotype is coupled with widespread redistribution of DNA methylation, particularly at the promoter region of pluripotency genes, such as Klf4. We further demonstrated that these methylation changes are mechanistically driven by the concurrent reduction of DNMT3A and a compensatory upregulation of DNMT1. This DNMT1 elevation likely represents a cellular attempt to preserve global DNA methylation and genome stability, as DNMT1 is primarily responsible for maintaining established methylation patterns during replication (Estève et al., 2006). These results position Nsd1 as a critical regulator of DNA methylation, potentially through its role in guiding de novo methyltransferase activity and maintaining appropriate methylation levels at key genomic loci (Baubec et al., 2015; Lu et al., 2025). The upregulation of DNMT1, which is known to maintain progenitor self-renewal by methylating and silencing differentiation-associated genes (Sen et al., 2010), may therefore help stabilize the naive pluripotent state.

Equally important, our data support a physical interaction between Nsd1 and Ezh2 within the context of pluripotency regulation. The opposing phenotypes of their respective KOs—with Nsd1 KO enhancing naive identity and Ezh2 KO driving premature priming—suggest a functional antagonism in regulating pluripotency (Schmitges et al., 2011). This model is robustly supported by reciprocal alterations in their catalytic products: elevated H3K36me2 in Ezh2 KO mESCs and aberrant H3K27me3 distribution in Nsd1 KO cells. These findings align with and extend emerging evidence that H3K36me2 can directly antagonize PRC2 recruitment and activity, thereby modulating H3K27me3 deposition genome-wide (Xu et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2011). Our results thus support a model in which Nsd1 serves as a key modulator of PRC2 function, facilitating the precise demarcation of repressive chromatin domains necessary for developmental gene silencing during pluripotency exit (Westcott et al., 2023). We propose that this antagonistic interplay forms a dynamic, state-dependent switch crucial for pluripotency transitions. In the naive state, predominant Ezh2 activity maintains basal H3K27me3 levels to suppress premature differentiation programs (Jiang et al., 2024; Much et al., 2025), while Nsd1-mediated H3K36me2 is restrained to prevent inappropriate silencing of the pluripotency network. Upon initiation of the transition toward the primed state, increased Nsd1 activity leads to elevated H3K36me2 levels. This histone mark directly recruits DNMT3A to establish de novo DNA methylation, thereby silencing the pluripotency transcriptional program. Concurrently, H3K36me2 antagonizes PRC2 at specific loci, resolving bivalent domains and facilitating lineage-specific gene activation (Streubel et al., 2018a). This model is consistent with recent findings that H3K36me2 reprogramming guides lineage-specific methylation during embryonic development (Lu et al., 2025).

Overall, our study proposes an integrated model wherein the functional antagonism between Nsd1 and Ezh2 fine-tunes the epigenetic landscape via crosstalk between H3K36me2, H3K27me3, and DNA methylation to balance self-renewal and differentiation in mESCs. These insights not only deepen our understanding of pluripotency regulation but also offer a new framework for understanding how coordinated activity of epigenetic modulators orchestrates cell fate decisions.

Limitations of the study

Although our overexpression and genetic rescue experiments support a functional antagonism between Nsd1 and Ezh2, the precise molecular basis of their physical association in mESCs under physiological conditions remains incompletely defined. While previous studies have reported endogenous NSD1-EZH2 interactions (Drosos et al., 2022), future investigations employing advanced proximity labeling or crosslinking strategies will be required to fully elucidate how Nsd1 and Ezh2 physically engage with each other to orchestrate the epigenetic balance during pluripotency maintenance or exit.

Theoretically, profiling H3K27me3 redistribution in the Nsd1 KO background would provide direct genome-wide evidence for the proposed antagonism. Critically, prior studies have already demonstrated that NSD1 loss leads to a widespread gain of H3K27me3, validating the antagonistic crosstalk between NSD1 and PRC2 at the epigenomic level (Streubel et al., 2018b). Future studies integrating ChIP-seq across multiple histone modifications will further refine our understanding of how H3K36me2 and H3K27me3 dynamically coordinate to regulate cell fate transitions.

Nevertheless, several key questions remain unresolved, particularly regarding the mechanism by which H3K36me2 coordinates with DNA methylation to pluripotent cell state transitions. While our study establishes a functional link between Nsd1-mediated H3K36me2 and the DNA methylation machinery (DNMT3A/DNMT1), the precise principles governing how this histone mark instructs locus-specific DNA methylation and whether this represents a direct or indirect relationship remain to be defined.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) (E14 line from C57, male) were a gift from George Daley’s laboratory (Harvard University). Rex1-GFPd2 mESCs were generously provided by Austin Smith (University of Cambridge). Cells were cultured on mitomycin C-treated mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) feeder layers in LIF/2i medium, as described below. All cell lines were routinely tested and confirmed negative for mycoplasma contamination. Nsd1 knockout, Ezh2 knockout, and Nsd1/Ezh2 double knockout mESCs were generated in this study using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing. Authentication of the E14 mESC line was performed by STR profiling using a mouse-specific panel, which confirmed its murine origin and excluded cross-contamination with human or other mammalian cells. No additional authentication was performed for derivative lines, as they were derived in our laboratory.

Method details

Cell culture

E14 were cultured on MEF feeder layers in LIF/2i medium consisting of DMEM/F12 (Gibco) and Neurobasal (Gibco) supplemented with N2 (Stem Cell Technologies), B27 serum-free supplement (Stem Cell Technologies), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco), 0.1 mM 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma), murine LIF (1,000 U/mL; Peprotech), and 2i inhibitors (3 μM CHIR99021 and 1 μM PD0325901; Stem Cell Technologies). E14 were thawed from cryopreservation in liquid nitrogen and subsequently maintained for experimentation between passages 18 and 30. Cultures were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 and medium was refreshed every 24 h. mESCs were passaged every 2 days (when the cell density reaches 60–80%), and the passaging ratio is 1:10-1:15. To induce a primed/EpiLC-like state, mESCs were cultured on MEF in N2B27 basal medium with FGF (12 ng/mL) (Proteintech) and Activin A (20 ng/mL) (Proteintech) for 48 h, after which cells showed upregulation of Otx2 and Fgf5 and downregulation of naive markers, consistent with a primed-like (EpiLC/formative) state (Kinoshita et al., 2021a).

Library preparation

Epigenetic gRNA library was designed with CRISPR-FOCUS (http://cistrome.org/crispr-focus/). CROPseq-Guide-Puro (Addgene 86708) vector was digested with BsmBI (NEB) at 55°C for 1h and inactivating the restriction enzyme at 80°C for 20 min gRNA library oligonucleotides were diluted to 100 mM and cloned to CROPseq-Guide-Puro fragment (8333bp) by Gibson’s isothermal assembly (NEB). De-salted ligation product was electroporated into Lucigen Endura E. coli cells (Lucigen) using prechilled 1 mm electroporation cuvettes (Lonza cat. no. VKA-1001) in a Lonza Amaxa Nucleofector Ⅱ with a bacteria electroporation procedure. 1mL pre-warmed (37°C) Recovery Medium (Lucigen) was added and bacteria were grown at 37°C for 1h while shaking at 225 rpm. Then, 1 mL of the bacterial solution was spreading on a 20 × 20 cm LB plate containing with 100 μg/mL ampicillin and cultured at 32°C for 22 h. The next day Bacterial colonies were harvest with 50 mL LB medium and plasmid DNA was extracted with TIANGEN’s EndoFree Plasmid Extraction kit (TIANGEN).

Production of lentiviral particles for CRISPR screen and cell transduction

HEK293T cells were plated onto 10 cm dish at 7 million cells in 10 mL of lentivirus packaging medium (Opti-MEM I (Gibco), 5% FBS (Gibco), 200 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco)). Cells were transfected with 10 μg CROP-seq-Guide-Puro (containing gRNA library) or LentiCRISPR V2 (containing validation gRNA),7.5 μg psPAX2 (Addgene 12260) and 2.5 μg pMD2.G (Addgene 12259) using lipofectamine 3000 and P3000 enhancer (Invitrogen). Changed for fresh lentivirus packaging medium 6 h after the transfection. Harvested Supernatant containing viral particles at 24 h and 48 h, pooled and passed through a 0.22 mm filter to remove cells. Final concentration of 10% PEG 6000 was added to the viral supernatant and incubate at 4°C overnight. Virus was centrifuged at 4200 rpm for 30 min at 4°C. Then discarded the supernatant and resuspended the viral deposit. Virus was stored at −80°C.Stable Cas9 expressing ESC single clone were infected with the lentiviral epigenetic gRNA library at a 1000-fold coverage with multiplicity of infection of 0.3. Stable integrations were selected for 7days in the presence of 1 μg/mL Puromycin. 106 cells for the library were plated on MEF coated dishes in the absence of LIF/2i but supplied with FGF and Activin A. After 48h of priming, both LIF/2i and FGF/activin state cells were collected and prepared for single cell RNA-seq.

Single cell RNA-seq

mESCs were dissociated with 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) for 5 min. After digestion termination, cells were incubated for 30 min to allow MEF attachment. Suspension cells were collected by centrifugation (1,100 rpm, 2 min), washed once with PBS/0.04% BSA, and resuspended in 1 mL of the same buffer. Cells were filter through 40μm cell strainer to obtain a single cell suspension. The cell density was adjusted to 1,000 cells/μl using a hemocytometer. The total loading amount of each sample is 10,000 + 10% cells. Then the cells were encapsulated with barcoding beads and cell lysis/RT reagents to form GEMs with 10×genomics V2 kit. The barcoded cDNA was subsequently collected and used for library construction. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform.

Production of single gene knockout cell lines

sgRNA for validation was designed with Zhang lab CRISPR design online tool (https://zlab.bio/guide-design-resources). LentiCRISPR V2 (addgene 52961) vector was digest and dephosphorylate with BsmBI (NEB) and FastAP (Thermo Fish) for 30 min at 37°C. Each pair of target gene gRNA oligos were phosphorylated and annealed with T4 PNK (NEB) for 30 min at 37°C, followed by 95°C for 5 min and then ramp down to 25°C at 5°C/min. Then set up the ligation with quick ligase (NEB) at room temperature for 10 min. Transfer the ligation production to stbl3 225rpm for 1h and then spread the bacteria solution onto an ampicillin agar plate. Single colony was identified and validated for correct ligation. Lentiviral was generated with two packaging plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G in HEK293T cells as described before. Stable integrations were selected for 7days in the presence of 1 μg/mL Puromycin and single clone was picked and validated with sanger sequencing.

Flow cytometry

Rex1-GFPd2 mES cell line was generated using lipofectamine 2000 transfection and a fluorescence-activated cell sorting was used to sort GFP positive cells. mESCs are prepared for flow cytometry by trypsinization and resuspension in PBS +1%FBS and filtered with 40 μm filter. A minimum of 10,000 events were acquired per sample for analysis. Flow cytometry is performed using the Beckman DxFLEX. Analysis and visualization of flow cytometry data was performed using FlowJo V10.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

RNA was isolated from cells using FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme). 1μg of RNA was converted to cDNA using HiScript Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme) and quantitative real-time PCR was performed with cDNA from each sample with ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). qPCR was performed on a C1000 thermal cycler and CFX96 Real Time System (Bio-Rad). qPCR data was analyzed and visualized with bio-rad CFX manager and GraphPad Prism 8.0.1.

AP staining

The cellular self-renewal capacity was measured through BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology) as described by the manufacturer’s instructions. Wash cells with PBS. Fix with 4% PFA for 20–30 min at room temperature. Rinse thoroughly with wash buffer. Incubate fixed cells with freshly prepared AP Staining Solution. Protect from light. Incubate at room temperature until desired color intensity develops (typically 5–30 min). Monitor under a microscope to avoid over-staining. Remove staining solution and rinse cells 2–3 times with excess wash buffer.

EB formation

For EB formation, mESCs were culture under feeder-free condition in knockout DMEM (Gibco) medium supplemented with 15% FBS. Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 to 2 × 105 cells/mL into non-treated, ultra-low attachment bacteriological dishes to promote suspension culture. Medium was changed every two days by allowing EBs to settle by gravity, then carefully removing half of the supernatant. Typical EBs formed by days 5–7 and were subsequently harvested for analysis.

Dot blot

150 ng of DNA was mixed with 0.9 μL of 1 M NaOH (final concentration 0.1 M). The volume was adjusted to 9 μL with DEPC H2O. The mixture was incubated at 95°C for 10 min and immediately placed on ice. Then, 1 μL of 10 M NH4Ac (final concentration 1 M) was added. Aliquots of 60 ng, 40 ng, 20 ng, 10 ng, and 5 ng of the treated sample were taken, and their volumes were adjusted accordingly. The samples were spotted onto a nylon membrane and air-dried. The membrane was baked at 80°C for 30 min and then blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with the primary antibody (5mC, MERCK) overnight at 4°C on a shaker. It was then washed three times with TBST for 10 min each on a shaker, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody (HRP, Goat Anti-Mouse IgG) at room temperature for 2 h. After another three washes with TBST (10 min each), the signals were detected using a chemi-imaging system with exposure solution.

Bisulfite sequencing

Bisulfite conversion was performed with the EZ DNA Methylation-Lightning Kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The converted DNA was then subjected to PCR amplification. PCR primers were designed using the online MethPrimer tool (Li and Dahiya, 2002) to specifically target regions of interest. The PCR products were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis and purified using a gel extraction kit. The purified products were cloned into a pMD19-T vector using a TA cloning kit, and the ligation products were transformed into competent E. coli DH5α cells. Following transformation, single colonies were picked and sent for Sanger sequencing. The resulting sequencing chromatograms were analyzed using the online BiQ Analyzer (Bock et al., 2005) to determine the methylation status of each cytosine within the amplified region.

Immunoprecipitation

mESCs (2×106) were lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer (50 mM Tris/HCl, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 0.5% Nonidet P-40, supplemented with protease inhibitors). A 500 μL aliquot of the cell lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting supernatant was subjected to immunoprecipitation by incubation with 10 μL of anti-FLAG affinity gel (450-FG, GNI, Japan) overnight at 4°C. For the co-immunoprecipitation of endogenous proteins, the lysate was incubated with a specific antibody Nsd1(Abbexa), followed by the addition of Protein A/G agarose beads. The precipitated complexes were washed three times with PBS and subsequently resuspended in 1× SDS loading buffer.

ChIP

mESCs (5×106) were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, quenched with glycine, and washed. Nuclei from 2×106 cells were isolated using Membrane Extraction Buffer and digested with Micrococcal Nuclease (MNase) at 37°C for 15 min. The digested chromatin was sonicated (15 s on, 30 s off, 7 cycles). After reserving 10% as Input, the remaining chromatin was incubated overnight at 4°C with target-specific antibody. Antibody complexes were captured with Protein A/G magnetic beads for 2 h, followed by sequential washes with Low Salt and High Salt buffers. ChIP-seq data visualization and normalization were performed using deepTools (version 3.5.1).

Quantification and statistical analysis

RT-qPCR and ChIP-qPCR data were analyzed using a one-sample or Student’s t test, with three experimental replicates as indicated in the figure legends. Immunoblot and gel images are representative of three experimental replicates unless otherwise noted. All graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1; GraphPad, Boston, MA). Statistical calculations were performed using SPSS (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless stated otherwise. Single cell RNA-seq data were analyzed using R (version 4.3.2), and the resulting exact p-values are shown directly in the figures. For all other experiments, statistical significance is denoted as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
