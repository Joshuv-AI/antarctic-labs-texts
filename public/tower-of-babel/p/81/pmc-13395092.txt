Coronary computed tomography angiography findings of anomalous left hepatic vein drainage into the coronary sinus with complex anomalies: a case report
Authors: Ömer Baran Yaman, Dursun Can Karakuş, Turgut Seber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrainage of the left hepatic vein into the coronary sinus is an extremely rare anomaly reported in the literature. Typically, haemodynamics remain unimpaired in the absence of concomitant anomalies, and detection is expected to be incidental.Case summaryWe herein report a case of a 7-year-old male patient presenting with atypical chest pain, exertional dyspnoea, and mild congestive hepatomegaly, in whom a left hepatic vein draining into a dilated coronary sinus was detected. This anomaly was associated with a superior–inferior type sinus venosus atrial septal defect, persistent left superior vena cava, and partial anomalous pulmonary venous return. Together, these defects created a significant left-to-right shunt, explaining the patient's symptomatic right ventricular strain.DiscussionThis case underscores the diagnostic necessity of computed tomography angiography in evaluating complex venous anatomy and its clinical implications. Our findings demonstrate that when echocardiography fails to fully explain right-sided volume overload and atypical symptoms, advanced cross-sectional imaging is decisive for a comprehensive anatomical diagnosis and multidisciplinary surgical planning.

Unexplained right-sided heart dilation on echocardiography should always prompt comprehensive cross-sectional imaging to rule out rare concomitant venous anomalies and shunts.

Case summary

A 7-year-old male patient presented to the paediatric cardiology outpatient clinic with complaints of atypical chest pain, mild exertional dyspnoea, and easy fatigability. Physical examination revealed a normal physiological status without any prominent pathological murmurs; however, the liver was palpable 2 cm below the costal margin, suggesting mild congestive hepatomegaly. Laboratory findings, including complete blood count and cardiac biomarkers, were within normal limits. Initial electrocardiogram demonstrated a right axis deviation, consistent with right heart strain. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a dilated right atrium and right ventricle with preserved left ventricular systolic function. A sinus venosus type atrial septal defect (ASD) and suspected partial anomalous pulmonary venous return (PAPVR) were also noted.

However, because standard echocardiography has limitations in visualizing posterior venous structures, it could not fully explain the severity of the right heart dilation and hepatomegaly. Therefore, a contrast-enhanced coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) was performed. CCTA confirmed that the right heart and pulmonary artery were markedly dilated. It definitively revealed a partial anomalous pulmonary venous return, with the right superior, middle, and inferior pulmonary veins draining into the right atrium, alongside a superior–inferior type sinus venosus ASD (Figure 2). Furthermore, an anomalous left hepatic vein and a persistent left superior vena cava were observed draining into a dilated coronary sinus (Figure 1).

Following the comprehensive radiological evaluation, a multidisciplinary paediatric cardiology and cardiovascular surgery council was planned to discuss the optimal management strategy, including potential surgical correction of the ASD and anomalous venous connections. However, as the patient was a foreign national, he was unfortunately lost to follow-up due to geographical relocation before a definitive therapeutic intervention could be initiated.

Discussion

Direct drainage of the left hepatic vein into the coronary sinus is an extremely rare anomaly. It arises from the persistence of the connection between the left vitelline vein and the coronary sinus during embryological life.1–3 Nearly all variations of the left hepatic vein are associated with the right hepatic vein, with 61% draining via a common trunk into the inferior vena cava, and 39% draining via separate trunks into the inferior vena cava.4 In the literature, the drainage of the left hepatic vein into the right atrium or the coronary sinus is limited to only a few reported cases. When other anomalies are present, they frequently include conditions such as ASD or PLSVC (Table 1). When isolated, it generally does not impair haemodynamics.3,6 Therefore, it is detected incidentally. As such, most reported cases are in adults or found in autopsy series.

Among adults, ASD has an incidence of 0.13% in the USA. Four types of ASD exist, with the most common being the ostium secundum defect. The rarest is the sinus venosus ASD. The sinus venosus type is subdivided into superior and inferior types. Sinus venosus ASD accounts for 5–6% of all defects.10 The vast majority of sinus venosus ASDs are accompanied by anomalous pulmonary venous return.11 In our case as well, the partial anomalous pulmonary venous return, in which the right superior, middle, and inferior pulmonary veins drained into the right atrium (Figures 2 and 3), was accompanied by a large superior-inferior type sinus venosus ASD.

Persistent left superior vena cava (PLSVC) is the most common thoracic vascular anomaly. The prevalence of PLSVC ranges from 0.2% to 3% in the general healthy population. In patients with congenital heart disease, its prevalence ranges from 1.3% to 11%. In up to 90% of cases, a right superior vena cava accompanies the PLSVC, a condition known as double SVC.12 The persistent left superior vena cava originates from the bifurcation of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins and extends to the coronary sinus. It is generally an isolated anomaly and does not impair haemodynamics.13 PLSVC usually drains into the right atrium via a dilated coronary sinus (CS) (80–92%), but in approximately 10% to 20% of cases, drainage into the left atrium (LA) can also be observed.14

Regarding the clinical context, chest pain in a 7-year-old patient is unusual and rarely of primary cardiac origin. However, in this particular case, it served as a critical clinical indicator of right heart strain. The massive left-to-right shunt caused by the combination of the sinus venosus ASD, PAPVR, and the anomalous LHV drainage led to significant right ventricular volume overload. We consider that the resulting increased right ventricular wall tension and relative subendocardial ischaemia likely manifested as atypical chest pain. This pathophysiology clearly links the complex anatomical findings to the patient's unusual symptomatic presentation, which ultimately prompted the initial investigations.

In conclusion, the frequency of observing variations and anomalies is increasing daily, owing to increased access to hospitals and advancements in diagnostic investigations. Although drainage of the left hepatic vein into the coronary sinus is generally a silent vascular anomaly detected incidentally, in our case, the accompanying anomalies (ASD, partial anomalous pulmonary venous return) increased the left-to-right shunt, thereby augmenting the right heart's workload and pulmonary circulation, which led to the aforementioned clinical symptoms and haemodynamic impairment.
