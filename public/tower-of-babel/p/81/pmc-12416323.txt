Epidural blood patch for cerebrospinal fluid leak–induced subdural hematoma in a pediatric patient: illustrative case
Authors: Ryan S. Chung, Lacin Koro, Benjamin Best, Virendra R. Desai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDIntracranial hypotension (IH) is a secondary headache syndrome that can result from spontaneous or iatrogenic CSF leaks. Subdural hematomas (SDHs) are a particularly dangerous sequela of IH. Although epidural blood patch (EBP) is a recognized treatment for IH, its use in pediatric patients with SDH as a solitary intervention has not been reported.OBSERVATIONSAn 11-year-old male with newly diagnosed acute myeloid leukemia developed persistent, postural headaches following a lumbar puncture performed for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. Initial imaging revealed a moderate left-sided SDH with midline shift, yet MRI of the lumbar spine failed to demonstrate an obvious CSF leak. Conservative management did not alleviate symptoms. Given the clinical picture of low CSF pressure, an EBP was administered, resulting in immediate headache improvement and subsequent radiographic evidence of SDH resolution.LESSONSThis case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for IH in pediatric patients with postural headaches after lumbar puncture, even when imaging is inconclusive. EBP can be a safe and effective intervention, potentially preventing further complications and clinical deterioration in this vulnerable population. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25254

Intracranial hypotension (IH) is a secondary headache syndrome, with an incidence of 5 in 100,000 persons.1 IH patients present with postural headaches due to low CSF pressure. Associated symptoms include neck pain, vestibulocochlear dysfunction, photophobia, and/or nausea.2 CSF loss may occur spontaneously, termed spontaneous IH,3 or secondary to iatrogenic spinal dural lacerations.4 First-line management involves bed rest, intravenous hydration, and caffeine supplementation,5,6 while epidural blood patches (EBPs) are reserved for resistant cases.5

Intracranial subdural hematomas (SDHs) are potentially serious complications of dural punctures, requiring attention in patients with persistent headaches after lumbar puncture (LP).7,8 However, the literature describing the use of EBP in pediatric patients presenting with SDH and clinical symptoms of IH remains limited. Here, we present the case of a pediatric patient with unremitting postural headaches and a moderate-sized SDH following an LP, successfully treated with an EBP alone.

Illustrative Case

Clinical Presentation

An 11-year-old male with no significant medical history was evaluated for a 3-month history of fatigue and nausea. He was found to be anemic and was referred to the emergency department. After further workup, he was diagnosed with acute myeloid leukemia (AML). As part of his AML workup, he underwent an L3–4 LP for CSF sampling and for chemotherapy injection. Following the LP, he developed progressively worsening postural headaches. He denied any recent history of head trauma. His neurological examination was otherwise unremarkable.

Diagnostic Workup and Management

Given his postural headaches and recent LP, a CSF leak was suspected. Initial CT imaging (Fig. 1A) showed a new left moderate-sized, mixed-density SDH with approximately 1 cm of midline shift. His clinical presentation at that time strongly suggested an SDH induced by low CSF pressure, evidenced by the postural nature of his symptoms and the absence of trauma history or other obvious etiologies. Additionally, his platelet counts remained above 50,000/μl throughout his hospital course, reducing our suspicion for a spontaneous SDH. His serial CT scans demonstrated neither worsening nor improvement of the SDH. MRI of the brain confirmed a stable SDH with no leptomeningeal enhancement (Fig. 1B), and MRI of the lumbar spine did not reveal any signs of an obvious leak. Despite conservative management for several weeks, his postural headaches persisted. Considering his persistent symptoms and the clinical suspicion for an SDH induced by low CSF pressure, an EBP was recommended. Importantly, SDH drainage was not recommended given his clinical stability and the suspected high recurrence risk in the presence of an untreated CSF leak.

EBP Technique

Under C-arm fluoroscopy guidance, a 20-gauge Tuohy needle was advanced to the L3–4 interspace using a loss of resistance technique. The L3–4 interspace was targeted given that this was the site of his prior LP. Omnipaque contrast confirmed an epidural location. Then, 15 ml of autologous blood was injected into the lumbar epidural space. The Tuohy needle was removed, and pressure was applied for hemostasis. There were no complications.

Postoperative Outcome

His postural headaches improved immediately after EBP treatment. Follow-up MRI of the brain (limited) demonstrated considerable SDH resolution (Fig. 2A), with a further interval decrease on 1-month postoperative MRI of the brain (Fig. 2B). Literature Review

Methods

A literature review was conducted to identify all studies that reported EBP use in pediatric patients for suspected SDH secondary to CSF leaks. A search using the terms “(epidural blood patch) AND (subdural hematoma)” was performed in the MEDLINE and Embase databases through January 20, 2025. References cited in the resulting publications were also reviewed. Inclusion criteria included age < 18 years and original research articles in the English language. Abstracts, presentations, review articles, and basic science research articles were excluded.

Results

The results of the literature search are shown in Fig. 3. Of the 389 studies yielded, only 1 article described a pediatric case aligning with our inclusion criteria (Table 1). In this article, Cheuret et al. reported on the case of a 14-year-old female with Marfan syndrome who presented with postural headaches for 1 month.9 MRI of the brain demonstrated a bilateral SDH, and MRI of the spine pointed to a diagnosis of IH caused by a meningocele.9 Despite 2 weeks of initial conservative management, her symptoms continued, prompting the decision to pursue EBP.9 One day after the EBP treatment, she became asymptomatic with subsequent resolution of her SDH.9 Unlike this reported case, our patients’ MRI findings neither suggested IH nor localized a CSF leak. Instead, the post-LP postural headaches and absence of alternative SDH etiologies supported our diagnosis of CSF leak–induced IH, emphasizing the critical role of clinical presentation in guiding management decisions for similar pediatric cases. Discussion

Observations

We described a case of a successfully treated SDH in a pediatric patient with unremitting postural headaches and signs of IH post-LP, despite negative MRI findings. Although several other conditions (e.g., viral meningitis, migraine, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and intracranial space-occupying lesions) can cause unremitting headaches, IH should be particularly suspected in pediatric patients with postural headaches following spinal intervention.

IH pathophysiology stems from CSF leakage secondary to iatrogenic or spontaneous disruption of the spinal dura mater, reducing the total CSF volume. The reduction in CSF volume leads to downward brain displacement during upright postures, thereby stretching pain-sensitive veins and meninges and triggering postural headaches.10 SDH in IH occurs similarly: reduced CSF pressure leads to sagging of the brain and meninges and results in vascular traction and tearing.7

When suspected, workup of IH involves imaging studies including CT imaging of the head, MRI of the brain/spine, radioisotope cisternography, and CT myelography.11 CT imaging of the head is a standard initial test and may demonstrate subdural fluid collections or tentorial herniation but lacks diagnostic power. MRI of the brain/spine may improve diagnosis of IH by demonstrating radiographic abnormalities such as pachymeningeal enchancement, the presence of SDH and hygromas, or extradural collections of CSF in the paraspinal soft tissue localizing the CSF leak.12,13 However, many patients with IH may not demonstrate imaging abnormalities on MRI.14,15 Radioisotope cisternography might detect active CSF leaks12,13 but may not detect intermittent leaks. CT myelography remains the most sensitive and specific test for CSF leak localization.16

Conservative management for CSF leaks involves bed rest, hydration, caffeine, and analgesics. If conservative management fails, an EBP, either targeted or blind, can be performed over the lumbar region. EBPs restore lost CSF volume and stop the leak while forming a hemostatic clot for long-term sealing,17 with efficacy rates of 85%–90%, increasing to 98% with repeat procedures.18–20

EBPs primarily act through two complementary mechanisms that are staggered in time. The immediate therapeutic impact can be explained by the mass effect theory. The mass effect theory posits that the injection of blood products into the epidural space pressurizes the spinal compartment of the subarachnoid space.21 As such, CSF becomes displaced to the more cephalad cranial compartment.22 This results in normalization of intracranial CSF volume. Furthermore, having the patient maintain a supine position for 1–2 hours after the EBP additionally reduces the negative pressure gradient in the lumbar epidural space. The delayed therapeutic impact can be explained by the epidural plug theory.21 The epidural plug theory posits that the interactions between the injected blood and procoagulant components of the leaking CSF result in the formation of a plug that seals off the dural tear and allows the natural healing process to restore the integrity of the dura.21,23,24 Indeed, MRI studies have confirmed the presence of this tamponade effect as early as 30 minutes after the EBP.25 The subsequent clot resolution also leaves a thick residual layer of mature clot over the dorsal part of the thecal sac that typically presents between 7 and 13 hours after the EBP.21,25 Despite resolution of the clot over time, the blood products can remain in the epidural space several days to several years after EBP.26

The optimal timing for EBP intervention remains a subject of debate in the literature.27 Studies have demonstrated that EBPs performed within 24,28 48,29 and 9630 hours of the suspected dural puncture may have increased failure rates. However, these investigations described observational case series with confounding factors such as heterogeneous patient populations (male and female, obstetric and nonobstetric), varying needle sizes, and different procedures performed. These variables might contribute to the severity of the headache, which could be reflective of the size of the dural leak.29,30 Urgent or early (< 24 hours) EBPs may be warranted in patients with postdural puncture cranial neurological findings31 or severe symptomatology.27 On the opposite end, there does not appear to be an outer limit for the effectiveness of an EBP.32 Therefore, in the absence of standardized guidelines for the timing of EBP, clinical decision-making should be tailored to the individual patient. This becomes even more important in patients with SDHs developing secondary to the CSF leak.

In our case, the patient was observed for several weeks with conservative management before an EBP was pursued. Indeed, postdural puncture headache symptoms can resolve spontaneously in more than two-thirds of patients within 1–2 weeks following conservative management.33 Our approach was further influenced by the patient’s stable SDH on radiographic imaging, the absence of concerning neurological deterioration, and the presence of thrombocytopenia, which deterred earlier intervention. Additionally, the lack of definitive radiographic findings to support the diagnosis of CSF leak initially complicated the decision-making process. Continued enlargement of the SDH on repeat imaging or demonstration of concerning neurological signs would have prompted an earlier trial of EBP in this patient’s management.

Few studies have outlined the approach to managing pediatric patients presenting with SDH secondary to a CSF leak.9 Although SDH evacuation alone has led to symptomatic resolution in some cases,34,35 SDH recurrence and subsequent clinical deterioration may result if the underlying CSF leak is not controlled.36,37 Therefore, the key in managing patients presenting with postural headaches and nontraumatic SDHs is recognizing an underlying CSF leak driving the pathogenesis.38 If IH is strongly suspected despite negative imaging, treating the CSF leak (i.e., EBP) should be considered before SDH evacuation in both adult and pediatric patients. However, when a patient with IH becomes obtunded and SDHs with mass effect are demonstrated on radiographic imaging, atraumatic SDH evacuation should be pursued.34,39 Nevertheless, in these complex situations, it is important to be aware of the risk of persistent or recurrent SDHs if the underlying CSF leak is left untreated.

While SDHs have been reported in pediatric patients with leukemia,40,41 several characteristics in our case strongly suggested IH as the primary etiology rather than a leukemia-related complication. Patients with leukemia-associated SDHs may present with mild to moderate symptoms ranging from headaches to altered mental status, seizures, or focal neurological deficits.40,41 Leukemia-associated SDHs occur in the context of leukocytosis, thrombocytopenia, or coagulopathy.40,41 In contrast, the patient in our clase presented with headaches that were distinctly postural in nature, a hallmark feature associated with IH. Furthermore, the temporal relationship between his LP and symptom onset strongly implicated a CSF leak rather than an acute or chronic leukemia-driven process. While our patient did have AML, his platelet counts remained above 50,000/μl and he had no other coagulopathies, reducing the likelihood of a spontaneous hemorrhagic complication. Additionally, the dramatic response to EBP with resolution of both his symptoms and the SDH provided additional evidence that the CSF leak was the primary pathophysiological mechanism. This case further emphasizes the importance of recognizing the clinical patterns that may differentiate post-LP IH from leukemia-associated SDH in pediatric oncology patients, as management approaches may differ.

Technical Considerations

The EBP needle entry site is determined by the known or expected location of the dural tear. In our case, MRI lumbar of the spine was negative, complicating the leak localization. Despite this, previous studies have suggested that 15-ml EBPs can spread up to six spinal segments cephalad and three segments caudad.42 MRI studies have similarly suggested that 20-ml EBPs can spread on average > 4.6 interspaces and create a ventral displacement of the thecal sac. Additionally, it had been demonstrated that the blood products spread initially along a craniocaudal direction, then circumferentially through the anterior epidural space, and finally through the intervertebral foramina into the paravertebral space.43 Therefore, L3–4 was selected for the EBP, aligning with the site of the LP. Blind EBP success rates vary,44 but reach approximately 90% when the CSF leakage site is undetermined.45 Future studies should evaluate blind versus targeted EBPs in pediatric CSF leaks.

Lessons

Our case demonstrated successful SDH treatment in a pediatric patient with suspected post-LP IH, despite equivocal MRI findings, by EBP alone. Clinical suspicion for IH in these cases is crucial as failure to recognize an underlying CSF leak may result in deterioration. When the clinical picture suggests IH, EBP should be considered in similar cases for pediatric patients.
