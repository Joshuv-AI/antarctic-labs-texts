Mitophagy: a novel avenue for herbal medicines alleviating myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury
Authors: Mengqi Liao, Ling Men, Ming Gong, Yuanyuan Li, Yan Wang, Desheng Xu, Jienan Luan, Hua Zhou, Mengnan Liu, Mingtai Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMyocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury (MIRI) has a high incidence and is difficult to cure. Studies have shown that mitophagy is the key mechanism. This review systematically summarizes all documented herbal preparations and bioactive monomers targeting mitophagy for MIRI treatment, which may serve as a valuable reference for future research on herbal medicine-mediated mitophagy regulation. We conducted comprehensive literature searches in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and CNKI databases using the keywords “cardiovascular diseases,” “mitophagy,” “myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury,” “herbal medicine,” “mechanism,” and “therapeutic” for studies published within the last five years up to July 2025. Studies on herbal medicine interventions unrelated to mitophagy were excluded. Our analysis reveals that mitophagy plays a crucial role in attenuating the detrimental effects of MIRI. Furthermore, herbal medicine demonstrates therapeutic efficacy in maintaining homeostatic balance of mitophagy during MIRI. Herbal medicines can precisely regulate mitophagy via the PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1)-parkin pathway, and modulate the expression of BCL2 interacting protein 3 (BNIP3), FUN14 domain-containing protein 1 (FUNDC1), NIP3-like protein X (NIX). Herbal medicines exert protective effects against MIRI through diverse mechanisms and signaling pathways by targeting mitophagy. While mitophagy represents a promising frontier for future cardiovascular research, current herbal medicine applications remain predominantly confined to animal and cellular models, with only limited clinical translation. The findings presented herein are anticipated to provide clinicians and cardiovascular researchers with valuable therapeutic strategies and novel research directions.Keywords: Mitophagy, Autophagy, Herbal medicine, Myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury, Treatment

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading global cause of mortality,with substantially higher mortality than cancer [1]. Furthermore, the incidence and mortality rates of CVD continue to rise [2]. The current estimation suggests that there are approximately 330 million CVD patients in China [3], with an annual mortality rate of approximately 18.6 million [4]. Timely reperfusion therapy is effective in treating coronary heart disease, a form of ischemic heart disease resulting from coronary artery occlusion [5]. However, reperfusion can lead to myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury (MIRI) due to the activation of various intracellular cascades upon the restoration of blood flow [6]. Despite advancements in revascularization technology that have reduced mortality rates associated with myocardial infarction (MI), no pharmaceutical interventions are currently available for preventing reperfusion injury [2]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to address the prevention and mitigation of MIRI to minimize postoperative complications and increase survival rates.

The pathogenesis of MIRI is closely linked to apoptosis, oxidative stress, mitophagy, calcium overload, and other processes [7]. Among these, apoptosis is a genetically regulated process of programmed cell death that orchestrates the orderly clearance of damaged or senescent cells through the activation of specific signaling pathways [8]. Oxidative stress refers to an imbalance where the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) exceeds the body’s antioxidant defense capacity, leading to oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA [9].Mitochondria constitute 32% of the total volume of cardiomyocytes [10] and serve as critical sites for cellular oxidation and energy conversion [11]. Mitochondrial damage is a pivotal factor contributing to MIRI [12], as damaged mitochondria are the primary source of toxic reactive oxygen species (ROS). The accumulation of ROS can lead to DNA damage, a reduction in the mitochondrial membrane potential, and the promotion of lipid and protein oxidation [13]. Therefore, timely removal of damaged and excess mitochondria is crucial.

Mitophagy is the process by which cells selectively encapsulate and degrade damaged or aging mitochondria through the activation of specific pathways or proteins, thereby maintaining mitochondrial and intracellular homeostasis [14]. Unfortunately,few limited studies have summarized the impact of alterations in mitophagy on MIRI. Currently, there is no widely recognized effective therapy for alleviating MIRI in clinical practice; primarily, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory drugs have been used, which might exhibit certain efficacy [15] but fail to address the underlying cause while potentially causing side effects at considerable cost.

Herbal medicine has been utilized for the treatment of heart disease since ancient times and has gained increasing popularity globally, particularly in the context of CVDs [16]. The multicomponent, multipathway, multitarget nature of herbal medicine, coupled with its therapeutic efficacy and safety, has garnered significant research interest [17]. Our findings indicate that, although herbal medicine monomers and preparations target mitophagy similarly, their mechanisms differ significantly, either by promoting or inhibiting mitophagy. In this review, we aim to elucidate the mechanisms by which herbal medicines modulate mitophagy in MIRI and explore the intricate relationship between mitophagy and MIRI. We also address the current challenges and limitations in applying herbal interventions to alleviate MIRI, while proposing potential solutions. These insights offer invaluable references for future research in this field.

Type of mitophagy

Mitophagy can be categorized into PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1)/parkin-dependent and PINK1/parkin-independent types on the basis of their reliance on the PINK1/parkin signaling axis [18]. The latter type primarily operates through receptors and is thus also referred to as receptor-mediated mitophagy, as illustrated in Fig. 1. PINK1-parkin dependent type

In 2003, genetic complementation experiments in Drosophila melanogaster demonstrated for the first time that PINK1 and PRKN collaborate to regulate mitochondrial quality control. Drosophila with nonfunctional PRKN mutations exhibit muscle degeneration, motor deficits, and mitochondrial dysfunction, indicating the involvement of PRKN in the regulation of mitochondrial mass [19]. Subsequent investigations revealed that overexpression of PRKN resulted in male sterility and mitochondrial defects in PINK1-mutated flies; however, overexpression of Pink1 did not rescue the phenotype of PRKN mutants, suggesting a sequential relationship between PINK1 and PRKN within the same pathway [20].

The concept of "mitophagy" was initially proposed in 2005 [21]. As a form of selective autophagy, mitophagy involves the aggregation of specific receptor proteins on the surface of mitochondria and the activation of distinct signaling pathways tailored to ensure prompt and precise initiation in response to diverse stimuli [18].

During the process of mitophagy, PINK1 (located on the outer membrane of mitochondria) collaborates with PRKN/parkin (located in the cytoplasm) to selectively target damaged mitochondria for degradation within lysosomes. Numerous studies have demonstrated the pivotal role of PINK1 in regulating mitophagy [20].

PINK1 is a Ser/Thr kinase comprising an N-terminal signal for targeting to the mitochondrial matrix, a putative transmembrane domain, and a serine/other amino acid. parkin functions as an E3 ubiquitin ligase with four zinc coordination domains, with only RING1 utilizing the classical ubiquitin ligase RING domain structure that interacts with E2 ubiquitin ligases and facilitates the transfer of ubiquitin from E2 to the substrate [22]. Under normal circumstances, the ubiquitin ligase activity of parkin in the cytoplasm is inhibited, and its activation and release require PINK1 signaling [23]. PINK1 specifically ubiquitinates damaged mitochondria by detecting and concentrating on the outer membrane of dysfunctional mitochondria, subsequently signaling to activate parkin which accumulates in the outer membrane of the mitochondria in the cytoplasm. Then, autophagy receptor proteins such as P62/sequestosome 1 (P62/SQSTM1), nuclear dot protein 52 (NDP52), and optineurin (OPTN) aggregate with the cytoplasm, where they bind specifically to the mitochondria. The activation of mitophagy subsequently occurs [22, 24]. p62 only plays a linking role in mitophagy; however, complete knockout of p62 prevents the clearance of damaged mitochondria, indicating the importance of autophagy receptor proteins [25]. Among these proteins, PINK1 acts as a mitochondrial damage sensor while parkin serves as a signal amplifier; together with the ubiquitin chain acting as a signal effector, these proteins collectively accomplish mitophagy [26].

Receptor-mediated mitophagy

Receptor-mediated mitophagy is characterized by a diverse array of proteins that independently recruit light chain 3(LC3)/parkin, in the absence of PINK1 to facilitate mitophagy. For example, BCL2 interacting protein 3 (BNIP3), protein 1 containing the FUN14 domain (FUNDC1), and NIP-3-like protein X (NIX) [27], along with Bcl-2-like protein 13 (BCL2L13), FK506 binding protein 8 (FKBP8) and autophagy-related protein 32 (Atg32), all possess LC3-interacting region (LIR) domains and have been identified as receptors for mitophagy [24, 28, 29].

Both BNIP3 and NIX are homologous proteins located in the outer membrane of mitochondria. BNIP3 primarily regulates mitophagy during hypoxia, potentially inducing excessive mitophagy, whereas NIX is involved in eliminating all mitochondria during red blood cell maturation, thereby preventing excessive mitophagy [30, 31]. Intracellular SCFFBXL4-mediated ubiquitination tightly controls the levels of BNIP3 and NIX to prevent excessive mitochondrial loss and fatal disease [32]. In response to low- oxygen conditions, HIF1a transcription upregulates BNIP3 and NIX to eliminate excess mitochondria and reduce oxygen consumption [33]. PPTC7 functions as a mitophagy sensor, integrating homeostasis and physiological signals to control SCFFBXL4-dependent degradation of BNIP3 and NIX, ensuring mitochondrial quality control homeostasis [32].

FUNDC1 is a mitochondrial outer membrane protein that is similar to BNIP3 in the mediation of hypoxia-induced mitophagy [34]. In the hypoxia reoxygenation (HR) model, overexpression of FUNDC1 in combination with Nod-like receptor X1(NLRX1) led to a reduction in intracellular ROS levels and maintenance of the mitochondrial membrane potential. Furthermore, unphosphorylated FUNDC1 was found to bind to and activate the mitophagy signaling protein NIPSNAP1-2, thereby promoting mitophagy and reducing apoptosis [35]. Additionally, Atg32 serves as a necessary receptor for mitophagy in yeast cells as a mitochondrial outer membrane protein. Upon phosphorylation by casein kinase 2 (CK2), Atg32 facilitates interaction with the adaptor Atg11, leading to the formation of autophagosomes around mitochondria and subsequent activation of mitophagy [36].

Mitophagy plays crucial roles in various biological processes, including inflammation, metabolic adaptation, and cellular reprogramming. However, mitophagy is not entirely beneficial, and excessive mitophagy can result in the accumulation of suboptimal mitochondria, thereby contributing to the development of cardiovascular, neurological, pulmonary, renal, hepatic, muscular and other disorders [37]. Therefore, timely and appropriate removal of damaged and senescent mitochondria is essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis and normal physiological functions.

Dual role of mitophagy in MIRI

Mitophagy imbalance is commonly observed in CVDs, including cardiac hypertrophy, myocardial infarction (MI), heart failure, MIRI, and diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) [38]. The pathogenesis of MIRI remains intricate and incompletely understood, involving complex and multifaceted interactions among genes, molecules, cells, and tissues, driven by the activation of multiple signaling pathways. The intricate interplay between apoptosis, necrosis, autophagy, and inflammation underlies these mechanisms. As depicted in Fig. 2, mitophagy alleviates MIRI through the following mechanisms. Autophagy, which is considered a cellular protective catabolic mechanism, can be induced by ATP depletion, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) activation, calcium overload, and reactive oxygen species (ROS). Early autophagosome formation necessitates high levels of Beclin-1, whose activity can be suppressed by B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL-2) or BCL-extra large (BCL-XL), thereby inhibiting autophagy and exacerbating disease progression [2].

Mechanism by which mitophagy alleviates MIRI. Mitophagy mainly regulates cell necrosis, apoptosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, and autophagy thereby alleviating MIRI

The heart demands substantial energy to meet its physiological demands for continuous contraction and relaxation, underscoring the critical role of healthy mitochondria. Furthermore, normal mitophagy is essential for proper heart differentiation [39]. In cardiomyocytes, both baseline mitophagy and stress-induced mitophagy actively maintain mitochondrial quality and are pivotal in the cellular response to stressors [40].

In the context of MIRI, moderate mitophagy functions as a protective or adaptive mechanism, whereas excessive mitophagy leads to the accumulation of defective mitochondria, ultimately exacerbating the condition by promoting uncontrolled mitochondrial proliferation [41]. In MIRI patients, impaired mitochondrial function results in the accumulation of a PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1) agonist and the activation of parkin, leading to the ubiquitination of mitochondrial proteins, mitochondrial fragmentation, and the induction of mitophagy [42]. Excessive autophagy further enhances the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), causing mitochondrial damage and exacerbating MIRI [43]. Conversely, AMPK activation can address insufficient mitophagy by upregulating OPA1 expression, thereby enhancing both mitochondrial fusion and autophagic processes while reducing myocardial ischemic injury [44]. Mild activation of PINK1-parkin-mediated mitophagy facilitates rapid clearance of ROS, mitigating MIRI-induced damage to cardiomyocytes [45]. Additionally, studies have shown that FUN14 domain-containing protein 1 (FUNDC1) activation enhances overall mitochondrial activity, promotes selective mitophagy, and inhibits apoptotic pathways within mitochondria. This contributes to reduced oxidative stress levels and increased ATP production within cardiomyocytes, while also helping maintain optimal mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby preventing further myocardial damage [46, 47].

Thus, mitophagy plays a paradoxical role in MIRI. Mitophagy-related proteins have the potential to mitigate MIRI by modulating excessive mitophagy; however, they can also exacerbate this process through dysregulation. Therefore, precise regulation of the degree of autophagy is crucial. Notably, high-resolution imaging of H9c2 cells has emerged as a valuable tool for assessing mitophagy levels and discerning whether mitophagy is aberrant, suggesting a novel avenue for investigation [48].

During MIRI progression, ATP production rapidly decreases during the early ischemic stage, leading to the activation of the AMPK pathway and the Unc-51-like kinase (ULK) complex to initiate autophagy. ULK1 also activates the FUNDC1 receptor, initiating mitophagy [49]. In the late reperfusion stage, there is an increase in the mitochondrial translocation of parkin/p62, along with the upregulation of autophagy markers such as microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3B (LC3B), Beclin-1, and lysosome-associated membrane protein 1 (LAMP-1), resulting in increased mitophagy [50]. While early-stage mitophagy may play a protective role, closely monitoring the degree of autophagy in later stages is crucial to prevent excessive mitochondrial degradation, which could compromise cellular energy metabolism and lead to extensive myocardial damage.

Researchers can determine whether herbal medicine regulates mitophagy by enhancing postischemic adaptation or postinjury repair, depending on its administration before or after modeling. Therefore, mitophagy can be categorized into the ischemia and reperfusion stages. Several studies have indicated that mitophagy primarily exerts a protective effect during the reperfusion stage but may not confer protection during the ischemia stage [51]. In the regulation of mitophagy, herbal medicine predominantly plays a reparative role following injury. As summarized in Table 1, 70% of the collected experiments administered drugs before MIRI modeling, while 20% delivered treatments after modeling, and a minority (10%) employed concurrent pre-and-post modeling administration. This finding provides actionable guidance for optimizing dosing timelines in future MIRI therapeutic studies.

Herbal medicine regulates mitophagy in the treatment of MIRI

Herbal medicine refers to the utilization of natural herbs and their derivatives for the prevention and treatment of diseases in accordance with the fundamental principles of herbal medicine. Numerous studies have demonstrated that, in comparison to conventional single-component and single-target therapies, certain herbal medicines exhibit characteristics of multicomponent, multipathway, and multitarget actions, resulting in significantly enhanced therapeutic effects [17]. Active monomers and compound preparations derived from herbal medicine are frequently involved in regulating mitophagy for the treatment of CVD [52], yielding favorable outcomes. This discussion focuses on the regulation of mitophagy by herbal medicine for the treatment of MIRI. Notably, recent research achievements within the past five years are presented in tabular format, with separate discussions on both activation and inhibition of mitophagy.

Herbal medicine monomer

Activation of mitophagy

Resveratrol

Resveratrol, a polyphenol plant antitoxin, possesses antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and anticancer properties [53]. It has been observed that the application of 20 µM resveratrol to neonatal rats after H/R can effectively regulate mitochondrial function, fission fusion, and biosynthesis. Additionally, it significantly enhanced parkin levels, induced strong interaction between parkin and p62, and promoted mitophagy in cardiomyocytes through a parkin-dependent pathway [54].

Gastrodin (GAS)

Gastrodin (GAS), the primary bioactive compound derived from Gastrodia elata, exhibits beneficial effects in CVDs by reducing blood pressure, regulating blood lipids, and exerting antithrombotic properties [55]. In the context of myocardial injury, elevated serum levels of creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) and Cardiac troponin I (cTnI) serve as markers, and it was observed that compared to the I/R group, the GAS group showed a significant decrease in these measurements. Furthermore, GAS demonstrated a protective role in cardiomyocytes by attenuating mitochondrial structural and functional damage while promoting mitophagy primarily through activation of the PINK1/parkin pathway [56].

Panax notoginseng saponins (PNS)

Panax notoginseng, a renowned herbal medicine, possesses the capability to disperse blood stasis, reduce swelling, and alleviate pain. Panax notoginseng saponins (PNS) are the bioactive constituents of Panax notoginseng, with ginsenosides and PNS R1 being the primary active components. These components are commonly utilized in the treatment of ischemic diseases, particularly in addressing myocardial ischemia [57]. In a rat MIRI model constructed by the researchers, it was observed that intraperitoneal injection of PNS could decrease levels of CK, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), Malondialdehyde (MDA) and ROS while increasing Superoxide dismutase (SOD) levels in rats. This led to alleviation of pathological changes in myocardial tissue and mitochondria as well as an increase in the expression of LC3, hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α), BNIP3, Atg5 and Beclin-1 in rats. Furthermore, mitophagy was enhanced and promoted through these mechanisms to mitigate MIRI damage [58].

Inhibition of mitophagy

Orientin

Orientin, a flavonoid compound found in the whole plant and rhizome of Odontosoria chinensis, is one of the primary active ingredients in Miaoyadu wood oil. It exhibits protective effects on the cardiovascular system [59]. In MIRI rats, orientin significantly downregulated the expression levels of P62, parkin and BNIP3, attenuated the interaction between P62 and parkin, inhibited excessive activation of parkin-mediated mitophagy through receptor-mediated pathways, and mitigated myocardial apoptosis, exerting a protective effect [60].

Astragaloside IV

Astragaloside, the primary bioactive compound in Astragalus membranaceus, exhibits anti-inflammatory properties and cardiomyocyte protection. In a study utilizing a high-fat rat model of MIRI, astragaloside was administered intragastrically at a dose of 30 mg/kg. The results demonstrated a significant decrease in the expression of PINK1 and parkin proteins, leading to alleviation of the inflammatory response. These findings suggested that astragaloside exerts protective effects on cardiomyocytes by inhibiting Pink1-parkin-mediated mitophagy [61].

Both activates and inhibits mitophagy

Ginsenoside Rg1(GRg1)

Panax ginseng is a valuable medicinal substance with various effects such as promoting vitality, regulating pulse and fluid balance, improving blood circulation, and calming the mind. It is widely used in clinical practice due to its remarkable efficacy. Ginsenoside Rg1 (GRg1), the primary bioactive compound extracted from Panax ginseng, exhibits anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, and anti-aging properties [62]. In a mouse model of left anterior descending artery ligation, GRg1 has been shown to enhance silent information regulator sirtuin 1 (SIRT1)/PINK1/ parkin-mediated mitophagy, reduce cardiac mitochondrial damage, and decrease left ventricular dilation and cardiac fibrosis, thereby improving cardiac remodeling [63]. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that ginsenoside Rg1 significantly reduces the ratio of LC3-II/LC3-I and Beclin-1 expression in hypoxic cardiomyocytes while inhibiting hypoxia-induced autophagy and excessive mitophagy. Furthermore, it protected cardiomyocytes from ischemia/reperfusion injury by activating the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT/mammalian target of the rapamycin (mTOR) signaling pathway [64].

Berberine (BBR)

Berberine, an odorless yellow powder, is an isoquinoline alkaloid that can be extracted from Coptis chinensis [65]. In MIRI rat models, BBR has been shown to enhance the HIF-1a/BNIP3 pathway by increasing the LC3II/I ratio and NIX expression, thereby promoting mitophagy, inducing cardiomyocyte proliferation, inhibiting cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and collectively mitigating H/R damage of H9c2 cells [66]. LDH and MDA are biomarkers for assessing the extent of I/R injury. Pretreatment with BBR can attenuate the release of LDH and MDA, thus reducing the injury. Further investigations have revealed that BBR can suppress the expression of autophagy-related proteins BNIP3 and Beclin-1 through its antioxidant activity, thereby inhibiting excessive autophagy activation during I/R and protecting cardiomyocytes by promoting cardiac angiogenesis, among other mechanisms to alleviate MIRI damage [67].

Herbal medicine compounds

Activation of mitophagy

Tongxinluo capsule (TXL)

The Tongxinluo capsule was developed by Academician Wu Yiling’s team as a representative drug for the treatment of cerebrovascular disease (CCVD). It exerts its main effects through promoting qi and blood circulation while clearing coagulates and relieving pain [68]. In adult male rats subjected to MIRI-induced damage, it was observed that increasing doses of TXL resulted in a corresponding increase in its efficacy in alleviating MIRI. High doses of TXL were found to mitigate mitochondrial damage caused by MIRI such as ridge swelling and disappearance as well as mitochondrial membrane destruction and disintegration. Furthermore, high dose TXL was shown to enhance mitophagy via stimulation of the PINK1/parkin pathway, leading to increased expression of mitochondrial LC3-II.

Additionally, TXL demonstrated an ability to reduce the apoptosis index, myocardial infarction size and cTnI levels following MIRI, thereby conferring protection on cardiomyocytes. Notably high doses of TXL exhibited similar effects to atorvastatin (a statin known for regulating autophagy), whereas chloroquine (an autophagy inhibitor) attenuated the efficacy of TXL, further supporting its role in promoting mitophagy [69].

Shenmai injection(SMI)

Shenmai injection (SMI) is a herbal medicine injection containing water extracts of Panax ginseng and Ophiopogon japonicus, with its main active ingredients being ginsenoside Rg1, ginsenoside Re, ginsenoside Rb1, and ophiopogon D [70]. It has been observed that pretreatment with 1–10 μL/mL SMI can concentration-dependently enhance myocardial cell survival and mitigate the adverse effects of H/R injury, including increased mitochondrial mass, decreased mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), impaired mitochondrial respiration, and cytoplasmic Ca2+ overload. Following SMI pretreatment, there was an increase in the expression of mitofusin 1(MFN1), MFN2 and ortho-phthalaldehyde (OPA) fusion proteins while the expression of dynamin-related protein (DRP) and fission (FIS) protein decreased. This suggested that SMI promotes mitochondrial fusion and inhibits excessive fission to protect mitochondria. Furthermore, SMI induced mitophagy by upregulating the expression of LC3, Beclin1, parkin and PINK proteins. Ultimately, it restored left ventricular function and reduced the myocardial infarction area to protect cardiomyocytes and alleviate MIRI [71].

Inhibition of mitophagy

Shenyuan Dan (SYD)

Shenyuan Dan (SYD) is a compound preparation of herbal medicine, consisting of eight types of herbal medicine including Salvia miltiorrhiza, Scrophularia ningpoensis, Astragalus membranaceus, Adenophora stricta, Corydalis yanhusuo, Hirudo nipponica, Pheretima aspergillum and Eupolyphaga sinensis [72]. The experiment involved four groups: control group, H/R model group, H/R model + 3MA group and H/R model + SYD group. It was determined that the optimal nontoxic concentration of SYD was 100 μg/mL. Therefore, the fourth group of H9c2 cells was pretreated with 100 μg/mL SYD for 6 h before being subjected to H/R stimulation. The results indicated that SYD reduced LC3, PINK1 and parkin protein levels, suggesting its potential in preventing excessive mitophagy by inhibiting the PINK1/parkin pathway. Furthermore, SYD increased the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) level in H/R cardiomyocytes, leading to enhanced mitochondrial networking and increased distribution and quantity of mitochondria as well as elevated ATP content and activated SOD activity, which ultimately protected cardiomyocytes and reduced MIRI damage [73].

Shuangshenningxin capsule(SSNX)

Shuangshenningxin capsule (SSNX) is a herbal medicine preparation containing Panax ginseng, Salvia miltiorrhiza and Corydalis yanhusuo, with its main active components being ginsenoside, salvia salvianolic acid, and corydalis alkaloid. SSNX is clinically utilized for the treatment of"chest pain and heartache" associated with Qi deficiency and blood stasis syndrome, demonstrating beneficial effects in promoting blood circulation, removing blood stasis, and alleviating pain [74]. Through the establishment of an MIRI miniature pig model and administration at a dosage of 20 mg/kg, it was observed that SSNX can reduce myocardial infarction size while preserving myocardial ATP content. Additionally, it was found to decrease serum CK, cTnT, and LDH levels, indicating that SSNX protected cardiomyocytes, improved cardiac function, and prevented myocardial edema and inflammatory infiltration. Furthermore, SSNX has been shown to enhance mitochondrial membrane potential while inhibiting mitophagy through downregulation of PINK1/parkin/FUNDC1/BNIP3-related proteins in MI/RI miniature pigs [75].

Differences and transitions between basic experiments and clinical applications

The research findings on herbal medicine discussed above primarily derive from cell and animal models, and their extrapolation to clinical practice faces significant limitations. These limitations stem from fundamental differences in model systems, disease comorbidity backgrounds, and the metabolic pathways of herbal agents in the body between humans and experimental subjects. Clinically, MIRI often occurs in patients with underlying conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and atherosclerosis, whereas experimental models typically feature simplified backgrounds that fail to replicate the multiple comorbidities and complex interaction networks seen in human patients. In MIRI animal models, ischemia and reperfusion times are predetermined by researchers, whereas human myocardial infarction involves uncertain vascular occlusion durations and variable collateral circulation, resulting in greater damage heterogeneity. In vitro, the H9c2 rat embryonic cardiomyocyte line differs significantly from terminally differentiated adult human cardiomyocytes in metabolic characteristics and response mechanisms. Furthermore, H/R models only simulate the core process of ischemia–reperfusion, lacking key in vivo complexities such as hemodynamic changes and intercellular interactions. The therapeutic characteristic of herbal medicine lies in its multi-target regulation, whereas current animal/cellular models typically validate only predetermined specific pathways. This approach fails to fully replicate the comprehensive mechanisms of herbal medicine in humans and its potential unknown targets, while also overlooking interactions with clinically co-administered drugs (such as antiplatelet agents and β-blockers). Furthermore, herbal preparations exhibit heterogeneity due to variations between batches, differences in compositional profiles of raw materials from various regions, and inter-individual metabolic transformations. Therefore, results from animal/cellular studies cannot be simplistically equated to clinical efficacy.

However, these animal and cellular models remain critically important as they elucidate mechanisms, identify therapeutic targets, and play an indispensable role in preliminary drug screening, providing scientific hypotheses and foundational evidence for clinical translation. To bridge the gap between basic research and clinical application, subsequent studies should prioritize advancing well-characterized herbal monomers with clinical potential – exemplified by gastrodin, which mitigates MIRI through the PINK1/parkin pathway to enhance mitophagy during the perioperative period [56]. Additionally, research should deepen investigations into herbal medicine effects within complex MIRI pathological environments to better simulate clinical conditions, while advancing studies on nanocarriers and targeted delivery systems to improve bioavailability, reduce inter-individual absorption variability, and enhance clinical efficacy. Finally, rigorous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) incorporating mechanistic research should be designed to strengthen credibility regarding clinical effectiveness and modes of action.

Discussion

MIRI is categorized under the term “chest obstruction” in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), with its fundamental pathogenesis attributed to a deficiency of “Qi” (essential life energy) and an excess of pathogenic factors, alongside the concurrent presence of deficiency and excess conditions. Consequently, the core therapeutic principle in treating MIRI should concentrate on reinforcing the body’s constitution and eradicating pathogenic influences [76]. Herbal medications possess the capability to augment mitophagy, facilitating the removal of damaged mitochondria, analogous to “Quxie (dispelling pathogens),” while concurrently inhibiting excessive mitophagy to uphold normal cellular metabolic processes, akin to “Fuzheng” (strengthening the body’s resistance). This dual regulatory mechanism of herbal medicines is comprehensively manifested throughout the treatment regimen. In contrast to herbal remedies, current medical interventions primarily rely on antioxidants [77], calcium antagonists, endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) inhibitors [78, 79], mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening inhibitors [80], inflammatory response inhibitors [81], and pyroptosis inhibitors [82] for addressing MIRI. However, these pharmacological agents frequently fall short of achieving optimal therapeutic efficacy due to their associated toxic side effects. Conversely, herbal medicines exhibit considerable therapeutic promise owing to their multicomponent nature and their capacity to target multiple biological pathways while maintaining consistent efficacy and safety. A growing number of studies have demonstrated the capacity of herbal medicines to modulate mitophagy via the PINK1/parkin signaling pathway. Furthermore, autophagy receptors, including BNIP3, FUNDC1, NIX, BCL2L13, and Atg32, have also been recognized for their pivotal roles in regulating the effects of herbal medicines.

Studies have shown that mitochondrial damage could manifest as early as 20 min following myocardial ischemia, with the extent of damage progressively intensifying with extended reperfusion durations [83]. Recent research findings have indicated that in the management of MIRI, mitophagy might, in most instances, suppress the formation of the NLRP3 inflammasome; however, excessive mitophagy has the potential to exacerbate inflammatory responses, thereby aggravating MIRI [84]. Therefore, regulating and monitoring intracellular mitophagy levels and identifying the optimal equilibrium point for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis are crucial for modulating cardiac energy metabolism. The aforementioned herbal medicines have exhibited efficacy in safeguarding cardiomyocytes and alleviating MIRI, albeit through distinct mechanisms of action. Specific herbal medicines, such as resveratrol, gastrodin, and Tongxinluo capsule, predominantly exerted their therapeutic benefits by promoting mitophagy. Conversely, others, like orientin and polygonum multiflorum extract, inhibit mitophagy. Additionally, certain herbal medicines, including ginsenosides and berberine, demonstrate both activating and inhibitory effects on mitophagy, thereby underscoring the unique bidirectional regulatory attributes of herbal medicines.

The aforementioned studies indicated that the timing of herbal medicine intervention in MIRI is intricately associated with diverse factors, including, but not restricted to, the model type, species employed in experimentation, and route of administration. When establishing in vivo MIRI models, it is customary to initiate herbal medicine administration seven days prior to surgery for rats subjected to intraperitoneal injection or intragastric administration. In the case of mice that received an intraperitoneal injection, the duration of administration was relatively brief, often occurring 15 min or three days before surgery. Conversely, in vitro H/R models typically involve a pretreatment period of up to 24 h. Additionally, there were instances where postoperative administration was necessary, necessitating flexible adjustments on the basis of the specific experimental design and conditions.

Preliminary comparisons indicate that the bidirectional regulatory effects of herbal medicine are influenced by the experimental species, administration timing, administration route, and dosage. Notably, the dosage required to activate mitophagy is generally greater than that needed for mitophagy inhibition, warranting further investigation into the underlying mechanisms involved.

An increasing number of people are utilizing herbal medicines to treat cardiovascular diseases. For example, Tongxinluo capsules have demonstrated significant efficacy in STEMI patients at both 30 days and 1 year through randomized controlled trial validation. Its mechanism is closely associated with mitigating MIRI and protecting the microcirculation [85].

Regarding clinical research on mitophagy, several clinical trial registries were searched, including the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR), ClinicalTrials.gov (US), and the ISRCTN registry. Only three public clinical studies related to mitophagy were identified. The first study (Registration number:ChiCTR2400084612, https://www.chictr.org.cn/)investigated the role of serum mtDNA and the mechanism of mitophagy in ischemia–reperfusion injury among STEMI patients before and after PCI. Eligible participants had to meet WHO diagnostic criteria for myocardial infarction, present with ischemic chest pain lasting > 30 min, and show ST-segment elevation on ECG. Patients aged 30–75 years were stratified based on PCI intervention. Primary outcome measures included mitochondrial DNA copy number, interleukin-6 levels, and cytochrome c concentrations. The second study (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT05770349, https://clinicaltrials.gov/)examined the ultrastructural characteristics of mitochondria in cardiomyocytes during heart failure. It enrolled patients with HFrEF or HFmrEF (left ventricular ejection fraction < 50%) and obstructive multivessel coronary atherosclerosis requiring surgical coronary blood flow correction. Participants were required to be ≥ 18 years old. Key outcome measures included mitochondrial DNA copy number, serum cytochrome c concentration, and mitochondrial DNA haplogroup K affiliation. The third study (ClinicalTrials.gov ID:NCT05708547, https://clinicaltrials.gov/)explored the role of mitophagy in myeloid cells in coronary atherosclerosis. It included adult patients (≥ 18 years) with atherosclerotic coronary artery disease scheduled for isolated coronary artery bypass grafting with extracorporeal circulation. Mitophagy levels were primarily assessed via flow cytometry. All three studies were designed as prospective cohorts, required informed consent, and are currently ongoing. However, clinical research specifically investigating the modulation of mitophagy as a therapeutic target—particularly for herbal medicine in preventing MIRI—remains scarce, warranting further exploration.

Currently, the regulation of mitophagy in the treatment of MIRI using herbal medicine remains in the nascent stages of exploration, with several limitations that require further scrutiny and resolution: (1) Precise control of mitophagy postadministration is challenging, potentially resulting in unpredictable myocardial damage and increased treatment uncertainty. (2) Existing studies have focused predominantly on the classical PINK1/parkin pathway, whereas research on alternative mechanisms of mitophagy is relatively rare and lacks a comprehensive understanding. (3) The herbal medicines utilized for regulating mitophagy in MIRI treatment are limited, necessitating enhancements in therapeutic efficacy and potential. (4) Current research on herbal medicine for MIRI has focused primarily on basic studies, whereas clinical research remains relatively scarce. Moreover, existing animal-based investigations tend to emphasize pathological mechanisms without adequately considering TCM syndrome differentiation, thereby limiting the clinical efficacy of these findings.

To address these challenges, the field of herbal medicine may offer innovative solutions: (1) Researchers should continue exploring novel herbal alternatives and bioactive compounds, while developing assays to measure mitophagy levels. This will help maximize the bidirectional modulatory effects of herbal medicine on mitophagy, thereby appropriately activating this process to mitigate further cellular damage. (2) Efforts should be made to elucidate the molecular mechanism of mitophagy in MIRI, identify more potent therapeutic targets through herbal medicine and further refine the regulatory mechanism of mitophagy. (3) Researchers should be encouraged to innovate by advancing herbal medicine research methods using modern scientific and technological approaches, such as nanotechnology, thereby broadening the scope of herbal medicine research to facilitate unexpected discoveries and breakthroughs. (4) Emphasis should be placed on clinical research, with increased implementation of large-sample randomized controlled trials. When the holistic concept of TCM is integrated, it is essential to organically combine the disease differentiation of Western medicine with the syndrome differentiation of TCM to enhance clinical outcomes.

The currently included studies may not have captured unpublished negative results or grey literature, thus the positive conclusions regarding the efficacy of herbal medicine in preventing and treating MIRI carry certain limitations. More comprehensive, detailed, and rigorously designed research is required to conduct multifaceted evaluations that would yield more objective conclusions.

This review examines the clinically relevant mechanisms and therapeutic effects of herbal medicine in regulating mitophagy for treating MIRI, aiming to foster innovation and advancement in cardiovascular research. We anticipate future clinical studies will increasingly target “mitophagy regulation” as an efficacy benchmark for herbal medicine interventions against MIRI, thereby providing viable therapeutic alternatives characterized by enhanced efficacy and reduced adverse effects.
