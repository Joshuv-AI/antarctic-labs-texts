Peroxisomal homeostasis in metabolic diseases and its implication in ferroptosis
Authors: Jiwei Han, Daheng Zheng, Pu-Ste Liu, Shanshan Wang, Xin Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeroxisomes are dynamic organelles involved in various cellular processes, including lipid metabolism, redox homeostasis, and intracellular metabolite transfer. Accumulating evidence suggests that peroxisomal homeostasis plays a crucial role in human health and disease, particularly in metabolic disorders and ferroptosis. The abundance and function of peroxisomes are regulated by a complex interplay between biogenesis and degradation pathways, involving peroxins, membrane proteins, and pexophagy. Peroxisome-dependent lipid metabolism, especially the synthesis of ether-linked phospholipids, has been implicated in modulating cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis, a newly discovered form of iron-dependent cell death. This review discusses the current understanding of peroxisome homeostasis, its roles in redox regulation and lipid metabolism, and its implications in human diseases. We also summarize the main mechanisms of ferroptosis and highlight recent discoveries on how peroxisome-dependent metabolism and signaling influence ferroptosis sensitivity. A better understanding of the interplay between peroxisomal homeostasis and ferroptosis may provide new insights into disease pathogenesis and reveal novel therapeutic strategies for peroxisome-related metabolic disorders and ferroptosis-associated diseases.Keywords: Peroxisome, Homeostasis, Lipid metabolism, Redox regulation, Ferroptosis, Metabolic disorders, Therapeutic strategies

Introduction

As a highly conserved organelle in eukaryotic cells, peroxisomes play crucial roles in cellular metabolism, redox homeostasis maintenance, intracellular metabolite transfer, and the development of human metabolic diseases [1]. There is growing scientific interest in the physiological role of peroxisomes in human health and disease. Peroxisomes are important metabolic organelles that contribute significantly to cellular lipid metabolism, participating in processes such as fatty acid β-oxidation and the synthesis of ether glycerophospholipids [2, 3]. They work in concert with other organelles, particularly mitochondria, to maintain lipid homeostasis. In addition, peroxisomes are crucial for hydrogen peroxide decomposition [2, 3]. Moreover, peroxisomes interact with other organelles to regulate a variety of cellular physiological processes. For example, extensive communication between peroxisomes and mitochondria coordinates comprehensive lipid catabolism and anabolism [2]. The peroxisome-anchored mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) cooperates with mitochondrial signaling to mount antiviral innate immunity [4]. However, the mechanisms by which cells integrate different signals to regulate peroxisome homeostasis remain enigmatic.

Peroxisomes are highly dynamic organelles that exhibit significant differences in number, size, shape, and protein composition across different cell types and physiological states. Under stress conditions, peroxisome gene expression is upregulated, and their morphology often shows elongation [5–7]. The abundance of peroxisomes increases in cancer cells and cells with enhanced lipid catabolism to meet elevated energy demands [2, 8]. Recent studies have shown that an increase in peroxisome abundance induces stem cell differentiation and promotes intestinal epithelial repair, potentially opening a new avenue for treating patients with inflammatory bowel disease [9]. In cancer cells, peroxisome-dependent synthesis of ether-linked phospholipids affects their susceptibility to ferroptosis, which may be exploited as a vulnerability in anticancer strategies [10]. The peroxisome also serves as an intracellular sensor for free fatty acids to regulate lipid homeostasis [11]. Peroxisome malfunction can have severe consequences. Loss of peroxisome function in the pancreas leads to β-cell death and reduced insulin secretion [12]. Furthermore, defects in peroxisomal biogenesis factors result in peroxisome biogenesis disorders, including Zellweger syndrome [13]. Elucidating the interplay between different cellular signaling pathways and peroxisome homeostasis can shed light on the pathogenic mechanisms of peroxisome-related metabolic diseases.

Ferroptosis has been characterized as an iron-dependent form of cell death resulting from lipid peroxide accumulation and the loss of membrane integrity [14]. Recent studies have implicated ferroptosis in a broad spectrum of pathogenic conditions, including inflammation [15], neurodegenerative disorders [16], cardiovascular diseases [17], ischemia/reperfusion injury [18], and cancer treatment [19]. Emerging evidence suggests that peroxisome composition and homeostasis significantly influence ferroptosis sensitivity [10, 20]. In this review, we discuss the current understanding of peroxisome homeostasis, examine its roles in redox regulation and lipid metabolism in the context of human diseases, summarize the main mechanisms of ferroptosis, and highlight recent discoveries regarding peroxisome-dependent metabolism and signaling in modulating ferroptosis sensitivity.

Peroxisome heterogeneity and biogenesis

Peroxisome heterogeneity

The fluctuation of organelle abundance significantly impacts its functional output, and dynamic changes in organelle activity accommodate cell growth and developmental needs [21]. Since its discovery, the peroxisome has been recognized as a highly dynamic organelle with varying numbers and protein contents in different cells. For example, remarkable differences in peroxisomal matrix protein contents between brain and liver cells were reported decades ago [22]. Well-documented functional heterogeneity of peroxisomes exists across different animal species and tissues [23]. Dramatic changes in peroxisomal membrane and matrix proteins were also observed during early fetal development in mammals [24–27]. These alterations in enzymatic activity within peroxisomes may accommodate the increasing metabolic demands during early development [25, 27, 28].

Mechanisms of peroxisome biogenesis

Peroxisomes in mammalian cells are primarily produced through two distinct mechanisms: de novo biosynthesis and asymmetric division [1]. De novo synthesis mainly involves the fusion of mitochondria-derived vesicles and endoplasmic reticulum-derived pre-peroxisomes. The uneven distribution of peroxisome matrix proteins into daughter organelles indicates the asymmetric fission of existing peroxisomes [29]. Cellular peroxisome heterogeneity is further compounded by the asymmetric segregation of peroxisome populations during cell division [30]. Moreover, peroxisomes in cultured cells exhibit significant variations in their protein import capacity over prolonged culture periods [31]. Despite these observations, the implications of peroxisome dynamics and heterogeneity in the biological control of cellular physiology remain largely unknown. Damaged or superfluous peroxisomes are degraded through pexophagy, an autophagy-mediated peroxisome clearance process [31, 32]. The generation and degradation of peroxisomes are highly coordinated to maintain their homeostasis. For example, excessive peroxisome metabolites, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS), can act as negative feedback signals to suppress peroxisome biogenesis and promote pexophagy, thereby achieving functional homeostasis of peroxisomes [33].

Peroxisomal protein import, recycling, and degradation

Peroxisome dynamics involve the integration of peroxisomal membrane proteins (PMPs) and matrix protein import. Peroxisomes uniquely import folded proteins through large membrane pores [34, 35]. Peroxisome-targeted proteins contain peroxisome targeting signals (PTSs): PTS1 and PTS2 for matrix import, and membrane PTS for PMP insertion [1, 34]. Peroxins (Pex proteins) are crucial for peroxisome biogenesis (Fig. 1). PMPs integrate into membranes directly post-translation or indirectly via the endoplasmic reticulum, using Pex3, Pex16, and Pex19 [36, 37]. Pex19 recognizes membrane-targeted proteins, while Pex3/Pex16 serve as docking sites. Super-resolution imaging reveals uneven PMP organization, suggesting functional compartmentalization [38].

Matrix proteins with PTS1 and PTS2 are recognized by Pex5 and Pex7 in the cytoplasm. The cargo-receptor complex docks on the membrane via the Pex13/Pex14 complex, and is imported via the membrane pore formed by Pex protein oligomerization [34, 35, 39]. PTS receptor recycling involves monoubiquitination of N-terminal Cys residues, while polyubiquitylation leads to proteasomal degradation [40, 41]. Pex2, Pex10, and Pex12 mediate ubiquitination for PTS receptor homeostasis [41, 42]. Monoubiquitylated receptors are recruited to a Pex1/Pex6-containing exporting complex. This complex, tethered to Pex26, unfolds and extrudes Pex5 via Pex1/Pex6 ATPase activity [43–45]. Conversely, Pex5 polyubiquitination occurs when recycling is impaired, targeting it for proteasomal degradation as a quality control measure [46].

Peroxisome formation and division

De novo peroxisome formation has been studied in cells lacking Pex3, Pex16, or Pex19. Cells with Pex3 or Pex19 mutations lack mature peroxisomes, but wild-type gene reintroduction restores biogenesis [47]. Pex3 and Pex19 are sequentially recruited to the endoplasmic reticulum to form pre-peroxisomes [47, 48]. Without Pex3 or Pex19, pre-peroxisomal vesicles fail to mature and are degraded by autophagy in yeast [49]. In human fibroblasts lacking Pex3 or Pex16, forced Pex3 expression generates pre-peroxisomal structures from mitochondria, while Pex16 targets the endoplasmic reticulum. Fusion of these vesicles leads to mature peroxisomes [50]. Asymmetric peroxisomal division involves membrane elongation, resulting in uneven daughter peroxisomes [48]. Pex11 (α, β, and γ isoforms) is key for peroxisomal multiplication, remodeling membranes and facilitating curvature before fission [51–54]. Intriguingly, Peroxisomes share fission machinery with mitochondria [55–57]. Pex11β acts as an anchor for fission adaptors, such as Fis1 (fission protein 1), Mff (mitochondrial fission factor), which recruit Drp1 (dynamin-related fission GTPase) for division [55, 57, 58]. Recent evidence shows that Mff is crucial for peroxisome functional maturation, indicating specialized roles of these fission proteins [59].

Functional divergence of peroxisomal proteins

Apart from the Pex proteins and fission machinery, PMPs include multiple metabolite transporters (Fig. 1), such as ABC transporters (ABCDs) for fatty acid uptake, monocarboxylate transporters (MCT1/2) for pyruvate or lactate, organic cation/carnitine transporter 3 (OCTN3), and PMPs (such as PMP34) that are related to the transportation of cofactors like coenzyme A (CoA), nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) [60]. Several lipid metabolism enzymes are also localized in peroxisomal membranes, such as acyl-CoA synthetase long chain family member 1/4 (ASCL1/4), fatty acyl-CoA reductase 1 (FAR1), and fatty aldehyde dehydrogenase (FALDH), which regulate the biosynthesis of ether lipids, coordinate lipid-metabolism between peroxisomes and other organelles, and protect against oxidative stress [61–65]. Additionally, peroxisomal membranes contain mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) and ubiquitin-specific protease 30 (USP30) [66, 67]. As MAVS and USP30 are also localized in mitochondria to regulate antiviral innate immunity and mitophagy, these findings suggest a high level of coordination in antiviral response and autophagy-dependent degradation between mitochondria and peroxisomes. The origins of these membrane proteins remain unclear, and it is possible that they originate from mitochondria- or endoplasmic reticulum-derived pre-peroxisomes. Their relative abundance in peroxisomes and physiological roles in different cells and tissues require further investigation, which could shed light on how peroxisomal membrane dynamics affect human diseases.

Peroxisomal quality control, homeostasis regulation and human diseases

Peroxisomal quality control by pexophagy

Peroxisomal quality is maintained through biogenesis and degradation. In mammalian cells, peroxisomes have a 1.5-2 day turnover, with 70–80% removed via pexophagy, a selective autophagy process [32, 68]. PMP ubiquitination signals pexophagy under stress (Fig. 2). NBR1 and p62 are key autophagic receptors recognizing ubiquitinated PMPs during pexophagy [69, 70]. Artificial PMP monoubiquitination or NBR1 overexpression is sufficient to trigger pexophagy. Pex5 monoubiquitination is a well-established signal for pexophagy. AAA exportomer complex (Pex1/Pex6/Pex26) defects sequester Pex5 on membranes, triggering NBR1-dependent pexophagy [71]. This resembles pexophagy induced by overexpressing Pex5 fused with SV40 large T antigen, which fails to be released from peroxisomal membranes [72]. However, the determinants for mono- and poly-ubiquitination of export-defective Pex5 need further clarification. NBR1 can mediate pexophagy without p62, while p62 enhances NBR1-dependent pexophagy under basal conditions [70]. The functional redundancy of p62 and NBR1 in stress-induced pexophagy requires further investigation.

Pex5 monoubiquitination at K209 triggers pexophagy under oxidative stress, requiring S141 phosphorylation by ataxia-telangiectasia mutated (ATM) kinase [3]. This phosphorylation facilitates K209 ubiquitination by E3 ligases Pex2, Pex10, and Pex12. P62 then targets ubiquitinated peroxisomes to autophagosomes. USP30, a deubiquitinase on peroxisomal membranes, maintains peroxisomal abundance by mitigating Pex5 ubiquitination [66, 73]. Originally identified as a mitochondrial deubiquitinase modulating mitophagy [74], USP30 suppresses basal pexophagy and antagonizes Pex2-induced Pex5 ubiquitination during amino acid deprivation [73]. The mechanisms controlling Pex2 and USP30 activities remain unclear. Various PMPs, including Pex5 and ABCD3, become ubiquitinated upon starvation, suggesting other membrane proteins may signal pexophagy [75].

Under favorable conditions, Pex5 imports matrix proteins and is recycled by the AAA complex [44, 46]. Pex14, the docking site for Pex5-cargo complexes, also participates in starvation-induced pexophagy by interacting with LC3-II on autophagosomes [76]. A competition model exists between LC3-II/Pex14 and Pex5/Pex14 interactions [77]. PTS1-competent Pex5 inhibits Pex14-LC3-II interaction under nutrient-rich conditions, while cargo-free Pex5 during starvation favors this interaction, promoting pexophagy. Ubiquitin-independent pexophagy can also occur through Tankyrase 1/2 (TNKS1/2)-Pex14 interaction. TNKS1/2 associates with Pex14 on peroxisomes, and its ablation suppresses starvation-induced pexophagy [78]. TNKS1/2 may target peroxisomes to autophagosomes via Atg9A interaction [78, 79]. Pex14 thus plays dual roles in matrix protein import and peroxisome degradation during starvation (Figs. 1 and 2). Other PMPs, like overexpressed Pex3, can induce pexophagy by interacting with autophagic receptors like Atg30 [80, 81]. An important question that remains largely unanswered is how different PMPs coordinate their activities to balance peroxisomal biogenesis and pexophagy.

Signaling pathways regulating peroxisome homeostasis

Many genes related to peroxisomal fatty acid β-oxidation and biogenesis are regulated by the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) family of transcription factors [2]. The biogenesis of peroxisomes is also positively modulated by peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α) [82]. Since PGC-1α is a key regulator of mitochondrial activity and metabolism, peroxisomal biogenesis is tightly linked to mitochondrial fitness and metabolic state. In cancer cells, histone deacetylase inhibitor (HDACi) treatment increases PMP expression and the abundance of peroxisomes. Pex3 knockdown promotes HDACi-induced cell death, suggesting a critical role for peroxisomes in conferring drug resistance [83]. Cold exposure also promotes peroxisomal biogenesis in adipose tissues, enhancing thermogenesis and mitochondrial fission [84]. In addition to oxidative stress, defects in PMPs, and starvation, the activation of hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF-2α) has been demonstrated to trigger pexophagy, possibly through promoting peroxisomal ubiquitination by upregulating an E3 ubiquitin ligase [85, 86]. These findings indicate the involvement of the hypoxia signaling pathway in regulating peroxisomal homeostasis. Nevertheless, there is a lack of studies investigating the potential involvement of different cell signaling pathways in controlling the biogenesis and degradation of peroxisomes.

Peroxisomal disorders

Mutations in peroxisomal enzymes or peroxins can lead to peroxisomal disorders. At least 14 Pex genes involved in peroxisomal membrane assembly, matrix protein import, and division are linked to peroxisomal biogenesis disorders (PBDs), including Zellweger syndrome, Heimler syndrome, and rhizomelic chondrodysplasia punctata [13]. PBD patients suffer from progressive metabolic disruption that culminates in developmental abnormalities. Interestingly, the most frequent mutations in PBD patients are found in the AAA exportomer complex (Pex1-Pex6-Pex26), accounting for about 65% of PBD mutations [87]. This may be because the AAA complex not only functions in matrix protein import, but a defective AAA complex also triggers pexophagy to accelerate peroxisomal clearance [71]. Furthermore, autophagy inhibition increases peroxisomal abundance and function in PBD patient fibroblasts harboring the PEX1G843D mutation [71]. Similarly, forced USP30 overexpression suppresses pexophagy and prevents peroxisome loss in the same PBD cell model [73]. These studies suggest that exacerbated pexophagy is a major contributor to peroxisomal loss in PBD syndrome. Loss of peroxisomal function and excessive pexophagy could potentially result in β-cell death in diabetes [12] or neuronal degeneration in Parkinson’s disease [88]. Although the causative relationships between peroxisomal activity and these metabolic disorders are not fully established, it is likely that future studies will uncover versatile roles for peroxisomes in different pathological conditions.

Redox balance and lipid metabolism in peroxisomes

Peroxisomal fatty acid metabolism

Lipid metabolism and the scavenging of reactive oxygen species (ROS) are the pivotal roles of peroxisomes. Mitochondria and peroxisomes are the main organelles for fatty acid β-oxidation. In contrast to mitochondria where linear fatty acids with less than 21 carbons are oxidized, peroxisomes can catabolize a wider range of fatty acids. These include very long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs, more than 22 carbons), long-chain fatty acids, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) such as C24:6 (the precursor of docosahexanoic acid, C22:6) and C20:4 (n-6, also known as arachidonic acid), branched-chain fatty acids, and dicarboxylic acids [2, 89, 90]. VLCFAs are imported by membrane transporters ABCD1/2, while long-chain fatty acids and branched-chain fatty acids are transported via ABCD3 (PMP70). The branched-chain fatty acids handled by ABCD3 include pristanoyl-CoA and phytanoyl-CoA, which undergo subsequent alpha-oxidation, as well as bile acid intermediates. Acyl-CoA synthetases localized on the membrane or inside the peroxisomal lumen, such as ACSL4 or solute carrier family 27 member 2 (SLC27A2), can activate fatty acids to fatty acyl-CoAs [91]. In the peroxisomal matrix, FAD-dependent acyl-CoA oxidases (ACOX1, ACOX2, and ACOX3) transfer electrons from fatty acyl-CoA to produce FADH2, which reduces oxygen to generate H2O2 as a byproduct, whereas mitochondrial FADH2 is utilized for ATP production in oxidative phosphorylation [92]. There is an increase in peroxisomal biogenesis and β-oxidation activity during cold exposure and hibernation in adipose tissues for thermogenesis, which is regulated by PGC-1α [2, 93, 94]. Defective peroxisomal biogenesis and β-oxidation impair cold tolerance and may be implicated in the development of hepatic steatosis [84, 95].

Ether glycerophospholipid biosynthesis

Peroxisomes specialize in the initial biosynthesis of ether glycerophospholipids, which are characterized by an ether bond in the glycerol backbone. This process involves several key peroxisomal enzymes, including glyceronephosphate O-acyltransferase (GNPAT), alkylglycerone phosphate synthase (AGPS), and fatty acyl-CoA reductase 1 (FAR1). GNPAT catalyzes the first step of ether lipid synthesis, while AGPS introduces the ether bond. FAR1 provides the fatty alcohol substrate necessary for this reaction [2]. Plasmalogens are the most abundant glycerophospholipids in the brain and heart tissues of mammals, and are proposed to be important regulators of the biochemical dynamics of the plasma membrane [96]. Defective biosynthesis of plasmalogens contributes to the pathogenesis of PBDs, including Zellweger syndrome and rhizomelic chondrodysplasia punctata (RCDP) [97]. Patients with severe plasmalogen deficiency exhibit developmental abnormalities in multiple organs, such as neurological defects, vision impairment, and skeletal malformation [98]. In a mouse model of RCDP, administration of a synthetic plasmalogen analogue ameliorates bone function [99]. Reduced plasmalogen levels are also commonly found in patients with neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease [100, 101], while cancer cells produce high levels of ether phospholipids [102]. These findings suggest a paramount role for peroxisomal metabolism of ether phospholipids in human health.

Peroxisomal redox regulation

In addition to lipid metabolism, peroxisomes also contain enzymes for metabolizing methanol, polyamines, and purine bases. These metabolic processes are also accompanied by the production of H2O2, necessitating an antioxidant system to counterbalance the oxidative stress [92, 103]. Among the ROS detoxification enzymes, catalase is the most abundant, being responsible for decomposing H2O2 [92]. Other antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1), glutathione S-transferase kappa 1, and peroxiredoxin 5, have also been identified in the proteome of peroxisomes [104]. A balanced peroxisomal redox status is critical for peroxisome homeostasis, as excessive oxidative burden triggers pexophagy. In a mouse model lacking Pex14, peroxisomal import of catalase is impaired, and the disrupted peroxisomal biogenesis leads to the development of PBD-like symptoms in the central nervous system [105]. Peroxisomal catalase deficiency induces ROS-dependent pexophagy, and impaired peroxisomal fitness may contribute to the progression of pathogenic conditions, including diabetic renal injury and age-related degenerative disorders [106–109].

Interplay between lipid metabolism and redox balance

Peroxisomal β-oxidation activity and ROS levels are interconnected. For instance, enhanced activity of acyl-CoA oxidase 1 (ACOX1), a key enzyme in fatty acid β-oxidation, leads to elevated levels of peroxisomal H2O2 [110]. Peroxisomal ACOX1 is negatively regulated by the deacetylase Sirtuin 5 (SIRT5), and the downregulation of SIRT5 promotes ACOX1 activity and oxidative progression in hepatocellular carcinoma [110]. A recent study further elucidated a feedback regulation between fatty acid metabolism and ROS sensing in peroxisomes [11]. Excessive fatty acids from lipolysis and enhanced β-oxidation promote ROS production. Consequently, elevated ROS stabilizes membrane Pex2 to promote the ubiquitination and degradation of adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL). The reduction in ATGL activity suppresses lipolysis in the lipid droplet and prevents further accumulation of free fatty acids. Given that excessive amounts of fatty acids can lead to lipotoxicity, peroxisomal β-oxidation-derived ROS signal a feedback mechanism to ensure intracellular fatty acid homeostasis [11].

Current landscape in ferroptosis regulation

Overview of ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is recognized as an iron-mediated chain reaction of lipid peroxidation that eventually disrupts the integrity of the plasma membrane [111]. This form of cell death occurs without typical apoptotic or necrotic features and cannot be suppressed by apoptosis or necrosis inhibitors [112]. It was first described by the Stockwell lab in a chemical screen study identifying a group of compounds that induce cell death with unique cellular features [113]. In 2012, Dixon et al. conceptualized ferroptosis as a novel form of cell death as a consequence of iron-dependent accumulation of phospholipid peroxides [14]. Intracellular labile Fe2+ ions are necessary for ferroptosis execution, as the peroxidation of poly-unsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) chains requires iron-containing lipoxygenase (LOX) enzymes and the iron-dependent Fenton reaction [112, 114]. Both lipophilic antioxidants and iron chelators can suppress ferroptosis [115].

Iron homeostasis in ferroptosis

Extracellular iron ions are loaded onto transferrin (TF) and then imported via the transferrin receptor complex (TFRC), while intracellular ferritin sequesters iron [116] (Fig. 3). The availability of intracellular free iron affects ferroptosis. TFR1 (transferrin receptor 1) overexpression or upregulation renders cells more sensitive to ferroptosis induction by increasing intracellular iron concentration [117, 118], while lowering TFR expression confers resistance to ferroptosis [119]. Autophagic degradation of ferritin promotes ferroptosis, whereas elevated ferritin expression produces the opposite effect [120, 121]. Iron catalyzes the Fenton reaction between H2O2 and superoxide (intracellular ROS) to produce hydroxyl free radicals, which are powerful and non-selective oxidizers of unsaturated fatty acids.

Lipid peroxidation in ferroptosis

The excessive amplification of phospholipid peroxidation is the ultimate cause of ferroptotic cell death. Accumulation of lipid peroxides may compromise membrane fluidity and induce curvatures of the lipid bilayer, culminating in membrane ruptures [122, 123]. The phospholipid bilayer consists of lipids with at least one chain of PUFA, which is susceptible to LOX-mediated oxidation and peroxidation by free radicals [124]. It has been demonstrated that free PUFAs are not implicated in ferroptosis until they are integrated into the phospholipid bilayer [125]. Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) are two key enzymes responsible for the incorporation of PUFA into phospholipids. Genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of ACSL4 and LPCAT3 alters the composition of polyunsaturated phospholipids in the plasma membrane and protects against ferroptosis [126, 127]. ACSL4 deficiency also suppresses ferroptosis-induced tissue damage in mouse models of acute kidney injury and intestinal ischemia/reperfusion [18, 128]. Interestingly, a recent study unveiled that protein kinase C beta-II (PKCβII) can amplify lipid peroxidation to boost ferroptosis. In this model, the production of lipid peroxides activates PKCβII to phosphorylate and stimulate ACSL4, and activated ACSL4 facilitates the amplification of lipid peroxidation by increasing the content of polyunsaturated phospholipids [129]. However, whether a similar positive feedback loop of ferroptosis occurs under pathological conditions requires further validation.

GPX4-dependent antioxidant system

Glutathione (GSH) is a thiol-containing small peptide that prevents cellular damage caused by ROS, including free radicals, peroxides, and lipid peroxides. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is a detoxification enzyme that utilizes GSH to reduce lipid peroxides, and GPX4 inhibition or GSH depletion can trigger ferroptosis [111]. Intracellular GSH biosynthesis relies on the availability of cystine, which is imported by the membrane antiporter system xc- in exchange for intracellular glutamate (Fig. 3). Since system xc- is driven by a glutamate concentration gradient across the plasma membrane, elevated extracellular glutamate concentration can sensitize cells to ferroptosis by dampening the function of system xc- [112, 130]. Therefore, in addition to GPX4 inhibition, the entrance of cystine into the cytosol via system xc- is another main target for ferroptosis induction. Glutaminolysis is the metabolic process by which glutamine is converted into glutamate and then channeled into the TCA cycle for catabolism [131]. Inhibiting glutaminolysis is detrimental to cystine-deprivation induced ferroptosis but is dispensable for GPX4 inhibition-triggered ferroptosis [132, 133]. This may be because active ROS production by glutaminolysis-fueled mitochondrial electron transport is required to lower the cellular antioxidant level so that cystine deprivation-induced ferroptosis can occur. However, it remains unclear how cells balance glutaminolysis and GSH synthesis to avoid ferroptosis.

Alternative anti-ferroptotic mechanisms

More anti-ferroptotic processes have recently been uncovered that counteract lipid peroxides in parallel with GPX4 activity. In the search for anti-ferroptotic mechanisms complementing the loss of GPX4, ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) was identified as a GSH-independent reductase that converts coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) to a lipophilic antioxidant, ubiquinol-10, to protect against ferroptosis [134, 135]. It is puzzling that FSP1 was previously described as a pro-apoptotic protein upon oxidative stress [136]. By forming lipid adducts with 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (HNE, a lipid peroxidation end product), FSP1 is deprived of NADH oxidoreductase activity and translocated from mitochondria into the nucleus to trigger cell death. Further clarification is needed on how cells prevent FSP1 lipid adduction to preserve its anti-ferroptosis function upon lipid peroxidation. Another study employing CRISPR-based activation screens demonstrated that GTP cyclohydrolase 1 (GCH1) serves as an independent anti-ferroptotic protein [137, 138]. GCH1-dependent production of tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4, a lipophilic antioxidant) prevents the depletion of phosphatidylcholine phospholipids with two PUFA chains upon ferroptosis induction. GCH1 overexpression or downregulation alters the sensitivity towards ferroptosis accordingly. Consistent with this finding, inhibiting the activity of dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) enhances ferroptosis induced by GPX4 inhibition, as DHFR supplies the precursor for BH4 biosynthesis [138].

It is worth mentioning that many hallmarks of cancers can be employed as targetable vulnerabilities for ferroptosis, including augmented glutaminolysis [131], increased demand for iron [139], elevated oxidative stress [140] and frequently upregulated system xc- activity [141]. Ferroptosis induction has been shown to synergize with immunotherapy and radiotherapy to boost anticancer treatment outcomes in mouse models [142, 143]. Consequently, combinatory therapies involving ferroptosis induction are speculated to benefit cancer patients who have developed resistance to existing treatments.

Emerging roles of peroxisomes in ferroptosis

Peroxisomal factors influencing ferroptosis sensitivity

It is intuitive to surmise that peroxisomal homeostasis affects ferroptosis due to the pivotal roles of peroxisomes in redox regulation and lipid metabolism. Indeed, a genome-wide CRISPR/Cas9 screening identified that multiple peroxisomal components are implicated in ferroptosis sensitivity in human renal and ovarian carcinoma cells [20]. Interestingly, different peroxisomal genes including Pex1, Pex2, Pex3, Pex6, Pex10, Pex12, Pex13, Pex14, Pex16, and Pex19 were enriched in the cell population surviving ferroptosis induction. Validation experiments showed that the ablation of Pex3, Pex10, or Pex12 decreased peroxisomal abundance and conferred resistance to ferroptosis. Conversely, overexpressing murine Pex3 or Pex10 restored ferroptosis sensitivity. These findings highlight the critical role of peroxisome homeostasis in dictating ferroptosis susceptibility.

Peroxisomal lipid metabolism and ferroptosis

Peroxisomal lipid-metabolizing enzymes, including acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), alkylglycerone phosphate synthase (AGPS), and fatty acyl-CoA reductase 1 (FAR1), were also identified as top hits in regulating ferroptosis resistance [20]. This suggests the potential contribution of ether glycerophospholipid biosynthesis to ferroptosis sensitivity. Furthermore, lipidomic analysis revealed diminished production of plasmalogens upon Pex3 or Pex10 depletion. Interestingly, tumor-forming GPX4−/− cancer cells in a mouse model also downregulated plasmalogen biosynthesis to survive ferroptosis [20]. Given that plasmalogens contain ester-linked PUFA chains and are essential glycerophospholipids in the plasma membrane [94, 144], these findings suggest that loss of peroxisomal homeostasis (e.g. Pex gene depletion) undermines peroxisome-dependent plasmalogen biosynthesis and renders cells more resistant to lipid peroxidation (Fig. 4).

An emerging role of peroxisome in ferroptosis regulation

The biosynthesis of ether glycerolipids such as plasmalogens through peroxisome-dependent fatty acid metabolism and ER confers sensitivity towards ferroptosis. FAR1 is the rate-limiting enzyme that supplies fatty alcohol precursors for plasmalogen synthesis. Genetic ablation of FAR1 renders cells resistant to ferroptosis induction. Peroxins are peroxisome biogenesis factors required to maintain peroxisome abundance and the activity of plasmalogen biosynthesis. Depletion of Pex genes leads to peroxisome deficiency and ferroptosis resistance. Activation of the transcription factor PPARδ suppresses ferroptosis by upregulating catalase and neutralizing ROS. Another screening using a library of 350 metabolites found that 1-hexadecanol (1-HE), a saturated fatty alcohol (SFA), could also promote ferroptosis induction [10]. The authors hypothesized that the peroxisomal enzyme FAR1, which is the rate-limiting enzyme for supplying fatty alcohol precursor for plasmalogen synthesis [64, 145], can dictate ferroptosis sensitivity. The depletion of FAR1 conferred resistance to GPX4-inhibition or GPX4-depletion induced ferroptosis, which was restored after ectopic FAR1 expression [10]. FAR1 expression level appears to be positively correlated with ferroptosis sensitivity in a panel of cancer cells, as well as the lipid peroxidation level in human liver cancer tissue [10]. Collectively, these observations support the notion that peroxisomal FAR1-dependent SFA synthesis provides plasmalogen precursors and enhances susceptibility to ferroptosis. Intriguingly, as previous studies suggest a negative feedback regulation of FAR1 level by plasmalogens [146], this mechanism may help maintain plasmalogen homeostasis and prevent hyper-sensitivity to ferroptosis (Fig. 4).

Peroxisomal antioxidant systems in ferroptosis regulation

The peroxisomal antioxidant system has recently been reported to protect against ferroptosis induced by system xc- inhibition [147]. Pharmacological activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor δ (PPARδ) suppresses ferroptosis in mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), an effect that can be reversed by PPARδ silencing or a PPARδ inhibitor. The protective effect of PPARδ activation against ferroptosis is accompanied by the upregulation of catalase and can be antagonized by a catalase-specific inhibitor. PPARδ-dependent catalase upregulation maintains peroxisomal redox balance upon ferroptosis induction by quenching hydrogen peroxide [147] (Fig. 4).

In other pathological scenarios, natural products can regulate catalase, superoxide dismutase, or glutathione peroxidase to promote or suppress ferroptosis. Brucine, an alkaloid extracted from Strychnos nux-vomica, is able to trigger ferroptosis in glioma cells by dampening the expression of catalase and system xc- [148]. Trilobatin, a natural sweetener extracted from Polysacca plants, has been reported to prevent oxidative stress and ferroptosis in muscle and myocardium after exhaustive exercise by increasing the activity of superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase [149]. Furthermore, we recently conducted a screening study of a target-selective inhibitor library using a U-2OS cell line with fluorescently labeled peroxisomes. Several inhibitors were identified that positively or negatively regulate peroxisomal abundance without altering the cell cycle, acting through ROS-dependent or independent mechanisms. Notably, peroxisome-promoting compounds sensitized U-2OS cells to ferroptosis induction [150]. These findings identify new targets for studying the regulation of peroxisome biogenesis. They also suggest that combining peroxisome-promoting compounds with ferroptosis inducers could potentially enhance anticancer effects by increasing cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis. As ferroptosis has been implicated in the onset and progression of diverse human diseases [151], understanding the potential involvement of peroxisome-dependent ferroptosis will shed light on pathogenesis mechanisms.

Conclusions and perspectives

Peroxisomal dysfunction due to heritable mutations in Pex genes has been implicated in the development of PBDs. Increasing evidence suggests a broad impact of peroxisomal function on human diseases, including obesity, diabetes, cancer, and neurodegenerative disorders [9, 11, 12, 101, 103, 106–108]. While our understanding of peroxisomal biogenesis and degradation has significantly progressed, several key questions remain unresolved. The relative abundance and functional divergence of peroxisomes in different cells and tissues remain unclear. Understanding the functional specification of peroxisomes may shed light on how peroxisome deficiency affects the development of different tissues and organs in PBDs. Pexophagy has been recognized as the main process to eliminate excessive or dysfunctional peroxisomes [68]. A critical question is how cells coordinate peroxisomal biogenesis and pexophagy to maintain peroxisomal homeostasis. Furthermore, there is a lack of knowledge regarding the impact of different cell signaling pathways on peroxisomal dynamics and activity. On the other hand, a recent study has revealed an intricate relationship between peroxisomal metabolism and autophagy, in which acetyl-CoA derived from peroxisomal β-oxidation was found to inhibit autophagy and promote steatosis in hepatic tissues [152]. These findings highlight the potential crosstalk between peroxisomal function and cellular metabolic homeostasis. Further investigation into this interplay could provide valuable insights into the role of peroxisomes in metabolic disorders and potential therapeutic strategies.

Ferroptosis has been conceptualized as a novel form of cell death regulated by intracellular iron homeostasis, redox status, as well as lipid metabolism [112]. Ferroptosis is involved in a wide spectrum of pathogenic scenarios and has been proposed as a targetable strategy [151]. Recent works have unveiled the engagement of peroxisomes in ferroptosis [10, 20], adding another layer of complexity to ferroptosis regulation. Nevertheless, the implications of peroxisome-dependent ferroptosis regulation in PBDs and other physiological conditions have not been studied. Since ether glycerolipids are essential components affecting biophysical properties of the plasma membrane, how does the cell balance peroxisome-dependent plasmalogen production to avoid hyper-sensitivity to ferroptosis? During the initiation of ferroptosis, do peroxisomes undergo structural and functional changes? And does the cell adopt pexophagy as a strategy to regulate ferroptosis under different physiological conditions? The answers to these questions will provide intervention opportunities to modulate peroxisomal homeostasis and ferroptosis in disease states.
