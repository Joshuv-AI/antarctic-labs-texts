Heat stress-induced metabolomic shifts in chickpea (Cicer arietinum L.) flowers insights from contrasting genotypes
Authors: Uday Chand Jha, Marilyn L Warburton, Harsh Nayyar, Dheeraj Chatti, Arindam Ghatak, Kadambot H M Siddique, P V Vara Prasad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChickpea (Cicer arietinum L.), a vital cool-season pulse crop, experiences significant yield losses when exposed to heat stress during its reproductive stages, a vulnerability exacerbated by weather extremes. Chickpea plants synthesize numerous metabolites when subjected to heat stress; however, the underlying metabolomic mechanisms of heat tolerance remain poorly understood. In this study, we employed widely targeted metabolomics to identify key metabolites and potential biomarkers in the flower buds of two contrasting chickpea genotypes: the heat-tolerant PI518255 and the heat-sensitive PI598080. The tolerant genotype showed improved chlorophyll index, photochemical efficiency, antioxidant activity, along with low electrolyte leakage and malondialdehyde content. In contrast, the sensitive genotype exhibited low chlorophyll index, photochemical efficiency and low antioxidant activity, and high electrolyte leakage and malondialdehyde content, under heat stress conditions (35/20 °C day/night). Volcano plot analysis identified 86 up-regulated and 230 down-regulated metabolites in response to heat stress. Heatmap analysis revealed that elevated levels of specific flavonoids, phenolic acids, lignans, coumarins, alkaloids, quinones, and terpenoids characterized the heat-tolerant genotype, suggesting their potential as biomarkers for screening heat stress tolerance. KEGG enrichment and pathway analysis highlighted isoflavonoid biosynthesis, flavonoid metabolism, lysine degradation, and butanoate metabolism as key pathways affected by heat stress. Introduction

Chickpea (Cicer arietinum L.), a cool-season pulse crop cultivated globally, plays a vital role in food security and human nutrition1. As an affordable and rich source of high-quality protein and essential nutrients, chickpea contributes significantly to efforts to reduce global hunger and malnutrition2. Its capacity for symbiotic atmospheric nitrogen fixation with root nodule rhizobacteria enhances soil fertility and reduces the need for synthetic nitrogen fertilizers, making it an environmentally and economically sustainable crop3. Chickpea is grown primarily under rainfed conditions in hot, dry regions of semi-arid agroecological zones4. Globally, chickpea is harvested from approximately 14.8 million hectares, yielding around 18.1 million tons annually, with an average yield of 0.96 tons per hectare5. India leads global production, contributing nearly 70% (11.6 million tons) of total output5 .

However, weather extremes increasingly threaten chickpea productivity, particularly the increasing frequency, intensity, timing and duration of heat stress events. These trends pose a serious risk to global food security6, with the projected global temperature rise of 1.5 °C by 2040 expected to further compromise crop yields7. High temperatures (heat stress) disrupt plant physiological, biochemical, and molecular processes, leading to substantial yield losses across many crops, including chickpea8. In chickpea, heat stress adversely affects vegetative and reproductive development9–12 with reproductive processes such as anthesis, pollen viability, stigma receptivity, and fertilization being particularly sensitive13. Air temperatures exceeding 32–35 °C during flowering can cause flower drop and floral abortion9,10, leading to decreased pod and seed formation, reduced seed quality, and yield losses of up to 39%8,11.

Plants deploy various biochemical strategies to withstand heat stress, including production of sugars, phenolic compounds, amino acids, fatty acids, and antioxidants12,14–17. Heat stress also induces oxidative stress18. In response, plants regulate cell membrane fluidity, synthesize unsaturated fatty acids, produce antioxidants, and modulate phytohormones to mitigate heat damage6,19–21. Additional protective responses include activating heat shock elements and factors, expressing heat-responsive genes, and synthesizing heat shock proteins22,23. Differences exists among genotypes of various crops for heat tolerance, which were identified using various traits related to leaves (e.g., membrane stability, photosynthetic efficiency, chlorophyll fluorescence), flower (e.g., gamete viability, seed-set), roots (e.g., architecture – angle and depth), and biomolecules (e.g., antioxidants, osmolytes, heat-shock proteins)24–28.

Flowers, as the primary reproductive organs in angiosperms, are exceptionally sensitive to heat stress29,30. Elevated temperatures often compromise reproductive development, from initiation to fertilization and seed set, more severely than vegetative growth29,30. Therefore, analysing flowers provides critical insights into reproductive thermotolerance mechanisms.

Metabolomics offers a powerful approach to comprehensively analyse the biochemical changes that occur in plants under heat stress, enabling the identification of crucial metabolite profiles and associated metabolic pathways involved in stress responses31. These metabolites will identify candidate genes, the study of which could lead to a better understanding of heat stress responses in plants. In addition, these differentially expressed metabolites could possibly serve as biomarkers for differentiating heat-tolerant and heat-sensitive genotypes. Despite its potential, metabolomic research in chickpea under abiotic stress conditions remains limited31–34.

We hypothesize that heat-tolerant chickpea genotypes, characterized by improved SPAD value and photosynthetic efficiency, lower electrolyte leakage and malondialdehyde levels, and enhanced antioxidant activity, produce specific metabolites that confer adaptive responses under heat stress, in contrast to heat-sensitive genotypes. Accordingly, this study is aimed to explore and compare the metabolite profiles of flower buds from heat-tolerant and heat-sensitive chickpea genotypes using a widely targeted metabolomics approach to identify candidate genes, biomarkers and metabolic pathways associated with heat tolerance for further study and validation.

Materials and methods

The two contrasting chickpea genotypes, PI518255 (heat-tolerant HT)35,36 and PI598080 (heat-sensitive HS)37(obtained from USDA-ARS national Plant Germplasm System), were initially grown in a greenhouse under controlled conditions (15/10°C day/night) for 60 days prior to flowering. They were then transferred to a growth chamber set at 35/20°C day/night (12 h photoperiod), “photosynthetically active radiation (400–700 nm)” at 600 µmol m−2 s−1 using cool fluorescent lamps, and 60% relative humidity. Plants were consistently watered to field capacity, thereby avoiding water stress and received nutrient supplementation every 7–14 days (1/2 teaspoon Miracle-Gro 24-8-16 per 4.5 L of water. Temperature data were recorded using a “HOBO® temperature logger (Onset Computer Corporation, USA)”38, presented in S1 Fig.

Electrolyte leakage (%), an indicator of leaf membrane damage

Electrolyte leakage (EL), indicator of membrane damage, was assessed using 100 mg of leaf tissue from the top branch of each genotype. Tissue samples were transferred to closed vials containing 10 mL of deionized water and incubated at 25 °C on a rotary shaker for 24 hours39. The initial electrical conductivity, designated as L1, was then measured using a conductivity meter. Leaf chlorophyll index and photochemical efficiency

Non-destructive measurements of chlorophyll index (SPAD value) were taken using a SPAD-502 chlorophyll meter. (Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan). Five readings were taken from the third fully expanded young leaf from the apex of each plant and averaged. Photochemical efficiency explaining Variable Fluorescence/Maximum Fluorescence (Fv/Fm), an indicator of photosynthetic performance, was determined using a dark-adapted protocol with an “OS1-FL modulated fluorometer” (Opti-Sciences, Hudson, New Hampshire, USA)39.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) content

To evaluate lipid peroxidation under heat stress, we measured malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. MDA content (µmol g FW −1) was measured following the method outlined by Heath and Packer40. A 0.5 g sample of leaf tissue was ground in 5 ml of a solution containing 0.25% thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARs) in 10% (w/v) trichloroacetic acid (TCA). The resulting homogenate was heated at 95 °C for 30 min and then quickly cooled in an ice bath40. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 30 min. Absorbance readings were recorded at 532 and 600 nm. MDA concentration was determined using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM− 1 cm− 1. A solution of 0.25% TBARs in 10% (w/v) TCA served as a blank40.

Antioxidant enzyme superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

SOD activity was measured following Kakkar et al.41. Leaf tissue (0.5 g in triplicate) from the third fully expanded leaf was ground in 3.0 mL of potassium phosphate buffer and centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 10 min. The assay mixture comprised 1.2 mL sodium pyrophosphate buffer, 0.1 mL PMS, 0.3mL NBT, and 0.2mL enzyme extract, with the final volume adjusted to 2.8 mL using distilled water. The reaction was initiated by adding 0.2 mL NADH and incubated at 30 °C for 90 s. The reaction was terminated with 1.0 mL glacial acetic acid. Subsequently, 4.0 mL n-butanol was added, and the mixture was left to stand for 10 min before centrifugation. The color intensity of the butanol phase was measured at 560 nm by UV-V is spectrophotometer. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme that resulted in 50% inhibition of NBT reduction per minute.

Physiological parameters—including SPAD value, chlorophyll fluorescence (Fv/Fm), EL, MDA content, and SOD activity—were measured in fully expanded uppermost leaves from each plant. Leaf sampling was conducted between 9:00 AM and 11:00 AM to minimize diurnal variation. At least three biological replicates per genotype were used, and each replicate consisted of pooled leaves from three individual plants. Samples intended for MDA and SOD assays were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until biochemical analysis.

Preparation of flower bud extracts for metabolomics analysis

Three flower buds per replicate were randomly selected based on uniform developmental stage—specifically pre-anthesis buds approximately 3–5 mm in size—to minimize variation in metabolite profiles due to ontogeny from heat stressed plants in the growth chamber, freeze-dried under vacuum, and homogenized using a ball mill (30 Hz, 1.5 min, MM 400, Retsch GmbH, Haan, Germany). Then, 50 mg of the powdered sample was extracted in 1,200 µL of chilled 70% methanol (–20 °C) containing internal standards (e.g., ribitol; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The mixture was incubated with vortex (30 s every 30 min, six times total) and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and stored for UPLC-MS/MS analysis (Waters, Milford, MA, USA)28 .

UPLC-MS/MS conditions

“An Agilent SB-C18 column (1.8 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) was used for chromatographic separation”. “The mobile phase comprised ultrapure water + 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile + 0.1% formic acid (B)”. The elution gradient started at 95% A/5% B (0 min), transitioned linearly to 5% A/95% B (9 min), was held for 10 min, and then returned to the initial ratio by 11.1 min, continuing until 14 min. The process maintained a 0.35 mL/minflow rate, 40 °C column temperature, and 2 µL injection volume28,42.

Mass spectrometry was conducted using an “AB6500 Q TRAP UPLC/MS/MS” system with linear ion trap and QQQ scanning modes. Data were acquired in both positive and negative ionization modes using electrospray ionization (ESI), with a source temperature of 550 °C and ion spray voltages of 5,500 V (positive mode) and − 4,500 V (negative mode). Quantification was carried out using multiple reaction monitoring28,42,43.

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Mass spectra were structurally annotated using an in-house MetWare database (MetWare Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Wuhan, China). Quantification of metabolite features was performed using a mass spectrometer during multiple reactions monitoring in Analyst 1.6.3 software. Peak areas were calculated and corrected using “MultiQuantTM3.0” for Quantitative LC-MS/MS Data software41 (https://sciex.com/products/software/multiquant-software). Metabolite intensities were normalized using internal standards to correct for variation in extraction and instrument response. Additionally, total ion current (TIC) normalization was applied to account for differences in sample loading and instrument. Each metabolite was mean-centered and divided by its standard deviation to give all variables equal weight, a necessary step for unsupervised analyses such as PCA and clustering. These pre-processed data were then used for multivariate statistical analyses including “principal component analysis (PCA), hierarchical clustering, and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA)”.

To evaluate group separations and identify key differentiating metabolites, PCA, “partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA)44”, and OPLS-DA were performed, each with accompanying cross-validation. OPLS-DA was executed using the “OPLSR.Anal” function from the “MetaboAnalystR” package in R45.

The OPLS-DA model evaluation includes three predictive parameters: “R2X, R2Y, and Q2”. “R2X and R2Y represent the proportion of variance explained by the model in the X and Y matrices, respectively, while Q2 reflects the model’s predictive ability”. The closer these values are to 1, the more stable and reliable the model is considered to be. A Q2 value greater than 0.5 indicates an effective model, whereas a Q2 value above 0.9 signifies an excellent model. VIP: “variable importance in the projection”45. The VIP value represents the effect of the differences between groups for a particular metabolite in various models and sample groups. It is generally considered that the metabolites with VIP > 1 are significantly different between the chickpea genotypes.

Volcano plots were used to represent fold changes and statistical significance of differences between groups. Metabolite abundance data were unit variance scaled (see calculations in the appendix) and visualized using the “ComplexHeatmap” package in R. Functional annotation was performed using “KEGG MAPPER”, with metabolites annotated using the KEGG database46,47. Based on these annotations, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted. The enrichment score, or “Rich Factor”, was determined by the ratio of differentially abundant metabolites to the total number of annotated metabolites in each pathway. Statistical significance was determined using the hypergeometric test48

Results

Physiological analysis

Under heat stress, heat tolerant PI518255 exhibited a significantly (p < 0.05) higher chlorophyll index or SPAD value (33.6) than PI598080 (22.2), indicating greater chlorophyll retention. Compared to PI598080, the flower bud of PI518255 was less affected by heat stress (see Fig. 1). Electrolyte leakage was lower in PI518255 (38.3%) than in PI598080 (53.4%), suggesting enhanced membrane stability. The Fv/Fm ratio, indicative of photosynthetic efficiency, was also higher in PI518255 (0.66) than in PI598080 (0.55), further supporting its heat tolerance (Fig. 2). Considering lipid peroxidation, PI518255 had lower MDA content (18) than PI598080 (27). In terms of antioxidant defense, PI518255 demonstrated higher SOD activity (164.7) than PI598080 (106.6), reinforcing its superior oxidative stress mitigation capacity under heat stress (Fig. 2).

Metabolome analysis

The metabolome analysis detected 2,504 (see S1Table) metabolites in flower buds of both genotypes under heat stress. Of these, 316 (see S2 Table) showed significant differential accumulation, with 86 upregulated and 230 downregulated in PI518255. Functional enrichment analysis revealed several metabolic pathways significantly enriched (p < 0.05) in the heat-tolerant genotype. These included isoflavonoid biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, flavone and flavanol synthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, phenylalanine metabolism, and lysine degradation (Figs. 3 and 4).

Multivariate analysis

The PCA analysis (Fig. 8) explained 45.08% and 16.4% of total variance along PC1 and PC2, respectively. Recognizing PCA’s limitations in detecting weak correlations, supervised methods (PLS-DA and OPLS-DA) were employed. The PLS-DA improved separation between genotypes by identifying co-variation between independent (X) and dependent (Y) variables. X-axis represents the predicted principal component, and the difference between groups can be seen in the horizontal direction (Fig. 9). The Y-axis represents the orthogonal principal component, and the vertical direction shows the difference within the group (Fig. 9). Further refinement using OPLS-DA enabled the removal of unrelated variation, enhancing the model’s discriminatory power.

Discussion

Heat stress during the growing season continues to negatively impact chickpea yields, a major concern for global food security49. While the heat stress response in chickpea has been investigated at vegetative and reproductive stages9–11,50–52, the role of metabolites produced in flower buds under heat stress has not been explored until now. Heat stress results in a reduction in Fv/Fm and signaling damage to the photosystem, while simultaneously causing an increase in TBARs levels. This increase in TBARs, an indicator of lipid peroxidation, clearly indicates deterioration of cellular membrane integrity, which is a characteristic of intense oxidative stress30. These detrimental effects were most evident in heat-sensitive genotypes and corresponded with heightened accumulation of lipid peroxidation products29.

Our study revealed significant genetic variation between chickpea genotypes PI598080 and PI518255 in chlorophyll index, Fv/Fm, and leaf electrolyte leakage. Under heat stress, PI518255 exhibited higher chlorophyll index and fluorescence and reduced electrolyte leakage compared to PI598080. These results contradict Kumari et al.53 who reported decreased chlorophyll index, relative water content, and root and shoot length in PI518255 during vegetative-stage heat stress. However, based on yield and yield traits, Wang et al.37 identified PI598080 as heat-sensitive due to a greater yield reduction than Xena under heat stress.

To elucidate the metabolic mechanisms underlying heat stress responses in chickpea, we analyzed flower bud metabolite profiles in one heat-tolerant and one heat-sensitive genotype. Our goal was to identify key metabolites involved in the heat stress response and determine those that may confer heat stress tolerance in the tolerant genotype. These results provide a comprehensive overview of cellular metabolite composition, identifying differentiating metabolites, highlighting the metabolic pathways and specific compounds that accumulate in floral tissues, and distinguishing heat-tolerant from heat-sensitive genotypes.

Heat stress induces oxidative stress during plant reproductive development11. Polyphenols and flavonoids are essential in mitigating oxidative stress by scavenging antioxidants and protecting against ROS damage. Upregulation of flavonoid metabolites in tolerant genotypes—such as quercetin, kaempferol, and rutin—suggests a metabolic compensatory mechanism that contributes to ROS scavenging and membrane stabilization54,55. Flavonoids thus not only correlate with lower MDA and electrolyte leakage but also serve as putative biomarkers for oxidative stress tolerance. Our findings suggest that the elevated accumulation of flavonoids, phenolic compounds, lignans, alkaloids, and terpenoids in the flower buds of the heat-tolerant genotype PI518225 may contribute to enhanced antioxidant activity and, consequently, greater heat tolerance than PI598080 (see Fig. 12). Flavonoids, in particular, serve broad protective functions in plants, including responses to abiotic stresses56. Their role in mitigating ROS damage under heat stress has been demonstrated in rice (Oryza sativa L.) 57, tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.)58,59, and grapevine (Vitis vinifera)60, among other plant species.

The diagram illustrates a proposed mechanism by which the heat-tolerant genotype PI518255 exhibits enhanced resilience to heat stress compared to the heat-sensitive genotype PI598080. In PI518255, elevated accumulation of specific secondary metabolites—such as alkaloids, flavonoids, phenolic compounds, coumarins, and quinones—may contribute to improved cellular protection, oxidative stress mitigation, and membrane stability under high temperature. These compounds likely enhance antioxidant capacity and maintain physiological integrity, thereby promoting flower bud viability during heat stress.

Plants also accumulate phenolic and polyphenolic compounds responding to heat and other stresses61. Higher levels of phenolic compounds have been associated with improved heat stress tolerance in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.)62, maize (Zea mays)63, tomato and watermelon (Cucumis melo L.)61. One such compound, coumarin – a hydroxycinnamic phenolic with known antioxidant properties64 can enhance antioxidant defences under abiotic stress65. Under stress conditions, coumarin biosynthesis is often upregulated as a part of the plant’s adaptive response. This enhanced production helps maintain redox homeostasis and stabilizes cell structures and cell membrane integrity thereby, reducing lipid peroxidation66,67.

Its role in mitigating ROS-induced damage has been reported under salinity stress in tomato65 and sorghum (Sorghum bicolor)68. These findings suggest that the production of coumarin and related compounds may also contribute to protecting PI518225 from heat-induced damage.

Lignans and tannins are other abundant secondary metabolites with antioxidant properties that enhance tolerance to various abiotic stresses69.Their role in conferring drought tolerance has been demonstrated in flax (Linum usitatissimum)70,71, implying a possible role in heat stress mitigation in PI518225. Lignans can serve as precursors in the synthesis of lignin under various abiotic stresses, including heat stress. As lignin being a crucial component of the cell wall, it is instrumental in providing structural rigidity and mechanical support under such stress conditions72. Enhanced lignin accumulation has been shown to help poplar (Populus spp) plants and coffee (Coffea arabica L.) acclimates to elevated temperatures73.

Terpenoids, the most abundant class of secondary metabolites, are recognized for their role in plant adaptation to abiotic stress74. Terpenoids, especially gibberellin precursors and brassinosteroids, are tightly linked to reproductive development, impacting flower initiation, pollen viability, and anther dehiscence—traits that are often impaired under heat stress75–77. These compounds help maintain oxidative balance by stabilizing cell membranes and protecting photosystem II protein complexes under heat stress, as shown in grapevine78. Terpenoids like carotenoids can improve heat stress tolerance by minimizing photo inhibition damage in tomato79. Elevated SOD activity in tolerant lines aligns with the enrichment of terpenoid biosynthesis pathways, particularly those producing isoprenoids and carotenoids, which are known to support antioxidant defense and thermal stability of cellular structures, including thylakoid membranes76,77. These metabolites may help maintain chlorophyll integrity (SPAD values) and photosynthetic efficiency (Fv/Fm) under stress. Moreover, SPAD and Fv/Fm values, which reflect photosynthetic competence, showed positive associations with certain terpenoid glycosides and flavonoids, indicating that these metabolites may act as protective agents in safeguarding the photosynthetic apparatus during high-temperature exposure76,77.

The superior heat tolerance of PI518225 is not just an isolated physiological response, but is likely supported by a well-coordinated defense system. Our study indicated that PI518225 exhibits reduced TBARs levels and enhanced photosynthetic performance (Fv/Fm ​) under heat stress, suggesting that its cells are better equipped to manage oxidative damage. Moreover, metabolomic analysis of PI518225 revealed a significant increase in the levels of specific terpenoids viz., Deoxymikanolide, Licoricesaponin H2 under heat stress during heat stress. Terpenoids such as carotenoids and tocopherols are recognized for their potent non-enzymatic antioxidant properties76,77. The elevated production of these compounds in this heat-resistant line suggests a complementary defense strategy, whereas SOD provides an initial enzymatic defense. These terpenoids likely neutralize residual free radicals and stabilize cell membranes, offering a more comprehensive cellular protection system76–79. This dual-pathway defense mechanism, integrating both enzymatic and non-enzymatic components, suggest an appropriate explanation for PI518225’s distinctive resilience in high-temperature conditions.

Thus, presumably, enhanced accumulation of (Deoxymikanolide, Licoricesaponin H2, Oxyphyllenone B see Table 1 and S2) terpenoid metabolites could improve antioxidant activity and thus, confer heat stress tolerance in PI518225.

Alkaloids, synthesized from aliphatic and aromatic amino acids, also contribute significantly to plant responses to various stressors80,81. Their accumulation under heat stress has been reported in Lycium barbarum L.82 and Catharanthus roseus83, where they appear to enhance heat tolerance by improving plant water potential and increasing ROS scavenging activity84,85 .

The presence of alkaloids in PI518225 may contribute to its enhanced tolerance to heat stress by functioning as signaling molecules or stress protectants. Alkaloids are recognized for their role in plant defense mechanisms, and their accumulation under elevated temperatures could represent an adaptive response84,85. The specific alkaloid viz., p-Coumaroylferuloylcadaverine identified in PI518225 have been implicated in the regulation of stomatal closure or the protection of cellular membranes from heat-induced damage. This association directly links the presence of these compounds to the observed physiological improvements, such as higher Fv/Fm ratios and sustained SPAD values.

Quinones, a class of redox-active secondary metabolites, play critical roles in plant stress responses, including heat tolerance, by functioning in antioxidant defense, electron transport, and signaling86,87. Plastoquinone and ubiquinone are among the quinones that serve as key components in both photosynthetic and respiratory electron transport chains. During heat stress, these molecules contribute to stabilizing electron transport, thus preserving photosynthetic efficiency (Fv/Fm) and shielding photosystem II from damage caused by heat86. In addition to safeguarding the photosynthetic apparatus, quinones play a dual role by acting as stress signaling molecules. They activate the expression of heat-responsive genes, encompassing those for detoxification, secondary metabolism, and protein folding (e.g., heat shock proteins)87. Quinones also modulate the activity of stress hormones like abscisic acid (ABA), assisting in stomatal closure and osmotic regulation under high temperature conditions88.

Quinones constitute a crucial element in the plant’s defense mechanism against oxidative stress87. Considering that heat stress markedly elevates reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, the presence of quinones viz., l-desmethylaurantio-obtusin in PI518225 likely augments its antioxidant capacity and reduces TBARs content. This indicates a synergistic interaction wherein quinones collaborate with antioxidant enzymes to scavenge ROS more effectively, thereby safeguarding cells from oxidative damage.

In the heat-tolerant genotype, the most highly enriched metabolic pathways included phenylpropanoid, isoflavonoid, and flavonoid biosynthesis, as well as broader secondary metabolite biosynthesis, phenylalanine metabolism, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, cutin, suberine and wax biosynthesis and pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis (Fig. 4). The enriched flavonoid and isoflavonoid biosynthesis pathways may contribute to protection from ROS-induced damage57–59. Moreover, unsaturated fatty acids such as oleic (18:1), linoleic (18:2), and α-linolenic (18:3) acids are known for their role in maintaining membrane stability under extreme temperatures85. In our study, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism likely contributed to heat acclimation by preserving membrane integrity85. Phenylalanine metabolism also appears to be a key contributor to heat stress tolerance. Its involvement has been reported in the algal species Sargassum fusiforme89 and plant species such as Clematis lanuginosa and C. crassifolia90. In a 2025 study, Lu et al.91 showed that the absence of “NAT1”, a negative regulator of thermo-tolerance, in the nat1 mutant in rice leads to the activation of bHLH110. This activation subsequently induces the expression of wax biosynthesis-related genes like “CER1, CER1L, and OsWR2”, ultimately promoting enhanced wax biosynthesis and deposition that contributes to heat stress tolerance in rice.

Enhanced transcriptional activity of cutin and wax biosynthesis genes, leading to increased wax accumulation and limited non-stomatal water loss, has been reported to confer drought tolerance in wheat and barley92,93and heat tolerance in rice94. The deposition of cuticular wax on plant surface reduces canopy temperature and minimizes water loss under heat stress in rice. This is further evidenced by expression of natural allele TT2HPS32, which functions as a loss-of-function mutant, regulating wax deposition under heat stress and increased thermotolerance in rice95.

In the current study, cutin and wax metabolism may also mediate heat tolerance by preventing non-stomatal water loss under heat stress. Waxes form a protective layer on the leaf surface, and an increase in wax deposition in PI518225 could be a direct adaptive response to heat stress. This waxy layer would reduce cuticle transpiration, helping the plant to maintain its water status and turgor pressure.

Conclusions

The superior performance of PI518225 under heat-stress conditions is associated with a combination of physiological and biochemical adaptations. The observed increase in SPAD values and Fv/Fm ratios indicates that PI518225 maintained superior photosynthetic capacity and integrity of its photosynthetic apparatus compared to the other genotypes. This suggests that the chloroplasts exhibit greater resilience to heat-induced damage. Moreover, the enhanced activity of SOD, a critical antioxidant enzyme, directly addresses the primary cause of damage related to heat stress. Heat stress frequently results in an increase in ROS levels, which can harm cellular components. The elevated SOD activity in PI518225 enabled more effective neutralization of these harmful ROS, thereby mitigating oxidative stress. In summary, thermotolerance of PI518225 represents a comprehensive response. The sustained photosynthetic efficiency, as indicated by high SPAD and Fv/Fm values, provides the necessary energy for cellular maintenance and repair, while the robust antioxidant defense system, as evidenced by high SOD activity, actively protects the cells from the deleterious effects of oxidative stress. This integrated physiological and biochemical framework allows PI518225 to survive and maintain its functionality at elevated temperatures.

A widely targeted metabolomic analysis of two contrasting chickpea genotypes –HT PI518225 and HS PI598080, revealed distinct differences in metabolite profiles and associated metabolic pathways under heat stress. The HT genotype exhibited elevated levels of flavonoids and other secondary metabolites linked to antioxidant activity. Enriched metabolic pathways included isoflavonoid and flavonoid biosynthesis, lysine degradation, and butanoate metabolism. Key distinguishing metabolites such as flavonoids, phenolic acids, lignans, coumarins, alkaloids, quinones, and terpenoids were identified as potential biomarkers of heat tolerance, likely helped protect the plant’s photosynthetic machinery and reduced oxidative stress in PI518225. These findings provide valuable insights for developing screening tools to identify HT chickpea genotypes, which could support breeding efforts to improve resilience to rising global temperatures.
