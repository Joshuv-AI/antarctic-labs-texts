Optimizing Cr(VI) reduction to Cr(III) using Pd-CNTs nanocatalyst: kinetic Monte Carlo simulation and experimental design insights
Authors: Hamideh Hajloo, Hadis Bashiri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this investigation, we explored the kinetics of Cr(VI) reduction to Cr(III) on carbon nanotube decorated with palladium (Pd-CNTs) nanocatalyst, using formic acid as the reducing agent. This study has been bone utilizing kinetic Monte Carlo simulation and experimental design methods. The mechanism and kinetic parameters of this reaction are provided. The effect various factors such as reaction time, pH level, dichromate (Cr2O72−) concentration, and formic acid concentration on Cr(VI) reduction was studied. Concentrations of HCOOH and Cr2O72− were identified as the crucial variables, while the HCOOH concentration has the most significant impact. Positive influences on Cr(VI) reduction were observed with increasing pH level and HCOOH concentration. Reaction time positively affects on Cr(VI) reduction efficiency. However, the concentration of Cr2O72− showed an increasing effect up to a threshold, negatively impacting the efficiency. The optimal conditions (Reaction time = 60 min, pH = 4.5, [Cr2O72−] = 5.05 × 10−3 M, and [HCOOH] = 0.95 M) for Cr(VI) reduction. At optimal conditions, the Cr(VI) reduction efficiency was obtained to be 100%.Keywords: Cr(VI) reduction, Pd-CNT nanocatalysts, Kinetic Monte Carlo simulation, Formic acid, Experimental design, Cr(III) productionSubject terms: Pollution remediation, Chemical physics

Introduction

Environmental pollutions are the pressing global issues due to their extraction and extensive industrial use, leading to elevated concentrations in water, wastewater, air, and soil1,2. The high toxicity and non-biodegradability of heavy metals intensify water pollution, their removal is necessary for public health and industrial effluent mitigation. Chromium, a toxic metal with various industrial uses, poses health and environmental risks in hexavalent and trivalent states3,4. Hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)), renowned for its carcinogenic attributes, induces significant health concerns, in contrast trivalent chromium (Cr(III)) exhibits reduced toxicity and can be readily eliminated in the form of a Cr(OH)3 precipitate.

Various techniques, such as chemical purification, solvent extraction, ion exchange, membrane filtration, electrochemical purification, biological processes, and catalytic reduction are utilized to eliminate Cr(VI). Nevertheless, each method has its own set of constraints5–8. In the catalytic reduction method, Cr(VI) is converted to a less toxic form through catalysis or electrochemistry. Cr(III) precipitates easily in neutral or alkaline solutions and can be adsorbed and separated by various adsorbents8–10. Palladium nanoparticles exhibit a significant surface area and catalytic activity, which make them highly proficient in the reduction of Cr(VI) to Cr(III)11. Using Pd composites for Cr(VI) reduction demonstrate an amplified stability, superior electron transport capabilities, simplified separation, and decreased cost compared to Pd nanoparticles12. Utilizing carbon nanotubes (CNTs) together with Pd nanoparticles to reduce Cr(VI) to Cr(III) has been extensively validated as advantageous. While the advancement of innovative and improved catalysts has traditionally relied on an empirical approach of trial-and-error, the computational methods are assuming a prominent role in the pursuit of “rational catalyst design”13,14.

The used technologies for Cr(VI) reduction are chemical, electrochemical, and biological methods. The electrochemical and chemical methods require take place under acidic conditions. The sulfur dioxide, Iron salt, and sodium bisulfite in an acid solution are the most common reducing agents. In biological methods, the carbon sources like polysaccharide are often reagents15. Kinetic study of water treatment is important, and many researches have been studies this concept16. Knowledge of the kinetics of Cr(VI) reduction is critical for assessing the fate of its complex natural environments. In a study, Gould expressed that Cr(VI) reduction kinetics dependents on hydrogen ion concentration17. This study indicates a 0.5 order for the kinetics of Cr(VI) reduction. Fiúza et al.18 showed that this reaction can be modelled using a pseudo-first-order kinetics which is inappropriate for a heterogeneous reaction. Until now, researchers have considered this reaction as a one-step reaction, and a complete and several steps mechanism for this reaction is not provided.

The examination of kinetics systems within a singular condition can be efficiently accomplished by utilizing a technique known as kinetic Monte Carlo simulation (KMC). The progress and successful application of the Chemical Kinetic Simulator (CKS) package has facilitated the KMC simulations concerning chemical reactions19,20. The utilization of design of experiments (DOE) represents a systematic approach aimed at augmenting the efficiency of processes through deliberate adjustments made to the attributes and influential factors21,22. Response surface methodology (RSM) is a technique utilized within the realm of DOE to analyze the interrelation between variables and response through mathematical approaches. Although, design of expert is usually used in the experimental studies, but recently, we have applied this method to design of the simulation studies23–26. The setup of the catalytic reaction has the complexity and it is almost expensive. Therefore, the researches almost do not focus on the optimization of these reactions. So, providing a theoretical method to optimize the reactions can be helpful. In simulation studies, using RSM can help us to determine the optimum conditions to get the maximum yield of reaction.

In this research, we want to use both the KMC simulation and the RSM approach to investigate the experimental endeavor conducted by Kim and Choi27. The main objective of this investigation is to propose a mechanism for Cr(VI) reduction by palladium/carbon nanotube (Pd-CNTs) nanocatalyst. Consequently, we shall closely scrutinize the outcomes of the experimental inquiry conducted by Yang and his colleagues28. Additionally, we shall examine the effect of various independent variables such as formic acid (HCOOH) concentration (as a reducing agent), dichromate (Cr2O72−) concentration, pH, and reaction time on the Cr(VI) reduction. Ultimately, we will try to identify the optimal conditions of this reaction.

Methods

In this study, we used kinetic Monte Carlo simulation to determine the mechanism and kinetic parameters of this reaction. Then, Design of expert has been used to optimize the reaction.

Kinetic Monte Carlo simulation

KMC efficiently illustrates state-to-state changes in a system using reaction rate information and is easily implemented29. The chemical Master equation provides an account of the system by presenting the likelihood of transitioning between different configurations throughout time. Gillespie’s methodology, a member of the various techniques under the domain of kinetic Monte Carlo, involves the simulation of the statistical advancement of a chemical system through the establishment of reactions, such as the bidirectional conversion of A to B30,31.

In the simulation algorithm, a set of reactions, Ri = (R1, R2,…, RM), is represented, with R1 as aA → bB and R2 as bB → aA. The rate constant (k) represents the concentration of chemical species (Ci), and the stoichiometric coefficients (ai and bi) describe raw materials and products. The algorithm considers one first-order reaction at a time, with the probability of each reaction (ai) calculated as ai = kiCi (i: 1, 2,…, M), determining the overall reaction probability (a) from the set of ai.

The algorithm serves as a crucial component within the kinetic Monte Carlo simulation, whereby the list of events is updated at each time. This process is repeated until the simulation duration closely resembles the true temporal evolution32.

The CKS version 1.01 software is employed in combination with Gillespie’s approach to conduct kinetic Monte Carlo (KMC) simulations in the present study. The CKS package, which has been diligently developed using the C and C++ programming languages, originated at the Almaden Research Center of IBM, taking inspiration from the algorithm devised by Gillespie in 2001. The CKS package has obtained significant acknowledgment in diverse areas of chemistry over time, serving as a reliable tool to reproduce chemical reactions16,33–35.

Response surface methodology

The process of implementing Response Surface Methodology (RMS) encompasses a sequence of actions that involve conducting two-factor experiments. These experiments aim to investigate and evaluate the critical variables. In addition, the methodology incorporates the application of regression analysis, which enables the assessment of the relationship between the input variables and the desired outcome. Moreover, optimization techniques are employed to ascertain the most favorable values for these input variables33,36. Factorial experimental designs provide a more efficient approach to exploring the concurrent impact of multiple input variables on the desired variable in situations where conducting a substantial number of experiments is challenging in terms of both financial and temporal resources37. A two-factorial experimental design is a particular variant of a factorial experimental design that investigates the effect of each variable on the response. Using statistical analysis, one can ascertain the variables that exert a significant influence and determine the extent to which they impact the response variable. It is essential to highlight that in the case where there are k variables, the implementation of two-factorial experimental designs would necessitate the undertaking of 2 k trials. These designs allow for the execution of a fraction of the 2 k tests, thereby facilitating performance analysis. In the domain of RMS, a prominently utilized approach is the Central Composite Design (CCD). This particular experimental design demonstrates significant efficacy in ascertaining the impact of the input variables on the response variable. As a result, it aids in fostering a comprehensive comprehension of the fundamental connections and dynamics at play. Within the CCD framework, the influential variables are meticulously chosen and assigned as the central point. They are functioning as a reference point from which the evaluations of the variations and impacts of the variables can be made38. In this investigation, the software Design Expert version 7.0.0 was employed to ascertain the pivotal factors.

Results and discussion

Mechanism

The theoretical study and simulation results presented in this study are based on the experimental work conducted by Kim and Choi27, which explored the catalytic efficiency of Pd-CNT catalysts in reducing Cr(VI) to Cr(III) under optimal conditions. In their system, formic acid served as the reducing agent. The simulation of this system incorporated input parameters such as test temperature, Reaction time, initial concentrations of reactants, initial concentration of the catalyst’s active sites, and the rate constants for each reaction step. The initial concentrations were established at 1 × 10−3 M for Cr2O72− and 45 × 10−3 M for formic acid. The initial concentration of H+ ions was adjusted to 107 M. To align the kinetic software’s output data with the experimental findings, the initial concentration of active sites on the nanocatalyst’s surface was treated as an adjustable parameter, ultimately set at 1 M. This value was selected because it yielded the highest correlation coefficient between the simulation data and the experimental results, indicating the optimal initial concentration of active sites on the nanocatalyst’s surface. The initial concentrations of all other reactants were assumed to be zero. The experiment was conducted at 25 °C and lasted for 15 min.

The precise the reaction process in question is unknown; however, a hypothetical mechanism was suggested based on the examination of comparable research39. This hypothesis was tested using the KMC technique, and the results aligned well with actual experimental data, supporting the validity of the proposed mechanism. The production of Cr(III) using a Pd nanocatalyst involves three key steps: the adsorption of a reducing agent on the catalyst’s surface, the adsorption of Cr2O72− on the catalyst, and finally, the production of Cr(III). To better align the data obtained from the modeling and the experimental study data, step 1 (k1, k−1) and step 2 (k2, k−2) were incorporated into the modeling mechanism based on investigations conducted in various articles. The rate constant for this first step (k1, k-1) was lower than all other steps. In the case of step 2, the equilibrium constant relationship was established, and the equilibrium value of H+ was determined. It is crucial to acknowledge that the primary concentration of HCOOH is 0.45 M, while the concentration of H+ ions is 10−7 M, owing to the chemical equilibrium of the reaction (ka = 1.7 × 10−4)40. The forward and reverse rate constants were determined to be 0.04 and 2.353 × 10−6, respectively. The rate constants for the third and fourth reaction steps were determined by iterative simulation and subsequent comparison of the resulting kinetic profiles. It is crucial to acknowledge that the Cr2O72− ions and the reducing agent adhere to the active sites of the nanocatalyst’s surface, engage in a reaction with one another, and ultimately generate the desired outcome, namely Cr(OH)6. The rate constants associated with this step were found to be adjustable and were obtained through iterative simulation steps and a comparison of the corresponding kinetic profiles. At step 6, the generated Cr(OH)6 undergoes a reaction on the nanocatalyst’s surface with the reducing agent, which leads to Cr(III) production. As for step 7, it involves the dissociation of HCO2+ into H+ and CO2. Subsequently, in step 8, the adsorbed Cr(III) is desorbed. The rate constants for steps 6, 7, and 8 were determined as adjustable parameters through iterative simulation runs, followed by comparing the theoretical and experimental graphs. Challenges and future perspectives

A substantial reduction in the drinkable water because of increasing industries and population is one of the challenges for human. One of the main barriers in the water treatment is its significant costs. Using theoretical methods to study and optimize the related processes, decreasing water treatment costs. As seen in section “Comparison of the results with other studies”, the used method in this study can provide a conditions with higher efficiency compared to experimental studies.

Conclusion

In this investigation, we conducted a theoretical examination of the rate of Cr(VI) reduction to Cr(III) on Pd-CNTs nanocatalyst, with HCOOH as a reducing agent. This study has been done using both KMC simulation and RSM. The mechanism and kinetics parameters of this reaction were determined using KMC simulation. The experiment design software was utilized to evaluate the impact of various variables pH, reaction time, [Cr2O72−], and [HCOOH] on the Cr(III) production. The concentrations of HCOOH and Cr2O72− have the most notable impacts on Cr(IV) reduction, respectively. An increase in pH has a positive influence on the yield of Cr(III) production. The concentration of HCOOH and Reaction time showed the positive influences on Cr(III) production efficiency. The optimal values obtained through the experiment design program were as follows: Reaction time = 45.25 min, pH = 5.75, [Cr2O72−] = 2.575 × 10−3 M, [HCOOH] = 0.525 M. Using KMC and RSM together, we could obtaine a reaction conditon which gives the Cr(IV) reduction efficiency as 100%.
