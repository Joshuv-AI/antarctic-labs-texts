Molecular Insights into Anabaenopeptin-Mediated Inhibition of Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase B in the Mycobacterium tuberculosis Complex
Authors: Nadera Yusuf Dada, Masirah Zahid Shah, Sukriti Sacher, Manjusha P. Govindh, Pranav Bhedsurkar, Remya R Nair, Reshma Kizhakethil, Sagar Barage, Arjun Ray, Abhishek Guldhe, Neelmegam Ramesh Kumar, Kayalvizhi Nagarajan, Aruni Wilson Santhosh Kumar, Shashank S Kamble
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Among the various virulence factors produced by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, PtpB plays a critical role in regulating the M.tb pathogenesis. Genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition of PtpB significantly impairs M.tb survival within the host, making it an attractive target for effective antitubercular therapy. Previously, Brunsvicamide B, a cyclic hexapeptide produced by the freshwater cyanobacterium Tychonema sp., was reported to inhibit the enzymatic activity of PtpB. However, the molecular details of the Brunsvicamide B–PtpB interaction remain unresolved, and elucidating this interaction could provide a structural framework for the rational design of peptide-based PtpB inhibitors. In the present study, we characterized the molecular interactions of Brunsvicamide B with PtpB, PtpA, and PstP, identifying key binding parameters responsible for the selective inhibition of PtpB. Pharmacophore models were generated for each interaction, and the complex stability was evaluated using binding energy calculations, RMSD, and RMSF analyses. Comparative analysis with the known PtpB inhibitor OMTS (PDB ID: 2OZ5) revealed that Brunsvicamide B binds to similar functional residues within the catalytic pocket of PtpB. Using the Brunsvicamide B-PtpB complex as a reference model, we screened a library of Anabaenopeptins for their ability to inhibit PtpB and its homologues across the members of the M. tuberculosis complex. This approach led to the identification of three candidate peptides exhibiting favorable safety, bioavailability, and excretion profiles compared to OMTS. Thus, the shortlisted Anabaenopeptins represent promising leads for the development of pan-MTBC PtpB inhibitors with potential application in next-generation TB therapeutics.

Introduction

According to the Global Tuberculosis Report 2024, an estimated 10.8 million tuberculosis (TB) cases were reported globally, with 1.25 million deaths attributed to the disease. The emergence of drug-resistant strains has further complicated efforts to eradicate TB.

Among the major virulence factors produced by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M.tb) are the serine/threonine/tyrosine phosphatases, protein tyrosine phosphatase A (PtpA), protein tyrosine phosphatase B (PtpB), and protein serine/threonine phosphatase (PstP). Together, these phosphatases control many essential aspects of M.tb pathogenesis such as stress response and bacterial adaptation inside the host The PtpB deletion mutant of M.tb has been reported to exhibit about 70% reduction in survival within the lungs of infected guinea pigs. Moreover, PtpB is crucial in promoting M.tb survival within macrophages through the inhibition of ERK1/2 and P38 signaling pathways, thereby reducing the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and reduced apoptosis of the infected macrophages. Recently, PtpB-mediated dephosphorylation of host phospholipids was shown to inhibit inflammosome-induced pyroptosis of the infected macrophages with reduced intracellular survival of M.tb. Due to the significance of PtpB in modulating host immune response and cellular signaling pathways, it has emerged as a promising target for the effective intervention of TB. Therefore, it is essential to screen for natural inhibitors of PtpB with the least toxicity on hosts. Previously, structure-based designing of PtpB inhibitors has shown successful inhibition of MDR TB in vitro and in vivo as well. Moreover, these inhibitors sensitized the MDR TB strains to the first-line anti-TB drugs.

Microalgae produce a variety of bioactive compounds with diverse biological activities such as peptides, enzymes, vitamins, fatty acids, alkaloids, and pigments. , A few genera of marine microalgae such as Lyngbya, Isochrysis, Chromulina, Tetraselmis, Nostoc, Tychonema, Westiellopsis, and Fischellera have shown their antimycobacterial potential with a few reporting the potential growth inhibition of drug-resistant M.tb as well. For instance, cyclic desipeptides, pitipeptolides A and B, isolated from the marine cyanobacterium, Lyngbya majuscula, and their four structural analogues C–F showed the potential for M.tb inhibition with pitipeptolide F showing the maximum efficacy. Malyngolide obtained from L. majuscula using the dichloromethane extraction method was effective against Mycobacterium smegmatis. Recently, malyngolide has been shown to interfere with the bacterial quorum sensing. However, majority of other studies have been performed using the crude cellular extracts and the identification of specific bioactive compounds with antimycobacterial activity has not been done. Moreover, there is no concrete discussion over the molecular targets of these bioactive compounds in M.tb and their mode of action. Therefore, it is imperative to identify specific microalgal bioactive compounds and elucidate their antimycobacterial mechanism to accelerate the drug discovery against M.tb.

One of the studies reported the inhibition of PtpB by cyclic hexapeptide, Brunsvicamide B (IC50 7.3 μM), produced by freshwater cyanobacteria, Tychonema sp. Brunsvicamide B was shown to selectively inhibit the enzymatic activity of PtpB but not PtpA and PstP. This was the first report toward the identification of natural inhibitors against PtpB from microalgae. However, this class of bioactive peptides, termed Anabaenopeptins, has not been thoroughly investigated thereafter for their potential application against PtpB. Additionally, the molecular details of the Brunsvicamide B-PtpB interaction have not been solved till date.

In the present study, we characterized the molecular interaction between Brunsvicamide B and the three M.tb phosphatases PtpB, PtpA, and PstP, highlighting key binding parameters for selective inhibition of PtpB. Using the Brunsvicamide B-PtpB complex as reference, a library of Anabaenopeptins (n = 91) was screened for PtpB inhibitory potential, leading to the identification of three promising candidate peptides. Since the other members of the M. tuberculosis complex (MTBC) also encode homologues of PtpB, we evaluated the ability of the shortlisted Anabaenopeptins to inhibit these homologues as well. Our findings indicate that these shortlisted Anabaenopeptins function as pan-mycobacterial PtpB inhibitors. ADMET analysis revealed favorable pharmacokinetic properties including the safety, bioavailability, and excretability of the shortlisted Anabaenopeptins compared to the synthetic inhibitor, OMTS. However, the in vivo effects of PtpB inhibition by these Anabaenopeptins on M.tb survival and pathogenesis require further validation using relevant infection models.

Results

Molecular Interaction of PtpB, PtpA, and PstP with Brunsvicamide B

Previously, Muller et al. showed the inhibition of the enzymatic activity of PtpB by Brunsvicamide B (IC50 7.3 μM) but not of PtpA (IC50 ≥100 μM). Therefore, to characterize the molecular interaction between Brunsvicamide B and M.tb Ser/Thr/Tyr phosphatases, molecular docking studies of Brunsvicamide B with PtpB, PtpA, and PstP of M.tb were performed. The active pockets of PtpB, PtpA, and PstP were accessed using a structure-guided approach, where the docking grid was centered around the known catalytic residues identified from UniProt. The catalytic residues of PtpA are Cys11, Asp126, and Arg130, whereas the catalytic residues of PstP are Asp215, His174, His176, and Arg140. For PtpB, the catalytic residues identified from UniProt are Cys160, Phe161, Lys164, Asp165, and Arg166. The amino acid residues involved in interaction with the known inhibitor, OMTS, which is found cocrystallized in the PDB structure, are Ser57, Arg57, Glu60, Pro81, His94, Phe98, Leu101, Tyr125, Glu129, and Phe133. Together, the catalytic residues from Uniprot and the residues interacting with OMTS are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. The binding pockets were visualized and confirmed by using PyMOL and CASTp analyses, ensuring accurate ligand placement within the functional catalytic cavity. To assess the suitability and dimensions of the active sites, the surface cavities of PtpA, PtpB, and PstP were analyzed using the CASTp 3.0 server. The active pockets were defined based on the spatial clustering of conserved catalytic residues from UniProt. The calculated cavity volumes of the functional catalytic sites were 583.422 Å3 for PtpB, 94.994 Å3 for PstP, and 60.970 Å3 for PtpA. These cavity volumes reflect the ability of each phosphatase to accommodate Brunsvicamide B. The larger and deeper cavity of PtpB corresponds to the OMTS inhibitor-binding pocket observed in the cocrystallized PDB structure (PDB ID: 2OZ5), whereas the relatively smaller cavity in PtpA may contribute to partial projection of Brunsvicamide B’s bulky side groups outside the pocket in postdocking poses.

In the molecular docking studies, the lowest binding energy of −12.2 kcal/mol was observed for Brunsvicamide B docked with PtpB. However, the binding score of Brunsvicamide B with PtpA and PstP was higher with the values of −6.9 and −7.1 kcal/mol, respectively. The results show that the binding affinity of Brunsvicamide B is stronger with PtpB compared to PtpA and PstP and showed lower binding energies among all the 10 poses with PtpB (Table ). Furthermore, molecular dynamics simulation was performed using the best docked pose of Brunsvicamide B with PtpB, PtpA, and PstP to assess the stability of docked complexes at the atomic level. The stability of Brunsvicamide B docked within the substrate binding domain of PtpB, PtpA, and PstP was evaluated within the simulation time frame of 100 ns.

Root-Mean-Square Deviation (RMSD)

The conformational stability of PtpB, PtpA, and PstP upon binding to Brunsvicamide B was evaluated by using RMSD analysis, as shown in Figure a. RMSD provides insight into structural deviations relative to the initial equilibrated conformation, with lower values indicating greater stability. Among the three proteins, it was observed that PtpA exhibited the highest stability throughout the 100 ns simulation, maintaining an RMSD close to 0.1 nm with minimal fluctuation. PtpB showed moderate backbone deviation, ranging from 0.2 to 0.3 nm, while PstP demonstrated the highest flexibility, with RMSD values fluctuating between 0.35 and 0.4 nm. Moreover, the ligand RMSD analysis in Figure b showed that Brunsvicamide B maintained a stable binding conformation with PtpB, with RMSD values consistently ranging between 0.5 and 0.7 nm. In contrast, Brunsvicamide B exhibited greater positional fluctuations when bound to PtpA and PstP, suggesting fewer stable interactions in these complexes.

Root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) for (a) PtpB, PtpA, and PstP when bound to Brunsvicamide B and (b) ligand (Brunsvicamide B) when bound to PtpB, PtpA, and PstP.

Although PtpB exhibited the largest pocket volume, postdocking analysis revealed partial projection of Brunsvicamide B’s bulky groups outside the cavity. In contrast, in PtpA and PstP, Brunsvicamide B was more deeply buried within the binding groove, with its bulky group fitting snugly into the cavity of an enclosed binding environment. This observation can be attributed to differences in pocket and residue orientation. The PtpB pocket, while volumetrically larger, is more solvent-exposed and open-ended, which allows greater flexibility but less constraint for ligand binding. This open architecture likely enables Brunsvicamide B to adopt energetically favorable conformations, even if part of the molecule protrudes. In contrast, PtpA and PstP possess narrower, more tunnel-like active sites, which promote deeper ligand insertion but can restrict conformational adaptation. Moreover, MD simulation revealed a notable shift in the ligand orientation within PtpB. Brunsvicamide B became more stable and deeply embedded in the catalytic pocket of PtpB over the course of the simulation. This contrasted with the behavior of PtpA and PstP, where the ligand exhibited higher fluctuations and did not settle as firmly within the catalytic pocket. This dynamic stabilization of the ligand in PtpB suggests that its broader cavity and surface complementarity allow Brunsvicamide B to optimize its interactions over time, leading to enhanced structural accommodation and likely stronger binding affinity.

Overall, in the case of PtpB, Brunsvicamide B was bound in the initial docked state and then moved to a different position (0.5 nm) but later stabilized. However, in the case of other two proteins, i.e., PtpA and PstP, Brunsvicamide B partially moved out of the binding site, indicating poor binding affinity.

The conformation of the protein–ligand complexes visualized for the 0 ns simulation frame and their two-dimensional (2D) interaction are represented in Figure (I). The 3D conformation of the initial complex is shown in Figure (I­(a, c, e)). Approximately, 10 amino acid residues were involved in the interaction of Brunsvicamide B and PtpB. There were two conventional hydrogen bond interactions with residues Arg79 and Arg83 and two carbon hydrogen bonds with Arg186 and Pro101 of PtpB, as shown in Figure (I­(b)). These interactions were identified using the BIOVIA Discovery Studio based on spatial criteria, including donor–acceptor distance and bonding angles. Although carbon–hydrogen bonds are weaker than conventional hydrogen bonds, they are recognized contributors to protein–ligand stability. Such interactions, often referred to as C–H···O hydrogen bonds, have been reported to support ligand orientation, enhance binding specificity, and contribute additively to the overall binding affinity within the active site. The structural and energetic significance of these interactions in biomolecular complexes has been previously demonstrated. − Brunsvicamide B formed a π-alkyl interaction with Phe118, Phe153, Met226, Phe181, Ile227, and Phe231 residues of PtpB. On the contrary, the interaction of Brunsvicamide B with PtpA showed a single conventional hydrogen bond with Asp123 (Figure (I­(d))) whereas multiple interactions were observed between Brunsvicamide B and PstP as shown in Figure (I­(f)). In the latter, four conventional hydrogen bond interactions were observed with residues His176, Asp215, Arg140, and Ala173 and two carbon hydrogen bond interactions with Ser175 and His174. Brunsvicamide B also formed a π-alkyl interaction with Pro177, Pro216, and Pro248 residues of PstP. The presence of both conventional and carbon–hydrogen bonds in the Brunsvicamide B–PtpB complex further substantiates the high binding affinity and stable interaction plot. Thus, Brunsvicamide B exhibited robust binding affinity toward PtpB and PstP due to its numerous observed interactions.

Root-Mean-Square Fluctuation (RMSF)

RMSF values were calculated to investigate the fluctuations of the docked complexes at each residue against the function of time. Majority of the residues of PtpA, PstP, and PtpB showed RMSF < 0.3 nm with very few fluctuations during the 100 ns of the simulation (Figure a). For PstP, although the full-length protein comprises 514 amino acids, molecular docking and MD simulations focused on the N-terminal catalytic domain (residues 1–250), which has all the conserved functional residues essential for catalysis and ligand interaction. The C-terminal region (∼250–514 residues) was excluded due to its intrinsically disordered nature, lack of structural templates, and absence of any established role in catalytic activity or substrate binding. Notably, the catalytic domain of PstP has been crystallized independently, confirming that the core phosphatase function is retained without the C-terminal end. This truncation approach aligns with prior studies investigating bacterial PP2C-type phosphatases, which consistently demonstrate that the C-terminal region is nonessential for enzymatic activity or inhibitor binding. In comparison, the full-length sequences of PtpA (183 aa) and PtpB (278 aa) already include the complete structured domains required for catalytic function and were used as it is. Hence, the absence of the C-terminal tail in PstP does not compromise the accuracy of the RMSF or MD simulation results as it does not contribute to either protein–ligand binding or overall conformational stability. This ensures a fair structural basis for comparing the three phosphatases during the MD run.

Solvent-Accessible Surface Area (SASA) Analysis

SASA is useful in quantifying the amino acid residues exposed to solvents during the molecular dynamic simulation on a time scale. SASA of the protein is the average for all of the amino acid residues during the 100 ns simulation. Changes in the SASA values for an amino acid indicate its involvement in the protein–ligand interaction. In the case of Brunsvicamide B/PtpB interaction, the value of SASA was 140 nm2 during the 100 ns simulation, whereas the value of SASA was 120 nm2 for Brunsvicamide B/PstP and 80 nm2 for Brunsvicamide B/PtpA interactions, respectively. Overall, the binding of Brunsvicamide B to all three proteins (PtpB, PtpA, and PstP) did not produce any global change to the protein conformation (Figure (b)).

Additionally, hydrogen bond analysis was performed to assess the binding strength of the PtpA, PtpB, and PstP bound with Brunsvicamide B. About 1 to 2 H-bonds were observed in a majority of the frames during the simulation for PtpB (Supplementary Figure S2). Furthermore, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to emphasize the similarities and differences in the conformation and energy profile of the MD trajectory data. PtpP exhibited lower dispersion indicating less variation in the conformation whereas PtpA showed conformational variation with high dispersion. In the case of PtpB, dispersion was observed, but most of the conformation was localized, suggesting less variation as depicted in (Supplementary Figure S3 (a, b, c)).

To calculate the free energy landscapes for all the three ligand–protein complexes, 2D free energy graphs were generated by plotting the first and second main components from the PCA. The lowest free energy configurations are shown in a darker color. The greater the area of the plot that is covered by the darker shade, the greater the prevalence of conformations with lower energy. The Brunsvicamide B–PtpB complex showed the minimum energy state in most frames of the simulation compared to PstP and PtpA (Supplementary Figure S3 (d, e, f)). The larger surface area of the minimum energy state of the Brunsvicamide B–PtpB complex reflects a higher chance of acquiring the minimum energy state. This aligns with the earlier observation.

Thus, overall Brunsvicamide B showed a relatively higher binding affinity with PtpB in comparison to PstP and PtpA. It demonstrated sufficient interactions with the active site residues of PtpB as seen in the best docked pose. Brunsvicamide B bound to PstP and PtpA showed relatively lower binding affinity compared to PtpB, which was also validated in the simulation study. Therefore, Brunsvicamide B was used as a reference peptide to screen other Anabaenopeptins for the potential inhibition of PtpB.

Furthermore, the selected Brunsvicamide B–PtpB complex was rerun for triplicates to ensure the reproducibility and reliability of the MD simulation results. In Supplementary Figure S4 (a), the RMSD of PtpB backbone remains relatively stable throughout the simulation, fluctuating between 0.1 to 0.4 nm, with all three runs showing similar trends and converging behavior after 20 ns, indicating consistent protein stability and convergence during the simulation. On the other side, Brunsvicamide B RMSD as in the Supplementary Figure S4­(b) shows a noticeable increase until 40 ns, followed by a sudden jump around that time point, stabilizing thereafter in the range of 0.6 to 0.9 nm. This shift may suggest a conformational rearrangement or reorientation of the ligand within the binding pocket, after which the ligand maintains a stable binding mode. Overall, the RMSD profiles confirm that the protein–ligand complex achieves structural equilibrium during the simulation, with consistent patterns across all three replicates.

Site-Directed Mutagenesis of PtpB

To assess the stability of the Brunsvicamide B-PtpB complex, site-directed mutagenesis was performed. A total of 100 single-point PtpB mutants were successfully generated. The point mutations were introduced through systematic substitution of selected amino acid residues with alternative amino acids, resulting in a comprehensive mutant library. Each mutant was saved as a separate PDB file for subsequent structural and functional analyses. Furthermore, the duplicates were removed, which resulted in a total of 95-point mutants. Drug–target interaction predictions between these 95 PtpB mutants and Brunsvicamide B were successfully performed using the DeepPurpose TransformerCNN model. The interaction score for each mutant was obtained and compared to that of the wild-type protein. 77 mutants showed predicted interaction scores equal to or higher than those of the wild type, indicating a potential enhancement or retention of binding affinity toward Brunsvicamide B. These 77 mutants were selected for further structural and functional evaluation. Structural stability analysis using FoldX successfully differentiated among the 77 high-affinity PtpB–Brunsvicamide B mutant complexes. Based on the calculated ΔΔG values, 45 mutants showed energies that were equal to or more favorable than the wild-type complex. The amino acid substitutions in these 45 mutants are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. On the other hand, the ΔΔG values and predicted IC50 values of these shortlisted PtpB mutants are provided in the Supplementary Tables S4 and S5. This additional screening step refined the PtpB mutants to structurally robust candidates suitable for further screening.

Molecular docking of the 45 selected PtpB mutants with Brunsvicamide B yielded a range of binding energies, from −8.6 to −6.2 kcal/mol. Among these, one mutant, F181P, demonstrated the lowest binding energy, indicating the strongest predicted interaction with Brunsvicamide B (Figure ). Furthermore, MD simulation of 100 ns duration confirmed the stability of the interaction. The RMSD trajectory of the F181P-PtpB bound to Brunsvicamide B remains relatively stable throughout the 100 ns simulation, fluctuating around 0.15 to 0.2 nm, indicating good structural stability of the mutant–ligand complex as given in Supplementary figure S7.

Screening of Anabaenopeptins for PtpB Inhibitory Activity

Brunsvicamide B belongs to the Anabaenopeptin family of cyanobacterial peptides. Apart from Brunsvicamide B, the other members of this peptide family are yet to be explored for their inhibitory potential against PtpB and its homologues in MTBC members. To address this, among the 91 Anabaenopeptins retrieved from the literature, 64 Anabaenopeptins were selected for further studies based on structure availability. These Anabaenopeptins were termed Inhibitor_PtpB1-64 for representation. Supplementary Table S6 shows the binding affinities of all 64 shortlisted Anabaenopeptins with PtpB along with the number of hydrogen bonds formed and the functional residues involved in the interaction. The interaction between these Anabaenopeptins and PtpB was plotted using the BIOVIA discovery studio, and their intermolecular interactions were visualized. These inhibitors (Inhibitor_PtpB1-64) were screened based on the binding affinity, involvement of functional residues, and the number of hydrogen bonds, which led to the shortlisting of three Anabaenopeptins, Inhibitor_PtpB3 (Anabaenopeptin-SA10), Inhibitor_PtpB10 (Anabaenopeptin-857), and Inhibitor_PtpB47 (Brunsvicamide-C) (Figure ). Our results strongly correlate with earlier study wherein Brunsvicamide-C was shown to inhibit the enzyme activity of PtpB.

Functional residues in a protein are vital for specificity, stability, and catalysis in protein–ligand interactions, which are crucial for drug design. MD Simulation of the Shortlisted Anabaenopeptins-PtpB Complex

The RMSD analysis was performed to evaluate the conformational stability of the shortlisted Anabaenopeptins within the PtpB binding site over a 100 ns simulation. Figure a shows the RMSD of PtpB when bound to Anabaenopeptin-SA10, which demonstrates the lowest and most stable RMSD values, consistently remaining around 0.15 nm. It indicates minimal deviation and high structural stability of the complex. In contrast, the PtpB-Anabaenopeptin-857 complex exhibits the highest RMSD values, ranging from 0.2 to 0.35 nm, indicating increased flexibility and conformational fluctuations in the protein structure. The PtpB-Brunsvicamide-C complex shows intermediate behavior with RMSD values between 0.2 and 0.3 nm, reflecting moderate structural stability. These observations imply that Anabaenopeptin-SA10 induces the least conformational perturbation in PtpB, whereas Anabaenopeptin-857 results in greater structural dynamics. Figure b shows the RMSD of ligands when bound to protein PtpB. Among the three, Brunsvicamide-C exhibits the most stable binding, with RMSD values consistently around 0.3–0.4 nm, indicating minimal fluctuation and strong retention within the binding pocket. Anabaenopeptin-857 shows moderate stability, with RMSD values fluctuating between 0.5 and 0.6 nm, suggesting some flexibility but overall stable interaction. In contrast, Anabaenopeptin-SA10 displays the highest RMSD values, reaching 0.9 nm toward the end of the simulation, reflecting significant conformational changes and potential displacement from the binding site. Overall, Anabaenopeptin-SA10 demonstrates the most favorable binding stability based on RMSD analysis.

Screening of Shortlisted Anabaenopeptins for PtpB Inhibition among MTBC Members

MTBC comprises various species of mycobacteria that cause TB-like infections in different hosts. PtpB is conserved among the members of MTBC and may play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of these MTBC members in their respective hosts. Therefore, the three shortlisted Anabaenopeptins were assessed for the inhibition of PtpB homologues from other members of MTBC.

Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was performed using ClustalW, as shown in Supplementary Figure S8 to compare the amino acid sequence of PtpB with its homologue from MTBC members. Comparing the MSA outcomes for the PtpB homologues, it is evident that M. bovis, M. orygis, M. africanum, and M. canetii had significant alignment scores of 99.63, 100, 100, and 98.18%, respectively, with respect to PtpB as shown in Supplementary Table S7. The modeled structures can have varied accuracy, and therefore, it is essential to evaluate the quality of the modeled structure. Additionally, the binding-free energy analysis from the MM/GBSA calculations of all of the protein–ligand complexes during the last 20 ns was determined and is given in Supplementary Table S10. The results show that the M. africanum-PtpB_Anabaenopeptin-SA10 complex has the most favorable total binding energy (ΔTOTAL = −59.23 kcal/mol), closely followed by the M. bovis-PtpB_Brunsvicamide-C complex (−58.81 kcal/mol), while the M. bovis-PtpB_Anabaenopeptin-857 complex shows the weakest binding affinity (−34.94 kcal/mol). Thus, based on this analysis, M. africanum-PtpB_Anabaenopeptin-SA10 is the best binder among the three complexes.

Overall, our results suggest that Anabaenopeptin-SA10, Anabaenopeptin-857, Brunsvicamide-C, and Brunsvicamide B can be used for the effective inhibition of PtpB homologues from M. africanum and M. bovis, respectively. Since PtpB is essential for the survival of mycobacteria inside their hosts, its inhibition using the shortlisted Anabaenopeptins could help to reduce the disease severity. Moreover, these Anabaenopeptins may be used along with the existing antitubercular drugs for combating drug resistance.

ADMET Prediction

The drug-likeness profile comparison as shown in Table clearly indicates that the inhibitors identified in this study, Anabaenopeptin-SA10, Anabaenopeptin-857, and Brunsvicamide-C, exhibit superior characteristics compared to the known inhibitor OMTS. Among these inhibitors, OMTS demonstrates the most favorable drug-likeness profile in terms of Lipinski’s rule, with only one violation, whereas anaebaenopeptin-SA10, anaebaenopeptin-857, and Brunsvicamide-C each show three Lipinski violations, indicating reduced oral bioavailability potential. However, the newly identified Anabaenopeptin inhibitors outperform OMTS across other drug-likeness filters. All these inhibitors score higher on the Ghose filter and Muegge filter, suggesting improved drug-like properties in terms of molecular weight, lipophilicity, and structural complexity. While the bioavailability score is higher for OMTS (0.56 vs 0.17), all compounds show zero PAINS alerts, indicating minimal interference in high-throughput screening assays. Despite having equal lead-likeness violations (3), the improved Ghose and Muegge scores suggest that the new inhibitors, particularly Brunsvicamide-C, may offer better overall drug-likeness profiles beyond just Lipinski compliance, making them promising candidates for further optimization.

The ADME-Tox profiles of the identified peptides were compared against the known inhibitor OMTS to evaluate their pharmacokinetic behavior and is given in Table . In terms of absorption, OMTS exhibited superior Caco-2 permeability (0.873 log Papp) and higher predicted intestinal absorption (65.68%) compared to anaebaenopeptin-SA10 (17.99%), anaebaenopeptin-857 (18.06%), and Brunsvicamide-C (3.35%), indicating a better oral absorption potential. In distribution, OMTS showed more favorable BBB permeability (−0.508 log BB) and CNS penetration (−2.571 log PS), while the Anabaenopeptins demonstrated lower CNS accessibility. In toxicity assessments, all the Anabaenopeptins were found to be nonmutagenic (AMES test), nonsensitizing to skin, and demonstrated comparable oral acute toxicity (LD50 values: 2.50–2.66 mol/kg). Collectively, although OMTS exhibited superior absorption and CNS distribution properties, the identified Anabaenopeptins demonstrated acceptable safety profiles with favorable plasma unbound fractions and comparable excretion profiles. Thus, despite certain pharmacokinetic limitations, the Anabaenopeptins may still serve as promising candidates with drug-like and nontoxic characteristics, warranting further investigation and potential optimization.

Discussion

M.tb has surpassed the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) to become the leading cause of global deaths due to infectious diseases. The increasing number of drug-resistant TB cases has hugely impacted developing countries such as India. Therefore, there is an urgent need to design and discover alternative drug candidates targeted against the key virulence factors involved in M.tb persistence within hosts. Among the major virulence factors produced by M.tb are the serine/threonine/tyrosine phosphatases PtpA, PtpB, and PstP, of which PtpA and PtpB are tyrosine phosphatases, while PstP is a serine/threonine phosphatase. Among these, PtpB has become an attractive drug target for antitubercular therapy due to its significance in modulating immune response and cellular signaling within hosts promoting bacterial persistence.

However, there is a lack of selective PtpB inhibitors with minimum host toxicity. Previous studies have screened various synthetic and natural compounds as probable PtpB inhibitors. For instance, owing to the molecular mimicry between PtpB from humans and M.tb, screening of human tyrosine phosphatase (hPTP1B) inhibitors against PtpB led to the identification of an oxamic acid derivative, (oxalylamino-methylene)-thiophene sulfonamide (OMTS), as the competitive inhibitor of PtpB. In a similar study, an inhibitor library targeted against hPTP1B was screened for PtpB inhibition, leading to the identification of an isoxazole derivative with dual-site inhibition of PtpB. Another screening approach of sulfonyl hydrazones against PtpB depicted the formation of a stable complex between shortlisted inhibitors and PtpB through van der Waals and hydrophobic interactions. Using a nonpeptide library of 800 natural compounds, Mascarello et al. identified one of the most potent inhibitors of PtpB, Kuwanol E, using a combination of in silico and enzymatic assays. Recently, the structure-based design of specific PtpB inhibitors led to successful inhibition of MDR TB in vitro and in vivo, as well. Moreover, these inhibitors sensitized the MDR TB strains to the first-line antitubercular drugs. Using a similar approach, another study designed and synthesized 100 chalcone derivatives and investigated their inhibitory potential against PtpB of M.tb.

Recently, Anabaenopeptins have emerged as specific inhibitors of various enzyme classes, such as proteases, phosphatases, carboxypeptidases, and aminopeptidases. These Anabaenopeptins are produced by cyanobacteria from diverse habitats. The first study depicting the antitubercular activity of Anabaenopeptins reported the inhibition of the enzymatic activity of PtpB by Brunsvicamide B, isolated from a cyanobacterium, Tychonema sp., at an IC50 of 7.3 μM. However, this family of peptides has not been thoroughly investigated thereafter for its potential application against PtpB.

In this study, the inhibitory potential of Brunsvicamide B and other Anabaenopeptins against the PtpB of M.tb was elucidated through extensive in silico investigations. On comparing the docking outcomes, it is evident that Brunsvicamide B has a stronger affinity for PtpB than PstP and PtpA with binding energies of −12.2, −7.1, and −6.9 kcal/mol, respectively. The results are consistent with the earlier study, which showed the inhibition of PtpB enzyme activity by Brunsvicamide B but not of PtpA and PstP. Previous in silico studies aimed at identifying di- and tripeptide inhibitors against PtpB also exhibited similar results. Among the seven shortlisted templates of dipeptides, Pro-Arg exhibited maximum potency for PtpB inhibition with binding energy of −10.11 kcal/mol. Similarly, a tripeptide template of Arg-Cys-Gln exhibited binding energy of −9.46 kcal/mol.

In our study, Brunsvicamide B along with the shortlisted Anabaenopeptins formed sufficient interactions with the catalytic residues of PtpB (Cys160, Phe161, Lys164, Asp165, and Arg166) along with the residues interacting with OMTS (Ser57, Arg57, Glu60, Pro81, His94, Phe98, Leu101, Tyr125, Glu129, and Phe133), the reference inhibitor against PtpB. These results are consistent with earlier reports. In one of the studies, a nonpeptide inhibitor, KuwE, derived from the natural library of compounds exhibited H-bond formation with Glu60, His94, and Tyr125 of PtpB involved in binding with OMTS and hydrophobic interactions with Phe161. In another study, Kuwanon G and H obtained from the root extracts of Morus nigra formed H-bonds with Cys160, Tyr125, Asp165, and Arg166 in addition to the hydrophobic interactions with Phe98, Leu101, Tyr125, Met126, Phe161, Leu199, Ile203, Met206, Leu227, Val231, and Leu232 of PtpB. The additional prenyl group in Kuwanol H interacted with Phe161. A small-molecule PtpB inhibitor, L5B47, exhibited H-bond formation with Cys160, Van der Waals interactions with His94 and Phe98, and hydrophobic interactions with Leu101 and Arg57. A virtual screening approach involving structure-based inhibitor design identified compound 15 containing the thiobarbiturate scaffold as a potent PtpB inhibitor. It formed H-bonds with Tyr125, Glu129, and Arg136 and π interactions with Arg166 and Glu60 of PtpB. A phosphotyrosine mimetic, N-phenyl oxamic acid inhibited PtpB activity through H-bond formation with Phe161, Ala162, Lys164, Asp165, and Arg166. Another phosphotyrosine mimetic, α-sulfophenylacetic amide (SPAA), derived from cefsulodin (3rd generation cephalosporin) exhibited polar interactions with Cys160, Phe161, Lys164, Asp165, and Arg166 in the catalytic pocket of PtpB.

Furthermore, to assess the stability and the binding mechanism of Brunsvicamide B with PtpB, site-directed mutagenesis was employed. Previous studies have utilized site-directed mutagenesis to understand the binding mode of PtpB inhibitors. One study substituted Phe161, K164, and Asp165 of ptpB with Ser, Gly, and Ile, respectively. Results showed the decrease in the binding affinity of the mutants compared to the wild-type ones. In our study, selected amino acid residues in the PtpB catalytic pocket were replaced with alternate ones to generate a library of 95 PtpB mutants. The 95 mutant sequences were screened based on their binding profiles, changes in Gibbs-free energy (ΔΔG), and predicted IC50 values. The resulting 30 PtpB mutants were docked with Brunsvicamide B, and one mutant F181P_PtpB was shortlisted upon comparing its binding affinity. F181P_PtpB showed the lowest binding energy when bound to Brunsvicamide B and showed stable RMSD trajectory (between 0.15 and 0.2 nm) during the 100 ns MD simulation study. Interestingly, the predicted IC50 value of Brunsvicamide B toward F181P_PtpB was slightly less (6.563 μM) compared to the wild-type PtpB (6.587 μM). The predicted IC50 value of Brunsvicamide B toward wild-type PtpB was found to be closer to the observed value of 7.3 μM. We observed that the mutants of PtpB showed almost equal or even greater binding affinities compared to the wild-type PtpB toward Brunsvicamide B with comparable IC50 values. Moreover, among the shortlisted Anabaenopeptins, Anabaenopeptin-SA10 shows stable interaction with F181P_PtpB. This suggests the robustness of Brunsvicamide B and Anabaenopeptin-SA10 as the PtpB inhibitor and indicates that the occurrence of point mutations in the catalytic residues of PtpB shall not affect their inhibition potential. Therefore, it could be used even against the M.tb strains harboring point mutations in PtpB.

Furthermore, molecular docking results were validated using molecular dynamic simulation of the 100 ns time frame to calculate the RMSD, RMSF, SASA and evaluate the hydrogen bond analysis during the Brunsvicamide B–PtpB interaction. In the molecular dynamic simulation studies, Brunsvicamide B showed positional stability and stronger binding affinity, establishing sufficient interactions with the active site residues of PtpB. It remains bound to the PtpB catalytic pocket throughout the 100 ns duration of the simulation. In the case of PtpA and PstP, it moved out of the binding pocket. The reproducibility of the results was confirmed through three independent MD simulation runs. These results highlight the potential of Brunsvicamide B as a therapeutic inhibitor of PtpB and provide a structural framework for the design of peptide-based inhibitors against PtpB.

In addition, using the molecular interaction between Brunsvicamide B-PtpB as a reference, other Anabaenopeptins were screened for the potential inhibition of PtpB. To the best of our knowledge, none of the previous studies have investigated the antitubercular potential of the Anabaenopeptin family of cyanobacterial bioactive peptides. Our screening led to the identification of three Anabaenopeptin inhibitors showing efficacious interaction with the functional residues of PtpB. The shortlisted anabenopeptins, anabenopeptin-857, anabenopeptin-SA10, and Brunsvicamide-C, also exhibited strong binding affinities toward PtpB with binding energies of −9.8, −9.1, and −8.4 kcal/mol.

M.tb is a member of the M. tuberculosis complex (MTBC) that contains closely related mycobacterial species. The members of MTBC include species such as M. africanum, M. orygis, M. bovis, M. canettii, M. marinum, M. ulcerans, and M. smegmatis. These members infect humans as well as animals but can also be found in soil, air, dust, and water. −

Among the MTBC members, M. africanum is known to cause TB in a significantly high number of patients particularly in West Africa. , M. orygis primarily affecting animals such as cattle, rhino, and monkey have been known to cause human infections as well particularly in South Asia. , M. bovis causes TB in cattle but can also be transmitted to humans through the ingestion of infected milk. , Infection caused by M. canettii leads to pulmonary TB and is particularly prevalent in the horn of Africa consisting of countries such as Djibouti and Ethiopia. It can also infect human adipose tissues. M. marinum causes TB-like infection in fish but can also cause mild to severe skin infections in humans upon exposure. M. ulcerans and M. smegmatis are nontuberculous mycobacteria. M. ulcerans is the causative agent of Buruli ulcer, characterized by necrotizing tissue of soft tissue and skin. On the other hand, M. smegmatis causes infections of the skin characterized by the formation of subcutaneous nodules and ulcers that do not heal but recently has been linked to the formation of cardiomediastinal tuberculoma.

Despite the significant human threat posed by these MTBC members, very few studies are focused on the accurate diagnosis, treatment, and management of the diseases caused by them. PtpB is essential for mycobacterial survival within the hosts, and its catalytic domain is highly conserved among the MTBC members. Therefore, inhibitors targeted against PtpB may also be used to treat infections caused by MTBC members. In our study, to assess the potential of Brunsvicamide B and the shortlisted Anabaenopeptins as the pan PtpB inhibitors across MTBC members, these shortlisted Anabaenopeptins were further screened against the PtpB homologues from other MTBC members. Among these three peptide inhibitors, Anabaenopeptin-857 had a better binding affinity with PtpB of M. bovis with the binding affinity of −12.7 kcal/mol. On the other hand, Anabaenopeptin-SA10 had a better binding affinity with PtpB of M. africanum, and Brunsvicamide-C showed good binding affinity with PtpB of M. bovis. The expansion of this screening process to include PtpB analogues from related mycobacterial species presents significant progress in the pursuit of broad-spectrum antimycobacterial medications.

To gain insights into the pharmacological properties of the shortlisted Anabaenopeptins for their subsequent applications in hosts, drug-likeness profiling and ADMET profiling were conducted. Evaluation of pharmacokinetic properties such as oral availability, molecular weight, structural complexity, and lipophilicity showed better drug-like profiles of the shortlisted Anabaenopeptins over OMTS. In terms of oral bioavailability, OMTS exhibited better ability whereas in all the other parameters, Anabaenopeptin-857, Anabaenopeptin-SA10, and Brunsvicamide-C outperformed OMTS. In the ADMET profiling, OMTS displayed better oral and intestinal absorption along with crossing of the blood-brain barrier, whereas the shortlisted Anabaenopeptins exhibited better unbound proportions in the plasma coupled with favorable renal excretion abilities. Moreover, the Anabaenopeptin-857, Anabaenopeptin-SA10, and Brunsvicamide-C exhibited non-mutagenic nature and comparable dermal absorption properties without inducing toxicity. Overall, the results highlighted the drug-like profile of the shortlisted anabenopeptins with less toxicity. The ADMET profiles of the shortlisted Anabaenopeptins were comparable to the previously reported di- and tripeptide inhibitors against PtpB. , However, extensive in vitro characterization is essential for the optimization of the effective dosage, mode of administration, and time duration of these Anabaenopeptins necessary for PtpB inhibition.

Overall, the present study identifies and validates the potential PtpB inhibitors from the Anabaenopeptin family of cyanobacterial bioactive peptides, which can also be implicated to curb down the infections caused by other MTBC members with higher specificity and favorable pharmacokinetic profile. Thus, our study contributes significantly to the development of targeted therapy against MTBC emphasizing the importance of investigating novel bioactive compounds.

Conclusions

This study represents a significant advancement in the search for novel inhibitors of PtpB from the members of MTBC using the Anabaenopeptin family of cyanobacterial peptides. The enzymatic inhibition of PtpB from M.tb using Brunsvicamide B, a cyanobacterial peptide belonging to the Anabaenopeptin family, has been reported previously. However, their molecular interactions have not been characterized to date. In our study, using the molecular interactions between Brunsvicamide B-PtpB as a reference, we screened the Anabaenopeptin family and identified three peptide inhibitors, namely, Anabaenopeptin-857, Anabaenopeptin-SA10, and Brunsvicamide-C. These peptide inhibitors showed good binding affinity with PtpB from MTBC members, further emphasizing the possibility for developing pan antimycobacterial therapeutic intervention. Overall, this study shows that Anabaenopeptin-857, Anabaenopeptin-SA10, and Brunsvicamide-C may be used as a pan mycobacterial protein tyrosine phosphatase (PtpB) inhibitor, which are non-toxic and have favorable bioavailability and good excretion profile.

Materials and Methods

Protein and Ligand Preparation

The crystal structure of M. tuberculosis protein in complex with a specific inhibitor OMTS was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank with the PDB ID: 2OZ5 , in PDB format. The structure of PtpA was downloaded from Protein Data Bank with the PDB ID: 1U2P and for PstP was carried out through homology modeling using PDB ID: 2CM1 as a template. The Autodock Tools, part of the MGL Tools molecular visualization interface, was used to create the protein structure. The three-dimensional chemical structure of Brunsvicamide B was retrieved through the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and then converted into PDB format using PyMol tool.

Structure Prediction

Initially, the amino acid sequences of protein tyrosine phosphatase A (PtpA), protein tyrosine phosphatase B (PtpB), and protein serine/threonine phosphatase (PstP) from different mycobacterial species were retrieved from Uniprot Knowledgebase (https://www.uniprot.org/help/uniprotkb). Multiple sequence alignment (MSA) was carried out using CLUSTALW (https://www.genome.jp/tools-bin/clustalw) to compare the phosphatase sequences. Prediction of structures was done through Phyre tool (http://www.sbg.bio.ic.ac.uk/~phyre2/html/page.cgi?id=indexhttp://www.sbg.bio.ic.ac.uk/~phyre2/html/page.cgi?id=index) that based on the homology modeling application.

Structure Validation

The validation of the predicted structures was done by using Ramachandran plots. The structures were also evaluated based on their QMEAN score (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/qmean/). The structural similarity of the predicted phosphatases from other members of MTBC with those from M.tb was performed using the mTM-align (https://yanglab.nankai.edu.cn/mTM-align/) tool, which provides the TM score along with RMSD values.

Site-Directed Mutagenesis

Site-directed mutagenesis was performed on the PtpB protein structure using a custom Python script to introduce specific amino acid substitutions at the functional residues. The resulting mutant structures were saved in the PDB format for subsequent structural and functional analyses. Furthermore, to assess the potential impact of mutations on ligand binding, drug–target interaction (DTI) predictions were conducted using the DeepPurpose framework. The SMILES representation of the control compound was used as the ligand input, while amino acid sequences were extracted from the mutated PDB files. The TransformerCNN model architecture, which combines a transformer encoder for ligand SMILES and a convolutional neural network for protein sequences, was employed. This model, pretrained on the BindingDB data set, was used to predict interaction scores for each mutant–control pair. Mutants exhibiting interaction scores equal to or greater than that of the wild-type were shortlisted for further evaluation. To examine the structural stability of these high-affinity mutants, energy calculations were performed by using FoldX. Mutant structures were first optimized by using the RepairPDB function to correct structural anomalies. The Stability function was then used to calculate the folding free energy change (ΔΔG) of each PtpB–control complex relative to that of the wild-type complex. Mutants demonstrating ΔΔG values less than or comparable to that of the wild type were considered structurally stable and selected for subsequent analysis. This was further evaluated for binding potency using the PharmelNet model available on the Analogue platform by Growdea Technologies. Each mutant–control complex was submitted to predict IC5 0 values. Mutants with lower IC5 0 values compared to the wild type were shortlisted for further docking studies.

Molecular Docking

The predicted and validated structure of serine/threonine/tyrosine phosphatases of MTBC (M. africanum, M. bovis, M. canetti, M. marinum, M. origis, M. smegmatis, M.tb, and M. ulcrans) and the structure of Brunsvicamide B were docked using Autodock Vina and Autodock Suite. The conformations of the ligands were obtained, and the complex with the lowest energy was considered. The coordinates of the active site for the PtpB homologues were considered the same as the PtpB of M.tb. Grid box was constructed with the help of inhibitor-binding residues or functional residues (Ser77, Arg79, Glu80, Pro101, His114, Phe118, Leu121, Tyr145, Glu149, Phe153, Cys180, Phe181, Ala182, Lys184, Asp185, Arg186, Ile223, Leu247, and Val251), using the Autodock tool, and configuration file was prepared. The grid dimensions at center were 113.11, −36.03, and 77.98 Å for the x, y, and z axes, respectively, while the size was 20 × 20 × 16 Å for x, y, and z, respectively. The parameters were set to 20, an energy range of 4 kcal/mol, and an exhaustiveness of 100. Post docking, the 20 ligand–protein interaction poses were predicted for each Anabaenopeptin, and the binding score was analyzed.

Molecular Dynamic Simulation

MD simulation was conducted for a 100 ns time frame using a GROMOS9643a1 force field, which was implemented on the Linux-based workstation using the GROMACS 2022 package. The top docked pose based on the dock score was used for the 100 ns simulation. The topology parameters of the molecules were calculated using CHARMM36 force field parameters, which were subsequently assigned to both the ligands and protein. The CGenFF server was used to develop the topologies and force-field parameters of the hit compound and control inhibitor. Afterward, the Ewald Particle Mesh approach was employed to compute the electrostatic force over the atomic distance. The system was placed into the solvation box and hydrated using the TIP3P model. Following that, Na+ and Cl– ions were added to attain neutralization. The system underwent 50,000 reduction steps using the steepest descent (SD) method to remove steric clashes. Later, the LINCS algorithm was employed to constrain the bonds, achieving stability in the system. In addition, the entire system was raised to a temperature of 310 K using a time step of 2 fs for a simulation time of 100 ps in the NVT ensemble and pressure (NPT) for a duration of 1 ns each at 310 K and 1 atm. The coordinates of the structure were recorded every 10 ps during the entire 100 ns (100,000 ps) of the production run. The results were analyzed through the calculations of RMSD, SASA, and RMSF values and the number of hydrogen bonds using GROMACS to determine the conformational stability and variation. Moreover, the velocity scaling approach was used as a temperature coupling to simulate a constant temperature. Subsequently, the Parrinello–Rahman pressure coupling method was used to ensure a consistent pressure throughout the production process. The post-MD analysis was performed on the visual platform called “Analogue” developed by Growdea Technologies , (https://growdeatech.com/Analogue/).

ADMET Predictions

The ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion) properties of the shortlisted Anabaenopeptins were studied and evaluated using SwissADME (http://www.swissadme.ch/). The toxicity of the compounds was assessed using ProTOX-II (https://toxnew.charite.de/protox_II/) and pkCSM (https://biosig.lab.uq.edu.au/pkcsm/) online web servers.
