Behavioral interventions and respiratory virus incidence in a two-cohort study
Authors: Behnam Nikparvar, Cara Exten, Nita Bharti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Behavioral interventions are a critical tool for managing novel and emerging pathogens. Examples include hand-washing, masking, or staying home when feeling ill. The dynamics of behavioral interventions have been difficult to measure and are poorly understood. This makes it difficult to assess their impact during outbreaks. In this study, we investigate the uptake, persistence, and waning, of behavioral interventions against respiratory viruses among two cohorts in Centre County, PA. We focused on the three years following the emergence of SARS-CoV-2. We detected clusters of behaviors that followed similar patterns of engagement and variations over time. We also identified novel behavioral interventions that were introduced to combat COVID-19 that coincided with a reduction in non-COVID-19 respiratory disease incidence. Additionally, we detected changes in risk perception and changes in behaviors over time. These findings strengthen our understanding of factors that may influence behavior and inform recommendations around behavioral interventions during outbreak management, including information dissemination and behavioral guidelines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17338-x.

Introduction

Behavioral interventions (BIs), also known as non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), can play a critical role in managing infectious diseases. They are especially important when managing novel or emerging pathogens1–3for which pharmaceutical resources are limited or nonexistent4,5. Extensive research conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic estimated that BIs likely reduced SARS-CoV-2 transmission significantly6,7 as well as the incidence of other respiratory viruses, including seasonal influenza8,9. Although the impact of BIs on respiratory diseases is extensively supported by literature, their uptake, persistence, and waning are not well understood.

BIs are diverse and their uptake and persistence are impacted by many factors. Examples of BIs include practicing personal hygiene, maintaining physical distance, wearing facemasks, following quarantine and isolation recommendations, regulating the sizes of gatherings, working remotely, closing schools, and restricting travel. In the United States, some BIs, such as maintaining physical distance, wearing facemasks, or regulating the sizes of gatherings were newly implemented in 2020, following the emergence of SARS-CoV-2. Other BIs, such as practicing personal hygiene and, to some degree, staying home when feeling ill, have been consistently promoted10. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the targeted behaviors for BI strategies changed to adapt to the unfolding situation, resulting in distinct dynamics for different behaviors.

BIs are often layered to amplify their impact7,11 and recent empirical research shows that BIs may be adopted as interconnected packages rather than isolated actions10. This raises the question of whether this tendency to cluster is only in the adoption or to a larger extent in dynamics (adoption, persistence, and waning) of BIs. BI engagement depends on cognitive factors (e.g., risk perception), psychosocial factors (e.g., anxiety, social support, fatigue from practicing BIs), socioeconomics (e.g., income and education), access to information, and demographics (e.g., age and gender)12–14. Among these factors, risk perception or perceived susceptibility is frequently the focal point of interventions that are designed to change health-related behaviors. Recent findings, including research on the COVID-19 pandemic, indicate that an increase in perceived risk leads to engagement in behaviors that reduce the risk of infection15–19. However, the strength of the relationship between risk perception and behavior varies situationally15,16. Understanding links between shifts in risk perception and behavior engagement can help identify BIs that are aligned with population risk perceptions for recommendations or promote accurate risk messaging to drive protective behavioral changes.

Study setting

Centre County, PA is home to a large university that is surrounded by a non-student community in a suburban to rural setting (Fig. 1a). In 2020, the county had a population of 158,17220 and with a full-time undergraduate student enrollment of approximately 40,60021 at the University Park campus in Centre County from the Fall of 2019. The non-student population is present throughout the year while the size of the undergraduate student population present fluctuates seasonally, corresponding to the academic calendar.

Based on the 2020 Census, census block groups (CBGs) for which > 50% of ACS respondents reported enrollment as a full-time undergraduate student had a median age of 21. We defined these CBGs as student-dominated CBGs. The median age of all other CBGs in the county was in the 45–49 range and primarily represented the non-student population. Additionally, the median household income for student-dominated CBGs was between $25,000 and $29,999, while for all other CBGs, it was within the $60,000 to $74,999 range, based on the 5-year ACS adjusted for the inflation rate in 2020.

Early in the pandemic, both the student and non-student populations demonstrated high levels of adherence to BIs that were suggested by the local government and the university in response to the newly emerging coronavirus. However, each cohort exhibited a distinct pattern of COVID-19 incidence22,23 (Fig. 1b). In the fall of 2020, the student population was only partially present because most university instruction occurred remotely. Despite this effort to reduce contact rates and transmission among students, student COVID-19 cases rose rapidly and infected a large proportion of the cohort. In contrast, during the same time, non-student cases increased slowly and infected a smaller proportion of the cohort. Upon resuming in-person instruction and students returning to campus and Centre County for the Spring 2021 semester, COVID-19 cases among students increased slowly from near absent levels while cases among non-students persisted in a smaller second wave. During this time, a smaller proportion of the population from each cohort became infected with SARS-CoV-2 compared to the first wave of COVID-19. In the following semester, the Fall of 2021, both student and non-student cases increased, but student cases decreased promptly, while non-student cases of COVID-19 grew to their highest numbers ever.

Globally, seasonal influenza significantly decreased during the first year of implemented COVID-19 interventions. This was also seen in Centre County, where the incidence of seasonal influenza during the 2020–2021 season was so disrupted, it was largely absent (Fig. 1b). This reduction in reported influenza incidence occurred despite increased testing capacity for respiratory viruses, including influenza, and no significant changes in vaccination rates. Weather factors that are usually strongly correlated with influenza seasons, such as absolute humidity, were not significantly different from prior years (Fig. S1). This indicates that the BIs implemented to mitigate COVID-19 likely contributed to reducing the transmission of seasonal influenza in Centre County.

We sought to understand student and non-student cohort responses to BIs during different phases of the COVID-19 pandemic in Centre County. Specifically, we wanted to understand how behaviors might have contributed to COVID-19 outbreaks that were different between the two cohorts and how they might have impacted influenza. It is unusual to observe such a large deviation from annual seasonal influenza patterns or asynchronous outbreaks of respiratory viruses in geographically overlapping groups; cohort-level incidence data from such groups are rare. We surveyed students and non-students in Centre County about their behaviors at various time points during the first three years of the COVID-19 pandemic (Fig. 1c). We conducted cluster analysis on their survey responses to identify cohort level behaviors that showed similar dynamics across these three years. To measure changes or stability in health behaviors and risk perceptions, we assessed responses to 22 behavioral items on the survey that spanned four timeframes across the study period.

We found a consistent pattern of declining compliance with protective behaviors and increasing engagement with risky behaviors over time in both student and non-student cohorts. We observed minor increases in the levels of protective behaviors among non-students that were correlated with the emergence of the Omicron variant. Within each cohort, we identified behaviors that exhibited similar dynamics (uptake, persistence, waning). Behaviors were clustered based on engagement levels and changes over time. We also compared the dynamics between newly introduced COVID-19 interventions and traditionally promoted BIs. We found that newly introduced COVID-19 BIs (e.g., facemask and controlling gatherings and events) likely impacted the dynamics of multiple respiratory pathogens. Finally, we explored potential links between changes in behaviors and changes in risk perceptions in both cohorts. We compared the demographics of the non-student participants to the county, state, and country to assess their generalizability. Our results identified some explanatory power for risk perception on the practice of BIs. These findings can help (1) categorize BIs to provide some predictability about their dynamics, and (2) leverage risk perception to develop effective BI recommendations for future outbreak management.

Methods

Recruitment and participation

We surveyed non-student and student cohorts in Centre County, Pennsylvania about their pandemic attitudes and behaviors, including their adoption of suggested BIs following the local emergence of SARS-CoV-2. We conducted five waves (W1-W5) of surveys from 2020 to 2023 for each cohort (Fig. 1c). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects (all were over 18) and all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

To gauge interest in participation from non-students and collect demographic details, we distributed a pre-survey to residents of Centre County24. To engage a representative sample of the county population, non-student participants were recruited through the following: radio spots, internet stories, YouTube videos, newspaper ads, messaging from the university, and flyers placed at community sites throughout the county (grocery stores, community centers, etc.). Non-student participants were eligible if they were ≥ 18 years old, not registered as full-time undergraduate students, were residing in Centre County and planning to reside in Centre County until at least June 2021 (W3), fluent in English, and capable of providing their own consent. The non-student community underwent a single recruitment stage from June through September 2020 and participated in longitudinal surveys for the entire study period.

Students were recruited using an internal survey through cold-emails, word-of-mouth, PSU online news stories, announcements in classes, and campus postings. Eligible student participants were ≥ 18 years old, enrolled at the University Park campus of Penn State as full-time undergraduate students, and intending to reside in the County for the next year. New students were recruited at various points throughout the survey period to compensate for attrition resulting from graduation. For this reason, the student cohort surveys were not truly longitudinal after W3.

Due to the urgent need to begin data collection during the rapidly evolving early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, we employed convenience sampling to facilitate timely study launch in summer 2020. This approach allowed us to rapidly recruit participants from Centre County and the Penn State student body, while constrained by staffing, resources, and the time-sensitive nature of the public health crisis.

We focused on survey responses to 22 items that asked specific questions on BIs to prompt cued recall. This approach is less susceptible to recall bias than vague questions that prompt free recall. Among the 22 survey items analyzed here, six were identical across all five waves of surveys and were used in our recall bias analysis. The remaining 16 items were identical across Waves 4 and 5 and assessed T1-T4. We evaluated the frequency at which participants engaged in these 22 behaviors for both student and non-student cohorts. All waves of the survey asked questions about the then-current time frame and W4 and W5 additionally asked about one prior period. W1, W2, and W3 took place during the first year of the pandemic, while W4 and W5 occurred in the second and third years of the pandemic, respectively.

In W4 and W5 surveys, participants were asked about their behaviors, perceptions, and attitudes across one current and one prior time periods, covering the entire 2.5 years that elapsed from the local emergence of SARS-Cov-2 until the final wave of the survey. Specifically, questions covering periods T1 and T4 (Fig. 1c) were asked in W5 while T2 and T3 were asked in W4. To address potential recall bias for retrospective survey items, particularly for the T1 period (labeled as the recall period in Fig. 1c), we assessed the agreement between responses in W5 and the first three waves for the six survey items that were included across all waves.

Measuring behavior and risk perception

We surveyed participants on various BIs, including face mask usage, social distancing, personal hygiene behaviors (specifically: hand washing, not touching eyes, covering nose and mouth when coughing or sneezing), attendance at gatherings of different sizes, attendance at concerts and sports events, attendance at indoor and outdoor gatherings, restaurant dining, and working or attending class remotely. For each of the 22 survey items, participants were consistently asked in W4 and W5, “During [specific time period], how often did you do the following?” followed by a list of the 22 BIs analyzed here. The survey offered participants five standard Likert frequency scale response options: never, rarely, sometimes, most of the time, and always as well as “not applicable” and/or “prefer not to answer.” The time periods were defined using significant periods (semesters for students), times (seasons or local events for non-students), or local events that were meaningful to all participants. This was possible because participation was limited to locally residing individuals.

We captured risk perception of participants associated with each behavior by asking, “To what extent do you associate any of the following with the risk of COVID-19 transmission or infection?” in two cross sections in time for each cohort. Participants were surveyed with five main response options: decreases safety a lot, decreases safety a little, does not impact safety, increases safety a little, and increases safety a lot, as well as “not applicable” and/or “prefer not to answer.”

Analyzing behavior and risk perception

We used the common ordered categories method, also known as Likert scale variables, to measure behaviors of participants and assess their perception of risk. In parametric methods, ordinal variables can be analyzed using equally spaced scores, Ridit scores25 conditional median26 or conditional mean and scoring functions27. Our analysis of behaviors using Ridit scores revealed minimal differences in behavioral changes over time compared to the equal interval method (see section S3 and Fig. S2 in supplementary materials for a comprehensive comparison of results using equal interval and Ridit analysis scales). Given that Ridit analysis introduces uncertainties related to baseline population and considering the simplicity of the equal interval method, we present results using the latter approach.

We assigned values from 0 to 4 to both behavioral action and risk perception survey items, where 0 represented “never” or “decreases safety a lot,” and 4 represented “always” or “increases safety a lot.” Then, we calculated the average of individual responses for each survey item to represent the cohort level behavior or risk perception during each time period. Answers of “not applicable,” “prefer not to answer,” or no response were treated as if the respondent was not asked the survey item, resulting in different sample populations for each item.

We assessed the shifts in risk perceptions and behaviors for both cohorts between timeframes T3 and T4. To visualize the shifts in risk perception and potential links to shifts in behavior, we regressed behaviors against risk perception. We need to interpret the results cautiously due to inherent assumptions associated with parametrizing cohort level behaviors using the equal interval method.

Behavioral clusters

BIs that are interdependent in their use or effect may be promoted as a set and they may increase and decrease together10. We used hierarchical clustering to identify clusters of behaviors for each cohort based on levels of engagement and variations in usage of BIs over time. We determined the number of clusters using silhouette scores and visual analysis.

Recall bias

Recall bias occurs when people inaccurately remember or misreport past events or experiences. In our study, responses of participants about the T1 period were subject to recall bias because they were collected approximately two years later, during W5. Because T1 spanned the first three waves of data collection, we assessed the agreement between estimated cohort level behaviors collected during the first three survey waves (W1-W3) and in W5. Cohort level behaviors were calculated as the average of reported behaviors from W1 to W3, weighted by the number of overlapped months with T1.

There are two approaches to comparing mean values of two cross-sectional sample populations. Paired samples are used when all participants are the same between the two samples and independent samples are employed when participants are not necessarily the same. Due to the static nature of the non-student cohort compared to the turnover of the student participants, we measured recall bias using both paired and independent sample methods with the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test and Mann-Whitney U test, respectively. These tests are distribution-independent and are used to determine the significance of recall bias in small sample populations.

Results

Data 4 action survey

33 non-student residents participated in W4, and 684 non-student residents participated in W5 while 238 and 356 students individuals participated in W4 and W5, respectively (Table S1). In W1, the median non-student age was 52.5, the median student age was 21, the self-reported median household income for non-students, it was $75k to $99,999, and for students was under $25k. These statistics aligned with census extracted age and income from non-student- and student-dominated CBGs (CBGs do not precisely represent student and non-student populations). Overall, Centre County had a mean age of 32.9 years and a median household income of $61,921. Comparing the non-student W1 cohort to the state of PA, the median age is lower in both the state, at 40.9 years, and the nation, at 38.2 years. However, the state and nation are slightly older than Centre County overall. Compared to the W1 non-student cohort, the median income is lower both statewide, at $63,627, and nationally, at $64,994, but is similar to the Centre County average. The non-student participants in this study were also disproportionately female (68.9%) compared to the county (47.5%), state (51.0%), and country (50.8%). Additional detailed information on participation, demographics, and socioeconomic characteristics of the two cohorts in each wave is provided in Table S1 in the supplementary materials. Demographic data on the non-student cohort and county, state, and country are in Table S2 and the potential implications of these comparisons are examined in the discussion.

Uptake and waning of BIs

We categorized pandemic related behaviors as protective when they were intended to reduce transmission (e.g., wearing a facemask, working remotely) or risky when they could increase transmission (e.g., attending gatherings or events). Certain behaviors were not easily classified because their likelihood of reducing or increasing transmission was subject to an undefined comparison behavior. For example, outdoor restaurant dining could be compared to indoor restaurant dining, which is riskier, or not dining out at all, which is less risky. Similarly, outdoor social gatherings could be compared to riskier indoor social gatherings or not attending social gatherings.

BIs were introduced in 2020 and had peak levels of engagement in 2020 (T1) (please see Faust et al. 2021 for details on BI recommendations over time in this county23. We found that both cohorts consistently decreased practicing protective behaviors and increased engagement with risky behaviors from T1 forward (Fig. 2a). In the non-student cohort, the regional emergence of the Omicron wave in November 2021 coincided with a slight decline in risky behaviors and outdoor activity (T3). This could represent a feedback loop between behavior and disease incidence.

Compared to students, non-students reported fewer changes in their practice of BIs over the study timeframes (Fig. 2b). When SARS-CoV-2 emerged locally (T1), both cohorts acted similarly, with the non-student cohort adopting protective behaviors and avoiding risky behaviors at a slightly higher average value than the student cohort. As the pandemic continued, students reported earlier reductions in protective behaviors and increases in risky behaviors. These trajectories continued through T4.

Personal hygiene behaviors remained relatively stable throughout the study period in both cohorts. Outdoor activities, which could not be consistently classified as risky or protective, showed consistency in T1 and T4 but displayed slight declines in T2 and T3 among non-students. T2 and T3 both included winter seasons, which can prohibit outdoor activities in central Pennsylvania. Other behaviors were monotonic in both student and non-student cohorts, such as masking, which was high at early timepoints and declined.

Behavior clusters

Using hierarchical clustering to explore the trajectories of BIs from T1 to T4, we found the optimal number of clusters for both student (triangles) and non-student (circles) cohorts was four, as determined by the silhouette score (Fig. 3). Two differences between student and non-student populations were (1) among students, wearing a facemask formed one distinct class, and (2) non-students exhibited a separate class associated with outdoor activities, namely eating outdoors at restaurants, and attending outdoor social gatherings.

To delve deeper into behavior clusters, we chose a lower cut-off value on the dendrogram, resulting in seven clusters for each cohort. We observed almost consistent clusters among both student and non-student cohorts. With seven clusters, wearing a facemask formed a distinct cluster for both cohorts. Gatherings and events, including small and large ones as well as indoor/outdoor group activities, social distancing, and trips also formed distinct clusters. However, we observed some BIs interchange when comparing the clusters from the student and non-student cohorts (arrows in Fig. 3).

Overall, students consistently decreased their reduction of trips outside the house and remote class attendance while increasing attendance at gatherings and events (Fig. 3, line plots below). Non-student behavior clusters exhibited trends that may indicate more caution, with increases in protective BIs coinciding with the local emergence of the Omicron variant during the T3 survey for this cohort. Notably, personal hygiene behaviors and staying home when feeling ill maintained consistent, high levels for both cohorts. Similarly, outdoor dining and attending outdoor gatherings and events remained stable with a moderate level of engagement. The use of face masks declined significantly after T3 in both cohorts.

Risk perception and behavior

We analyzed links between the perception of risk and behavioral interventions. We measured both at the time of data collection and the self-reported practice of that behavior at the same time. We captured risk perception in W4 and W5.

For most behaviors surveyed, the risk perception and behavior engagement changed in the same direction over time (increase or decrease) (Fig. 4). Within the student cohort (Fig. 4, left panel), the greatest change in risk perception was associated with the frequency of attending gatherings and events and the size of gatherings and events, while the least change was associated with eating outdoors at restaurants, followed by personal hygiene behaviors. In the non-student population (Fig. 4, center panel), we observed similarly large changes in the perception of risk associated with the frequency of attending and the size of gatherings.

We assessed the link between the change in risk perception and the change in BIs among both cohorts (Fig. 4, right panel). The ordinary least squares regression analysis for the student cohort revealed an R-squared of 0.416, implying that approximately 41.6% of the change in BIs could be explained by change in risk perceptions. The R-squared for the non-student community regression was 0.70, indicating that changes in risk perceptions may explain 70% of the changes in BIs for this cohort. We are cautious about interpreting these statistics since ordinal variables were transformed to variables with equal interval digits.

BIs with large changes in risk perception and action (trips, gatherings, and attending work/class remotely) demonstrated that perceptions could change quickly and give rise to rapid changes in behaviors. Personal hygiene and staying home if feeling unwell remained stable in both risk perception and engagement level. Notably, both cohorts perceived masking as a behavior that greatly increased safety with minimal change in perception between the two time points while reporting a significant reduction in masking behavior. This indicates masking behavior was heavily impacted by factors other than risk perception.

Recall bias

Across W1-W5, we asked individuals about their actions with respect to a subset of six protective BIs (washing hands, wearing a facemask, trying not to touch eyes, covering mouth when sneezing, staying home if feeling unwell, maintaining 6 feet distance). Based on the methodology explained in section S4 (Supplementary Materials, Table S3, Fig. S3), we used the responses to these six questions collected from the first three waves of the survey (W1-W3), which took place during the first year of the pandemic, to calculate the recall bias of these six behaviors from retrospective questions asked in W5.

We find recall bias is statistically significant for almost all these behaviors. The mean differences indicated that non-students tended to overstate their prior compliance with most of these six BIs whereas students tended to understate their prior compliance with these BIs. Among these six behaviors, the average recall error was small for wearing facemasks, washing hands, avoiding touching eyes, covering the mouth when sneezing, and maintaining six feet (among non-students) and we anticipate no significant impact on our results. For maintaining six feet among students, the average recall error was large, indicating that students understated their compliance in T1. Despite this, we confirmed that the discrepancy did not alter clusters of BIs.

Recall bias for staying home when feeling unwell could have significant implications for our results. This behavior was understated by both cohorts in T1, with large average recall errors for students and non-students. That means both cohorts stayed home when sick during T1 more than in subsequent timeframes, resulting in a less stable behavior dynamic. For the student cohort, we are cautious when interpreting the average differences, as they are based on a small population size (n = 37). A detailed analysis of recall bias is in section S4 of the supplementary materials.

Discussion

Across the first three years of the COVID-19 pandemic, both student and non-student populations in Centre County showed a trend of declining engagement with protective behaviors and increasing engagement with risky behaviors. However, non-students displayed smaller changes in BIs than students. The emergence of the Omicron variant temporally coincided with a slight decline in risky behaviors among the non-students.

Previous research, using data that were not included in this study, showed that the novel behaviors introduced in Centre County in response to COVID-19 (e.g., masking, physical distancing, avoiding crowds) and traditional symptom-management behaviors that are reinforced annually during flu season (covering coughs and sneezes, staying home if ill) appeared as packages of adopted behaviors10. Our findings add to that by demonstrating tendencies of behaviors to cluster not only based on adoption but their level of engagement and change over time. Behaviors that were first introduced after the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic were found in multiple separate clusters and distinct from the BIs that are traditionally promoted. Across students and non-students, clusters were largely composed of similar behaviors.

Cluster dynamics showed differences, which may help identify behaviors that coincided with the massive disruptions in influenza incidence during the 2020–2021 season, seen as an absence of cases. The subsequent two influenza seasons experienced dynamics that were similar to pre-pandemic seasonal influenza (Fig. 1b). BIs were the primary factors that significantly changed during the 2020–2021 season only. Among implemented BIs, behaviors including personal hygiene behavior and outdoor activities (dining and gatherings) remained stable during the study period for both cohorts (Fig. 3). This indicates that these stable behaviors, despite high levels of engagement, were not the primary contributors to the observed disruptions in seasonal influenza during 2020–2021. Instead, it is more likely that BI clusters with high levels of engagement early in the pandemic followed by waning levels could have influenced disruptions in seasonal influenza transmission. These include behaviors related to less masking, fewer reductions in the number of trips outside the house, less remote work and class attendance, and more indoor gatherings.

A national survey completed before the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 showed that 55% of adult workers in America went to work when they were feeling sick ‘most of the time’ or ‘always’28. In Centre County, following the emergence of COVID-19, 89.79% of non-students and 63.72% of students in our survey responded that they stayed home when feeling sick ‘most of the time’ or ‘always.’ Although our data collected during W4 and W5 showed staying home when feeling ill displayed stable dynamics from T1 to T4, our recall bias analysis showed that participants reported practicing this behavior during T1 significantly more than during subsequent time frames. Together, this suggests that ‘staying home when feeling ill’ in Centre County was likely highest during T1, then was lower during T2-T4 but still higher than before the pandemic. It is likely that more Centre County residents stayed home when feeling sick after the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 than before and that, after the first year of COVID-19, they maintained a slightly reduced form of that behavior. Staying home when feeling ill could potentially have played a role in disrupting seasonal influenza during the first year of COVID-19.

Importantly, this does not imply that traditionally promoted hygiene practices have no impact on respiratory disease transmission. Our methods cannot measure the impact of BIs that are consistently in place until there is a significant change in their level of compliance.

Our analysis showed a strong correlation between changes in risk perception and engagement levels for behavioral interventions. Changes in risk perception were more strongly correlated with changes in BIs among non-students than among students. This suggests that factors other than risk perception had a greater impact on student behavior. It is important that we understand the relationship between risk perception and behavior to appropriately motivate behavioral changes for public health.

When examining specific behaviors, we categorize the relationship between perception and action into three categories. In the first category, both behavior and risk perception change, either in the same or opposite directions. For instance, attending gatherings and events in both cohorts, as well as outdoor dining among non-students, showed changes in behavior and risk perception in the same direction. We found no instances of both behavior and risk perception changing significantly and in opposite directions within this category.

In the second category, either the behavior or risk perception changes, while the other factor remains relatively stable or experiences minimal change. For example, in both cohorts, participants reported behavioral changes regarding wearing facemasks and, to a lesser extent, maintaining a 6-feet distance, taking trips outside the house, and attending classes or working remotely while the associated risk perceptions did not change. This suggests that factors beyond risk perception, such as fatigue from practicing BIs, vaccination, or institutional policies, may have exerted significant influence on behavior. For example, T4 occurred in the third year of the pandemic when a large proportion of Centre County’s population was vaccinated and the university relaxed facemask requirements and held in-person classes. The third category encompasses instances where both behavior and risk perception do not change. This category included traditional personal hygiene BIs for both cohorts as well as dining outdoors at restaurants for students.

Understanding the relationship between risk perception and behavior can help tailor BI recommendations. For example, basic hygiene BIs are generally taught to children and heavily reinforced annually for influenza season while novel BIs, such as wearing facemasks, were only recently promoted and can change quickly. A well-timed informational campaign could focus on the risks associated with respiratory viruses and the benefits of wearing a mask to reduce seasonal influenza transmission early in flu season.

Our study faced several limitations. First, responses regarding the T1 period that were collected during W5 were susceptible to recall bias. Additionally, the turnover in the student cohort throughout the study prevented the formation of a true longitudinal cohort. Although cohort surveys were rolled out in parallel, student and non-student cohorts were not always surveyed across identical time frames (specifically in T2 and T3). Even small offsets meant that one cohort might be more aware of an emerging novel variant or a new BI recommendation during the rapidly changing global pandemic. The statistical analysis of Likert score variables, especially with a high number of variables and categories, presented challenges. We employed the equal interval parametric method to interpret population-level results, necessitating caution in interpreting the statistical findings. Our survey began after the emergence of COVID-19, limiting our ability to accurately measure behaviors that preceded COVID-19, which would provide insights into the full range of variations of traditionally promoted behaviors.

Some aspects of the sample may also impact broader applicability of these results. While the study relied on convenience sampling methods, we maintained methodological rigor to ensure internal validity29. However, we recognize that the use of a non-probability sample limits the external validity of our findings. Generalizability is therefore restricted to individuals within Centre County and the Penn State student population who share characteristics with our sample. The findings should not be extended to broader populations beyond these defined groups. Specifically, the non-student population does not perfectly represent the population of the surrounding county, state, or nation in a few critical aspects. First, it is disproportionately female, as discussed earlier, as well as wealthier and older. The latter two characteristics may have enabled this population to engage in BIs that were not possible for others, such as staying home or working remotely. Additionally, 57.4% of this cohort holds a graduate or professional degree, which is far more than is found in the county (16.1%), state (11.3%), or country (11.2%). The proximity to the university and its role as a major employer in the area likely drives this large difference. This may have also influenced the likelihood of participants to follow the university messaging and guidelines, which directly reflected the CDC and PA Department of Health’s recommended BIs. The profile of the student population is similar to other undergraduate student populations at large American universities.

Future studies could follow the behavior of these cohorts over an extended timeframe. Specifically, they could explore whether behaviors that remained stable during this study might undergo changes in the future (e.g., change in staying home when feeling unwell or personal hygiene behavior). Monitoring the levels of these behaviors and tracking them through perturbations, such as future emerging respiratory pathogens, could provide valuable insights.

Conclusions

This study explored BIs, their dynamics, patterns of clustering by changes in engagement levels, and the changes in risk perception and behavior among students and non-students within Centre County, Pennsylvania. Each of these cohorts experienced slightly different dynamics of respiratory viruses from 2020 to 2023. To understand why, we focused on behaviors that were targeted by pandemic BIs, highlighting a wide range of interventions such as longstanding personal hygiene practices and behaviors that were introduced in response to COVID-19. Our findings indicate BIs waned over time with some cohort specific fluctuations. Additionally, we observed a significant tendency for behaviors to cluster based on engagement levels and variations in those levels. Our results highlighted correlations between risk perception and pandemic-related behaviors, which were different between the two cohorts studied. Categorizing behaviors into clusters based on their temporal variations and examining their relationship with risk perception can offer a systematic approach to understanding behavioral dynamics and assist in designing effective interventions.
