Moderating role of familial relationships in the efficacy of rTMS intervention on cognitive function in patients with schizophrenia
Authors: Cheng Zhu, Jing Yao, Hengyong Guan, Fengchun Wu, Meihong Xiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt has been hypothesized that rTMS improves cognitive performance in SCZ by altering neuronal activity through magnetic field pulses. However, these findings were controversial. The present study examined whether familial relationships impacted the effect of rTMS targeting the left DLPFC on cognitive function improvement in patients with SCZ.MethodsA total of 84 SCZ patients were randomly allocated to the active rTMS or sham rTMS groups and received a 6-week intervention session. Patients were followed up at the end of the 24th week. Cognitive functions were evaluated at baseline and at the end of the 2nd, 6th, and 24th weeks. Patients’ relationships with their family members were also evaluated.ResultsSignificant interactions of time × treatment on immediate memory and total scores were observed in patients with SCZ treated with rTMS. Active rTMS stimulation showed a beneficial effect on immediate memory in SCZ. In addition, we found that after controlling for confounding factors, patients with positive familial relationships experienced greater improvements in immediate memory after rTMS than those with negative relationships. More importantly, linear regression analysis revealed that familial relationship was a predictor of immediate memory improvement following rTMS intervention.ConclusionsOur results reveal that familial relationships significantly influence the outcome of rTMS treatment in patients with SCZ. Relative to patients with negative familial relationships, those with positive relationships showed greater cognitive improvement.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03774927, registered on 20,181,211.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07251-y.

Introduction

Schizophrenia (SCZ) affects approximately 21 million people worldwide [1]. SCZ is characterized by prominent positive symptoms, persistent negative symptoms, and pervasive cognitive dysfunction. Cognitive dysfunction in SCZ is a core feature of the disease, leading to poor prognosis and a substantial burden [2, 3]. It includes working memory, visual memory, attention, and processing speed, as well as abstraction-executive functions [4, 5]. These dysfunctions exist before the onset of psychosis and persist throughout SCZ [6, 7].

Despite the significant efficacy of antipsychotic medications in alleviating positive symptoms of SCZ, the treatment of cognitive dysfunctions in patients remains elusive [8, 9]. Understanding the mechanism underlying cognitive dysfunction in SCZ and developing effective therapeutic strategies is an ongoing challenge in biomedical research [10]. Studies have reported the critical role of prefrontal brain abnormalities, in particular, the cellular architecture and associated circuits of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), in the pathophysiology of higher cognitive processing deficits in SCZ, including working memory, decision-making, and cognitive control. The DLPFC is a large frontal brain region that includes the lateral parts of Brodmann’s areas 9 and 46. It may enhance complex planning and reasoning in non-human primates and humans [11]. It integrates information processing across nodes in control networks and provides top-down inhibitory control of information processing [12]. structural and functional neuroimaging studies have shown aberrant neuronal connectivity in prefrontal regions in cognitive dysfunction [13–15]. The DLPFC is involved in executive function and working memory [16–18], and studies have suggested that the left DLPFC (l-DLPFC) is a target of rTMS [19–21].

rTMS is a non-invasive brain stimulation technique that has shown promise in treating various mental disorders, including depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, SCZ, and bipolar disorder [22–25]. By targeting the l-DLPFC, rTMS modulates cortical excitability and metabolic activity and alters functional connections between different brain areas [26–29]. The application of rTMS to the l-DLPFC has been associated with improvements in cognitive functions in psychiatric disorders [30–32]. In depression disorder, rTMS has demonstrated beneficial effects, particularly in improving psychomotor speed in patients [33]. For patients with SCZ, the effects of rTMS on cognitive performance are controversial. Some studies have indicated that rTMS can improve auditory-verbal memory and facial emotion recognition in individuals with SCZ [23, 24, 34–37]. However, other studies have not found similar cognitive-enhancement effects of rTMS in SCZ [38–41]. This discrepancy in findings may be attributed to a variety of factors, including the heterogeneity of SCZ, frequency, and intensity of stimulation, duration of treatment, characteristics of the patient population, and the specific methods used to assess cognitive performance.

Most patients with SCZ have regular contact with family members. Researchers have focused on the etiological role of family communications or relationships and family interaction variables combined with a genetic variable in complex causal models of the etiological process associated with SCZ [42]. Parental communication deviations have long been considered a potential risk factor for the development of psychotic disorders in genetically sensitive offspring [43]. There is substantial clinical evidence indicating that family factors, e.g., expressed emotion, family-rearing environment, or family communication, can impact the development of psychiatric disorders [44–47]. The literature on familial relationships shows that 12% of marriages among nursing home residents with SCZ ended in divorce [48]. Another study showed that 35% of veterans with SCZ experienced divorce, compared to 28% among those without SCZ [49]. SCZ makes it difficult for some patients to establish intimate relationships. A small study revealed that some patients with SCZ reported facing internal obstacles in love relationships due to the disorder, including emotional changes, decreased trust toward others, and deficits in affective, social, and cognitive skills [50]. This study suggests that episodes of SCZ bring particular stress to familial relationships. Indeed, compared to other diseases, caring for a relative with SCZ can be particularly challenging, and caregivers of patients with SCZ tend to report poorer psychological or physical health than other caregivers.

It is widely accepted that the family environment should also be considered an important concern in clinical interventions. The family is not only the basic unit of an individual’s growth and development, but is also a key factor in influencing an individual’s mental health and behavioural patterns. In the field of clinical psychology and mental health, assessment in the family environment is an important part of the therapeutic process. Considering the potential impact of negative relationships with family members frequently reported in SCZ [43], we hypothesized that participants with negative relationships (NR) would show lesser effects and/or improvements of the interventions compared to participants with positive relationships (PR).

Materials and methods

Participants

This study was a randomized, sham-controlled, double-blind clinical trial. The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s Medical Ethics Committee and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study follows CONSORT guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

The inclusion/exclusion criteria have been described in our previous study [51]. The inclusion criteria were: (1) male veterans; (2) able to provide informed consent; and (3) stable dosage and type of antipsychotics in the past 24 weeks. Exclusion criteria included: (1) diagnosis of another mental illness; (2) major brain disease or physical illnesses; and (3) modified electroconvulsive therapy (MECT) treatment in the past six months. Ultimately, a total of 84 patients with SCZ based on the DSM-5 criteria were recruited for this study. Sixty-seven patients (79.8%) were unmarried, and ten patients (11.9%) were divorced. All unmarried and divorced patients (n = 77, 91.7%) lived with their parents before admission. Only seven patients (8.3%) were married and lived with their partners.

Sixteen patients were visited by their parents or relatives once every six months, 51 patients were visited once a year, and 17 patients were visited once every two years. Those who were visited less by their parents or relatives had longer disease duration and lower scores in attention index, delayed memory, and total RBANS scores at baseline (all p < 0.05). In our study, there were no restrictions imposed by the hospital on the frequency or duration of family visits to the patients. The decision on how often family members visited was primarily based on their circumstances and preferences. All patients enrolled in the study were residents of the area surrounding the hospital. The patients’ homes were located close to the hospital, and travel would not pose a significant obstacle for family members. Therefore, the location of the hospital did not make travelling difficult for some family members and less so for others, influencing the frequency of visits.

Procedure

All participants were randomly allocated to three treatment groups (sham, 10 Hz, and 20 Hz) (28 patients per group). The randomization list was produced by a computer and kept by a nurse. Researchers, raters, and patients were blinded to the randomization list. The nurse was the only person with access to the randomization list.

For neuronavigation-guided rTMS therapy, we used the MagStim Rapid2 system applied to the left DLPFC. All participants underwent one rTMS session each workday, with five sessions per week for six consecutive weeks. The Brainsight navigation system was employed to accurately target the DLPFC, as described in our previous studies [23, 52]. A 20 Hz stimulation frequency was used in the 20 Hz group, while a 10 Hz frequency was used in the 10 Hz group. Sham-rTMS was administered using a Magstim sham coil.

The rTMS treatment was discontinued at the end of the 6th week. At the end of the twentieth week, all patients underwent a further 18-week examination of cognitive function. Twenty-one patients (6 in the sham group, 7 in the 20 Hz group, and 8 in the 10 Hz group) were lost at the end of week 24 (Supplementary file 1).

Assessments

Cognition was assessed using the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS) at baseline and the end of weeks 2, 6, and 24. Cognition was assessed by 4 raters. The same RBANS Forms were used for all time points.

We also assessed the severity of clinical symptoms using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). To ensure consistency and reliability of ratings, raters simultaneously attended a training session in using the PANSS before the recruitment of participants in this study. After training, raters maintained an inter-rater correlation coefficient greater than 0.8 for the total score.

A brief interview was used to assess the quality of relationships with family members or relatives who lived with the patient, including spouses, parents, siblings, and children. We asked patients whether they had close and good relationships with their cohabiting family members or relatives. Responses were coded as 1 if the relationship was described as good and 0 if it was not. Based on these responses, all patients were divided into two groups: a positive-familial relationship group (PR group) and a negative-relationship group (NR group). There were 33 patients (39.3%) in the PR group and 51 patients (60.7%) in the NR group.

Statistical analysis

The missing values were imputed using the LOCF method. The analysis was done using all patients enrolled using an Intent-to-Treat (ITT) analysis. Differences in demographic variables and cognitions were analyzed by ANOVA between the groups. The main analysis was conducted using the repeated-measures ANOVA (RM ANOVA) between groups. In this study, we focused more on the intervention group × time interaction effect on patients’ cognitive performance. Comparisons of cognitive improvements between patients with positive familial relationships and negative relationships at week 2, week 6, and week 24 were analysed using ANCOVA in the active group.

As an indicator of the ability to identify the discriminatory capacity of improvements in immediate memory after rTMS stimulation, the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUCROC) was calculated. A concordance statistic between 0.7 and 0.8 was generally considered acceptable.

Cognitive improvements between PR and NR groups treated with active rTMS

Our analysis of the comparisons between the PR and NR groups demonstrated differences between the two groups in terms of the improvement in immediate memory scores after stimulations (Zweek2= −1.7, p = 0.08; Zweek6=−1.9, p = 0.06; Zweek24=−2.6, p = 0.01). The change in immediate memory scores was significantly greater in the PR group than in the NR group.

When the change in immediate memory was entered as the dependent variable, familial relationship, age, smoking status, disease duration, age of onset, educational years, total dose of antipsychotics, and baseline immediate memory as the independent variables, linear regression analysis revealed the familial relationship to be a significant predictive factor of improvement in immediate memory index (β = 0.369, t = 2.645, p = 0.011) (R2 = 0.152).

The AUCROC demonstrated that the familial relationship was satisfactory in determining the improvements in immediate memory (AUC = 0.701, S.E.=0.075, 95% CI = 0.555–0.847, p = 0.010).

Discussion

This study compared the efficacy of high-frequency rTMS applied to the l-DLPFC for 24 weeks on cognitive functions between SCZ patients with positive and negative relationships with family members. Familial relationships showed a determining role in the efficacy of rTMS on immediate memory. Patients with PR had greater improvements in immediate memory compared with those with NR after 24 weeks of treatment with rTMS.

People with SCZ usually live with their relatives, especially with their parents. In our study, we found that 91.7% of patients lived with their parents before admission. The high percentage of patients living with their parents underscores the pivotal role that family dynamics play in the lives of patients. We found that up to 60.7% of SCZ patients reported that they had a negative relationship with their family members, indicating that a large proportion of SCZ patients may have strained relationships with their family members. A lot of previous studies have focused on assessing the burdens experienced by carers and have reported significant disruption and distress in coping with disruptive behaviors and thinking of family members, including psychological distress, financial difficulties, and barriers to employment or leisure activities [51]. Consequently, this may also lead to poor familial relationships. Research has shown that higher levels of caregiver burden were associated with higher levels of clinical symptoms in patients [52]. However, our study did not find a significant difference in the baseline cognitive functions between chronic SCZ patients with NR and PR.

We further found that patients in the PR group showed greater cognitive improvements relative to those in the NR group, suggesting that the family environment may play a critical role in modulating the response to rTMS in patients with SCZ. We speculate that this association could be explained by the interplay between cognitive function and familial relationships. Specifically, patients with better baseline cognitive function not only responded better to rTMS but also maintained better familial relationships. rTMS exerts its effects by modulating neural activity in specific brain regions [53], and the efficacy of modulation may depend on the adaptability of the brain network. Patients with better baseline cognitive function likely have greater neuroplasticity, which enables them to respond more robustly to the stimulation, ultimately leading to greater improvements. In addition, previous literature has provided evidence for the relationship between familial relationships and cognitive functions [54]. Specifically, supportive familial relationships have been shown to correlate with better performance in cognitive domains, such as episodic memory, working memory, and executive functions, which are often disrupted in SCZ.

Notably, our study found that after treatment with active rTMS, immediate memory improved in both groups. Specifically, improvements in the PR group were slightly more than those in the NR group. However, this difference was relatively small, indicating a modest effect of family relationships on the response to rTMS in patients. We give a possible explanation for this. All recruited patients had a disease duration of more than 8 years, and all patients were hospitalized during this study. Family members, including parents, rarely visited the patients in the hospital, and most patients were visited by their parents or relatives once every 6 months. Thus, the effect of familial relationships was relatively small.

Several limitations were noted in the present study. First, the sample size was small, which has a significant influence on the interpretation and generalizability of our findings, e.g., reduced power, potential bias, and limited generalizability. Second, all recruited patients were male. Male patients have physiological and psychological characteristics that differ from female patients, which may limit the applicability of study results across genders. Previous studies have shown that SCZ may present differently in males and females, which could affect the efficacy of the rTMS interventions and limit the generalizability of the results to a wider population. Third, all participants were hospitalized veterans. The reason for their hospitalization was not due to the severity of their psychiatric conditions, but rather because the military service provides care facilities specifically for veterans. Additionally, veterans may face a variety of health issues unique to their military service. These health issues might differ from those seen in individuals with SCZ in the general population, which could limit the generalizability of the study findings. Fourth, familial relationships were assessed through verbal inquiry of patients rather than using standardized scales. It can provide insights into a patient’s unique perspective and feelings about their familial relationships, which may not be reflected in a standardized scale. However, verbal inquiries rely on the patient’s self-report, which may be influenced by personal biases, memory biases, or current emotional states. In addition, information obtained through verbal inquiries was difficult to quantify and compare, which may be a challenge to evaluate the effectiveness of treatments. Fifth, all patients were hospitalized throughout the study, and most were visited by their parents or relatives only once every six months. Given the limited frequency of these visits, the impact of familial relationships was relatively small. Consequently, it is challenging to explain how familial relationships could meaningfully influence rTMS effects in our study.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that rTMS was a promising intervention regardless of their perceptions of their familial relationships. Notably, after 24 weeks of treatment with rTMS, patients in the PR group exhibited significantly greater improvements in immediate memory compared with those in the NR group. This study adds a unique perspective to the existing literature that the therapeutic outcomes of rTMS are not dependent on the rTMS protocols but are also significantly influenced by the psychosocial environment, particularly the quality of familial relationships.
