Effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions for post-stroke depression: protocol for an umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses
Authors: Mali Zhang, He Tian, Yuandi Yang, Fangfang Feng, Li Yan, Yu Cai, Yanan Wei, Cong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-stroke depression (PSD) is one of the most common neuropsychiatric complications following stroke, affecting approximately one-third of patients and substantially impairing functional recovery, quality of life, and survival. Pharmacological therapies, although commonly used, are limited by adverse effects and potential drug–drug interactions. Non-pharmacological interventions have gained increasing attention as safer alternatives, and numerous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have been published. However, their conclusions remain inconsistent due to methodological variability, heterogeneity, and overlapping evidence. To date, no umbrella review has comprehensively synthesized this evidence.ObjectiveTo comprehensively synthesize evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses regarding the effectiveness and safety of non-pharmacological interventions for post-stroke depression (PSD).MethodsThis umbrella review will be conducted in accordance with PRISMA-P guidelines. A comprehensive search will be conducted for systematic reviews and meta-analyses evaluating non-pharmacological interventions, either as monotherapy or adjunctive therapy, for the treatment of post-stroke depression (PSD). The following databases will be searched from inception to December 2025: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (CDSR), Web of Science, CINAHL, and the major Chinese literature databases (CNKI, WanFang, VIP, SinoMed). No language restrictions will be applied. Search strategies will combine Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms tailored to each database. Reference lists of included reviews will also be screened, and the search will be updated before the final analysis to ensure inclusion of the most recent evidence.ConclusionThis study will provide the first umbrella review of non-pharmacological interventions for PSD. By applying rigorous methodological tools, it aims to deliver a comprehensive and methodologically robust synthesis of current evidence, support clinical decision-making, and identify priorities for future research.Systematic review registrationThis protocol was submitted for registration with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) on September 17, 2025 (registration number: CRD420251149507).

Introduction

Stroke, caused by sudden rupture or obstruction of cerebral vessels, is a leading cause of death and disability worldwide [1]. According to the Global Burden of Disease study, stroke remains the second leading cause of death globally and a major contributor to disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), imposing a substantial health and socioeconomic burden [2]. Among its neuropsychiatric sequelae, post-stroke depression (PSD) is one of the most prevalent, affecting about one-third of survivors during recovery [3]. Symptoms typically arise within the first year, particularly in patients with more severe strokes or a prior psychiatric history [4]. The pathogenesis is multifactorial, involving neurobiological changes and psychosocial stressors [5, 6].

PSD is characterized by loss of interest, psychomotor retardation, irritability, pessimism, sleep disturbance, and fatigue [7]. These symptoms not only impair psychological well-being but also hinder rehabilitation, reduce treatment adherence, and increase disability and mortality rates [8]. Furthermore, PSD substantially elevates healthcare costs and imposes long-term economic burdens on families and society [9].

Pharmacological therapy remains the primary management strategy [10]. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs) are commonly prescribed [11]. However, no single agent has shown consistent superiority, and adverse effects—such as gastrointestinal upset, sexual dysfunction, sedation, and elevated bleeding risk—limit their use. Drug–drug interactions further complicate clinical management, particularly in patients with multiple comorbidities [12].

Given these limitations, non-pharmacological interventions are increasingly recognized as safer and more acceptable alternatives [13]. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have examined therapies including acupuncture, exercise, and cognitive-behavioral therapy [14, 15]. Several meta-analyses suggest potential benefits [16, 17], yet results remain inconsistent due to heterogeneity in study design, populations, and outcome measures. Importantly, the methodological rigor of these reviews also varies: some focus narrowly on specific modalities, others pool diverse interventions, and few have applied standardized frameworks such as GRADE to assess evidence certainty. In addition, overlap among primary studies across different reviews raises concerns about redundancy and potentially inflated conclusions.

In recent years, the number of systematic reviews and meta-analyses on non-pharmacological interventions for PSD has expanded rapidly [16]. Despite this, evidence synthesis remains fragmented, with conflicting findings across reviews. Crucially, no umbrella review has comprehensively evaluated these interventions, nor has there been a rigorous appraisal of the certainty and overlap of existing evidence.

These gaps highlight the need for an umbrella review to summarize and critically assess systematic reviews and meta-analyses on non-pharmacological management of PSD. By applying established methodological tools—such as AMSTAR 2 for quality appraisal [18], GRADE for certainty of evidence [19], evidence mapping [20], and overlap analysis [21] —this work will provide a comprehensive and methodologically robust synthesis. Such evidence is crucial to inform clinical decision-making, guide individualized patient management, and identify priorities for future research.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This umbrella review will be conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines [22]. The completed PRISMA-P checklist is provided in Additional file 1. Published systematic reviews and meta-analyses that quantitatively synthesize randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating non-pharmacological interventions for post-stroke depression (PSD) will be included. Reviews that include both RCTs and observational studies will only be considered if results from RCTs are analyzed separately, whereas reviews based solely on observational studies will be excluded.

Eligible comparators include placebo, usual care, standard rehabilitation, or other conventional controls. Narrative reviews, scoping reviews, protocols of systematic reviews, conference abstracts, letters, non-quantitative reviews, and meta-analyses without RCT data will be excluded. By applying these criteria, selection bias will be minimized, ensuring that the synthesized evidence reflects a high level of methodological rigor. No restrictions will be applied regarding language or publication status. This protocol has been registered in the PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251149507).

Search strategy

A comprehensive search will be conducted for systematic reviews and meta-analyses of non-pharmacological interventions for the treatment of PSD, either as monotherapy or adjunctive therapy. The following electronic databases will be searched from inception to December 2025: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (CDSR), Web of Science, CINAHL, CNKI, WanFang Data, VIP Database for Chinese Technical Periodicals (VIP), and SinoMed [23]. No language or publication status restrictions will be applied to minimize selection bias.

The search strategy will combine Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms, tailored to the indexing systems of each database. In addition, reference lists of included reviews will be manually screened to identify further relevant studies. The search will be updated prior to the final analysis to ensure inclusion of the most recent evidence. Types of participants

Patients with a definitive diagnosis of post-stroke depression (PSD) following either ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, based on established clinical diagnostic criteria or validated screening instruments (e.g., Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) [24], International Classification of Diseases (ICD) [25], or standardized depression scales [26]), will be included. No restrictions will be applied regarding gender, age, race, or disease duration. Patients from both inpatient and outpatient settings will be eligible.

Types of interventions

This umbrella review will include systematic reviews and meta-analyses that examine non-pharmacological interventions for post-stroke depression (PSD) [16, 17]. Eligible interventions will include either stand-alone non-pharmacological therapies or adjunctive approaches used alongside pharmacological treatment.

Non-pharmacological interventions will be broadly categorized as follows:i.Psychological therapies: cognitive behavioral therapy, psychotherapy, mindfulness-based interventions, and relaxation training.ii.Exercise and physical therapies: aerobic exercise, yoga, tai chi, and baduanjin exercise.iii.Traditional and complementary therapies: acupuncture, moxibustion, and massage.iv.Other emerging or device-based interventions: music therapy, hyperbaric oxygen therapy, and electrical stimulation.

Comparators may include placebo, usual care, waiting-list controls, or standard rehabilitation programs.

Types of outcomes

The primary outcome will be the improvement of depressive symptoms, measured as changes from baseline using validated instruments such as the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) [27], Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) [28], or other standardized depression scales.

The secondary outcomes will include:i.Neurological function: assessed using the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) [29];ii.Activities of daily living: assessed with validated instruments such as the Barthel Index (BI) [30] or the Activities of Daily Living Scale (ADL) [31];iii.Cognitive function: evaluated with the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [32];iv.Health-related quality of life: assessed using validated patient-reported outcome measures (e.g., Stroke-Specific Quality of Life Scale [SS-QOL], EuroQol-5 Dimension [EQ-5D]), where reported in the included systematic reviews;v.Acceptability of interventions: evaluated using indicators such as dropout rates, adherence, or discontinuation due to intolerance, where available;vi.Safety and adverse events: including any reported adverse events or harms related to the interventions (e.g., exercise-related injuries or acupuncture-related discomfort), as documented in the included reviews.

Selection of studies

All retrieved records will be independently screened by two reviewers according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Titles and abstracts will be screened first, and potentially relevant studies will undergo full-text review. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion, and if consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will adjudicate.

To ensure accurate screening of Chinese-language literature, the review team includes reviewers who are native Chinese speakers. For studies published in other languages (e.g., German or Spanish), preliminary screening will be conducted using translation software (e.g., Google Translate). If a study is considered potentially relevant, full-text assessment will be performed with the assistance of individuals fluent in the respective language to ensure accurate interpretation.

To ensure transparency, reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage will be recorded. When necessary, corresponding authors of the included reviews will be contacted to obtain missing or unclear information. The study selection process will be presented in a PRISMA flow diagram [33].

Data will be independently extracted from all included reviews by two reviewers using a standardized data extraction form. Extracted data will be cross-checked by a third reviewer to ensure accuracy and completeness. Data will be managed using Microsoft Excel (version 2021), and statistical analyses will be performed in R software (version 4.3.0) [35].

Methodological quality evaluation

Quality assessment and evidence grading

The methodological quality of included systematic reviews and meta-analyses will be independently assessed by two reviewers using the AMSTAR 2 tool [18]. This validated instrument provides a structured appraisal and classifies confidence in the results of each review as high, moderate, low, or critically low.

Evidence mapping and overlap analysis

The certainty of evidence for each outcome will be assessed using the GRADE framework [19], which considers study limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, and categorizes evidence into high, moderate, low, or very low certainty. An evidence map [20] will then be constructed to visually summarize the breadth, strength, and gaps in non-pharmacological interventions for PSD, thereby highlighting research priorities. Finally, overlap analysis will be performed using the Corrected Covered Area (CCA) method [21] to quantify duplication of primary studies across reviews, as excessive overlap can distort effect estimates and undermine validity in umbrella reviews. A CCA score > 5% will be considered indicative of substantial overlap [21]. In cases where multiple reviews cover the same intervention and outcome with high overlap, the most recent and highest-quality review (based on the AMSTAR 2 overall confidence rating) will be prioritized to serve as the primary source for the summary of findings. If overlapping reviews present conflicting conclusions, a narrative comparison will be conducted to explore potential reasons for the discrepancy (e.g., differences in primary study inclusion criteria or statistical methods).

Data analysis

Pooled effect sizes and their 95% confidence intervals will be re-estimated based on the summary-level data (e.g., effect estimates, confidence intervals, and sample sizes) reported in the included systematic reviews. Analyses will be performed using fixed-effects (Mantel–Haenszel) or random-effects (DerSimonian–Laird) models, depending on the degree of heterogeneity. To ensure comparability across studies utilizing different measurement scales, Standardized Mean Differences (SMD) will be used as the primary summary statistic. Where original reviews report Mean Differences (MD), these will be converted to SMDs provided that sufficient data (e.g., standard deviations) are available. Data extraction from graphical representations (e.g., forest plots) will only be performed as a last resort when numerical data are not explicitly reported in the text or tables. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the potential impact of data extracted from graphs on the overall findings.

Ninety-five percent prediction intervals will also be calculated to assess the expected range of effects in future studies. Heterogeneity will be evaluated with Cochrane’s Q test and the I2 statistic [36]. To address potential heterogeneity arising from diverse intervention modalities, evidence synthesis and interpretation will be stratified according to specific intervention types. Where sufficient data are available, subgroup analyses will be conducted based on intervention modality (e.g., distinguishing cognitive behavioral therapy from general psychotherapy, or separating aerobic exercise from mind–body interventions such as Tai Chi), rather than pooling fundamentally different interventions into a single summary estimate. Small-study effects and potential publication bias will be examined using Egger’s regression asymmetry test [37], while excess significance bias will be assessed by comparing the observed (O) versus expected (E) number of significant findings [38]. All statistical analyses will be performed using R software (version 4.3.0).

Credibility assessment

The credibility of the evidence will also be graded according to established criteria [39]. Specifically, evidence will be considered convincing if it meets all of the following: (1) P < 0.05 in a fixed-effects model or P < 0.001 in a random-effects model; (2) total sample size > 1000; (3) low to moderate heterogeneity (I2 < 50%); (4) 95% prediction interval excluding the null; and (5) no evidence of small-study effects or excess significance bias. Evidence meeting criteria (1–4) will be considered highly suggestive, criteria (1–2) as suggestive, and criterion (1) alone as weak.

Study timeline

The anticipated timeline for this umbrella review is as follows. Preliminary searches and pilot screening will be conducted in October 2025, followed by formal search and study selection in November 2025. Data extraction and methodological quality assessment are scheduled for December 2025 to January 2026. Data analysis and evidence grading will be performed in February 2026, with manuscript drafting and revision expected to be completed by March 2026.

Discussion

Umbrella reviews, or reviews of systematic reviews, represent a high level of evidence synthesis as they quantitatively integrate data from multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses [40]. By re-estimating pooled effect sizes, calculating prediction intervals, and assessing evidence credibility, umbrella reviews address limitations of individual reviews, such as methodological variability and overlapping studies.

Several systematic reviews have examined non-pharmacological interventions for post-stroke depression (PSD), yet their conclusions remain inconsistent due to methodological differences and heterogeneity. The reliability of these findings has therefore not been fully established, and robust evidence is lacking to guide clinical practice.

This umbrella review will apply a comprehensive methodological framework, including AMSTAR 2 for methodological quality, GRADE for certainty of evidence, evidence mapping to visualize research gaps, and overlap analysis (CCA) to quantify redundancy. Furthermore, evidence will be graded into credibility levels based on statistical significance, sample size, heterogeneity, prediction intervals, and bias assessments.

The findings are expected to provide clinicians, nurses, and policymakers with credible evidence on effective non-pharmacological strategies for PSD, while also identifying gaps to inform future research. Potential limitations include reliance on the quality of included reviews, possible language or publication bias, and variability across interventions. Finally, we acknowledge that the applied credibility criteria (e.g., P < 0.001, N > 1000) are relatively stringent, particularly for non-pharmacological interventions, where large sample sizes are less common than in pharmacological trials. This stringency may theoretically increase the risk of Type II errors (i.e., failing to classify truly effective interventions as supported by “convincing” evidence). However, these criteria are established standards in umbrella reviews to ensure robust and conservative conclusions. To mitigate potential misinterpretation, we will explicitly clarify that the absence of “convincing” (Class I) evidence should not be interpreted as evidence of ineffectiveness, but rather as an indication that further large-scale, high-quality randomized controlled trials are needed to strengthen the existing evidence base. Despite these limitations, this study is anticipated to deliver the most rigorous and comprehensive synthesis of current evidence on non-pharmacological interventions for PSD.
