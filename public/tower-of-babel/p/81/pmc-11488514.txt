Understanding the factors that impact federal rulemaking: a survey of former EPA regulators
Authors: Rachel J Topazian, Emma E McGinty, Shelley A Hearne
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile federal rulemaking is an essential part of American governance, it is not well understood by researchers and advocates. We surveyed 115 former regulators at the Environmental Protection Agency to understand their views on the kinds of information they valued most and their communication preferences (June-August 2023). Respondents highly valued information about the scope of a problem (96%), legal analysis (97%), technology assessments (96%), and impacts of a proposed rule (99%). Regulators had difficulty accessing several kinds of information: 16% of respondents viewed racial equity information as easy to access and 30% thought financial information was easy to access. Respondents valued communications that provided data (99% viewed as effective), made compelling arguments (97%) or technical recommendations (93%), and storytelling (88%). Respondents indicated that the content of comment letters was important: 94% viewed letters containing data as important and 90% valued technical recommendations. Only 22% thought that repetition of the same comments across letters was important. Our findings reveal opportunities for researchers and advocates to help fill information gaps and identify communication strategies that might resonate with federal regulators.

Introduction

Federal rulemaking is a powerful but opaque part of the policy process in the United States. During the rulemaking process, administrative agencies develop and publish regulations to implement legislation as authorized by Congress.1 The rulemaking process includes opportunities for external stakeholders to offer input, including through formal comment periods on draft regulations.1,2 Scholars and regulators agree that public input is valuable because it can ensure that rules are practical and address the concerns of impacted stakeholders.3,4 However, the factors impacting rulemaking are not well understood by the public, academics, and nongovernmental organizations.5,6 Several studies have examined state-level rulemaking but much of the research at the national level focuses on legislative, as opposed to regulatory activity.7-10 Overall, there is little empirical research directly assessing regulator perspectives.7,8,11

Regulators are interested in engaging academics but find it challenging in practice.11,12 Studies across different levels of government and governing entities have identified barriers that prevent engagement, including time limitations, mistrust, differing priorities, and fears that researchers might lack effective communication skills, push political agendas, or struggle to produce timely or practical evidence.9,11,13 Other studies examining favored communication channels concluded that policymakers prefer to receive information via trusted intermediaries and value personal relationships.11,14,15 A few studies that specifically examined regulators’ preferred kinds of information indicated that economic evaluations and other types of financial analyses are valued.16,17

The Biden administration has signaled interest in enhancing public participation in rulemaking.4,18 In 2023, the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs, an office within the White House's Office of Management and Budget that reviews federal regulations, issued guidance on enhancing public engagement, with particular emphasis on community engagement.18 The memo acknowledged barriers to engagement, including lack of awareness about the rulemaking process and effective participation strategies, and logistical hurdles.

Our study sought to understand regulators’ preferred information sources and communication preferences via a survey of former regulators at the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). The EPA is a highly active regulatory agency—in 2023, the administrator signed over 50 rules—and it is responsible for a host of environmental topics including air and water.19,20 The agency has active alumni networks of former staff who can offer insights on the rulemaking process. Our study contributes novel insights on the factors that influence rulemaking at the EPA and has implications for advocates and researchers who might engage in the federal policymaking process.

Methods

Survey sample

We recruited former EPA regulators who were members of the EPA Alumni Association. We restricted eligibility to former employees to encourage candid responses and excluded individuals who had not been involved in rulemaking. Collaborators at the Alumni Association identified eligible individuals using members’ profiles to select individuals who had worked in rulemaking offices.

Survey design and fielding

We conducted pilot interviews with 6 former regulators at a range of federal agencies, recruited through study team networks and snowball sampling. The interview guide examined views on information and communication preferences. Based on these interviews, we developed a survey instrument which we shared with pilot interview participants and EPA Alumni Association collaborators for refinement.

We asked participants to rate what kinds of information were most useful to them using a 5-point scale (very useful to not useful at all) and whether these kinds of information were easy or difficult to find (5-point scale, very easy to very difficult). We asked participants how much they preferred different channels for receiving information and about the communication approaches that made stakeholders effective at incorporating their positions into final rules, using 4-point scales (a lot to not at all). We asked about the factors that affected whether a comment was incorporated into a final rule (5-point scale, very important to not important at all). At the end of the survey, we asked demographic questions (Appendix S1). The survey was fielded online using Qualtrics from June 23-August 21, 2023. This study was approved by the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health Institutional Review Board.

Statistical analysis

For ease of interpretation, we collapsed all response scales into dichotomous measures (see Appendix S1). Data analysis was conducted in Stata Version 16.21

Results

Sample

Of the 397 eligible individuals, there were 149 respondents. Of these, 11 were ineligible and 23 did not complete the survey and were dropped, resulting in 115 respondents. Participants were predominately white (95%), male (71%), and 60 years of age or older (97%). Most had at least 20 years of experience (75%) and had served in the Carter (59%), Reagan (77%), George H.W. Bush (78%), Clinton (77%), George W. Bush (70%), or Obama administrations (58%). A small number served in the Trump (28%) or Biden administrations (6%). Nearly all respondents served only in career (vs political appointee) positions (90%). Approximately half served in GS-15 positions or lower (55%), and 70% had not worked in other federal agencies and 67% identified as Democrats. Appendix S2 contains detailed demographic information.

Information preferences

Large majorities of respondents valued information related to the scope of a problem (96%), legal analysis (97%), tech assessments (96%), and impacts of a proposed rule (99%) (Figure 1). Racial equity (63%), public support (73%), and financial information (82%) were valued by a smaller, but still majority, share of respondents. These kinds of information were not easy to find. Respondents viewed racial equity information as difficult to access (16% believed it was somewhat or very easy to access), as well as financial information (30% easy to access). Half or less than half of respondents thought information on public support (48%) or impacts of the proposed rule (50%) were easy to access.

Preferred methods of communication

Respondents strongly preferred in person meetings (95%), comment letters (96%), hearings and testimony (83%), written materials (99%), and events (84%) (Table 1). A smaller share of respondents valued receiving information via traditional media (40%) or social media (10%). Respondents identified highly effective communication strategies, including providing data (99% viewed as effective), making compelling arguments (97%), making technical recommendations (93%), and storytelling (88%). Using regulatory language in general communications was less uniformly valued (66% viewed as effective).

Discussion

Our findings offer insights on how researchers and advocates can most effectively engage in the policymaking process. We identified areas where information gaps could be filled. For instance, there was a disconnect between the types of information that regulators valued and those that were easy to access. While over 60% of respondents valued racial equity information, only 16% indicated that it was easy to access. We observed similarly large gaps for financial information, which over 80% of respondents valued but only 30% found easy to access, and for analysis of the impacts of a proposed rule, which 50% found easy to access despite nearly all respondents (99%) valuing this kind of information. Provision of information around racial equity, public opinion, financial information, and impact analyses is needed. These information gaps are consistent with prior research indicating that regulators value economic and financial analyses and identify additional kinds of information that might be useful to regulators.16,17

We found that the former regulators in our sample valued data-driven, substantive information delivered in persuasive fashion, both in general and through comment letters. Respondents strongly preferred to receive information through in person forums or written communication. Over 90% of respondents indicated that they preferred in person meetings, comment letters, and written materials, whereas media was not highly valued. While it was clear that data and technical recommendations were valued, we found that communication style also mattered, as large majorities of respondents viewed compelling arguments (97%) and storytelling (88%) as effective.

Respondents expressed these views consistently, both when asked about general communication preferences and specifically about comment letters. Studies have suggested that industry entities are highly effective in using comment letters to influence regulations, potentially due to the large number and high proportion of business commenters and their ability to draft detailed comments as regulated entities.6,22,23 Other studies scrutinizing mass letter campaigns to EPA, typically from advocacy organizations, have found that they lacked detail and failed to impact final rules, leading scholars to question their utility.24-26

Our findings suggest that regulators agree with these conclusions. Respondents indicated that letters including data or technical recommendations are more impactful than the style of writing or volume of letters. Advocacy campaigns often center efforts around letter campaigns which replicate the same message in multiple letters.25 Our results indicate that effective advocacy should focus on providing the right kinds of information as opposed to increasing the volume of letters. If advocacy organizations conduct mass letter campaigns, which might be helpful for other purposes, they should ensure that these letters communicate substantive suggestions, not merely general positions. Finally, while respondents clearly valued data, we discovered that nearly 90% viewed stories and examples as an effective form of communication. Comments from individuals who would be directly impacted by proposed rulemaking may be well received by regulators.

Our study had a relatively small and homogenous sample that limits its generalizability. Participants were predominately over the age of 60, white, male, and Democrats. We surveyed former regulators to encourage candid responses, which means that we could not assess the views of regulators working in the current policy and political environment. More work is needed to understand how regulators who are younger, female, non-white, and Republican or Independent view these topics. Our sample only included former EPA officials; however, our findings align with general recommendations from the federal government on comment letters,27 and 30% of our participants had worked in other federal agencies. Although there are likely differences across agencies, these findings offer helpful guidance in the absence of agency-specific evidence. Finally, participants may have been swayed by social desirability bias, though we aimed to mitigate this by making the survey anonymous. Despite these limitations, our survey is among the few that directly assessed the views of federal regulators.

Participation in rulemaking offers the American public uniquely powerful opportunities to inform federal regulations. Our findings indicate that researchers can fill essential gaps in knowledge and that researchers and advocates alike can make more effective use of comment letters to shape public policy. Substantive engagement from advocates and researchers may help ensure that federal agencies issue evidence-based regulations that address the needs of impacted individuals.
