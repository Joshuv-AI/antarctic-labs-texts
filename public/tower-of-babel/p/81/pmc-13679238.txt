Experiences of non-prescribed opioid use during opioid agonist therapy: an interpretative phenomenological analysis
Authors: Sarah Clarke, Kelly Holloway, Daniel Werb, Nav Persaud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough the goal of opioid agonist therapy is typically understood by clinicians to be the cessation of the use of non-prescribed opioids, many people taking opioid agonist therapy continue to use other opioids. The purpose of this study was to explore how people who use both prescribed opioid agonist therapy and non-prescribed opioids understand their use of both agents.MethodsAn interpretative phenomenological analysis was conducted with ten participants recruited from harm reduction sites in Toronto who were concurrently using opioid agonist therapy and non-prescribed opioids. Semi-structured interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Data were analysed to explore participants’ understanding of their use of both opioid agonist therapy and non-prescribed opioids.ResultsParticipants had unique reasons for the use of opioid agonist therapy and non-prescribed opioids; while opioid agonist therapy is free, relatively safe, and helps control opioid withdrawal symptoms, non-prescribed opioids satisfy opioid cravings, ease physical and emotional pain, and can be used with more autonomy. The participants’ relationships with different opioids are complicated, and some struggle to reconcile their use with their goals.ConclusionsParticipants shared that opioid agonist therapy and non-prescribed opioids fill complementary roles in their lives, each presenting their own benefits and challenges. Their experiences highlight opportunities for improving the provision of opioid agonist therapy in order to make it more effective and acceptable for people who use non-prescribed opioids, which is a crucial strategy in combatting mortality from the opioid toxicity crisis. Opioid agonist therapy can thus be situated within the harm reduction continuum as a practice that can be beneficial whether or not it results in abstinence, and clinicians should offer tailored regimens that take patient preferences and goals into account.

Background

The opioid crisis in Canada and the United States, marked by increased rates of opioid-related morbidity and mortality that was triggered by illegal marketing of long-acting opioid products, is currently fueled by unregulated opioids [1–4]. Fentanyl has become increasingly prevalent as a direct contributor to opioid-related deaths [5]; it has been the most commonly detected opioid at death in Ontario, Canada since 2014 [4], and its prevalence in cases of opioid-related mortality has increased, rising from 68.1% in 2018 to 89.1% in 2022 [1]. Ontario’s unregulated supply has also become increasingly contaminated with benzodiazepines and other adulterants [6, 7], significantly increasing the risk of adverse outcomes.

One tool to combat opioid-related morbidity and mortality is opioid agonist therapy (OAT), a form of treatment in which a long-acting opioid agonist, usually methadone or buprenorphine, is prescribed. OAT works on a principle of substitution: a substance upon which a person has become dependent is replaced with a pharmacologically similar substance that has fewer harmful effects [8]. The mechanism of OAT is to eliminate a person’s need to use non-prescribed opioids through the substitution of a prescribed opioid, thus reducing the risk of harmful consequences. A systematic review and meta-analysis has shown that being on OAT reduces the risk of both all-cause and overdose mortality in people with opioid use disorder (OUD) [9].

Although the use of OAT is intended to replace the use of non-prescribed opioids, many people on OAT have exposures to other opioids [10–12]. While non-prescribed opioid use during OAT is often framed as a “failure” by OAT prescribers [13, 14], it is not clear whether people taking OAT share this view. There have been a small number of studies that examine people’s reasons for non-prescribed opioid use during OAT [15–18]; however, there have not been any explorations of people’s motivations for continuing to take OAT while using non-prescribed opioids. Understanding how people think and feel about their use of each type of opioid will provide insight into the phenomenon of non-prescribed opioid use during OAT; these understandings are crucial for building strategies to maximize the benefits of OAT.

The purpose of this study was to determine how people understand their concurrent use of both prescribed OAT and non-prescribed opioids. We define concurrent use as daily or near-daily OAT use combined with at least weekly use of a non-prescribed opioid. The findings might provide insight into how people who use OAT understand and experience how it is prescribed, reveal whether OAT practice meets the needs of people taking it, and inform suggestions to make OAT more supportive.

Methods

Methodology

This study uses interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) [19]. The IPA approach is phenomenological (i.e., it is about the experience of a phenomenon), hermeneutic (i.e., it investigates how participants make sense of their experience), and idiographic (i.e., it considers cases individually before looking across cases). This approach allows for an in-depth analysis of the meaning that each participant assigns to their experiences of different types of opioid use. This assigned meaning provides insights into participants’ views of the role that each opioid plays in their lives and how the roles intersect.

Data collection

Data for this study were collected through single one-on-one semi-structured interviews. The questions, which served as a guide for conversations rather than a script, were open-ended and focused on uncovering the meaning that participants assign to their use of prescribed and non-prescribed opioids by asking about their experiences of both, the role of each in their lives, their feelings about them, and the relationship between them. The interviews started with more emotionally neutral questions in order to allow time to develop rapport before moving on to more potentially sensitive questions [10]. Criterion sampling was used for recruitment. The eligibility criteria for participation were being at least 18 years old; being able to speak and understand English; currently taking prescribed methadone or buprenorphine for OUD; having been at a stable OAT dose for at least three months; and regularly (i.e., at least weekly) using at least one other opioid that has not been prescribed to them for at least two months. The target sample size for this study was ten to fifteen participants [19–21].

One author (SC) recruited participants from two harm reduction sites located within community health centres in Toronto. Access to the harm reduction services at these sites is open on a drop-in basis to all individuals who use drugs; the harm reduction services offered include supervised drug consumption, drug testing, naloxone training and kit distribution, access to harm reduction supplies including injection kits, inhalation kits, and safer sex supplies, and education and support delivered by harm reduction staff. After granting permission for recruitment, the harm reduction staff at both sites posted recruitment flyers onsite with the weekly hours during which the author would be present at the site and inviting potential participants to get in touch by phone if they wanted more information. Some participants phoned to confirm a meeting time; others expressed interest in participating while the author was onsite. The author introduced herself to each interested individual and briefly explained the purpose of the study. The author asked all interested individuals about each eligibility criterion and invited those that reported meeting all criteria to participate. Interviews with each participant were conducted in a private office at the recruitment site immediately after consent was obtained and documented; each interview lasted between 17 and 58 min. Participants received $50 in cash following the interview. Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Data analysis

The data were analysed by one author (SC) using the interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) approach [21, 22], which seeks to uncover the experience of each participant as well as the breadth of experience across participants. The author read the transcript of the first interview and noted statements that revealed how the participant thought about their use of OAT and non-prescribed opioids. A list of emergent themes was made, then the themes were clustered and relationships drawn between them. This resulted in a table of major themes and sub-themes, each illustrated with key quotes. This process was repeated for each interview transcript. Once all the transcripts were interpreted, a final structured list of major themes representing the whole dataset was created, showing how the participants’ experiences converge and diverge. Each participant was assigned a random pseudonym, and they are referred to using these pseudonyms in order to connect the thread of their personal lived experiences throughout the analysis [22].

The purpose of OAT

OAT can help you stay off other opioids

Almost all the participants started OAT hoping that it would help them get out of a stressful and escalating cycle of opioid use and withdrawal. Several participants use language evoking this distressing cycle:I wanted to stop being sick every 2nd, 3rd day. I wanted the merry-go-round to end. (Magnus)I was tired of pills, tired of pills, pills, buying pills…It was getting a build-up, I was getting higher tolerance…so it was stressful, very stressful for me. (Joyce)

I wanted to stop being sick every 2nd, 3rd day. I wanted the merry-go-round to end. (Magnus)

I was tired of pills, tired of pills, pills, buying pills…It was getting a build-up, I was getting higher tolerance…so it was stressful, very stressful for me. (Joyce)

One exception to this pattern is Angelina, whose goal for OAT was to ensure that she would be able to maintain opioid use if she were arrested (see below). However, she knew that she needed to tell her prescriber that she wanted to stop non-prescribed opioid use to gain access to OAT:I just told [the prescriber] I used heroin and I wanna get off of it. I told him a lie. (Angelina)

OAT is insurance against opioid withdrawal

Suzette and Angelina stated that being on OAT was insurance against withdrawal in case they ended up in jail. Both described being afraid of being arrested for stealing; they wanted to be on OAT proactively to ensure ongoing access to opioids and thus avoid withdrawal while incarcerated. Both described getting started on OAT while incarcerated as impossible:If you go to jail and you’re not on methadone or [buprenorphine], you cannot get it. (Suzette)The methadone was just there in case I do get caught, then they have to give it to me in jail. Try getting it if you didn’t have it already. You’ll get sick first. (Angelina)

If you go to jail and you’re not on methadone or [buprenorphine], you cannot get it. (Suzette)

The methadone was just there in case I do get caught, then they have to give it to me in jail. Try getting it if you didn’t have it already. You’ll get sick first. (Angelina)

How well OAT serves its purpose

OAT’s effectiveness at controlling withdrawal symptoms and cravings varies

Participants reported varying effectiveness of OAT at controlling opioid withdrawal and cravings. This was likely due to multiple factors, including the non-prescribed opioid they used, the volume of their use, and OAT agent and dose. Some participants experienced complete relief from withdrawal and cravings on OAT. Peter described initially being puzzled by his reaction to sublingual buprenorphine, which he eventually realized was the absence of desire for opioids:[A]t the very beginning, I remember a feeling, not a high, but a feeling…and I didn’t physically or mentally notice the effects, I just knew I didn’t feel like using fentanyl. (Peter)

[A]t the very beginning, I remember a feeling, not a high, but a feeling…and I didn’t physically or mentally notice the effects, I just knew I didn’t feel like using fentanyl. (Peter)

Maureen recalled that she had no desire for opioids during a previous experience with injection buprenorphine:[O]nce I got on [injection buprenorphine], I had no sickness, no cravings, didn’t even think about wanting to do it. (Maureen)

[O]nce I got on [injection buprenorphine], I had no sickness, no cravings, didn’t even think about wanting to do it. (Maureen)

Maureen was subsequently on sublingual buprenorphine, which she found kept her from experiencing withdrawal but did not control her cravings. These cravings drove her to use non-prescribed hydromorphone despite wanting to stop:Once I get that craving, it’s like, I know, oh fuck. I’m trying to keep my mind off of it because no matter what, I’m not getting over it. Until I have it. (Maureen)

The structure of OAT is difficult to maintain

Several participants described challenges related to the structures in which OAT is delivered. One well-attested challenge is the burden of witnessed daily dispensing. Siddiq’s description of this evokes the common metaphor of liquid handcuffs [23, 24]:[Y]ou can’t do nothing on methadone. (Siddiq)

[Y]ou can’t do nothing on methadone. (Siddiq)

The need to attend the pharmacy daily affected his ability to work (“I can’t get a job and then go to methadone, get a drink…like, it doesn’t work like that”), parent (“You can’t take your son to school”), and do things for himself (“You can’t go on trips”). The structure of methadone therapy was a tether, keeping him from engaging in meaningful activities.

Another challenge of the structure of OAT is the frequency of clinic appointments. Joyce needed to go to her OAT clinic every three days to leave a urine sample and get her prescription, which she found challenging given limited clinic hours:It’s like…Tuesdays and Thursdays…they’re only there for an hour, two hours, so you gotta be there. (Joyce)

It’s like…Tuesdays and Thursdays…they’re only there for an hour, two hours, so you gotta be there. (Joyce)

Discussion

Key findings

The global themes arising from the data yields the following findings regarding concurrent use of prescribed OAT and non-prescribed opioids: the purposes of OAT and non-prescribed opioids can be complementary; autonomy can be exercised in creative ways; and OAT optimization is important for the participants, although it may still not meet the needs that non-prescribed opioid use meets.

The participants’ reflections on the roles of OAT and non-prescribed opioids in their lives revealed that, for most of them, neither agent alone can provide what they need. The advantages and disadvantages that the participants identified of OAT and non-prescribed opioids are supported by the literature. OAT is advantageous for people because it is free, relatively safe, can be accessed daily, and can help control withdrawal [26, 27]; it can also be insurance against potential future withdrawal [28]. However, there are restrictions on when and where it can be used [23, 29], not everyone finds it effective at controlling withdrawal and cravings, especially people who use fentanyl [18, 30–32], and some have negative views of it, particularly methadone [33, 34]. The participants reflected that while non-prescribed opioids satisfy cravings, ease pain and stress, and can be used with more autonomy, they are expensive, can be dangerous, and can be difficult and time-consuming to acquire [18, 35, 36]. While some participants shared goals of eventually changing their patterns of use, there were factors that made the use of both prescribed OAT and non-prescribed opioids important. These types of use are thus complementary, satisfying different needs.

Patient autonomy has been identified as a crucial factor in OUD treatment [37–41], and there are qualitative studies exploring how people exercise autonomy within the often-strict constraints of OAT [42, 43]. This desire for autonomy is shown in the results; several participants demonstrate autonomy in their treatment by finding creative ways to use OAT to help them achieve their self-identified goals. For example, Angelina, Ruth, and Magnus all chose to remain on low methadone doses. Current recommendations that see the elimination of non-prescribed opioid use as the treatment goal would suggest that their methadone doses should be increased; however, this would not be in line with their goals. Angelina and Suzette both used their methadone prescriptions as insurance against withdrawal in jail. Peter made an opportunistic decision to eliminate all opioid use by discontinuing his buprenorphine rather than restarting it at a lower dose. These participants found creative ways to exercise some control over their care in line with their goals.

The participants’ descriptions of their experiences revealed instances where a different clinical approach to OAT might have been helpful. Joyce and Maureen reported being told that they could not receive the buprenorphine injection until they had used only sublingual buprenorphine for least eight days. However, although there is a requirement that people must have at least seven days of buprenorphine use before receiving the injection [44], there is nothing in the product monograph that indicates they cannot use other opioids. Maureen felt that the buprenorphine injection would eliminate her need to take hydromorphone, but she would not receive the injection until she stopped taking hydromorphone. Joyce’s challenge was attending her clinic twice a week to obtain her buprenorphine prescription; she used hydromorphone when she missed her appointments. These difficulties would be mitigated if she were given a prescription for a week’s worth of buprenorphine. Laura described needing to take fentanyl to sufficiently ease her withdrawal to get to the pharmacy to receive methadone; this might be mitigated if she had take-home methadone doses. Suzette found that methadone alone did not eliminate her cravings for fentanyl; the addition of SROM or a short-acting opioid like hydromorphone might provide greater relief from fentanyl cravings. The participants receiving OAT that included some of these techniques (Carol and Ruth were taking SROM in addition to methadone, and Angelina and Ruth were being prescribed hydromorphone) described finding them beneficial.

These examples illustrate the importance of optimizing the provision of OAT and tailoring the clinical approach to the individual [45–47] without the expectation that this will necessarily lead to a cessation of use. Guidance for initiating injection buprenorphine, flexible take-home OAT doses, adding SROM to methadone, and combining short- and long-acting opioids to reduce unregulated opioid use can be found in recent recommendation documents [48–53]. Through OAT, these participants are trying to solve a problem in their lives, and they are helped and hindered in various ways by their OAT delivery. Although it is not clear from the participants’ accounts why particular clinical choices have been made, more flexible and person-centered OAT would potentially maximize the benefits of the therapy in a way that aligns with their goals, whether or not these goals include the cessation of non-prescribed opioid use. A tailored approach requires clinicians and people taking OAT to have a shared understanding of the purpose of the therapy. A great deal of research and clinical guidance on substance use has been predicated on the medical or “brain disease” model of addiction, which holds that addiction is a “chronic, relapsing disease of the brain” [54], albeit one that is influenced by social and personal factors. The intention behind this model was to reframe addiction as a health condition rather than a moral failing, thus reducing the associated stigma. However, this framing of OUD does not resonate with everyone; some individuals who seek medical treatment for opioid-related concerns—Siddiq, for example—may hold a different narrative about their opioid use, and OAT may not be desired as a long-term intervention [25]. A tailored approach to OAT requires that the clinician understands the individual’s narrative about their opioid use and their goals.

Implications

There is an urgent need to address the opioid crisis that is now in its “third wave” [55], characterized by a toxic unregulated opioid supply that continues to claim thousands of lives each year in Ontario. The harms of this toxic supply are exacerbated by the fact that harm reduction–oriented services for people who use drugs are closing [56–59], putting people at increased risk of death. The dismantling of these services, which have been shown to reduce overdose rates and improve outcomes for individuals who use them [60, 61], is leaving people who use opioids with fewer options for safer use.

OAT is protective against opioid-related mortality [9, 62]; thus, it is essential to ensure that it is readily available to people who want it and to maximize retention through patient-centered approaches [63], including adapting titration and dosing to the toxicity of the unregulated opioid supply [31, 51, 64]. The key findings above—the use of different agents to meet different needs, the desire for autonomy in treatment, and the importance of flexible, tailored therapy—can all be applied as strategies to increase the effectiveness and acceptability of OAT to people who use opioids.

In addition to the challenges associated with OAT, the participants described other circumstances, including poverty, loss, and trauma, that are unlikely to be resolved through their treatment. Many of these issues are largely outside the scope of practice of OAT clinicians and require broader structural solutions. However, improvements to OAT practice would likely make a material difference in the lives of these participants.

There is no single strategy that will bring an end to the opioid crisis, and it has been noted that an exclusively medical approach, particularly one that is focused on treatment and recovery, will not reach everyone at risk of adverse effects from the toxic unregulated drug supply [65]. Nonetheless, comprehensive, patient-centered OAT is a crucial tool that prescribers can offer to reduce people’s risk of opioid-related mortality, whether or not it results in the cessation of non-prescribed opioid use. OAT can thus be understood as a part of the harm reduction spectrum; people can derive benefits from it even if they do not intend to pursue abstinence. Prescribers should be supported to provide OAT that considers each individual’s needs, challenges, and goals through flexible care that emphasizes agency and choice.

Limitations

The IPA approach to inquiry is idiographic; its findings are specific to the participants rather than generalizable. The insights this study provides into the experience of using non-prescribed opioids during OAT are specific to the ten individuals under investigation, all of whom were recruited from two specific harm reduction sites in Toronto. These findings represent a single interpretation of the descriptions of the experiences of these particular individuals as they understood them at the time of the interview and are not representative of all individuals concurrently using prescribed OAT and non-prescribed opioids. This approach does not provide information about the prevalence of experiences or circumstances.

This study does not explore how participants’ experiences might have been influenced by facets of their identities (e.g., race, gender, sexuality). People who use drugs are a highly marginalized group, and this experience of marginalization can be compounded by other intersecting axes of oppression. The current study does speak to any experiences that are unique to a particular sociodemographic group.

Conclusion

This interpretative phenomenological analysis study contributes to the literature by providing insights into the experience of concurrently using prescribed OAT and non-prescribed opioids through the interpretation of the lived experiences of ten individuals; this study is the first to explicitly consider the unique reasons participants have for both their non-prescribed opioid use and their OAT use. The participants shared that OAT and non-prescribed opioids fill complementary roles in their lives, each presenting their own benefits and challenges; the participants’ relationships with different opioids are complicated, and some struggle to reconcile their use with their goals. Their stories highlighted opportunities for improving the provision of OAT to make it more effective and acceptable for people who use non-prescribed opioids, which is a crucial strategy in combatting mortality from the opioid toxicity crisis; clinicians should offer tailored OAT regimens that take patient preferences and goals into account. Recent recommendation documents have provided guidance for approaches that incorporate these strategies; future research should focus on measuring on the uptake of these recommendations and their impact on the satisfaction, treatment retention, and outcomes of people taking OAT.
