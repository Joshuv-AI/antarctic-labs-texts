Synergistic antimicrobial and antibiofilm effects of mycosynthesized MnO–MgO bimetallic nanoparticles against drug-resistant pathogens
Authors: Fathy M Elkady, Sulaiman A Alsalamah, Amr Hashem, Ebrahim saied, Amer M Abdelaziz, Ahmed Abdel Tawab, Ayman Salama, Faisal Alsenani, Nasir A Ibrahim, Nosiba S Basher, Mohammed S Abdulrahman, Mohammed Aufy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultidrug-resistant bacterial diseases pose an increasing hazard to worldwide public health. These bacteria reduce the effectiveness of traditional medicines, resulting in recurring infections, longer hospital stays, higher medical expenses, and higher death rates. Nanomedicine integrates nanotechnology with disease management and represents a promising direction in modern healthcare. In this study, manganese oxide–magnesium oxide bimetallic nanoparticles (MnO–MgO BNPs) were mycosynthesized using Aspergillus niger PQ269689.1 extract, where fungal metabolites acted as natural reducing and stabilizing agents. Several analytical techniques were applied for characterization and compositional analysis of the obtained MnO–MgO BNPs. The UV–vis spectroscopy confirmed BNPs’ formation with a characteristic absorption band around 300 nm, while FTIR analysis verified functional groups involved in stabilization. Also, EDX analysis revealed Mn- and Mg-rich compositions, and TEM/SEM imaging showed angular, flake-like crystalline structures with sizes ranging from 10 to 85 nm. Additionally, XRD patterns confirmed the crystalline phases of MnO and MgO. Biological evaluation demonstrated selective, dose-dependent cytotoxicity against MCF-7 breast cancer cells (IC₅₀ = 96.09 µg/mL). The biosynthesized MnO–MgO BNPs demonstrated remarkable broad-spectrum antibacterial effectiveness, with the largest inhibition zone against Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 (25.67 ± 0.57 mm) and a percent inhibition of 106.96% when compared to ciprofloxacin. The minimal inhibitory concentrations (MIC) varied from 32 to 256 µg/mL, with Staphylococcus saprophyticus ATCC 35552 having the lowest MIC₅₀ of 11.56 µg/mL, according to a quantitative analysis. The BNPs mainly showed a bactericidal effect when tested against most uropathogens, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 25668 and Escherichia coli ATCC 25922. Additionally, significant biofilm clearance of Pseudomonas aeruginosa (67.97 ± 0.29%) and outstanding antibiofilm properties were noted, particularly against Proteus mirabilis ATCC 29906 (92.04% inhibition at sub-MIC levels). Checkerboard studies showed a high synergistic interaction with amoxicillin against Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923 with a fractional inhibitory concentration index of 0.375, significantly reducing the required dosage of both medications. These findings highlight the multifunctional therapeutic potential of mycosynthesized MnO–MgO BNPs for biomedical applications.Keywords: MnO-MgO BNPs, Bactericidal, Antibiofilm, Checkerboard, Synergism

Introduction

The continuous antimicrobial resistance (AMR) development represents a worldwide health-associated risk with economic and social consequences [1]. The annual record of deaths in 2021 with direct attribution of multi-drug resistant (MDR) microbial phenotypes was about 1.14 million. Unless there is the discovery of new therapeutic alternatives, the estimated number of deaths by 2050 from bacterial infections with the AMR phenotype will be around 1.91 million [2]. There are several well-established challenges with the existing antibacterial treatments. Conventional antimicrobials often lose their efficacy due to the rapid development of resistance mechanisms like efflux pumps, enzymatic degradation, and target site change [3]. Bacterial infections of the urinary tract (UT) are associated with microbial occurrence, multiplication, and spreading in the UT with consequent illness and healthcare-associated costs. These infections commonly resulted from bacterial pathogens of gastrointestinal tract sources [4]. The UT infections (UTIs) are globally recorded in about 150 million persons annually and represent one of the most ubiquitous human bacterial disorders [5]. The clinical manifestations of these infections, which occur in individuals with different age groups, comprise localized illness or more complicated disorders, including cystitis and pyelonephritis. Severe UTIs may consequently cause renal failure, sepsis, and other life-threatening conditions [6]. Bacterial colonization of UT occurred through either reversible attachment of free planktonic cells to the epithelial cell surface or biofilm formation by their aggregation surrounded with extracellular self-excreted matrix [7]. Noteworthy resistance of bacterial biofilms is exhibited towards phagocytosis and other human body defense mechanisms [8]. So, bacterial biofilm growth forms represent a major risk to human health, which is implicated in 65–80% of bacterial infections [9]. Additionally, bacterial biofilms consistently exhibit a profoundly elevated level of AMR compared to their planktonic counterparts, rendering them exceptionally challenging to eradicate [10]. This heightened recalcitrance stems from multiple sophisticated mechanisms, including the dense extracellular polymeric matrix, which acts as a physical and chemical barrier impeding antimicrobial penetration and sequestering agents,cells within the biofilm adopt altered physiological states such as slower growth rates and metabolic dormancy, making them less susceptible to drugs targeting active processes,and the presence of persistent cells further contributes to tolerance and recurrence. This intrinsic resistance profoundly complicates clinical management, frequently leading to persistent infections, treatment failures, and chronic disease states that are difficult to resolve with standard antimicrobial regimens [11]. The recent AMR situation underlines the critical need to search and develop effective antibacterial alternatives [12].

Nanotechnology, a current forefront technical field, deals with the study of tiny materials having at least one dimension less than 100 nm. These materials exhibited considerably improved biological activities, physicochemical characters, and functional ability compared to their bulky equivalents [13]. Initially, nanoparticles (NPs) were synthesized employing only one noble metal. However, two or three different metals are recently employed for the synthesis of different nano-mixtures with enhanced stability and improved activity [14]. Among the rapidly advancing scientific areas over the past few decades, nanotechnology has elaborated primarily due to its ability to engineer different innovative nanosized substances of less than 100 nm [15]. These materials exhibit unique physicochemical and biological characteristics that are remarkably distinct from their bulk counterparts, largely because of their elevated ratio of surface area to volume. These distinctive features have positioned the obtained NPs as promising candidates for diverse applications covering biomedicine [16]. To meet growing demand while addressing concerns about sustainability, extensive international efforts have been directed toward the development of green synthesis approaches [17, 18].

Although monometallic NPs are powerful antimicrobials, there are serious toxicity concerns associated with them. Sosa et al. [19] showed that Ag NPs were cytotoxic to fibroblasts and keratinocytes, as well as developmentally toxic and cardiotoxic in zebrafish embryos at concentrations required for antimicrobial efficacy . Also, ZnO NPs exhibit toxicity to mammalian Vero cells at effective antimicrobial doses, despite their potent antibacterial properties [20]. Additionally, MgO NPs are generally considered less toxic compared to heavy metal oxides; recent research suggests that elevated concentrations can still cause adverse biological effects, indicating that their safe dosage range requires careful determination [21]. Integrating MnO and MgO into a single nanostructure in the form of BNPs provides a strategic pathway to develop advanced multifunctional materials [21]. Such BNPs can display synergistic physicochemical and biological properties, combining the catalytic potential of MnO with the superior biocompatibility of MgO. Consequently, the combined MnO-MgO BNPs formulation is highly attractive for both medical and environmental applications [22]. Furthermore, this integration provides an enhanced stability and multi-target activity,the bimetallic formulation overcomes the drawbacks of its monometallic equivalents. According to the sources, bimetallic systems can outperform monometallic ones in terms of antibacterial efficacy [23]. Moreover, the MnO–MgO BNPs offer a "multi-hit" strategy: MgO encourages cell membrane rupture and inhibits enzymatic activities, hence overcoming AMR, while MnO facilitates ROS-mediated oxidative stress and redox cycling. Finally, this strategy delivers biosafety and selective cytotoxicity [24]. Additionally, synergistic interactions with traditional antimicrobials decreased the dosages needed, improving therapeutic efficacy while lowering the risk of toxicity. To highlight the combined benefits of safety and antibacterial efficacy in specific biomedical circumstances, these arguments will be explicitly incorporated into the introduction [25]. Consequently, the combined MnO-MgO BNPs formulation are highly attractive for both medical and environmental applications.

These eco-friendly methods, unlike conventional chemical and physical techniques, utilize benign routes that minimize hazardous by-products and enhance safety and efficiency. Among the different classes of nanomaterials (NMs), metal oxide NPs have attracted exceptional attention. Particularly, manganese oxide NPs (MnO NPs) and magnesium oxide NPs (MgO NPs) are regarded as especially promising. The MnO NPs exist in several oxidation states, including MnO, MnO₂, and Mn₃O₄, which provides versatility for applications such as heterogeneous catalysis, energy storage (e.g., lithium-ion batteries), environmental remediation, and various biomedical uses [26]. These biomedical applications extend to directed drug conveyance, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), biosensors, and even malignant immunotherapy. However, despite their advantages, studies have revealed that uncontrolled exposure to MnO NPs can trigger oxidative stress and neurotoxicity, underscoring the need for careful toxicological evaluation [27]. By contrast, MgO NPs are recognized for their excellent biocompatibility, stability, and biodegradability. Upon dissolution, they release readily metabolized and easily defecated Mg2⁺ ions [28]. These properties make MgO NPs appealing in fields such as bone tissue engineering, antibacterial, and antifungal therapies, MRI contrast agents, and advanced drug delivery platforms. They are also widely used in industrial applications, including water purification, catalysis, defence materials, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetics [29]. Within this framework, biological synthesis methods have gained increasing importance [30, 31]. Fungal secondary metabolites constitute a significant and valuable reservoir of natural products including alkaloids, terpenoids, polyketides, peptides, and phenolics [32–34]. They don't directly help fungi grow or reproduce, but they do help with defense, competition, and signalling in complicated environments [35, 36]. Mycosynthesized NPs have many benefits because they are made from fungal metabolites that are eco-friendly, cheap, and safe. Also, these NPs are a promising alternative to traditional nano-synthesized particles [37, 38]. In addition to their eco-friendliness and low cost, these methods allow the formation of NPs with controllable surface properties, shape, and size [39, 40]. Moreover, mycosynthesized NPs often exhibit enhanced stability and improved biological compatibility, specifically valuable properties for applications [41, 42]. As such, mycosynthesis represents a critical and sustainable strategy for the design of multifunctional NMs, offering a green route for the development of advanced systems like MnO-MgO BNPs. Although prior research has demonstrated their potent antibacterial activity, it has also documented drawbacks, such as cytotoxicity to mammalian cells, environmental persistence, and decreased effectiveness against infections linked to biofilms. By combining the redox activity of MnO with the biocompatibility of MgO, the MnO–MgO combination, on the other hand, provides synergistic benefits that lead to improved antibacterial and antibiofilm efficacy with reduced toxicity.

From the previous background, the current research aimed to address AMR as a worldwide escalating challenge, particularly in uropathogenic bacteria and their biofilm-associated health disorders, via investigating the potential antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities of newly biosynthesized MgO-MnO BNPs as a novel therapeutic alternative.

Material and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Sigma-Aldrich, Cairo, Egypt, supplied manganese acetate tetrahydrate [Mn(CH₃COO)₂0.4H₂O] and magnesium nitrate hexahydrate [Mg(NO3)2.6H2O] as metal ion sources. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) acted as the precipitating agent, while Czapek Dox broth (CDB) and Czapek Dox agar (CDA) media were provided by Himedia, Cairo, Egypt. Distilled water was used consistently during the entire reaction procedure.

Fungal strain isolation and identification

The Aspergillus niger (A. niger) PQ269689.1 fungal strain previously isolated and characterized in an earlier study [43] was utilized in the green mycosynthesis of MnO-MgO BNPs.

Biosynthesis of MnO-MgO BNPs

Fungal strain A. niger PQ269689.1 was cultured on 3 disks (0.9 cm) for 72 h, then introduced into 100 ml of CDB (pH = 7) and incubated under shaking at 150 rpm for 5 days at 28 ± 2°C. The obtained fungal biomass was harvested via filtration using Whatman No. 1 paper followed by washing 3 times using distilled water to eliminate any remaining media components. Ten grams of the obtained fungal biomass were then resuspended in distilled water (100 mL), followed by shaking incubation at 150 rpm for 48 h to prepare the fungal biomass filtrate (FBF). The solution obtained was then exposed to centrifugation for 6 min at 10,000 rpm and used as a biocatalyst for BNP synthesis. In this process, 2.0 mM Mn(CH3COO)2.4H2O and 2.0 mM Mg(NO3)2.6H2O were added to the FBF (pH 8) and kept protected from light for 24 h at 28 ± 2 °C and 150 rpm. After the development of a copper-yellow color, oven-drying for 24 h at 120 °C was applied to the obtained filtrate [44]. The full process of MnO-MgO BNPs mycosynthesis summarized in Fig. 1.

Characterization of MnO-MgO BNPs

The color shift of the reaction mixture during BNPs biosynthesis was analyzed by measuring the absorbance at 200–800 nm, employing UV–vis spectroscopy (JENWAY 6305, Staffordshire, UK) for maximum surface plasmon resonance (SPR) determination. The Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectroscopy (Cary-660 model) using the KBr pellet technique was employed for the identification of functional groups within the MnO-MgO BNPs at the 400–4000 cm⁻1 wavenumber range. Additionally, the obtained BNPs size and morphology were assessed based on Transmission electron microscope (TEM) imaging (TEM, JEM-2100 Plus, JEOL, Japan), while their size distribution was evaluated using dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis (Nano ZS, Malvern, UK). As well, the scanning electron microscope (SEM) image was employed for examination of the surface structure (SEM, ZEISS, EVO-MA10, Germany), while the energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) spectroscopic analysis was conducted to confirm the purity and elemental composition and distribution of the mycosynthesized MnO-MgO BNPs (EDX, Bruker, Germany). Finally, the crystal size and other crystalline properties were determined based on the X-ray diffraction (XRD) technique (XRD-6000, Shimadzu Scientific Instruments, Japan).

Anticancer effect

The 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay was employed for the investigation of the biosynthesized MnO-MgO BNPs cytotoxic properties [45]. The evaluation was carried out on WI-38 (ATCC CCL-75), a normal human lung fibroblast cell line, to examine the effect of BNPs on non-cancerous cells. Optical density (OD) values were recorded at the wavelength of 560 nm, and the background correction was performed at 620 nm to ensure accuracy. In parallel, the antiproliferative efficacy was tested towards the breast cancer cell line MCF-7 (ATCC HTB-22) using the same protocol. For both experimental models, the percentages for both viable cells and growth inhibition were calculated according to Eqs. (1) and (2), respectively.

Well diffusion assay

The inhibition zone diameter (IZD), which reflects the biosynthesized MnO-MgO BNPs antimicrobial activity against some uropathogenic bacterial strains, including Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) ATCC 25923, Staphylococcus saprophyticus (S. saprophyticus) ATCC 35552, E. coli ATCC 25922, Proteus mirabilis (P. mirabilis) ATCC 29906, Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) ATCC 25668, and Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) ATCC 700603, was determined based on the well diffusion (WD) method carried out under aseptic conditions. Briefly, the tested standard strain inoculum adjusted to 1.5 X 108 CFU/ml was cultured onto a sterile Muller Hinton agar (MHA) surface to produce a lawn growth form. A sterile borer was then used to create 6 equidistant wells in the inoculated agar surface. Subsequently, 100 μL from either MnO-MgO BNPs (1000 μg/mL), MgSO4 (1000 μg/mL), MnSO4 (1000 μg/mL), fungal filtrate, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) 10% (V/V) as a negative control (NC), or ciprofloxacin (30 μg/mL) as a positive control (PC) was aseptically added to each well; the tested BNPs and salts were dissolved in DMSO. To allow the tested materials to diffuse into the agar, the inoculated plates were kept at 4 °C for 30 min. Following the plates’ incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, the IZD appeared around the inoculated wells were measured [46]. Subsequently, the inhibitory percentage (PI) caused by MnO-MgO BNPs was calculated following Eq. (3) [47].

Microdilution method

The MnO-MgO BNPs ability to combat uropathogenic microbial strains was assessed based on the quantitative microdilution technique that determines the MIC and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of the biosynthesized BNPs for the tested bacteria [48]. Briefly, DMSO was used to dissolve and prepare the tested MnO-MgO BNPs stock concentration, and Muller Hinton broth (MHB) was used to construct twofold serial microdilution in the microtiter plate row, with BNPs concentrations of 512–16 µg/mL. The tested strain inoculum of 1.5 X 108 CFU/mL was transferred into the well with a final well volume of 200 µL using MHB, followed by aerobic incubation for 24 h at 37 °C. Resazurin indicator (0.015%) was then inoculated into the wells, followed by incubation for 2 h at 37 °C. Based on the blue/red colors of resazurin, the MIC of the tested MnO-MgO BNPs was reported. The MBC of the biosynthesized MnO-MgO BNPs was recorded after transportation of samples from the wells containing MIC and other higher concentrations into sterile MHA with further incubation at 37 °C for 24 h [46]. The MIC index (MICi), or tolerance level, was consequently determined [49] based on this Equation.

Finally, the calculated MICi values of ≤ 4 and > 4 were respectively interpreted as the MnO-MgO BNPs bactericidal and bacteriostatic effects [50]. Furthermore, the broth microdilution method was used to spectrophotometrically estimate the MIC50 (concentration needed to inhibit 50% of the bacterial population). A standardized bacterial suspension (0.5 McFarland standard) was used to inoculate a 96-well microtiter plate with two-fold serial dilutions of the tested agent. After incubation for 16–20 h at 35 ± 2 °C, the inhibition in bacterial growth was evaluated via determination of the OD at a wavelength of 600 nm, instead of visual examination, where the MIC was equal to the tested MnO-MgO BNPs dose, causing ≥ 90% reduction in OD compared to the growth control. The MIC50 was then derived as the median MIC value from a set of isolates, calculated by ordering MICs ascendingly and selecting the concentration at the 50th percentile [51].

Biofilm inhibitory assay

The procedure for assessing biofilm inhibition using the crystal violet (CV) method involves several sequential steps. Initially, bacterial cultures were grown overnight and diluted to a consistent OD to serve as the inoculum. This diluted bacterial suspension was then dispensed into sterile microtiter plates, and the tested MnO-MgO BNPs at various sub-inhibitory concentrations and appropriate PC and NC wells were also prepared. After static incubation for 18–72 h at 37 °C, the time sufficient for biofilm formation depending on the bacterial species, the planktonic bacterial growth was carefully discarded via aspiration, followed by 2 washes using sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), ensuring the removal of unbound cells without disturbing the formed bacterial biofilm. The adhered biofilm was fixed with methanol with the subsequent addition of 0.1% CV, followed by incubation for 15 min at ambient temperature, allowing dye binding to the biofilm biomass. The excess unbound CV was thoroughly washed off with PBS (3 washes), and the plate was air-dried. Finally, the bound CV was solubilized by adding 95% ethanol to each well, and the solubilized dye absorbance at 595 nm was then determined. The MnO-MgO BNPs biofilm inhibitory percentage, relative to untreated controls, was then calculated based on the absorbance values that are directly proportional to the biofilm biomass [52].

Biofilm clearance assay

The procedure for assessing the clearance of pre-formed bacterial biofilms by MnO-MgO BNPs, quantified via the CV staining method, involves several critical steps. Initially, bacterial suspensions were used to establish 48 h robust biofilms in sterile 96-well microtiter plates over a specified incubation period, depending on the tested microorganism. Following this, planktonic cells and spent medium were carefully removed, and the pre-formed biofilms were gently washed with sterile PBS. Subsequently, the established biofilms were exposed to various test concentrations of MnO-MgO BNPs, alongside untreated positive biofilm controls and sterility controls, for a defined treatment duration, usually 18 to 24 h. After this treatment, the BNP-containing medium was aspirated, followed by careful washing for complete elimination of any unbound material and residual BNPs. The CV (0.1%) solution was then added, followed by incubation for 15 min, allowing the remaining adhered biomass staining via dye binding to the negatively charged components of the biofilm matrix and bacterial cells. Unbound CV was then meticulously washed away, the plates were air-dried, and ethanol (95%) was used to solubilize the bound CV. Finally, a microplate reader was used to measure the solubilized CV dye absorbance at 595 nm. The percentage of biofilm clearance is then calculated by comparing the absorbance values of treated wells to those of untreated control biofilms, reflecting the NMs' efficacy in disrupting or removing the pre-formed biofilm structure [53].

Drug synergy assay

The checkerboard assay was employed, after determination of the MIC for amoxicillin (AMX) against the tested S. aureus ATCC 25923 strain, for evaluation of the possible interaction between MnO-MgO BNPs and AMX towards the tested strain. According to the obtained MICs of both agents, MnO-MgO BNPs and AMX were properly mixed in MHB to obtain various mixed doses. The prepared broth was then inoculated with the S. aureus strain, followed by incubation for 24 h at 37 °C and inspection of microbial growth. The combined MIC was recorded, and the fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) for MnO-MgO BNPs and AMX, as well as their FIC index (FICi), according to Eq. (5), was then determined (Pa [54].

where the FIC is the MIC of the tested agent in the combined formulation divided by its MIC when used separately. According to the obtained FICi, the interaction was categorized as synergistic when FICi ≤ 0.5, additive when 0.5 < FICi ≤ 1, and antagonistic when FICi > 1 [55].

Statistical analysis

The mean and standard deviation were used to present the findings of the experiments performed in triplicate. Combenefit software (University of Cambridge, Cambridge, UK) was used for data analysis and visualization of the drug combinations’ effect. GraphPad Prism (version 8.0, USA) was employed for statistical analysis. P values of < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of MnO-MgO BNPs

The visible transformation in the color of the A. niger fungal extract from dark brown to copper yellow upon the addition of manganese and magnesium precursors (Fig. 2) provided the first preliminary indication of MnO–MgO BNPs formation. Such color changes are commonly associated with NPs biosynthesis and have been widely reported in fungal-mediated synthesis systems. Numerous studies have demonstrated the remarkable ability of fungal strains to biosynthesize a wide range of NPs. For instance, Aspergillus spp. have been extensively utilized for NPs production, exhibiting diverse biosynthetic capabilities [56]. In the present study, metabolites secreted by Aspergillus sp. played a crucial role in facilitating the biosynthesis of MnO–MgO BNPs by acting as reducing and capping agents, thereby minimizing particle aggregation and promoting the formation of smaller-sized NPs [57]. Functional groups present in fungal metabolites, such as proteins, phenolics, and polysaccharides, have been reported to significantly influence NPs nucleation and stabilization processes [58]. The biosynthesis of nanocomposites and bimetallic systems using fungal extracts has also gained increasing attention. Hasanin et al. [59] reported the successful fabrication of a ZnO–CuO NPs/CSC nanocomposite using metabolites of A. niger AH1. Similarly, Zhang et al. [60] demonstrated the efficient biosynthesis of manganese NPs using the fungal strain Arcopilus globulus, highlighting their potential in bisphenol A degradation.

Color change of biomass filtrate of A. niger PQ269689.1 to copper yellow indicating the formation of MnO-MgO BNPs

Endophytic fungi have also been explored for NPs synthesis. Trichoderma virens was employed for MnO NPs production by El-Moslamy et al. [61], while Colonostachys rosea was used to fabricate ZnO/MnO nanocomposites [62].

Collectively, these studies support the feasibility and efficiency of fungal-mediated synthesis of metal and metal oxide NPs. The MnO–MgO BNPs synthesized in this study demonstrate considerable potential for various applications, particularly in plant disease management, owing to their enhanced physicochemical properties and synergistic effects [63].

MnO-MgO BNPs characterization

The UV–vis absorption spectrum of the synthesized MnO–MgO BNPs exhibited a distinct absorption band at approximately 300 nm (Fig. 3). This absorption behavior is consistent with previously reported standard values for manganese and magnesium oxide NPs. MnO NPs typically show absorption bands in the range of 320–410 nm and around 300–350 nm depending on particle size and synthesis conditions [64–66], while MgO NPs exhibit characteristic absorption in the UV region between 260 and 320 nm [67–70]. The observed absorption peak in the present study lies within these reported ranges, confirming the successful formation of MnO–MgO bimetallic oxide NPs. These variations in absorption position can be attributed to differences in particle size distribution, crystallinity, surface defects, and interfacial charge-transfer interactions within the heterostructure. Notably, no additional absorption peaks corresponding to impurities were detected, indicating the high purity of the synthesized nanostructure. A similar observation has been reported by Elhawary et al. [71], who investigated the biosynthesis of MnO₂–MgO BNPs, where a characteristic UV–vis absorption peak was observed at approximately 350 nm. Despite the difference in oxidation state, the comparable absorption behavior supports the formation of Mn-based oxide nanostructures. Additionally, the disappearance of the extract-related absorption band confirmed its role in the reduction and stabilization processes. This is consistent with the present study, where an absorption peak at 300 nm indicates the successful formation of MnO–MgO BNPs.

A UV–vis spectrum of mycosynthesized MnO-MgO BNPs

In metal oxide NPs, UV–vis absorption behavior is strongly influenced by particle size, morphology, crystallinity, and surface defects rather than SPR phenomena, which are characteristic of metallic NPs [72]. Comparable absorption features have been reported for various biosynthesized metal oxide and bimetallic nanostructures. Gaber et al. [73] observed a UV–vis absorption band at 375 nm for ZnO–CuO NPs, while Fouda et al. [74] reported a characteristic absorption band at 250 nm for MgO NPs. Similarly, El-Moslamy et al. [62] documented absorption bands at 350 and 400 nm for ZnO–MnO nanocomposites. Furthermore, El-Moslamy et al. [61] reported increased absorption intensity with a maximum at 350 nm, confirming the successful synthesis of MnO NPs. Zhang et al. [60] observed a distinct absorption peak at 324 nm for AGE-mediated manganese NPs, while Ameen, [75] reported an absorption band at 560 nm for Ag–Cu BNPs. Additionally, Egbewole et al. [76] documented absorption bands at 454, 420, and 437 nm for biosynthesized Ag–Ni bimetallic NPs using Mallotus oppositifolius leaf extract. The variation in absorption wavelengths among different nanostructured systems reflects differences in composition, particle size, morphology, and electronic interactions, further supporting the successful formation of MnO–MgO BNPs in the present study.

Additionally, the biosynthesis of MnO–MgO BNPs mediated by A. niger PQ269689.1 fungal extract was confirmed by FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 4). The obtained FTIR spectrum was consistent with previously reported standard spectra of MnO and MgO NPs, confirming the successful formation of the bimetallic oxide structure. The fungal metabolites acted as natural reducing, stabilizing, and capping agents, facilitating NPs growth, nucleation, and stabilization. The FTIR spectrum exhibited prominent absorption bands at 3686, 2217, 2160, 2027, 1568, 1363, and 450 cm⁻1. The broad band at 3686 cm⁻1 corresponds to O–H stretching vibrations, which is in good agreement with reported values (3200–3700 cm⁻1), indicating the presence of hydroxyl groups involved in NPs stabilization [77, 78]. The absorption band at 2217 cm⁻1 is attributed to C≡N or C≡C stretching vibrations, comparable to standard ranges reported for biomolecules involved in metal coordination.

FTIR spectrum of mycosynthesized MnO-MgO BNPs

The bands observed at 1568 and 1363 cm⁻1 correspond to C–N stretching and phenolic or amine bending vibrations, respectively, which are consistent with literature reports for bio-capped NPs [79]. Importantly, the absorption band around 450 cm⁻1 falls within the characteristic region (400–700 cm⁻1) assigned to metal–oxygen (Mn–O and Mg–O) stretching vibrations, confirming the formation of MnO–MgO NPs in agreement with previously reported standard spectra [80]. Slight shifts in peak positions compared to standard values may be attributed to nanoscale effects and the interaction of metal oxides with fungal biomolecules acting as capping agents.

Transmission electron microscopy was employed to investigate the morphology, structure, and formation of MnO–MgO BNPs synthesized using A. niger PQ269689.1 fungal extract. The TEM micrograph revealed angular and aggregated NPs with well-defined crystalline domains, which can be attributed to capping by organic metabolites secreted by the fungal extract. These biomolecules not only stabilized the NPs but also contributed to controlling their nanoscale dimensions. The TEM analysis indicated that the MnO–MgO BNPs possessed particle sizes ranging from approximately 10 to 55 nm, as estimated from the micrographs using a 200 nm scale bar (Fig. 5A). The observed crystalline features and uniform morphology confirm the successful biosynthesis of MnO–MgO BNPs and suggest their suitability for various functional applications [81]. Comparable morphologies have been reported for other biosynthesized BNPs. Ameen [75] reported Ag–Cu BNPs with particle sizes predominantly in the range of 20–30 nm. Omran et al. [82] observed biogenic ZnO–CuO BNPs with semi-spherical morphology and an average size of 54.18 ± 1.9 nm using HR-TEM analysis. Similarly, Aldahasi et al. [83] documented spherical Ag–FeO BNPs with an average diameter of 30.21 nm. Lagashetty et al. [84] demonstrated the effectiveness of tea powder in synthesizing spherical Ag–Au BNPs, while Malik et al. [85] reported spherical Ag–FeO BNPs biosynthesized using Salvia officinalis extract with an average size of 27.48 nm.

The TEM image (A) and DLS graph (B) of mycosynthesized MnO-MgO BNPs

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis was performed to determine the hydrodynamic size distribution and polydispersity index (PDI) of the biosynthesized MnO–MgO BNPs. The DLS results revealed an average hydrodynamic particle size of 85 nm (Fig. 5B) with a PDI value of 0.253. A PDI value below 0.5 indicates a relatively uniform size distribution of the NPs [86]. The larger particle size observed by DLS compared to TEM measurements can be attributed to the presence of surface-bound biomolecules and the hydration layer surrounding the NPs in the colloidal system. Similar trends have been reported in previous studies. Ryu et al., [87] observed Ag NPs with an average hydrodynamic size of 102 nm biosynthesized using Angelica gigas. Additionally, Aldahasi et al. [83] reported Ag–FeO BNPs with a mean hydrodynamic size of 176.2 nm and PDI values of 0.25, 0.22, and 0.08 for Ag NPs, FeO NPs, and Ag–FeO BNPs, respectively. El-Batal et al. [88] also documented Ag–CuO BNPs with an average particle size of 157.34 nm.

Scanning electron microscopy analysis was employed to investigate the surface architecture and morphological features of the biosynthesized MnO–MgO BNPs. At a magnification of 80,000 × (Fig. 6A), the MnO–MgO BNPs exhibited a heterogeneous, layered, and flake-like morphology characterized by noticeable agglomerated clusters and fractured regions. Such irregular and porous surface features indicate a high degree of structural heterogeneity, which is commonly associated with bimetallic and mixed metal oxide nanostructures. These characteristics can enhance surface area and promote interparticle interactions, rendering the material favorable for surface-dependent applications [89]. The observed submicron-scale features appeared densely packed, a morphological trait frequently reported for materials synthesized via biological or chemical reduction routes, where rapid nucleation and growth processes often lead to particle agglomeration [90]. This morphology supports the suitability of the MnO–MgO BNPs for applications requiring high surface area, such as catalysis, adsorption, and antimicrobial activity [91].

The SEM image (A) and EDX graph (B) of the elemental composition of mycosynthesized MnO-MgO BNPs

To complement the SEM observations, EDX spectroscopy was employed to confirm the elemental composition of the mycosynthesized MnO–MgO BNPs (Fig. 6B). The EDX spectra confirmed the presence of Mn, Mg, O, C, and Na, with no detectable chlorine, which is consistent with the precursor salts used (manganese acetate and magnesium nitrate). Quantitative EDX analysis revealed atomic percentages (At%) of C (8.4%), O (52.8%), Na (3.5%), Mg (28.2%), and Mn (7.1%), corresponding to weight percentages (Wt%) of approximately C (4.8%), O (40.2%), Na (3.8%), Mg (32.6%), and Mn (18.6%). When normalized to metallic elements only, Mg and Mn accounted for approximately 72.7% and 18.3% of the atomic composition, respectively. Based on the EDX-derived weight percentages of Mg and Mn, the corresponding oxide compositions were estimated. The Mn and Mg contents (18.6 wt% and 32.6 wt%, respectively) correspond to approximately 24.0 wt% MnO and 54.1 wt% MgO, confirming that MgO is the dominant phase in the synthesized nanocomposite. The relatively higher Mg content may be attributed to differential nucleation and growth kinetics during the mycosynthesis process, as well as the localized sampling nature inherent to EDX analysis. Overall, the elemental profile strongly supports the successful biosynthesis of MnO–MgO BNPs with high compositional fidelity to the intended bimetallic oxide formulation [92]. Comparable morphological and compositional features have been reported in previous studies. Naaz et al. [93] demonstrated the biosynthesis of Ag NPs using Syngonium podophyllum leaf extract, yielding diverse morphologies including spherical, triangular, rectangular, and rod-shaped structures. Similarly, Khan et al. [94] reported that green-synthesized Al–MnO BNPs prepared using Abutilon indicum exhibited uniformly distributed spherical morphology, with EDX analysis confirming elemental purity. Furthermore, Mohsin et al. [95] investigated Ag–Au BNPs and observed temperature-dependent variations in elemental composition, emphasizing the influence of synthesis conditions on elemental distribution and reduction efficiency. Such observations are consistent with the compositional variations observed in the MnO–MgO BNPs synthesized in the present study.

The crystallinity and phase composition of MnO–MgO BNPs biosynthesized using A. niger PQ269689.1 were investigated by X-ray diffraction analysis (Fig. 7). The XRD pattern exhibited multiple sharp and well-defined diffraction peaks, indicating the highly crystalline nature of the synthesized NPs. Distinct diffraction peaks were observed at 2θ values of 18.6°, 29.1°, 37.01°, 37.9°, 42.6°, 50.10°, 58.8°, 62.2°, 75.6°, and 78.5°, corresponding to various crystallographic planes of MnO and MgO phases. The peaks located at 2θ values of 18.6°, 29.1°, 37.9°, 42.6°, 50.10°, and 58.8° were indexed to the (200), (310), (211), (200), (411), and (600) planes of MnO NPs, respectively, in good agreement with the standard JCPDS card No. 44–0141 [96]. Similar diffraction features were previously reported by EL-Moslamy et al. [97], who observed characteristic MnO peaks at 2θ values of 18.56°, 28.90°, 37.34°, and 63.74°, corresponding to the (200), (310), (211), and (512) planes. In addition, the diffraction peaks observed at 2θ values of 37.01°, 42.6°, 62.2°, 75.6°, and 78.5° were assigned to the (111), (200), (220), (311), and (222) crystallographic planes of cubic MgO NPs, respectively, consistent with JCPDS file No. 89–7746 [44]. Lekota et al. [98] similarly reported diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 42.6°, 62.2°, and 78.6°, confirming the formation of cubic MgO NPs, while peaks at lower angles were associated with intermediate Mg(OH)₂ phases. The presence of diffraction peaks corresponding to both MnO and MgO phases, along with partial peak overlap, confirms the successful formation of MnO–MgO bimetallic oxide nanostructures. The sharpness and intensity of the diffraction peaks further indicate the high crystallinity of the synthesized BNPs. The average crystallite size of the MnO–MgO BNPs was estimated using the Scherrer equation [99] and was found to be approximately 50 nm. This crystallite size is in good agreement with the nanoscale dimensions observed in TEM analysis, supporting the successful biosynthesis of crystalline MnO–MgO BNPs.

Evaluation of cytotoxic and anticancer effects

The cytotoxic profile of the biosynthesized MnO-MgO BNPs against the normal WI-38 cell line was presented in Fig. 8A. The IC₅₀ value of the tested BNPs was 324.44 µg/mL, which indicates their biosafety (considered non-toxic when IC₅₀ ≥ 90 µg/mL) [100]. At lower concentrations (31.25 and 62.5 µg/mL), the tested cell viability remained high, within the range of 90–100%. However, a marked reduction in cell survival was observed with increasing concentrations of the BNPs (≥ 250 µg/mL). Specifically, cell viability declined to 25% upon exposure to 500 µg/mL and reached approximately 6% for a BNPs dose of 1000 µg/mL. The anticancer evaluation of MnO-MgO BNPs demonstrated a significant inhibitory effect towards the breast MCF-7 cancer cell line (Fig. 8B), with BNPs IC₅₀ of 96.09 µg/mL. Moreover, treatment with 31.25, 62.5, 125, and 250 µg/mL of the biosynthesized MnO-MgO BNPs caused cell growth inhibition rates of 0, 35.02, 59.19, and 85.15%, respectively, indicating a dose-dependent cytotoxic effect.

Cytotoxic effects of biogenic MnO-MgO BNPs against normal WI-38 human fibroblast (A) and breast MCF-7 cancerous cell lines (B)

Several reports have highlighted the cytotoxic properties of biogenic and composite NPs against diverse cancer models. For example, Selim et al. [101] observed that biosynthesized nanocomposites displayed an IC₅₀ value of 179.13 µg/mL on the normal WI-38 fibroblast cell line, suggesting acceptable biosafety, while exhibiting marked anticancer effects with IC₅₀ values of 73.61 µg/mL and 31.25 µg/mL towards Hep-G2 and MCF-7 cell lines, respectively. In another study, Afzal et al. [102] documented a concentration-dependent reduction in cell viability, with more pronounced effects noted for Ag-ZnO BNPs at elevated doses. Likewise, Selim et al. [103] reported that Ag-TiO2 BNPs were relatively safe toward WI-38 cells (IC₅₀ = 169.6 µg/mL) but showed strong growth inhibition against MCF-7 cells (IC₅₀ = 33.5 µg/mL). Additional investigations by Lu et al. [104], Mangalampalli et al. [105], and Ullah et al. [106] consistently emphasized the enhanced anticancer performance of green-synthesized NPs compared with their bulk or chemically derived analogues. Similarly, Ciorița et al. [107] confirmed that Ag-MnO₂ nanostructures effectively reduced the survival of A375 melanoma cells while exerting only moderate toxicity toward normal keratinocytes. In the current work, the fabricated MnO-MgO BNPs demonstrated notable anticancer effects through several plausible mechanisms. Once internalized, the particles are capable of stimulating excessive reactive oxygen species formation, which promotes oxidative stress, damage of DNA, mitochondrial impairment, and protein denaturation, ultimately leading to apoptosis [108, 109]. The MnO moiety enhances redox cycling and supports Fenton-like hydroxyl radical generation, whereas MgO facilitates mitochondrial membrane disruption and caspase-dependent cell death [110]. The combination of both oxides provides a synergistic advantage, increasing selectivity toward malignant cells while minimizing harm to normal cells due to their distinct metabolic activity and vulnerability to redox imbalance [111]. Furthermore, MnO-MgO BNPs are suggested to interfere with oncogenic signaling cascades, improve chemosensitivity, and suppress tumor cell proliferation and survival pathways.

Qualitative growth inhibition

The increasing prevalence of AMR infections, particularly those of UT, emphasizes the necessity for the development of new drugs suitable for combating such disorders. The present study demonstrated broad-spectrum antibacterial activity of MnO-MgO BNPs towards a wide range of pathogens, with variable efficacy depending on the bacterial strain (Table 1 and Figs. 9 and 10). The highest IZD was observed against E. coli ATCC 25922 (25.67 ± 0.57 mm, PI: 106.96%), surpassing even ciprofloxacin (24.00 ± 1 mm), suggesting a potent inhibitory effect against this common uropathogen. Additionally, S. aureus ATCC 25923 (23.00 ± 1 mm) and P. aeruginosa ATCC 25668 (19.33 ± 0.57 mm) showed significant susceptibility, reinforcing the potential of MnO-MgO BNPs as an alternative to conventional antimicrobials. However, weaker activity was noted against K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603 (11.00 ± 0.00 mm, PI: 40.25%), indicating possible resistance mechanisms or limited NPs penetration in encapsulated strains. Statistical analysis of MnO-MgO BNPs results, in comparison to ciprofloxacin, showed no significant difference with P-value = 0.3202. Recent research supports the antimicrobial potential of BNPs. For instance, Ag-Fe BNPs exhibited a strong inhibitory effect towards MDR S. aureus and E. coli, aligning with our findings on Gram-positive and Gram-negative susceptibility [112]. Likewise, Zn-MgO BNPs effectively inhibit P. aeruginosa, corroborating our observed IZD of 19.33 mm [113]. However, Au-Mn BNPs similarly demonstrated limited efficacy against K. pneumoniae[114], suggesting the critical influences of bacterial structure and NPs composition on its antibacterial performance. Accordingly, the biosynthesized MnO-MgO BNPs could serve as adjunctive therapy where traditional antimicrobials fail. Future studies should assess in vivo MnO-MgO BNPs efficacy to validate clinical applicability.

Quantitative inhibitory effects

The broth microdilution method determined the inhibitory concentrations of MnO-MgO BNPs against selected bacterial isolates (Table 2 and Fig. 11). The MIC ranged from 32 µg/mL for uropathogenic S. saprophyticus ATCC 35552 to 256 µg/mL for P. mirabilis ATCC 29906 and K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603. The MBC varied from 128 µg/mL for S. saprophyticus to 1024 µg/mL in the case of S. aureus, K. pneumoniae, and P. mirabilis. The MICi indicated a predominantly bactericidal effect, except for S. aureus, which showed a bacteriostatic response. These findings demonstrate the variable antimicrobial efficacy of MnO-MgO BNPs across different bacterial species. As the MIC₅₀ offers a robust and reproducible measure of central tendency for susceptibility, it was calculated alongside the MIC value. The lowest potency was observed against K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603 with an MIC₅₀ of 184.91 µg/mL, indicating significant resistance. Moderately, higher activity was noted against P. mirabilis ATCC 29906 and P. aeruginosa ATCC 25668, organisms renowned for their intrinsic resistance mechanisms, with values of 107.37 and 83.7 µg/mL, respectively. In contrast, the compound demonstrated superior efficacy against S. saprophyticus ATCC 35552, yielding the lowest MIC₅₀ of 11.56 µg/mL.

The observed MnO-MgO BNPs bacterial inhibitory effect highlights their potential as novel antibacterial agents, particularly against MDR pathogens. The lower MIC values for S. saprophyticus and E. coli suggest their higher susceptibility and cell wall structure-independent BNPs activity, with Gram-positive bacterial strains typically showing higher susceptibility than the tested Gram-negative strains. However, the variable MIC and MBC values indicate resistance mechanisms that may limit NPs penetration or interaction. Notably, the bacteriostatic effect on S. aureus contrasts with the bactericidal effect seen in other strains, suggesting differential modes of action depending on the target organism. These results align with recent studies on metal-based NPs. For instance, a study by [115] demonstrated that Mn-doped ZnO BNPs exhibited a strong bacterial inhibitory effect towards E. coli and S. aureus, with MIC values in the range of 62.5–250 µg/mL, like those observed in this study. Another study by [116] reported enhanced antibacterial effects when combining Mg with other metals, showing synergistic broad-spectrum antibacterial activity. Such findings support the hypothesis that bimetallic systems like MnO-MgO can enhance antimicrobial performance compared to monometallic formulations.

Biofilm inhibition by MnO-MgO BNPs

The antibiofilm activity of the sub-MIC of the mycosynthesized MnO-MgO BNPs, including the 0.5 MIC, 0.25 MIC, and 0.125 MIC, was evaluated against a panel of uropathogenic bacteria. As presented in Table 3 and Fig. 12, the tested BNPs demonstrated significant biofilm inhibition across various strains. Notably, P. mirabilis exhibited the highest susceptibility to biofilm reduction, achieving 92.04% inhibition at 0.5 MIC. Strong inhibitory effects were also observed at 0.5 MIC against S. saprophyticus (76.89%) and K. pneumoniae (79.79%). At the lowest concentration tested (0.125 MIC), P. mirabilis consistently showed robust biofilm inhibition (53.92%), outperforming other strains, which generally displayed reductions ranging from 10.48% for S. aureus to 30.4% for E. coli and P. aeruginosa. Statistical analysis of antibiofilm inhibition showed significant difference with P value < 0.001.

Biofilms are notoriously difficult to treat due to their protective matrix, which shields bacteria from antimicrobials and host immune responses, leading to chronic and recurrent infections, particularly in the UT [117, 118]. The observed potent antibiofilm activity of MnO-MgO BNPs, particularly against P. mirabilis, S. saprophyticus, and K. pneumoniae, underscores their significant potential as novel therapeutic agents for uropathogenic infections. The high percentage of biofilm clearance achieved at sub-inhibitory concentrations is particularly important, as it suggests that these NPs could be effective even at doses below those required to kill planktonic bacteria, potentially reducing the risk of toxicity to host cells while disrupting the persistent biofilm structure [119]. The remarkable efficacy against P. mirabilis (92.04% clearance at 0.5 MIC) is highly relevant, given its role in complicated UTIs, catheter-associated UTIs, and struvite stone formation, which are often biofilm-mediated [120]. Similarly, the strong activity against S. saprophyticus (76.89%) is clinically significant, as this pathogen represents one of the prominent causes of young females’ UTIs [121], and K. pneumoniae (79.79%) is a major concern due to its increasing MDR and biofilm-forming capabilities in both hospital and community settings [122]. Our findings align with a growing body of recent research demonstrating the antibiofilm potential of various metal and metal oxide NPs. For instance, studies within the last 5 years have shown that ZnO NPs can disrupt E. coli and P. aeruginosa biofilms [123], and MgO NPs have exhibited significant activity against S. aureus and P. aeruginosa biofilms [124]. The combined Mg-Mn formulation, in our study, may confer synergistic antibiofilm properties, leveraging the distinct mechanisms of action of each metal. Zinc NPs are known to generate ROS, interfere with bacterial metabolism, and damage cell membranes, while magnesium NPs can disrupt cell integrity and inhibit enzymatic activities [125].

The differential efficacy observed where P. mirabilis showed particularly high susceptibility even at lower concentrations, while S. aureus exhibited less reduction at 0.125 MIC, warrants further investigation. This could be attributed to variations in bacterial cellular components, differences in biofilm matrix architecture, or specific efflux pump mechanisms present in these uropathogens that interact differently with the NPs. The Gram-negative outer membrane of P. mirabilis might present a unique target or allow for more efficient NPs interaction compared to the thicker peptidoglycan layer of Gram-positive S. aureus. With increasing AMR and persistent biofilm-associated infections in UTIs, particularly in vulnerable populations, novel strategies are urgently needed. Nanoparticle-based interventions, such as the MnO-MgO BNPs, offer a promising avenue to augment conventional antimicrobial therapy by directly targeting the biofilm, potentially reducing bacterial load, improving antimicrobial penetration, and preventing recurrent infections. This approach could lead to more effective treatment regimens and better patient outcomes, especially in complicated and recurrent UTI cases where current options are limited. Future studies should focus on optimizing NPs size, shape, and surface chemistry and conducting researches for in vivo evaluation of biosafety and pharmacokinetics, as well as their efficacy for controlling appropriate UTI animal models.

Biofilm clearance by MnO-MgO BNPs

The biofilm clearance assay demonstrated that MnO-MgO BNPs exhibited varying efficacy against uropathogenic bacteria, with the highest activity observed against P. aeruginosa (67.97 ± 0.29%), followed by S. aureus (61 ± 0.74%) and S. saprophyticus (52.67 ± 0.54%) (Fig. 13). Moderate clearance was noted against P. mirabilis (28.67 ± 0.91%), while the lowest activity was seen in K. pneumoniae (18.03 ± 0.37%) and E. coli (16.28 ± 0.13%). These findings suggested the effectiveness of MnO-MgO BNPs towards Gram-positive bacterial strains and P. aeruginosa but less so against Gram-negative enterobacteria. The superior activity against P. aeruginosa aligns with recent studies highlighting the enhanced penetration of metal NPs into pseudomonal biofilms due to their extracellular matrix composition [126]. The moderate performance against P. mirabilis contrasts with earlier reports using Ag NPs, which showed higher efficacy [127], possibly due to differences in NPs composition and bacterial resistance mechanisms. The low clearance in E. coli and K. pneumoniae may stem from their dense lipopolysaccharide layer, limiting NPs penetration [128]. Clinically, the strong activity against S. aureus and P. aeruginosa is promising, as these pathogens are frequently implicated in catheter-associated UTIs. However, the limited effect on E. coil warrants further investigation, given its prevalence in UTIs.

Synergistic interaction of MgO-MnO BNPs with amoxicillin

The combined formulation of MnO-MgO BNPs with AMX demonstrated synergistic antimicrobial activity against S. aureus. While both agents individually exhibited an MIC of 128 µg/mL, their combination significantly reduced the required concentrations to 16 µg/mL for BNPs and 32 µg/mL for AMX. This yielded FICs of 0.125 and 0.25, respectively, resulting in a FICi of 0.375. This FICi value unequivocally confirms a synergistic relationship, indicating that the combined application of MnO-MgO BNPs and AMX substantially enhances antimicrobial efficacy against S. aureus. Using the Combenefit platform to analyze synergy and antagonism, a pronounced synergistic effect against S. aureus ATCC 25923 was observed, particularly at low to moderate concentrations. This synergy is quantitatively and visually demonstrated through checkerboard plots and contour maps, which identify regions where the combined treatment exhibits greater efficacy than the additive effects of the individual agents (Fig. 14). Furthermore, the Loewe model corroborated these findings, with peak synergy scores indicating that MnO-MgO BNPs significantly potentiate the antimicrobial activity of AMX. The dose–response curves for both AMX and the MnO-MgO BNPs individually revealed strong, concentration-dependent inhibition, with half-maximal effective concentration (EC₅₀) values of 3.07 µg/mL and 3.01 µg/mL, respectively. Notably, the combination treatment was highly effective in suppressing bacterial growth, achieving near-complete eradication. These results underscore the therapeutic potential of this combinatorial approach for managing infections caused by S. aureus ATCC 25923. The observed synergistic interactions between MnO-MgO BNPs and AMX against S. aureus represent a critical finding, especially considering the escalating challenge of AMR. The ability of these BNPs to significantly enhance AMX's effectiveness, particularly at lower concentrations of both agents, offers a promising strategy to overcome bacterial defense mechanisms and potentially reduce the required antimicrobial dosage. This is highly relevant in an era where S. aureus, a major human pathogen, frequently develops resistance to conventional antimicrobials, including β-lactams like AMX, leading to persistent and difficult-to-treat infections [129]. Recent research within the last five years has increasingly explored the potential of NMs to augment antimicrobial activity. Studies have demonstrated that various metal and metal oxide NPs, including those containing magnesium, could be associated with bacterial membrane disruption and generation of ROS, as well as interference with vital metabolic pathways, thereby sensitizing bacteria to antimicrobials or acting as direct antimicrobial agents [130]. Our findings align with this trend, suggesting that the combined properties of magnesium and manganese within a NPs structure provide a robust platform for improving antimicrobial efficacy. From a clinical perspective, this synergy could pave the way for novel combination therapies, potentially enabling the reintroduction or enhanced use of antimicrobials that are currently less effective due to resistance. This approach holds the promise of improving patient outcomes for S. aureus infections by facilitating more complete bacterial eradication, preventing treatment failures, and potentially slowing the emergence of further resistance.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated the successful mycosynthesis of MnO-MgO BNPs using A. niger PQ269689.1. Physicochemical and structural characterization using UV–vis, FTIR, TEM, SEM, EDX, and XRD analyses confirmed the crystalline nature of the BNPs, with particle sizes ranging from 10 to 85 nm and a relatively uniform size distribution (PDI = 0.253). The in vitro cytotoxicity test revealed an appropriate safety margin against normal WI-38 fibroblasts (IC50 = 324.44 µg/mL) and selective, dose-dependent activity against MCF-7 breast cancer cells (IC50 = 96.09 µg/mL). The BNPs showed strain-dependent antibacterial action, with comparably weaker activity against K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603 and the greatest inhibition against E. coli ATCC 25922 and S. aureus ATCC 25923. The checkerboard experiment showed a synergistic interaction with amoxicillin against S. aureus ATCC 25923 (FICi = 0.375), and sub-MIC concentrations of the BNPs showed significant antibiofilm activity, especially against P. mirabilis ATCC 29906 (92.04% inhibition at 0.5 MIC).

Limitation and future work

It should be mentioned that this study has certain limitations. The in vitro conditions used for all antimicrobial, antibiofilm, and cytotoxicity evaluations may not accurately reflect the complexity of in vivo biological environments, including host immune responses, protein corona formation on NPs surfaces, and pharmacokinetic variables like biodistribution and clearance. Second, the antimicrobial evaluation was limited to ATCC reference bacteria; testing against clinical MDR isolates from UTI patients would offer a more clinically relevant assessment of the BNPs' therapeutic potential. Third, the synergistic interaction with amoxicillin was only tested using S. aureus ATCC 25923; the data's generalizability might be enhanced by extending the checkerboard testing to other bacterial species and antimicrobial classes. Fourth, the long-term stability of the biosynthesized MnO–MgO BNPs in different physiological and storage conditions was not assessed. Finally, even though the MTT assay demonstrated an acceptable safety profile against WI-38 normal cells (IC50 = 324.44 µg/mL), a more comprehensive biocompatibility assessment using multiple normal cell types, hemolysis assays, and genotoxicity testing would be necessary to create a strong safety profile. This illustrates a single cytotoxicity endpoint and a single normal cell line.

To evaluate biocompatibility, pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and therapeutic efficacy, future research should focus on in vivo evaluation utilizing suitable UTI animal models. The translational potential of these BNPs would also be further advanced by examining the antimicrobial mechanisms of MnO–MgO BNPs at the molecular level (e.g., ROS quantification, membrane integrity assays, and transcriptomic or proteomic analyses), optimizing NPs size and surface functionalization for targeted delivery to urinary tract tissues, and investigating synergistic combinations with other antibiotic classes against a wider panel of clinical MDR uropathogens.
