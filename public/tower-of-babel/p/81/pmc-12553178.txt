The MyCancerGene study: a hybrid type 1 effectiveness-implementation randomized study comparing a patient-centered digital genetic health portal to usual care after receipt of cancer genetic testing

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenetic testing has become more complex with the transition from single gene testing to multi-gene panels and whole-exome sequencing. The adoption of more complex genetic testing and the promise of precision medicine has created a critical and urgent need for longitudinal clinical follow-up with patients who undergo cancer genetic testing given the residual uncertainty. Background

The promise of precision medicine is the ability to tailor treatment or screening of patients based on their genotype, and to translate those recommendations into modified patient behaviors or treatment. Advances in clinical sequencing leave many patients who have undergone clinical genetic testing with uncertainties regarding interpretation, implications and recommendations, which will change over time. BRCA1/2 and Lynch Syndrome testing for predisposition to breast, colon and gynecologic cancers are examples of evidence-based standard-of-care applications of precision medicine [1, 2]. Mutations in many other genes (e.g. PALB2 and CHEK2) have been also been associated with elevated risks of cancer [3–7]. As such, multi-gene panel testing (MGPT) has entered clinical practice to screen many cancer susceptibility genes simultaneously [6, 8–10]. Many of the genes included in MGPT are moderate-penetrance genes, increasing the risk of cancer by only 2–4 fold. In many cases risks estimates are based on limited data and continue to evolve over time [7, 9]. Further, there remains uncertainty regarding optimal screening given constantly evolving medical management recommendations (e.g. NCCN and other guidelines are evolving as new data emerges). As such, even positive results leave many unknowns regarding cancer risks, optimal management and the value of testing unaffected relatives [7, 9] MGPT has also been associated with higher rates of variants of uncertain significance (VUS), which are difficult for patients to understand and will be reclassified over time. This clinical transition from discrete (i.e. single gene) to broad (i.e. MGPT, whole-genome) sequencing in clinical genetic testing presents challenges that will only increase as precision medicine applications expand [9]. With “broader” sequencing, an increasing number of patients will be left at risk for misunderstanding, uncertainty and evolving interpretations and recommendations thus needing to address knowledge [6, 11]. These uncertainties will often be clarified over time, but we lack clinical care models to maintain longitudinal post-test communication with patients in order to provide updated information regarding their test result or medical recommendations and to obtain updates in their personal and medical history [11, 12]. In the current standard-of-care, the commercial lab contacts the ordering provider, who in turn contacts the patient. This model is not sustainable and unsuccessful due to outdated contact information [12]. Alternatively, patients can “call–in” annually [13], but this places a burden on patients and has not been proven to be successful [14–16]. Thus, effective, evidence-based alternative strategies for longitudinal communication and care for genetic patients are critical to realizing the promise of precision medicine [17].

Some recent studies have shown that genetic knowledge declines after cancer genetic testing [18, 19], and that longitudinal informational needs persist after testing [20–22]. Understanding of the implications of testing has been identified as an important outcome of clinical genetic testing, although how to best measure understanding and what minimum knowledge is needed for patients to take appropriate action remains unclear [23–26]. Longitudinal data in the Communication Of GENetic Test Results by Telephone (COGENT) study revealed that patients who did not return for in-person medical management after remote phone disclosure had lower mammography adherence [27]. Multiple studies have shown that longitudinal distress and cancer worry are higher among patients with a positive results, even in moderate risk genes [28–30]. Patients with VUS results have similarly shown increased longitudinal distress and uncertainty in some studies [17, 29, 31]. Similar findings have been reported in other subsets of patients, including those with lower formal education, cancer patients and specifically those without a family history of cancer [28, 32]. Collectively, these data highlight the need for post-test longitudinal follow-up with patients after genetic testing [29, 30, 33, 34].

The Institute of Medicine has highlighted the importance of patient-centered care and improved transparency to enhance the delivery and outcomes of medical care [35–37]. Data from the Pew Research Center reports that 95% of Americans use the internet [38], and patients have high interest in communicating electronically with providers [39–41]. In response, there has been a growing movement to create online patient-centered digital health portals (DHPs) to promote communication both provider and curated educational materials to promote medical care [42]. These patient portals may be particularly effective in chronic disease, where longitudinal care is crucial, informational needs change over time or vary among patients, when coping and adjustment are part of the “journey”, and improved medical outcomes requires changing health behaviors [43]. Studies from a variety of chronic disease settings have shown that patient-centered DHPs can improve knowledge [43], self-efficacy [43–46], satisfaction [40, 47–49], clinical outcomes [43, 47, 49, 50], and unmet communication needs [46, 49, 51]. Recent randomized studies have shown reductions in distress with DHPs [40, 44, 52–54], and that DHPs may provide education in a way that is more simple, accessible and salient resulting in better knowledge retention [43]. However, only a few patient-centered DHPs have been launched with a focus on genetic testing and counseling [55]. Our intervention was designed to serve as patient-centered DHP to provide longitudinal (short-term and long-term) care after genetic testing.

MyCancerGene

The MyCancerGene study is informed by our theoretical model to evaluate innovations to delivery of genetic information (Fig. 1). This model, informed by the Self-Regulation Theory of Health Behaviors (SRTHB) and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), provides an innovative framework for studying the effectiveness of an eHealth intervention (Aims 1 & 2) and barriers and facilitators to future dissemination and implementation (Aim 3).

Improved patient outcomes are contingent upon high-risk individuals adopting preventive health behaviors and communicating their results to at-risk relatives. Thus, the effectiveness of genetic testing for disease susceptibility is contingent upon successful communication and behavior modification [56–58]. SRTHB has been utilized in intervention-based research involving the study of health threats and health behavior [59–63]. Simplistically, the conceptual model, grounded in SRTHB proposes that the reaction to and use of health (genetic) information is the product of an individual’s understanding, (i.e. knowledge and perceptions of the disease threat) and the biopsychosocial impact of the risk and the health behavior [59, 61, 63–66]. The SRTHB, emphasizes “common-sense” representations rather than medical or scientific definitions, and incorporates individual experiences, and the impact of knowledge and perceptions on risk modification for individuals and their families. As the model is iterative and dynamic, it is responsive to changes in understanding, reactions to and use of genetic information that might occur as a result of engagement with an DHP intervention to provide longitudinal updates to genetic information, communication and support.

Even after health-related interventions have proven effectiveness, many fail to translate into clinical settings [35, 67]. Thus, there is increasing recognition of the importance of evaluating and addressing barriers to implementation across diverse health care settings. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) provides an overarching theoretical framework to evaluate barriers, enhancements and adaptations to increase successful implementation of evidence-based interventions [68]. We have included constructs from each of the 5 major CFIR domains (intervention characteristics, outer setting, inner setting, characteristics of individuals and process) as they relate to our intervention, which will be evaluated in our multi-stakeholder mixed-methods process evaluation (Aim 3b). As in other studies [69–71], evaluation of CFIR components is expected to identify patient, provider and system barriers and enablers of MyCancerGene to future dissemination to increase access and adoption of genetic services.

To address this clinically significant gap in the delivery of genetic medicine, we obtained patient and provider input to develop a patient and provider informed electronic patient-centered genetic DHP called “MyCancerGene” to enhance longitudinal patient understanding of, and affective and behavioral responses to genetic testing, particularly in the era of evolving evidence and risk information (Table 1). Our five-step process for developing and testing our DHP is reported in greater detail in Trieu et al. [72] Patient and provider input identified advantages and disadvantages to the tool and how it would differ from usual care practices (see Table 2). This input informed eight key components of MyCancerGene, including: (1) Landing Page, (2) Summary of Care, (3) My Genetic Test Results, (4) My Family History, (5) Provide an Update, (6) Review an Update, (7) Resources, and (8) a Screenings Tracker. Providers and patients also recommended key functions, including the ability to download and print materials and the inclusion of reminders and engagement functions (Table 1). Given the increasing complexity of genetic testing applications (e.g. multi-gene testing, whole exome sequencing) and the need for longitudinal follow-up, we expect this study to inform evidence-based practice guidelines for delivery of genetic medicine and potentially change the paradigm to include longitudinal care in precision medicine.

Present study (Fig. 2)

Study Schema

The overall goal of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of the MyCancerGene intervention to improve post-disclosure short-term and long-term understanding (e.g. knowledge) and reactions to (e.g. anxiety) clinical genetic testing compared to usual care. We hypothesize that access to MyCancerGene will be associated with longitudinal (short-term and long term) increases in knowledge, decreases in distress, increases in communication with relatives and health care providers and performance of risk reducing health behaviors compared to usual care arm patients (Aims 1 & 2). Equally important, we hypothesize that our CFIR-informed process evaluation (Aim 3) will inform recommendations for future adaptation and sustainability for Precision Medicine applications to improve short-term and longitudinal outcomes.

Specific aim 1

Our primary aim is to evaluate the effectiveness of the MyCancerGene to improve post-test short-term understanding (e.g. knowledge) and reactions to (e.g. anxiety and uncertainty) clinical genetic testing compared to usual care. The primary outcomes include knowledge and general anxiety from baseline to 1 month. Secondary outcomes include additional cognitive and affective outcomes.

Specific aim 2

Our secondary aim is the evaluate the longitudinal (6 month to 18 month) effectiveness of MyCancerGene to improve understanding of (e.g. knowledge), reactions to (e.g. anxiety and uncertainty) and use of (e.g. communication to relatives and health care providers and performance of risk reducing health behaviors) genetic test results, and to reduce genetic provider time, as compared to usual care.

Specific aim 3

Our third aim is to understand (a) potential moderators (e.g. intervention usage, adherence and attrition, sociodemographic factors, genetic test result) of short-term and longitudinal changes in understanding of, reactions to and use of genetic test results to understand who benefits more or less from access to MyCancerGene (Aim 3a); and (b) facilitators and barriers to clinical implementation of MyCancerGene and recommendations for future adaptation and sustainability of MyCancerGene for widespread clinical use (Aim 3b).

Methods

Study design

This study is a Hybrid Type 1 effectiveness-implementation study [73]. Participants are randomized to access to MyCancerGene or usual care (control condition) and complete assessments at baseline and at 1, 6, 12, and 18 months (Fig. 2). After 12 months, participants in the usual care arm will be provided access to MyCancerGene to evaluate moderators of responses to genetic information. The study includes a concurrent multi-stakeholder, mixed-methods implementation process evaluation to inform future adaptation and dissemination of the MyCancerGene intervention.

Setting

This study was approved by the institutional review board of the University of Pennsylvania. Potential subjects include individuals who have had germline genetic testing at the University of Pennsylvania Cancer Risk Evaluation Program (CREP) and the University of Pennsylvania Telegenetics Program, which provides cancer genetic services in community practices and patients across the US.

Study participants

Eligibility

Eligibility criteria include adult (18 years and older) male and female individuals who received traditional pre- and post-test genetic counseling delivered in-person and/or virtually (by phone or videoconference) with one of 9 genetic counselors within 90 days. These genetic counselors were selected at study start and additional genetic counselors will not be added given research budget limitations. Only patients seen by the designated genetic counselors were approached for participation. For patients with a personal history of cancer, this was extended to 6 months given competing medical burdens and ongoing treatment. Exclusion criteria include not speaking English and not having internet and/or mobile access (including email access).

Recruitment

Individuals meeting the study specified eligibility criteria are contacted by members of the research study team after the receipt of their genetic test results. Eligible participants are first electronically sent the study introduction letter by email and/or the Electronic Health Record (EHR). The letter has details regarding the study purpose and procedures as well as the direct REDCap web-link (a secure, web-based data platform, accessible to and managed by research team members only) to the electronic Informed Consent and HIPAA document. Those approached who actively decline participation are asked to provide a reason, which is recorded along with de-identified characteristic data including age, race/ethnicity, marital status, and education. A total of 4 reminders are sent, alternating electronic and telephone communications, to eligible individuals who do not respond to the initial invitation, for a potential total of 5 contact attempts.

Enrollment goals

As outlined below, our enrollment goal for the primary aim 376 participants total, providing at least 137/arm with evaluable data.

Study arms

Once participants complete the informed consent and the baseline (T0) survey, they are randomly assigned to the Intervention or Usual Care arm using a permuted block design stratified by test result and referral source (CREP v. Telegenetics Program).

Intervention arm: Access to MyCancerGene

Patients in the intervention arm are provided a password and link to the secure intervention website. They are asked to provide a personal, private email address to ensure the password and link are securely delivered, and to facilitate engagement messages regarding updating family history and messaging for other updates. Every three months, genetic providers and clinician investigators will review and discuss any updates in the literature (e.g. new risk estimates, new testing or screening recommendations). If indicated, general updates in addition to tailored alerts will be sent to relevant participants. All updates regarding variant classification, based on changes to family history and by gene will initially be sent out via MyCancerGene. Participants receive 5 reminders over 14 days. If the update is not viewed within that time, their genetic counselor will notify them utilizing the usual care process. While participants can receive updates and message the genetics team through MyCancerGene, they also have all usual care options available to them as well. Tracking of intervention use and usual care communication and visits in the EHR are recorded.

Usual care arm

Patients randomized to the usual care arm receive updates from the clinical genetics program consistent with usual care practices at the time of the study (see examples in Table 2). At the time the study initiated, this includes the option to message or call their genetics provider if questions emerge. The clinical team contacts patients by letter or phone with any lab updates of VUS results. In some circumstances, the clinical program may create a letter or message update about new testing or guidelines, although these mass update notifications are limited to highly clinically significant changes. Patients are encouraged to contact the genetics program if there are changes in their family history. Some patients may return for annual medical follow-up based on provider recommendations and their preferences, although there were no systematic methods for such longitudinal follow-up. Tracking of usual care communication and visits in the EHR are recorded. Any changes to usual care that occur during the study are recorded. Access to MyCancerGene is provided to usual care participants at 12 months post consent.

Outcomes

Effectiveness outcomes (Aims 1 and 2)

Implementation outcomes

Implementation outcomes were determined by identifying key CFIR-informed constructs across the five domains to evaluate factors related to uptake of MyCancerGene, and implementation facilitators, and barriers and adaptations (Table 4). We plan a multi-stakeholder mixed-methods process evaluation. We will conduct key informant interviews with patients and providers seeking to better understand (a) potential moderators (e.g. intervention usage, adherence and attrition, sociodemographic factors, genetic test result as listed in Table 4) of longitudinal (short-term and long-term) changes in understanding of, reactions to and use of genetic test results to understand who benefits more or less from access to the intervention; and (b) facilitators and barriers to implementation and recommendations for future adaptation and sustainability of MyCancerGene for widespread clinical use. CFIR constructs of interest from these key informant groups will include: (1) relative advantages and disadvantages; (2) compatibility; (3) complexity; (4) impact on social relations; (5) observability; (6) time; (7) trialability; (8) communication needs; and (9) intent to adopt. Interviews will be conducted in the last six months of the randomized study. We seek to continue recruitment to the above enrollment goals or until data saturation occurs across the theoretical domain (e.g. no new themes introduced within the constructs included in key informant interviews) [93]. Interviews will be conducted by phone by research staff, audio recorded and transcribed.

Data analysis plan

Primary effectiveness analysis (Aim 1)

We hypothesize that our intervention will be associated with short-term: (a) increases in knowledge, and (b) reductions in anxiety as compared to the usual care control group. Based on our patient and provider stakeholder interviews (endorsing improvements and maintenance of understanding of their results as a benefit of a DHP), our conceptual model and outcomes from DHPs in other settings, we have two primary outcomes: changes in (1) knowledge, and (2) anxiety. All other outcomes are considered secondary outcomes. Our primary change scores are the change between baseline (T0) and the first (T1) follow-up at one month. We will use T-tests for the continuous variables (change in knowledge and anxiety). We use an intention to treat approach in which we compare outcomes between randomization arms for the primary analyses. We assess the balance of potential confounding variables between randomization arms using T-tests or Fisher’s Exact tests for continuous or binary variables as appropriate. Potential confounders may include age, race, recruitment source (CREP v. Telegenetics), and time since genetic disclosure counseling or testing, for example. In secondary analyses, we use multiple linear regressions (continuous outcomes) and a logistic regression (intention to communicate) to control for potential confounding variables that are found to be inadequately balanced by randomization. As covariates in the regressions, we include an indicator (binary 0/1 variable) to indicate randomization arm as well as the potential confounding variables. We repeat these analyses for the secondary outcomes and also examine the per-protocol and as-treated effects to investigate dose-response relationships. To account for missing data, we use multiple imputation methods for the primary analyses of interest to maintain the intention to treat approach [104]. We use complete case analyses as secondary sensitivity analyses.

Sample size justification for aim 1

We chose our initial sample size of 188/arm (376 total) to have sufficient power to detect changes between arms using a T-test after accounting for loss to follow-up or lower than expected accrual. We set power to 85% (Table 5) and the Type I error rate (alpha) to 2.5% (2-sided). We chose a 2.5% Type I error by applying a Bonferroni correction to the typical Type I error rate of 5% with 2 comparisons (5%/2 = 2.5%). To obtain pilot data we use the change scores from those who used the web intervention in our Returning Genetic Research Panel Results for Breast Cancer Susceptibility (RESPECT) Study (R01 CA11574119: Bradbury). In the RESPECT study, we developed a web-based version of pre-disclosure education and informed consent as an alternative to pre-disclosure counseling with a genetic counselor. 85% of patients at the time of analysis chose the on-line option and outcome measures were similar to those proposed in this study. Since RESPECT is a one arm study, we assumed that the control change for this trial would be zero with the same standard deviation as reported in Table 5. The sample sizes in the above table are well below what we expect to recruit in the study (N = 376). Thus, we have excellent power for the overall effect and select subgroup effects.

Evaluation of the effectiveness of the intervention to change longitudinal outcomes and provider time (Aim 2).

We hypothesize that our intervention will be associated with longitudinal (a) increases in understanding; (b) reductions in anxiety; and (c) use of (e.g. communication or screening behaviors) genetic test results, and (d) reduced provider time as compared to the usual care control group. For this analysis, we examine linear (for continuous outcomes) and logistic (for binary outcomes) regressions estimated by Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) to account for within subject correlation over time to investigate time trend trajectories. Survey time point will be included via indicator variables (0/1 binary variables) in the longitudinal models. We also include the intervention arm indicator and the interaction between intervention indicator and survey timepoint indicators [105]. The interaction terms enable us to examine whether the effect of the intervention varies over timepoints. While Aim 2 analyses will be secondary analyses, we examine both the intention to treat, per-protocol, and as-treated effects on time trends.

In addition to the psychosocial variables, provider time are evaluated throughout the study in a subset of patients. We again account for repeated measures within participants via the GEE estimated models. In secondary exploratory analyses we additionally account for clusters of patients within provider using provider-level fixed effect terms in the models to investigate the provider-specific heterogeneity in time spent with participants across staff.

Evaluation of potential moderators outcomes and facilitators and barriers to implementation (Aim 3)

Our first phase of Aim 3 is to understand the potential moderators (e.g. intervention usage, adherence and attrition, sociodemographic factors, genetic test result) of short-term and longitudinal changes in understanding of, reactions to and use of genetic test results to understand who benefits more or less from access to MyCancerGene. We hypothesize that MyCancerGene access and use, sociodemographic factors, genetic test result, time since genetic disclosure counseling or testing, and personal and family history of cancer will moderate outcomes between arms. For moderation (i.e. effect modifier or interaction analyses), we include in the GEE-estimated multiple linear or logistic regressions for the outcomes of interest an indicator variable for randomization arm, the potential moderator variable, and an interaction term between the two (i.e. an interaction term will be created by multiplying the indicator variable by the moderator variable) [105]. We also examine models in which we further include the survey timepoint indicators, interaction terms between survey timepoint indicators and randomization arm, interaction terms between moderators and survey timepoints, and the three-way interaction terms created by multiplying survey timepoints indicators, randomization arm, and the moderator. To maintain power to detect moderation, we examine non-survey timepoint moderators separately. We also examine multiple regression models in which we include potential confounding variables, as well as the main effects and interaction terms of interest. We repeat the analyses in which we investigate (1) per protocol or (2) as-treated effects by replacing the randomization arm indicator with indicators representing (1) per protocol or (2) actual treatment, regardless of randomization assignment.

Evaluation of dissemination and implementation processes (Aim 3b)

Finally, our last evaluation process is to conduct a multi-stakeholder mixed-methods process evaluation to understand facilitators and barriers to implementation and recommendations for future adaptation and sustainability of MyCancerGene for widespread clinical use. We quantitatively characterize factors associated with uptake using T-tests (continuous predictors), chi-squared tests (categorical predictors), and multiple logistic regressions (multivariable models). We control for randomization arm in multivariable models. Next, qualitative data are analyzed using an iterative, deductive content analysis approach and CFIR as the coding framework. As described above, the qualitative data include: (a) an open-ended item from patient surveys (How can we improve MyCancerGene?) and key informant interviews; (b) notes from our IT Integration Committee, regular team meetings and team debriefing. Guided by consensual qualitative methods, two independent coders review notes and develop a coding schema. The two coders and a third qualitative researcher review and reach consensus on any coding discrepancies and then assign final codes to the CFIR model. Finally, using a convergent mixed-methods approach, we merge the quantitative and qualitative data, organized by the 5 CFIR domains to evaluate which constructs were associated with, or more prevalent among patients who accessed MyCancerGene once, more than once and never, providing an opportunity to comprehensively evaluate facilitators and barriers to use of MyCancerGene and to make recommendations for future adaptation and sustainability beyond our study population.

Discussion

We expect to have evaluated the effectiveness of one of the first patient and provider informed genetic DHPs, MyCancerGene at the conclusion of this of this randomized trial. Equally important, we expect to have both use data, revealing which intervention components are most efficacious, and patient and provider reported facilitators and barriers to future clinical implementation. While designed for cancer genetic testing, we expect this stakeholder and theoretically informed DHP to be adaptable for other precision medicine applications, providing opportunities to more broadly optimize the clinical outcomes of precision medicine for a diverse population of patients and their families.

Limitations and potential challenges

There are potential limitations we may encounter with this study. We acknowledge that some patients in real-world clinical settings do not have internet access. To address this limitation, we will develop our MyCancerGene to be mobile ready. Second, as reported by others, there are a range of current barriers to EHR integration for a range of eHealth interventions [106–109]. While we will populate baseline data from the EHR, full integration of MyCancerGene with the EHR is not possible at this time. Nonetheless, we have created an Informatics Integration Committee to consider future integration options, while we are conducting the study. We acknowledge that clinically meaningful understanding of results can be challenging to measure and current knowledge scales have limitations. Some items may not be necessary for patients to perform the appropriate screening and risk reduction measures and what patients need to know varies based on their result and personal and family history. While knowledge is a primary outcome, we have included additional cognitive outcomes as secondary measures (e.g. test result recall, perceived risk) as well as health behaviors. We recognize that it is difficult to anticipate the frequency and timing of future changes in the literature (e.g. changes in associated cancer risks with particular genes, changes in medical management recommendations) or interpretation of the clinical significance of VUS results. We chose the 12-month period for release of MyCancerGene to the usual care arm to maximize the time for updates to occur. A shorter period could limit our ability to identify the effectiveness of the intervention for participants with updates. If updates are lower than expected, further longitudinal data may be necessary to evaluate this benefit but does not impact our primary outcomes.

Potential for impact and implications

This study will provide critical empiric data on the effectiveness of this patient-centered genetic DHP to improve longitudinal patient understanding, psychological adjustment, communication to relatives and other medical providers and adoption of screening and risk reducing behaviors. If effective, MyCancerGene could fill an important clinical gap in clinical cancer genetics practice and improve outcomes for patients and families undergoing genetic testing and making possible the promise of precision medicine.
