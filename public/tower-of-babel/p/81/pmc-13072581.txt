Thermal ablation versus hepatectomy for solitary colorectal liver metastases up to 5 cm: a multicenter target trial emulation on safety, efficacy, and cost-effectiveness

Background

Despite advancements in treatment options, colorectal cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. The management of solitary colorectal liver metastasis (SCLM) presents a critical clinical dilemma, particularly in patients deemed ineligible for surgical resection. Among patients with potentially curable colorectal liver metastases, approximately 38% to 55% present with a solitary lesion [2, 3]. Current consensus recognizes hepatectomy (HT) as the sole potentially curative intervention for SCLM. However, evolving clinical data from multicenter studies, including the EPOC trial series [4, 5], reveal that only approximately 20% of patients meet conventional surgical criteria due to multifactorial limitations such as tumor localization, functional liver reserve, and comorbidities. This therapeutic gap has driven intensified research into alternative treatment modalities, with recent guidelines from ESMO [6] and NCCN [7, 8] emphasizing the need for multidisciplinary evaluation and emerging technologies like thermal ablation (TA), stereotactic body radiation therapy and irreversible electroporation in non-surgical candidates. Image-guided TA techniques, such as radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and microwave ablation (MWA), provide alternative treatment options for elderly, frail, or non-surgical candidates with comorbidities. The ablative guideline has confirmed that TA is less invasive and effective for non-surgical candidates [9].

Historically, retrospective studies have shown that 5-year overall survival rates for patients with SCLM after HT range from 50 to 70% [10, 11].According to recently trials, the Landmark trials (COLLISION trial [2] and MAVERRIC study [3]) demonstrated that TA is non-inferior to HT for treating small colorectal liver metastasis (< 3 cm), yet data for larger tumors (3–5 cm) remained limited. No randomized trials or large-scale observational studies directly compared TA and HT for SCLM ≤ 5 cm with long-term follow-up. To address this evidence deficit, we conducted a nationwide multicenter study using a target trial emulation framework, leveraging observational data from real-world clinical practice to rigorously evaluate the comparative effectiveness of these two approaches for SCLM.

Methods

Target trial

Conducting this nationwide, multicenter, retrospective cohort study (2009–2024) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, we obtained ethics approval from all participating centers. Given the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent from patients was waived. We emulated the target trial using observational data (see Table S1 in the supplementary material). This approach relies on an established framework that incorporates inclusion and exclusion criteria comparable to those used in randomized controlled trials. Additionally, we employed propensity score matching (PSM) to mimic the randomization process of treatment allocation, thereby enhancing the validity of our observational study by reducing selection bias.

Data source and population

This retrospective cohort study, spanning from 2009 to 2024, enrolled patients with SCLM from 21 Chinese hospitals across three distinct healthcare tiers: tertiary general hospitals (constituting 60%), gastrointestinal specialty centers (30%), and oncology hospitals (20%). By integrating retrospective and prospective data collection protocols, the study ensured comprehensive, multidimensional data capture.

The eligibility criteria were as follows: a) age ≥ 18 years; b) multidisciplinary team (MDT) diagnosis of SCLM without imaging-confirmed extrahepatic metastases, and lesions deemed resectable or ablatable with curative intent; c) prior resection of the primary colorectal tumor or planned curative resection of the primary tumor; d) deemed suitable for liver resection or thermal ablation by the MDT; and e) radiology-based minimum ablation margin (MAM) ≥ 5 mm and pathology-based surgical margin (SM) ≥ 1 mm (applied to all enrolled patients). Recurrent SCLM was defined as a new solitary metastasis arising after prior curative treatment for a different liver metastasis.

A total of 1660 patients were initially enrolled in this study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) extrahepatic metastasis identified on initial CT or MRI (n = 103); (b) receipt of palliative therapy or presence of residual tumor (n = 86); (c) MAM < 5 mm and SM < 1 mm (n = 70); (d) liver metastases larger than 5 cm in maximum diameter, as confirmed by contrast-enhanced MRI (n = 41); and (e) a history of other malignancies within the past five years (n = 30). After applying these criteria, 1334 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Demographic, clinical, pathological, treatment, and outcome data were systematically retrieved from electronic medical records at each participating institution. This data extraction was performed by local investigators with specialized experience in managing SCLM.

Study flowchart

Treatment decision-making

Individualized treatment strategies were formulated by local multidisciplinary teams, which consisted of surgeons, radiologists, medical oncologists, gastroenterologists, and radiation oncologists. These decisions were made while considering the standardized factors, including: (1) tumor factors: size (≤ 5 cm), number (solitary), location (accessibility for ablation and resection), proximity to major vessels/bile ducts (> 5 mm preferred for ablation), and imaging characteristics; (2) patient factors: age, ECOG performance status (0–2), comorbidities, hepatic functional reserve (based on future liver remnant volume estimated via CT/MRI), and prior abdominal surgeries; (3) patient preference: after MDT recommendation, detailed discussion of risks/benefits of both options (TA vs. HT) was conducted with the patient/family, and patient choice was documented and respected.

All surgeons and radiologists participating in the study had prior experience performing the relevant procedures (HT or TA) on at least 50 patients with liver metastases. Study day 0 was defined as the calendar day when the HT or TA procedure was first initiated.

Treatment assignment

To identify patients who underwent TA and were most similar to those who underwent HT, we utilized propensity score matching. We considered a wide range of potential confounders, which were associated with treatment assignment and outcomes based on prior epidemiological evidence (see Fig. 2A). The propensity score was calculated using binary logistic regression, with treatment group as dependent variable and the following covariates as independent variables: sex (male, female), body mass index (BMI), plasma CEA, plasma CA199, ECOG performance status (0, 1, 2, 3), comorbidity (no, yes), location of primary tumor (left, right), WHO classification (low, high), tumor category (T1, T2, T3, T4), lymphatic spread (N0, N1–2), diameter of SCLM, types of SCLM (initial SCLM, recurrent SCLM), sequence of SCLM (simultaneous metastases, metachronous metastases), and disease-free interval (≥ 12 months, < 12 months). Treatment assignment was performed through 1:1 propensity score nearest neighbor matching without replacement to mimic the randomization process of a hypothetical trial. We set a caliper width of 0.05 standard deviations of the propensity scores on the logarithmic scale to create two groups of patients with the same distribution of all known baseline characteristics (Fig. 2B).

Treatment protocols

Multidisciplinary teams implemented standardized yet center-adapted protocols for both TA and HT. For TA, all procedures were performed percutaneously under image guidance. MWA was predominantly used (n = 780, 98.7%) with the KY-2000 system (2450 MHz, 40–60 W power, 3–10 min duration), employing single antennas for tumors < 2 cm and dual antennas for ≥ 2 cm lesions to ensure 5–10 mm circumferential margins. RFA (n = 10, 1.3%) utilized the Cool-tip™ system (Medtronic) with impedance-modulated energy delivery. Subdiaphragmatic lesions required ultrasound-guided hydrodissection to prevent thermal injury, and contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) optimized tumor targeting. For HT, preoperative CT/MRI assessed resectability, with open (n = 388; 71.3%) or laparoscopic (n = 156; 28.7%) approaches selected based on tumor characteristics (size, location, vascular proximity). Both TA and HT aimed to completely eradicate or remove visible tumors with adequate margins. Detailed information concerning the procedures can be found in Supplementary Information 1–2.

Outcome ascertainment

The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from HT or TA treatment to the first event, which included any of the following: local recurrence, regional recurrence, extrahepatic/systemic recurrence, or death from any cause. Secondary outcomes included overall survival (OS), local tumor progression-free survival (LPFS), new intrahepatic metastasis-free survival (IMFS), extrahepatic recurrence-free survival (ERFS), cancer-specific survival (CSS), post-treatment complications, and cost-effectiveness. OS was defined as the time from treatment to death from any cause. LPFS was defined as the time from treatment to the first imaging confirmed recurrence within or adjacent to the treated lesion. IMFS referred to the time to the development of new liver metastases outside the treated area. ERFS was defined as the time to any recurrence outside the liver. CSS was calculated as the time from treatment to death attributable to colorectal cancer, excluding deaths from other causes. Post-treatment complications for both TA and HT were rated using the Clavien-Dindo classification system [12, 13]. Hospital stay and hospitalization cost for both TA and HT were documented based on the central database records. If post-procedural imaging indicated an anticipated ablation margin < 5 mm, a planned supplementary ablation session was performed within one week. This was considered a completion of the primary treatment protocol. The follow-up clock for PFS and OS started from the date of the first ablation session. According to guidelines for ablative therapy and surgery [6, 9, 14–16], a radiology-based MAM of ≥ 5 mm (Figure S1) and a pathology-based SM of ≥ 1 mm was respectively recognized as optimal operative margins.

Follow-up

During the initial two-year period, all patients were subjected to follow—up evaluations every 3 to 6 months. Subsequently, annual assessments were conducted. Tumor status was evaluated through contrast-enhanced MRI or CT, contrast-enhanced ultrasound, or a combination of both imaging techniques. These modalities were chosen due to their capacity to yield detailed anatomical and functional data, which are essential for precise tumor assessment. Patient follow-up continued until the occurrence of the study outcome, loss to follow-up, or the study's conclusion on December 1, 2024, whichever event transpired first.

Statistical analysis

In the propensity score matching dataset, baseline characteristics were summarized as the mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. For continuous variables, comparisons between the two groups were performed using Student's t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on the data distribution. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson's chi-square tests or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate.

The Kaplan–Meier method was utilized to estimate the cumulative incidence of PFS and OS in each group. Following propensity score matching, a Cox proportional hazards model was applied to assess the comparative effectiveness of TA versus HT. Hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated as key effect measures, emulating the principles of an intention-to-treat analysis to ensure robust and unbiased results.

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on the following variables: age (< 60 years, ≥ 60 years), sex (male, female), comorbidity (no, yes), ECOG status (0, ≥ 1),WHO classification (low, high), lymphatic spread (N0, T1–2), tumor category (T1–2, T3–4), diameter of SCLM (≤ 3 cm, > 3 cm), sequence of SCLM (simultaneous metastases, metachronous metastases), disease-free interval (< 12 months, ≥ 12 months), and Clinical risk score (CRS) (0–1, 2–4).

Results

Patient characteristics

Among all patients with SCLM, 1334 were eligible for our emulated trial. The selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. Of the included patients, 790 (59.22%) received TA (mean ± SD age, 59.98 ± 11.37 years; 565 [71.52%] male), while 544 (40.78%) received HT (mean ± SD age, 59.05 ± 10.25 years; 349 [64.15%] male). After PSM, 437 patients in each group were matched to receive either TA or HT. The main baseline characteristics were well-balanced between the two groups. The TA group had a mean ± SD age of 60.35 ± 10.95 years, with 301 (68.88%) male patients, while the HT group had a mean ± SD age of 58.68 ± 10.22 years, with 286 (65.45%) male patients. The TA group exhibited a mean ± SD MAM of 6.51 ± 3.19 mm, whereas the HT group exhibited a mean ± SD SM of 2.03 ± 0.80 mm (Table 1).

Primary analysis

Among all enrolled patients, the median PFS was not significantly different between the TA group (1.81 years, 95% CI: 1.60–2.04) and the HT group (1.95 years, 95% CI: 1.67–2.34) (HR: 0.94, 95% CI: 0.82–1.09, P = 0.41). Post-treatment progression was observed in 457 patients (57.85%) in the TA group and 314 patients (57.72%) in the HT group. After propensity score matching (PSM), the TA group continued to show a similar hazard ratio for PFS compared to the HT group (adjusted HR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.79–1.13, P = 0.52). In the matched cohorts, post-treatment progression occurred in 243 patients (55.61%) in the TA group and 246 patients (56.29%) in the HT group (Table 2). The 5-year PFS rates were 30.05% (95% CI: 25.32%−35.67%) for the TA group and 32.16% (95% CI: 27.49%−37.63%) for the HT group (Fig. 3A).

The sensitivity analysis results for PFS consistently aligned with the primary findings across different methodologies. Specifically, the HR was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.80–1.11; P = 0.45) when using multivariable Cox regression analysis in all patients. When excluding 156 patients with missing covariates and applying multivariable Cox regression, the HR was 0.89 (95% CI: 0.76–1.04; P = 0.14). Additionally, when employing covariate-adjusted propensity scores on the matched dataset, the HR was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.78–1.12; P = 0.47). Furthermore, when covariate-pattern-based propensity score matching (CPSM) was also performed to further control for multiple confounders, the HR was 0.91 (95% CI: 0.76–1.09; P = 0.28). Furthermore, after excluding 29 patients with recurrent SCLM (19 in the TA group and 10 in the HT group) from the matched cohort, the results remained consistent, with an HR for PFS of 0.97 (95% CI: 0.81–1.16; P = 0.73). Secondary analysis

The median OS did not significantly differ between the TA group (7.22 years, 95% CI: 6.43–9.05) and the HT group (8.09 years, 95% CI: 6.68–10.58) among all enrolled patients (HR: 0.89, 95% CI: 0.71–1.11, P = 0.30). In the TA group, 196 patients (24.81%) experienced mortality, compared to 134 patients (24.63%) in the HT group. After PSM, the TA group exhibited a similar HR for OS compared to the HT group (adjusted HR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.63–1.09, P = 0.17). In the matched cohorts, the incidence of all-cause death was 99 patients (22.65%) in both the TA and HT groups (Table 2). The 5-year OS rates were 62.36% (95% CI: 56.24%−69.14%) for the TA group and 67.79% (95% CI: 62.38%−73.66%) for the HT group. The 10-year OS rates were 36.59% (95% CI: 26.02%−51.46%) for the TA group and 43.71% (95% CI: 33.14%−57.66%) for the HT group, respectively (Fig. 3B).

Results of sensitivity analysis for OS were all consistent to main findings, when conducting multivariable Cox regression in all patients (HR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.71–1.20; P = 0.57), excluding 156 patients with missing covariates by multivariable Cox regression (HR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.65–1.05, P = 0.12), performing covariate-adjusted propensity scores were applied to the matched dataset (HR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.61–1.08, P = 0.15), or performing CPSM was additionally performed to further control for multiple confounders (HR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.62–1.11; P = 0.21). After further excluding patients with recurrent SCLM, the HR for OS was 0.81 (95% CI: 0.61–1.08; P = 0.15) (Table 3).

The CSS outcomes were consistent with the OS results. In the matched cohort, the median CSS was 8.10 years (95% CI: 5.89–NA) for the TA group and 9.12 years (95% CI: 8.10–NA) for the HT group (HR 0.77, 95% CI: 0.57–1.04; P = 0.08). In the entire cohort, the comparative result was similar (HR 0.85, 95% CI: 0.67–1.07; P = 0.17) (Table S3).

Regarding complications, 9 patients (2.06%) receiving TA treatment experienced significantly lower Clavien-Dindo grade III–IV complications compared to 22 patients (5.03%) in the HT group (P = 0.017). Meanwhile, TA group exhibited a shorter length of hospital stay (median 3 days vs. 10 days) and lower total costs (median $4,820 vs. $10,239) compared to HT group (all P < 0.001) (Table 4).

Impact of anatomical factors

To formally evaluate the potential influence of tumor anatomy, an additional sensitivity analysis was performed. After adjusting for tumor location (peripheral vs. central/perihilar) and adjacency to major vessels in a multivariable Cox model, the comparative effectiveness of TA versus HT remained consistent with the primary findings. In the entire cohort, the adjusted HR for PFS was 1.07 (95% CI: 0.79–1.09; P = 0.36) and for OS was 0.86 (95% CI: 0.66–1.11; P = 0.24). Similarly, in the matched cohort, the adjusted HR for PFS was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.78–1.13; P = 0.51) and for OS was 0.80 (95% CI: 0.60–1.07; P = 0.14). These results confirm that the observed outcomes are robust to adjustment for key anatomical variables (Table S4).

Recurrence patterns and post-progression management

To further delineate the nature of treatment failure and subsequent clinical management, we analyzed the patterns of first progression and salvage therapies in the PSM cohort (Table S5, Figure S2). The 3-year LPFS rate was significantly lower in the TA group compared to the HT group (87.3% vs. 98.0%; HR 0.15, 95% CI 0.07–0.34, P < 0.001). In contrast, the 3-year rates for IMFS (56.9% vs. 53.0%; HR 1.12, 95% CI 0.91–1.39, P = 0.26) and ERFS (74.0% vs. 73.6%; HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.77–1.35, P = 0.90) were comparable between the two groups. Following disease progression, the majority of patients in both groups received active salvage treatment (Table S6). For patients experiencing isolated local progression, repeat local therapy (ablation or resection) was the predominant approach in both the TA (11/11) and HT (1/1) groups. For those with new intrahepatic or extrahepatic recurrences, a multimodal strategy encompassing repeat local ablation/resection, systemic therapy, and radiation was commonly employed in both cohorts.

Outcomes by tumor size subgroup

To evaluate TA for SCLM > 3 cm, outcomes were stratified by SCLM size within the matched cohort. For SCLM > 3 cm, 3-year PFS did not differ significantly between TA and HT (32.7% vs. 37.7%; P = 0.455), but OS diverged (72.6% vs. 83.1%; P = 0.039). In contrast, for SCLM ≤ 3 cm, both 3-year PFS and OS were comparable between treatments (P = 0.662 and P = 0.846, respectively). Thus, while TA yields outcomes equivalent to HT for SCLM ≤ 3 cm, HT may confer an OS advantage for selected SCLM > 3 cm (Table S7).

Discussion

This nationwide multicenter study offers over 10 years of long-term results for 1,334 patients with SCLM, comparing thermal ablation (TA) with hepatectomy (HT). Previous studies have indicated that hepatectomy may provide better outcomes than RFA in terms of recurrence and survival [11, 17]. However, the advent of laparoscopic RFA has made it a potentially viable therapy for patients who are not candidates for hepatectomy [18]. Previous trials (COLLISION, MAVERRIC) [2, 3], established TA as non-inferior to HT for SCLM < 3 cm, but evidence for 3–5 cm tumors was limited, and no studies had demonstrated TA's superiority. Addressing this, the current analysis is the first to provide real-world long-term outcomes for SCLM ≤ 5 cm. Our results, including a 5-year PFS of 30.05% that aligns with hepatectomy study ranges [10, 19–21], confirm that TA can achieve oncological outcomes comparable to HT in this broader patient population.

In this study, the 5-year OS rates for TA and HT in treating SCLM were 62.36% and 67.79%, respectively. These rates are comparable to those reported in other hepatectomy-related studies (43.1% to 65.7%) [10, 19, 21, 22]. The prospective multicenter MAVERRIC trial, as reported by Tinguely et al. [3], demonstrated that microwave ablation (MWA) is a valid curative-intent alternative to surgical resection for small liver metastases, with 5-year OS rates of 56% in the MWA group compared to 58% in the resection group. Furthermore, the analysis of CSS yielded results consistent with OS, indicating no significant difference in disease-related mortality between the two treatment modalities.

Our analysis of recurrence patterns showed higher local progression after TA than HT (3-year LPFS 87.3% vs. 98.0%), consistent with the better local control of resection. However, this did not compromise overall survival. The discrepancy may be explained by three factors: local recurrences after TA are often detected early and remain amenable to repeat local ablation or resection; TA preserves more functional liver parenchyma, allowing more options for managing subsequent intrahepatic recurrences; and the rates of new intrahepatic and extrahepatic recurrences, events that more directly affect survival, were similar between groups. Thus, while HT offers superior local control, TA's liver-sparing approach enables effective salvage of recurrences, balancing out its higher local progression rate and leading to comparable long-term survival.

One significant advantage of initial local ablation treatment is the reduced liver damage it causes. This approach preserves more functional liver volume, thereby broadening the options for subsequent liver-directed treatments compared to hepatectomy. Patients who initially undergo hepatectomy may be left with insufficient liver volume for subsequent procedures [23]. Thus, MWA, being minimally invasive, achieves OS rates comparable to those of surgical resection. Notably, this study is the first to report the 10-year OS rates for SCLM treated with TA and HT, which were 36.59% and 43.71%, respectively. These rates are close to those reported for HT by Acciuffi et al. [19].

Several retrospective studies have shown that patients with SCLM treated with parenchyma-preserving hepatectomy may achieve a survival rate as high as 43.0% [21]. Two recent meta-analyses have highlighted the superiority of surgical resection in terms of reduced local recurrence compared to thermal ablation [24, 25]. This might be because local ablation preserves the entire liver parenchyma, potentially leaving more micro-metastases undetected. Moreover, our results are strengthened by the use of multivariable analysis and CPSM. These methods helped control confounding factors and reduce selection bias associated with treatment assignment.

Overall, TA was found to be minimally invasive, safe, and well-tolerated compared with HT. The percutaneous approach for TA contributes to its favorable safety profile by avoiding surgical incisions, reducing post-procedural pain, and facilitating faster recovery. The present study found the rates of ablation-related complications were 2.06% and 5.03% for TA and HT, respectively. These results are in line with reported ablative complication rates ranging from 3.7% to 11.21% [26, 27]. The ablation-related complications of TA are lower than the postoperative complication rates following HT’s reported, which range from 15% to 22.8% [28]. The median hospital stay for TA (3 days) reflects China's extended post-procedure monitoring protocols, differing from Western practices [2]. Cost savings (52.9% reduction vs. HT) persisted despite longer stays, driven by lower procedural expenses. It’s important to note that the absolute cost figures and the magnitude of savings reported here are derived from the Chinese healthcare billing context. However, the underlying drivers of cost reduction for TA, such as shorter or no operating room time, less expensive equipment and disposables compared to major surgery, lower costs associated with complication management, are likely to translate into economic advantages in most healthcare systems. Therefore, while the exact monetary values may vary across settings, the principle of TA being a cost-effective alternative to HT is expected to hold broad relevance.

Our subgroup analysis underscores tumor size as a pivotal factor in therapeutic decision-making. For SCLM ≤ 3 cm, TA achieved comparable 3-year PFS and OS to HT, supporting its role as a first-line, minimally invasive option. In contrast, for SCLM > 3 cm, while 3-year PFS was similar, a significant OS advantage was observed with HT (72.6% vs. 83.1%; P = 0.039). This suggests that HT may be preferred for larger lesions when feasible, whereas TA remains a valuable alternative when prioritizing minimal invasiveness or if surgery is contraindicated.

The choice between TA and HT should be individualized and guided by MDT. Both modalities can be delivered via minimally invasive approaches (e.g., laparoscopic or image‑guided ablation versus laparoscopic or robotic resection), and the optimal strategy depends not only on patient comorbidities, tumor size, location, and biological behavior, but also on local expertise and available technological resources. Institutions with high volume experience in interventional oncology may achieve excellent outcomes with TA, whereas centers with advanced hepatobiliary surgical teams may favor HT, particularly for tumors > 3 cm or in complex anatomical locations. Therefore, the treatment decision should integrate patient preferences, tumor specific factors, and the institutional proficiency in both surgical and ablation techniques to ensure the best possible oncologic and safety outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, residual confounding bias may still exist in this observational study. Although we used a modified new-user design to mirror real-world clinical practice and support decision-making, we also applied various bias-reduction methods, including propensity score matching and extensive sensitivity analyses. Second, the assessment of treatment margins differed between the two interventions: TA was evaluated using radiology-based minimum ablation margin (MAM ≥ 5 mm), while HT was assessed via pathology-based surgical margin (SM ≥ 1 mm). Although each criterion represents the established clinical standard for its respective modality, this methodological variation limits direct comparability of margin metrics and reflects the inherent differences between image-guided ablation and surgical resection. Third, despite efforts to minimize missing data due to the data source's retrospective nature, the study's findings, derived from a nationwide population across 21 Chinese centers, have been validated in diverse patient cohorts to enhance generalizability. Fourth, the subgroup analyses were not prespecified, so we advise against over—interpreting their results. Fifth, the study began in October 2010, before the 2015 NCCN guidelines that highlighted the importance of RAS (KRAS and NRAS) testing for guiding systemic therapy decisions. Insufficient RAS/BRAF data (40% missing pre-2015) and heterogeneous chemotherapy regimens (Table S2) prevent meaningful biomarker-based subgroup comparisons and limit interpretability, potentially leading to biased outcomes. Sixth, while the target trial emulation approach aims to estimate causal effects and strengthen observational study analysis, this study's non-experimental design means its findings' causality should be interpreted cautiously. Seventh, the findings of this study are specific to the Chinese clinical context, which differs from Western settings in patient profiles, surgical care, and economics, thus, global extrapolation requires caution. Nevertheless, building on evidence from the COLLISION [2] and MAVERRIC [3] trials, our population-based findings offer crucial real-world validation of thermal ablation's therapeutic profile.

Conclusions

This nationwide, multicenter study provides valuable real-world evidence on the long-term outcomes of TA versus HT for SCLM. Our findings support TA as a first-line therapeutic alternative for carefully selected patients with solitary colorectal liver metastases ≤ 5 cm, particularly for lesions ≤ 3 cm where it offers comparable oncologic outcomes with superior safety and cost-effectiveness.
