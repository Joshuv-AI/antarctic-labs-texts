Impact of hepatocellular carcinoma on the risk of liver decompensation: A comparative analysis of patients with and without HCC
Authors: Andrea Martini, Massimo Iavarone, Roberta D’Ambrosio, Anna Barone, Pietro Guerra, Elisabetta Degasperi, Giulia Tosetti, Eleonora Alimenti, Silvia Cagnin, Antonio Bonato, Enrico Libralesso, Simone Incicco, Nicola Zeni, Roberta Gagliardi, Valeria Calvino, Marta Tonon, Carmine Gambino, Patrizia Pontisso, Salvatore Piano, Pietro Lampertico, Paolo Angeli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsHepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of death and the large majority of HCC occurs in the setting of cirrhosis. Nevertheless, its impact on the development of decompensation in these patients has not been investigated, yet. Our aim was to investigate the role of HCC in the development of decompensating events in patients with cirrhosis.MethodsClinical data of outpatients with cirrhosis from two Italian tertiary centers (Padua and Milan) were collected and followed prospectively from January 2000 to December 2021, until the end of the study, death, or liver transplantation. Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were collected. The primary outcome was the development of decompensating events after the diagnosis of HCC. HCC and effective etiological treatment were considered as a time-varying covariate for the statistical analysis.ResultsOverall, 1,176 patients with cirrhosis of any etiology were enrolled (Padua 876, Milan 300), and 358 (30.4%) developed HCC. In the study cohort (Padua cohort), patients who developed HCC on compensated cirrhosis had a higher risk of developing the first decompensation event (hazard ratio [HR] = 4.05; p <0.001), primarily occurring as ascites (HR = 4.79; p <0.001), hepatic encephalopathy (HR = 3.68; p <0.001), and gastrointestinal bleeding (HR = 2.98; p = 0.004). All these findings were confirmed in the extended cohort (Milan cohort). HCC remained an independent predictor of first decompensation even considering the study period in two eras (2000–2013 and 2014–2021) (HR 3.64, 95% CI 2.10–6.31, 2000–2013; HR 4.95, 95% CI 1.62–15.1, 2014–2021).ConclusionsThe occurrence of HCC is associated with a high risk of first decompensation. Further prospective studies are needed to confirm these results.Impact and implicationsHCC frequently arises in patients with cirrhosis and is associated with poor clinical outcomes, particularly in the presence of decompensating events. Although cirrhosis progresses from a compensated to a decompensated stage characterized by severe complications, the contribution of HCC to this transition remains inadequately understood and underinvestigated. This multicenter study demonstrates that the occurrence of HCC in compensated patients with cirrhosis significantly increases the risk of first decompensation. These results highlight the importance for hepatologists and researchers to include HCC in algorithms aiming to stratify the risk of decompensation in patients with HCC and cirrhosis, thereby enhancing patient management strategies.

Introduction

In patients with compensated cirrhosis, the occurrence of complications, such as ascites, hepatic encephalopathy (HE), and gastrointestinal bleeding (GIB) mark the transition from the compensated to decompensated stage of cirrhosis.1,2 The new onset of a decompensating event is the most common outcome in patients with compensated cirrhosis, occurring at a rate of 5–7% per year.1 First decompensation is associated with a substantial worsening of patients prognosis and is therefore considered among the most important predictors of liver-related death.3 The progression of liver disease to decompensated stages may be accelerated by the development of further decompensation episodes. Further decompensation has recently been defined by the Baveno VII Consensus as the following: (a) any second decompensating event, (b) any recurrent decompensating event, and (c) some specific complications such as spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) and hepatorenal syndrome-acute kidney injury.3 Further decompensation, in each of its definitions, is associated with a poorer survival as compared with first decompensation.3 The main driver of first decompensation is the development of clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH), whereas systemic inflammation is thought to play a pivotal role in the development of further decompensation.4 The pathophysiological interaction between hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and decompensation is complex, largely unknown and, probably, bi-directional. In fact, HCC, which accounts for >80% of liver cancers and represents the sixth most common cancer and the fourth most frequent cause of cancer-related death globally, occurs mostly in the setting of advanced chronic liver disease.5 It has been observed that the presence of CSPH, defined by a hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) ≥10 mmHg, is able to predict the occurrence of HCC. More in detail, patients with CSPH have a sixfold increase in HCC incidence, suggesting a potential role of CSPH not only in the pathophysiology of decompensation, but also of HCC.1,2 This hypothesis led some authors to consider HCC as a decompensating event.6 However, the development of HCC, in turn, can potentiate both drivers of decompensation. HCC can increase portal hypertension through several pathophysiological pathways including: (a) arteriovenous shunts opening within the tumor, (b) development of angiogenesis, and/or (c) vascular invasion of the portal vein and/or its branches.7 In addition, it is well known that systemic inflammation is a hallmark of cancer, playing a pivotal role in HCC development and progression.8 These observations support the hypothesis of a potential direct role of HCC in the pathophysiology of decompensation. Although, there is an universal agreement on the detrimental impact of the development of HCC in patients with cirrhosis,9,10 it remains unclear the role of HCC as direct trigger of decompensation. Of course, the occurrence of decompensation in patients with cirrhosis and HCC can prevent the application of an effective treatment strategy for the tumor10 favoring its growth and dissemination.11 Finally, it has been observed that the treatment of HCC can induce per se decompensation.12 Thus, the aim of our study was to compare the impact of HCC on the development of the first and further decompensation in patients with cirrhosis of any etiology.

Patients and methods

Study design

In this retrospective study based on prospectively collected data, we considered all outpatients with cirrhosis with or without HCC who attended two tertiary centers in Italy, that is, the Care Management Program,13 from the University Hospital of Padua (Training Cohort), and the Outpatients’ Clinic at Foundation IRCCS Ca’ Granda Ospedale Maggiore Policlinico in Milan (extended cohort). In the Padua group (n = 876), the authors collected data of all patients attending the Care Management Program from January 2000 to December 2021, whereas in the Milan group (n = 300) data were collected from January 2003 to December 2021.

Inclusion criteria were the following: (a) age >18 years; (b) cirrhosis of any etiology diagnosed through histology or by the evidence of clinical, biohumoral, or instrumental data (endoscopic, ultrasound [US], or liver stiffness measurement assessed through transient elastography); (c) incident HCC diagnosed according to international guidelines or the recommendations of the Italian consensus on HCC available at the time of HCC diagnosis;14,15 (d) availability of complete clinical, biochemical, and radiological data at the time of inclusion and at the time of HCC diagnosis; and (e) the ability to provide written informed consent.

Exclusion criteria were: (a) presence of extrahepatic malignancies and other highly disabling pathologies of extrahepatic origin at the time of inclusion (e.g. heart failure [New York Heart Association class ≥3] or GOLD chronic obstructive pulmonary disease grade ≥3); (b) recurrence of cirrhosis or HCC after liver transplantation (LT); and (c) refusal or inability of the patient to provide informed consent. Patients’ flowchart of the two study cohorts is available in Fig. S1.

The protocol was approved by the local Ethics Committees (218n/AO/2022). Data were collected prospectively, and all patients gave informed consent according to local regulations. All patients were followed until the end of the observation period (December 2021), death, or LT.

In the training cohort (Padua cohort), a subgroup of patients with cirrhosis developed HCC during follow-up: demographic and clinical data were collected at inclusion for patients without HCC, and at diagnosis of liver tumor for patients with HCC (patient disposition of Padua cohort is available in Fig. S2). In the extended cohort (Milan cohort), all patients with HCC had liver cancer at inclusion.

For patients with cirrhosis without HCC at baseline an abdomen-US was performed at the time of inclusion or within the following 2 months. During follow-up patients underwent an abdomen-US every 6 months. All patients had an esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) performed within 6 months before or after the inclusion in the study, and subsequently according to international recommendations.[16], [17], [18] Patients younger than 70 years who had model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) values >15, or an HCC within LT criteria were considered for LT.[19], [20], [21] The date of diagnosis of HCC, the diagnostic modality (i.e. under regular US surveillance, incidental or symptomatic), tumor morphology (i.e. single lesion, multinodular [less than three nodules, three or more nodules], diffuse/infiltrating, and massive HCC) as well as the maximum size of the largest nodule were recorded. The main treatment for HCC was recorded and subdivided into: surgical (liver resection and LT), ablative (radiofrequency ablation, ethanol injection, and other ablative treatments), transarterial (transcatheter arterial chemoembolization [TACE], transarterial embolization, and transarterial radioembolization), systemic (i.e. tyrosine kinase inhibitors), and best supportive care.

Patients with compensated cirrhosis irrespective of HCC status, were defined by the absence of present or past cirrhosis complications, and first and further decompensation were defined according to the Baveno VII criteria.3 The occurrence of decompensating events was recorded for all patients enrolled in the study.[22], [23], [24] Differently from the CANONIC study, a bacterial infection at the time of hospitalization was considered as a precipitating rather than a decompensating event, with the only exception of SBP, which was included among further decompensating events.25 An ‘effective etiological treatment’ was considered the elimination of the etiological cause of the liver disease by a specific treatment (i.e. a sustained virological response after antiviral treatment for patients with HCV, undetectable HBV DNA for patients with HBV during treatment with nucleos(t)ide analogs, achievement of complete abstinence from alcohol) at any time during follow-up. The primary outcome was the development of decompensating events (first decompensating events and further decompensating events) after the diagnosis of HCC.

Statistical analysis

Normally, distributed continuous variables were reported as means and SD and compared with Student’s t test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were reported as median and IQR and compared with Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as count and percentage and compared with Χ2 or Fisher’s exact test, when appropriate.

Univariate and multivariate analyses assessing the risk of decompensation were performed using a cause-specific Cox model. Transplant patients were censored at the time of transplantation. Effective etiological treatment and HCC were included as time-dependent covariates. All parameters that were judged as clinically relevant by the clinicians together with the results of the univariable analysis were included in a multivariable model. Albumin has been removed from the multivariate model because of the collinearity with other covariates. The hazard ratios (HRs) and their 95% CIs were calculated. To evaluate the risk of first decompensation in patients with or without HCC, an era-stratified analysis (before vs. after 2014) was performed in the Padua training cohort. This stratification reflects the introduction of direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) for HCV-related cirrhosis, which has profoundly changed the prognosis of these patients. Decompensation risk curves were developed through Simon–Makuch plots and compared with the Mantel-Byar test to account for time-dependent variables.

To assess the effect of different types of HCC treatment and complete response on the development of decompensation, a further analysis was performed on patients with HCC only. Time to event was calculated from the time of HCC diagnosis. Cumulative incidence functions of decompensation were developed through competing risk analysis and compared with Gray’s test. LT and death were considered as competing events for decompensation. Univariable analyses were performed using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard model. The subdistribution hazard ratios (sHRs) and their 95% CIs were calculated. The analysis was performed with and R 4.3.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, 2021).

Results

In the Padua cohort, 292 (35%) patients died and 169 (20%) patients underwent LT. In the Milan cohort (n = 300), 70 (23%) patients died, and 15 (5%) were transplanted during follow-up. Rates and different types of decompensating events are shown in Table S1. Of note, ascites was the most frequent first decompensation event for both groups of patients (patients with or without HCC) in the Padua and Milan cohorts.

Characteristics of HCC at the diagnosis in patients with compensated cirrhosis in both the Padua and Milan cohorts are reported in Table S2. Patients with HCC in the Milan cohort were significantly older (67.8 vs. 62.7 years; p = 0.002) and had better-preserved liver function compared with those in the Padua cohort (ALBI [albumin–bilirubin] grade 1 60% vs. 29%; p <0.001). In addition, 80% of patients with HCC in the Milan cohort were diagnosed at BCLC stage 0 or A, compared with 70% in the Padua cohort. Regarding the type of first HCC treatment, the two cohorts were comparable in terms of the proportion of patients receiving surgical or locoregional therapies. In both cohorts, most HCC cases—regardless of prior history of decompensation—were detected during surveillance (79% in the Padua cohort and 63% in the Milan cohort). Of the remaining cases (15% in the Padua cohort vs. 36% in the Milan cohort) were diagnosed incidentally, whereas only 6% and 1% of patients, respectively, were diagnosed based on symptoms.

Overall, patients with HCC had significantly lower overall survival than those without HCC, regardless of prior decompensation status, in both study cohorts (p <0.001; Fig. S3A–D).

Risk of decompensation in patients with HCC in the extended cohort (Milan cohort)

In the Milan cohort, liver decompensating events were prospectively assessed during follow-up in 211 patients with compensated cirrhosis (63 patients with HCC and 148 patients without HCC) with a median follow-up time of 76 months (IQR 41–157). The clinical characteristics of these patients are summarized in Table S5.

Discussion

In recent years, increasing attention has been given to the impact of hepatic decompensation on the prognosis of patients with HCC. However, studies focusing on the specific role of HCC on first and/or further decompensation in patients with cirrhosis remain limited. In our study, HCC occurrence emerged as an independent predictor of first decompensation across different etiologies, increasing the risk by more than threefold. In fact, we showed that compensated patients with cirrhosis and HCC were at higher risk of developing ascites, HE, and GIB also when results were adjusted for several covariables. Among them, we analyzed the achievement of an effective etiological treatment of liver disease, presence of varices, ALBI grade and use of non-selective-beta-blockers (NSBBs).

We observed that patients with HCC more frequently showed portal hypertension, as expressed by higher prevalence of EV and lower albumin values (Table 1). Although study design does not allow a pathophysiological interpretation of our results, it should be highlighted that worse portal hypertension can explain the increased incidence of ascites and, to some extent, of HE. Moreover, further analysis indicated that HCC was associated with a higher risk of refractory ascites, although not confirmed in multivariate analysis.

Although it has been firmly established that predictivity should not be confused with causality, the boundary between these two concepts is extremely thin in our study. Cirrhosis and HCC are extremely connected to each other on a pathogenetic and pathophysiological level. Particularly, cirrhosis and HCC have potentially synergistic effects on portal hypertension and systemic inflammation, which are the two main drivers of decompensation, either first or further. Furthermore, it is well established that HCC treatment may itself precipitate decompensation, and that this risk—closely related to preoperative portal hypertension,26—is higher after hepatectomy than after ablative therapies.12 Conversely, CSPH has a detrimental impact on outcomes in patients with HCC undergoing surgery, ablative treatments, or TACE27,28 as well as in those receiving systemic therapy.[29], [30], [31]

However, we acknowledge that this study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design introduces potential biases and makes it challenging to determine whether hepatic decompensation should be attributed to HCC progression or to treatment. Another limitation stems from the heterogeneity of the two cohorts and the lack of detailed information regarding the sequence of HCC treatments. Nevertheless, our observations highlight relevant key points in the management of patients with cirrhosis and HCC for two reasons. First, the development of ascites32 or GIB33 in patients with HCC is an independent predictor of poor survival. Second, the development of decompensation in patients with cirrhosis and HCC, even at an early stage, may negatively impact on curative treatment options. Consequently, the only viable therapy would be LT.9 Therefore, it should be a priority in the management of patients with cirrhosis and HCC to prevent the development of decompensation. The findings of this study suggest that incorporating HCC into risk-stratification algorithms for patients with cirrhosis may improve the assessment of decompensation risk, thus contributing to understand the natural history of decompensation in patients with and without HCC. Although previous studies[31], [32], [33] primarily focused on the impact of decompensation on post-HCC survival, our analysis addresses the complementary question of whether and to what extent the presence of HCC itself increases the burden of decompensation. The study compared patients with compensated cirrhosis with or without HCC, offering new insight into the role of HCC as a driver of initial and further decompensation.

In conclusion, among patients with compensated cirrhosis, the onset of HCC significantly increases the risk of disease decompensation, thereby reducing the feasibility of curative treatments and leading to poorer prognoses. Although our results need to be confirmed in larger prospective studies, they try to improve our understanding of the natural history of decompensation in patients with HCC, thus highlighting the need of algorithms optimizing their management.
