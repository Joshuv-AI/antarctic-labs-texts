Extensive coronary thrombosis following cannabis use in a young adult: a case report

Introduction

Acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) in young adults is uncommon and typically prompts evaluation for nontraditional or substance-related risk factors. Cannabis, one of the most widely used psychoactive substances worldwide, has been increasingly recognized as a potential contributor to cardiovascular complications, including arrhythmias, myocardial infarction, vasospasm, and sudden cardiac death, even in patients without traditional cardiovascular risk factors [1–3]. Recent reports have also described rhythm disturbances associated with cannabinoid use, including ventricular bigeminy in the setting of cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome [4].

The principal psychoactive component of cannabis, Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), affects the cardiovascular system via multiple mechanisms. It induces sympathetic stimulation, leading to tachycardia and transient hypertension, which increases myocardial oxygen demand. THC can also produce coronary vasospasm through endothelial dysfunction and vascular smooth muscle hyperreactivity. Furthermore, THC modulates platelet function by activating cannabinoid receptors (CB₁ and CB₂), increasing thromboxane A₂ and P-selectin expression, and promoting a prothrombotic state [5–7]. Chronic use may amplify these effects, predisposing to thrombotic coronary events even in the absence of significant atherosclerosis.

While most reports describe STEMI with angiographically normal coronary arteries in cannabis users [8, 9], large-vessel thrombosis involving the left main coronary artery (LMCA) and left anterior descending artery (LAD) remains exceedingly rare. Here, we report a case of extensive LMCA and LAD thrombosis in a chronic cannabis user, successfully managed with intracoronary and intravenous fibrinolysis, and discuss the mechanistic implications of THC-mediated platelet activation and coronary vasospasm.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old male, a regular cannabis user in the form of smoking cannabis, presented to our hospital with a history of sudden onset of severe chest pain for thirteen hours. On presentation, he was having tachycardia and stable blood pressure. Electrocardiography (ECG) showed ST elevations in V3-V6, II, III, and aVF. Figure 1 Echocardiography suggested anterior wall hypokinesia with an ejection fraction of 45% (Fig. 2). Among blood investigations, his complete blood count showed mild leucocytosis with neutrophilia and liver function was mildly deranged. He was loaded with double antiplatelets, high-intensity statin, and anticoagulated. In view of persistent chest pain, he was immediately taken to the cath lab, and an emergent coronary angiogram revealed left main coronary artery thrombus grade III (thrombolysis in myocardial infarction grade), which extended to the proximal left anterior descending artery and a grade V thrombus in mid LAD, causing complete occlusion. Figure 3 In view of the high thrombus burden, he was given 3,00,000 units of intracoronary streptokinase, followed by 12,00,000 units of streptokinase over one hour through a peripheral intravenous line. A review coronary angiogram after one hour revealed a thrombus resolution with the resumption of TIMI II flow. Figure 4 He was anticoagulated for another 3 days. The patient was discharged on dual antiplatelet therapy, high-intensity statin therapy, and guideline directed medical therapy, with advice regarding strict abstinence from cannabis use. He was enrolled in a cardiac rehabilitation program and planned for close outpatient cardiology follow-up.

Discussion

Cannabis use has emerged as a potentially underrecognized risk factor for acute myocardial infarction, particularly in young adults without conventional cardiovascular risk factors [1, 2]. Multiple mechanisms have been proposed to explain this association. Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) may induce sympathomimetic activation with increased catecholamine release, resulting in tachycardia, hypertension, and elevated myocardial oxygen demand [3]. In addition, endothelial dysfunction and vascular smooth muscle hyperreactivity may contribute to coronary vasospasm and transient myocardial ischemia [3, 5]. Experimental studies have also suggested that THC may promote platelet activation through cannabinoid receptor (CB₁ and CB₂) mediated increases in thromboxane A₂ and P-selectin expression, thereby favoring thrombus formation [6, 7]. Chronic cannabis exposure has further been associated with inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial injury, which may contribute to a prothrombotic milieu [6, 7]. In this context, our patient’s extensive thrombus burden involving the left main coronary artery (LMCA) and left anterior descending artery (LAD) is notable, particularly in the apparent absence of significant atherosclerotic disease. Previous reports have similarly described ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) in young cannabis users with angiographically non-obstructive or minimally diseased coronary arteries, suggesting a possible association between cannabis exposure and coronary thrombosis [8, 9].

Management considerations

High thrombus burden (TIMI grade ≥ 4) is associated with distal embolization, no-reflow, and adverse remodelling [10]. Standard PCI with stenting can be hazardous in this setting. Intracoronary fibrinolysis (ICF) has re-emerged as a rescue therapy, demonstrating improved microvascular perfusion and reduced infarct size in selected patients [11]. In our patient, the thrombus burden was extensive, involving the left main coronary artery and proximal LAD with complete mid LAD occlusion. Given the high risk of distal embolization and no reflow with aggressive instrumentation, an initial pharmacologic strategy was preferred over immediate stenting or mechanical thrombectomy. Intracoronary streptokinase was administered locally to reduce thrombus burden and improve distal perfusion before systemic fibrinolysis. Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors were not administered because satisfactory angiographic improvement was achieved following fibrinolytic therapy. Intracoronary vasodilators were not required as angiographic findings predominantly suggested thrombotic occlusion rather than dynamic vasospasm. In our case, ICF, combined with systemic fibrinolysis, achieved effective thrombus resolution, thereby avoiding complications from mechanical thrombectomy or emergent stenting.

Limitation

This report has several limitations. Toxicological confirmation of cannabis exposure, including serum or urine THC testing, was not available. Additionally, other prothrombotic conditions or concomitant substance exposure could not be completely excluded. Although the temporal association is notable, causality cannot be established from a single case report. Further observational and mechanistic studies are required to better define the relationship between cannabis use and coronary thrombosis.

Conclusion

Cannabis can precipitate severe coronary thrombosis in young adults through a combination of sympathomimetic activation, endothelial dysfunction, and platelet activation. In cases of high thrombus burden where immediate PCI is unsafe, intracoronary fibrinolysis may serve as an effective alternative. Recognition of cannabis-related cardiovascular risks, mechanistic understanding, and timely intervention are critical for optimizing patient outcomes. This case adds to the limited literature on large vessel thrombosis associated with chronic THC use and underscores the need for increased awareness among clinicians.
