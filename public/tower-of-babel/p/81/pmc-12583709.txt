Clinical needs assessment for non-invasive intraoperative bladder volume monitoring devices: a cross-sectional study based on the Kano model
Authors: Yanhua Guo, Wenzhi Cai, Ling Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntraoperative urine output is a key indicator of fluid balance and renal function. With the rise of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocols, there is a growing demand for non-invasive bladder monitoring alternatives to traditional catheterization, which poses risks such as infection and urethral injury. This study aimed to identify user-centered requirements for a non-invasive intraoperative bladder volume monitoring device using the Kano model. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 388 surgical professionals, including anesthesiologists, surgeons, and nurses from tertiary hospitals. A Kano-based questionnaire evaluated 24 features related to device function, operation, and design. Image-assisted prototypes were used to enhance clarity. Features were classified using the Kano model and analyzed with Better-Worse coefficients. Seven features, including measurement accuracy, safety, and real-time monitoring, were identified as must-be attributes. Eight one-dimensional attributes, such as urine volume alarms and ergonomic design, positively influenced user satisfaction. Attractive features like trend analysis and affordable pricing showed high satisfaction potential. Role-specific preferences were observed across professional groups. This is the first Kano-based study assessing intraoperative non-invasive urine monitoring needs. Findings offer actionable insights for developing ERAS-compatible, user-centered monitoring technologies tailored to multidisciplinary workflows.Keywords: Intraoperative monitoring, Non-invasive bladder volume, Ultrasound, Catheter-free management, Kano model, User-centered designSubject terms: Health care, Medical research, Urology

Introduction

During surgery, bladder urine volume serves as an important indicator for assessing fluid balance and renal function1. Although traditional intraoperative indwelling catheterization provides accurate urine output data, it is associated with complications such as urinary tract infections and urethral injury, which not only increase patient discomfort but also escalate healthcare costs2. With the widespread promotion of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols and the implementation of catheter-free wards, minimizing unnecessary medical interventions has become a prevailing trend3. Advances in surgical and anesthetic techniques have further shortened operative times and reduced intraoperative blood loss, thereby lowering the requirements for intraoperative fluid management4,5. Consequently, strategies for early catheter removal and catheter-free management during the postoperative period have gained increasing attention and implementation6,7. However, in the absence of an indwelling catheter, how to accurately and continuously assess intraoperative bladder volume remains a major concern among surgical and perioperative healthcare providers, as this directly affects the safety and feasibility of catheter-free management.

In current clinical research and applications, the primary non-invasive monitoring technologies include ultrasonography, bioimpedance analysis (BIA), and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS). Ultrasonography is currently the most widely adopted method, in which bladder volume is estimated using two- or three-dimensional imaging, through formulas such as the empirical equation V = 0.7 × L × H × W or the VOCAL technique8,9. BIA measures changes in tissue conductivity before and after bladder filling, making it suitable for continuous monitoring10,11. NIRS, on the other hand, uses specific wavelengths (e.g., 950 nm) to assess the absorption properties of bladder fluid, with advantages of portability and wearability12. Each technique has its strengths: ultrasonography offers relatively high accuracy, but conventional rigid transducers are bulky, inflexible, and poorly suited to complex skin surfaces or integration into wearable devices. Recently, wearable ultrasound diagnostics have become a research hotspot, with flexible ultrasound emerging as a promising alternative. Compared with traditional rigid transducers, flexible ultrasound devices are thinner, lighter, and more comfortable to wear, while enabling prolonged monitoring or continuous therapeutic use without reliance on professional operators. Their superior conformability allows closer skin contact, expanding the acoustic field and improving monitoring performance13.

At the same time, user-centered device design is critical to ensure clinical feasibility and adoption. The Kano model, proposed by Noriaki Kano in 1984, provides a robust framework for analyzing user needs and guiding product development14. It classifies user requirements into must-be, one-dimensional, attractive, indifferent, reverse, and questionable attributes, thereby clarifying how each feature influences user satisfaction. Although originally applied in industries such as banking and hospitality, the Kano model has been increasingly utilized in healthcare since Zhang et al.15 pioneered its application in radiological service evaluation. It has since proven valuable in nursing, pharmaceutical logistics, and medical device design16,17. The model not only categorizes user requirements but also employs Better-Worse coefficient analysis and importance–satisfaction mapping, enabling prioritization of features into enhancement, improvement, maintenance, or low-priority zones18. For medical devices, this approach provides a scientific and user-oriented method for aligning innovations with actual clinical needs19.

Against this background, the present study introduces a wearable noninvasive urine volume monitoring device based on flexible ultrasound, and applies the Kano model to systematically assess the needs of intraoperative users, including surgeons, anesthesiologists, operating room nurses, and anesthesia nurses. By identifying critical product attributes and their respective impacts on user satisfaction, this study aims to provide empirical evidence to guide the optimization of noninvasive bladder volume ultrasound monitoring devices, thereby advancing safe and feasible catheter-free intraoperative management.

Methods

Study design and ethics

A cross-sectional study was conducted from November 1 to 31, 2024 by using a self-designed questionnaire. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study have been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Questionnaire design

Based on the Kano model, we developed a questionnaire to assess users’ specific requirements for non-invasive urine volume ultrasound monitoring devices. The questionnaire was collaboratively designed by the research team, drawing on preliminary research findings and an extensive literature review. It consisted of two sections: the first section was a self-designed sociodemographic questionnaire covering gender, age, user role, and years of experience; the second section focused on collecting user requirements for device features. To facilitate classification, items were grouped by category: functional-level attributes were labeled with the prefix “A,” operational-level attributes with “B,” and feature-level attributes with “C.” (see Table 1).

Prototype interactive demonstration

Prior to actual device development, a paper-based prototype interaction demonstration was designed to facilitate the Kano model survey, with the aim of better understanding user needs and optimizing device functionality. As a physical prototype was not yet available, we used image-based prototypes (see Figs. 1 and 2) to simulate the device’s appearance and functionality. These visual materials illustrated key aspects such as the overall design, wearable configuration, position of the ultrasound probe, display screen layout, and user interface. By introducing the background, design rationale, and intended clinical scenarios through visual aids, we ensured that participants had a clear and intuitive understanding of the proposed device during the survey process.

Quality control

To ensure the validity of the questionnaire, domain experts were consulted after the initial draft was developed. The three-round Delphi expert consultation involved a total of 22 experts, aged between 34 and 62 years, with an average age of 47.4 years, predominantly middle-aged and young experts with extensive clinical or research experience. There were 11 male Homo sapiens and 11 female Homo sapiens, with a balanced gender ratio. The shortest working experience was 15 years, the longest was 38 years, and the average working experience was 27 years, indicating a highly experienced expert group with strong professional judgment. In terms of education, there were 3 Homo sapiens with bachelor’s degrees, 7 Homo sapiens with master’s degrees, and 12 Homo sapiens with doctoral degrees or higher, with master’s degrees or higher accounting for 86.3%, reflecting a high overall educational level. Regarding professional backgrounds, there were 7 Homo sapiens in clinical medicine, 4 Homo sapiens in clinical nursing, 8 Homo sapiens in nursing management/education, and 2 Homo sapiens in biomedical engineering. In terms of professional titles, there were 6 Homo sapiens with associate senior titles and 14 Homo sapiens with full senior titles, with associate senior titles or higher accounting for 100%, demonstrating extremely high expert authority. Among them, 15 Homo sapiens served as graduate advisors (including master’s and doctoral advisors), with over half of the experts having research supervision experience.

Based on their feedback, revisions were made to remove items with low content validity. The overall Scale-level Content Validity Index using Universal Agreement (S-CVI/UA) reached 0.93. A pilot test was subsequently conducted in November 2024, and adjustments were made based on the preliminary findings. Additionally, all investigators responsible for questionnaire distribution received training to ensure a thorough understanding of the study methodology and ethical considerations. During data collection, principles of voluntary participation and confidentiality were strictly followed, and the purpose and importance of the study were clearly explained to each participant by the investigators.

Participants

This study employed a convenience sampling method to recruit healthcare professionals involved in intraoperative catheter-free management from several tertiary hospitals. Participants included surgeons, anesthesiologists, operating room nurses, and anesthesia nurses. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) currently employed at a tertiary or higher-level general hospital; (2) holding an associate degree or above; (3) possessing valid medical or nursing practice qualifications; and (4) willing to voluntarily participate in the study. The exclusion criteria included: (1) participation in other studies within the past six months that could interfere with the results; and (2) physical or health conditions that would prevent safe participation in the research.

Sample size calculation

According to existing literature20, the sample size for a cross-sectional study should be 5 to 10 times the number of independent variables. In this study, the number of independent variables was set at 24. Considering a potential non-response rate of 20%, the estimated sample size ranged from 150 to 300 participants [24 × 5]/(1 − 0.2)[24 × 5]/(1 − 0.2) to [24 × 10]/(1 − 0.2)[24 × 10]/(1 − 0.2). Considering practical considerations, a total of 400 participants were ultimately enrolled in the study.

Data collection

We used the “Wenjuanxing”, a main online platform in China used for surveys, to generate a web-based link for questionnaire distribution. The research team first visited relevant departments within the target hospitals and communicated with head nurses to understand the implementation status of catheter-free management. Eligible participants received the questionnaire link via WeChat. If participants had any questions about the content, they could contact investigators through phone or WeChat. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time. Prior to beginning the survey, a dedicated information page explained the study’s purpose and obtained informed consent. A total of 400 questionnaires were collected. All responses were reviewed by the principal investigator and two trained data collectors to ensure logical consistency and completeness. In total, 388 valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis, yielding a response rate of 97%.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS version 26.0. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants. Based on the Kano model, we conducted a detailed classification of user requirements for the non-invasive urine volume ultrasound monitoring device. To assess the relative importance of different requirements within each attribute category, we referred to the customer satisfaction and dissatisfaction coefficients (Better-Worse coefficients) proposed by Berger et al.21. These coefficients evaluate the impact of adding or removing a feature on user satisfaction or dissatisfaction. According to the Kano model, requirements were classified into four categories: must-be (M), one-dimensional (O), attractive (A), and indifferent (I).

We further quantified and refined the Kano classification results. Specifically, we calculated the Better-Worse coefficient for each quality attribute and ranked the items within each attribute category accordingly. To explore variations in device requirements across different user groups, including surgeons, anesthesiologists, operating room nurses, and anesthesia nurses, we conducted additional statistical analyses. Since the continuous variables for each requirement did not follow a normal distribution and exhibited heterogeneity of variance, we used the median to describe their distribution. The Kruskal–Wallis H test was employed to compare group differences in device requirement preferences among these professional roles.

Evaluation of user requirement attributes based on the Kano model

As shown in Tables 4, 5 and 6, the final Kano classification identified various product attributes. Specifically, 7 attributes were categorized as must-be (M), 8 as one-dimensional (O), 8 as attractive (A), and 6 as indifferent (I). The influence of each attribute on user satisfaction was quantified using the “Better” coefficient, calculated as: “Better” = (A + O) / (A + O + M + I), where A, O, M, and I represent the number of respondents who classified the item as attractive, one-dimensional, must-be, and indifferent, respectively. The “Worse” coefficient, which reflects the degree of dissatisfaction if the feature is absent, was calculated as: “Worse” = − (O + M) / (A + O + M + I). Both the “Better” and “Worse” coefficients range from 0 to 1. A higher “Better” value indicates that the presence of the feature greatly enhances user satisfaction, while a higher (absolute) “Worse” value indicates that the feature is essential to avoid dissatisfaction. Values near zero suggest minimal impact on either satisfaction or dissatisfaction.

Design implementation plan

Product requirement design

The design of this device is fully aligned with the findings of the preliminary research and the Kano model analysis, ensuring that must-be attributes (the essential user requirements) are incorporated. These include accurate measurement results, real-time bladder urine volume monitoring, automatic saving of each measurement, user safety, and non-interference with surgical procedures. One-dimensional attributes identified in the analysis, such as compact size, lightweight form, and secure wearability, are addressed to enhance comfort during surgery. Additionally, the device is designed to issue alerts when urine volume exceeds 500 ml or drops below 17 ml/hour, satisfying key one-dimensional expectations related to clinical decision-making. To address attractive attributes, the device is manufactured in both sterile and non-sterile versions, which accommodates different types of surgical procedures and reduces the risk of surgical site infections. Based on these integrated needs, we developed a flexible ultrasound-based intraoperative non-invasive urine volume monitoring device. Overall system design

The measurement of bladder urine volume currently relies primarily on ultrasound technology (as shown in Fig. 5). This involves transmitting ultrasonic beams across the bladder, receiving the reflected signals, and converting them into electrical signals. These signals are then amplified, filtered, and digitized to identify the bladder wall boundaries, which are subsequently used to calculate the internal urine volume. This approach integrates modern ultrasound and computer technologies to create a high-performance diagnostic system (as shown in Fig. 6).

Schematic diagram of flexible ultrasonic sensor array.

Diagram of the diagnostic system and supporting APP.

Most existing intraoperative non-invasive urine monitoring devices use multiple single-element transducers to form ultrasound beams that scan the bladder. However, these systems typically capture only a few boundary points, resulting in relatively large measurement errors and limited accuracy.

To improve measurement precision, the present design adopts two orthogonally arranged array transducers (as shown in Fig. 7) to transmit and receive ultrasound signals. This setup enables scanning of two perpendicular cross-sections of the bladder—Section A and Section B (Figs. 8 and 9). By capturing boundary data from both cross-sections, a more accurate estimation of bladder urine volume can be achieved.

With advancements in digital beamforming, highly integrated front-end hardware now allows for the amplification, filtering, and analog-to-digital conversion of multichannel ultrasound signals on a single chip. Combined with high-capacity digital processing using FPGA technology, the entire hardware system for bladder urine volume detection can be significantly miniaturized, making wearable application feasible (Fig. 10).

Discussion

This study is the first to systematically apply the Kano model to prioritize design requirements for an intraoperative noninvasive bladder volume monitoring device. The findings revealed a complex set of clinical design imperatives: core functions such as measurement accuracy and real-time monitoring were classified as must-be (M) attributes, while cost-effectiveness and advanced capabilities emerged as attractive (A) attributes. These insights provide empirical support for device design and deepen our understanding of user-centered needs in high-risk surgical environments.

Clinical necessity of must-be (M) attributes

Seven requirements were categorized as M attributes, among which device safety (B2) and non-interference with surgery (B11) exhibited the highest user dissatisfaction coefficients (Worse < -0.54). This aligns with Patel et al.22, who emphasized that safety forms the foundational expectation in equipment research and development. While indwelling catheters provide accurate measurements, their association with urinary tract infections and urethral trauma23 contradicts the ERAS principle of minimizing invasive interventions24. Our proposed device integrates noninvasive wearability (A1) with precise monitoring (A3), thereby directly supporting ERAS protocols. Notably, measurement accuracy (A3) was classified as an M attribute (Worse = -66.9%), contrasting with Waite et al.25, who identified accuracy as a one-dimensional attribute in radiologic equipment studies. This discrepancy stems from the intraoperative context: excessive bladder distension can lead to tissue injury26, whereas low urine output may indicate renal hypoperfusion27. Consequently, users perceive accuracy as a non-negotiable baseline, which highlighting a stark contrast with the “approximate accuracy is acceptable” tolerance often observed in home medical devices.

Clinical decision-making value of one-dimensional (O) attributes

Eight requirements were classified as O attributes. Among them, urine volume alarms (A4/A5) demonstrated the highest user satisfaction coefficients (Better > 0.57), reflecting clinicians’ heightened sensitivity to intraoperative urinary thresholds. Unlike studies focusing on catheter-associated infection prevention28,29, our respondents, which refers to primarily clinicians, prioritized actionable real-time alerts: urine volume > 500 mL necessitates emergency catheterization, while < 17 mL/h may indicate acute kidney injury30. Automated alarms reduce cognitive burden, which also explains the high value assigned to rapid result feedback (B7) as an O attribute (Better = 59.5%).

The classification of ergonomic factors (B3/B5/B8) as O attributes challenges conventional medical device paradigms. Previous studies prioritized functionality over comfort31, yet our data suggest that during prolonged surgeries, features such as lightweight design (B3) and anatomical compatibility (B8) significantly influence adoption willingness (Better > 0.57). This shift reflects the evolution of ERAS from “patient-centered” care to models that also emphasize provider experience32.

Market differentiation potential of attractive (A) attributes

Reasonable pricing (C4) emerged as the strongest A attribute (Better = 94.35%), significantly exceeding conventional expectations in medical device development. Two main factors drove this finding: (1) the current cost of noninvasive bladder scanners exceeds 100,000 RMB, limiting departmental accessibility; and (2) recurring costs from disposable components, such as ultrasound sensors, render ongoing affordability a key consideration. This finding contrasts with Moosivand et al.33, who concluded that cost was a secondary concern in equipment research and development, highlighting the distinct economic dynamics of acute care, where utility must align with cost-effectiveness.

Sterilization options (C3) and dynamic analysis (C6) also possess strategic value. The availability of sterile and non-sterile versions accommodates diverse surgical environments (e.g., operating rooms versus clinical wards), aligning with ERAS-driven tiered care delivery models34. While not essential, dynamic analysis (C6) enables trend-based interventions, such as using downward urine flow slopes to predict shock, supporting precision anesthesia practices35.

Professional heterogeneity and methodological implications

Research indicates that different user groups (surgeons, anesthesiologists, operating room nurses, and anesthesia nurses) exhibit significant differences in demand for wearable non-invasive urine detection devices across four dimensions: single-dimensional attributes, attractiveness attributes, undifferentiated attributes, and reverse attributes. Anesthesiologists prioritize alarm thresholds (A4/A5), while nurses emphasize wearing comfort (B3). This finding validates the effectiveness of the Kano Model in capturing user-specific needs36, while also highlighting the limitations of treating “clinicians” as a homogeneous group.

Methodologically, our approach refined traditional Kano classification by introducing a Better-Worse coefficient matrix (Fig. 3) to quantify requirement sensitivity. For example, while traditional Kano methods ambiguously categorized maintenance convenience (B9) as an O attribute, its frequency distribution was close to I attributes; here, the Worse coefficient ( − 0.511) confirms its moderate impact. Mobile app integration (C2) was categorized as an Indifferent (I) attribute despite a high “Better” score (53.94%), as its absence caused limited dissatisfaction (Worse = − 44.79%).

Strengths, limitations and future directions

The wearable device developed in this study demonstrates significant advantages over conventional ultrasound monitoring, particularly in terms of comfort and conformability, as the flexible sensor array effectively resolves the discomfort caused by rigid probes, and in real-time continuous monitoring, as it enables 24-h intraoperative surveillance, offering clear superiority over intermittent catheterization.The array transducer can more accurately estimate bladder urine volume.

This study has several limitations: (1) sampling was skewed toward tertiary hospitals, with 74.42% of participants having over five years of experience, which may not fully reflect primary care needs; (2) reliance on paper-based prototypes may have limited perception of advanced features such as C6; (3) no weighted ranking was performed within attribute categories. (4)The developed equipment has limited applicability in surgical procedures, being suitable only for non-laparotomy and non-laparoscopic surgeries.

Future studies should expand sampling to secondary hospitals to verify generalizability, develop functional prototypes to conduct A/B testing of high-impact features, and apply Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) to assign weights to design requirements. As AI-enhanced ultrasound technologies advance, attributes like dynamic analysis (C6) may shift from attractive to must-be standards.

Conclusion

This study is the first to systematically apply the Kano model to identify and prioritize user requirements for intraoperative non-invasive bladder volume monitoring devices within the context of catheter-free management. The results indicate that measurement accuracy, device safety, and real-time monitoring are must-be attributes that form the essential foundation of user satisfaction. In contrast, cost-effectiveness and advanced functionalities such as dynamic analysis are attractive features that can significantly enhance user satisfaction and offer considerable market differentiation potential. Attributes including urine volume alarms and ergonomic design exhibit a one-dimensional relationship with satisfaction, meaning their presence directly promotes adoption and supports clinical decision-making.

A deeper analysis of the questionnaire results reveals notable variations in expectations and priorities across different professional groups. Surgeons placed greater emphasis on real-time data integration and accuracy to support surgical decision-making, whereas anesthesiologists prioritized device safety and alarm functionalities to manage patient physiology during procedures. Nurses valued ergonomic design and ease of use highly, which align with workflow efficiency and reduced operational burden. These role-based disparities highlight the critical importance of incorporating segment-specific design considerations to meet diverse user needs effectively.

Overall, these findings provide empirical support for user-centered product development and innovation aligned with ERAS protocols. Future work should focus on validating these requirement priorities across broader clinical settings and integrating emerging technologies—such as AI-enhanced ultrasound—to enable continuous performance improvement and adaptive functionality.
