Prevalence and Incidence of Abdominal Obesity and Cardiometabolic Risk in Early Adolescence
Authors: Augusto César F. De Moraes, Tiago A. Oliveira, John Virostko, Marcus V. Nascimento‐Ferreira, Ethan T. Hunt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAbdominal obesity and related cardiometabolic risk (CMR) factors frequently begin in adolescence and contribute to long‑term health burden, yet longitudinal trajectories in U.S. youth are poorly characterized. This study quantifies the prevalence and 2‑ and 4‑year incidence of emerging CMR, with emphasis on excess adiposity, in a diverse adolescent cohort. We analyzed longitudinal data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study, collected at three time points: 2018–2020 (mean age, 12.1 years), 2020–2022 (mean age, 14.2 years), and 2022–2024 (mean age, 16.1 years). Adolescents (n = 4788 to 3313) with anthropometric and laboratory data were included. Abdominal obesity was defined as a waist‐to‐height ratio > 0.5. Additional CMR factors included elevated total cholesterol (≥ 145 mg/dL), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c ≥ 5.7%), and high blood pressure (≥ 95th percentile for age, sex, and height). Weighted prevalence and 2‐ and 4‐year incidence were calculated. At baseline, abdominal obesity affected 38.4% (95% CI: 36.4%–40.4%), followed by elevated cholesterol (17.6%), HbA1c (8.7%), and blood pressure (3.0%). Over a four‐year period, new‐onset abdominal obesity occurred in 10.5% of individuals, compared with 15.4% for cholesterol, 4.7% for HbA1c, and 3.1% for blood pressure. The steepest CMR progression was observed for lipid and adiposity markers. Abdominal obesity is highly prevalent by early adolescence, and new cardiometabolic risk develops rapidly, particularly for adiposity and dyslipidemia. These results highlight the need for early obesity screening and interventions to mitigate long‑term cardiometabolic risk in youth.

Introduction

Abdominal obesity and cardiometabolic risk (CMR) factors that begin in adolescence are known to persist into adulthood and contribute to the early onset of cardiovascular diseases and premature mortality [1], and account for approximately 50% of the global burden of cardiovascular disease [2]. Many U.S. adolescents already enter adulthood with substantial CMR [1]. However, longitudinal patterns of classic risk factors across multiple years in this population are unclear. To our knowledge, this is the first national, population‐based study to quantify both the 2‐year and 4‐year burden of emerging CMR in a large, sociodemographically diverse cohort of U.S. adolescents [3]. We assessed the prevalence and incidence of abdominal obesity, as well as four key CMR factors: BMI‐defined obesity, elevated total cholesterol, elevated glycated hemoglobin, and high blood pressure.

Methods

Prospectively collected data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study were analyzed at three time points: the 2nd follow‐up (2018–2020; mean age, 12.1 years), the 4th follow‐up (2020–2022; mean age, 14.2 years), and the 6th follow‐up (2022–2024; mean age, 16.1 years) [3]. Participants were included if they had available anthropometric and laboratory data at any of these waves (n = 4788–3313). Outcomes were defined according to pediatric cutoffs for abdominal obesity (> 0.5) [4], total cholesterol (≥ 145 mg/dL), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥ 5.7%, and high blood pressure (≥ 95th percentile for age, sex, and height) [5]. Descriptive statistics with 95% CIs were used, and 2‐year cumulative incidence was estimated for each cardiometabolic outcome among participants with ideal baseline values. Survey weights were applied to align the sample distribution with national population benchmarks from the U.S. Census American Community Survey.

Results

Among adolescents with available cardiometabolic data, the prevalence of risk factors at the 2nd follow‐up (mean age 12.1 years) was substantial (Table 1). Abdominal obesity was present in 38.4% (95% CI, 36.4%–40.4%), elevated total cholesterol in 17.6% (95% CI, 16.1%–19.2%), elevated HbA1c in 8.7% (95% CI, 7.6%–9.9%), and high blood pressure in 3.0% (95% CI, 2.2%–3.9%). Over follow‐up, new cardiometabolic abnormalities emerged among adolescents with initially ideal profiles. The 4‐year cumulative incidence was 10.5% (95% CI, 9.3%–11.8%) for abdominal obesity, 15.4% (95% CI, 13.2%–17.9%) for elevated cholesterol, 4.7% (95% CI, 3.6%–6.1%) for elevated HbA1c, and 3.1% (95% CI, 2.1%–4.4%) for high blood pressure (Figure 1). Across the three assessment waves, prevalence increased progressively, with the most pronounced rises observed in adiposity and lipid markers between ages 12 and 16. Although blood pressure and glycemic abnormalities were less frequent, their incidence over time indicates early vascular and metabolic vulnerability during adolescence.

Discussion

In this national cohort, abdominal obesity, combined with CMR factors, including traditional CMR (dyslipidemia, impaired glucose tolerance, and high blood pressure), was prevalent by early adolescence, with new‐onset risk occurring within just two years in a substantial proportion of youth with previously ideal markers. These patterns align with prior findings that U.S. adolescents exhibit a high prevalence of CMR, and that early excess CMR persists into adulthood and predicts future health risks [1]. Importantly, while blood pressure and glycemic indices remained more stable, small early deviations may still have long‐term neurocognitive consequences [6]. Our findings align with prior reports, which demonstrate that cardiometabolic profiles in adolescence, particularly an elevated waist‐to‐height ratio and lipid levels, are associated with poorer cardiovascular and brain health trajectories. This study contributes to the growing evidence that adolescence is a critical period for the emergence of metabolic risk [5], supporting early screening for adiposity and lipid abnormalities, as recommended by pediatric and cardiovascular societies. Given the cumulative nature of risk, public health strategies targeting sleep and diet quality, physical activity, and obesity prevention remain essential. Future work should investigate whether enhancing Life's Essential 8 [5] components during adolescence mitigates the long‐term risk of both cardiovascular and secondary health outcomes (e.g., brain and mental health).
