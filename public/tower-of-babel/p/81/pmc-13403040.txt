Epicardial Lead Dysfunction in a Patient With Mechanical Tricuspid Valve—A Challenging Complication
Authors: Tuba Guctekin, Mucahit Yasin Guctekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTComplete atrioventricular block after mechanical tricuspid valve replacement represents a challenging pacing scenario because transvalvular right ventricular lead placement is contraindicated. We report a 51‐year‐old woman with mechanical mitral and tricuspid valves who presented with syncope and complete AV block due to late epicardial lead dysfunction. Transient restoration of capture during transthoracic echocardiography suggested dynamic lead–myocardial contact or intermittent lead continuity. Coronary sinus pacing was attempted but abandoned because of intolerable diaphragmatic stimulation. The patient later deteriorated and died from septic shock. This case highlights the limitations of available pacing strategies after mechanical tricuspid valve replacement.

Case Report

A 51‐year‐old female presented to the emergency department with dizziness, syncope, nausea, and fatigue. Her medical history was significant for primary biliary cirrhosis, mechanical mitral valve replacement (28 years ago), and mechanical tricuspid valve replacement (10 years ago). Following the tricuspid valve surgery, she had developed postoperative complete atrioventricular (AV) block, necessitating epicardial pacemaker implantation. Subsequent generator replacements were performed six and three years prior to the current admission.

Upon physical examination, the patient was bradycardic (40 bpm) with a blood pressure of 100/60 mmHg. Auscultation revealed irregular heart sounds with distinct metallic S1 and S2. Abdominal examination demonstrated significant, extensive ascites. The admission electrocardiogram (ECG) confirmed complete AV block with ineffective pacemaker spikes (pacing failure) (Figure 1).

Surface ECG recorded at admission demonstrating complete atrioventricular block with a ventricular escape rate of 36 bpm. No atrial activity is visible. Regular pacemaker spikes are present but show no consistent relationship to the narrow QRS complexes, indicating complete failure of ventricular capture. The narrow QRS morphology of the escape rhythm suggests a junctional or high‐septal origin.

Electronic pacemaker interrogation was immediately performed. The generator remained functional with adequate battery longevity (estimated remaining life of 4 years). However, lead parameters revealed a critical dysfunction of the epicardial system: the ventricular lead impedance was elevated (1200 Ω), raising concern for impaired lead–myocardial contact. The pacing threshold exceeded the maximum output (10 V at 1.0 ms) with no ventricular capture, and sensing was negligible (< 0.5 mV). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) showed normally functioning mechanical prosthetic valves in both the mitral and tricuspid positions. Notably, during the TTE examination, a transient restoration of ventricular capture was observed on the monitor whenever the ultrasound probe was firmly pressed against the chest wall to obtain apical views. This suggested impaired lead–myocardial contact, where external mechanical pressure transiently improved electrode–tissue apposition and restored capture. (Figures 2 and 3).

Surface ECG recorded immediately before application of transthoracic echocardiographic probe pressure. The first 6 s show regular pacemaker spikes with no consistent association with QRS complexes and a ventricular rate of 35 bpm, consistent with ongoing pacing failure. In the final 4 s of the recording, pacemaker spikes are followed by broad QRS complexes, suggesting intermittent ventricular capture.

Surface ECG recorded during application of firm transthoracic echocardiographic probe pressure over the cardiac apex. Each pacemaker spike is consistently followed by a broad QRS complex at a rate of 52 bpm, corresponding to the programmed pacing rate and indicating sustained ventricular capture. The contrast with Figure 2 illustrates the dynamic nature of the lead–myocardial interface impairment in this patient.

Consequently, a percutaneous approach via the coronary sinus (CS) was attempted. Following subclavian vein puncture, the CS was successfully cannulated (Figure 4); selective venography was performed to identify a suitable side branch, such as the middle cardiac vein (Figure 5). Measurements were then taken by advancing the lead; pacing was achieved with r‐wave sensing 9 mV, lead impedance 692 Ω, and threshold 5 V, stable lead implantation could not be achieved because of persistent and intolerable diaphragmatic stimulation. The procedure was then terminated.

While awaiting a specialized epicardial pacing system—which required a 20‐day procurement period—the patient's clinical status deteriorated. She developed hepatic encephalopathy and was intubated due to a declining Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score. Following intubation, the patient developed aspiration pneumonia. Despite aggressive broad‐spectrum antibiotic therapy and intensive care support, the patient's inflammatory markers continued to rise, and she ultimately succumbed to septic shock on the 38th day of hospitalization.

Discussion

Permanent pacemaker leads are conventionally implanted via a transvenous approach to the right ventricular apex, typically traversing the tricuspid valve. However, epicardial leads are required in selected patient populations, including pediatric patients with congenital heart disease, individuals undergoing tricuspid valve surgery, and those with mechanical or bioprosthetic tricuspid valves. Dysfunction of epicardial leads produces clinical manifestations similar to complete atrioventricular (AV) block, as the patient reverts to an underlying bradycardic rhythm. In the present case, the patient exhibited classical symptoms of pacing failure, including generalized weakness, fatigue, and syncope.

The patient had undergone mechanical tricuspid valve replacement (TVR) 10 years earlier. TVR is relatively uncommon, as tricuspid valve repair is generally preferred unless severe leaflet degeneration–most frequently associated with advanced rheumatic heart disease–precludes reconstruction. During such procedures, sutures must be carefully placed to avoid injury to the conduction system and prevent iatrogenic AV node damage (Cheng et al. 2021). Even when AV block is absent in the early postoperative period, delayed conduction disturbances may develop as a consequence of progressive fibrosis within the conduction system during cardiac remodeling. This mechanism likely explains the occurrence of complete AV block 30 days after TVR in our patient.

Prior tricuspid valve surgery significantly complicates subsequent trans‐tricuspid interventions. Leads crossing the tricuspid valve may interfere with the function of both repaired valves and prosthetic devices. Although transvenous right ventricular (RV) endocardial pacing remains the standard pacing strategy, structural tricuspid valve disease requiring repair or replacement may render the RV inaccessible through the conventional route, necessitating alternative pacing approaches (Zha et al. 2017). Following mechanical TVR, transvalvular lead placement is strictly contraindicated (Glikson et al. 2021). In patients at high risk of developing high‐grade AV block, implantation of an epicardial RV lead during the index valve surgery represents the most straightforward preventive strategy (Glikson et al. 2021). Although concerns regarding the long‐term durability of epicardial leads persist, recent evidence suggests that their performance may be comparable to transvenous systems, particularly in the context of left ventricular epicardial pacing (Burger et al. 2020).

In our patient with an epicardial pacemaker, the initial failure of effective pacing, followed by restoration of capture upon application of apical pressure with the echocardiographic probe, was most compatible with impaired lead–myocardial contact, potentially due to microdislodgement or progressive fibrotic tissue formation at the electrode–myocardium interface. The transient restoration of capture with externally applied mechanical pressure likely reflected improved electrode–tissue apposition and increased local current density, thereby overcoming the elevated capture threshold. Because surgical inspection was not performed, the exact mechanism of lead dysfunction remains speculative. Such dynamic findings underscore the importance of careful physical and echocardiographic assessment in the evaluation of epicardial lead dysfunction.

Ventricular pacing through the coronary sinus (CS) has emerged as a feasible alternative following mechanical TVR, although current evidence is largely limited to small observational series. One study reported a procedural success rate of 100% in 23 patients, with 96% lead functionality maintained at five‐year follow‐up (Noheria et al. 2018). CS‐based ventricular pacing offers effective sensing and pacing parameters while preserving a minimally invasive approach (Noheria et al. 2018). Nevertheless, several complications have been reported, including diaphragmatic stimulation, CS dissection, lead dislodgement, and elevated pacing thresholds. The evolution of cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) and biventricular pacing has led to the development of specialized guiding catheters and dedicated left ventricular leads, increasing procedural success rates to as high as 99%. Despite these advances, pacing‐related complications—particularly lead dislodgement and loss of capture—remain more frequent with CS leads than with conventional RV leads. Consequently, isolated CS‐based left ventricular pacing may not represent the most reliable long‐term strategy in patients who are, or are likely to become, pacing‐dependent.

More recently, His bundle pacing (HBP) has emerged as a physiologic pacing modality that preserves native ventricular activation and may represent an alternative option following tricuspid valve surgery. In a study of 30 patients undergoing permanent HBP after cardiac valve operations—including individuals with tricuspid annuloplasty—the procedural success rate reached 93% (Sharma et al. 2017).

Leadless pacing systems represent another potential option in patients with limited venous access or high infection risk, as they eliminate the need for leads and subcutaneous pockets. However, it is important to note that conventional leadless right ventricular pacemaker implantation via the standard femoral trans‐tricuspid route is generally not feasible in patients with a mechanical tricuspid valve prosthesis. Alternative implantation approaches, such as transhepatic or transseptal access, have been described in selected cases but require specialized expertise and carry additional procedural risks; their use in this population therefore remains limited to highly experienced centers.

Left ventricular endocardial pacing via alternative access routes, such as transseptal or transhepatic approaches, has been described in highly selected patients when conventional options have been exhausted. While this strategy offers physiological ventricular activation and avoids tricuspid valve crossing, it requires specialized techniques, carries a risk of systemic thromboembolism, and necessitates long‐term anticoagulation, limiting its applicability to experienced centers.

Given the complexity of pacing management in patients with mechanical tricuspid valves and the absence of a universally optimal approach, a structured comparison of available strategies may be clinically useful. In the present case, CS lead implantation was attempted and pacing capture was achieved with acceptable electrical parameters; however, the procedure was abandoned due to intolerable diaphragmatic stimulation. Following the failed CS pacing attempt, transcutaneous pacing was initiated as a bridge and was maintained throughout the intensive care unit admission. Surgical consultation was obtained and a multidisciplinary council reviewed the case; however, surgical epicardial lead implantation was deemed prohibitively high‐risk given the patient's advanced liver cirrhosis, massive ascites, coagulopathy, and overall frail clinical status. Transvenous temporary pacing via femoral access and re‐attempted CS pacing via an alternative venous branch were considered but not pursued, as the attendant procedural risks were judged to outweigh the anticipated benefit in the context of the patient's comorbidities. A specialized epicardial pacing system capable of being implanted with a minimally invasive approach was identified as the most feasible option; however, this device was not available in Turkey and required procurement from abroad, resulting in an unavoidable 20‐day delay. During this waiting period, the patient remained hemodynamically stable under transcutaneous pacing support. The subsequent development of sepsis markedly altered the clinical trajectory. Infective endocarditis could not be excluded in this setting given the presence of mechanical prosthetic valves and an ongoing infectious process; however, the diagnosis was not confirmed, as transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) could not be performed because of the high bleeding risk associated with Grade III esophageal varices. Infective endocarditis was therefore considered in the differential diagnosis, and empirical antimicrobial therapy with piperacillin‐tazobactam, daptomycin, and anidulafungin was initiated accordingly. As hemodynamic instability progressed, transcutaneous pacing failed to achieve consistent electrical capture, likely due to increased thoracic impedance related to terminal‐stage liver cirrhosis and massive ascites. Although transient capture could intermittently be achieved by applying manual pressure over the left precordial region—mimicking the mechanical effect of a transthoracic echocardiography probe—progressive systemic edema ultimately rendered this maneuver ineffective. Despite intensive medical management, the patient died on the 38th day of hospitalization.

Taken together, this case highlights the complex challenges associated with pacing management in patients with mechanical tricuspid valve prostheses. Conventional transvenous RV pacing is not feasible in this population, and alternative strategies such as epicardial pacing, coronary sinus–based ventricular pacing, or conduction system pacing must be considered on an individualized basis. Our case underscores that, despite the availability of multiple pacing strategies, anatomical limitations, comorbid conditions, and logistical constraints may critically restrict therapeutic options. Therefore, careful preoperative planning—including consideration of prophylactic epicardial lead implantation at the time of tricuspid valve surgery—may be essential in patients at risk of developing AV conduction disturbances. This case further emphasizes the need for continued refinement of alternative pacing techniques and highlights the importance of multidisciplinary decision‐making in managing complex pacing scenarios following tricuspid valve interventions.
