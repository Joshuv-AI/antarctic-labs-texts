Intergenerational trauma in forcibly-displaced populations in Latin America: a scoping review
Authors: C Correa-Salazar, D S Torres-Benítez, E Agudelo-Avellaneda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOverlapping humanitarian crises make the Americas home to one in every five forcibly displaced or stateless persons worldwide. Although a robust body of literature describes the intergenerational effects of traumatic experiences related to forced displacement, evidence from Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC) and with Latinx groups is scarce and contains wide variations in operationalizations of intergenerational trauma, transmission mechanisms and coping strategies. The aim of this scoping review was to improve our understanding of intergenerational trauma in forcibly displaced populations from LAC. We addressed these objectives: (i) to map existing literature on intergenerational trauma in forcibly displaced groups in LAC; (ii) to identify operationalizations of intergenerational trauma, transmission mechanisms and coping strategies in forcibly displaced groups in LAC; and (iii) to identify knowledge gaps.MethodsSystematic searches were conducted in 20 online databases to identify articles that studied intergenerational trauma, associated transmission mechanisms and coping strategies in forcibly displaced Latinx groups originating in LAC. Narrative analysis was conducted to describe the relevant themes extracted from the included literature.ResultsSix English and Spanish-language articles were included, from which three general themes were identified regarding intergenerational trauma in forcibly-displaced Latinx populations: (i) intergenerational trauma operationalizations, (ii) transmission mechanisms and coping strategies, and (iii) identified gaps in research and evidence. Two subthemes themes were identified relating to intergenerational trauma operationalizations: (i) measures and samples, (ii) forced displacement motives. Evidence gaps in the literature were also elaborated.ConclusionWe identified gaps in intergenerational trauma definitions and operationalizations. Greater methodological rigor and consistency in trauma reporting is warranted to support future evidence-based research and inform supportive systems that promote the health of forcibly-displaced populations and their descendants in the Americas.Keywords: Intergenerational trauma, Migrants, Refugees, Forcibly-displaced, Latinxs, Latin America

Introduction

In 2025, there was a projected sharp increase in relocation needs for forcibly displaced populations (FDPs) in the Americas (North, Central and South) [45], especially through movements from South America to northern countries crossing the Darién Gap [50]. The Americas host around 20 million forcibly displaced or stateless people, a situation primarily driven by displacement in Mesoamerica, the Andean region, and the Caribbean [50]. Overlapping humanitarian crises in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC) have caused one of the most pronounced increases in human mobility globally, which have grown in scale and complexity over the last decade [51]. In 2025, there was a projected sharp increase in relocation needs for forcibly displaced populations (FDPs) in the Americas (North, Central and South) [45], especially through movements from South America to northern countries crossing the Darién Gap [50]. The Americas host around 20 million forcibly displaced or stateless people, a situation primarily driven by displacement in Mesoamerica, the Andean region, and the Caribbean [50]. Overlapping humanitarian crises in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC) have caused one of the most pronounced increases in human mobility globally, which have grown in scale and complexity over the last decade [51].

Migration is a structural determinant of health [16, 44]. Given that the U.S. is no longer a desired or plausible destination for the larger portions of FDPs, countries in the LAC region face major challenges to relocate FDPs seeking safe passage, security and better life conditions. FDPs are composed by migrants, refugees, exiles and internally-displaced persons (IDPs) who cross national and/or international borders as a result of some element of coercion, including threats to life and livelihood, whether arising from natural or human-made causes [24, 42] like armed conflict, situations of generalized violence, and violations of human rights [33]. FDPs often decide to leave their homes seeking for better life conditions, safety and improvement of their wellbeing. FDPs face traumatic exposures prior to and during the migratory journey, which are strongly linked to detrimental brain health impacts [18], mental health problems and psychological distress [21]. Evidence indicates these challenges can persist years after relocation through intergenerational mechanisms of trauma transmission [46].

Vulnerable groups like women, children and adolescents experience greater mental health disparities given pre-, transit and postmigration conditions [15, 17, 38]. Mental health disparities are even greater if FDPs have survived armed conflict/war, human rights violations and/or humanitarian crises [4, 8, 26, 32, 39, 43, 48]. These conditions hinder integration efforts and negatively impact mental health outcomes [38] of this and coming generations.

Intergenerational trauma (IT), also conceptualized as transgenerational, historic [34], and/or cultural trauma [27], is defined as experiences of pain and suffering that can be transmitted as beliefs, behaviors, roles and ideologies through primary and community relationships [54]. IT is a complex form of collective trauma that impacts health and wellbeing of future generations by means of sharing a common identity, being exposed to survivors of trauma and learned coping strategies [12, 13, 46]. IT is moderated, reproduced or buffered by culture through structures like families, community networks and institutions [34]. The study of IT with FDPs in LAC supports the understanding of how structural determinants of health present in the region connect to traumatic responses, intergenerational transmission mechanisms and coping strategies in relocation contexts.

Although there have been previous scoping and systematic reviews on the subject of IT in Latinxs [12] and IT in refugees [22, 46], there is scarce evidence on how intergenerational trauma is transmitted and coped with in FDPs in the Americas, particularly in LAC or with LAC populations facing push factors like high inequality, armed conflict, social violence, drug trafficking dynamics [3, 9, 23, 25], climate change and overlapping humanitarian crises [37]. In the scoping review presented by Sangalang & Vang (2017) [46], there was only one study reported that included American Canadian offspring of Holocaust survivors. Kim & Jordan (2025 ) [35] only found studies on survivors and descendants relocated in North America but originating in Asia. Flanagan et al. (2020) [22]only included in their systematic review Western, high income-profile countries that do not reflect current displacement and migration trends in LAC or with Global South populations.

There is a lack of evidence from LAC and with South-to-South FDPs not originated or relocated in high income countries. Furthermore, there are no systematic or scoping reviews that include a focus on Latinx populations. There is also little evidence on what factors protect from adverse life events, and prevent increased stress related to migration in descendants relocated in the Americas. Literature on the efforts, both behavioral and psychological, that people employ to master, tolerate, reduce, or minimize stressful events beyond problem focused strategies [7] in relocation contexts is lacking. Finally, there are few or nonexistent culturally sensitive measures to assess IT in LAC populations or conceptualize the structural determinants that shape forced migration in the Americas [31, 46].

The purpose of this scoping review was to understand IT in FDPs originating and/or relocating in LAC by addressing the following objectives: i) to map existing literature on intergenerational trauma in FDPs in LAC; (ii) to identify operationalizations of intergenerational trauma, transmission mechanisms and coping strategies in FDPs in LAC; and (iii) to identify gaps within the knowledge base.

Methods

Search strategy, study selection and exclusion criteria

A scoping review was conducted to identify relevant academic literature focusing on intergenerational trauma in forcibly-displaced populations from LAC contexts. This review was informed by [1] methodological framework and recommendations from Levac et al. (2010) & Peters et al. (2020) [36, 41]. This scoping review was reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [47].

A search strategy was implemented to find relevant publications, which were screened by a group of eight junior psychologists and an experienced research assistant with clinical and social training. All team members were trained in inclusion and exclusion criteria. After the first screening, the title and abstracts screen was approved by all three authors. Screening started in August 2024 and continued through February 2025. Studies were selected using our predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies were included if (1) study subjects were forcibly displaced Latinx populations, including internally displaced, migrants, refugees, and exiles of LAC origin (2) the experience of trauma was collective in nature and affected a targeted group of people bound by a common LAC cultural, social or political identity; (3) the article investigated intergenerational trauma or some variation of the term (historic, cultural, transgenerational trauma), (4) children or descendants did not experience political trauma directly themselves; (5) and the article included more than one generation in the study of trauma.

Publications were excluded if: (1) they were systematic reviews, theoretical or conceptual in nature in which no direct or empirical data was collected from individuals or groups; (2) research was based on non-LAC populations or non-FDP populations (e.g. survivors of war, populations sharing a common religious or cultural identity only, indigenous groups and racial minorities that were not FDPs per se1); (3) centered around trauma experiences that did not take place in the context of migration, displacement or populations’ movements; (4) the reviewers were unable to obtain an English or Spanish translations of the articles; (5) the full-text of the article was unavailable; (6) the article only addressed psychological interventions or treatments.

No restrictions were added to the location or year of publication. Additionally, all study designs and articles were eligible for inclusion due to the limited number of empirical research studies on this topic. Subsequently, the eight junior psychologists and the research assistant independently extracted the data from the included publications, which were discussed in weekly meetings by the complete team including all three authors. Subsequently, the authors organized, summarized, and presented the results.

The search parameters included studies from peer reviewed literature relevant to intergenerational trauma in forcibly displaced groups in LAC. To locate peer-reviewed literature the following twenty electronic bibliographic databases were searched: MEDLINE ALL, Embase, PsycINFO, Global Health, Web of Science Complete, Scopus, PubMed, ProQuest, PsycINFO, PsycARTICLES, ERIC, SCOPUS, Google Scholar, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, EBSCOhost, CLACSO, Latindex, and Scielo. Initial searches were conducted between August-September 2024 and rerun and updated in February 2025. Broad search terms, with variations and relevant synonyms (intergenerational, transgenerational, cultural, historic trauma; or, forced-to-leave, forced migrants, exiles, refugees) were deliberately chosen to capture the scope of the literature. The outcome of interest was explored as detrimental/negative impacts to mental health (e.g., mental health/trauma/stressful life events/adverse childhood events). The population groups were also searched broadly to include diverse LAC origins (Latino/a/x, Hispanic, Chicano/a/x). We also searched for broad contextual situations that could act as push factors for populations and/or be the causes of trauma (i.e., war, conflict, trauma, well-being, adverse events, childhood adversity). Searches were conducted in English and Spanish. The search strategies were adjusted for the syntax appropriate for each database/platform and language. The search term strategy was systematically used across all queried databases in English and Spanish.

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by eight junior psychologists previously trained, an experienced research assistant with clinical and social training, and authors ENA and DST (conducting a last update in February 2025). Data were organized in Rayyan®. After excluding duplicates, the team conducted an article title and abstract review to determine eligibility for inclusion. For each study, members extracted the study design and research methodology, the sample size and percentage of Latinx participants, the geographic location of the study, demographic characteristics of the sample (e.g., whether the study included maternal-child dyads or pre-screened for trauma exposure), operationalization and measurement of intergenerational trauma, mediators or moderators tested as potential mechanisms of transmission, risk or protective factors impacting mental health, cping strategies, and a summary of findings. For qualitative studies, the summary of findings was narratively synthesized from full-text review of author data analysis, results, and conclusions. For quantitative findings, we identified statistical tests examining the relationship between parental trauma and descendants’ health outcomes and identified the type of statistical test, coefficients, and significance level of findings to assess results and measures. Wherever possible, we report standardized coefficients. Data extraction and coding were conducted by the second and third authors, and revised by the P.I. (CCS).

Data synthesis and integration

Evidence was synthesized narratively by authors ENA and DST after team meetings where papers were presented, discussed and ruled upon to be included or excluded. Team meetings included the three authors and the eight junior psychologists trained in research. Reflective discussion and iterative review of the themes occurred weekly for eight months. Content for challenges in intergenerational trauma, forced migration and mental health impacts was grouped into meaningful themes and sub-themes. Furthermore, content on source article objectives, findings, and limitations were grouped into additional themes which do not directly focus on intergenerational trauma evidence but point to gaps in knowledge or relevant information in general.

Results

Study characteristics

The initial search led to the identification of 11,040 potential articles generated across the search engines. We proceeded to conduct subsequent searches for relevant citations within the remaining articles. A total of 8,035 were screened in full texts reviews. Figure 1 illustrates the study selection process using a PRISMA diagram. After exclusion of duplicates, and application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, eleven (n = 11) papers were selected for inclusion, all of which were peer-reviewed in either English or Spanish. We found six (n = 6) articles after a full-text examination that met all our criteria.

Abstract and article review following the PRISMA guidelines for scoping reviews

To maintain our focus on FDPs, we excluded studies on non-FDP populations (as grouped here) that studied IT [14] cultural or historical trauma. We also excluded samples that were not from LAC or of LAC-origin, as we were interested in limiting our search to geographic, historical and cultural parameters. We further excluded studies of third generations relocated or born outside of LAC that did not analyze the intergenerational component of transmission (transmission mechanisms, care responsibilities or family relations), did not focus on trauma or did not analyze the cultural link to LAC. Even though third generations are affected by intergenerational trauma, without the cultural, community or family component of transmission as described here we found it hard to assess according to our criteria.

We included studies with overlapping samples but ensured that such articles contained unique findings. Table 1 details the included study designs, participant characteristics, and relevant outcomes. Of the included sources, 2 were quantitative studies, 3 were qualitative studies, 1 was a mixed methods design. The quantitative and qualitative parts of the mixed methods study were separated for analysis and included in the relevant sections. We did not find any studies that included or focused on Caribbean populations.

Quantitative studies

Of the six studies [5, 11, 20, 49, 52, 56], only two had an exclusively quantitative design [49, 52] and one had a quantitative section of a mixed methods design [5]. All applied structured questionnaires, but all searched for different mental health outcomes. The quantitative section of the mixed methods article mapped parent report of stressful life events, child emotional and behavioral problems and family functioning [5]. The other two analyzed Hispanic stress and symptom inventories [49], and suicidal potential and ego functions of reality [52]. All three studies worked with two generations of the same families [5, 49, 52]. Only one applied a longitudinal design [49].

Qualitative studies

Of the six studies [5, 11, 20, 49, 52, 56], three had an exclusively qualitative design [11, 20, 56] and one had a qualitative section of a mixed methods design [5]. The most common form of data collection was semi-structured individual interviews [5, 11]. One had pre-formatted open-ended questions [56], and another life stories and participant observation [20]. Two studies worked exclusively with women [20, 56]. Between the two, women were from México, Guatemala, Colombia, Salvador and Chilean origin. Three worked with two generations of the same families [5, 11, 20].

Mixed methods studies

Only one study had a mixed methods design [5]. This article reported the implementation of standardized tests and culturally adapted semi-structured interviews. This study worked with children and their care takers of different origins, including Latinx/Hispanic families relocated in the United States. Further details in Table 1.

General themes

Intergenerational trauma operationalizations

Table 1 provides an overview of how empirical studies conceptualize and operationalize intergenerational trauma in LAC populations. The theme of intergenerational trauma (IT) is defined differently in all studies. Some articles explicitly mention intergenerational trauma [11, 20, 49] or ongoing cultural trauma [56], while others use terms such as Stressful Life Events (SLE) [5], but without an explicit mention of transmission or impact in second generations. Another just mentions intergenerational transmission [52] to describe mental health outcomes in second generation descendants. No studies repeat or use the same or even similar IT definitions.

Only two mention trauma associated with structural violence, abuse and factors like discrimination, racism, gender-based or partner violence [5, 56]. None of the articles mention forced migration as a structural determinant of health that can cause or contribute to IT in-and-of- itself. In the included studies, migration is framed either as relocation and loss of place, instability, and fractured identity affecting family roles [11], or as a gendered and unequal phenomenon, particularly for women and children linked to social values [20, 56]. Conversely, forced migration is treated as both a traumatic event and a context that amplifies existing vulnerabilities [11, 49, 56]. Only Espinoza Cartes mentions refugee status, in which participants fleeing political persecution and threats to their integrity can acquire protection under a different State [20].

Studies use proxies to operationalize IT and assess the cumulative impact of migration in parents’ and offspring’s health. In the case of stressful life events (SLEs), these are conceptualized as discrete, acute stressors that disrupt an individual’s usual activities and typically result in substantial change [5]. Some studies agree that trauma involves a disruption in the life of individuals due to loss of home, culture, or personal projects [20], or violence and isolation [56]. Only one study mentions how intergenerational trauma affects the sense of identity, emotional health, and future choices of children or descendants [20].

Torres, Espinoza Cartes, Carbonell Blanco et al., and Vega et al. [11, 20, 49, 52] have a focus on how trauma is transmitted between generations but only Zolnikov et al. and Espinoza Cartes [20, 56] share the idea that these transmissions are often linked to internalized cultural norms. In Zolnikov et al. [56], trauma for immigrant Latina women is related to cultural, historical, and gendered factors, while for Espinoza Cartes [20] the trauma of forced exile is linked to the loss of cultural identity and adaptation to a new social reality. However, all studies agree that IT is transmitted not only through explicit narratives, but also through silences, emotions, roles, and family practices situated within a social conext or shared cultural identity [11, 20, 52]. Conceptualizations and operationalization of intergenerational trauma are varied and inconsistent in the measurement of predictors and outcomes in FDPs.

Measures and samples

Despite the high prevalence of negative mental health outcomes in FDPs and their descendants, research specifically focusing on intergenerational trauma remains limited, especially in LAC. Studies included in this scoping review do not consistently measure IT with the same instruments, populations, or generations. Even the generations included in the studies do not share the same characteristics in terms of age, origin or previous experiences. None of the articles present culturally adapted measures for IT in Latinx or Hispanic populations. Most studies applied surveys to measure behaviors, symptoms and Stressful Life Events (SLE) in the different generations presumed to be impacted by IT [5, 49, 52]. Three studies were conducted in the United States [5, 49, 56], one in Spain [20], one in Colombia [11], and one in Argentina [52].

Measures of IT also differ by emphasis on first, second or third generation outcomes. Three studies focus on parent-offspring dyads [5, 20, 49], one focuses mostly on the affected FDP generation [56], another on the descendants of FDPs [52], and one study interviews parents about both the forced displacement experience, and the outcomes in the offspring [11].

Populations are also described in differential terms. Four articles study immigrant families [5, 11, 20, 49]. Three of those center exclusively around Latinx immigrants [11, 20, 49]. Four articles study only populations that have been displaced internationally [5, 20, 49, 56] and another article studies a mix of internal and internationally displaced groups [52]. Carbonell Blanco et al. (2024) [11] focus on internally displaced Colombian populations (IDPs) due to armed conflict and war. Thus, all studies engage with and analyze intergenerational trauma in different migration flows and samples based on each study’s geographical and/or social context. Measures and sampled generations varied by study locations, as the historical and cultural context impacted what was conceptualized as IT in the context of forced displacement.

As shown in Table 1, all studies include LAC forcibly-displaced populations but almost none coincide on how to name these diverse migration flows in terms of geographical and/or cultural origin (varying between Latinos/xs, forcibly displaced, migrant, refugees or IDPs). Only Zolnikov [56] describes the Latino culture and its shared values in relation to a wider social context.

Displacement motives

As for displacement motives, studies do not list the reasons for displacement consistently. Torres, Espinoza Cartes and Carbonell Blanco et al. [11, 20, 49] refer to violence as the main reason for forced displacement but describe it differently. Torres [49] states displacement as a consequence of political and interpersonal violence, mentioning poverty, lack of economic opportunities and family reunification reasons as push factors. Espinoza Cartes [20] describes political persecution, risk of detention, torture or disappearance after the 1973 military takeover in Chile as the main displacement motive. However, the paper also mentions “unsustainable living” and the need to protect children as a displacement motive. For Carbonell Blanco et al. [20], the main push factor is listed as the armed conflict violence in Colombia. Studies included in this scoping review present a wide array of reasons for forced displacement that refer to individual, interpersonal and social factors indistinguishably.

Transmission mechanisms and coping strategies

Studies vary widely in the identification and study of mechanisms of transmission of IT and coping strategies in FDPs to resist adverse effects that might be associated with this phenomenon. Regarding mechanisms of transmission, some studies coincide that communication patterns and narratives around displacement can impact the identity formation of descendants and meanings around loss, trauma and pain [11, 20]. According to Carbonell Blanco et al. [11], when there are silences, cover-ups or inability to cope with displacement, children have few resources to re-signify the story of their families and the loss they suffered. Espinoza Cartes [20] reports a similar finding, describing exile not only as an experience of loss, but also a space for identity transformation where descendants raised abroad inherit family stories from an ambivalent position of both loss and gain.

Furthermore, culture and values were identified as mechanisms of IT transmission in the family and community spheres [11, 56]. Specifically, values of fatalism and marianismo found in Latina women that impact their decision-making processes and behaviors through deep-rooted cultural beliefs and gender roles that perpetuate violence [56]. In this sense, social learning of roles and values can mediate how women interpret and transmit experiences of trauma to descendants [56]. Conversely, where IT was measured by proxies, stress, disruptive and violent behaviors within the household were the transmission mechanisms assessed in relation to the presence of trauma in offspring [5, 11, 20, 49, 52, 56].

As for coping mechanisms, silence and avoidance were identified as coping strategies for minimizing pain and “moving forward” in families [11, 20]. Similarly, some studies identified narratives around past events, collective memory building (i.e., embroidery, and bonding with others in exile) [20] or help-seeking behaviors based on hope, community support, and awareness of mental health needs [56] as effective ways to deal with intergenerational trauma related to forced displacement and loss for FDPs originating in LAC [56]. Only one study referred to resilience as a coping mechanism, defined as the ability of exiled women to rebuild their lives and adapt to new cultures, create support networks, and participate in the labor, educational and political spheres [56].

Identified gaps in research and evidence

Key gaps in the knowledge base relate to the limited study of intergenerational trauma in LAC-originated FDPs, lack of culturally adapted or validated tools for the LAC context and populations. We also found a lack of standardized measures across disciplines and fields, sampled populations and consensus around definitions of IT. This is also the case for conceptualizations of transmission mechanisms and coping skills, which we found had varied and differential approached to their study. There is a scarcity of high-quality empirical studies consistently operationalizing intergenerational trauma, transmission mechanisms and coping strategies in forcibly-displaced populations of Latin American origin and/or relocated to Latin American contexts (in South-to-South forced displacement). Generally, there is a lack of literature on the nature of intergenerational trauma related to the forced displacement experience and of its consequences for descendants. Research in the area uses measures by proxy lacks agreement on what constitutes trauma, stressors, adverse events and what is transmitted and how. Research and clinical practice often fail to address the different levels of push factors that generate the forced displacement, mixing individual, interpersonal and social factors. Operationalizations of intergenerational trauma specific to LAC populations, cultural contexts and regional displacement motives are needed, as well as mixed methods longitudinal studies that can provide insight into the development of trauma, the mechanisms of transmission of traumatic responses and the of coping skills that hell adaptation and support health in relocation contexts.

The interdisciplinary nature of this research is reflected in its findings across publications in various types of journals, primarily psychology journals, public health, family and health systems, and broader health dialogues. Bibliographic trends suggest that research on intergenerational trauma in Latinxs is gaining prominence, as all included studies in our review were published between 2016 and 2024.

Discussion

This scoping review identified 6 articles on intergenerational trauma, its transmission mechanisms and coping strategies in forcibly-displaced populations of LAC origin or relocated in LAC. The field suffers from a dearth of empirical research, standardized measures and samples, consensus on proxies and conceptualization of forced migration as trauma or cumulative traumatic events.

The purpose of this scoping review was to understand intergenerational trauma in forcibly-displaced populations originating and/or relocating in LAC by addressing the following objectives: i) to map existing literature on intergenerational trauma in FDPs in LAC; (ii) to identify operationalizations of intergenerational trauma, transmission mechanisms and coping strategies in FDPs in LAC; and (iii) to identify gaps within the knowledge base. This paper answers key empirical and theoretical gaps in knowledge referring to intergenerational trauma (IT) operationalizations and conceptualizations in forcibly displaced groups with LAC origin or relocated in LAC -including groups characterized as migrants, refugees, IDPs and exiles. As we characterized and examined empirical research that includes LAC FDPs, we found critical insights that can help inform future research, interventions, programmatic efforts and policy.

We identified key distinctions in IT operationalizations, research designs and measures used that hinder the replication of findings and understanding of this phenomenon. Different conceptualizations and measured outcomes for trauma survivor and descendants lack culturally adapted instruments and a broader understanding of displacement motives comprehensive of the LAC broader context. Some articles explicitly mention intergenerational trauma [11, 20, 49] or ongoing cultural trauma [56], while others use terms such as Stressful Life Events (SLE) [5] or just intergenerational transmission [52]. Consequently, conceptualizations and operationalizations of IT are varied and inconsistent with culturally adapted measures, standardized predictors and similar outcomes for physical, mental and community health.

Even though we included empirical studies with FDPs, as defined above, none conceptualize migration-specific stressors as determinants of health, or stressors specific to each migration phase as potential sources of trauma beyond the understanding of “pre-migration adverse events”. Migration [16, 44] and violence [2, 30] are structural determinants of health that often coincide across migration phases in the Americas [15, 17, 19, 40]. None of the included studies understand or operationalize forced displacement as a structural determinant of health [16, 30, 44] that can cause or contribute to IT and mediate its impact on the health of descendants. Violence, when mentioned, refers to different life dimensions (personal, interpersonal, community-based or social) [6, 28] but is not explored in designs or measured pre-, during and post-migration as a cumulative factor contributing to vulnerability and detrimental health outcomes in different generations. Albeit discrimination and stress are considered within some studies [5], they are not related to the community as a whole or to the intergenerational nature of a social problem being transmitted and/or resisted and coped with, as IT’s definition warrants.

As the diverse LAC context and cultures ultimately impact how researchers are understanding IT, its potential predictors (violence, armed conflict, gender roles or other), outcomes (offspring trauma, disruptive behaviors, communication patters or identity formation) and impacted populations, we need more culturally adapted measures, methodologies and understanding of how transmission and coping strategies operate within families and communities in the region. The conceptualization of Latinx/Latino culture from a U.S. standpoint (depicting it as marianismo/fatalism opposites [56], for example) reproduces colonialism and erases religious oppression and cultural backgrounds of LAC populations, as well as potential mechanisms of resistance to trauma grounded in cultural praxis and identities. As the first scoping review on this subject in the Americas, we push for more culturally grounded and inclusive definitions of forcibly-displaced groups, IT definitions and measures that can support culturally and contextually adapted health science.

Much of the existing literature on FDPs/refugee/migrant/exiles/IDPs families has centered on populations more distantly removed from war-related traumas and issues of immediate resettlement [46]. This causes IT research in LAC to be still in its infancy [12]. This research provides opportunities to study diverse populations, measurements, mechanisms of transmission and coping strategies by which trauma among first-generation survivors is processed and transmitted over time to descendants in relocation contexts, connecting cross-border communities and South-to-South migration flows. However, there are notable differences between empirical approaches that leave major gaps in knowledge. With no culturally grounded definitions, measures and approaches, this body of research will remain incomplete.

As no one-size-fits-all problem, more empirical and theoretical research is needed to assess how the intergenerational transmission of trauma among nonclinical samples of FDPs and their families is transmitted and adapts to the region’s changing context. Given the tremendous growth of FDPs, migrant and refugee flows in recent years [51], future research should examine the effects and processes of intergenerational trauma transmission in diverse and more current populations. Future research should consider the inclusion of positive mental health outcomes associated to migration, such as forms of resilience and post-traumatic growth, in order to bolster protective factors and account for strengths within FDPs families across generations [46].

Most studies in this scoping review explored the measures of IT in forcibly displaced parents and the mechanisms by which trauma is transmitted to their offspring as well as different meanings attributed to FDPs parents’ trauma. Despite the heterogeneity in populations, forms of trauma, measurements and their effects, the literature was consistent in describing parenting and family interactions as playing significant roles in the ways in which parental trauma is processed within families, as well as on the detrimental effects that unprocessed past experiences impact family and community life. Included studies identified family and parent–child relationships as potential mediators in the link between the experiences of traumatized refugee parents and the emotional and behavioral health of their children and described the ways in which parental trauma is transformed and infused with meaning for the next generation’s identity formation and cultural narratives around forced displacement. In a growing stigmatizing climate for migrants/refugees/exiles/asylum seekers, research, programmatic efforts and policies will need to adapt to protect future generations across the continent.

We propose that empirical studies can benefit from greater methodological and conceptual rigor in various ways. First, a consistent definition of IT and how it applies to different populations, contexts and socioecological levels of human life [6, 28, 29] can better assess how structural factors impact generations across time (i.e., migration and violence). Second, empirical investigations that also test for clinical symptoms with culturally-adapted measures can support the identification of transmission mechanisms and coping strategies. Third, the inclusion of third generation descendants can help clarify how trauma changes over time and what coping strategies are best depending on the context. Fourth, respond to previous research’s call on identifying and including different and diverse FDPs populations to assess how pre-, during and post-migration stressors impact these groups [46]. Finally, differ between migration phases [55] to account for pre-migration adverse life events (i.e. war-related traumatic experiences) as well as post-migration stressors in families (i.e., discrimination) [46].

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting this scoping review’s findings. Firstly, the relatively small number of selected articles, may reflect the current state of the literature but also raises the possibility of inadvertently excluding relevant articles. The scarcity of appropriate studies represents a notable limitation for this review and its future applicability, as well as generalizability of findings. Additionally, restricting inclusion to English and Spanish-language articles could have omitted valuable information available in other languages studying other LAC populations (like Portuguese, French, Dutch). Access issues could have further limited article inclusion. Another challenge is the variability of measurement tools, sampled populations, study locations and criteria for generations used to assess intergenerational trauma, impeding direct study comparisons. Future research should prioritize standardized operationalizations, similar conceptual approaches, harmonizing and standardizing methodologies for intergenerational trauma, mechanisms of transmission and coping strategies to facilitate meaningful comparisons and enhance the robustness of findings. The authors acknowledge the lack of a-priori protocol registration as a limitation of this study, which may introduce potential biases in study design and analysis.

Conclusion and recommendations

This review sought to address the limited knowledge on IT, transmission mechanisms and coping strategies in FDPs in LAC. To our knowledge, there are still no policy efforts seeking to translate this knowledge into the protection of future generations. Considerable research is needed to address current gaps on operationalization, conceptualization, measures and insight into the nature of trauma transmission in FDPs in the Americas. As migration, war and persecution of refugees continues [10, 53], IT will become central on the debate around how to protect future generations’ health and wellbeing. It is our hope that the presented research, recommendations and description of findings contribute in the advancement of programs and policies that improve the long-term health and well-being of FDPs in LAC.
