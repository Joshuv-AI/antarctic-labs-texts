Development of economic selection indexes for beef-on-dairy systems and implication for sire re-ranking in U.S. production systems
Authors: Larissa E Meier, Bruce L Golden, Scott Newman, Matthew L Spangler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid expansion of beef-on-dairy (B×D) crossbreeding in the United States has increased the need for selection tools tailored to this unique production system. The objectives of this study were to develop economic selection indexes specific to B×D systems under U.S. conditions and to evaluate the extent of sire re-ranking relative to conventional beef-on-beef (B×B) indexes. The current study utilized iGENDEC, a web-based decision support platform hosted by the Beef Improvement Federation. iGENDEC enables users to enter enterprise-specific information related to breeding goals, breeding systems, economic costs/returns, and current phenotypic performance. Using stochastic simulation, iGENDEC creates a simulated population informed by the user’s parameters to enable calculation of the marginal economic value of each trait in the specified breeding objective. Using iGENDEC, economic selection indexes were constructed for multiple crossbreeding scenarios incorporating traits related to carcass merit, feed intake, and calving performance. Beef bulls (n = 27,123) were evaluated to quantify differences in ranking across production systems and index assumptions. Results indicated that BxD systems placed greater economic emphasis on feed intake and hot carcass weight, while B×B systems placed more emphasis on carcass marbling. Despite strong Spearman rank correlations between indexes (average = 0.931), only 70.7% of the 100 top-ranked sires overlapped between B×D and B×B systems, indicating meaningful differences in sire selection. Differences in production assumptions, including carcass weight targets and days on feed, further influenced economic weights and selection outcomes. Opportunity costs associated with using B×B-based indexes in B×D systems averaged $3.24 per exposure with a maximum value of $8.26 per exposure. These findings demonstrate that even small differences in index construction can lead to suboptimal sire selection and reduced profitability. Consequently, selection indexes should be tailored to specific production environments to ensure alignment with economic drivers and to maximize genetic progress in beef-on-dairy systems.

Introduction

The use of artificial insemination to breed dairy cows with beef bulls has increased substantially (NAAB 2015, 2025). From 2015 to 2025, dairy breed semen sales decreased by 7,136,795 units, whereas beef semen sales increased by 7,177,158 units. This shift reflects fundamental restructuring of dairy breeding strategies, driven largely by improvements in reproductive efficiency and the widespread adoption of sexed semen technologies (Berry 2021; Poock and Beckett 2022). These advancements have enabled dairy producers to generate sufficient numbers of replacement heifers from elite matings, thereby creating an opportunity to breed the remaining proportion of the herd to beef sires. As a result, beef-on-dairy (B×D) crossbreeding has rapidly expanded and is now considered a strategic component of modern dairy production systems, rather than a secondary practice (Poock and Beckett 2022). The economic rationale for this shift is well documented. For example, crossbred B×D calves have higher market values than purebred dairy calves, which are often discounted due to inferior growth performance, lower muscling, and reduced carcass yield potential (Cabrera 2022; Ahmed et al. 2023; Jaborek et al. 2023). Beyond calf value, the integration of beef genetics into dairy systems has implications for the broader beef supply chain. The contribution of dairy-influenced cattle to the U.S. beef supply has increased over time due to declining beef cow numbers and changes in dairy management practices (Jaborek et al. 2023; USDA 2025) with evidence indicating comparable eating quality relative to conventional beef (Foraker et al. 2022).

A key advantage of B×D systems is the potential to exploit breed complementarity. Crossbreeding dairy cows with beef sires can improve carcass gain, carcass weight, and carcass conformation compared with pure dairy cattle. For example, Eriksson et al. (2020) reported improvements in carcass conformation (up to 67%) and carcass gain (up to 17%), although these benefits varied by sire breed and were accompanied by increased risk of calving difficulty. Although B×D systems improve growth and performance of traditional dairy calves, U.S. feedlot studies have shown that B×D cattle require extended days on feed to achieve greater carcass weights and marbling, thus reducing feed efficiency (Husz et al. 2024).

Despite the system-wide advantages of B×D crossbreeding systems, there are key trade-offs. Traits associated with calf value such as growth and carcass composition are often antagonistic to traits impacting the dam, including calving difficulty, gestation length, and stillbirths (Berry 2021; Ahmed et al. 2023). Late-maturing beef breeds can enhance carcass performance but increase dystocia risk and gestation length (Eriksson et al. 2004, 2020). Breed differences in calving ease and gestation length have been reported previously, highlighting the importance of breed choice in mitigating these risks (McGuirk et al. 1998; Ahlberg, 2016).

To manage these trade-offs, several studies have developed selection tools tailored to B×D systems in a European context. Berry et al. (2019) introduced a dairy-beef index (DBI) that integrated calving performance, carcass traits, and other economically relevant characteristics into a single selection criterion. Selection based on this index aims to maximize carcass output while minimizing negative impacts on the dairy dam. Subsequent validation studies confirmed that high-index sires produced progeny with superior carcass characteristics and profitability compared with selection based solely on calving traits (Berry and Ring 2020). More recently, decision-support tools incorporating both sire and dam information have been developed to optimize mating decisions and maximize expected profit while minimizing risk (Berry and McCarthy 2025).

Despite this progress, many important gaps remain. Most existing research has been conducted in European systems, which differ from the U.S. in breed composition, management practices, and economic conditions, limiting direct application. Additionally, relatively little U.S. based research has quantified economic sire selection for B×D systems or evaluated how B×D specific indexes may re-rank sires compared with traditional beef breeding objectives. Consequently, the objectives of the current study were to develop economic selection indexes tailored to the B×D breeding objective under U.S. conditions and to quantify the extent of re-ranking among selection candidates when evaluated under beef-on-beef versus beef-on-dairy index frameworks.

Materials and methods

Approval from the Animal Care and Use Committee was not needed as the data used were simulated, or obtained from breed association performance recording programs

Breeding objectives and index construction

This study was conducted using the Beef Improvement Federation (BIF) implementation (http://igendec.beefimprovement.org/) of iGENDEC (Spangler et al. 2022, https://github.com/blgolden/igendec), which facilitates construction of economic selection indexes tailored to U.S. beef production systems. This software enables users to enter herd-specific information, such as calf marketing endpoint, phenotypic means, breed composition, and relevant economic parameters, to generate indexes that reflect their production goals.

To guide index construction, breeding objectives were identified for B×D and beef × beef (B×B) systems. For B×D systems, all cattle were sold based on carcass merit. The breeding objectives for B×D and the resulting indexes included the following traits: hot carcass weight (HCW), ribeye area (REA), fat depth (FAT), marbling score (MS), feed intake (FI), and calving ease-direct (CE). For B×B systems, indexes were constructed under a terminal breeding objective, assuming animals were sold at slaughter based on carcass merit. The traits included in both the breeding objective and resulting indexes were HCW, REA, FAT, MS, weaning weight-direct (WW), yearling weight (YW), FI, and CE. Weaning weight and YW were included in only B×B indexes because, in conventional beef production systems, calves remain with the dam for a longer pre-weaning period, making these traits important indicators of pre-weaning feed intake and post-weaning gain. In contrast, dairy calves are typically separated from the dam shortly after birth, limiting the biological and economic relevance of WW and YW in B×D systems. Importantly, the economic consequences of calving difficulty included potential calf, and in extreme cases cow, death loss but did not include potential disruptions to lactation of the dairy female.

A total of six crossbreeding systems were evaluated, comprised four B×D and two B×B mating combinations. The B×D systems included Angus and Limousin sires mated to Holstein and Jersey cows, producing four distinct crosses: Angus × Holstein (A×H), Angus × Jersey (A×J), Limousin × Holstein (L×H), and Limousin × Jersey (L×J). The B×B systems consisted of Angus and Limousin sires mated to Angus × Hereford dams, resulting in two crosses: Angus × Angus-Hereford (A×AH) and Limousin × Angus-Hereford (L×AH). For these systems, sires were considered purebreds, while the dams were equal proportions of Angus and Hereford. Consequently, the expected breed composition of the offspring was one-half sire breed, one-quarter Angus, and one-quarter Hereford. Angus and Limousin were chosen as sire breeds to enable comparison of both British and Continental germplasm from breeds with distinct performance differences.

A total of six index scenarios (Table 1) were created to account for potential changes in the breeding goal. Scenario 1 represented baseline phenotypic means for each crossbreeding system as reported in Table 2. Phenotypic means were selected to represent contemporary estimates of performance and known breed differences (Jaborek et al. 2023; Basiel et al. 2024; Husz et al. 2024; Kuehn and Thallman 2024). In the case of B×D, baseline phenotypic means were assumed the same across sire and dam breeds to reflect a consistent carcass endpoint target and to enable comparisons when HCW and DOF were perturbed. Reproductive assumptions included a breeding season length of 360 days for the B×D systems and 60 days for the B×B systems with conception rate fixed at 0.90 for both systems. The distribution of cow age within the simulated herds is presented in Table 3. For the B×D systems, days on feed (DOF) was assumed to be 322 days (Jaborek et al. 2023; Basiel et al. 2024; Husz et al. 2024) after a 140-day period at a calf ranch. For the B×B systems, cattle were assumed to be weaned at an average age of 205 days before undergoing a 120-day backgrounding period followed by 170 days on feed (Kansas State University, Department of Animal Sciences and Industry 2025). Given the value of HCW is non-linear in some U.S. markets, discounting animals above a certain HCW threshold, subsequent scenarios investigated increased mean HCW both with and without discounts for heavy carcass weights. Additionally, adjustments were made to DOF and the resulting average daily gain (ADG). In scenarios two and four, all progeny from each B×D crossbreeding system were maintained at the baseline parameter of 322 DOF, resulting in ADG values of 0.76 kg/day and 1.04 kg/day, respectively. In scenarios three and five, DOF were reduced according to dam breed, with progeny from Holstein dams assigned 240 DOF and progeny from Jersey dams assigned 300 DOF. These reductions in DOF resulted in increased ADG from 0.76 kg/day in scenario two to 1.02 kg/day for Holstein progeny and 0.81 kg/day for Jersey progeny. Similarly, in scenario five, ADG values increased to 1.39 kg/day for Holstein progeny and 1.11 kg/day for Jersey progeny. Scenario 6 was identical to scenario 4 with the except for the fact that there was no discount for heavy weight carcasses.

Economic parameters

Grid premiums and discounts (Table 4) (USDA 2025), and carcass weight pricing (Table 5) (USDA 2025) were obtained from 10-year averages of reported USDA pricing data. The cost of feedlot rations for both production systems was assumed to be $0.0348/kg on a dry matter basis (Kansas State University, Department of Animal Sciences and Industry 2025). For B×D and B×B systems, cull cow price was set at $1.72/kg (LMIC 2025). Costs at the calf ranch were valued at $94.00 per calf. For the B×B systems annual per cow variable cost were estimated at $579.96 per AUM (USDA ERS 2025) and backgrounded calf cost was set at $48.00 per head per month.

Using these parameters, the marginal economic value (MEV) of each trait was calculated via iGENDEC by creating a simulated cattle population and perturbing the genetic values of sires by one unit, one trait at a time, following similar approaches used by others (MacNeil et al. 1994). Relative emphasis (RE) of each trait was calculated by taking the absolute value of the MEV multiplied by the genetic standard deviation then divided by the sum of this product for all of the traits in the breeding objective (Ochsner et al. 2017).

Selection candidate re-ranking

A list of 27,123 beef bulls provided by the American Hereford Association with estimated progeny differences (EPD) were used to investigate potential re-ranking of selection candidates. Selection index values for each sire breed/dam breed (n = 6) and scenario (n = 6) combination (36 in total) for each bull were calculated by multiplying the MEV of each trait by the corresponding EPD and summing these products across all traits. Following index calculation, Spearman rank correlations were calculated using R v4.5 (R Core Team 2026) to evaluate the similarity of bull rankings within and across production systems and index scenarios. Additionally, the commonality among the top 100 selection candidates was investigated to determine if selection decisions would differ. Specifically, the top 100 bulls ranked under each B×B index were compared to the top 100 bulls ranked under each B×D index.

Opportunity cost was also estimated using the calculated index values for each scenario. To do this, the B×D index values of the top 100 bulls ranked based on their B×B index were compared to the B×D index values of the top 100 bulls ranked based on their B×D index values. The difference between these two groups, in terms of their B×D index values, was used to estimate the average opportunity cost associated with selecting bulls based on a B×B index for use in B×D production systems.

Results and discussion

Production system differences (B×B vs B×D)

Averaged across sire breeds and scenarios, differences in MEV between B×D and B×B systems were greatest for HCW, FI, and MS. B×D indexes also resulted in larger differences for FAT and REA, compared with B×B indexes. Despite larger magnitudes of economic weight for HCW, REA, and FAT, relative emphasis was lower for B×D than B×B for these traits because of the substantial influence of FI in B×D breeding objectives of nearly $50 for every 1 kg/day change (Table 6). Consequently, FI was associated with the largest relative emphasis for B×D systems, whereas HCW was associated with the largest relative emphasis in B×B systems. The relative emphasis of MS for B×B objectives was 16.7%, while the same value for B×D indexes was only 12.8%. These results indicate that B×D progeny represent a biologically and economically distinct production type in which profitability is more strongly driven by efficiency-related traits and less by incremental improvements in carcass quality attributes.

The greater MEV observed for HCW and REA in B×D systems is consistent with selection for increased feedlot gain and carcass yield. Dairy cattle are generally characterized by a lower dressing percentage and reduced muscling compared with beef breeds (Foraker et al. 2022; Jaborek et al. 2023). Improvements in muscling, is this case via selection for increased REA, contribute more directly to carcass yield in B×D cattle that originate from a lower baseline for meat yield. This is supported by previous research demonstrating improvements in carcass characteristics from B×D crossbreeding. For example, B×D progeny exhibit increased carcass weight and conformation scores relative to dairy × dairy (DxD) cattle, although the B×D calves remained inferior to their B×B counterparts (Twomey et al. 2020). Using large-scale slaughter data and accounting for genetic merit of both sire and dam, they showed that residual differences in carcass performance persist between animals born in dairy versus beef systems, suggesting that early life management and inherent biological differences also contribute to these outcomes.

In contrast, B×D systems exhibited lower economic values and reduced relative emphasis for MS compared with B×B systems. Dairy genetics are associated with greater intramuscular fat deposition and a higher likelihood achieving acceptable or premium quality grades (Boykin et al. 2017). This tendency is largely attributed to differences in energy allocation, where DxD cattle deposit fat earlier and are more consistent than B×B cattle. Consequently, B×D cattle may already meet key market thresholds for marbling, reducing the marginal economic benefit for further improvements in MS.

Differences in FI and its economic importance between production systems were closely tied to assumptions regarding DOF and growth performance. Although both B×B and B×D animals have similar daily feed intake (Berry 2021), B×D cattle are commonly associated with extended feeding durations and greater cumulative feed requirements over the finishing period (Foraker et al. 2022; Husz 2024). Therefore, the economic weight assigned to FI is driven primarily by total feed consumed rather that daily intake and is directly influenced by the duration and efficiency of growth. Interestingly, Berry et al. (2019), developed a dairy-beef index incorporating feed intake, carcass traits, and calving traits and reported economic values of HCW and FI that, when converted to RE, accounted for more than half of the total breeding objective. The RE values of 0.432 and 0.192 for HCW and FI, respectively, derived from the study by Berry et al. (2019) differ from those reported herein. Although the sum of the RE is similar, in the present study the average RE for HCW and FI were 0.304 and 0.386, respectively. This agreement, in terms of the sum of RE for HCW and FI, reinforces the importance of both intake and carcass weight as primary drivers of profitability in B×D systems. However, the differences in relative importance between the current study and that of Berry et al. (2019) for the two traits individually reflects the need for system-specific indexes to account for differences in economic parameters as well as general production system differences.

Although both the economic value and relative emphasis for CE were slightly lower in the average B×D index than the average B×B and CE exhibited the lowest RE among all breeding goal traits, its inclusion remains critical due to its direct impact on the dairy dam. The importance of CE on dairy production is well supported in literature. Beef × dairy crossbreeding has been associated with increased gestation length, dystocia, and stillbirth risk compared with DxD matings (Ahmed et al. 2023). Furthermore, genetic correlations indicate antagonist relationships between calving performance and carcass traits, where selection for increased carcass weight and muscling can lead to increased calving difficulty (Eriksson et al. 2004). Despite these trade-offs, appropriate sire selection can mitigate negative impacts. Studies have shown that selecting beef sires with favorable CE can reduce dystocia risk while still achieving improvements in carcass merit (Eriksson et al. 2020). Collectively, these results indicate that while CE plays an essential role in risk mitigation within B×D systems, it contributes minimally to overall profit variation relative to economically dominant traits such as HCW and FI. As such, CE functions primarily as a constraint to prevent adverse outcomes rather than a trait that drives economic gain. Selection decisions must therefore ensure adequate risk mitigation through CE while maintaining emphasis on terminal performance traits that determine carcass value and feeding efficiency.

Sire breed differences

In addition to the impact of production systems, sire breed influenced both MEV and RE across index scenarios and production systems (Table 6). Breeding objectives using Angus sires exhibited greater MEV and RE for REA, but lower values for MS compared with Limousin sires. These differences reflect underlying biological distinctions between sire breeds. Based on breed of sire means reported by the U.S. Meat Aniaml Research Center (USMARC), Angus cattle are associated with superior marbling (6.26 vs 5.28 MS Units; Kuehn and Thallman 2024). Therefore, for enterprises using Angus sires, an additional unit of MS contributes less incremental profit, resulting in smaller MEV and RE for MS. Conversely, Limousin sires excel in carcass yield traits such as larger REA (94.00 vs 88.52 cm2) and less fat (1.35 vs 1.69 cm; Kuehn and Thallman 2024), which reduces the selection emphasis for these traits compared to their Angus-sired contemporaries.

Economic weights and relative emphasis for HCW, FI, early growth traits (WW and YW), and CE were largely consistent between Angus and Limousin sires. Regardless of sire breed, HCW and FI maintain substantial relative emphasis, reinforcing their role in determining profitability in terminal selection indexes. Conversely, WW and YW were associated with small economic weights (negative) and relative emphasis. This agrees with MacNeil et al. (1994) who demonstrated that early growth traits often have reduced economic important when compared to later performance.

Production assumption differences

Averaged across production system (B×B vs B×D) and sire breed, increasing mean HCW from the baseline values in Table 1 (Scenario 1) to the scenarios where HCW was increased to 397 kg (Scenario 2) and 454 kg (Scenario 4) resulted in a greater economic penalty for FAT, as indicated by more negative MEVs (Table 7). The MEV for REA and MS increased from Scenario 1 to Scenario 2 and then plateaued in Scenario 4 (Table 7). Realtive emphasis increased for REA, MS, and FAT. These changes indicate a shift in economic value towards carcass composition rather than additional weight gain. As mean HCW approached the heavy-weight discount threshold (499 + kg), both economic weights and relative emphasis for HCW declined, reflecting diminishing economic returns to further increases in carcass weight. In Scenario 6, where the heavy-weight discount was removed, MEV and RE for HCW increased and exceeded that observed in Scenarios 2 and 4, demonstrating the sensitivity of HCW value to pricing structure. These findings are consistent with previous work demonstrating that the economic importance of HCW is dependent on both population means and pricing structure. Valasek et al. (2024) evaluated how changes in mean HCW relative to a discount threshold influenced the relative emphasis and reported that emphasis declined as the mean approached the threshold and increased again once the threshold was exceeded.

When averaged across sire breeds and production systems, differences in DOF were evaluated by comparing changes in MEV and RE when DOF was 322 (Scenarios 2 and 4) to the scenarios when DOF was reduced to 300 and 240 for the offspring for Holstein and Jersey dams, respectively (Scenarios 3 and 5). As DOF decreased, economic weights for FI substantially shifted, while MEVs for all other traits remained relatively stable. Reducing DOF decreased the magnitude of economic values for FI while increasing the relative importance of carcass traits such as HCW, FAT, and MS. This shift indicates that shorter feeding periods reduce the economic burden of intake, while placing greater emphasis on traits that influence carcass weight and composition at harvest.

Previous research has demonstrated that extending DOF increases HCW, REA, and MS, but reduces feed efficiency, highlighting the biological and economic trade-offs associated with feeding duration (Husz et al. 2024). Taken together, these results indicate that variation in DOF alters the economic importance of intake compared to carcass traits, reinforcing the role of feeding duration as a key driver of selection emphasis in terminal breeding.

Re-ranking, selection implications, and opportunity cost

Taken together, the results of this study demonstrate that production system, sire breed, dam breed, and production assumptions all influence the economic importance of traits within selection indexes. Consequently, selection decisions based on conventional beef breeding objectives may not fully capture the economic drivers of B×D systems. It is evident that selection indexes should be tailored to specific production environments, and failure to do so may result in misaligned selection decisions and reduced selection efficiency. To further illustrate the importance of this approach, rank correlations, selection candidate overlap, and opportunity cost were evaluated to quantify the implications of misaligned selection outcomes.

Spearman rank correlations between B×B and B×D indexes averaged 0.933 for Angus-sired systems and 0.928 for Limousin-sired systems. Within scenario, correlations between B×B and B×D indexes ranged from 0.856 to 1, with the lowest values observed in Scenario 1 when A×AH was compared with all B×D crossbreeding systems (Fig. 1). Although these values indicate strong overall agreement, they do not imply identical selection decisions. Even small differences in rank can substantially influence selection decisions, particularly when selection intensity is high.

Heat map of Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients among indexes for each scenario (Sc) and sire breed (A, Angus; L, Limousin) and dam breed (AH, Angus × Hereford; J, Jersey; H, Holstein) combination.

To further assess the impact of these differences on practical sire selection, the proportion of overlapping bulls was evaluated. Averaged across sire breed and scenarios, 70.7% of bulls were common between B×B and B×D indexes. When averaged across sire breeds, within-scenario comparisons between B×B and B×D crosses showed overlap ranging from 54% to 86%. The least overlap occurred in Scenario 4 where comparisons of L×AH to A×H, L×H, A×J, and L×J ranged from 54% to 55%. Differences in top ranking bulls between L×AH and the average B×D cross in Scenario 4 were primarily driven by substantial divergence in MEVs for MS (74.220 vs 55.984 $/MS Unit) and FI (−51.846 vs −111.547 $/kg/day). The greatest overlap was observed in Scenarios 3 and 6 for comparisons between A×AH to A×H and L×H. The similarities were largely associated with reduced DOF in B×D crosses in these scenarios. Animals from Holstein dams had 240 DOF, while those from Jersey dams had 300 DOF. Given FI is a key driver of profitability, larger reductions in DOF for the A×H and LxH animals effectively decreased their feed related penalties. This shift made B×D animals more comparable to B×B animals in overall economic performance, contributing to greater similarity in index values and increased overall sire rankings. This pattern is further supported by the trait differences observed within these scenarios. In Scenario 3, differences between traits were minimal, except for MS (35.988 and 49.791 $/MS Units) and FI (−52.137 and −83.048 $/kg/day). A similar trend was observed in Scenario 6, where largest differences again occurred in MS (39.659 and 55.959 $/MS Units) and FI (−52.112 and −83.047 $/kg/day). When averaged across sire breed scenarios, Scenario 1 had the lowest overlap (60.5%), whereas Scenario 6 had the highest (81%). The contrast in Scenario 1 is likely driven by differences in the baseline phenotypic means used to parameterize the index (Table 1). These pronounced differences in economically relevant traits led to greater divergence in sire rankings, and consequently, lower overlap. In contrast, Scenario 6 was defined by more similar phenotypic assumptions, including a standardized HCW of 474 kg and reduced DOF for B×D animals. Because HCW and DOF are key drivers of profitability, the increased similarity resulted in more comparable index values, leading to higher overlap in the bulls selected. These results demonstrate that despite high rank correlations, meaningful re-ranking occurs that can alter sire selection. Differences not only between production systems, but also among scenarios, suggests that reliance on indexes that are not aligned with the target production system may lead to suboptimal genetic selection decisions and reduced long term genetic improvement.

Similar patterns have been reported in previous studies evaluating differences in selection decisions when using competing selection indexes. For example, Valasek et al. (2024) evaluated selection indexes across different planning horizons and market endpoints. They reported high overall rank correlations (r = 0.96) when averaged across planning horizons and sale endpoints, but correlations declined (r = 0.58 to 0.92) as assumptions diverged. Within the same slaughter endpoint, overlap among top selection candidates ranged from 68.9% to 76.5%, indicating substantial re-ranking despite generally strong correlations. Collectively, these findings reinforce the notion that moderate to high correlations can mask important differences in selection outcomes when breeding objectives differ.

Opportunity cost associated with using B×B-based selection indexes to choose sires to use in B×D systems averaged $3.24 per exposure and ranged from $0.79 to $8.26 per exposure across all system/sire breed/scenario combinations. The lowest opportunity cost was observed in Scenario 3 when bulls selected using the A×AH index were applied to A×H and L×H systems, whereas the highest cost occurred in scenario 1 when bulls selected using the L×AH index were applied to A×H, A×J, L×H, and L×J systems ($8.25 to $8.26). When averaged within scenarios, Scenario 1 resulted in the greatest opportunity cost ($7.18), whereas Scenario 3 resulted in the lowest ($1.53). The differences were primarily driven by divergence in economic weights for MS and FI, which carry substantial economic weight within the index. In Scenario 1, large differences in economic values for MS (53.272 vs 41.904 $/MS Unit) and FI (−51.996 and −111.642 $/kg/day) contributed to greater misalignment in selection decisions, whereas Scenario 3 exhibited more similar values for MS (56.939 vs 55.984 $/MS Unit) and FI (−51.959 vs −93.473 $/kg/day), resulting in reduced opportunity cost. Although these values appear modest on a per exposure basis, they can accumulate substantial economic penalties for having used an index not optimized for the target breeding objective.

Conclusions

The results of this study demonstrate that sire breed, production assumptions, and production system have a substantial impact on the economic importance of traits within selection indexes. In B×D systems, profitability was more strongly influenced by carcass weight and feed intake, whereas B×B systems maintained greater emphasis on marbling. Additionally, changes in production assumptions, particularly carcass weight targets, pricing thresholds, and days on feed resulted in meaningful shifts in trait emphasis, highlighting the sensitivity of index construction to economic and management conditions. Differences in index weightings translated to measurable changes in sire selection decisions, as evidenced by less than perfect overlap in selected sires despite generally high rank correlations. These differences resulted in opportunity costs when B×B based selection indexes were applied to B×D systems, demonstrating that even small discrepancies in index construction can influence genetic progress and profitability. Collectively, these findings emphasize that selection indexes should be tailored to specific production environments to ensure alignment with system-level objectives. As beef-on-dairy production continues to expand in the U.S., the development and application of system-specific selection tools will be critical for optimizing genetic improvement, improving economic efficiency, and ensuring long-term sustainability across both dairy and beef production sectors.
