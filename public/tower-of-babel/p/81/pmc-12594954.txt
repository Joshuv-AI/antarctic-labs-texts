Electrochemical deutero-(di)carboxylations for the preparation of deuterium-labeled medicinal building blocks
Authors: Subhojit Mondal, Sahil, Alex Brown, Michael W. Meanwell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sacrificial anodes have been broadly deployed in electro-synthesis for the development of reductive electrosynthetic reactions. The metal cations released from sacrificial anodes during these processes are widely believed to not affect reaction outcomes. Here, we disclose an electrochemical deutero-(di)carboxylation of acetylenes and cinnamic acids that in fact relies on anodically generated Mg2+ cations to achieve regioselective α-carboxylation to afford deuterated malonic acids with precise control over both the site and amount of deuteration. The unusual, beneficial role of Mg2+ cations on product selectivity is supported by mechanistic studies and density functional theory [ZORA-B3LYP-D3BJ/def2-TZVP/DMF(SMD)] calculations, and is believed to mimic enzymatic α-carboxylation mechanisms. The deuteration patterns in the malonic acid products can be precisely controlled, providing a platform for the concise synthesis of high-value β-d₂- and β-d₁-α-amino acid analogs, as well as other precisely deuterated frameworks.

Introduction

The use of sacrificial anodes has been instrumental in advancing modern electrosynthetic chemistry, playing a crucial role in avoiding side reactions caused by anodic oxidation or the addition of chemical reductants in electroreductive reactions1–5. Conventionally, sacrificial anodes readily undergo oxidation where they release seemingly innocuous metal cations into the reaction mixture, ultimately resulting in cathodic chemistry driving the overall reaction outcome1–5. Though it has been long established that the metal cations released from sacrificial anodes are inconsequential, rare examples have demonstrated that these cations can, in fact, have either non-innocent6–10 or beneficial effects11–15 on product formation. Stahl and coworkers recently studied the non-innocent roles sacrificial anodes play in electrochemical cross-electrophile coupling reactions, finding that the metal cations generated from sacrificial anode oxidation contribute to the formation of undesired side products derived from homocoupling and protodehalogenation6. In 1984, Silvestri and coworkers were among the first to document beneficial effects of anode-derived cations while studying the electrochemical carboxylation of benzal chloride to α-chlorophenylacetic acid. They discovered aluminum cations released from a sacrificial aluminum anode prevented the formation of mandelic acid side products by coordinating with the intermediate carboxylate anions11. In the decades since this discovery, the beneficial roles of anode-derived cations have been occasionally noted or exploited in electrosynthesis.

We wondered if chelation-control by the metal cations generated from a sacrificial anode could be used to strategically alter the product selectivity in electrochemical hydrocarboxylations of unsaturated hydrocarbons. Metal cations play essential roles in mediating enzymatic CO2 fixation, which inspired us to consider this possibility16–19. In carboxylase-catalyzed carboxylation of carbonyls, (1) bivalent metal cofactors, such as Mg2+, serve to not only stabilize the formed enolate but to also activate CO2 (2) for α-carboxylation through a six-membered transition state to form 1,3-dicarbonyl products (3) (see Fig. 1A)16,17. Carboxylase catalyzed ortho-carboxylation of phenols proceeds through similar mechanisms that rely on metal cation cofactors to activate and direct regioselective ortho-carboxylation with CO218–20. In fact, this biosynthetic pathway for ortho-carboxylation is mimicked in the Kolbe-Schmitt ortho-carboxylation of phenols where the sodium counterion of the base coordinates with CO2 (5) to form a six-membered transition state and direct selective ortho-carboxylation over para-carboxylation (see Fig. 1A; 4 → 6)14,21–23. While Nature has evolved mechanisms for performing regioselective CO2 fixation in the presence of water, rare examples of such carboxylation reactions exist in the realm of synthetic chemistry24–29. To date, the product selectivity in electrochemical hydrocarboxylation reactions has been largely dictated by the intrinsic electronics of the substrate and the relative concentrations of H2O and CO2 employed in the reaction. While these determining factors are often difficult to control to achieve high product selectivity, a few elegant examples have been reported for the selective hydrocarboxylation of certain unsaturated hydrocarbons such as styrenes26,28, cinnamate esters29, and conjugated dienes27. In contrast, electrochemical hydrocarboxylations of aryl acetylenes are underexplored30, while hydrocarboxylations of cinnamic acids have never been investigated. In this present study, we introduce the use of anodically generated Mg2+ cations as an element of regiocontrol in electrochemical deutero-carboxylations of aryl acetylenes (7) and cinnamic acids (8) to access an array of precisely deuterated malonic acids (Fig. 1B; 9–12). Such precisely deuterated building blocks are valuable for site-selective deuterium incorporation in drug development as it avoids problematic isotopic contaminations that would otherwise result from using unselective hydrogen isotope exchange protocols32–34. The burgeoning interest around deuterium in drug discovery32,35 and other fields36 (e.g., mechanism elucidation) has created a need for new synthetic methods and deuterium-labeled building blocks that allow for its precise introduction into biologically relevant molecules37–42. Ultimately, these deuterated malonic acid building blocks (9–12) provide direct entry to not only various deuterium-labeling patterns in natural amino acids (13) but also to unnatural amino acids containing deuterium labels. This approach helps address synthetic challenges associated with site-selective generation of amino acid isotopologs43. Other deuterated carbon frameworks (14) relevant to synthesis in drug discovery can also be accessed from malonic acid building blocks (9–12).

Results

Optimization

We commenced our study by assessing the carboxylation of 4-ethynyltoluene (15) in the absence of D2O to understand the factors (i.e., temperature, electrode, current, solvent, etc) affecting product selectivity. The initial conditions employed a platinum cathode and magnesium as a sacrificial anode immersed in a DMF solution containing 15 and the TBABF4 electrolyte. This reaction mixture was electrolyzed with a 16 mA current for 4.5 F/mol while CO2 gas was bubbled into the reaction mixture, an open system, at room temperature. Under these conditions, 15 was converted into a product mixture of 1,1-dicarboxylic acid (16), 1,1,2-tricarboxylic acid (17), and 1,2-dicarboxylic acid (18) (see Table 1 A). Simply heating the reaction mixture to 65 °C greatly improved the product distribution to 12:1:1 (16:17:18) (see Supplementary Information for full optimization). At higher temperature, CO2 concentration decreases in the reaction medium thus disfavouring the formation of 17 and 18 which we presumed were products of undirected carboxylation and thus their formation was more likely to be impacted by a lower CO2 concentration. Finally, changing the cathode to a cobalt electrode and doping 0.50 vol% of H2O into the reaction mixture established the optimal conditions. For this next series of optimizations, anhydrous DMF was used to help maximize deuterium incorporation. The addition of 1 vol% D2O resulted in the formation of the 1,1-dicarboxylic acid 19 in a 6:1 ratio with the 1-monocarboxylic acid 20 (see Table 1B entry 2). Though the yield (64%) and product distribution were favourable, the % deuterium incorporation was only 75% at the benzylic position. In contrast, adding 2 vol% D2O led to complete deuteration (100%) and selective formation of the 1-monocarboxylic acid, as well as products derived from hydrogenation, in very poor yield (see Table 1B entry 3). In general, adding too much D₂O resulted in deuterons outcompeting CO₂ as electrophilic traps for the anionic intermediates and led to unproductive D₂O reduction dominating the reactivity by suppressing other productive pathways. In contrast, using too little D₂O resulted in poor deuterium incorporation. Eventually, through screening increments of D2O between 1 vol% and 2 vol%, 1.6 vol% was found to deliver the ideal compromise as 19 was afforded in 64% yield with 94% deuterium incorporation and excellent regioselectivity (Table 1B entry 1). Increasing the current from 16 mA to 19 mA resulted in a modest improvement in the regioselectivity and product yield (see Table 1B entry 5). Employing a zinc anode instead of magnesium (entry 6) led to selective product formation but in poor yield.

Mechanistic investigation

We sought to understand the factors leading to the selective formation of malonic acid 19 over the three other possible products. The first carboxylation event may occur via two plausible mechanisms (Fig. 2A): 1) radical β-addition of CO2• − into 7 (route A) or 2) alkyne reduction followed by electrophilic CO2 trapping of the formed radical anion 22 (route B). Route B appears unlikely to be the operative pathway since competitive trapping between D2O and CO2 would lead to significantly decreased yields of malonic acid (9) and products derived from deuterogenation. Further, we verified intermediate styrene (not shown) resulting from trapping with D2O cannot lead to the formation of malonic acid (9). In considering route A, we conducted cyclic voltammetry (CV) of CO2 in DMF but failed to observe the CO2 reduction peak (see Fig. 2A). GC-MS headspace analysis of the reaction mixture revealed oxalate formation (see Supplementary Information for details), a product derived from CO2 dimerization which only occurs in the presence of CO2• −, therefore supporting that route A is indeed possible. However, the cyclic voltammogram of 4-butylphenylacetylene (21) changed significantly when in the presence of CO2. On its own, the reduction of 21 shows an irreversible peak at -2.05 V (vs Ag/AgCl) but when the reduction of 21 is carried in a DMF solution saturated with CO2 two irreversible peaks appear at -1.88 V (vs Ag/AgCl) and -2.11 V (vs Ag/AgCl)44. This observation may be explained by two successive reductions of adduct 25 leading to radical 22 which subsequently undergoes a second single electron transfer (SET) reduction followed by rapid protonation (route C). The formation of 25 itself is postulated to occur through π-π interaction between a π* orbital of CO2 and a π orbital of the alkyne45. Such interactions between CO2 and π-systems have been previously proposed and would be expected to have a less negative reduction potential relative to 21. Using the ZORA-B3LYP-D3BJ/def2-TZVP level of theory in DMF (using SMD)46–54, we computed this π-π interaction between CO2 and the alkyne (see 25 A) results in a stabilization energy of 13.3 kJ/mol, further supporting the presence of this interaction (see Supplementary Dataset). Taken all together, while each mechanism is plausible, route C appears to be the most likely pathway for the first carboxylation event given the CV data and the supporting computational data. Regardless of which of these proposed pathways is actually operative, they all lead to a cinnamic acid intermediate. The intermediacy of cinnamic acid was validated by transforming cinnamic acid 26 into malonic acid 16 (Fig. 2B) where malonic acid 16 was formed selectively over succinic acid 18 and hydrocinnamic acid 27 side-products. Presumably, this transformation occurs through reduction of the cinnamic acid and subsequent electrophilic trapping of the radical anion with CO2. Running the same reaction but instead with ethyl cinnamate led to a nearly identical result where α-carboxylation was once again favored (not shown, see Supplementary Information). This result contrasts strongly to work from Buckley and coworkers where their electrochemical hydrocarboxylation of cinnamate esters proceeds with complete reversal of selectivity (β-carboxylation)29. The key difference between our conditions and Buckley’s is that we deploy a magnesium sacrificial anode, which is likely responsible for the different selectivity. Under these conditions (Fig. 2B; entry 2), no malonic acid 16 was produced as only 18 and 27 were observed in the crude 1H NMR. These contrasting experimental results support our initial hypothesis that anodically generated Mg2+ cations could exert chelation control, via a six- membered transition state 29 that directs α-carboxylation through magnesium cation chelation to the formed carboxylate and the incoming CO2 molecule, as a way of altering product selectivity (Fig. 2C). The energy barrier of the proposed 6-membered transition state (29/29 A), possessing a planar conformation, was estimated computationally (ZORA-B3LYP-D3BJ/def2-TZVP level of theory in DMF (using SMD))46–54 to be 24.0 kcal/mol, an easily surmountable barrier at the reaction temperature. To further study the effects of anodically generated Mg2+, we carried out our reaction in the presence of well-known magnesium cation chelators 18-crown-6 and citric acid to reduce the amount of “free” Mg2+ in the reaction mixture and analyzed their effects on the product distribution for substrates 35, 36, and 3755,56. For each of these substrates, the addition of 18-crown-6 (1 equiv.) was found to greatly decrease the amount of 1,1-dicarboxylic acid product (32) formed, shifting the product distribution towards the 1,2-dicarboxylic acid (33) and mono-carboxylic acid (34) side-products (see Fig. 2D). The same effect on product distribution was observed upon adding increasing amounts of citric acid (see Fig. 2E). While in all cases a significant shift in product distribution was observed upon addition of either 18-crown-6 or citric acid, the overall yields, taken as the combined yield of all carboxylated products, remained mostly unaffected. There is no denying that the intrinsic electronics of the substrates factor into the product distribution; however, based on our investigations, magnesium cation chelation plays an important role in altering the product selectivity towards favor 1,1-dicarboxylation.

Substrate scope

The reaction’s broad applicability is shown in Fig. 3. A wide range of aryl and heteroaryl acetylenes underwent successful dicarboxylative deuteration to afford (hetero)benzylic-d2 malonic acids with good-to-excellent deuterium incorporation. Electron-neutral and electron-rich arenes were both well-tolerated and proceeded with excellent selectivity for the formation of the 1,1-dicarboxylic acid over the monocarboxylic acid and the 1,2-dicarboxylic acid side-products. For electron poor arenes, we observed increased 1,2-dicarboxylic acid formation presumably due to the stabilization of electron density at the benzylic position in the intermediate radical anion 40 (see Fig. 2F). This reaction proved to be broadly tolerant of variety of functional groups including halogens (45, 60), esters (48, 72, 73), amides (50, 67, 69, 70), nitriles (46), unactivated alkenes/alkynes (72, 73) and heterocycles (61 – 63, 65, 66, 68, 71). Our protocol can be modified, without significant changes to reaction outcome, to transform TMS-protected aryl acetylenes into benzylic-d2 malonic acids (not shown, see Supplementary Information), albeit in modest yields, thus obviating the need for a separate deprotection step. In all cases, we observed partial α-deuteration (28-92%); however, owing to its inherent acidity, the deuterium incorporation at the α-position can be precisely controlled by changing the reaction workup. Mild heating with acetic acid-d4 and D2O of the isolated product in MeCN and subsequent solvent evaporation afforded up to 96% α-deuterium incorporation. Alternatively, carrying out the same workup with acetic acid and H2O instead, led to 0% deuterium incorporation at the α-position. Replacing D2O with H2O (0.25 vol%) in our reaction, followed by a workup with acetic acid-d4, preferentially afforded malonic acids containing only α-deuteration in excellent yields and %D incorporation (93-95%). Based on our mechanistic work demonstrating that cinnamic acids could undergo monocarboxylative deuteration, we envisioned that such a strategy could provide broad access, through precise control over the amount of deuterium incorporated at the benzylic position, to benzylic-d1 malonic acids. Towards this end, a series of benzylic–d1 malonic acid derivatives (see Fig. 4A) were synthesized with excellent %D incorporation (86-96%) from their corresponding cinnamic acids. This reaction proceeded with good selectivity for α-carboxylation over β-carboxylation.

Despite their propensity to undergo proto-decarboxylation, malonic acids and their derivatives can be versatile building blocks for diversification. We envisioned our deuterated malonic acids as precursors for the preparation of β-d2 – and β-d1 – α-amino acid analogs, whose broad applications in biochemistry, mechanism elucidation, and drug discovery are attracting increased attention32–43. Recent strategies towards accessing β-deutero-α-amino acids have involved transition-metal catalyzed deuteration of amidoacrylates to afford α,β-dideuterated α-amino acids57,58. As an alternative approach, Buller reported a site-selective enzymatic C-H deuteration that allowed for the introduction of deuterium at β- or α-positions43. Uniquely, our route allows direct access to different deuterium labeling patterns in α-amino acids through a short synthetic sequence (Fig. 4B). Following DCC coupling with L-menthol, 77 was then treated with phosphoryl azide to form the acyl azide which upon heating undergoes a Curtius rearrangement and subsequent trapping with benzyl alcohol to afford deuterated Cbz-protected α-amino esters (78) in excellent overall yield and ~ 1:1 diastereomeric ratio. Prior to ester hydrolysis of 78 with LiOH, it is potentially possible to separate out the diastereomers in 78 to afford enantiopure N-Cbz amino acid analogs (79) upon deprotection. Both β-d2 – α- amino acid analogs (82–85) and β-d1 – α-amino acid analogs (86–90) were prepared in good-to-excellent yields and %D incorporation. Overall, our process employs inexpensive and accessible reagents for making high value deuterated amino acids (see Fig. 4B; 80 ($431/100 mg) and 81 ($721/1 g)).

In a broader context, deuterium labeling at (hetero)benzylic positions in medicinally relevant molecules offers an effective strategy to prevent undesired metabolic oxidation, as C-D bonds are more stable than C-H bonds, making them less prone to oxidative processes32,35. Given the overwhelming popularity of alkyl substituted -aryl and -heteroaryl scaffolds in drug discovery, building blocks containing (hetero)benzylic deuteration, while also having synthetic handles for diversification, provide a modular platform for incorporating (hetero)benzylic-d2 motifs into compound libraries. Towards this end, we performed a non-exhaustive set of simple transformations that would allow for facile incorporation of the (hetero)benzylic-d2 fragment into a variety of medicinally relevant frameworks (Fig. 5). Following optimization (see Supplementary Information for details), we synthesized half-malonic -ester (95), - amide (92), and -thioester (94) through a modified DCC coupling protocol. These products themselves were converted into amido-alcohol (93) and NHS-ester (96). Additionally, lithium aluminum hydride reduction of the malonic acid led to diol 91. In summary, we have developed an electrochemical (di)carboxylative deuteration of alkynes and cinnamic acids that engenders access to a variety of deuteration patterns, with precise control of the site and amount, in malonic acids. Supported by mechanistic and computational studies, the anodically generated magnesium cations uniquely influence the observed product selectivity by directing α-carboxylation through a six-membered transition state. This protocol enables the synthesis of a wide range of deuterated building blocks and deuterated products, including high-value deuterated amino acid analogues with excellent % deuterium incorporation. This sustainable approach, utilizing synthetic CO2 fixation and D2O as an affordable deuterium source, serves as a unique example of bioinspired electrochemical CO2 valorization, mimicking the action of carboxylase enzymes to achieve high product selectivity.

Methods

General procedure A for deutero-dicarboxylation of aryl acetylenes

A 5 mL ElectraSyn vial was charged with (hetero)arene acetylene (0.25 mmol) and tetrabutylammonium tetrafluoroborate (0.25 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) in dry DMF (3.0 mL) and D2O (48 μL, 1.6 vol%). The vial was then capped with an ElectraSyn vial cap equipped with a cobalt cathode and a magnesium anode and the reaction mixture was stirred until it became homogenous. After attaching it to an IKA ElectraSyn GOGO Module, the ElectraSyn vial was carefully immersed in an oil bath heated to 65 °C. The reaction mixture was then electrolyzed under a constant current of 19 mA for 4.5 F/mol. A balloon filled with CO2 was used to bubble CO2 into the reaction mixture for the duration of the electrolysis. Note: For certain substrates with electron-withdrawing groups and some electron-donating groups, we found a constant current of 16 mA performed better than 19 mA and these are indicated in the procedures below. Following electrolysis, the ElectraSyn vial cap was removed, and the electrodes were rinsed with ethyl acetate (ca. 2 mL). The reaction mixture was then transferred to a separatory funnel, diluted with 10 mL of ethyl acetate, and washed with 3 M HCl. The organic layers were dried over Na2SO4, filtered, concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by silica flash chromatography as indicated.

General procedure B for deutero-carboxylation of cinnamic acids

A 5 mL ElectraSyn vial was charged with cinnamic acid (0,25 mmol) and tetrabutylammonium tetrafluoroborate (0.25 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) in dry DMF (3.0 mL) and D2O (48 μL, 1.6 vol%). The vial was then capped with an ElectraSyn vial cap equipped with a cobalt cathode and a magnesium anode and the reaction mixture was stirred until it became homogenous. After attaching the IKA ElectraSyn GOGO Module, the ElectraSyn vial was carefully immersed in an oil bath heated to 35 °C. The reaction mixture was then electrolyzed under a constant current of 25 mA for 6.0 F/mol. A balloon filled with CO2 was used to bubble CO2 into the reaction mixture for the duration of the electrolysis. Following electrolysis, the ElectraSyn vial cap was removed, and the electrodes were rinsed with ethyl acetate (ca. 2 mL). The reaction mixture was then transferred to a separatory funnel, diluted with 10 mL of ethyl acetate, and washed with 3 M HCl. The organic layers were combined, dried over Na2SO4, filtered, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude product was purified by silica flash chromatography as indicated.
