Putrescine Detection Via PMMA-Zinc(II) Complex Optical Chemosensor: Meat Spoilage Screening Tool
Authors: Muhammad Ameerullah Sahudin, Jian Xiong Tan, Khairun Nasriah Azmi, Nur Afny Suhaimi, Shameer Hisham, Nor Kartini Abu Bakar, Muhammad Nidzhom Zainol Abidin, Khairul Anwar Ishak, Nurul Huda Abd Karim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiogenic amines serve as valuable indicators within the food industry, as they can identify signs of food spoilage. Excessive intake of these amines can potentially result in scombroid poisoning, headaches, respiratory issues, and other health-related issues. In recent times, more streamlined and rapid techniques for detecting biogenic amines have emerged, utilizing metal complex as optical sensing materials. This research focused on the development of putrescine fluorescence sensor based on PMMA-zinc(II) Schiff base complex. In the first stage, zinc(II) N,N’-bis[4-(hydroxysalicylidene)] phenylenediamine, complex 1 was synthesized and characterized using spectroscopic techniques. The second stage of this work involved studying the interaction between the complex with putrescine using UV-Vis titration and fluorescence emission titration. UV-Vis titration findings indicate that the complex was able to interact with putrescine strongly with binding constant, Kb = (2.92 ± 0.45) × 105 M−1. Optical putrescine sensor with zinc(II) complex deposited onto polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) nanoparticles as immobilization support was fabricated and successfully responded to putrescine. Under ideal conditions, the developed sensor can detect down to 3.59 × 10−8 M in the range of between 1.0 × 10−7 M and 1.0 × 10−2 M (R2 = 0.9884). Selectivity performance of the sensor towards putrescine over other amines was confirmed. Additionally, the sensor exhibited excellent reproducibility, as indicated by low relative standard deviation values. Furthermore, the proposed sensor has been effectively utilized to detect putrescine in buffalo meat, yielding satisfactory recovery rates. This method holds significant promise in the food quality assurance, particularly in improving food safety to ensure healthy consumption.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10895-025-04486-6.Keywords: Biogenic amines, Fluorescence sensors, Food safety, Metal complexes

Introduction

Biogenic amines (BAs) are relatively small-molecule organic bases present in microbes, plants, and animal species. Their toxicity to human health serves as an important indicator of food quality [1]. Consuming a high concentration of BAs inhibits the ability of human’s body to detoxify, leading to food poisoning symptoms such as dizziness and itching, as well as more serious health concerns. Poor hygiene in raw materials, the presence of microorganisms that produce BAs, and the optimal temperature and pH for decarboxylation activity in food may result in the formation of BAs.

The most prevalent BAs found in food are putrescine, histamine, tyramine and cadaverine [2]. BAs are produced by decarboxylation of amino acids and transamination of ketones and aldehydes. Ornithine, for example, was converted to putrescine via decarboxylation by the enzyme ornithine decarboxylase. Among of these BAs, putrescine is commonly found in meat products and serves as a marker for meat spoilage [3].

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), capillary electrophoresis (CE), and gas chromatography (GC) are the primary traditional methods for detecting biogenic amines in food. These instruments are selective and capable of producing precise results, but they are also costly and time-consuming to prepare. Since they are advanced technologies, only trained professionals can operate them [4].

As a result, sensor development has captured the interest of many researchers as a replacement for conventional methods. Not only are they simple, quick, and inexpensive to prepare, but it has also been proven that the corresponding sensing material is highly selective towards the analyte. These benefits have led to the widespread acceptance and use of sensors in the food industry.

In recent years, inorganic complexes have garnered significant attention among researchers as sensing materials in various fields of study [5–7]. Their versatility, characterized by the ability to adopt multiple geometries, enhances their specificity and selectivity for detecting analytes. Interest in metal complexes as the indicator for biogenic amines is mostly focused on metalloporphyrin complexes. Guo et al. [8] reported that a zinc(II) tetraphenylporphyrin complex visualized changes in UV-Vis spectrum upon interaction with histamine via hydrogen bonding between the carboxyl group of the ligand and the nitrogenous base of histamine. The binding strength was calculated and showed a binding constant, kb value of (8.74 ± 3.29) × 105 M−1. However, research on metal complexes as the sensing tools for BA determination is still limited.

Zinc(II) Schiff base complexes have emerged as promising candidates for biogenic amine sensing due to their unique coordination behavior and photophysical responses. The Zn(II) center, being a borderline Lewis acid, readily coordinates with primary amine groups such as those found in putrescine, forming stable complexes via nitrogen lone pair donation. The Schiff base ligand in this study, N,N’-bis[4-(hydroxysalicylidene)]phenylenediamineprovides a rigid, planar structure with multiple coordination sites, facilitating selective binding while maintaining structural integrity. These features enable strong coordination-induced fluorescence enhancement (CHEF), which is central to the optical detection mechanism employed. This coordination interaction also enables observable spectral shifts, forming the basis for sensitive and selective detection of putrescine.

In this work, zinc(II) Schiff base complex has been synthesized and characterized. The interaction between the complex and the putrescine was studied using spectroscopic studies. A solid-state fluorescence sensor was developed based on polymethyl methacrylate-zinc(II) complex for putrescine detection. This highly potent metal complex shows great potential as candidate for monitoring meat spoilage in the food industry.

Experimental

Chemicals and Reagents

1,4-diaminobutane (putrescine), o-phenylenediamine and 2,4-dihydroxybenzaldehyde were obtained from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), N,N’-dimethylformamide (DMF), diethyl ether, and ethanol (EtOH, 98%) were all purchased from R&M Chemicals (Semenyih, Malaysia). All chemicals were used without further purification. In order to prepare a putrescine stock solution (5 mM), 10.0 µL of the liquid putrescine was accurately measured using a 0.5–10 µL micropipette and dissolved in 10 mL of deionised water. The solution was subsequently diluted to 1 mM in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution. To prepare PBS solution, sodium chloride (8.0 g), potassium chloride (0.2 g), disodium phosphate (1.44 g), and monopotassium phosphate (0.24 g), were mixed in 800 mL of deionized water inside a 1 L beaker. Using a small concentration of potassium hydroxide solution or hydrochloric acid solution, the pH was adjusted to 7.4. After achieving a pH of 7.4, deionized water was added to bring the total volume to 1 L. The stock solution was refrigerated until further use and brought to room temperature prior to dilution.

Instrumentation

Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) spectra were obtained on a Jeol 400 MHz FT-NMR spectrometer (Billerica, MA, USA), employing tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the standard and deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO-d6) as the solvent. Fourier transform-infrared (FTIR) spectra were obtained using the PerkinElmer Spectrum 400 FT-IR (Waltham, MA, USA) within the wavenumber range of 4000 to 600 cm−1. Mass spectra were recorded using Bruker Daltonics (Bruker MicrOTOF-Q (Billerica, MA, USA)) spectrometer using electrospray ionization (ESI-MS) technique. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectra were recorded using a Shimadzu/UV-1650 PC spectrophotometer (Nakagyo-ku, Kyoto, Japan). Fluorescence spectra were analysed with a Cary fluorescence spectrophotometer (Santa Clara, CA, USA) with the excitation and emission wavelength set to 300 nm and 340–460 nm, respectively.

Synthesis of the Schiff Base Complex

In this work, 0.283 g (2.5 mmol) of 1,2-phenylenediamine was reacted with 0.722 g (5 mmol) of 2,4- dihydroxybenzaldehyde following the 1:2 ratio, in 40 ml of ethanol, under reflux condition at 75 °C for 30 min. After 30 min, 0.458 g (2.5 mmol) of zinc(II) acetate was introduced into the reaction, and the reflux was continued for 3 h at 75 °C. A magnetic stirrer was employed to continuously agitate the reaction mixture. The reaction mixture was analysed using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) to confirm the completion of the reaction. Fig. S1 demonstrates the complexation reaction of the complex. The yellowish precipitate was filtered via suction filtration and washed in the following order: ethanol (40 mL), deionized water (30 mL), and diethyl ether (20 mL). After washing, the product was oven-dried overnight. The yellow precipitate was collected and ground into a fine powder, weighed and stored in a vacuum desiccator. In conclusion, the complex 1, Zinc(II) N, N’-bis[4-(hydroxysalicylidene)]phenylenediamine, was successfully synthesised and obtained in the form of a yellow powder. The complex 1 was characterized using Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy-Attenuated Total Reflectance (FTIR-ATR) to confirm its molecular structure.

Fabrication of the solid-state Putrescine Fluorescence Sensor: Optimisation and Performance Studies

A solution of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) nanoparticles of 20 w% was prepared. About 5.0 g of PMMA solid powder was solubilised in 20 g of n-butylamine (NBA) within a 100 mL scotch bottle on a hotplate at 60 °C and 300 rpm. The solution was formulated for fabrication purposes. The luminescence sensor was fabricated by applying 100 µL of PMMA solution onto a 2 × 2 cm transparent glass slide using a spin coater at a speed of 800 rpm for 30 s. The PMMA was thereafter allowed to air-dry at ambient temperature for about one day. The PMMA-coated slide was subsequently coated using 100 µL of a 1 mM complex solution utilising the identical spin coating procedure. The glass slide was left to air-dry at room temperature for a day. To conduct a leaching test, the fluorescence intensities of a blank sensor glass slide and another slide that had been soaked in PBS for 15 min were compared. Results and Discussion

Spectroscopic Studies of Zinc(II) Complex

The synthesis of the zinc(II) complex was performed following the procedure detailed in Scheme 1. The Schiff base complex was subjected to a TLC test to verify the completeness of the reaction. The compound was characterised through NMR, FTIR, mass spectrometry, CHNS analysis, and UV-Vis techniques.

1H-NMR spectroscopy was performed to identify the number of proton in complex 1. The DMSO-d6 solvent used to dissolve the complexes showed a signal at δ 2.45 ppm. While signal at δ 3.30 ppm was water, which may come from the solvent itself. 1H-NMR spectrum for complex 1 (Fig. S2) shows a correct integration for zinc(II) complex. The integration ratio obtained is 1:1:1:2:2 where it shows the number of protons available in each peak. Since complex 1 is symmetrical, seven protons were present and showed the signal peaks. The aromatic proton, imine proton N = CH and phenol proton show signal at δ 6.00–7.72 ppm, δ 8.76 ppm and δ 9.69 ppm, respectively.

Based on the spectrum in Fig. S2, the loss of the signal peaks at approximately δ 9.92 ppm and δ 4.67 ppm indicates the loss of the aldehyde proton (O = CH) and the primary amine (NH2) group from the starting materials 2,4-dihydroxybenzaldehyde and 1,2-phenylenediamine, respectively. The conversion of the aldehyde proton and primary amine to the imine group (N = CH) confirms the formation of the Schiff base for the Schiff base ligand. Similarly, the loss of the signal peak at approximately δ 13 ppm (free OH proton) in the spectra of complex 1 demonstrates the complexation between Schiff base ligand and zinc(II) acetate. The coordination of the oxygen atom to the zinc(II) metal centre results in the deprotonation of the OH group in 2,4-dihydroxybenzaldehyde, as evidenced by this loss. Complex 1 has therefore been successfully synthesised.

The ¹H-NMR spectrum (Fig. S2) of the zinc(II) complex in DMSO-d₆ reveals a singlet at δ 8.76 ppm corresponding to the imine (–CH = N–) protons, confirming successful Schiff base condensation. The phenolic OH proton appears at δ 9.69 ppm, although coordination to Zn(II) likely reduces its intensity or causes partial disappearance. Multiple aromatic proton signals are observed between δ 6.00–7.72 ppm, consistent with the expected proton environment of the salicylidene and phenylenediamine rings. Disappearance of aldehydic (~ 9.9 ppm) and amine (~ 4.6 ppm) proton signals from the starting materials confirms ligand formation. The integration pattern (1:1:1:2:2) further supports the complex’s symmetrical structure.

The ¹³C-NMR spectrum (Fig. S2) shows 10 distinct carbon signals due to the symmetrical nature of the complex. The signal at δ ~ 160 ppm corresponds to the imine carbon (C = N), while signals between δ 120–150 ppm are attributed to aromatic carbons, including those bearing hydroxyl groups. Downfield shifts relative to free ligand values suggest electronic deshielding due to metal coordination. These spectral features confirm the successful formation of the zinc(II) Schiff base complex.

FTIR was performed to identify the functional groups in the compound and also to determine their vibrational modes. IR spectrum for complex 1 was displayed in Fig. S3. In order to show the formation of both complexes, a comparison was made between the starting material and the complexes. Absorption at 1601–1609 cm−1 corresponds to the imine C = N, confirming the formation of the Schiff base in complex 1. Additional functional groups have been found, including O-H, C = C aromatic, and C-O stretching, at wavenumbers of 3230–3306 cm−1, 1546–1584 cm−1, and 1178–1182 cm−1, respectively. The Zn-N and Zn-O stretching vibrations were expected to occur at around 400–500 cm−1. The ESI-MS m/z value obtained for the compound is 433 a.m.u. based on the peak [M + Na]+ obtained. While the CHN analysis measured for complex (C20H14N2O4Zn): C, 58.35%; H, 3.43%; N, 6.80%; found: C, 58.41%; H, 3.49%; N, 6.91%.

UV-Vis Solution Interaction Study

A UV-Vis solution interaction investigation was performed on the complex that targets putrescine, a signal for food spoilage. The binding constant (Kb) was calculated to estimate the intensity of the interaction between the complex and putrescine from a plotted graph of D/Δɛap against the concentration of putrescine (D). Figure 1a displays the UV-Vis absorbance spectrum for zinc complex titration experiment with putrescine. The hypochromic and the bathochromic (red) shift were observed. Hypochromicity was noted when the peak intensity diminished, while a bathochromic shift occurs when the absorption transitioned to a longer wavelength (shift to the right). From the spectrum obtained, it shows absorbance band at 310 nm corresponds to intraligand charge-transfer (ILCT) of electronic transition π − π*. Meanwhile, absorbance bands at 374 nm refer to the transition of n-π* which involved chromophore C = N. The strength of the interaction between the complex and putrescine can be determined by calculating the binding constant (Kb) from a plotted graph of D/Δɛap against the concentration of putrescine (D). Fig. S4 shows the plotted reciprocal graph for the titration result. The hypochromic effect of the complex is 48% and the absorbance of the respective peaks decreases with each titration with putrescine. This result indicates that putrescine interacts with and is bound strongly to the complex. Hypochromicity indicates the interaction between putrescine and the complex while bathochromic effect indicates the formation of a ‘complex-putrescine’ compound. A red shift of 6.0 nm was observed, and the Kb value obtained was (2.92 ± 0.45) × 105 M−1. To deduce, The UV-Vis titration spectra demonstrated significant hypochromicity and a red shift upon addition of putrescine, indicating a strong interaction with the zinc(II) Schiff base complex. These spectral changes are attributed to the coordination of the primary amine groups of putrescine with the Zn(II) center, which perturbs the ligand’s electronic environment. Hypochromicity suggests a reduction in π→π transition intensity due to decreased electron delocalization, while the red shift is indicative of a reduced HOMO-LUMO energy gap as a result of metal-ligand interaction. This supports the formation of a stable complex-putrescine adduct.

Fluorescence Emission Solution Binding Study

A fluorescence emission titration study was performed by preparing a complex solution at a concentration of 30 µM in PBS solution (pH 7.4). The putrescine solution was subsequently incorporated into the complex solution, maintaining a concentration of 1.5 µM for each titration until the fluorescence emission reached saturation. Figure 1b shows the fluorescence titration spectra with putrescine. Based on Fig. 1b, the complex displayed an increase in fluorescence intensity following the titration with biogenic amines, putrescine. The interaction between the complexes and the biogenic amines was represented by the coordination bonding of basic nitrogenous putrescine and histamine with the metal centre of the complexes. The fluorescence intensity for complex 1 was observed at around 441 nm and 350 nm. The increase in intensity indicates an interaction between the Zn(II) complex and putrescine via the chelation effect, recognised as chelation-enhanced fluorescence (CHEF) [12]. The complex most likely binds to putrescine through coordination binding, where the nitrogen base of putrescine interacts with the zinc metal centre. In a study conducted by Nakamura, Sanji, and Tanaka [13], an increase in fluorescence intensity reading of 300 a.u. was observed when the analyte was introduced to the modified tetraphenylethene solution, similar to the current observation. Complex 1 showed a 7% enhancement prior to the addition of putrescine. To deduce, fluorescence enhancement observed upon putrescine binding is consistent with the chelation-enhanced fluorescence (CHEF) mechanism. The coordination of putrescine rigidifies the complex, suppressing non-radiative decay and facilitating enhanced emission. Furthermore, Zn(II)’s closed-shell d10 configuration minimizes fluorescence quenching via d–d transitions. These factors collectively result in the observed fluorescence amplification, making the complex suitable as a luminescent sensor.

Optimization

Leaching Test

Figure 2 shows the schematic diagram of sensor fabrication. A leaching test was conducted to verify that our sensing material did not undergo leaching when the sensor was immersed in a putrescine solution. The test involved comparing the fluorescence intensity of a controlled sensor before and after immersion in a PBS buffer solution for 15 min. The results from Fig. S5 indicate that there is no leaching of sensing material from the glass slide, as the fluorescence intensity remained consistent before and after being dipped in PBS. The percentage error between the expected real reading and the bare sensor value was 2.88%, indicating a low level of discrepancy. Therefore, we can infer that both readings are identical. PMMA demonstrates a strong interaction with our sensing material, resulting in minimal leaching of the sensing material when immersed in a solution. Carbone, G.G. et al. [14] claimed that PMMA exhibits great biocompatibility and can interact with metal particles. Additionally, PMMA exhibits excellent optical properties and can enhance material characteristics like mechanical strength, solubility, and optical properties.

Concentration of Complex

Figure S6 indicates the response of the putrescine optical sensor with five different complex concentrations towards putrescine after the sensor was immersed into putrescine solution (1mM, PBS (pH 7.4)). The graph shows that 1000 µM (1 mM) exhibits the highest intensity peak which means that the optical sensor works optimally at this concentration. The intensity starts to drop when the complex concentration increases. This is because the molarity of the complex is too high until the particles inside overlap with each other through π- π electrostatic interaction, which limits the complex’s ability to interact with putrescine. Furthermore, the optimum complex concentration obtained from this optimisation efficiently reduces the amount of sensing material needed throughout the entire study. Therefore, 1000 µM (1 mM) is the optimum complex concentration and was used in the subsequent optimisation and performance parameter processes.

Response Time

The response time of the sensor was identified in this study. This parameter is very important in the development of our portable sensor, as it enables the detection of food freshness in a much shorter time compared to other conventional techniques used in the research lab to analyse putrescine in food, which are time-consuming. Fig. S7 illustrates the effect of different response time towards the sensing material when reacting with putrescine. The response time range investigated in this research was 1, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 min. According to the graph, the intensity increases after one minute, peaking at 15 min. After that, it remain relatively stable for 20 min, with the readings beginning to decline between 20 and 30 min. The results suggest that putrescine has fully bound with the sensing material at 15 min, which is why no further increment in intensity is observed when the response time is extended. Based on the research conducted, the sensing material in this study exhibits shorter response time compared to what was reported by [3]which required 4 h to achieve a strong response indicating sensitivity and dynamic range. The method they used was a paper-based biosensor with a cell-free expression system through fluorescence detection.

Performance Assessment

Linear Range and Limit of Detection (LOD)

Linear range and limit of detection (LOD) investigation are key performance test factors for optical sensors. This test was conducted by observing the changes in fluorescence intensity of the sensing material at different concentrations of putrescine under optimal conditions (Fig. 3a). The optimum condition for this test was 1000 µM of complex, PBS buffer (pH 7.4) and 15 min of response time with putrescine.

Figure 3b illustrates the response of fluorescence intensity of the sensing material towards the linear concentration of putrescine from 1.0 × 10−10 M to 1.0 × 102 M. The fluorescence intensity is linearized from 1.0 × 10−7 M to 1.0 × 10−2 M as shown in Fig. 3c. The equation of the linear plot is y = 16.962x + 208.04 with R2 value of 0.9884. From this trend, we can conclude that the higher the concentration of putrescine, the stronger the binding interaction between complex and putrescine. However, the fluorescence intensity starts to remain constant from the concentration of 1.0 × 10−1 M to 1.0 × 102 M, indicating the concentration limit has been reached. At this point, putrescine can no longer bind to the complex, causing the reading remain constant.

The ability to detect an analyte is a critical performance parameter in any measurement process. A key characteristic of an analytical method is its capacity to identify or quantify the lowest concentration or mass of the analyte (the component of interest in the sample) with a defined level of confidence. This capability is commonly expressed as the limit of detection (LOD), as defined by the International Union for Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) [15]. The developed sensor demonstrates the capability to detect concentrations as low as 3.59 × 10−8 M within the range of 1.0 × 10−7 M to 1.0 × 10−2 M. The findings can be compared with the research carried out by [16], which employed a biosensor incorporating screen-printed carbon electrodes (SPCE) augmented with monoamine oxidase (MAOx) enzyme and platinum nanoparticles (PtNP). The LOD value obtained in their research was 1.8 × 10–5 M. In the investigation conducted by [17], the amperometric biosensor using the MAOx enzyme showed an LOD value for phenylethylamine at 3.8 × 10^−5 M. The observed LOD value is higher than the one documented in our present study. Sudalaimani et al. [18]reported a significant LOD value of 40 ppm (3.915 × 10−4 M) using a colorimetric approach with ninhydrin as the reagent. Selectivity and Repeatability

The next performance parameter is selectivity. This parameter is one of the most significant and crucial in the sensor’s development, as it determines whether the constructed sensor is selective for the target analyte. Consequently, the researchers aim to design a sensor that exhibits high selectivity for putrescine to provide precise results without errors when real samples are tested on the sensor. This section examines five distinct amines; four biogenic amines–putrescine, histamine, tyramine, cadaverine and one primary amine–2-fluoroaniline. Figure 4a shows that putrescine gives the strongest response toward the developed sensor in comparison to other amines. The results indicate that the sensing material selectively reacts with putrescine at a wavelength of 370 nm, in contrast to the other amines. This may be attributed to the sensor optimisation, which ensure that the sensing material does not experience interference during the analysis of putrescine.

(a) The fluorescence sensor’s emission intensity pertaining to each type of amine (n = 3) and (b) the fluorescence intensity of the optical sensor after each cycle (n = 3)

A repeatability investigation was conducted to determine the entire duration of repeated usage of the optical sensor in putrescine analysis. This involved removing putrescine from the surface of the sensor by rinsing it with distilled water. The sensor was then dipped into putrescine solution again, and the reading of fluorescence intensity was measured and observed. Distilled water was used because putrescine is soluble in water, while the sensing material is only partially soluble in the water [12, 26]. Therefore, putrescine can be removed easily without the need for other chemical compounds. Figure 4b shows the results from the repeatability test of the optical sensor. The results show that the fluorescence intensity drop slightly during the second cycle compared to the first cycle. The percentage change from the first cycle to the second cycle is 1.53%, which s considered a small change. Next, the optical sensor was rinsed with distilled water to remove the putrescine and then re-dipped into the putrescine solution for the third cycle. The fluorescence intensity for this cycle drops drastically when compared to the first and second cycles. Using the first cycle as the reference, the percentage change for the third cycle is 17.08%, which s a significant change, indicating that the sensor is malfunctioning.

Additionally, the observed decline in sensor performance after the second cycle may be attributed to several factors. One plausible explanation is the irreversible coordination between the zinc(II) center and the amine groups of putrescine, which may not fully dissociate during the water rinsing step, thereby reducing the number of available binding sites in subsequent cycles. Additionally, although the leaching test indicated minimal material loss in static PBS immersion, repeated immersion and rinsing could promote gradual detachment of loosely adsorbed sensing complexes from the PMMA matrix. Furthermore, minor physical changes in the PMMA support, such as surface swelling or reduced adhesion under repeated use, may contribute to decreased sensor sensitivity. These effects cumulatively limit the sensor’s ability to maintain high repeatability beyond two cycles.

Characteristics of Immobilization Support for the Sensing Material

A few additional analyses, such as Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM) and Infrared (IR) Spectroscopy, were carried out on each layer of the sensor. The image produced by FESEM help in identifying the size of immobilization support of the sensor, which is the PMMA layer, while IR spectroscopy provide information on the interaction mode of each layer of the developed sensor.

Figure 5 presents the FESEM image, indicating that the average particle size of PMMA is 66 nm, thereby confirming that PMMA qualifies as a nanoparticle, as its average particle size is below 100 nm [27]. Nanomaterials possess distinctive physicochemical properties that can significantly enhance the development of novel recognition and transduction processes for chemical and biological sensors. Additionally, they contribute to the improvement of the signal-to-noise ratio through the miniaturisation of sensor components [28]. Additionally, FESEM-EDX analysis has been conducted to determine the composition of the immobilisation support, PMMA, and PMMA combined with the sensing material, the zinc(II) complex. Figure 5b and c illustrate the image of FESEM-EDX for PMMA and PMMA-complex. Based on Fig. 5b, it is clear that only PMMA particles (C and O particle) are present, indicating the immobilization support of the sensor. In Fig. 5c, when the sensing material, zinc(II) complex is coated on the immobilization support, zinc (Zn) and nitrogen (N) elements appear in the EDX image. This confirms that the sensing material has successfully bound to the immobilization support.

(a) FESEM image of PMMA. FESEM-EDX image of (b) PMMA and (c) PMMA-Complex

Meanwhile, Fig. S8 shows the IR spectrum for each layer of the sensor which are PMMA, complex and PMMA/complex. For PMMA, the peak is present at 1030.05, 1231.48, 1737.7 and 2961.32 cm−1. Based on the literature reported by [29], the absorption bands at 1159, 1442, 1723 and 2951 cm−1 correspond to O-CH3 stretching, CH3 stretching, C = O stretching and C-H asymmetric stretching vibrations, respectively, in PMMA. The peaks of PMMA from this research are almost identical to the literature values. For the second layer of the sensor, PMMA/complex shows new peaks at 1450.88 and 3424.92 cm−1. These peaks represent the functional groups OH (bending and sretching) of zinc complex from the compound. Moreover, it is evident that the peaks of PMMA/complex are nearly compatible with the two peaks shown in the spectra of PMMA and complex. Chemical interaction occurs when the IR peaks differ from those of the original compound [14]. This shows that there is no chemical interaction (covalent bonding) between PMMA and complex. Therefore, the mode of interaction for these two compounds is through physical adsorption, where the complex is coated on the PMMA surface. The binding mechanism of PMMA-Zinc(II) complex is through physisorption, while the interaction between the complex and putrescine occurs via a coordination bond between the zinc metal centre and the nitrogen-based ligand donor of the putrescine, as illustrated in Fig. S9.

Real Sample Analysis and Method Validation

The efficacy of the devised sensor in analysing actual samples was assessed by determining putrescine level in buffalo meat at three distinct concentrations: 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ M, 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ M, and 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ M. These concentrations were selected to ensure they fell within the sensor’s linear detection range (Fig. 3c). The putrescine levels measured employing the proposed method are presented in the Table 2. The outcomes indicated that no emission intensity was observed in either distilled water or unspiked buffalo meat, suggesting that the meat remained fresh and had not undergone enzymatic conversion of ornithine due to bacterial activity during storage [18, 30]. The concentrations of putrescine in the spiked samples were determined by comparing the measurements of emission level with the linear working ranges depicted in Fig. 3c. The sensor exhibited a commendable recovery rate of 95–98%, alongsde elevated measurement accuracy (RSD < 1%), suggesting its potential compliance with FDA, EU, and WHO standards for putrescine detection in the food industry [31, 32].

Conclusion

In conclusion, zinc(II) N,N’-bis[4-(hydroxysalicylidene)] phenylenediamine has been successfully synthesized and characterized by spectroscopic methods. The newly synthesized compound showed good binding interaction with the target analyte, putrescine, with Kb value of (2.92 ± 0.45) × 105 M−1. The synthesised compound was applied directly in the fabrication of the solid-state luminescence sensor. The PMMA-zinc(II) complex sensor has been fully optimized under optimal conditions. The developed sensor demonstrated an extensive operational range, a reduced limit of detection relative to earlier reported sensors, significant selectivity for putrescine over other amines present in food samples, and excellent repeatability and reproducibility. Finally, the fabricated sensor was subjected to real samples to demonstrate its suitability for putrescine detection and to validate the analytical approach developed. This study shows that the proposed solid-state luminescence sensor may serve as a potential screening instrument for food safety monitoring.
