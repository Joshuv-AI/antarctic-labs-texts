Functional Neurological Disorder Among Neurology In‐Patients
Authors: Johannes Jungilligens, Emma Marcinkowski, Tim Wehner, Sabine Skodda, Stoyan Popkirov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe high prevalence of functional neurological disorders (FND) in specialized neurological services such as epilepsy centers or dizziness clinics is well established. Few studies exist that investigate the frequency of FND among neurology in‐patients.MethodsIn‐hospital patients with functional neurological symptoms as part of the presenting complaint were identified by combining prospective and retrospective case ascertainment over a period of 2 years. Demographic and clinical features as well as reimbursements were analyzed.ResultsAcross a total of 4648 in‐hospital patients, 267 (5.7%) had functional neurological symptoms as a presenting complaint, and 217 (4.7%) were given a standalone FND diagnosis. All age groups and subtypes of FND were represented. FND comprised 2.4% of all cases admitted to the stroke unit for suspected stroke; 11.8% of all cases undergoing specialized epileptological workup; and 7.6% of cases admitted through the emergency department with a seizure disorder. Diagnostic coding of FND was inconsistent and associated with significantly lower per‐day revenue when performed accurately.ConclusionsFND is the third most common diagnosis among in‐hospital neurological patients, which stands in stark contrast to its underrepresentation in clinical training, service provision, and research funding. Stroke‐like presentations and seizures are common forms of FND among emergency admissions. Reimbursement mechanisms of the diagnosis‐related groups system might disincentivize accurate diagnostic coding and contribute to a cycle of neglect within health care.Keywords: costs, dissociative seizures, FND, functional neurological disorder, functional seizures, stroke mimics

Introduction

Functional neurological disorders (FNDs) are impairments of behavioral control and perception that arise primarily from aberrant neural processing and higher‐order psychological processes rather than structural alterations of the nervous system. They comprise a diverse set of symptoms, from convulsions to paralysis and many kinds of sensory disruptions. Despite this variety of presentations and justified subgroup distinctions, they share common pathophysiological mechanisms, diagnostic principles, and therapeutic approaches [1, 2]. Although distinct subtypes of FND such as functional seizures, stroke‐like presentations, or functional dizziness require focused research, a broader view on FND is also warranted.

The prevalence of FND has been conservatively estimated at 80–140/100000, far surpassing that of other common neurological conditions such as multiple sclerosis [3]. This is reflected in studies from general neurological outpatient clinics, where functional disorders account for about one in six new presentations [4, 5]. Studies investigating the frequency of FND among in‐patients in neurology departments of general hospitals are rare. An older retrospective analysis of 4470 consecutive in‐patients treated between 1985 and 1987 at an academic neurology department in Germany found that FND accounts for 9% of all cases [6]. A more recent study from Italy (2021–2022) found a rate of 4.4% among 843 consecutive patients [7]. This constitutes a remarkable proportion of all neurological in‐hospital patients and deserves more attention from all stakeholders than it currently does [8, 9]. FND is a notoriously neglected and underserved area of neurology, and bringing clarity to its relative frequency and clinical characteristics within hospital care is an important step towards improving care.

Large‐scale epidemiological studies often rely on coding of discharge diagnosis and thus entail the risk for underreporting. Studies have repeatedly shown that cases of clinically diagnosed FND are often not documented or coded as such [10, 11, 12, 13]. For this reason, studies based solely on decontextualized insurance or hospital coding data can introduce significant biases in reporting. Prospective screening across entire institutions, in contrast, is logistically challenging for long study periods and large sample sizes.

Here, we investigate the frequency of FND in in‐hospital care over a 2‐year period employing a dual prospective and retrospective case identification strategy. The cohort is then characterized from various perspectives to reveal clinically, scientifically, and economically salient features.

Methods

Study Setting

This study investigates the frequency, treatment pathways, and associated costs of cases with functional neurological symptoms among in‐patients of a department of neurology at a German university hospital (Knappschaft Kliniken University Hospital Bochum). The department is a representative mid‐sized neurology unit, featuring a specialized epilepsy center and a supraregional stroke unit that provides round‐the‐clock thrombectomy services. Other treatment specializations are neuro‐oncology and movement disorders. The department has no rehabilitation program; elective admissions (about a third of total inpatient volume) are primarily for diagnostic assessment or acute treatment. The study received approval by the ethics committee of the medical faculty, Ruhr University Bochum (reg.‐no.: 20‐7038_1‐BR).

Case Data Inclusion

During a period of 2 years (July 30, 2020 to July 30, 2022), cases of neurology in‐patients with functional neurological symptoms as primary or secondary diagnoses were prospectively identified during the final coding of the case files for hospital accounting after discharge. Additionally, medical records of all patients discharged from neurology in this time period were retrospectively screened using ICD‐10 codes to identify cases that were erroneously missed in the prospective screening. This two‐pronged approach was chosen, since functional disorders in neurology are oftentimes coded inconsistently [10, 11, 12, 13]. In total, 267 cases of in‐patients with functional neurological symptoms were identified (241 prospectively with 26 additional cases retrospectively). The list of all inpatient cases treated in the department of neurology additionally included 4381 cases without functional neurological symptoms. Thus, a total of 4648 cases were included in the analyses. Patients treated at other departments that received neurological consults were not included.

Detailed individual case data was obtained both through in‐depth review of the discharge letters of in‐patients with functional neurological symptoms (Table S1) and through automated data retrieval from the department of medical billing and controlling (duration of stay, revenue per day, procedures and treatments, whether the functional symptom was coded as primary diagnosis) for all cases. In the German healthcare system, hospitals are reimbursed for inpatient care through the diagnosis related groups system: For each patient case, a code is assigned based on the primary diagnosis, comorbidities, and illness severity. The code is related to a fixed reimbursement amount, modified to a degree by length of stay and by some procedures, although generally not by non‐invasive diagnostic procedures. Here, we used the total revenue generated by each case divided by the duration of stay to compare cases on a revenue‐per‐day basis.

Statistics

All analyses were performed using jamovi running R version 4.4 [14, 15]. Most analyses rely on displaying frequencies, with some additionally comparing frequencies between groups using Fisher's exact test with Monte Carlo simulation or Chi‐Square tests. Additionally, Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare data between groups.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Of the 4648 inpatient cases treated at the department of neurology in the 2‐year timeframe, 267 (5.7%) presented with suspected or confirmed functional neurological symptoms as part of their main complaint. In 217 cases (4.7%), clinically confirmed or suspected FND was given as a final standalone diagnosis (Figure 1). In the remaining cases, symptoms were part of another neurological or psychiatric disorder (i.e., functional component, n = 41), or did not reach diagnostic threshold for suspected FND (i.e., medically unexplained neurological symptoms, n = 9). At 4.7%, FND was the third most common diagnostic group (after stroke/transient ischemic attack with 29% and epilepsy with 14.8% of cases), more common than Parkinson's Disease or Multiple Sclerosis (Table 1).

Nine patients were admitted twice within the study period (one of whom was admitted a third time). The median time between admissions was about 6 months (range: 24–500 days). Six of those cases were patients with functional seizures.

While 19% of patients with functional neurological symptoms presented with mixed symptoms, the most common leading symptoms were functional seizures (35.6%), followed by functional movement disorders (24%), functional sensory symptoms (13.5%), functional limb weakness (10.9%), functional cognitive symptoms (7.9%), functional dizziness (3.7%), other functional symptoms (3.4%), and fatigue (1.5%).

Of the 267 cases with functional neurological symptoms, 66.7% (178) were female and 33.3% (89) were male; this female predominance was reflected across all subtypes, except for functional limb weakness, which had an equal ratio of male and female patients (Figure 2). Functional symptoms were seen across all ages, with a peak around middle age (Figure 3).

Treatment and Associated Costs

Next, we focused on patients with clinically confirmed or suspected FND (n = 217), excluding patients with functional neurological symptoms in the context of other diagnoses.

In 69% of cases, symptoms were chronic (persisting for more than 1 month), while 31% presented with acute complaints (less than a month). Among those with chronic symptoms, most (59%) were admitted electively, whereas in those with acute symptoms, most (77%) were admitted as emergency cases.

The emergency admissions rate across all FND cases was lower than for in‐patients without functional symptoms (49% vs. 59%); conversely, elective admissions accounted for about half of all FND cases, while in the sample of cases without FND this only applies to every third case (χ 2(3) = 25.0; p < 0.001).

Cases with FND remained in the hospital for a median of 4 days (range: 1–31 days, interquartile range 2–7 days). A Mann–Whitney U test (Z = 402,656; p < 0.001) showed that this was significantly shorter than for cases with non‐functional diagnoses (median 5 days, range 1–161 days, interquartile range 2–9 days).

The median total revenue per day was 595 €/day for FND cases (range 187–1330 €; inter quartile range 230–884 €) and 767 €/day for cases without FND (range 122–5681 €; inter quartile range 509–1077 €). This represents a significantly lower median total revenue per day for FND cases (Mann–Whitney‐U test: Z = 326,626; p < 0.001; effect size rank‐biserial correlation = 0.310, Figure 5).

Distribution of the revenue per day for cases with functional neurological disorder (FND) compared to those without FND.

In those with clinically confirmed or suspected FND, the functional disorder was coded as the primary diagnosis in 72%, while in the other 28%, a non‐functional primary diagnosis was given, and the functional symptoms were coded as secondary diagnoses. There was a significant difference in the mean total revenue per day between these two groups (t‐test: t(214) = 2.68; p = 0.008; effect size Cohen's d = 0.409), with daily revenue for cases with FND as a primary diagnosis 19% lower than for those for whom functional symptoms were coded as secondary diagnoses (563 € vs. 692 €). Unlike between cases with clinically confirmed or suspected diagnoses of FND and cases with non‐functional diagnoses, there was no difference in the average length of hospital stay (p = 0.787). Furthermore, among those with a clinically confirmed or suspected standalone FND diagnosis, those with an F44 or F45 primary diagnosis (n = 150; 69%) had a 16% lower mean daily revenue than those with alternative coding: 565 € versus 672 € (t‐test: t(214) = 2.27; p = 0.024; effect size Cohen's d = 0.334). Median length of stay was 4 days in both groups (p = 0.834).

Overall, the most common examinations were cranial computed tomography (34% of patients), routine EEG (33% of patients), and brain MRI (32% of patients). The most common examinations were different between the different groups of leading functional symptom domains (see Table 2 for details).

Special Consideration: Functional Seizures

Our department of neurology houses a level 4 epilepsy center with a supra‐regional outreach; thus, seizure patients might be over‐represented in our sample compared to other hospitals. In the 2‐year timeframe, 812 patients received a specialized epileptological diagnostic workup; 7.9% (64 cases) of those were patients with functional seizures, with 31 additional cases that were diagnosed with functional seizures but did not receive a specialized epileptological diagnostic workup. In sum, 11.8% of the overall epileptological workload was due to functional seizures. Among the 444 seizure patients admitted as emergency cases, 7.6% were discharged with a diagnosis of functional seizures. In 51 cases, patients were treated for status epilepticus, with five of those (9.8%) later diagnosed as functional seizures.

Special Consideration: Stroke

Our hospital features a supra‐regional stroke unit (1105 stroke cases treated in the 2‐year timeframe). Functional stroke mimics (defined as cases that were admitted under the suspicion of a stroke) were seen in 38 cases, which represent 3.4% of all stroke patients. Of these, 27 cases (2.4% of all 1105 stroke cases) were treated in the stroke unit. Two patients with functional stroke mimics received thrombolysis due to initial misdiagnosis.

Discussion

Our study found that among 4648 consecutive neurological in‐patients over a 2‐year period, 5.7% had functional neurological symptoms as a presenting complaint, most of whom (4.7% of total) were specifically diagnosed as having FND. This proportion is about half of what Lempert et al. found using similar methodology and sample size in Germany 35 years prior [6] and very close to the 4.4% recently reported by Pilotto et al. in Italy [7] This number is remarkable when contrasted with the minimal attention the topic of FND receives in neurology training, service provision, and research funding [8, 9].

FND accounted for 2.4% of cases admitted to the stroke unit for suspected stroke. This is lower than the 8% reported from the UK and Qatar [16, 17] likely due to national differences in treatment service organization (pre‐hospital triage by emergency physician and neurology‐led in‐hospital emergency workup in Germany). Still, this confirms FND's position as an important differential diagnosis in the emergency work‐up of suspected stroke [18] highlighted further by the fact that two of those patients (7%) received thrombolysis.

The importance of considering, recognizing, and treating functional seizures in the context of both acute neurology care and in epilepsy monitoring units is well established [19, 20]. In our study, 11.8% of all cases undergoing specialized epileptological workup were discharged with a primary diagnosis of functional seizures, similar to the 18% reported recently by another German epilepsy center [21]. Interestingly, functional seizures also accounted for 7.6% of all cases admitted through the emergency department for a seizure disorder, which is on the lower end of previously reported rates [22, 23]. This is likely explained by our methodology based on discharge diagnoses, whereby reasons for emergency admissions other than acute seizures (e.g., medication side effects or subacute changes in seizure frequency) were not differentiated, possibly increasing the relative rate of epilepsy patients.

In line with previous observations, [10, 11, 12, 13] only 69% of all FND cases in our study received a primary diagnostic code reflecting it unambiguously (i.e., F44 or F45). Under‐recognition and misdiagnosis are commonly cited as sources of underreporting, but since our study looked at actual final diagnoses only, diagnostic coding variability might be subject to other influences. The fact that mean daily revenue is approximately 16% lower for FND cases that are coded correctly as a primary diagnosis than for cases that are coded differently or as secondary diagnoses reveals a potentially distorting incentive structure of reimbursement. This might contribute to a cycle of underreporting and neglect of patients with FND within health care services.

The generalizability of our results is chiefly limited by our monocentric study design. Bochum is part of the densely populated Ruhr Area that has a population of over 5 million and several academic neurology hospitals within its bounds, leading to regional sub‐specializations of individual hospitals. This introduces imbalances in the relative frequency of specific disorders such as multiple sclerosis (likely underrepresented in our department) or epilepsy (overrepresented in our department). Furthermore, our study was conducted during the Coronavirus‐Pandemic, which could potentially have affected both the incidence of FND and the likelihood for hospital admission.

In conclusion, our analysis reveals that around 5% of in‐hospital neurology patients have FND, positioning it as the third most common condition after stroke and epilepsy. There is significant heterogeneity of presentations regarding age, gender, and presenting symptoms, necessitating broad training and service provision efforts. Lastly, it suggests that the previously reported inconsistency in diagnostic coding might be associated not just with diagnostic uncertainty, but with financial incentive structures that contribute to underreporting and obfuscation of the true relevance of FND.
