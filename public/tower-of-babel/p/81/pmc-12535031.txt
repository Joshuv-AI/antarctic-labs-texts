Suppression of pentraxin 3 inhibits human osteosarcoma cell metastasis by repressing MAZ through STAT3 pathway
Authors: Chia-Hsuan Chou, Yi-Hsien Hsieh, Pei-Ni Chen, Jia-Sin Yang, Chih-Hsin Tang, Shun-Fa Yang, Ko-Hsiu Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPentraxin 3 (PTX3), also known as TNF-inducible gene 14 protein (TSG-14), exerts pleiotropic roles in inflammation, immune responses, and a myriad of cancers, but there is limited knowledge regarding the role of PTX3 in the metastasis of human osteosarcoma.MethodsUsing RNA sequencing technology, transfection, western blotting, flow cytometry, and luciferase reporter, colony formation, quantitative reverse transcription–polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), boyden chamber assays, and a xenograft model, the effects of PTX3 on cell migration and invasion in human osteosarcoma cells were investigated.ResultsPTX3 silencing repressed migration and invasion in U-2OS cells and PTX3 overexpression accelerated migration and invasion in HOS cells. Recombinant human PTX3 (rhPTX3) increased migration and invasion of HOS cells. In PTX3 knockdown U-2OS cells, the heatmap revealed the downregulation of Myc-associated zinc finger protein (MAZ), which is associated with the epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathway and osteosarcoma tissues. MAZ overexpression rescued the inhibition of migration by PTX3 knockdown in osteosarcoma cells. The activity of the MAZ promoter was reduced with PTX3 knockdown in osteosarcoma cells. While Stat3 overexpression rescued the inhibition of MAZ expression and migration by PTX3 knockdown in osteosarcoma cells. Moreover, in tail vein injection experiments, shPTX3 U-2OS cells reduced pulmonary metastasis formation in immunodeficient nude mice. By repressing MAZ through Stat3, suppression of PTX3 inhibits migration and invasion in osteosarcoma cells, and knockdown of PTX3 reduces pulmonary metastasis.ConclusionsOur results raise interest in further study of PTX3’s anti-metastasis properties in osteosarcoma, providing molecular insight into PTX3’s role in osteosarcoma metastasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04008-1.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma, the most common primary malignancy of the bone, arises from mesenchymal cells and occurs commonly in children and adolescents. Highly malignant osteosarcoma is characterized by a high propensity for lung metastasis, rapid disease progression, high mortality, and poor clinical outcomes [1, 2]. The 5-year survival rate for patients with osteosarcoma is approximately 60%70% [3]. However, recurrent or metastatic osteosarcoma is a major obstacle to treatment, reducing the survival rate to < 20% [4, 5]. The molecular mechanisms underlying the metastasis of osteosarcoma must be urgently studied to improve the survival rate of patients with this cancer.

Metastasis of epithelial carcinomas and mesenchymal sarcomas, such as osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma, involves cancer cell migration and invasion, intravasation, transit through lymphatic and blood circulation, extravasation, and eventual colonization at a secondary or distant location. The metastatic signaling cascade encompasses several pathways, such as the focal adhesion kinase (FAK) pathway, mitogen-activated protein kinase pathway, and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (Stat3) pathway. MYC-associated zinc finger protein (MAZ) is a transcription factor whose expression varies across organs. MAZ is highly expressed in several human cancers to promote tumorigenicity, progression, and metastasis [6–13]; however, its role in the metastasis of osteosarcoma remains unclear.

Pentraxin 3 (PTX3) is a member of the PTX superfamily involved in the innate immune response to cytokines, such as interleukin-1β and tumor necrosis factor-α [14–16]. PTX3 contributes to tumor-associated inflammation. Several studies have reported that its expression is upregulated in various malignancies, such as liposarcoma [17], lung cancer [18], pancreatic cancer [19], glioma [20], and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [21]. A study conducted using the human cervical cancer cell lines SiHa and HeLa demonstrated that the overexpression of PTX3 promoted the migration and invasion of these cells [22]. PTX3 overexpression exerted similar effects on hepatocellular carcinoma cells; in addition, it induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [23]. However, Ronca et al. demonstrated that the overexpression of PTX3 in transgenic mice suppressed tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis. NSC12—a small, PTX3-derived molecule that inhibits fibroblast growth factor (FGF)—was found to be beneficial in cancer therapy [24]. The potential role of PTX3 in the metastasis of osteosarcoma and the underlying mechanisms remain to be clarified. Therefore, in this study, we investigated the biological role of PTX3 in osteosarcoma through in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Materials and methods

Materials

Cell culture materials comprised fetal bovine serum (FBS) purchased from Gibco Life Technologies, and minimum essential medium (MEM) and Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) purchased from Gibco-BRL (Gaithersburg, MD, USA). Antibodies specific for phosphorylated FAK397, FAK925, ERK1/2, JNK1/2, Akt, and Stat3, were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Antibodies specific for PTX3 and β-actin was purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA). Antibodies specific for MAZ was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Antibodies specific for RPIA and GLO1 was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA). Additionally, propidium iodide (PI) and antibodies specific for phosphorylated p38 were acquired from BD Biosciences (San Jose, CA, USA). In this study, all chemicals used were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) unless otherwise specified.

Cell culture

The human osteosarcoma cell line Saos-2 (originally isolated from an 11-year-old Caucasian girl) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). The cell lines U-2OS (originally isolated from a 15-year-old girl), HOS (originally isolated from a 13-year-old girl), and MG-63 (originally isolated from a 14-year-old boy) were acquired from the Food Industry Research and Development Institute (Hsinchu, Taiwan). Saos-2 and U-2OS cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 5 mL of glutamine. HOS and MG-63 cells were cultured in Eagle’s Minimal Essential Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 5 mL of glutamine. All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2, as described in other studies [25, 26].

Protein extraction and western blotting

The pathways mediating the role of PTX3 in osteosarcoma metastasis were investigated through Western blotting. We plated U-2OS cells with or without knockdown of PTX3/MYC associated zinc finger protein (MAZ) or overexpression PTX3 and HOS cells with or without overexpression of PTX3/MAZ/Stat3 or knockdown PTX3 on 6-cm cell culture dishes for 16 h. Total lysates were prepared from U-2OS and HOS cells following previously described protocols [26, 27]. Western blotting was performed using primary antibodies against PTX3, MAZ, and the phosphorylated forms of FAK397, FAK925, ERK1/2, JNK1/2, p38, Akt, and Stat3. Subsequently, the blots were incubated for 1 h with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated goat antirabbit or antimouse immunoglobulin G and imaged using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA). Finally, the blots were imaged by ECL reagent (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA).

Transient transfection

Cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. As previously described, the plasmids pcDNA3.1-PTX3, pCMV6-MAZ, and pcDNA3.0-Stat3 were diluted in 500 µL of Minimum Essential Medium and incubated with the transfection solution for 20 min. Each small interfering RNA (siRNA; 150 pmol) was transfected into osteosarcoma cells for either 24–48 h. Afterward, the cells were collected for cell migration and invasion assays, Western blotting, and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR).

Small interfering RNA (siRNA)

Silence of PTX3 and MAZ genes, and negative-control siRNA were purchased from Applied Biosystems Instruments (Foster City, CA, USA). In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, each siRNA (150 pmol) was transfected into the cells for 48 h using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX™ reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The PTX3 sense siRNA sequence (5’->3’) was CAAUGAGGCUUGAGUCUUUtt and the PTX3 antisence siRNA sequence was AAAGACUCAAGCCUCAUUGgt. The MAZ sense siRNA sequence (5’->3’) was CAAUCUCCGGAGGCACGAAtt and the MAZ antisence siRNA sequence was UUCGUGCCUCCGGAGAUUGta.

Quantitative reverse transcription–polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and RNA sequencing

RNA was extracted using a Total RNA Mini Kit (Geneaid, New Taipei City, Taiwan), and reverse transcription into complementary DNA (cDNA) was performed with a High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). All procedures for cDNA synthesis and the PCR amplification assay were performed as described in a previous study [25, 27]. For RNA-seq, RNA extracted from U-2OS/control siRNA and U-2OS/PTX3 siRNA was sent to the BIOTOOLS CO., LTD for transcriptomic profiling and analysis.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was performed to evaluate the effects of PTX3 knockdown or overexpression on cell cycle distribution, DNA content, and apoptosis in U-2OS and HOS cells. PTX3 knockdown was achieved by siRNA transfection, whereas PTX3 overexpression was performed using the pcDNA3.1-PTX3 plasmid. The experiment was conducted as described previously [28–30]. Cells at different phases of the cell cycle were distinguished by measuring DNA content after propidium iodide staining.

Colony formation assay

U-2OS cells with or without PTX3 knockdown and HOS cells with or without PTX3 overexpression were cultured in 6-well plates. The culture medium was replenished every 4 days for a total duration of 1 week. The colonies were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline, fixed in 100% methanol, and stained at room temperature with 5% Giemsa. Finally, the number of colonies was counted.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

A commercially available osteosarcoma tissue microarray (OS804d; US Biomax, Rockville, MD, USA) was utilized for immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis. Paraffin-embedded mouse lung sections were first deparaffinized in xylene and then rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was carried out by heating the slides in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 10 min. Primary antibodies against human PTX3 was applied at a 1:200 dilution and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. The immunoreactivity was detected using the NovoLink Polymer Detection System (Leica Microsystems), with 3–3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) serving as the chromogen. Hematoxylin was used for nuclear counterstaining. Staining intensity results were semi-quantitatively assessed with ImageJ software (version 1.53, Nation Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Cell migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion assays were performed as described previously [31]. For the migration assay (microchemotaxis chamber: Neuro Probe, Cabin John, MD, USA), U-2OS or HOS cells were seeded (at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in serum-free medium) in the upper chamber of the apparatus. The lower chamber was filled with growth medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum. After 24 h, the membrane diving the chambers (pore size 8 μm) was fixed in methanol for 10 min and stained with 10% Giemsa (Sigma Chemical Co.). The same procedure was followed for the invasion assay, except that the upper chamber was coated with Matrigel before seeding the cells. The cells’ migratory and invasive abilities were quantified under a light microscope [27, 32].

MAZ promoter luciferase reporter assay

U-2OS cells with or without PTX3 knockdown and HOS cells with or without PTX3 overexpression were used in this experiment. The plasmids pGL3-basic and pGL3-MAZ promoter (containing a 1.6 kb fragment of the human MAZ promoter, chr16:29805092–29806703, GRCh38/hg38, UCSC Genome Browser) were cotransfected into cells with a β-galactosidase expression vector, as described previously [33]. The construct was verified by sequencing prior to use. After 24 h of transfection, cell lysates were harvested. Luciferase activity, which was assessed using a luciferase assay kit, was normalized to β-galactosidase activity. MAZ promoter–driven transcriptional activity was measured by calculating the ratio of luciferase activity to β-galactosidase activity.

In vivo experiments

In vivo experiments were performed in strict accordance with the recommendations laid down by the National Institutes of Health in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chung Shan Medical University. Immunodeficient nude mice (C.B17/lcr-Prkdcscid/CrlNarl) were obtained from the National Laboratory Animal Center. For the lung metastasis assay, the mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (5 mice per group). PTX3-knockdown U-2OS cells (7 × 105), constructed through lentivirus-mediated transfection of small hairpin RNA, were intravenously injected into the tail vein of the mice. Mice injected with luciferase-knockdown U-2OS cells, constructed through the transfection of shLuc, served as the control group [22]. The mice were euthanized after 8 weeks. Their lungs were isolated, fixed in neutral-buffered 5% formalin, sliced into thin sections, and stained using hematoxylin/eosin or immunostained using anti-Ki67 antibodies for morphological studies. The cells were imaged, and their numbers were quantified. The expression of PTX3 was measured in shLuc- and shPTX3-transfected U-2OS cells.

Statistical analysis

We conducted statistical analyses using the Student’s t-test for differences between two independent samples and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post hoc Tukey’s test for comparisons involving more than two groups with equal sample sizes per group. Linear regression analysis was employed for the correlation between PTX3 and MAZ mRNA levels. Each experiment was independently conducted in triplicate. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

PTX3 expression and functional evaluation in osteosarcoma

We selected U-2OS for PTX3 knockdown and HOS for PTX3 overexpression based on previous evidence demonstrating that HOS cells display higher tumorigenic potential compared with U-2OS [34], thus providing complementary models to investigate the functional role of PTX3 in osteosarcoma. The efficiency of transient PTX3 knockdown by siRNA and transient PTX3 overexpression by plasmid transfection was confirmed through Western blotting and qRT-PCR (Fig. 1a, b). Next, we investigated whether PTX3 knockdown or overexpression affected the viability of or induced toxicity in U-2OS and HOS cells. Flow cytometry revealed no occurrence of apoptosis in any of the cells after PTX3 knockdown or overexpression (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, the colony formation assay indicated no differences in U-2OS or HOS cells after PTX3 knockdown or overexpression (Fig. 1d). Overall, PTX3 knockdown or overexpression exerted no effects on cell viability. To investigate the clinical relevance of PTX3, we performed IHC staining on a human osteosarcoma tissue microarray. The analysis revealed that PTX3 expression was significantly elevated in osteosarcoma tissues with higher clinical stage, whereas lower expression was observed in tumors of lower clinical stage (Fig. 1e). These data support the upregulation of PTX3 in osteosarcoma and its potential association with tumor progression.

Suppression of PTX3 inhibits the migration and invasion of human osteosarcoma cells in vitro

Cancer-related deaths are mainly due to the metastasis of primary tumor cells to distant sites from their tissue of origin. Thus, we explored the role of PTX3 in the migration and invasion of osteosarcoma cells. For this, we resorted to the Boyden chamber assay. PTX3 knockdown significantly reduced the migratory and invasive abilities of U-2OS cells (Fig. 2a), whereas PTX3 overexpression enhanced these abilities in HOS cells (Fig. 2b). Transfection of 100 ng/mL rhPTX3 led to the maximum enhancement in the migration (p < 0.001) and invasion (p < 0.001) of HOS cells (Fig. 2c, d). Moreover, transfection of PTX3-overexpressing plasmid upregulated PTX3 expression and promoted migration and invasion in U-2OS cells (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Conversely, PTX3 knockdown downregulated PTX3 expression and suppressed migration and invasion in HOS cells (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). These results indicated that PTX3 directly influenced the motility of osteosarcoma cells.

PTX3 regulates downstream MAZ expression

To identify the downstream pathways through which PTX3 regulates the metastasis of osteosarcoma cells, we compared transcriptomes between U-2OS cells transfected with PTX3 siRNA and those transfected with control siRNA. The top 20 significantly upregulated and downregulated genes following PTX3 knockdown were annotated and are presented in the form of a volcano plot and a heatmap (Fig. 3a, b). To identify candidate genes associated with cancer metastasis, we first ranked the differentially expressed genes from RNA-seq according to log2 fold change with a cutoff of |log2FC| ≧ 1 and p < 0.05, and selected the top 20 upregulated and top 20 downregulated genes. We then performed a literature search to determine which of these candidates had previously been reported to be functionally linked to cancer metastasis, and only those genes with consistent evidence and directionality were chosen for further validation by qRT-PCR (Fig. 3c, d). RPIA, GLO1, and MAZ were the most significantly downregulated genes in PTX3-knockdown cells (Fig. 3c), whereas these genes were significantly upregulated in PTX3-overexpressing cells (Fig. 3d). Western blotting revealed that MAZ was the only gene downregulated in PTX3-knockdown U-2OS cells and upregulated in PTX3-overexpressing HOS cells (Fig. 3e, f). Overexpression of PTX3 in U-2OS cells significantly upregulated the expression of MAZ (Supplementary Fig. 2a), whereas knockdown of PTX3 in HOS cells downregulated the expression of MAZ (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Analysis of the GSE68591 dataset from the Gene Expression Omnibus, which contains transcriptomic profiles of 68 human sarcoma-derived cell lines, revealed a positive correlation between PTX3 and MAZ expression in human sarcoma cells (Fig. 3g).

Suppression of PTX3 downregulates MAZ to inhibit migration and invasion of osteosarcoma cells

To investigate the direct impact of MAZ on cell motility, transient knockdown of MAZ using siRNA was performed in U-2OS cells, while transient overexpression of MAZ via plasmid transfection was conducted in HOS cells. The efficiency of MAZ knockdown and overexpression was validated through Western blotting and qRT-PCR (Fig. 4a, b). The effects of MAZ on the migratory and invasive abilities of osteosarcoma cells were evaluated through the Boyden chamber assay. MAZ knockdown reduced the migratory and invasive abilities of U-2OS cells (Fig. 4c), whereas MAZ overexpression enhanced these abilities in HOS cells (Fig. 4d). The luciferase reporter assay was performed to understand how PTX3 regulates MAZ expression in osteosarcoma. The activity of MAZ promoter was significantly attenuated in PTX3 siRNA–transfected cells compared with its activity in control siRNA–transfected cells (Fig. 4e). By contrast, the activity of MAZ promoter was significantly enhanced in PTX3-overexpressing cells (Fig. 4f). These results suggest that PTX3 regulates MAZ expression, at least partially, at the transcriptional level. Moreover, MAZ overexpression significantly increased the levels of MAZ and cell migratory abilities in U-2OS cells and appeared to partially reverse the inhibitory effect of PTX3 knockdown (Fig. 4g). Conversely, MAZ knockdown significantly reduced MAZ levels and cell migratory abilities in HOS cells and descriptively attenuated the enhancing effect of PTX3 overexpression (Fig. 4h). Overall, the findings indicate that the antitumor effect of PTX3 knockdown is mediated, at least in part, through regulation of MAZ in osteosarcoma cells.

Suppression of PTX3 downregulates MAZ through repressing STAT3 to inhibit migration of osteosarcoma cells

To identify the signaling pathways through which PTX3-mediated MAZ influences cell migration, we prioritized examination of the FAK, MAPK (ERK/JNK/p38), PI3K/Akt, and STAT3 pathways because of their well-documented roles in regulating tumor cell migration, metastasis, cytoskeletal remodeling, and EMT in osteosarcoma and other malignancies [35–38]. We then explored the activation status of these pathways in U-2OS cells with or without PTX3 knockdown and in HOS cells with or without PTX3 overexpression. Western blotting indicated that PTX3 knockdown in U-2OS cells downregulated the expression of phosphorylated Stat3, whereas PTX3 overexpression in HOS cells upregulated the expression of phosphorylated Stat3 (Fig. 5a, b). Furthermore, the inhibitory effect of PTX3 knockdown on MAZ expression could be reversed by overexpressing STAT3 in U-2OS cells (Fig. 5c), whereas the positive effect of PTX3 overexpression on MAZ expression could be reversed by treating HOS cells with 30 µM C188-9, an inhibitor of STAT3 (Fig. 5d). These findings suggest that PTX3 may influence MAZ expression, at least in part, through STAT3 signaling. To further evaluate the functional role of this axis, migration assays were performed. As shown in Fig. 5e, MAZ overexpression also reversed the PTX3 knockdown–mediated inhibition of U-2OS cell migration. Conversely, treatment with 30 µM C188-9 reversed the PTX3 overexpression–induced enhancement of HOS cell migration (Fig. 5f). These results suggest that Stat3 signaling is involved in the PTX3 knockdown–mediated suppression of MAZ expression.

Downregulation of PTX3 inhibits pulmonary metastasis in vivo

Finally, we explored the effects of PTX3 expression on tumor metastasis in vivo. PTX3 expression was downregulated through the administration of specific small hairpin RNAs. The efficiency of knockdown was validated through Western blotting, which revealed a significant reduction in the expression level of PTX3 in shPTX3-U-2OS cells compared with its level in shLuc-U-2OS cells (Fig. 6a). We further used a xenograft model to examine the effects of PTX3 knockdown on the metastasis of osteosarcoma cells in vivo. The tail veins of immunodeficient nude mice were injected with shLuc- or shPTX3-transfected U-2OS cells. The shPTX3 group had a significantly lower number of lung metastatic nodules than did the shLuc group (P < 0.01; Fig. 6b). Hematoxylin/eosin staining of lung sections indicated a significant reduction in the number of invading tumor cells in the shPTX3 group (Fig. 6c, upper). Immunohistochemical staining revealed that the expression levels of Ki-67 were significantly lower in the shPTX3 group than in the shLuc group (Fig. 6c, lower). These findings suggest that PTX3 knockdown reduces the incidence of pulmonary metastasis.

Discussion

Compared with its expression in normal tissues, PTX3 is either upregulated or downregulated in tumor tissues, serving pro- or antioncogenic functions in different types of cancer. Recent evidence indicates that PTX3 is significantly upregulated in both tumor tissues and adjacent liver tissues of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma; it can accelerate the progression of this cancer by triggering EMT, thereby acting as a promising prognostic marker and treatment target [19]. Higher levels of PTX3 expression are associated with lower rates of overall survival and recurrence-free survival in patients with small cell lung cancer [23]. This association signifies the potential of PTX3 to serve as a prognostic marker and therapeutic target for the cancer. Furthermore, PTX3 contributes to the metastasis of breast cancer by regulating the formation of chemotherapy-induced, tumor-derived small extracellular vesicles [39]. PTX3 expression is associated with poor prognosis and outcomes in triple-negative breast cancers, and disruption of the interaction between PTX3 and CD44 inhibits tumor growth and metastasis [40]. The metastasis of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma is promoted by oleate through the PTX3/vimentin pathway [41] and by epidermal growth factor through the phosphoinositide 3-kinase/Akt- and nuclear factor-κB-dependent pathways, leading to the binding of c-Jun to transcription factor activator protein-1 [42]. In melanoma, PTX3 promotes migration through the Toll-like receptor 4/nuclear factor-κB pathway [43]. Spen paralogue and orthologue C-terminal domain-containing 1 enhances the proliferation and metastasis of glioma cells by regulating PTX3 [44]. PTX3 knockdown suppresses tumorigenicity and metastasis in human cervical cancer [22]. All these findings highlight the pro-oncogenic functions of PTX3.

Several studies have demonstrated the antioncogenic functions of PTX3. PTX3 suppressed the metastasis of gastric cancer to milky spots by repressing the polarization of M2 macrophages [45]. Ronca et al. reported that tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis were inhibited in transgenic mice overexpressing PTX3. NSC12, an FGF inhibitor derived from PTX3, is useful in cancer therapy [24]. The N-terminus of PTX3 inhibited angiogenesis and tumor growth in FGF-dependent murine prostate cancer and melanoma by binding to FGFs such as FGF2 and FGF8b [46, 47].

We explored the functions of PTX3 in human osteosarcoma cells through knockdown and overexpression experiments. The in vitro results were verified in nude mice; shPTX3 was used to induce tumor metastasis to the lungs. We identified molecules from the PTX3-Stat3-MAZ pathway involved in the PTX3 knockdown–mediated inhibition of the migration of U-2OS and HOS cells. This study validated the involvement of PTX3 in the metastasis of osteosarcoma and established an experimental basis for the development of targeted therapy for this cancer. In addition, our immunohistochemical analysis of a human osteosarcoma tissue array revealed detectable PTX3 expression across tumor samples, with higher levels observed in advanced-stage disease. These findings not only corroborate our in vitro data but also emphasize the clinical relevance of PTX3 in osteosarcoma, suggesting that PTX3 may serve as a potential biomarker of disease progression and therapeutic target.

MAZ enhanced the metastasis of prostate cancer to bone tissue through the transcriptional induction of the KRAS/Ral guanine nucleotide exchange factor pathway [6] and the reciprocal regulation of the androgen receptor [7]. High levels of MAZ expression are associated with poor prognosis in thyroid cancer, influencing disease progression by triggering EMT [8]. MAZ is also involved in the tumorigenicity, progression, and metastasis of breast cancer [9, 10], gastric cancer [11], and hepatocellular carcinoma [12, 13]. We confirmed that Stat3 mediates the antimetastatic effects of the PTX3-related MAZ pathway in osteosarcoma. However, this study lacks animal experiments investigating the manipulation of PTX3 in the context of clinical practice. Moreover, our study demonstrates that downregulation of PTX3 significantly reduces pulmonary metastasis in vivo (Fig. 6b), we did not assess whether overexpression of PTX3 enhances metastasis in a similar in vivo setting. This represents a limitation, as the functional effects of PTX3 overexpression were systematically explored in vitro and in earlier parts of the study. Including an in vivo metastasis model with PTX3 overexpression would have provided a more comprehensive understanding of PTX3’s role in promoting metastatic potential. Future studies should incorporate this approach to fully delineate the contribution of PTX3 to metastasis progression.

In conclusion, suppressing PTX3 reduces the migratory and invasive abilities of human osteosarcoma cells by downregulating MAZ through the Stat3 pathway. In vivo experiments confirmed that PTX3 knockdown diminishes the pulmonary metastatic potential of osteosarcoma, while IHC results provided clinical evidence of PTX3 upregulation in advanced-stage osteosarcoma tissues. Together, these findings highlight the role of the STAT3-mediated PTX3–MAZ axis in osteosarcoma progression and underscore the potential of targeting PTX3, STAT3, and downstream MAZ to inhibit osteosarcoma metastasis (Fig. 7).
