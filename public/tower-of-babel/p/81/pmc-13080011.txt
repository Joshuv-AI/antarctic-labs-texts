Hydraulic stress limits thermal acclimation in trees under chronic drought
Authors: Alyssa T. Kullberg, Arianna Milano, Alvaro Poretti, Yike Ma, Patrick Favre, Kate M. Johnson, Giovanni Bortolami, Maxwell Bergström, Thibaut Juillard, Jin Wu, Zhengfei Guo, Jonas Gisler, Marcus Schaub, Charlotte Grossiord
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceFuture forest resilience depends on whether trees can acclimate to rising heat and drought. Using multiyear manipulations of soil moisture and air temperature, we tested how long-term acclimation shapes leaf cooling and damage in Fagus sylvatica and Quercus pubescens. Trees subjected to warming alone maintained effective leaf cooling, showing that thermal acclimation is possible when water is not limiting. However, when exposed to both heat and drought, this acclimatory response did not occur. Soil water limitation induced stomatal closure, which prevented evaporative cooling and led to severe leaf scorching in beech. Our findings reveal that chronic exposure to combined heat and drought causes a hydraulic–thermal cascade that defines a critical vulnerability of temperate forests to intensifying hot droughts.

Forests worldwide are increasingly threatened by short-lived climate extremes, chronic rise in air temperature (Tair), and increasing aridity driven by anthropogenic climate change. Over the coming decades, growing-season temperatures and vapor pressure deficit (VPD) are projected to rise, while many regions will experience long-term declines in soil water availability (1, 2). Such gradual but persistent shifts can trigger acclimatory responses in trees, including morphological, physiological, and phenological adjustments that may reshape water use, heat tolerance and avoidance, and carbon balance (3–7). Whether long-term acclimatory adjustments can protect trees during extreme events, when thermal and hydraulic stresses peak simultaneously, remains a critical uncertainty. Crown dieback and mortality are already rising globally, even in forests once thought resilient, suggesting that acclimation to current conditions may not ensure survival under future extreme conditions (8–10).

Drought-induced hydraulic stress arises as declining soil and plant water potentials limit the supply of water to leaves. Leaf hydraulic safety margins (HSMs)—the buffer between the threshold of wilting (turgor loss point, ΨTLP) and operating leaf water potentials—are typically narrow and remarkably conserved across species, averaging ~−1 MPa (11). Such tight safety margins imply that even modest increases in drought intensity can push trees toward turgor loss and reduced hydraulic conductivity. With further drying, hydraulic stress can progress toward hydraulic failure, a loss of functional water transport caused by the formation of air bubbles (emboli) in xylem conduits that reduce the capacity for water flow (12, 13). Progressive loss of conductivity limits water delivery to leaves, contributing to leaf desiccation, canopy dieback, and ultimately even tree death (11).

In parallel to water transport, leaves must maintain thermal balance. Photosynthetic tissues are heat-sensitive, with damage often initiated by loss of photosystem II (PSII) integrity above a critical temperature threshold (Tcrit) (14, 15). When water is abundant, transpirational cooling can moderate leaf temperatures (Tleaf) to maintain them below harmful thresholds (16), although canopy leaves often track or exceed Tair under high radiative load (17). Under soil drought, however, stomatal closure reduces latent heat flux, increasing thermal coupling to the atmosphere and often shifting leaves toward poikilo- and megathermy (Tleaf closely tracking and exceeding high air temperatures, respectively). Such high Tleaf can lead to narrow or even breached leaf thermal safety margins (TSMs, the difference between Tcrit and Tleaf), raising the risk of permanent damage to PSII and even leaf scorching.

Leaf scorching refers to rapid, stress-induced necrosis that prevents nutrient remobilization and can cause defoliation. Importantly, it differs fundamentally from senescence. Senescence is a regulated, adaptive process in which cells undergo a degradation sequence, allowing chlorophyll breakdown and redistribution of macronutrients (18). Repeated scorching may deplete reserves, impair hydraulics, and heighten mortality risk (19).

The coupling between hydraulic and thermal processes becomes most apparent during compound events. During the 2021 Pacific Northwest Heat Dome, leaf scorching occurred within hours at unprecedented Tair, particularly in species with shallow roots or greater hydraulic vulnerability (20–22). Such events highlight that leaf scorching arises not simply from high Tair or drought, but from the breakdown of thermoregulation once transpirational cooling fails. Yet, the mechanistic pathways linking long-term acclimation, HSMs, TSMs, and leaf scorching remain poorly described.

Intraspecific variation and phenotypic plasticity in hydraulic and thermal traits may influence the success of species responses to emerging climate regimes, yet the extent to which such acclimation can buffer against future extremes remains uncertain (23, 24). Various types of adjustments—from altered leaf structure and stomatal properties to changes in tissue water relations and Tcrit (25–27)—may enhance either water conservation or heat resistance, although sometimes in opposing ways. For example, changes in leaf anatomy and stomatal density can shift the balance between water conservation and evaporative cooling by altering maximum conductance and energy exchange. Although both adaptation (28, 29) and acclimation (30, 31) of thermal tolerance have been documented across species and climates, it is unclear whether these adjustments can sustain thermoregulation under combined heat and drought, which remains a central question to predicting forest persistence under future climates.

Here, we investigate how 5 y of exposure to heating (+5 °C), reduced soil moisture (−50% volumetric soil water, RM), and their combination (heat-RM) in experimental open-top chambers (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) affects leaf thermoregulation, HSMs, TSMs, and leaf scorching in saplings of two temperate tree species with contrasting water-use strategies: the more drought-vulnerable European beech (Fagus sylvatica L.) and the more drought-tolerant downy oak (Quercus pubescens Willd.). Results

Does Long-Term Exposure to Heat and Soil Drought Affect Leaf Thermoregulatory Outcomes?

Trees exposed to long-term RM had strongly impaired leaf thermoregulation in both species, whereas heating alone had minimal impact. Under RM, the leaf-air temperature difference (ΔT) was relatively greater at higher Tair than in controls (Fig. 1 A and B and SI Appendix, Table S1), indicating a shift from limited homeothermy (maintenance of Tleaf despite changes in Tair; mean ΔT ~ Tair with ample soil water were −0.14 ± 0.07 for beech and −0.19 ± 0.04 for oak) toward poikilothermy (mean ΔT ~ Tair in RM were 0.02 ± 0.11 for beech and −0.03 ± 0.10 for oak). Both mean ΔT and maximum Tleaf were significantly greater under both heating and RM in both species (Fig. 1 C and D and SI Appendix, Table S1). There was no interactive effect of heating and RM for any of these relationships. Leaf energy balance modeling, parameterized only with radiative and atmospheric drivers (i.e., not explicitly accounting for soil water limitation), reproduced observed Tleaf under well-watered conditions but systematically underpredicted Tleaf at high temperatures under RM (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Table S2). This growing divergence under soil drought indicates that additional stomatal limitation reduced latent heat dissipation beyond that expected from atmospheric forcing alone, resulting in higher-than-predicted Tleaf under RM. In oak, this bias was further amplified by heating.

Leaf-level hydraulic stress peaked in August, when predawn water potentials were reduced for beech in all treatments and for oak under RM (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), and nearly all trees under RM surpassed their HSMs at midday (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Table S1). Heating further intensified this stress in beech. Stomatal conductance to water vapor (gs) was sharply reduced under RM throughout the summer, especially under combined heat-RM in oak, and slightly under heating in beech (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Table S1). Under RM, higher ΔT was associated with significantly lower gs than compared to controls, indicating reduced latent heat dissipation under RM—in contrast to maintained gs in controls—as hydraulic stress increased (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 and Table S1).

How are Thermoregulatory Outcomes Modulated by Structural and Physiological Acclimation?

Leaf structural acclimation was modest but aligned with water-conservative strategies (SI Appendix, Table S7). Neither species altered leaf area or width across treatments. In beech, RM led to thinner epidermal and parenchyma layers. Additionally, reductions in stomatal density and size, significant under heating and marginal under RM, resulted in highly significant declines in theoretical maximum stomatal conductance (gs,max). Oak showed no changes in leaf thickness, but heated trees had a lower parenchyma-to-epidermis ratio, and RM trees had less dense stomata, both of which can reduce minimum leaf conductance.

Tcrit did not differ among treatments in either species (overall mean 44.25 ± 2.87 °C for beech and 43.97 ± 2.38 °C for oak), although there was a nonsignificant tendency toward higher Tcrit under RM (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Table S3). Notwithstanding, in both species Tcrit increased with maximum Tleaf (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Table S4), indicating partial acclimation to higher Tleaf. However, Tcrit acclimation was insufficient to fully compensate for Tleaf increase (0.37 ± 0.16 °C increase in Tcrit per °C maximum Tleaf for beech and 0.38 ± 0.17 for oak), leading to significantly reduced TSMs under both heating and RM, with no interactive effect under heat-RM (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Table S3).

Despite clear hydraulic limitations to leaf thermoregulation, TSMs were unrelated to HSMs in both species (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Table S4). Nonetheless, in beech, heating altered this relationship, such that trees under moderate hydraulic stress (HSMs approaching zero) showed broader TSMs, indicating a possible tradeoff between hydraulic and thermal safety. Under combined stresses, this tradeoff reversed, with greater hydraulic stress (often breaching HSMs) leading to lower TSMs, indicating a coordinated collapse of both safety margins. Oak showed similar but nonsignificant trends.

Is Physiological Vulnerability Associated with Leaf Damage?

Leaf scorching, tracked continuously with cameras with integrated infrared sensors attached to leaves, occurred in both species but was frequent and severe (up to 30% of the leaf area of interest at once) only in beech (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). Because the cameras partially shaded leaves, the observed incidence and severity of scorching are conservative. For oak, scorching was observed on some sun-exposed leaves, but its low apparent frequency within the camera field of view provided insufficient data for quantitative analysis. For beech, in which scorching was more frequent overall, the probability of scorching occurring on a given day was low but rose sharply under RM, especially when TSMs were breached (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Table S5). The combined effect of heating and RM was not additive; however, the interaction of heat-RM with TSMs significantly increased scorching probability, indicating that even leaves with higher TSMs became vulnerable to scorching under compound stress.

Then, looking only at days when scorching occurred in beech, we predicted scorching severity. Overall, scorching events were marginally more severe when TSMs were low. RM alone strongly increased scorching severity (Fig. 4B and SI Appendix, Table S6), and this effect was also significantly amplified by low TSMs. Heating further marginally enhanced scorching severity (i.e., heat-RM), even at wider TSMs. In other words, heating and RM interacted to increase scorching severity, indicating that once hydraulic cooling fails, even thermally buffered leaves become vulnerable.

Discussion

This study shows that 5 y of exposure to heating allows juvenile, temperate trees to maintain relatively stable leaf temperatures, but conversely, chronic soil drought cuts off thermoregulation through hydraulic limitation. When both stresses co-occur, leaf-level hydraulic limitation triggers stomatal closure and consequently rising leaf temperatures, leading to leaf scorching when stressors reach their peak intensity—a chain reaction most pronounced in the drought-vulnerable beech trees, whereas oak generally showed similar safety margins across treatments while exhibiting less visible damage under comparable stress. These findings reveal that acclimation to warming of up to 5 °C allows for sustained thermal balance, but only when soil water remains ample.

Hydraulic Limitation Drives the Breakdown of Thermoregulation.

Our results identify leaf-level hydraulic limitation as the key mechanism linking soil drought to thermoregulatory failure. In well-watered trees, elevated gs supported sustained transpiration, enabling partial homeothermy even under prolonged heating. Under RM, narrowed HSMs and consequently lower gs forced leaves toward poikilothermy. When heating was also present (heat-RM), this hydraulic stress was amplified, triggering a feedback loop between declining water potential, stomatal closure, and leaf overheating. Overall, responses were remarkably similar between beech and oak, suggesting that soil drought imposed a common hydraulic bottleneck on thermoregulation.

Residuals from leaf energy balance modeling reinforce this interpretation: Model predictions matched observed Tleaf in well-watered trees but increasingly underestimated Tleaf under RM, especially in combination with heating. This mismatch demonstrates that actual leaves overheat beyond expectations once water transport is constrained. The energy balance model used in this study considers atmospheric drought (VPD) and does not explicitly incorporate soil water availability or plant hydraulic constraints (32). We therefore used the model as a baseline expectation of leaf temperature driven solely by radiative and atmospheric forcing. Systematic underprediction of Tleaf under RM indicates that additional limitations on gs arising from soil water stress (33, 34), not captured by the model, further reduced latent heat dissipation. These systematic residuals indicate that hydraulic limitation of gs likely drove the excess leaf heating under soil drought.

Safety Margin Tradeoffs Reveal the Cascade from Hydraulic to Thermal Stress.

Thermal tolerance acclimated to prevailing leaf thermal regimes, as shown by the positive relationship between Tcrit and maximum Tleaf. This finding mirrors interspecific trends, where species from warmer climates exhibit higher thermal thresholds (29). However, acclimation was insufficient to offset overheating under drought, leading to reduced TSMs. Notably, Mas et al. (35), working on the same experimental system, reported increased ΨTLP under chronic RM in both species, leading to earlier stomatal closure as soils dry. This shift conserves water but further constrains evaporative cooling (36).

The interaction between hydraulic and thermal stress manifested in context-dependent HSM–TSM dynamics. Trees under heating alone preserved their safety margins, whereas RM slightly narrowed both. When both stressors co-occurred, breaching of HSMs triggered stomatal closure, loss of cooling, and rising Tleaf that often surpassed TSMs. Although the magnitudes of HSMs and TSMs were broadly comparable between species, heating intensified hydraulic stress more strongly in beech, whereas oak showed similar trends with greater variability.

The interaction between hydraulic and thermal safety mirrors findings in other systems. For instance, Posch et al. (37) showed that Populus fremontii from hotter, drier, low-elevation populations maintained relatively wider TSMs by sacrificing HSMs, demonstrating that safety margins can adapt to different environments, which can lead to varied outcomes even under common growing conditions. Our results extend this finding by showing that under combined heat and soil drought, the tradeoff between TSMs and HSMs observed in both studies under well-watered conditions collapses entirely. More broadly, this highlights that acclimation effective under single stressors may fail when stressors interact (3), suggesting a tipping point for forest resilience under intensifying hot droughts.

Morphoanatomical Acclimation Favors Water Conservation over Thermal Safety.

Structural acclimation prioritized water saving over heat protection despite known links between leaf traits and energy balance (16). In beech, thinner leaves and coordinated reductions in stomatal density and dimensions translated into lower gs,max, indicating decreased anatomical capacity for gas exchange. This conservative leaf design likely delays hydraulic failure but constrains latent cooling, highlighting a trade-off between thermal safety and hydraulic stability. Oak, in contrast, showed little change in gs,max, reflecting weaker morphoanatomical adjustment and greater reliance on whole-plant regulation, although reduced parenchyma:epidermal thickness under RM indicates shifts toward protection from water loss that are not captured by gs,max.

Indeed, adjustments at the canopy scale can further modify plant water demand. In the same experimental system, Mas et al. (35) reported substantial reductions in total leaf area under RM in both species, delaying modeled time to hydraulic failure by lowering whole-plant transpirational demand. These structural changes may therefore compensate when morphoanatomical acclimation is insufficient, although leaves remain vulnerable to overheating once stomata close. The comparatively weaker morphoanatomical response in oak likely reflects regulation at the whole-plant scale: Oak reduced leaf area more strongly and exhibited a less negative ΨTLP than beech, suggesting greater reliance on canopy-level demand reduction and dynamic stomatal control rather than anatomical modification of conductance capacity, while maintaining a high gs,max that allows the species to take advantage of periods of favorable water availability. Nevertheless, because HSMs were still exceeded under severe drought, these adjustments mitigated but did not fully prevent hydraulic stress.

Leaf Scorching Reflects the Interaction of Hydraulic and Thermal Stress.

Even using conservative thresholds of physiological vulnerability, we observed parallel patterns between safety margins and leaf damage. In beech, both the probability and severity of scorching increased when TSMs were breached, especially under RM, supporting a mechanistic link between high Tleaf, PSII impairment, and tissue necrosis. Notably, both species exhibited comparable thermal and HSMs, yet scorching was far more frequent and severe in beech. This contrast indicates that species differed not in intrinsic tolerance or safety margins, but in how thermal and hydraulic stress translated into irreversible tissue damage in vivo. The more pronounced late-summer decline in predawn water potentials observed in beech likely contributed to this pattern by accelerating stomatal closure and reducing evaporative cooling, increasing susceptibility to scorching.

Similar outcomes to our findings in beech have been reported elsewhere, for example, Fraxinus mandshurica (38) and Quercus ilex (39) showed stronger scorching under combined stress than either stressor alone. Marchin et al. (40) linked crown dieback to drought vulnerability, finding that drought-avoiding species with lower Tcrit were at greater risk, and observed widespread leaf scorching during hot droughts. Our results also extend previous observations that lower Tcrit is associated with increased leaf scorching (41) by demonstrating in real-time that 1) increased scorching is related to breaches of these thermal thresholds and that 2) scorching events are exacerbated by hydraulic stress. Here, we provide experimental confirmation of this mechanism, showing that hydraulic suppression of cooling can negate the protective effect of higher intrinsic Tcrit and lead to permanent leaf damage.

Lifelong Acclimation to Chronic Heat, but Not Combined with Soil Drought.

Trees can acclimate to long-term heat alone, but this capacity is markedly reduced when combined with chronic soil drought. Beech, despite its plasticity, cannot maintain hydraulic or thermal safety once soil water becomes limiting. This vulnerability aligns with field evidence of drought-induced crown dieback and legacy mortality in the same species following recent European hot droughts (42, 43). In contrast, downy oak’s more conservative water-use strategy [i.e., higher ΨTLP, Mas et al. (35)] may have reduced the frequency with which leaves experienced damaging thermal conditions, consistent with the lower incidence of scorching observed in this species. This interspecific difference highlights that water-use strategy—not thermal tolerance per se—may underlie resilience to compound stress.

Together, our results show that leaf-level hydraulic limitation governs thermoregulation in juvenile beech and oak trees under chronic stress. Once water transport ceases, structural acclimation and thermal tolerance cannot prevent overheating. The interaction between hydraulic and thermal stress is decisive for leaf scorching in beech, with the greatest risk occurring when both safety margins are exceeded. These findings underscore a limit to tree acclimation even in temperate trees and suggest that increasing hot droughts may push forests beyond physiological limits as climates warm and dry.

Materials and Methods

Study Site and Specimens.

This study was conducted at the Model Ecosystem Facility (Modoek) of the Swiss Federal Institute for Forest, Snow, and Landscape Research in Birmensdorf, Switzerland (47° 21’ 42”N, 8° 27’ 18”E). Modoek comprises 16 hexagonal open-top chambers, each subdivided into eight compartments, and planted in 2018 with 2-y-old beech (F. sylvatica) and oak (Q. pubescens). Each chamber was assigned one of four fully crossed climatic treatments (four chambers per treatment): Ambient Tair and irrigation at field capacity (control), Tair elevated by 5 °C above control chambers (heat), reduced soil moisture to about 50% compared to control (RM), and a combined treatment (heat-RM), reflecting projected future conditions in the region (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). For this study, we sampled six trees per species per treatment (n = 48). To avoid confounding effects within chambers, only one tree per compartment was randomly selected, with a maximum of two trees per species per chamber (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Further methodological details are provided in the SI Appendix, Supporting Text. The data produced from this study are available at https://www.doi.org/10.16904/envidat.720 (44).

Leaf Temperatures.

Leaf temperatures (Tleaf) were collected from June 26 to September 25, 2024, using infrared cameras (Optris Xi400, 382 × 288 pixels, 80° × 54°, f = 5.7 mm, ±2 °C accuracy; Optris GmH & Co. KG, Berlin, DE) integrated into chamber roofs. See SI Appendix, Supporting Text for details on camera calibration and validation.

We selected a 1-cm diameter area covering sun-exposed leaves for each focal tree in which the minimum pixel value within the area of interest (to avoid capturing ground temperature when branches sway) was averaged every 10 min. Some cameras malfunctioned, leading to successful Tleaf recordings for 40 of the 48 trees, with at least four per species per treatment. We retained Tleaf between 7:00 and 17:00 h (local time) for analysis, corresponding to daylight hours during which other measurements were taken. Species-level averages of daily maximum Tleaf are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2.

Structural Leaf Traits.

Measured leaf traits included leaf area, effective leaf width, stomatal density, guard cell width, stomatal pore length, leaf thickness, parenchyma thickness, epidermal thickness, and parenchyma:epidermis. Two beech trees (one RM and one heat-RM) had no remaining leaves and were excluded, and two additional heat-RM trees were excluded from leaf thickness measurements due to premature senescence. We calculated theoretical maximum stomatal conductance (gs,max) with stomatal density and pore length data, according to Monda et al. (45–47). See SI Appendix, Supporting Text for further details.

Stomatal Conductance.

We measured leaf-level stomatal conductance to water vapor (gs) with an infrared gas exchange analyzer (Licor LI-6800 Portable Photosynthesis System; LI-COR Environmental, Lincoln, Nebraska, USA) between 7:00 and 17:00 h (local time). One healthy, sun-exposed leaf was chosen at the beginning of the study for each tree, which was measured once diurnally in June, July, August, and September. Reliable gs measurements could not be obtained for one RM and four heat-RM beech trees in August and September due to senescence. Relative humidity, Tair (5 °C above ambient in heated chambers), and photosynthetic photon flux density were set to ambient conditions, the CO2 concentration to 420 ppm, the flow rate to 500 µmol s-1, and the fan speed to 10,000 rpm.

Leaf Energy Balance Modeling.

We applied a steady-state leaf energy balance model (32) to evaluate how well Tleaf could be predicted across treatments. The model solves Tleaf by balancing absorbed shortwave and longwave radiation with sensible and latent heat fluxes. It was parameterized with leaf trait data averaged to the tree level, including visible light absorptance (PolyPen RP 410 UVIS, Photon Systems Instruments, Drásov, Czech Republic), Medlyn-style stomatal slope g1 (48), the maximum rate of Rubisco carboxylation at 25 °C [Vcmax25 estimated by Deluigi et al. (49, 50)], and effective leaf width. The model was also parameterized with hourly environmental parameters, including air temperature, relative humidity, windspeed, and photosynthetically active radiation. Model performance was assessed by comparing modeled with observed Tleaf and analyzing residuals using linear mixed models with individual tree as a random intercept to detect systematic biases and treatment effects.

Leaf HSMs.

We measured predawn and midday leaf water potential of each tree once in June, July, August, and September with a Scholander-type pressure chamber (M1505D; PMS Instruments, Albany, Oregon, USA). HSMs for each tree and month were determined as the difference between ΨTLP [measured by Mas et al. (35, 51)] and midday water potential.

Leaf Thermal Tolerance and TSMs.

We determined photosynthetic thermal tolerance as the temperature inducing an initial decline in PSII function (Tcrit). In July, August, and September, we treated one leaf disc per tree in hermetically sealed bags at each of 11 temperatures (25, 30, 35, 38, 41, 44, 47, 50, 55, 60, and 65 °C) in a circulating water bath (TSCIR19; ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) for 15 min, within 2 h of sampling. Discs rested 24 h in the dark on moist paper towels to account for potential recovery of PSII before dark-adapted maximum quantum yield of PSII (Fv/Fm) was measured using a pulse-amplitude modulation fluorometer (FP-100 FluorPen; Photon Systems Instruments, Drásov, Czechia).

We modeled the relationship between Fv/Fm and Tleaf using a four-parameter logistic function fitted with the nls function in the “stats” package in R (as in 31). Starting estimates were set at 0.8 for the upper asymptote, and slope and lower asymptote estimates were derived from the coefficients of a logit-transformed linear regression of Fv/Fm over Tleaf. Tcrit was defined as the Tleaf at which the slope of the Fv/Fm decline reached 15% of its most negative value. Tcrit was not successfully estimated in September for several beech trees in control (n = 2), RM (n = 2), and heat-RM (n = 4) due to high variability in the data or insufficient leaf material.

TSMs were determined for each tree and campaign as the difference between Tcrit and maximum Tleaf in the week preceding Tcrit determination.

Leaf Scorching.

To detect leaf scorching, we used Raspberry Pi-based time-lapse cameras with 30× magnification, LED backlighting (Cavicams; https://cavicams.com), and integrated infrared sensors (i.e., ensuring precise Tleaf for the field of view) fitted onto sun-exposed leaves (2.7 cm above leaf surface) of 24 trees (three per species and treatment), which simultaneously recorded photos of the leaf surface (3 × 3 cm) and Tleaf every 10 min throughout the study period (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). The integrated LED lighting was activated only during image capture and never left on continuously, minimizing artificial heat or light exposure. Cameras partially shaded leaves from direct sunlight, and Tleaf was measured directly with the integrated sensors, ensuring Tleaf was accurately captured despite the camera presence. Due to power supply issues, three cameras did not produce sufficient photos for analysis, including one control beech, one heat-RM beech, and one control oak. Photos were visually inspected for leaf scorching occurrence, defined as discrete discoloration events affecting well-defined patches. Scorching severity was determined in ImageJ as the percentage of the leaf area visible in the field of view affected during a single scorching event. TSMs as they relate to leaf scorching (i.e., results presented in Fig. 4) were determined by subtracting the maximum Tleaf (determined with the integrated infrared sensor) within 3 d before scorching from Tcrit, where the Tcrit is taken as the subsequent Tcrit measurement after a given scorching date (or the most recent Tcrit measurement for individuals that were not successfully remeasured in August and/or September).

Statistical Analysis.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (v. 4.4.2; R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria, 2022). To test the single and interactive effects of heating and RM on leaf thermoregulation and underlying mechanisms, we conducted two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for each species. Dependent variables were summarized to the tree level, and trees were treated as independent observational units. Because only one or two individuals per species were sampled per chamber, inclusion of chamber as a random effect resulted in singular boundary fits and an unidentifiable variance component; moreover, sampled trees were always located in separate compartments to minimize potential within-chamber dependence. Dependent variables included the slope of ΔT over Tair, mean ΔT, maximum Tleaf, and mean gs. We also conducted two-way ANOVAs to assess variation in HSM (using August data to capture midsummer stress) and in the slope of ΔT over the natural log of gs, where ΔT was averaged hourly when gs was measured for each tree. We likewise used two-way ANOVAs for each species to test leaf morphoanatomical acclimation to each treatment.

To test how physiological acclimation modulated thermoregulation, we conducted linear mixed models (LMMs) separately for beech and oak with the lmer function in the “lme4” R package (52), using individual as a random effect. Then, we tested for physiological acclimation in terms of Tcrit and resulting TSM. First, we set Tcrit or TSM as the dependent variable, with the single and interactive effects of heating and RM as the independent variables. Then, we added maximum Tleaf (during each campaign, see SI Appendix, Fig. S2) to the model explaining Tcrit acclimation to account for variation in Tleaf between individuals and time periods, and we added HSM to the model explaining TSM.

Finally, we conducted a two-part analysis to assess the probability and severity of leaf scorching in beech, focusing on heating, RM, their interaction, and TSM. We used the glmmTMB function in the “glmmTMB” package to construct generalized LMMs (53). HSMs were not included in the analyses due to low sampling resolution (n = 4 per tree). The first model predicted the probability of scorching, using a binary response variable (whether scorching was observed on a given day for each tree) with a binomial distribution and logit link. The second model predicted scorching severity (when present), using the percentage of scorched area as the response, modeled with a Poisson distribution and log link. Both models used individual tree as random intercept.
