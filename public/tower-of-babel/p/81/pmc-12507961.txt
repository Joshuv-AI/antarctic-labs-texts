Radiologic definition of lateral pelvic nodal compartments–The next frontier in rectal imaging
Authors: Harmeet Kaur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The accurate localization of lateral pelvic nodes is essential for staging and surgical planning in rectal cancer. The objective of this article is to review existing radiologic and surgical definitions of the boundaries of lateral pelvic lymph node compartments on cross-sectional CT/MR images. In addition, we propose a simplified approach to facilitate the application of these boundaries to CT/MR images. We also discuss a few pitfalls in the localization of pelvic nodes in rectal cancer.

Introduction

In the past 40 years, there have been incremental advances in the treatment of rectal cancer. This started with the recognition of the importance of resecting the intact mesorectum to prevent disruption of the meso-rectal lympho-vascular network, aptly named total meso-rectal excision (TME) [1]. This surgical approach resulted in a significant decline in postoperative local recurrences from up to 36% in the pre-TME era to 8.2% or less with TME [2–4]. When TME was combined with neoadjuvant chemo-radiation, local relapse rates further declined by 50% [4, 5]. In the West, lateral pelvic nodal metastasis in rectal cancer have been treated primarily by chemoradiation. With the sharp decline in regional recurrence related to the rectal primary, most local relapses (54–82.7%) are now seen to develop in the lateral pelvic nodal compartment. This suggests that reliance on chemo-radiation as a means of sterilizing lateral pelvic nodes may not be sufficient and a subcategory of rectal cancer patients require surgical resection [6, 7]. It is essential to be able to identify the rectal cancers that are most likely to have persistent lateral pelvic nodal metastasis and would benefit from surgical resection after chemoradiation.

The dominant paradigm of assessing nodal involvement on CT or MRI relies on size and/or morphologic features, although it is widely recognized that this is fraught with limitations. This is reflected in the low accuracy and lack of prognostic significance of MR mesorectal node staging [98]. For lateral pelvic nodes, this limitation can be improved upon by understanding pathways of rectal lymphatic drainage. Therefore, rather than an indiscriminate application of size criteria to any pelvic node, this approach limits evaluation to nodes in the appropriate lymphatic drainage zone. This method can be combined with factors like T stage, tumor differentiation and size, presence of mesorectal nodes, extra-mural vascular invasion, etc., which increase the pretest probability of lateral nodal metastasis and improve the accuracy of nodal staging [9].

Radiologic classification of lateral pelvic lymph nodes

The Lateral Node Study Consortium recently proposed a color-coded MR atlas clarifying boundaries of lateral pelvic lymph node compartments [16]. There have also been previous radiologic papers defining the pelvic nodal compartments. These approaches have been detailed in the sections below with discussion on areas of overlap and points of difference [1918].

External iliac nodes

External iliac nodes are sub-classified in the radiology literature into lateral, middle, and medial chain nodes based on the relationship to the external iliac vessels [1918]. This subclassification is relevant as different subgroups have distinct afferent and efferent lymphatic pathways or lymphatic drainage zones. The lateral and middle external iliac nodes are located lateral to and between the external iliac artery and vein. These subgroups receive lymphatic drainage from the lower extremity, perineum, anatomic anal canal (below the dentate line), and distal third of the vagina. However, they do not receive visceral lymphatics from the rectum, and consequently enlarged nodes in these locations in rectal cancer are rarely metastatic (Fig. 5). The exception is rectal tumors arising or extending below the dentate line. The nodes located posterior medial to the external iliac vein receive visceral lymphatics from the rectum and other pelvic organs and have been previously classified as medial chain external iliac nodes [19]. The medial chain external iliac nodes are not clearly distinguished from obturator nodes in the radiology literature; the terms have been used interchangeably and are a source of considerable confusion. Common iliac nodes

Common iliac nodes accompany vessels of the same name and are also sub-classified into medial, middle, and lateral subgroups. The medial common iliac nodes are in the triangular space between the common iliac arteries below the aortic bifurcation. The middle chain subgroup nodes are in the lumbosacral fossa posterior to the common iliac artery and medial to the psoas muscle.

Controversies and limitations in classification of lateral pelvic lymph nodes.

Although the Consortium Color Atlas has provided some clarity regarding the boundaries of the external iliac, obturator, and internal iliac compartments, these visual outlines can be difficult to recall unless the atlas is available while reviewing CT/MR scans. In addition, questions persist, for example, how should nodes at the boundaries of compartments be categorized? The Consortium also does not address the issue of external iliac subgroups . This is significant as lateral and middle external iliac nodes do not receive visceral lymphatics from pelvic organs and the assessment of nodes based only on size will lead to overtreatment (Fig. 5).

The most common sources of persistent confusion are separating the obturator from external iliac nodes and high obturator from common iliac nodes, which impacts staging, and internal iliac from obturator nodes, which impacts the probability of nodal involvement and surgical planning [19,18].

Simplified landmarks to define lateral pelvic node compartments

We have previously proposed a simplified approach using placement of planes at the pelvic brim, mid, and lower pelvis, which delineate the internal iliac and obturator compartments using the Lateral Node Study Consortium Color Atlas landmarks [2019]. These planes can be supplemented by using the internal iliac artery and its branches as a landmark for the internal iliac compartment nodes and the obturator nerve as a landmark for defining obturator nodes particularly in the upper and mid pelvis. The Lateral Node Study Consortium Atlas uses the obturator nerve to define the upper and mid portion of the obturator compartment without explicating stating this, reflecting the surgical guidelines. We have observed that the obturator nerve is consistently and easily defined on CT/MR images and propose this structure should be used to facilitate definition of obturator nodes (Figs. 1,2,3, and 4).

Internal iliac nodes

The internal iliac artery and its visceral branches are the key anatomic structures for defining internal iliac nodes. This is because lymphatics and nodes from the lower rectum follow along these arteries. On CT/MR images, all lymph nodes seen along the course of the internal iliac vessels and its anterior branches, i.e., the middle rectal, inferior, and superior vesical branches, can all be included in the internal iliac node compartment. We have also explicitly defined the boundaries of the internal iliac compartment based on boundaries shown in the Consortium Color Atlas (Table 1). Application of size criteria for the assessment of lateral pelvic nodal involvement

The involvement of lateral pelvic nodes is related to lateral lymphatic drainage from the pelvic viscera. Since the rectum above the anterior peritoneal reflection drains almost exclusively upward along the inferior mesenteric artery, lateral pelvic nodal metastasis are rarely seen in upper rectal tumors with an incidence of 0–1.5% [21]. Rectal tumors located at and below the anterior peritoneal reflection have lymphatic drainage both upwards and laterally to lateral pelvic nodes and can develop lateral pelvic nodal metastasis.

T stage is the next most significant factor impacting the incidence of lateral pelvic nodal metastasis. Lateral pelvic nodal metastases develop in only 5.4–8.2% of T1-2 low rectal tumors but are found in 16.5–37.2% of T3-4 low rectal tumors [21].

Additional factors increasing the incidence of lateral pelvic nodal involvement include tumors > 4 cm, non-well-differentiated tumors, and the presence of metastatic mesorectal nodes [21].

Various size criteria have been proposed for lateral pelvic nodes both prior to and post chemo-radiation. The study cohorts in studies used to generate nodal size criteria are confined to T3–4 low rectal tumors < 8 cm from the anal verge. Consequently, the size criteria have different implications for T1–2 rectal tumors related to low pretest probability of lateral nodal metastasis. The criteria are also not applicable for rectal tumors above the anterior peritoneal reflection and have been validated primarily for nodes located in the obturator and internal iliac compartments.

A recent, well-designed, multi-institutional Lateral Node Study Consortium found ≥ 7 mm (short axis) nodes on the pretreatment scan were associated with 19.5% incidence of lateral recurrence post-neoadjuvant chemo-radiation in the absence of surgical resection. In smaller, ≤ 5 mm nodes, the reported lateral recurrence was 6.4% and rose to 33–36.7% for nodes > 10 mm [15, 22, 23]. Since 19.5% lateral recurrence was deemed unacceptably high, this was suggested as an appropriate size cutoff for lateral pelvic nodes on pretreatment MRI scans [6].

Consideringing these factors, it seems reasonable to suggest a size range rather than a size cutoff for assessment of lateral pelvic nodes. Radiologists and clinicians should be aware of the probabilities reflected by size criteria and make informed clinical decisions taking additional factors into consideration; 5–10 mm (short axis) is a reasonable size range for obturator and internal iliac nodes [20].

Regarding size criteria on post-treatment scans, the Lateral Node Study Consortium found if enlarged nodes on pretreatment scans decreased to < 4 mm in size post chemo-radiation, no lateral recurrences were identified. The conclusions of a post hoc analysis of the initial Lateral Node Study data for the implications of residual nodes > 4 mm are controversial. However, an interesting observation that merits mention and emphasizes the importance of the location of lateral pelvic nodes in predicting metastasis is that persistence of ≥ 4 mm nodes in the internal iliac region had a 52.3% lateral recurrence rate [1615].

In confusing cases, this can be supplemented by using the obturator nerve as all nodes adjacent or abutting the nerve from the point it crosses lateral to the common iliac bifurcation till it exits the obturator foramen are classified as obturator nodes (Figs. 3 and 4).

This is most commonly an issue for superior rectal nodes at the level of the pelvic inlet, which are often misclassified as left common iliac nodes. The definition of adjacent vessels as anatomic landmarks and use of multiplanar imaging can prevent such errors. Conclusion

Lateral pelvic lymph node metastases develop in rectal tumors located at and below the anterior peritoneal reflection. The size criteria proposed for lateral pelvic nodes are for T3–4 tumors, < 8 cm from the verge. Almost all lateral pelvic nodal metastases in rectal cancer localize to the obturator and internal iliac nodal compartments. We review the boundaries and subclassification of lateral pelvic node compartments. In addition, we have proposed strategic placement of anatomic planes to reflect the Lateral Node Study Consortium Color Atlas landmarks. We also suggest the obturator nerve as a landmark on CT/MR images to identify obturator nodes, a method informed by the surgical classification of these nodes.
