Biological sex and its influence on delirium in adult ICU patients: a single-centre retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jessica Cassavaugh, Marina Sennaraj, Mei Chen, Meiyang Liu, Siddhartha R Janga, Carsten Marcus, Margaret M Hayes, Balachundhar Subramaniam, Somnath Bose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDelirium is common among ICU patients and has been shown to be consistently associated with worse outcomes. Distinct outcomes among biological sexes in ICUs are being increasingly recognised; however, their role in the risk of delirium remains poorly characterised.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included adult patients admitted to ICUs at a tertiary-level academic medical centre from 2011 to 2024. The primary outcome was the incidence of delirium, measured using the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU assessment tool as part of routine clinical care. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to characterise the association between biological sex and ICU delirium. Incidence of delirium between differing age groups and ICU admission types was also evaluated.ResultsOf the 38 192 patients included, 56.1% (21 541) were male. In total, 46% (17 447) of patients experienced delirium during their ICU stay. Compared with female patients, male patients had a lower incidence of ICU delirium (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 0.88; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.83–0.93; P<0.001). Stratified by admission type, male patients, both <60 yr and 60 yr and above, admitted to medical ICUs had a lower risk of developing delirium compared with female patients (aOR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.72–0.93; P=0.002; aOR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.83–0.97; P=0.008, respectively), whereas this association was not significant among patients admitted to surgical ICUs, regardless of age group.ConclusionsMale patients had a lower incidence of ICU delirium. This association was most notable among patients admitted to medical ICUs. Assessment for delirium risk may include consideration of biological sex.Keywords: biological sex, critical illness, delirium, medical ICU, neurocognitive dysfunction

Delirium, or acute brain dysfunction, is characterised by fluctuating changes in cognition, disturbance of consciousness, and attention.1 Delirium remains highly prevalent in ICUs, with varying reports affecting up to 87% of admitted patients.2, 3, 4, 5 ICU delirium is associated with adverse outcomes, including increased mortality, prolonged length of stay in the ICU and hospital, long-term cognitive impairment, and greater hospital costs.6, 7, 8

The development of delirium involves a complex interplay of patient attributes, comorbidities, clinical characteristics, therapeutic interventions, and environmental influences, but the relative contributions and interactions of these risk factors remain unclear. Recently, the role of biological sex in patient outcomes in the ICU has gained recognition, particularly for overall 30-day mortality and in specific diagnoses such as cardiac arrest.9, 10, 11 However, the impact of biological sex on ICU delirium remains understudied and, as a result, current understanding is limited.

Differences between biological sexes include distinct hormonal, anatomical, physiological, and pharmacokinetic attributes. However, a substantial, comprehensive assessment of these attributes and their relationship to ICU delirium is lacking. Limited studies with small, specialised cohorts have explored this relationship, often with either conflicting conclusions between studies or no association.5,12,13 As a result, significant knowledge gaps exist in our understanding of the role of this key patient characteristic factor in the development of ICU delirium. Systematic evaluation of this association is vital to understand and characterise determinants of sex-based differences in ICU delirium.

Our current study investigates the link between biological sex and ICU delirium through the hypothesis that females have a higher incidence of ICU delirium. The primary aim of the study is to identify an association between self-identified biological sex and ICU delirium among a large, heterogeneous population of adult patients admitted to tertiary-level ICUs over a 13-yr period. Secondarily, we aim to assess whether this association is modified by age and admission type (medical vs surgical).

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted between 2011 and 2024 at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Centre (BIDMC), a tertiary-level academic medical centre in Boston, USA, with 77 licensed multidisciplinary ICU beds currently. Patient data was abstracted from electronic health records and analysed after de-identification. The study was approved by the Committee on Clinical Investigations at BIDMC (Protocol number 2024P000433), with the need for informed consent waived. Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines were followed to report this study.14 Adult patients (>18 yr old) admitted to any ICU at BIDMC for ≥48 h over a 13-yr period (2011–2024) with valid Confusion Assessment Method-Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) assessments were included.3 Supplementary methods display a detailed description of the missing data, exploratory, and sensitivity analyses.

Primary analysis

The primary exposure was self-reported biological sex, as recorded in the electronic medical record, hereafter referred to as biological sex in the manuscript. Biological sex was coded as a binary variable: male or female. The incidence of delirium, defined as a positive CAM-ICU assessment at any point during the ICU stay, served as the primary outcome. CAM-ICU assessments recorded by ICU nurses, typically once per shift as part of routine care, were used for this analysis. The outcome variable was calculated as the number of positive CAM-ICU assessments reported during the ICU stay divided by the ICU length of stay, which yielded the proportion of ICU days with delirium. As this variable was highly zero-inflated, it was recoded into a binary variable to improve model stability and clinical interpretability (Supplementary Fig. 1). Therefore, patients with any reported delirium during their ICU stay were coded as 1 (delirium present), and those with no reported ICU delirium were coded as 0 (no delirium).

Secondary, exploratory, and sensitivity analysis

To investigate whether the association between biological sex and ICU delirium was modified by age, secondary analyses were conducted. Patients were stratified into three age groups: 18–39, 40–59, and ≥ 60 yr, and the association was tested within each age group. Patients were then categorised into a binary classification: <60 yr and ≥60 yr, and the association was investigated for these categories. The ≥ 60-yr threshold was chosen to reflect the combination of the geriatric consensus definition of “older” age and the likelihood of completely transitioning through menopause.15 We conducted a post hoc propensity-matched analysis to confirm the robustness of our primary findings. Further exploratory analyses were conducted to assess whether the relationship between biological sex and delirium was affected by the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic (1 January 2020 to 31 December 2022), the type of admission (i.e. medical vs surgical ICUs), length of ICU stay, history of depression, and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) severity score.

Statistical analyses

A multivariable logistic regression model was used to explore the association between ICU delirium and self-reported biological sex. We compared male and female sex in the total study population, and within medical and surgical ICU cohorts across the under-60 and 60-yr-and-above age groups. Confounding variables for the multivariable logistic regression analyses were selected a priori based on literature and clinical plausibility, which included key patient characteristic, clinical, and ICU care characteristics detailed in the Supplemental Methods and Supplementary Fig. 2. The performance of our multivariable logistic regression model was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic curve (Supplementary Fig. 3). E-values, as described in the Supplemental Methods, were calculated for the model to evaluate the robustness of our primary analysis findings to any unmeasured confounding. Variables are reported as absolute numbers (percentages) for categorical variables and means (standard deviations [sd]) for normally distributed variables. APACHE severity score and ICU length of stay are reported as continuous variables but were dichotomised for subgroup analysis (Supplementary Table 1). Results from the multivariable logistic regression model are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All tests were two-sided, and a P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Standardised mean differences (SMDs) were calculated to quantify differences between male and female groups; SMD≥0.10 indicates a potentially meaningful difference between male and female groups. All data processing and statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2).

Results

Study cohort and characteristics

A total of 38 192 adult patients with valid CAM-ICU assessments were admitted to any ICU at BIDMC for ≥48 h between 2011 and 2024. After the application of the exclusion criteria, the final cohort consisted of 38 192 patients (Fig. 1). Descriptive characteristics of the cohort, including patient characteristics, comorbidities, illness severity scores, and ICU admission characteristics, are shown in Table 1. Of the 38 192 patients, 21 541 (56.4%) were male. The mean age of the cohort was 65.5 (sd 15.87) yr, with 9880 (45.9%) males and 7567 (45.4%) females experiencing delirium (OR, 1.017; 95% CI, 0.98–1.06; SMD, 0.008). Sex-based differences in comorbidities were evident, with females having significantly higher rates of dementia (13.6% vs 10.2%; SMD, −0.077) and depression (34.1% vs 23.4%; SMD, −0.238), while males had significantly higher Elixhauser comorbidity index (19.86;sd 14.06 vs 18.95; sd 13.73; SMD, 0.065) and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment scores (6.10; sd 4.06 vs 5.41;sd 3.91; SMD, 0.173).

For patients ≥60 yr, the cumulative average CAM-ICU assessment ranged from 2.6 assessments on ICU day 1 to 8.8 assessments on ICU day 14 (Supplementary Table 2). No difference was observed in the total number of daily CAM-ICU assessments between males and females (Supplementary Fig. 4). The reported average incidence of CAM-ICU-positive delirium varied between 41.9% and 48.9%, depending on each 4-yr period (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Primary analysis

A total of 46.7% (n=17 447) of the study population experienced delirium during their ICU stay (Table 1). After adjusting for covariates, male sex was associated with significantly lower odds (aOR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.83–0.93; P<0.001) of experiencing delirium compared with female sex (Table 2).

Secondary analysis

Our secondary analysis explored the effect of age on the association between biological sex and ICU delirium. Male sex was specifically associated with significantly lower odds of delirium in patients aged 40–59 yr (aOR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.76–0.97; P<0.018). Similarly, among patients ≥60 yr, male sex was associated with lower odds of experiencing ICU delirium (aOR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.84–0.96; P<0.004). In contrast, no statistically significant association between sex and delirium was observed in younger adults aged 18–39 yr (aOR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.72–1.19; P=0.56).

Sensitivity analysis

To address imbalances in baseline characteristics across the two sexes, we conducted a post hoc propensity score–matched sensitivity analysis. In the matched cohort, male sex was associated with lower odds of ICU delirium compared with female sex (aOR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.86–0.95; P<0.001), supporting the robustness of the primary findings (Supplementary Fig. 6).

Exploratory analyses

Effect of coronavirus disease 2019

To assess whether the association between biological sex and ICU delirium was driven by the concomitant spike noted during the COVID-19 pandemic, we conducted a sensitivity analysis among patients ≥60 yr that excluded the peak COVID-19 period. After exclusion of this period, male sex among patients ≥60 yr (n=19 540) remained significantly associated with lower odds of delirium (aOR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.83–0.97; P=0.014). These findings are consistent with those reported in the original cohort. The similarity of the effect estimate before and after exclusion of the COVID-19 period indicates that the association between male sex and lower odds of delirium was not driven by COVID-19 alone.

Admission type

Our exploratory analysis examined whether admission type (medical ICU [MICU] vs surgical ICU [SICU]) modified the relationship between biological sex and ICU delirium, as stratified by two age groups: <60 yr and ≥60 yr. Descriptive characteristics of patients admitted to MICU and SICU by age group stratification are defined in Supplementary Table 3. Accounting for inter-ICU differences in our multivariable model, we found delirium to be more common in medical ICUs than in surgical ICUs across both age groups. For the <60 age group, 4201 (46.0%) patients in the medical ICU were CAM-positive compared with 840 (29.6%) surgical ICU patients (SMD, 0.343). Among patients ≥60 yr, 10 079 (50.7%) medical ICU patients had delirium, compared with 2327 (36.8%) surgical ICU patients (SMD, 0.283) (Supplementary Table 3).

Male sex was associated with significantly lower odds of delirium among MICU patients <60 yr (aOR, 0.82; 95% CI, 0.72–0.93; P<0.002), when adjusted for other covariates. This corresponds to up to an 18% reduction in the odds of developing delirium compared with female sex (Table 3). Similarly, among MICU patients aged ≥60 yr, males had up to an 11% (aOR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.83–0.97; P<0.008) lower odds of developing delirium. In contrast, no statistically significant association between sex and delirium was found among SICU patients in either age group (Table 3).

Length of stay and APACHE severity score

Male sex was associated with lower odds of developing delirium regardless of APACHE scores (<20 or ≥20), with aOR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.82–0.97; P=0.005, and aOR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.82–0.97; P=0.009, respectively (Table 4). Among ICU patients with a shorter length of stay (<5 days), male patients were found to have lower odds of delirium (aOR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.78–0.91; P<0.001) (Table 4). In contrast, there was no statistically significant association between sex and delirium in patients with a longer length of stay (≥5 days) (aOR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.9–1.07; P=0.6).

Depression

Depression has previously been shown to have a significant role in the development of delirium.16,17 To evaluate whether the association between biological sex and ICU delirium was mediated through depression, we conducted a product-of-coefficients mediation analysis (Fig. 2). Male sex was significantly associated with lower odds of depression (path a: aOR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.56–0.63; P<0.001). A history of depression was associated with higher odds of delirium (path b: aOR, 1.11; 95% CI, 1.04–1.19; P<0.005). After adjusting for a history of depression, male sex remained associated with lower odds of delirium (direct effect, path C’: aOR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.84–0.94; P<0.001). Adjustment for the mediator attenuated, but did not eliminate, the direct association between sex and delirium, consistent with partial mediation. The proportion mediated was calculated as the average causal mediation effect divided by the total effect, both estimated on the probability scale, indicating that depressive symptoms accounted for approximately 7.5% of the total association between sex and delirium (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

In our study population of over 38 000 adult ICU patients admitted to a tertiary-level academic medical centre for over a 13-yr period, male sex was associated with lower odds of developing delirium. These results demonstrate that, in a large ICU population with diverse admission diagnoses, female sex remained associated with a greater risk of delirium, with the risk being more pronounced among patients admitted to MICUs. These primary results remained robust across several complementary analytic approaches. Our report investigating the specific influence of biological sex on delirium incidence in a large, heterogeneous ICU population addresses a critical gap in the literature. These insights may facilitate the development of more accurate risk stratification tools to identify and guide targeted strategies for delirium prevention among patients at high risk.

The relationship between biological sex and ICU delirium remains poorly understood, with existing studies reporting inconsistent findings.13 Prior research has yielded inconsistent findings. Several studies observed no association between biological sex and ICU delirium.2,5,18,19 Specifically, Serpytis and colleagues13 found males <65 yr old were more frequently affected by delirium, whereas females older than 85 yr had a higher likelihood of delirium. In contrast, previously reported pooled analyses have identified female sex as a risk factor for delirium after cardiac surgery.20,21 Importantly, these studies are limited by their small, highly specialised cohorts, including limitations to specific ICUs and restriction to specific patient interventions or both.12,13 Our study improves on prior work with a larger sample size and broader inclusion of ICU settings and admission types to examine sex-specific delirium risk.

Oestrogen deficiency, as occurs in post-menopausal women, increases cognitive vulnerability in ageing females and is strongly linked to depressive symptoms, which may further elevate delirium risk through disruptions in hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activity, neuroinflammatory processes, and monoaminergic signalling pathways.16,17,22,23 Our mediation analysis suggests that depressive symptoms partially mediate the relationship between biological sex and ICU delirium, highlighting that males may be relatively protected from delirium in part because of lower vulnerability to depression with ageing. Oestrogen depletion after menopause may underlie the elevated rates of neurocognitive dysfunction observed in our study, potentially explaining the greater susceptibility to delirium seen in older female patients. Additionally, most women experience significant hormonal fluctuations during the 4–10 yr preceding menopause.24 This perimenopausal transition may explain the differences observed in the 40–59 age group. The mediation analysis provided additional mechanistic insight into the pathway linking biological sex to delirium risk; explanation of the biological underpinnings of sex-based differences in delirium warrants further prospective validation.

The observed increased incidence of ICU delirium from 2016 to 2020 compared with 2011 to 2015 likely reflects a combination of improved delirium education, including recognition and documentation, increasing patient acuity and multimorbidity, and prolonged ICU exposures. Additionally, the latter part of this period coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, which has itself been independently associated with a markedly increased risk of delirium, though this association is attenuated when disease severity is taken into account.25,26 The decline in delirium incidence observed from 2021 to 2024 likely reflects maturation of ICU delirium prevention strategies after the COVID-19 pandemic, including reduced deep sedation, earlier mobilisation, restoration of family engagement, and normalisation of ICU staffing and workflows.

The exploratory analyses identified a significant association between the type of admission and delirium risk. The multivariable model adjusted for several notable differences in MICU vs SICU patient populations, including increased rates of dementia and stroke, and higher frailty scores, thereby reducing their possible contribution to the observed difference. MICU patients had higher illness severity and more systemic disease, including sepsis, respiratory failure, and metabolic derangements—all established delirium risk factors that are less common in the protocolised, recovery-focused SICU, thus suggesting that female sex may confer additional vulnerability specifically in the context of higher baseline illness severity.27,28

Although our study yielded robust findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. Despite adjusting for an extensive set of confounders, the possibility of residual confounding, which is inherent in the observational design of the study, cannot be ruled out. For example, sex-based differences in comorbidities or unmeasured characteristics of ICU care may have influenced the observed associations. To mitigate differences in baseline characteristics, we performed a propensity score–matched analysis, which remained consistent with the primary analysis.

The single-centre nature of the study, coupled with the predominantly white population, may limit the generalisability of the findings to more diverse ICU populations and may attenuate external validity. The design of a relatively homogeneous, single-centre study reduces clinical and practice-pattern heterogeneity, thereby strengthening internal validity and improving the ability to detect biologically meaningful associations. Prioritisation of multicentre prospective studies will validate these findings across more diversified ICU populations. Furthermore, the study definition of biological sex is self-defined by the patient; thus, discrepancies between biological sex and gender identity may be present. Our study also used binary gender categories, which excludes non-binary and gender-diverse participants. This discrepancy possibly resulted in patients not completing the gender question or forced misclassifications. These possible measurement errors are not expected to be widely discordant enough to negate consistently strong associations, which remained robust across several approaches.

Despite standardisation of ICU practices through the PADIS guidelines (Pain, Agitation/sedation, Delirium, Immobility [rehabilitation/mobilisation], and Sleep [disruption]), data on adherence to different elements of the ABCDEF (A2F) bundle, a framework that puts PADIS guidelines into practice and could have strengthened our analysis, were lacking.29 Adherence to A2F bundle guidelines is, however, expected to be uniform across ICUs in our centre and was unlikely to have contributed to significant variability across sexes. Relatedly, inter-operator differences in the CAM-ICU assessment are also unlikely. Previous studies have demonstrated excellent inter-rater reliability, and the ICU nursing staff are expertly trained in standardised protocol.3,30 Lastly, hypoactive delirium is frequently underdiagnosed, leading to lower estimates of delirium rates. As female patients are more prone to hypoactive delirium, the risk of under-reporting is unlikely to significantly compromise the validity of our primary findings.31

Conclusions

The findings from this study indicate that males are less likely than females to develop ICU delirium, with this difference most clearly observed among patients in the MICU. This highlights the importance of incorporating biological sex in delirium risk assessments to optimise risk stratification and to provide early identification of patients at high risk. Further prospective studies, including biomarker identification and detailed mechanistic investigations, are warranted to unravel the biological pathways behind this relationship, with the aim of developing targeted preventative and therapeutic interventions for delirium in this ICU population.
