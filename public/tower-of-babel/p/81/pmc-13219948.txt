Complement C5 inhibitors as add-on rescue treatment in severe AQP4-antibody neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder attacks: a case series and literature review
Authors: Kai Xin Gan, Sarah Hasnor Binti Abu Hassan, Xiu Ling Tan, Kelvin Zhenghao Li, Umapathi Thirugnanam, Tianrong Yeo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute attacks of aquaporin-4 antibody neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (AQP4-Ab NMOSD) are typically managed with intravenous methylprednisolone, with therapeutic plasma exchange (PLEX) reserved for severe episodes. Despite these interventions, a subset of patients fails to respond and may sustain significant neurological disability. Complement C5 inhibitors, currently approved for long-term relapse prevention, have recently gained attention as a potential rescue therapy by rapidly inhibiting the terminal complement cascade, thus preventing downstream astrocytic injury. We report three patients with AQP4-Ab severe optic neuritis refractory to IV methylprednisolone and PLEX who demonstrated significant clinical improvement following administration of C5 inhibitors during the acute attack phase. Case 1 (right optic neuritis) and Case 3 (bilateral optic neuritis) received eculizumab, achieving near-complete and complete recovery of visual acuity, respectively. Both patients subsequently transitioned to rituximab within 8 weeks after their fourth weekly eculizumab infusion, while on a moderate dose of prednisolone. Case 2 (left optic neuritis) received ravulizumab, achieving partial visual recovery, and was continued on maintenance ravulizumab. Meningococcal prophylaxis with penicillin V was administered for all three patients. A literature review identified 10 publications utilising C5 inhibitors as acute attack therapies, totalling 31 cases, including our present series. Of these, 30 patients (97%) experienced neurological improvement within 4 weeks, with 28 (90%) showing improvement within the first week. Complete or near-complete recovery was observed in 42% of patients, while 55% achieved partial recovery, and one patient (3%) had no recovery. Patients achieving complete or near-complete recovery received C5 inhibitors significantly earlier than those with partial or no recovery (median 15.0 vs 24.0 days from symptom onset, p = 0.010); an optimal analytical cutoff of 23 days was identified. Infections were uncommon with no severe infections reported. In conclusion, emerging evidence supports the use of C5 inhibitors for acute NMOSD attacks, but larger studies are needed to establish robust evidence and inform clinical practice.Keywords: acute attack, AQP4 antibody, C5 inhibitor, case report, case series, complement inhibitor, eculizumab, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, ravulizumab, relapse, treatment

Introduction

Aquaporin-4 antibody neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (AQP4-Ab NMOSD) is an immune-mediated, inflammatory disorder of the central nervous system affecting the brain, spinal cord, and optic nerves. 1 The AQP4-Ab targets the AQP4 channels at the astrocytic foot processes, resulting in an astrocytopathy predominantly via complement-dependent cytotoxicity, with secondary demyelination and neuronal damage. 2 Clinical attacks are often severe, resulting in significant disability due to incomplete recovery. 3

To date, advances in the treatment of AQP4-Ab NMOSD have mainly focused on relapse prevention. An improved understanding of effector pathomechanisms has enabled the development of highly efficacious, FDA-approved monoclonal antibodies for preventive treatment, including complement C5 inhibitors (eculizumab and ravulizumab), anti-CD19 and anti-CD20 B-cell depleting agents (inebilizumab and rituximab, respectively), and IL-6 receptor antagonists (tocilizumab and satralizumab). However, regarding rescue treatments for NMOSD attacks, aside from intravenous (IV) corticosteroids and therapeutic plasma exchange (PLEX), no novel treatment strategies have emerged in recent years. Indeed, it is not uncommon to encounter patients who fail to improve or even worsen after conventional rescue treatments. These patients are usually retreated with another course of IV corticosteroids and/or increasing the number of PLEX sessions, but often with limited efficacy. Given their rapid onset and targeted inhibition of downstream pathogenic complement pathways, C5 inhibitors have been proposed as potential rescue therapies, with recent case series reporting on their efficacy when utilised in this scenario. Herein, we describe three cases of AQP4-Ab NMOSD presenting with severe acute optic neuritis in which C5 inhibitors were used as add-on rescue therapy following conventional treatments.

Case 1

A 78-year-old gentleman with a history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, ischaemic heart disease, and transient ischaemic attack, presented with right eye blurring of vision for 4 days. The deficit began in the nasal field of his right eye and progressively worsened to involve the entire visual field (VF). There was no eye pain or redness, fever, headache, or other neurological symptoms. Ophthalmological examination demonstrated a Grade 3 relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) in the right eye, with visual acuity (VA) reduced to light perception. VA of the left eye was normal, and fundus examination showed pink optic discs bilaterally. MRI of the orbits revealed enhancement of the intracanalicular segment of the right optic nerve, suggestive of optic neuritis. MRI of the brain and whole spine was unremarkable. Lumbar puncture yielded clear, colourless cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) with two nucleated cells/μL, protein 0.59 g/L, and negative results for CSF-restricted oligoclonal bands, microbial cultures, and viral/bacterial PCRs. Serum AQP4-Ab was positive on fixed cell-based assay (Euroimmun, Lübeck, Germany). Serum antibodies against myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG) and intracellular paraneoplastic neural antigens were negative.

IV methylprednisolone (1 g daily for 5 days) was commenced on day 6 of symptom onset. Given the severity of his optic neuritis, he underwent PLEX on the same day and subsequently completed seven sessions. Post-PLEX ophthalmological assessment showed improvement in VA from light perception to counting fingers (CF); however, the right VF remained restricted. Given the suboptimal response, the use of C5 inhibitors as add-on rescue therapy was discussed with the patient. He was initiated on IV eculizumab (900 mg) on day 20 from symptom onset, together with meningococcal vaccines (serogroups ACWY and B) and meningococcal prophylaxis (oral penicillin V 500 mg twice daily; Figure 1(a)).

One day after infusion, the patient reported subjective improvement in his VA with increased light perception. After two doses of eculizumab, VA improved to 6/9 – 2, with accompanying improvement in VF (Figure 1(b)). Fundus examination revealed a pink right optic disc with early temporal pallor. After the fourth weekly eculizumab infusion, VA improved further to 6/9; temporal pallor of the right optic disc remained stable. Oral prednisolone was continued and tapered gradually by 10 mg every 2 weeks until 10 mg and eventually tapered off after 6 months. Given the prohibitive cost of eculizumab, which is not subsidised in Singapore, the patient elected to commence rituximab for maintenance immunotherapy. Rituximab was initiated within 8 weeks after the fourth dose of eculizumab, with the second dose of meningococcal vaccines administered 2 weeks prior. Meningococcal prophylaxis was discontinued on the day of rituximab initiation. Apart from a mild urinary tract infection 2 weeks after the first dose of eculizumab, there were no adverse events or severe infections.

Case 2

A 65-year-old lady with a previous history of impaired fasting glucose presented with blurring of vision in her left eye for 1 week, associated with a dull, throbbing pain in the eye. She had no eye redness, fever, or other signs of infection. On admission, her left eye had a Grade 4 RAPD, with VA reduced to light perception. VA of the right eye was normal. Fundus examination showed pink optic discs bilaterally, with the left disc showing slightly elevated margins. An MRI of the orbits demonstrated left retrobulbar long-segment optic neuritis with optic nerve swelling, contrast enhancement, and associated perineural orbital fat stranding. MRI of the brain and whole spine was unremarkable. Lumbar puncture showed clear CSF with three nucleated cells/μL, protein 0.29 g/L, normal glucose, and negative results for oligoclonal bands, viral/bacterial PCR, and microbial cultures. Serum AQP4-Ab was positive on fixed cell-based assay while MOG antibodies were negative.

She received IV methylprednisolone (1 g per day for 5 days) starting on day 9 of symptom onset and seven sessions of PLEX, with the first session commencing 4 days after steroid initiation. After PLEX, VA improved to hand movement (HM), with the fundus showing slight pallor compared to initial presentation. As improvement was suboptimal, she agreed to add-on rescue therapy with ravulizumab – the loading dose (2400 mg) was given on day 27 of symptom onset, after administration of meningococcal vaccines and meningococcal prophylaxis with oral penicillin V (Figure 2(a)).

Within 10 days following ravulizumab, VA improved to CF. However, fundoscopy showed early temporal pallor of the left optic disc and there was no improvement in the VF (Figure 2(b)). She received the second infusion of ravulizumab (3000 mg) 2 weeks later, followed by 8-weekly maintenance dosing. Two months following the second dose of ravulizumab, she developed a zoster infection involving the left S2 dermatome that was treated with oral acyclovir with no complications. Aside from zoster, no other adverse reactions or severe infections were observed. Meningococcal prophylaxis was discontinued 2 weeks after the third dose of serogroup B meningococcal vaccine. Her VA remained at CF 3 months after the first ravulizumab infusion.

Case 3

A previously healthy 64-year-old gentleman presented with bilateral, sequential visual loss over 1 week. He first developed darkening of the superior VF in his right eye, which improved gradually over several days, followed by blurring of vision in the left eye. There were no associated fever, painful eye movements, or other systemic and neurological symptoms. Ophthalmological assessment revealed a grade 3 RAPD in the left eye with a sluggish pupillary reflex. VA was 6/48 and 6/7.5 in the left and right eyes, respectively. Fundal examination revealed slight pallor with no disc swelling bilaterally. MRI of the orbits demonstrated focal T2 hyperintensity and enhancement of the left optic nerve at the junction of the intracranial and intracanalicular segments. Additionally, there was T2 hyperintensity without enhancement involving both sides of the optic chiasm and the right optic nerve intracranial segment. The patient declined lumbar puncture. Serum AQP4-Ab was positive on fixed cell-based assay; MOG antibodies were negative.

IV methylprednisolone was initiated (1 g per day for 5 days) beginning on day 9 of symptom onset. Serial ophthalmological assessments revealed improvement of the left eye VA during methylprednisolone treatment. Given the persistence of significant VF defects, PLEX was discussed; however, the patient declined. He was discharged with mycophenolate mofetil and a tapering dose of oral prednisolone. Ophthalmological examination before discharge revealed VA of 6/12 and 6/7.5 in his left and right eyes, respectively. A review 2 weeks after discharge revealed VA of 6/9.5 in both eyes.

Three months later, he represented with new subacute onset of bilateral visual blurring, more pronounced in the left eye – this occurred while he was on prednisolone 2.5 mg daily and mycophenolate mofetil 500 mg twice a day. On examination, a left grade 1 RAPD was noted; VA was CF at 0.5 m and 6/60 + 1 in the left and right eyes, respectively. Confrontation VF testing of the left eye revealed light perception in the inferior temporal quadrant, with CF vision in the remaining quadrants. VF on the right was CF in all quadrants. MRI of the orbits revealed marked enhancement of the optic chiasm extending into the optic radiations with associated oedema.

IV methylprednisolone was administered (1 g daily for 5 days), with PLEX initiated after the last dose of methylprednisolone. Despite PLEX, VA remained poor at 6/45 bilaterally. The patient subsequently agreed to add-on rescue therapy with a C5 inhibitor. Following the fifth PLEX session, he received the first dose of IV eculizumab (900 mg) on day 15 of relapse onset (Figure 3(a)), after administration of meningococcal vaccination and meningococcal prophylaxis. Eye assessment 2 days after the first eculizumab dose demonstrated VA of 6/15 and 6/45 in the left and right eyes, respectively. One week after completing four doses of weekly eculizumab, VA and VF improved in both eyes, with left VA 6/9.5 + 3 and right VA 6/9.5 (Figure 3(b)). Two months later (at day 78 of relapse onset), VA continued to improve to left VA 6/9.5 + 4 and right VA 6/7.5. He was commenced on rituximab 7 weeks after the last eculizumab infusion, with the second dose of meningococcal vaccines administered 2 weeks prior. There were no infective complications or adverse events from eculizumab infusions.

Discussion

Eculizumab and ravulizumab are C5 inhibitors that have emerged as highly efficacious treatments for the prevention of NMOSD attacks. They act on the terminal complement pathway by inhibiting the cleavage of complement C5 into C5a and C5b, thereby preventing the critical step required for the formation of the membrane attack complex. 4 The PREVENT clinical trial showed that eculizumab, administered as 900 mg weekly for four induction doses followed by 1200 mg every 2 weeks thereafter, reduced NMOSD relapse risk by 94.2% compared to placebo. 5 Ravulizumab is structurally similar to eculizumab but is engineered for enhanced pH-dependent endosomal dissociation of C5 and more efficient neonatal Fc receptor-mediated recycling, resulting in a prolonged half-life.6,7 The CHAMPION-NMOSD trial demonstrated a relapse risk reduction of 98.6% using 2-monthly ravulizumab dosing, with no adjudicated relapses. 6 Other monoclonal antibodies have been developed for relapse prevention in NMOSD, including B-cell depleting therapies (inebilizumab and rituximab) and IL-6 receptor antagonists (satralizumab and tocilizumab). 8 However, due to their mechanism of action, these agents are less likely to rapidly halt astroglial and neuronal injury, limiting their utility as rescue therapies during the acute attack phase. In contrast, C5 inhibitors may function as rescue therapies because of their fast onset, achieving rapid inhibition of the terminal complement pathway, halting neuronal damage, and promoting faster recovery and remyelination. 9

All three of our patients presented with acute, severe optic neuritis and received C5 inhibitors as add-on rescue therapy, achieving partial to complete improvement in VA. We observed a clear temporal association between initiation of C5 inhibitors and rapid visual recovery, particularly in cases 1 and 3. Although these observations may be partially attributable to the delayed therapeutic effects of high-dose corticosteroids and PLEX, the timing and predictability of such delayed responses in clinical practice remain uncertain, and inferences can only be made retrospectively. Accordingly, early initiation of C5 inhibitors represents a reasonable therapeutic consideration in cases where clinical status remains static or deterioration occurs after steroids and PLEX. In Case 2, the response was more limited, given the relatively delayed presentation and the greater extent and enhancement of optic nerve involvement on MRI compared to the other two cases. This is consistent with a reported case showing poorer visual outcome due to delayed rescue treatment and more pronounced optic nerve involvement on imaging. 10 Although all of our three cases improved, the magnitude of response varied, likely reflecting differences in the extent of neuroinflammation and consequent astroglial and neuronal injury.

A literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar for articles published up to 15 January 2026. The search strategy utilised a combination of the following keywords: (“C5 inhibitor” OR “complement inhibitor” OR “eculizumab” OR “ravulizumab”) AND (“acute attack” OR “relapse”) AND (“NMOSD” OR “neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder”) AND (“AQP4” OR “aquaporin-4 antibody”). Studies were included if they reported on AQP4-Ab seropositive patients who received a C5 inhibitor specifically as acute treatment or rescue therapy for an NMOSD attack. Studies were excluded if they involved AQP4-Ab seronegative patients, evaluated C5 inhibitors solely as long-term maintenance/preventive therapy rather than rescue therapy, or were published in a non-English language. We identified 10 English language publications reporting on the use of C5 inhibitors in acute NMOSD attacks (Table 1).10–19 In total, 31 cases were identified, including the three cases in this report. Twenty-nine were adults and two were paediatric patients. The most common clinical syndrome was optic neuritis (n = 20), followed by myelitis (n = 6), brainstem syndrome (n = 4), with 1 case presenting with cardiac syncope and area postrema syndrome. Before initiation of C5 inhibitors, 29 patients received conventional acute attack therapies, including 12 patients with suboptimal response following IV methylprednisolone, 1 who received IV methylprednisolone and IVIG, and 16 who were refractory to both IV methylprednisolone and PLEX (including 1 who also received IVIG). Twenty-nine patients received eculizumab; 2 patients received ravulizumab (including case 2 in our series). Two patients received eculizumab concurrently with IV methylprednisolone during the acute attack phase.

Descriptive statistics (median and interquartile range (IQR)) were calculated using the available time-to-treatment data from published reports (GraphPad Prism 10.6.1, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Time-to-treatment data that were not reported were excluded from analysis. Approximate dates were treated as exact values, and a sensitivity analysis varying these estimates by ± 20% yielded a similar median. Time from symptom onset to IV methylprednisolone and to C5 inhibitor treatment was available for 30 of 31 patients, while time to PLEX was available for 14 of 16 patients. The median time-to-methylprednisolone was 6 days (IQR 3.0–10.0); median time-to-PLEX was 6 days (IQR 4.0–15.0); and median time-to-C5 inhibitors was 20.5 days (IQR 11.5–27.8).

We categorised clinical outcomes into four distinct groups, referenced against the patient’s pre-attack baseline neurological status whenever available: (i) Complete recovery: full return to the documented pre-attack baseline; (ii) Near-complete recovery: significant clinical improvement to near-normal function, with only mild residual deficits that do not significantly impair daily activities or functional independence (e.g. VA better than 6/9, or return to baseline ambulatory status/schooling); (iii) Partial recovery: mild-to-moderate improvement compared to the peak severity of the attack (pre-C5 inhibitor nadir), but with substantial and functionally limiting residual deficits remaining (e.g. persistent severe VF restriction or requirement for walking aids); and (iv) No recovery: no measurable or clinically meaningful improvement following treatment. Of the 31 patients, 30 (97%) showed neurological improvement after C5 inhibitor administration, with 28 (90%) demonstrating rapid improvement within the first week of treatment. Indeed, C5 inhibitor therapy was associated with immediate cessation of neurological progression with subjective improvement as early as 30 min after infusion, 15 and objective neurological recovery was observed within 2–5 days,11,12,19 including in two of our cases. Over the longer term, the reported cases continued to demonstrate progressive neurological improvement for 2–6 months after treatment, suggesting a sustained therapeutic effect. Eight cases (26%) achieved complete recovery, 5 (16%) had near-complete recovery, while 17 (55%) experienced partial recovery. Only one reported case showed no clinical improvement – the authors attributed this to delayed initiation of methylprednisolone (i.e. 9 days from symptom onset), 10 highlighting the importance of early initiation of rescue therapies to prevent irreversible neurological disability.

To evaluate whether outcomes differed according to the timing of intervention, we analysed the interval from symptom onset to initiation of C5 inhibitor therapy, stratified by neurological outcomes. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Of the 31 cases, 1 case (Gorriz et al. 2024) was excluded as data on time-to-C5 inhibitor initiation were unavailable. 13 For cases in which only approximate dates were available from the source literature, these were treated as exact values for statistical analysis. Amongst the 30 cases analysed, patients with favourable outcomes (complete or near-complete recovery) received C5 inhibitors significantly earlier, with a median of 15.0 days (IQR: 6.5–21.0), compared with 24.0 days (IQR: 13.0–37.0) in those with unfavourable outcomes (partial or no recovery) (Mann–Whitney U = 49.0, p = 0.010; Figure 4(a)). Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis demonstrated moderate discriminatory ability (area under curve [AUC] = 0.778, 95% CI: 0.612–0.945) (Figure 4(b)). An optimal cutoff of < 23 days was identified using Youden’s index, corresponding to a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 52.9% for predicting favourable outcomes. This threshold is potentially clinically meaningful, as it represents the earliest time point at which sensitivity reached 100%, suggesting that C5 inhibitor use at ≥ 23 days is unlikely to result in favourable outcomes. We also identified a second cutoff of < 8.5 days, which was the latest time point at which specificity was maximal. At this threshold, sensitivity and specificity were 38.5% and 94.1%, respectively, indicating that C5 inhibitor use within ~9 days is associated with favourable outcomes.

While early treatment is a significant predictor of favourable outcomes, the specific C5 inhibitor utilised may influence early treatment response. Although eculizumab and ravulizumab share the same mechanism of terminal complement inhibition, their distinct pharmacokinetic profiles may result in differences in tissue penetration and onset of action during an acute attack. However, drawing comparative conclusions regarding their acute rescue efficacy is limited by the small sample size (n = 2) who received ravulizumab. Both patients had only partial recovery, but this was confounded by either delayed administration (our case 2) or severe deficits at attack nadir (Valdés et al. 2025). 16 The comparative efficacy between eculizumab and ravulizumab in the acute setting will be an important area for future investigation.

Meningococcal and encapsulated bacterial infections are serious complications of C5 inhibitors requiring appropriate mitigation strategies, especially when given during acute attacks as patients are unlikely to have completed prior vaccinations. Our patients received the first dose of meningococcal vaccines (against serogroups ACWY and B) and meningococcal prophylaxis with penicillin V before or on the same day as the initial C5 inhibitor infusion, with no delay in treatment initiation. Antibiotic prophylaxis was administered until 2 weeks after completion of meningococcal vaccinations. In the acute rescue setting, balancing infection risk with the need for rapid treatment is challenging. We opted for expedient C5 inhibition under the cover of antibiotic prophylaxis instead of waiting for full vaccine immunogenicity, as delaying treatment could pose a further risk of irreversible neurological damage. Our literature review identified no cases of meningococcal infection associated with the use of C5 inhibitors as rescue therapy, underscoring that these agents are likely to be safe in the acute setting when appropriate preventive measures are implemented. Other vaccinations against encapsulated bacteria, including Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae, should also be given. Our literature review, including our case series, demonstrated a low overall rate of infectious complications, comprising two urinary tract infections, one case of herpes zoster, and one case of pneumonia.

The use of C5 inhibitors as long-term preventive treatment after acute treatment may not be feasible in some patients due to their high cost, necessitating the introduction of other maintenance therapies. Relapses after withdrawal of C5 inhibitors have been reported, commonly between the second and third month post-eculizumab.15,20–22 Soni et al. reported a patient (included in our literature review) who experienced a myelitis relapse 10 weeks after rituximab, despite being transitioned within 8 weeks from the fourth dose of eculizumab. 15 Care should be exercised when transitioning from C5 inhibitors, when used as rescue therapies, to other maintenance treatments, taking into account the onset of efficacy of the subsequent therapies. For cases 1 and 3 in our series, both patients completed four infusions of weekly eculizumab and transitioned to rituximab within 8 weeks from the last eculizumab dose, while receiving a moderate dose of prednisolone (i.e. 30 mg). Further studies are needed to ascertain the efficacy and safety of these transitional approaches.

Conclusion

We report three AQP4-Ab NMOSD patients presenting with severe optic neuritis refractory to standard rescue therapies, who experienced partial to complete recovery following C5 inhibitor therapy as add-on rescue treatment. There is emerging evidence supporting the use of C5 inhibitors in NMOSD attacks; however, larger observational studies and clinical trials are needed to provide robust data and guidance on the optimal timing and indications for their use in routine clinical practice.
