A novel selective medium Sucrose-Bacitracin agar 10 for accurate isolation and identification of Streptococcus mutans
Authors: Yuwen Fang, Qizhao Ma, Wei Zhao, Nian Liu, Lei Cheng, Jing Chen, Jing Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Background

Dental caries is one of the most prevalent chronic infectious diseases, characterized by progressive destruction of the hard tissues of teeth [1, 2]. The disease arises from the combined effects of acid-producing microbe, fermentable carbohydrates, and host factors, such as tooth structure and saliva composition [3–5]. Among the microbial contributors, Streptococcus mutans is a key cariogenic pathogen due to its ability to produce acids, resist acidic environments, synthesize extracellular polysaccharides, and form robust biofilms [6, 7]. These biofilms, primarily composed of an extracellular polysaccharide matrix, enable S. mutans to adhere to the acquired pellicle formed by salivary proteins and glycoproteins on the tooth surface, further driving caries progression [8].

Caries risk assessment tools, including the American Dental Association (ADA) caries risk assessment, the Caries Risk Assessment Tool (CAT), the Caries Management by Risk Assessment (CAMBRA), and the Cariogram, have been widely used to guide clinical treatment planning [9–14]. These systems evaluate various factors, including socioeconomic, demographic, behavioral, and biological parameters, with S. mutans as a key determinant in the biological category [15–17]. For example, the Cariogram system incorporates tests like the Strip Mutans Test to quantify S. mutans levels in saliva, directly influencing caries risk scores [18, 19]. The isolation of S. mutans often relies on selective media that suppress non-target bacteria growth while not affecting target organisms’ growth [20]. Mitis Salivarius Bacitracin agar (MSB) is a commonly used media in screening S. mutans from saliva and plaque [21, 22]. However, MSB with 20% sucrose (MSB-20) exhibits limited differentiation capability between S. mutans and Streptococcus sobrinus, which can hinder the identification of S. mutans from mixed populations [23].

To address these challenges, this study introduces Sucrose-Bacitracin agar with 10% sucrose (SB-10), an improved selective medium designed to enhance the isolation and identification of S. mutans. SB-10 supports better colony growth, with larger diameters and distinctive morphological features, such as the presence of sugar particle substances around colonies, aiding in differentiation. The medium also performs better in screening common oral bacteria while maintaining a high specificity for S. mutans. This novel selective medium offers significant advantages in clinical and research settings by accurately quantifying and identifying S. mutans, facilitating caries risk assessment and advancing our understanding of its pathogenic mechanisms.

Methods

Saliva sample source

The research received formal ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital of Stomatology (WCHSIRB-D-2024–221). The study involved five individuals aged between 19 and 59 years old. These patients were recruited from the Endodontic Department at West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University, and provided informed consent to enrollment. The patients were enrolled based on the following criteria [24]: (1) No administration of corticosteroids and immunosuppressants within the past six months; (2) No administration of antibiotics within three months; (3) No use of oral, topical antimicrobials and fluoride protectors within the past seven days; (4) No significant systemic diseases or acute infection.

Medium component

The composition of the medium per liter is:

BHI agar (BHIA) medium: Brain-Heart Infusion broth 37.0 g (Difco, Sparks, MD, USA); Agar 15.0 g (Macklin, China); Distilled water.

SB agar medium: Casitone 15.0 g (Solarbio, China); Yeast extract 5.0 g (Thermo Scientific, USA); L-cysteine 0.2 g (Solarbio, China); Sodium sulfite 0.1 g (Solarbio, China); Sodium acetate 20.0 g (Macklin, China); Agar 15.0 g (Macklin, China); Distilled water; Adding to 0.2 U/mL bacitracin (Solarbio, China). The final sucrose concentrations (w/v) added to the medium were 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%, denoted as SB-0, SB-5, SB-10, SB-15, and SB-20, respectively.

MSB agar medium: Mitis Salivarius agar 90.08 g (Casein hydrolysate 15.0 g; Animal tissue peptic digestives 5.0 g; Crystal violet 0.0008 g; Glucose 1.0 g; Potassium dihydrogen phosphate 4.0 g; Sucrose 50.0 g; Trypan blue 0.075 g; Agar 15.0 g) (Hopebio, China); Distilled water; Add 1.16 mL 1% potassium tellurite (Hopebio, China) and bacitracin to final concentration to 0.2 U/mL. The final sucrose concentrations of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% were denoted as MSB-5, MSB-10, MSB-15, and MSB-20, respectively. Note that each MSB medium contains an inherent base sucrose concentration of 5%, with additional sucrose added to reach the indicated total concentrations.

Bacterial strain and culture condition

All the strains and primers used in this study are listed in Tables S1 and S2. S. mutans UA159 was commercially obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). The gtfB and gtfB/C mutants (UA159 ΔgtfB and ΔgtfB/C) of S. mutans were from our previous study [25]. S. mutans and its derivatives were routinely grown in the BHI broth (Difco, Sparks, MD, USA) or BHIA, MSB and SB agar with different sugar concentrations under anaerobic conditions (85% N2, 5% CO2, 10% H2) or aerobic conditions (5% CO2) at 37 °C for 72 h [26, 27].

S. sobrinus SL1, Streptococcus gordonii DL1, Streptococcus sanguinis DSS-10, Streptococcus salivarius C699, Streptococcus oralis M7A, Enterococcus faecalis PCI 1325, and Staphylococcus epidermidis 255-01B were commercially obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and routinely grown in the BHI broth (Difco, Sparks, MD, USA) or MSB-20 and SB-10 agar under anaerobic conditions (85% N2, 5% CO2, 10% H2) at 37 °C for 72 h.

Porphyromonas gingivalis W83 and Fusobacterium nucleatum 25,586 were commercially obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and routinely grown in the BHI broth (Difco, Sparks, MD, USA) containing 0.5 µg/mL Vitamin K (Hopebio, China) and 5 µg/mL Hemin (Hopebio, China) or MSB-20 and SB-10 agar under anaerobic conditions (85% N2, 5% CO2, 10% H2) at 37 °C for 72 h.

Escherichia coli DLC101 Trelief 5α chemically competent cell (Tsingke, China) was grown in the LB broth (Thermo Fisher, USA) or MSB-20 and SB-10 agar under anaerobic conditions (85% N2, 5% CO2, 10% H2) at 37 °C for 72 h.

Prevotella denticola strains were clinically isolated from State Key Laboratory of Oral Diseases, Sichuan University and routinely grown in the PYG broth (Hopebio, China) or MSB-20 and SB-10 agar under anaerobic conditions (85% N2, 5% CO2, 10% H2) at 37 °C for 72 h.

Morphological recording and identification

Bacterial strains were initially cultured in BHI broth and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C until an OD600nm of 0.5–0.6 was reached. The bacterial suspension was subsequently serially diluted tenfold in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) up to a dilution of 10^−6. Then, 10 µL of each dilution was spotted onto the respective agar media plates and further incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 72 h. A single calibration inspector (Kappa > 0.8) performed CFU counting and morphological observation under a stereomicroscope (Leica, German) at 20 × magnification. The number and diameter of the colonies were recorded against a dark background to amplify the typical morphological characteristics of the colonies. The diameter of bacteria was measured using a vernier caliper with an accuracy of 0.01 mm. Specifically, each colony’s longest diameter and the perpendicular diameter were measured, and the average value was recorded as the colony diameter to minimize bias caused by irregular colony shapes.

To systematically record bacterial colony morphology, the typical characteristics of S. mutans and other tested bacterial species on agar medium were observed under a stereomicroscope (Leica, German). Morphological observations were documented, including colony shape, surface texture, and the presence or absence of sugar particle deposition.

Liquid chromatography analysis

When S. mutans was incubated on SB-10 agar for 72 h, approximately 0.2 g of agar containing sugar particles surrounding individual colonies was carefully excised using a sterile scalpel. As a control, an equivalent amount of agar (0.2 g) from areas without sugar particle formation was similarly collected. For sample preparation, 1 mL of ultrapure water was added to each excised agar sample to facilitate the dissolution of extracellular glucans. The samples were then subjected to sonication using a bath sonicator (KQ-500DE, Kunshan Ultrasonic Instruments, China) at 40 kHz and 100 W for 10 min to ensure complete dissolution. Following sonication, the solution was centrifuged at 11,200 × g for 10 min, and the resulting supernatant was collected for analysis.

Glucan identification was performed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with a Shimadzu LC-20AT system equipped with a refractive index detector and a size-exclusion chromatography column [28]. The mobile phase consisted of 50 mM phosphate buffer, delivered at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. A 20 µL aliquot of each prepared sample was injected into the HPLC system for separation and detection. Retention times and peak areas were used to identify and quantify glucans in the samples, using standard glucans as references. The detection and quantification were carried out in accordance with the Chinese Pharmacopoeia 2020 Edition, General Principle 0512 [28].

Genomic DNA extraction and 16 S rRNA gene sequencing for strain identification

A 10 µL aliquot of saliva sample was spread onto SB-10 and MSB-20 agar medium and incubated under anaerobic conditions (85% N2, 5% CO2, 10% H2) at 37 °C for 72 hours. Typical S. mutans colonies were selected from SB-10 agar plates and inoculated into BHI broth, followed by overnight incubation at 37 °C under anaerobic conditions. Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted using a bacterial genomic DNA extraction kit (Tiangen, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR amplification targeting the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was performed using universal primers (27F: 5’-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3’ and 1492R: 5’-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3’). PCR products were purified using a PCR purification kit (Takara, China) and subsequently sequenced using the Sanger sequencing method (Tsingke Biotechnology, China). The obtained sequences were analyzed using BLAST in the NCBI nucleotide database to determine strain identity.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, California). The unmatched T-test, one-way analysis of variance, and two-way analysis of variance were used to detect statistical significance. In general, if P < 0.05, the difference between the mean of the study data is statistically significant.

Results

Growth of S. mutans on BHIA under aerobic and anaerobic conditions

To assess the impact of aerobic and anaerobic conditions on the colony-forming units (CFU) of S. mutans, we cultured the bacteria on BHIA plates. As shown in Fig. 1, S. mutans exhibited significantly better growth under anaerobic conditions, with a CFU count of approximately 10.5 ± 1.4 × 10^8 CFU/mL. In contrast, no detectable growth (ND) was observed under aerobic conditions. These results indicate that the cultivation conditions significantly influence the CFU count of S. mutans and that anaerobic conditions provide a more accurate representation of its growth, consistent with its obligate anaerobic nature. This underscores the importance of using anaerobic conditions for optimal cultivation and precise quantification of S. mutans in microbiological studies.

Impact of sucrose concentration on the count and morphology of S. mutans in MSB and SB media

MSB-20 is currently a widely used medium for selecting and counting S. mutans colonies [22]. To compare the colony selection capacity of SB medium, we selected MSB-20 as the control medium. MSB-20 contains 20% sucrose, which is crucial because S. mutans is particularly adept at metabolizing sucrose to form extracellular polysaccharides. Consequently, we evaluated the impact of different sucrose concentrations on the CFU counts and colony diameter of S. mutans grown on MSB and SB media. As shown in Fig. 2a, CFU counts remained consistent across all sucrose concentrations tested in both MSB and SB media, with no significant differences observed. This suggests that sucrose concentration does not significantly impact bacterial growth regarding CFU count.

Subsequently, we assessed the effect of sucrose concentration on the diameter of S. mutans colonies. Fig. 2b shows colonies formed in SB medium supplemented with 10% sucrose (SB-10) displayed significantly larger diameters than those grown on MSB medium or other SB media with 0%, 5%, 15%, and 20% sucrose. This suggests that SB-10 promotes more expansive colony growth. Based on these findings, we further evaluated the effectiveness, selectivity, and morphological differentiation advantages of SB-10 for S. mutans by using the well-established selective medium MSB-20 as a control.

Interestingly, while observing colonies on SB-10, crystalline particles were detected around the S. mutans colonies (Fig. 2c). Mass spectrometric analysis revealed these particles to be glucan, a polysaccharide derived from sucrose metabolism (Fig. S1). These sugar particles deposited surrounding S. mutans colonies may be an essential marker for identifying and selecting S. mutans.

These results suggest that SB-10 may offer advantages over MSB-20 in identifying and counting S. mutans colonies, especially considering the formation of extracellular polysaccharides, which could improve the accuracy of selection and enhance the specificity of colony counting.

Role of glucosyltransferases in the formation of sugar particles around S. mutans colonies

In S. mutans, glucosyltransferases B (GtfB) and C (GtfC) primarily synthesize water-insoluble extracellular polysaccharides [29, 30]. To investigate whether GtfB/C enzymes are responsible for the formation of sugar particles around colonies, we analyzed colony morphology and sugar particle composition using S. mutans wild-type strain UA159 and its mutant strains, UA159 ΔgtfB and UA159 ΔgtfB/C.

As shown in Fig. 3a, the CFU counts of UA159, UA159 ΔgtfB, and UA159 ΔgtfB/C displayed no significant differences when grown on SB-10 or MSB-20 media, indicating that the deletion of gtfB or gtfB/C does not affect bacterial viability on these media. Regarding colony diameter, Fig. 3b shows that colonies of UA159, UA159 ΔgtfB and UA159 ΔgtfB/C grown on SB-10 were significantly larger than those on MSB-20.

The colony morphology of UA159, UA159 ΔgtfB, and UA159 ΔgtfB/C on SB-10 was observed further to investigate sugar particle formation (Figs. 2c and 3c and d). The surface of UA159 colonies appeared rough and grainy, with prominent sugar particle deposition around the colony periphery. In contrast, UA159 ΔgtfB colonies were relatively smooth and round, with significantly fewer sugar particles observed at the colony edges. Notably, no sugar particles were detected around the colonies of UA159 ΔgtfB/C. Together, these results highlight that GtfB and GtfC are essential for synthesizing extracellular polysaccharides that contribute to forming sugar particles around S. mutans colonies.

Growth of different common oral bacteria on SB-10 and MSB-20

To assess the selectivity of SB-10 for S. mutans, we evaluated its performance with other common oral bacteria, including Gram-positive species such as S. epidermidis, E. faecalis, S. sobrinus, S. oralis, S. salivarius, S. gordonii, and S. sanguinis [31–37], as well as Gram-negative species such as E. coli, P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum, and P. denticola [38–40]. The growth characteristics and colony morphology of these bacteria on SB-10 and MSB-20 were compared.

As shown in Fig. 4a and b, S. oralis, S. salivarius, S. gordonii, S. sanguinis, E. coli, P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum, and P. denticola could not be detected on either SB-10 or MSB-20 at a dilution of 10^−8, suggesting that these selective media effectively suppress their growth. S. epidermidis was only detected on SB-10, but undetectable on MSB-20. E. faecalis and S. sobrinus showed no significant difference in CFU counts between SB-10 and MSB-20, indicating that both selective media supported their growth. Regarding colony diameter, S. sobrinus showed no significant difference between SB-10 and MSB-20. However, S. sobrinus colonies on SB-10 were significantly smaller than those of S. mutans, whereas on MSB-20, S. sobrinus colonies were significantly larger than S. mutans colonies. Additionally, E. faecalis colonies grown on SB-10 were notably smaller than those cultivated on MSB-20, further highlighting media-dependent morphological differences.

Distinct morphological features of these colonies on SB-10 were observed under the stereomicroscope and are shown in Fig. S2. Colonies of S. epidermidis appeared smooth and round, with a milky-white dispersion in the center. E. faecalis colonies were smooth, transparent, and round. Colonies of S. mutans appeared large, with rough margin and a granular surface resembling frosted glass. In contrast, colonies of S. sobrinus were smaller, with smooth margin and a semi-translucent region on the surface (Fig. 4c and d). These findings further support the selective capabilities of SB-10 for S. mutans from other common oral bacteria based on the growth characteristics and colony morphology.

Efficacy of SB-10 in isolating S. mutans from saliva

To evaluate the efficacy of SB-10 as a selective medium for isolating S. mutans from saliva, saliva samples from five clinical subjects were cultured on SB-10 and MSB-20. After 72 h of incubation, colonies exhibiting typical S. mutans growth and morphological characteristics were selected for further analysis (Fig. 5a and b). To confirm colony identity, 60 colonies from each medium were subjected to PCR amplification targeting the 16S rRNA gene, resulting in amplification products ranging from 1000 to 2000 bp (Fig. S3). Subsequent sequencing and BLAST analysis of the 16S rRNA gene confirmed that 100% (60/60) of randomly selected colonies from SB-10 were S. mutans, with ≥ 99% identity to the reference sequence. In comparison, only 80% (48/60) of randomly selected colonies from MSB-20 were identified as S. mutans with ≥ 99% sequence identity (Tables S3, S4, and S5).

Discussion

Dental caries remains a significant public health challenge, largely driven by the cariogenic activities of S. mutans [41–43]. Accurate isolation and identification of S. mutans are critical for advancing our understanding of its pathogenic mechanisms and for improving caries risk assessment [44, 45]. In this study, we developed and evaluated SB-10, a novel selective medium designed to address the limitations of existing media, such as MSB-20, in isolating S. mutans. Our findings highlight the superior performance of SB-10 in supporting S. mutans growth, promoting distinct colony morphology, and facilitating accurate differentiation from other oral bacteria.

Several selective media have been developed previously, such as MSB agar, Trypticase yeast-extract cystine sucrose-bacitracin (TYCSB) agar, and Trypticase Soy with 20% sucrose and Bacitracin (TYS20B) agar, widely used in clinical and laboratory settings for selective isolation of S. mutans [46]. Among these, MSB-20 is widely recognized for its high sensitivity in detecting mutans streptococci. Compared with MSB-20 agar, SB-10 agar medium offers distinct advantages by supporting significantly higher colony recovery, larger colony sizes, enhanced morphological differentiation, and characteristic sugar particle deposition around colonies. Moreover, the lower sucrose concentration in SB-10 enhances sensitivity and recovery of S. mutans without compromising selectivity. These unique morphological features improve the ease of identification and quantification of S. mutans, enhancing accuracy in clinical diagnosis, epidemiological surveillance, and caries risk assessment.

Our findings demonstrated that S. mutans exhibited consistent CFU counts across SB-10, MSB-20 and BHIA, confirming that SB-10 supports robust growth of S. mutans. Importantly, SB-10 promoted significantly larger colony diameters and distinct morphological features, such as sugar particle deposition at colony edges, compared to MSB-20. These unique features of SB-10 improve its utility in differentiating S. mutans from other oral bacteria. SB-10 also demonstrated high specificity in screening S. mutans from mixed populations. For example, when tested against other common oral bacteria, such as S. sobrinus, S. epidermidis, and E. faecalis, SB-10 effectively differentiated S. mutans based on its unique colony morphology or/and sugar particle formation. SB-10 demonstrated higher specificity by suppressing the growth of non-target bacteria such as S. oralis, S. salivarius, S. gordonii, S. sanguinis, E. coli, P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum, and P. denticola which were undetectable on SB-10 at high dilution levels as this experiment adopted a higher dilution level of bacterial solution (10^8 CFU/mL) than previous study [47].

The role of GtfB and GtfC in sugar particle formation was further elucidated in this study. While wild-type S. mutans colonies displayed prominent sugar particles, deletion of gtfB or gtfB/C resulted in significantly decrease or absent sugar particle deposition. These findings confirm that GtfB and GtfC are essential for the synthesis of sugar particle around colonies. The distinctive sugar particle formation around S. mutans colonies was observed specifically on SB-10, likely due to its optimized sucrose concentration (10%) and simplified nutrient composition. SB-10 provides a more favorable environment for the enzymatic activity of GtfB and GtfC compared to MSB-20, which contains higher sucrose levels (20%) that may impose osmotic stress. In addition, SB-10 lacks inhibitory compounds such as potassium tellurite and synthetic dyes (e.g., crystal violet and trypan blue) present in MSB, which may hinder glucan synthesis. These factors collectively promote more efficient production and deposition of glucans around S. mutans colonies on SB-10, leading to the formation of distinct sugar particles and enhancing the morphological differentiation of S. mutans from other oral bacteria.

In addition to laboratory strains, SB-10 was successfully applied to saliva samples, demonstrating its practical utility in clinical samples. All S. mutans colonies selected based on their distinct morphology on SB-10 were confirmed by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, validating the medium’s high specificity and reliability. In contrast, the accuracy of S. mutans identification using MSB-20 was 80%. Although morphological identification may initially require some operator expertise, the distinctive colony characteristics observed on SB-10, such as larger colony size and characteristic sugar particle deposition, significantly facilitate efficient screening compared to existing selective media such as MSB-20. Therefore, SB-10 represents a feasible, practical, and reliable medium for clinical identification and quantification of S. mutans from mixed oral microbial populations, ultimately supporting effective caries risk assessment and management strategies.

The selectivity of SB-10 and MSB-20 agar media for S. mutans is primarily due to the inclusion of bacitracin, an antibiotic that inhibits the growth of most non-mutans streptococci while allowing S. mutans to proliferate due to its intrinsic resistance. The main differences between SB-10 and MSB-20 lie in their selective agents, base composition, and sucrose concentration. While both media contain bacitracin, MSB-20 also includes potassium tellurite, potentially causing over-inhibition and reduced recovery of S. mutans. SB-10 uses a simplified nutrient base without dyes or potassium tellurite, reducing unnecessary growth suppression, whereas MSB-20 is based on Mitis Salivarius agar containing crystal violet and trypan blue dyes that inhibit unwanted bacterial growth. Regarding sucrose concentration, SB-10 employs a lower sucrose concentration (10%) compared to MSB-20 (20%), minimizing osmotic stress and promoting the formation of larger, more distinct colonies. Specifically, SB-10 uniquely supports the formation of characteristic sugar particle deposition surrounding S. mutans colonies, significantly enhancing morphological differentiation from other oral microorganisms. These advantageous features of SB-10 lead to higher colony recovery, easier identification, and improved sensitivity without sacrificing selectivity, making it particularly suitable for accurate quantification and differentiation of S. mutans in both clinical and epidemiological applications. We believe that SB-10 has good potential clinical applications, including but not limited to routine microbial screening of the clinical mouth, providing a more convenient means of counting S. mutans for existing caries risk assessment systems, and later epidemiological investigations of caries.

Conclusion

In conclusion, SB-10 represents a significant improvement over existing selective media for S. mutans isolation. Its ability to support robust growth, facilitate visual differentiation, and enhance specificity makes it a powerful tool for advancing research on S. mutans and for improving caries risk assessment. Future studies should explore the application of SB-10 in larger-scale clinical settings and investigate its performance in identifying S. mutans in mixed-species biofilms, further solidifying its utility in microbiological and clinical research.
