Tracing the Satisfactory Care of the Chronically Ill in Emergency Departments: A Scoping Review
Authors: Eleni Naki, Agapi L Batiridou, Christine Mantzouka, Mary Gouva, Stefanos Mantzoukas, Zoe Konstanti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims and ObjectivesThe growing prevalence of chronic diseases has heightened the demand for emergency department (ED) services, underscoring their increasingly essential role within healthcare systems. To improve outcomes and decrease needless ED use, it is essential to comprehend what constitutes appropriate care in this context.Methodological DesignA scoping review was conducted following PRISMA‐ScR reporting guidelines. Relevant studies published between 2013 and 2024 were identified through systematic searches conducted in PubMed, Cochrane and Scopus. The methodological approach was based on the Arksey and O'Malley framework, further refined by Levac et al. and Peters et al., and complemented by the Framework ‘N’ to integrate principles of caring science and patient‐centred care within the synthesis process.ResultsFifteen studies were deemed eligible for inclusion. Four overarching motifs emerged from the thematic synthesis. (1) Health systems: Age and multimorbidity are the main causes of ED dependence. This emphasises the necessity of service reform and customised care programs. (2) ED use and care transitions: Results are enhanced by functional evaluation, timely pain management and organised follow‐up. Delays and disjointed aftercare, however, are frequent. (3) Interdisciplinary collaboration: Coordinated multidisciplinary teams and integration with primary care enhance patient satisfaction and continuity. Nevertheless, these methods are rarely used. (4) Barriers and challenges: Errors, undertreatment and repeated ED visits are caused by stigma, poor staff training, a lack of resources and poor communication.ConclusionsStronger integration between emergency departments (EDs) and primary care, along with standardised procedures and individualised care pathways, is essential in response to population aging and the increasing complexity of chronic diseases. Prioritising digital technologies, enhancing staff training and improving care transitions can not only enhance patient outcomes and experiences but also reduce avoidable ED visits.Keywords: care transitions, chronic disease, emergency department, interdisciplinary collaboration, older adults, patient experience, scoping review

Background

Chronic illnesses, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, chronic respiratory failure and neurological disorders, are among the leading causes of disability and mortality worldwide. The International Classification of Diseases (ICD‐10 and ICD‐11) of the World Health Organization (WHO) provides the internationally recognised framework for their classification [1, 2], whereas for mental disorders, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) of the American Psychiatric Association (APA) is commonly used [3].

These conditions require continuous care and frequently result in acute exacerbations that prompt visits to emergency departments (EDs), where timely and specialised interventions are critical to preventing complications and death [4, 5]. Beyond clinical outcomes, chronic illnesses impose substantial social and financial burdens, including increased hospitalisations, medication costs and service utilisation. Adequate care for chronically ill patients extends beyond acute treatment, encompassing psychological support, patient education and self‐management guidance, all of which may reduce ED reliance and improve health outcomes [5, 6]. Health systems are increasingly adopting digital health technologies and telemedicine to enhance continuity of care, patient satisfaction and daily monitoring [7, 8].

Despite these initiatives, EDs face significant challenges in delivering satisfactory care. Overcrowding, time constraints, fragmented communication and poorly managed transitions to follow‐up care can negatively affect both patient satisfaction and clinical outcomes [9, 10]. Although many countries have implemented policies to strengthen chronic disease management, gaps remain in understanding how patients themselves perceive and experience care in emergency settings [11].

The existing literature has predominantly focused on ED utilisation patterns, costs, or system efficiency, rather than on the patient experience of care. The concept of ‘satisfactory care’ in EDs remains inconsistently defined, often reduced to clinical or operational outcomes, overlooking relational, communicative and transitional dimensions that are central to caring science and person‐centred practice. For individuals with chronic and complex conditions, satisfactory care includes timely pain relief, clear communication, respect and dignity, continuity across care settings and meaningful involvement in care decisions. These dimensions are particularly salient in emergency contexts, where time pressures and fragmented care pathways frequently undermine patient experience, especially among older adults and individuals with multimorbidity [12].

While previous reviews have highlighted structural deficits in ED and primary care systems, a synthesis focused on how patients and healthcare professionals conceptualise and experience satisfactory care in EDs is lacking. Addressing this gap is crucial for informing care models that align with the principles of caring science and support more integrated, person‐centred approaches.

Aim of the Review

This scoping review does not evaluate the effectiveness of specific interventions but seeks to map and synthesise existing evidence on how satisfactory care for adults with chronic illnesses is defined and experienced in EDs. By integrating findings across diverse conditions and care contexts, this review clarifies key experiential and system‐level factors influencing satisfactory care and informs the development of future interventions, care pathways and policy initiatives.

Methods

Study Design

This scoping review followed an a priori protocol (OSF: https://osf.io/8tmcf) and was reported in line with the PRISMA‐ScR guidelines (Table S1) (EQUATOR Network). The methodological framework was based on the structure proposed by Arksey and O'Malley [13], and further refined according to Levac et al. [14] and Peters et al. [15, 16].

Given the heterogeneity of chronic conditions, care pathways and ED contexts addressed in this review, additional methodological flexibility was required to capture both patient‐centred and system‐level dimensions of satisfactory care. Recognising this need, the framework ‘N’ was used to combine analytical rigour with practical applicability, enhance stakeholder engagement and support interpretive evaluation of findings (Table 7). Specifically, the Framework ‘N’ operationalises principles of caring science within the scoping review process, enabling the systematic integration of experiential, relational and system‐level dimensions of care that are not explicitly captured in traditional scoping review frameworks. This approach accommodates heterogeneous patient populations, varying chronic conditions and complex ED contexts, allowing for a flexible and person‐centred analysis of satisfactory care. The Framework ‘N’ therefore served as an interpretive analytical layer within the scoping review process, supporting the integration of experiential, relational and system‐level dimensions of satisfactory care.

Search Strategy

A systematic and comprehensive search was conducted across the PubMed, Cochrane and Scopus databases for studies published between January 2013 and February 2024. Prior to manuscript submission, the search was updated to ensure that no additional eligible studies published within the predefined timeframe had been missed. The inclusion of PubMed, Cochrane and Scopus was intended to cover medical, interdisciplinary and system‐level literature.

The search strategy was guided by the PEO framework (Population: adults with chronic diseases; Exposure: ED use; Outcome: satisfactory care) to ensure that studies addressing relevant populations, interventions and outcomes were identified (Table 1).

Keywords, MeSH terms and synonyms were combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR) and truncations to capture variations in terminology [17, 18, 19]. Filters were applied for publication year (2013–2024), adult populations (≥ 18 years), and English language.

Duplicate records were removed using EndNote X20, and screening was conducted in two stages: (1) title and abstract review, and (2) full‐text review. Study selection was performed independently by two authors to ensure accuracy and reduce selection bias.

The search strategy was designed to balance comprehensiveness and relevance, ensuring the inclusion of studies that directly address satisfactory care for adults with chronic conditions in emergency department settings. Limiting the search to the period 2013–2024 reflects the need to focus on contemporary developments in healthcare, including policy reforms, the integration of digital health technologies, and updated approaches to chronic disease management. This time frame also aligns with broader European health policy initiatives, as the European Union invested significantly in health strategies during the 2007–2013 period, promoting cross‐national collaboration aimed at fostering healthy lifestyles and improving care for people living with chronic conditions. Within this context, Member States were encouraged to develop and implement national health plans with a strong emphasis on the prevention and management of chronic diseases [20].

The full search strategies and Boolean search strings used for each database are provided in Supporting Information (Table S5) to enhance methodological transparency and reproducibility.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

Studies were included if they met all of the following conditions:

Population: Adults aged 18 years or older with chronic medical conditions, ensuring focus on adult populations experiencing long‐term physical illnesses.

Setting: ED contexts, capturing experiences of acute care encounters.

Outcomes: Investigated experiences, perceptions, or outcomes related to satisfactory care, including relational, communicative and transitional dimensions central to patient‐centred care.

Study design: Primary research studies, including qualitative, quantitative, cross‐sectional, or cohort designs, to allow comprehensive mapping of available evidence.

Language: English‐language publications, to allow consistent interpretation and appraisal, and due to inadequate translation sources.

Exclusion Criteria

Studies were excluded if they involve the following:

Population exclusions: Involved paediatric populations (< 18 years) to maintain conceptual consistency with adult chronic care experiences.

Condition exclusions: Focused solely on mental health disorders, personality disorders, or military‐related populations to ensure alignment with chronic somatic conditions.

Temporal or situational exclusions: Related to COVID‐19, as pandemic‐specific care experiences represent atypical emergency contexts outside the scope of this review.

Professional exclusions: Included non‐health professionals as participants, as the review focused on patient experiences and healthcare professional perspectives.

Intervention exclusions: Examined only pharmaceutical treatments or medical procedures without assessing patient experiences, to maintain relevance to patient‐centred and system‐level care dimensions.

Relevance exclusions: Were deemed irrelevant to the review objectives based on title or abstract screening, ensuring focus on studies addressing satisfactory care in ED settings.

Systematic reviews: We did not use systematic reviews because their primary objective was to synthesise original research or primary studies. Including systematic reviews could have led to duplication of evidence, potentially introducing bias and distorting results due to the over‐representation of certain studies. If systematic reviews had been included, the overlap could have complicated interpretation and diluted the clarity of the findings [21].

These criteria ensured that the review captured research directly relevant to understanding how adults with chronic illnesses experience satisfactory care in EDs, while maintaining methodological transparency and conceptual clarity.

Study Selection

The systematic search yielded a total of 2896 records. Following removal of 317 clinical studies (e.g., trials, protocols) and 42 duplicates, 2537 records remained for screening. Title and abstract review resulted in 135 articles selected for full‐text assessment. One article was not retrieved (Table S4). Fifteen studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the scoping review. The reasons for exclusion are described in Table S3 (Supporting Information).

To ensure methodological rigour and minimise selection bias, study selection was performed independently by two authors, with discrepancies resolved through discussion. A PRISMA flowchart (Figure 1) provides a visual overview of the screening and selection process.

Quality Appraisal

The authors independently assessed each included study to evaluate its methodological quality. The Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) was used for qualitative studies (Table 2) and cohort studies (Table 3). For qualitative studies, the CASP tool was used, comprising 10 questions, while the CASP checklist for cohort studies includes 12 questions. Quantitative studies were appraised using the checklist developed by Coughlan et al. [29], which consists of 16 questions (Table 4). Cross‐sectional studies were evaluated using the AXIS tool, which includes 20 questions (Table 5) [38]. Across all study designs, each item was assessed using a standardised scoring approach, with responses categorised as ‘Yes,’ ‘No,’ or ‘Can't tell’. According to Harrison et al. [39], all above studies had a 9‐point rating system employed to categorise study quality in order to maintain uniformity among designs. Scores of 7–9 were deemed high quality, scores of 5–6 medium level and scores of 0–4 low quality. Strong methodological quality was generally demonstrated by the high grades received by the majority of the included research [39]. Quality appraisal was undertaken to enhance the interpretive value of the findings.

Data Extraction and Synthesis

Key attributes of each included study—such as authorship, year of publication, study design, population characteristics and principal findings related to satisfactory care—were systematically extracted into a standardised form [40]. Data extraction was performed independently by two authors to minimise error and ensure consistency, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus.

Following extraction, a thematic synthesis was conducted to identify, code and categorise recurring patterns across studies. The synthesis followed an inductive coding approach commonly used in qualitative evidence synthesis, informed by methodological guidance for scoping review data synthesis [40]. Initially, line‐by‐line coding was applied to capture concepts related to patient experiences, care processes and system‐level factors. These codes were grouped into four overarching themes: (a) health systems; (b) transitions in care and ED usage; (c) interdisciplinary collaboration; and (d) obstacles and difficulties in service delivery. This approach allowed for a structured, transparent and reproducible synthesis that integrates both patient‐centred and system‐level dimensions of satisfactory care in emergency departments.

Health Systems

Population aging, increased life expectancy and demographic shifts contribute to higher ED utilisation among older adults and patients with chronic illnesses [26, 41]. Improving primary care teamwork and restructuring EDs can enhance patient follow‐up after hospital discharge and reduce the risk of recurrent exacerbations [33]. Individualised treatment plans optimise resource use and improve hospitalisation outcomes [27], while patient education and support for disease self‐management help decrease readmissions [35]. Insurance coverage, multimorbidity, sociodemographic characteristics and early integration of palliative care further influence ED use [31, 34, 37]. Palliative care may reduce unnecessary ED visits among elderly nursing home residents, although inadequate pre‐referral evaluation highlights gaps in primary care services [32]. Strategies to improve ED management include strengthening primary care [33, 34] and implementing technology‐based interventions and educational programs [31, 34]. Standardising the management of chronic diseases may enhance care; yet, in emergency departments, time constraints may impede efficient patient communication [34].

Use of the ED and Care Transitions

Functional and cognitive assessments prior to admission are associated with better outcomes, prevention of complications such as delirium and muscular dysfunction, and shorter hospital stays [26, 28]. Patients with limited mobility or support are more likely to require hospitalisation, which can negatively affect older adults, especially those with dementia, through increased risk of infections, bedsores, disorientation and functional decline [26, 28, 30]. Pain management is critical; early opioid administration improves ED length of stay and patient experience for sickle cell disease [27], while prompt pain control is a priority for patients with cardiovascular disease or diabetes [25]. Care for patients with mental health disorders remains inconsistent, with some ED‐based interventions, such as HIV testing, showing better outcomes, whereas post‐discharge support is often inadequate [22]. Follow‐up for patients with conditions like COPD may be continuous but delayed [33]. ED wait times range from 32 to 82 min, and patient satisfaction is influenced by timely pain management, patient education, and follow‐up [24, 25, 27, 33]. Persistent gaps include fragmented post‐discharge care and insufficient mental health management, highlighting the need for improved follow‐up, timely interventions and service coordination.

Interdisciplinary Collaboration

Coordinated multidisciplinary teams play a crucial role in providing satisfactory care. Patients frequently experience difficulty managing medications, understanding disease processes, or scheduling follow‐up appointments, contributing to gaps in care transitions [22, 35]. Effective collaboration among healthcare professionals improves outcomes, as seen in patients with HIV who see multiple providers and in sickle cell disease management [22, 36]. Strengthening primary care through physician assistants, nurse practitioners, home visits and virtual consultations can reduce unnecessary ED visits and alleviate patient anxiety [25, 30]. Enhanced staff‐patient interaction and primary care physician involvement in post‐hospital care improve treatment adherence and outcomes [23, 24]. Coordination between geriatric specialists and ED physicians reduces complications such as pressure sores and delirium and lowers readmission rates [28, 32]. Despite these improvements, frequent ED visits often result from poor coordination and communication, emphasising the need for integrated care models and structured interdisciplinary approaches.

Barriers and Challenges

Multiple factors affect patient satisfaction and the quality of ED care. Inadequate training and stigma hinder care for vulnerable groups, including patients with chronic illnesses and those living with HIV [22, 36, 42]. Organisational commitment, clear guidelines and targeted education are recommended to address these barriers [22]. Structural and resource constraints, such as variability in geriatric assessment networks, limited personnel, tight budgets and infrastructure gaps, contribute to inequities in care delivery [28, 43, 44]. Socioeconomic factors and patient fear or uncertainty further exacerbate disparities and drive unnecessary ED visits [25, 31, 45]. Inadequate teamwork, staff burnout and poor communication compromise patient safety and continuity of care [46]. Significant knowledge gaps remain regarding the long‐term effectiveness of interventions addressing resource allocation, stigma reduction and staff training, highlighting the need for evidence‐based strategies to improve ED services.

Discussion

This scoping review identified four overarching themes related to the experiences of older adults and individuals living with chronic conditions in ED settings: health systems, ED utilisation and care transitions, interdisciplinary collaboration and barriers to care. The discussion interprets these findings in relation to existing literature and relevant theoretical perspectives, and considers their implications for healthcare policy, service organisation and clinical practice.

Health Systems

Population aging and increasing multimorbidity place growing pressure on healthcare systems, a trend consistently reflected in the studies included in this scoping review. The findings indicate that inadequate organisation of primary care services and limited integration with EDs contribute substantially to patients' reliance on emergency care [26, 33, 41]. Consequently, the need for tailored care plans and structural reorganisation of services emerges as critical [47, 48]. Similar conclusions have been reported in international literature emphasising the importance of integrated chronic disease management through coordinated approaches between primary and secondary care [27, 49]. The use of digital health tools and patient education programmes appears to reduce readmissions and improve adherence; however, further evaluation of their long‐term effectiveness remains necessary [31, 34, 37].

ED Utilisation and Care Transitions

The results highlight that individuals with chronic conditions face significant challenges during transitions from hospital to community care, including delays in follow‐up and insufficient communication across services [24, 26, 28]. Evidence from previous studies suggests that the implementation of transitional care protocols, timely pain management and ongoing patient education can improve patient experiences and reduce repeated ED visits [25, 27]. To illustrate potential solutions, several interventions have been identified in the literature: transitional care programs, nurse navigator roles and community paramedicine initiatives have demonstrated improvements in care coordination and patient outcomes [50, 51, 52, 53, 54, 55, 56, 57]. Similarly, evaluations of digital health tools and geriatric ED models offer examples of innovative approaches to enhance continuity of care, although their long‐term effectiveness requires further study [58, 59, 60]. Importantly, delays and discontinuities in care are not solely organisational in nature but are also influenced by social factors, such as lack of family support or social isolation, highlighting the need for multidimensional interventions that address both clinical and social determinants of care continuity [58, 59].

Effective Care Models and Interventions Identified in the Review

Beyond describing gaps in emergency and primary care integration, this review identified several care models and intervention approaches that have shown potential to improve outcomes and patient experience among adults with chronic conditions in ED settings. Transitional care programs, particularly those incorporating structured discharge planning, early follow‐up scheduling and patient‐centred education, were associated with reduced readmissions and greater continuity of care [24, 33, 35, 58, 59]. Nurse‐navigator or liaison roles contributed to improved coordination across services by supporting patients in understanding treatment plans, arranging follow‐up care and managing medication regimens [30, 35, 50], thereby reducing anxiety and avoidable ED revisits. Geriatric‐oriented ED models and multidisciplinary team approaches—integrating geriatric specialists, physiotherapists and social workers—were reported to prevent complications such as functional decline and delirium and to enhance individualised care for frail older adults [26, 28, 32, 57, 58, 61, 62, 63]. In parallel, community‐based and technology‐supported approaches, including telehealth follow‐up and digital communication tools, facilitated safer transitions to home and strengthened links between emergency and community care [31, 34]. Taken together, these examples illustrate that satisfactory care in EDs is best supported by integrated, team‐based and continuity‐oriented models rather than isolated clinical responses. However, implementation remains inconsistent across settings, and few studies provided robust longitudinal evaluation. Future research should prioritise comparative assessment of these models to determine which components are most effective, for which populations, and under which organisational conditions [64, 65, 66, 67, 68, 69, 70, 71, 72].

Interdisciplinary Collaboration

Interdisciplinary collaboration emerged as a key determinant of satisfactory care. Coordinated teamwork among physicians, nurses, physiotherapists and other healthcare professionals contributes to reduced repeat ED visits and improved patient adherence to treatment plans [22, 24, 25, 30, 35, 36]. These findings align with principles of caring science, in which holistic and person‐centred approaches enhance patient experience and care safety. Despite demonstrated benefits, the implementation of interdisciplinary models remains limited due to gaps in staff training, infrastructure and organisational support, underscoring the need for investment in education, coordination mechanisms and technological resources [23].

Barriers to Care

Patients with chronic illnesses encounter multiple barriers within ED settings, including stigma, insufficient staff training, limited resources and inadequate communication [23, 36, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46]. These barriers are associated with lower patient satisfaction and increased reliance on emergency services. Existing literature supports the view that addressing such challenges requires system‐level interventions, including targeted staff education, development of protocols for vulnerable populations, and strengthening of primary care as a first point of contact [22, 43, 70, 71]. Moreover, the adoption of digital tools and integrated information systems may enhance communication, patient safety and continuity of care [65, 66].

Overall, this review demonstrates that the experience of patients with chronic conditions in EDs is shaped by a complex interplay of systemic, organisational and individual factors. Improving satisfactory care requires not only reducing ED burden, but also adopting a holistic approach that strengthens links with primary care, promotes person‐centred care pathways and prioritises workforce education. The integration of digital technologies and the development of interdisciplinary teams appear to be key strategies for enhancing patient experience and reducing avoidable ED utilisation.

By adopting a scoping review methodology, this study extends existing literature by synthesising patient‐centred and system‐level dimensions of satisfactory care across diverse chronic conditions and emergency care contexts. Rather than focusing on single diagnoses or utilisation metrics, it maps how satisfactory care is conceptualised and experienced, thereby offering an integrative perspective that can inform future intervention development and policy design.

The findings of this review can also be interpreted through the lens of caring science and person‐centred care. Across the included studies, satisfactory care was not defined solely by clinical efficiency or symptom resolution, but by relational and contextual dimensions such as respect, trust, communication clarity, continuity and emotional security during and after the ED encounter [23, 30, 35]. These elements reflect core caring‐science principles, whereby care is understood as a relational, ethical and holistic process grounded in recognition of the person's vulnerability, lived experience and need for continuity across care settings [57, 70]. In this sense, satisfactory care in EDs emerges not only as an organisational or operational outcome, but as a moral and relational achievement shaped by how professionals collaborate, communicate and support patients throughout transitions of care. By integrating these perspectives, the present review contributes a conceptual bridge between emergency care research and caring science, offering a more comprehensive understanding of what constitutes meaningful and person‐centred care for individuals with chronic illness in emergency contexts [71].

Implications

The findings of this scoping review highlight the urgent need for structured, person‐centred care models in EDs that address the complex and multifaceted needs of people living with chronic illnesses. In line with the identified themes—particularly health system fragmentation, challenges in care transitions and limited interdisciplinary collaboration—strengthening primary care integration and enhancing coordination between EDs and community services should be a key priority for health systems [72].

The results further suggest that promoting interdisciplinary teamwork within EDs, alongside the systematic use of digital health tools and telemedicine, may improve continuity of care, reduce unnecessary ED visits and enhance patient satisfaction [73]. These strategies are particularly relevant for older adults and individuals with multimorbidity, for whom fragmented care pathways and poor communication were recurrent barriers to satisfactory care.

At a policy and organisational level, the findings support the development of targeted clinical training programmes, integrated care pathways and health policies that prioritise effectiveness, equity and high‐quality care for people with chronic conditions [74]. Collectively, these implications underscore the need for health systems to move beyond episodic emergency care toward more coordinated, person‐centred and sustainable models of chronic care management.

Limitations

This review was restricted to English‐language articles, which may have limited both the scope and quantity of data retrieved. Additionally, although three major databases (PubMed, Scopus and Cochrane) were included to capture medical and interdisciplinary literature, the exclusion of nursing‐specific databases such as CINAHL may have resulted in the omission of some relevant nursing‐focused studies. Furthermore, as different methodologies might provide different results, the quality of the included studies and their varied analytical approaches may have had an impact on the results. The overall findings may have been skewed by the overrepresentation of some illnesses, such as COPD, as a result of the wide phrase ‘chronic disease.’ Conditions affecting other body systems were not thoroughly addressed; instead, the majority of investigations concentrated on respiratory and haematopoietic disorders. In order to produce more thorough and trustworthy information, these limitations underscore the need for future research that covers a larger spectrum of chronic illnesses and uses more consistent and methodical approaches. Finally, as a scoping review, this study did not aim to assess effectiveness or establish causal relationships, but rather to map the existing evidence, which should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusions

Health systems are under increasing pressure due to population aging and the growing complexity of chronic illnesses, with EDs frequently functioning as a primary point of care. Insufficient supportive structures, functional limitations and suboptimal communication between care providers contribute to higher readmission rates, fragmented care and challenges in pain management. The findings of this scoping review highlight the need to move toward more continuous and coordinated care models, emphasising tailored care plans and structured care pathways to improve outcomes and reduce avoidable ED use.

Comprehensive and effective care for people with chronic conditions relies heavily on interdisciplinary collaboration and specialised geriatric expertise. To address inefficiencies and inequities in care delivery, health system reforms that promote integrated models of care and stronger coordination between primary and secondary services are warranted. Future research should focus on optimising care transitions and informing policy development to ensure equitable access to high‐quality care for this growing population.
