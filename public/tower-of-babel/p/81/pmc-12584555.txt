The relationship between digital burnout and academic procrastination and the mediating roles of life satisfaction and the fatigue in this relationship
Authors: Buğra Çağatay Savaş, Murat Turan, Selim Asan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn parallel with developing technologies, university students may experience digital burnout and postpone their academic tasks because of the need to be in digital environments. This study aimed to examine the relationship between digital burnout and academic procrastination and the mediating roles of life satisfaction and fatigue in this relationship. For this purpose, the direct relationships between the variables and the indirect effects of digital burnout on academic procrastination through the mediating variables were tested.MethodsA total of 277 sport sciences students participated in the study conducted with the relational survey model. The Personal Information Form, Digital Burnout Scale, Satisfaction with Life Scale, Calder Fatigue Scale, and Academic Procrastination Scale were used as data collection tools. Descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, SEM and mediation analysis were used in the analysis of the data. Mediation analysis is a popular statistical procedure for testing hypotheses about the mechanisms by which a causal effect operates. Path analysis of the research model was conducted.ResultsReferences numbered 53 in Question 7 and 107 and 126 in Question 10 were retrieved from the Turkish National Thesis Center, where the corresponding thesis titles can be located through the database search function. Additionally, Reference 116 is available as an open-access source provided by the University of Alabama. As no specific citation guidelines for theses were supplied, these references have been formatted in accordance with the available information. I look forward to your response and any suggestions you may have. Sincerely,The results revealed that digital burnout had a negative effect on life satisfaction, a positive effect on fatigue and academic procrastination, and that life satisfaction and fatigue had positive and significant effects on academic procrastination. It was concluded that digital burnout had a positive and significant effect on academic procrastination in the absence of other mediating variables and that life satisfaction and fatigue had a partial mediating effect on the effect of digital burnout on academic procrastination.ConclusionsNegative and significant relationships were found between digital burnout and life satisfaction, as well as between life satisfaction and academic procrastination. In contrast, positive and significant relationships were observed between digital burnout and fatigue, and between digital burnout and academic procrastination. Furthermore, both digital burnout and fatigue were found to increase academic procrastination. The analyses indicated that life satisfaction and fatigue played a partial mediating role in the relationship between digital burnout and academic procrastination.Future research may investigate potential strategies to reduce the effects of digital burnout on academic procrastination (e.g., social activities, sports). Additionally, longitudinal data could be used in various disciplines to examine differences in mediation effects across demographic variables, and cultural differences could be explored in samples from diverse cultural contexts.

Introduction

In parallel with developing technologies, the need to be present in digital environments continues to increase. A study revealed that the number of social media users reached 4.74 billion (59.3% of the world’s population) at the beginning of October 2022 and increased by 4.2% globally in one year [1]. In terms of the world average, the average time individuals spend on the internet is 6 h and 58 min per day, whereas the average time spent using social media platforms is 2 h and 27 min [2]. At the beginning of 2023, it was estimated that there were 71.38 million internet users in Turkey (83.4%), of which 62.55 million (73.1%) were social media users. 81.68 million cellular mobile connections were active, constituting 95.4% of the total population [3]. Strong indicators indicate a rapid increase in the use of digital devices, especially during the pandemic [4]. This situation shows that the dependence on technological software and hardware is increasing daily in the world and in Turkey and that people spend a significant amount of time in technological environments. In this framework, university students may exhibit academic procrastination behavior due to their obligation to be in the digital environment, digital burnout, fatigue and life satisfaction levels. This study, in which students’ academic procrastination behaviors are examined in terms of the variables mentioned above, is expected to contribute to the literature.

Crawford [5] stated that the overuse of social media can be referred to as background listening, where users are inundated with information, news, conversation, and chatter throughout the day. This situation causes people to create a cyber-based burden for themselves with online activities for whatever purpose, such as work or leisure. These are behaviors such as the perceived pressure to reply to someone, to join a conversation, meetings, fear of missing out, updating information, increased use of the internet and smartphones to watch TV shows or movies, or for educational purposes. Over time, these behaviors may begin to manifest in ways that can be associated with psychological distress [6].

A previous study indicated that academic procrastination in college students involves the interaction of behavioral, cognitive, and emotional components and a lack of study or time management [7]. A student’s level of burnoutdirectly affects that student’s effectiveness and productivity. Burnout-related emotional, cognitive, and behavioral reactions affect students’ performance [8]. In this study, university students may exhibit academic procrastination behavior depending on their obligations to be present in the digital environment, digital burnout, fatigue, and life satisfaction levels. This study, which examines students’ academic procrastination behavior in terms of the specified variables, is expected to contribute to the literature.

Conceptual framework

This article proposes the Conservation of Resources theory (COR) as an integrated framework for understanding the direct and mediated effects of exhaustion on procrastination. This theoretical model explains the motivation that drives people to conserve their existing resources and seek new ones. Therefore, since burnout arises when resources are threatened or lost, this theory can be considered significant in different areas, such as burnout and, more specifically, student burnout [9].

Definitions of key terms

Burnout

Burnout is an occupational phenomenon in the 11th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11). It is not considered a medical condition. It is described in the “factors affecting health status or contact with health services” section, which includes the reasons why people seek health services but is not classified as a disease or health condition and is defined as “burnout is a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that cannot be successfully managed” [10].

Burnout is a feeling of burnout due to many workloads, but it is the least reciprocated. Burnout is an emotional, exhausting, and cynical process often observed in people who perform person-related work [11]. It is known to be among the precursors of decreased academic achievement [12].

Digital burnout

Digital burnout can be defined as fatigue and stress due to the time spent on digital media and devices. Digital burnout has negative physical, psychological, and social consequences. Digital burnout is defined by digital aging, deprivation, and emotional burnout [13].

According to Quill [14], excessive use of digital devices leads to digital burnout. Digital burnout is triggered by prolonged and excessive use of digital devices [15].

The situation in which employees experience burnout emerges and develops with the stressors that create technostress, and ultimately, technostress leads to digital burnout [16]. Digital burnout is caused by prolonged use of digital devices [17]. Today, information overload and digital overstimulation lead to digital burnout. Digital burnout is a type of burnout triggered by prolonged and excessive use of digital devices [18].

Employees’ prolonged online presence is associated with low levels of physiological activation, high levels of feelings of tension, perceived expectations, discomfort and anxiety. A delay of 1–2 s in response to digital meeting platforms causes people to perceive the respondent as less friendly or less focused on the conversation. All these factors lead to burnout in the form of digital burnout [19].

Mobility restrictions due to coronavirus have restricted people’s daily lives, and the intensive use of digital space, such as social media, webinars, and virtual meetings, has started to create a new addiction in this area; this virtual overload has started to be called digital burnout [20].

According to Breytenbach [21], digital burnout is defined by the amount of burnout experienced. Individuals with digital burnout are constantly tired and unable to maintain their concentration or cope with their routines. This burnout leads to decreased productivity, as they have difficulty focusing on their tasks because of constant multitasking [22]. Not wanting to go to work or work can indicate digital burnout [23]. Specifically, digital burnout refers to decreased interest in a job, fatigue, anxiety, depression, or diminished feelings resulting from spending too much time on digital devices [6].

Those who experience digital burnout are constantly tired and less productive because they find it difficult to focus on the task while constantly multitasking. When they feel demotivated, they often associate their work with negative emotions (such as restlessness, cognitive problems, emotional fatigue, and anxiety or stress). The increased use of digital devices and long working hours create a risk of digital burnout [24].

Academic procrastination

Procrastination is a multidimensional concept that includes cognitive, affective, and behavioral components [25]. Procrastination is defined as delaying or postponing fulfilling a task, taking responsibility or making a decision on a matter [26], a voluntary delay in the action plan despite the expectation that the consequences will be bad [27], and it can also be considered a self-regulation failure [28–30].

Procrastination is common not only in people’s daily life or work but also in higher education, which is also referred to as academic procrastination. Milgram et al. [31] noted that there are five types of procrastination: general procrastination, academic procrastination, decision-making procrastination, neurotic procrastination, and compulsive procrastination [32].

Academic procrastination is often defined as the inability to complete an academic task within the expected timeframe or postponing the work/task/assignment that needs to be completed [33], voluntarily procrastinating despite expecting the worst [27, 34], and a purposeful and unnecessary delay in completing academic tasks [35]. Academic procrastination behavior, which is a common problem among students [36], can cause them to feel bored and tired [37] while negatively affecting their academic performance [36]. It is generally observed in middle, high, and university level students [38, 39].

Excessive use of online technology increases the risk of problematic digital behavior, such as excessive use of social media, online betting, webinar fatigue, and digital burnout. The relationship between burnout and portable devices can be attributed to hyper connectivity and compulsive use. This compulsive use further contributes to digital burnout. However, burnout can be characterized as a syndrome with three dimensions: fatigue, negativity or cynicism, and reduced effectiveness. There is a need to explore evidence on the mediating processes that make online digital technology beneficial and harmful for its users [40].

Fatigue

Fatigue is a multifactorial condition defined as a personal and unpleasant symptom involving whole-body feelings, interfering with the individual’s ability to cope with activities of daily living, and involving the embodiment of emotions ranging from fatigue to burnout. It is associated with various diseases [41].

Fatigue is a concept that adversely affects both physiological and psychological aspects. Chronic fatigue is a typical symptom of some diseases, but it can also occur in people without any identified somatic illness [42].

Fatigue is a multidimensional concept encompassing physiological and psychological aspects [42] and is a significant determinant of impaired quality of life and inability to work [43]. Fatigue can be envisioned as the depletion of an individual’s metabolic reserves, stimulation, or a disturbance of energy balance and availability [44]. Fatigue is an acute, subjective, sometimes overwhelming, but transient (physical, emotional, and behavioral) condition resulting from the stress and overwork inherent in life in individuals without any health problems [45].

While it is important to consider the fatigue of university students and their ICT experience, it is also important to consider the possible effects of the intensity of use. It can be assumed that the high frequency of students’ ICT use belongs to their communication areas. However, it should also be considered that the use of ICTs in academic life has increased as universities aim to ensure academic success for their students [9].

In environments where information communication technologies are used intensively, university students can say that their quality of life [46] and mental health are related to various factors (psychological, academic, biological, lifestyle, social, and financial) [47].

Life satisfaction

One of the primary goals of individuals is to experience lives that provide personal well-being. Likewise, most generally consider a high degree of average life satisfaction desirable, indicating that individuals in a country or region tend to experience fulfilling lives [48].

Life satisfaction is an assessment of the extent to which a person is satisfied with the achievements they have achieved thus far [49]. Life satisfaction, as a very important variable in the psychological health of young people, is “a distinct construct that represents a cognitive and general assessment of one’s quality of life as a whole” [50], a general assessment of one’s feelings and attitudes about one’s life at a given point in time, ranging from negative to positive. Life satisfaction is “the subjective assessment of current quality of life, which is a key indicator of psychological health and well-being” [39].

It is possible that information sharing in social media, as a social exchange behavior with positive reciprocal reactions, helps increase individual life satisfaction, and information sharing leads to informational reciprocity and thus contributes to life satisfaction [51].

Hypotheses

Relationship between digital burnout and life satisfaction

The problem of digital burnout specifically refers to decreased interest in a job, fatigue, anxiety, depression or decreased emotions resulting from spending too much time on digital devices [6]. Studies have shown that as the level of life satisfaction increases in adolescents, internet addiction decreases or, as internet addiction increases, life satisfaction decreases [52], and as burnout syndrome increases, life satisfaction decreases [53]. Determining the variables associated with the burnout and life satisfaction of students is also important in terms of quality of life and academic success [54].

In the literature, studies have concluded that there is a negative relationship between burnout and life satisfaction [54–56]. Similar to the conclusion reached by Masur [57] for digital communication, the negative effect of digital burnout on life satisfaction can be explained within the framework of the displacement hypothesis.

It is a condition where physical and mental fatigue is caused by spending too much time in front of the screen. Symptoms of this condition are apathy, indifference, or mental fatigue caused by exposure to too much information [18].

Materials and methods

Research model

The research was designed with the “relational survey model”, which is a quantitative method in terms of its purpose and scope. A relational survey model can be defined as a “research model used to determine the existence and/or degree of relationship between variables” [75, 76]. SPSS 25.0, AMOS 23 and PROCESS version 4.2 were used to analyze the data [77]. The relationships between digital burnout and academic procrastination and between life satisfaction and fatigue in this relationship were examined. PROCESS macro model 4 was used to examine this role. This model allows controlling for the indirect effects of individual mediators while controlling for other variables. Model 4 was chosen because it can be used for both simple mediation models and parallel multiple mediation models [78]. Furthermore, PROCESS is much easier to use than any SEM program. In addition, one of the most important differences between PROCESS and SEM programs is that SEM solves the entire equation system simultaneously through iteration, typically using maximum likelihood (ML) rather than estimating the parameters of each equation independently. Since it was thought that the interpretation of the findings failed to account for the downward trend in ML standard errors when SEM was used with smaller samples [79], PROCESS Model 4 was used. Model 4 allows for up to 10 mediators (M) working in parallel [80] (Fig. 1). There are two mediator variables in this study.

In this study, it was hypothesized that digital burnout (X) would indirectly influence academic procrastination (Y) behavior through multiple mediators causally linked by life satisfaction (M1) and fatigue (M2).

In the path analysis of the model, x2 = 45.415, sd = 17 and x2/sd = 2.671 (p < 0.000).

Sample of the study

The study population consists of students at Erzurum Technical University Faculty of Sports Sciences. In the sample selection, the convenience sampling method [81] was used from among random selection methods, and 277 participants (57 women, 20.5%, and 220 men, 79.5%) were reached. The average age of these participants was 25 ± 7.4, with 95 (34.2%) being first-year students, 79 (28.5%) second-year students, 55 (19.9%) third-year students, and 48 (17.4%) fourth-year students. The effect of demographic characteristics was not examined in the study.

Data collection

Four scales were used to collect the data together with the “Personal Information Form” of the participants. The data was collected using a random selection method.

The data used in the study were obtained by applying two questionnaires face-to-face in each class during the period of February 06–March 08, 2024, without affecting the course flow. In each class, the two applications could be applied at least one month apart. Thus, since it was not possible to collect the study data from different sources to prevent methodological bias, data collection was spread over a period of two months to create a temporal separation between the measurements, as suggested by Podsakoff et al. [82]. Before each application, the researchers explained to the participants that the answers would be used for scientific purposes and that their consent would be obtained.

In this study, the statistical common method bias of the data was also examined. Harman’s single-factor test is a statistical method used to detect common method bias in confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Bias can inflate the relationships between variables and lead to erroneous results. When the bias is less than the critical value of 50%, it indicates acceptable bias [82–84]. Therefore, Harman’s one-way analysis was conducted. As a result of the factor analysis conducted with all the variables, 7 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were found, and the variance contribution rate of the first factor was determined to be 29.361%. Since this value is less than the critical value of 50%, there is no common method bias in the data of this study.

Personal information form

Demographic information about the participant students was obtained with a personal information form. The researcher created the Personal Information Form. This form includes gender and class variables.

Digital burnout scale

The digital burnout scale developed by Erten and Özdemir [15] is a 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree) consisting of three subdimensions and 24 items. The subdimensions of the measurement tool are named digital aging, digital deprivation, and emotional burnout. The Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficients are 0.919, 0.885, and 0.865, respectively. The entire scale explained 41.419% of the total variance, and the Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient was 0.946.

In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the overall scale was 0.952. The item-total correlation values ranged between 0.575 and 0.733. KMO = 0.929 (Chi-Square 4601,958, df: 276, p: 0.000). It was determined that 65.673% of the variance was explained. In path analysis, x2 = 678,328, sd = 240 and x2/sd = 2,826 (p < 0.000).

Life satisfaction scale

The Satisfaction with Life Scale, which was developed by Diener et al. [85] and whose validity and reliability study was conducted by Dağlı and Baysal [86], has a single-factor structure and consists of a total of 5 items. The scale’s Cronbach’s alpha internal consistency coefficient was 0.88, and the total variance explained was 68.389%. The scale was scored on a 5-point Likert scale. The minimum score is 5, and the maximum score is 25. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the overall scale was 0.736. The item–total correlation values ranged between 0.323 and 0.666. KMO = 0.754 (Chi-Square 309,220, df: 10, p: 0.000). It was determined that 49.454% of the variance was explained. In path analysis, x2 = 10,169, sd = 4 and x2/sd = 2,542 (p < 0.000).

Calder fatigue scale

A validity and reliability study of the Calder Fatigue Scale developed by Cella & Chalder [87] was conducted by Adın et al. [88]. The scale consists of 11 items and two subdimensions. The first subdimension is named physical fatigue, and the second subdimension is named mental fatigue. The total variance explained by the scale is 56.396%. The internal consistency coefficients of the scale are 0.862 and 0.704, respectively, and 0.863 for the overall scale. The scale is a 4-point Likert-type scale (0/1 = as much as usual to 3/4 = more than usual), and scores ranging from 0 to 11 to 33–44 can be obtained from the whole scale. Low scores indicate a low level of fatigue.

In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the overall scale was 0.929. The item-total correlation values ranged between 0.656 and 0.744. KMO = 0.920 (Chi-Square 1819,300, df: 55, p: 0.000). It was determined that 58.527% of the variance was explained. Path analysis revealed that x2 = 116,287, sd = 42 and x2/sd = 2,769 (p < 0.000).

Academic procrastination scale

The Academic Procrastination Scale was developed by McCloskey [89]. Yockey examined the psychometric properties of the scale [90]. Balkıs and Duru [91] stated that the short form of the Academic Procrastination Scale can be used as a valid and reliable measurement tool for a sample of university students in Turkey. There are 5 items in the short form of the Academic Procrastination Scale. The scale is a 5-point Likert-type scale. The internal consistency coefficient of the scale was calculated as Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88.

In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the overall scale was 0.799. The item-total correlation values ranged between 0.457 and 0.756, and KMO = 0.771 (Chi-Square 456,477, df: 10, p: 0.000). It was determined that 56,473% of the variance was explained. Path analysis revealed that x2 = 4,334, sd = 2 and x2/sd = 2,167 (p < 0.000).

Data analysis

The data obtained from the participants were checked and transferred to the statistical package program. The normality status of the data was tested, and it was found that it was in the range of −1.5–+1.5 and, therefore, had a normal distribution [92]. Descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, structural equation modeling (SEM), and mediation analysis were used to analyze the data. Mediation analysis is used to quantitatively test and inform theory and discussion of indirect effects in a mediation model [93]. At the beginning of the analysis, the necessary assumptions were checked as applied in regression analyses, and the suitability of the dataset for the analysis was checked. Scatter plots were examined to test whether the variables were linear. The regression analysis used the “enter method” to ensure that all the variables were included. The absence of multicollinearity between the variables was examined through correlations, which revealed that the variables did not have correlations greater than r = 0.80. To check for the absence of multicollinearity, the variance inflation factor (VIF) and condition index (condission index) values were checked. There were no outliers in the observation values, the observed errors were normally distributed, and the Durbin‒Watson coefficient was used [94]. The accuracy of the obtained coefficients at the 95% (p < 0.05) confidence interval was taken as a basis. In this study, as stated by Hayes [95], all regression/path coefficients were used in the unstandardized form. The bootstrap technique and 5000 resampling options were preferred in the analysis of mediation effects. In mediation analyses with bootstrapping, the 95% confidence interval values obtained from the analysis should not contain zero to support the research hypothesis [95]. To reveal the level of mediation, the variance account for (VAF) was calculated via the following formula: VAF = indirect effect/total effect. A calculation of less than 20% indicates no mediation, between 20% and 80% indicates partial mediation, and more than 80% indicates full mediation [96].

Discussion

This section discusses the findings obtained in the study within the literature framework. This study investigated the relationship between digital burnout and the academic procrastination behaviors of sport sciences students and the mediating roles of life satisfaction and fatigue in this relationship. For this purpose, the direct relationships between the variables and the indirect effects of digital burnout on academic procrastination through the mediating variables were examined.

Participants' levels of digital burnout, academic procrastination, life satisfaction, and fatigue

The mean scores of the students who participated in the study were moderate for academic procrastination, digital burnout, life satisfaction and fatigue (Table 1). Sidekli et al. [104] reported the digital burnout levels of teachers, and Karakaya Özyer and Altınsoy [105] reported that the academic procrastination tendencies of undergraduate and graduate students were moderate. Hayat et al. [106] reported that the academic procrastination scores of medical students, Günay [107] of university students, Özçelik Bozkurt [108] of undergraduate students of the tourism department, Ataş [109] of prospective teachers, and AlQudah et al. [110] of university students were moderate. Durmuş et al. [13] reported that in nursing students, Alimoradi et al. [111] reported a moderate relationship between problematic internet use and academic procrastination, so students who reported higher levels of problematic internet use had higher academic procrastination scores. Özçelik Bozkurt [108] reported a positive and moderately significant relationship between social media addiction and academic procrastination behavior. These results parallel our findings.

Unlike our findings, Güngör [112] reported that the academic procrastination scores of university students were weak (low). Gower et al. [17] reported that university students had above-average levels of digital burnout, whereas Khatatbeh et al. [113] reported that nurses had moderate to high levels of burnout.

Relationships between digital burnout, academic procrastination, fatigue, and life satisfaction

A statistically significant, positive, and high-level relationship was found between academic procrastination and digital burnout and between academic procrastination and fatigue among the sports science students who participated in the study. However, a negative, moderate, and statistically significant relationship was found between academic procrastination and life satisfaction. There was a negative, moderate, and statistically significant relationship between digital burnout and life satisfaction and a positive, high, and statistically significant relationship between digital burnout and fatigue. In addition, a negative, moderate, and statistically significant relationship was found between life satisfaction and fatigue. The fact that life satisfaction has a weaker mediating effect than does fatigue may suggest that students can push themselves to fulfill their academic tasks even when they are dissatisfied with their lives, while their cognitive and physical fatigue may prevent them from doing so.

Balkıs [65] reported a positive relationship between academic procrastination and students’ sense of burnout; academic procrastination significantly predicted the sense of burnout, and there was a significant negative relationship between students’ academic procrastination tendency and sense of burnout. Bernales-Turpo et al. [114] reported a negative correlation between life satisfaction and burnout. Similarly, Hawi and Samaha [115] reported a mediated negative relationship between social media addiction and life satisfaction among university students. Narci [116] reported that university students’ problematic internet use was positively associated with academic procrastination and negatively associated with life satisfaction. Hayat et al. [106] reported a positive and significant relationship between internet addiction and academic procrastination in medical students. These results are in line with our findings.

The Relationship Between Digital Burnout and Life Satisfaction

This study revealed that digital burnout had a negative and significant (B=−0.5931, p < 0.001) effect on life satisfaction and that digital burnout had a positive and significant (B = 0.8260, p < 0.001) effect on fatigue (Table 3-Model 1–2). Khatatbeh et al. [113] reported that burnout was negatively associated with quality of life. Kıvrak [117] determined a negative and inversely proportional relationship between the burnout levels of school administrators and their life satisfaction. Springer et al. [118] reported a strong relationship between academic stress caused by an overly demanding work environment and occupational burnout and chronic fatigue.

The literature contains numerous studies reporting a negative relationship between life satisfaction and burnout [119–122]. In addition, some studies have found that fatigue reduces life satisfaction [123, 124]. Therefore, it can be said that fatigue may affect burnout through life satisfaction. For this reason, future studies may test the mediating effect of fatigue on burnout through life satisfaction. Atasever et al. [125] reported that digital addiction negatively predicts life satisfaction in university students and that digital addiction has a partial mediating effect on the relationship between academic motivation and life satisfaction. Excessive use of social media is negatively related to life satisfaction [115, 126, 127].

The relationship between digital burnout and life satisfaction with academic procrastination

In this study, the effect of digital burnout on academic procrastination was positive and significant (B = 0.2278, p < 0.001), whereas the effect of life satisfaction on academic procrastination was negative and significant (B=−0.2739, p < 0.001). Other findings were that the effect of fatigue on academic procrastination was positive and significant (B = 0.4133, p < 0.001) and that digital burnout had a positive and significant effect on academic procrastination in the absence of other mediating variables (B = 0.7316, p < 0.001) (Table 3-Model 3–4). Life satisfaction has a weaker mediating effect than fatigue. This may be because there is a negative and moderate relationship between participants’ digital exhaustion and life satisfaction, and a positive and high relationship between fatigue and life satisfaction. Karakaya Özyer and Altınsoy [105], in their study with distance education undergraduate and graduate students, reported that, in correlation analysis, problematic internet use was positively related to academic procrastination, and in parallel mediation analysis, internet addiction had a direct predictive effect on students’ academic procrastination behaviors. Yang et al. [128] reported that addictive smartphone use positively affects academic procrastination (β = 0.21, p < 0.001) in university students. According to Altınok [51], digital addiction decreases and life satisfaction increases as high school students’ perceived academic achievement level increases. Demir and Kutlu [129] reported that internet addiction in adolescents negatively affects academic motivation and positively affects academic procrastination. Hinsch and Sheldon [130] reported that when social internet use was reduced, participants generally benefited, their life satisfaction increased, and their academic procrastination tendencies decreased. Demir Güdül et al. [131] reported that university students with high levels of life satisfaction had low levels of academic procrastination. Uzun et al. [132] reported that life satisfaction negatively predicts academic procrastination tendencies. Li et al. [39] reported that the smartphone addiction of university students was positively correlated with academic procrastination. Caratiquit and Caratiquit [133] reported that there is a positive and significant relationship between social media addiction and academic procrastination, which negatively affects academic achievement, and that academic procrastination fully mediates the relationship between social media addiction and academic achievement. In a parallel and recent study, Kaya [134] found that academic procrastination increases the risk of smartphone addiction as a risk factor for psychological well-being. This finding may support the idea that academic procrastination could directly or indirectly mediate digital burnout when evaluated in reverse causality. However, further research is needed in this area.

Theoretical Implications

When the research findings are examined from a theoretical perspective, it can be said that digital exhaustion is positively related to life satisfaction and fatigue, while these three independent variables are negatively related to the dependent variable of academic procrastination, which is consistent with the Conservation of Resources theory.

Practical Implications

This study revealed that digital exhaustion, which has emerged due to the increasing necessity to use digital systems and devices as a result of technological developments, increases fatigue and academic procrastination among university students while decreasing life satisfaction. At the same time, it was found that life satisfaction and fatigue have a mediating effect on academic procrastination.

Limitations

The limitations of the study include the fact that demographic characteristics were not included in the study, that data from participants from different academic disciplines were not included, and that no longitudinal study was conducted. The study was not extended to demographic variables such as age and class level. Data were collected solely from students in the field of sports sciences, without examining the situation of students from other disciplines (e.g., engineering, literature, health sciences). In addition, a time-centered approach to data collection was not employed, and thus the longitudinal invariance of the findings could not be demonstrated. Conducting the study with a Turkish student sample can also be considered another limitation.

Conclusion

In this study, a model was developed to investigate the direct role of digital addiction on academic procrastination among students of the Faculty of Sport Sciences, as well as the mediating roles of life satisfaction and fatigue. The findings indicated that digital burnout exerts a direct role, while life satisfaction and fatigue function as mediating variables in the relationship with academic procrastination. In this regard, the mean scores of academic procrastination, digital burnout, life satisfaction, and fatigue among the participants were at a moderate level.

Correlation analyses revealed a statistically significant, positive, and strong association between academic procrastination, digital burnout, and fatigue, whereas a statistically significant, negative, and moderate association was found between academic procrastination and life satisfaction. Furthermore, digital burnout demonstrated a statistically significant, negative, and moderate association with life satisfaction, and a statistically significant, positive, and strong association with fatigue. In addition, life satisfaction and fatigue were negatively, moderately, and significantly correlated.

The study showed that digital burnout is associated with reduced life satisfaction, which in turn is linked to higher academic procrastination. Digital burnout was also positively related to fatigue and academic procrastination. Even in the absence of mediating variables, digital burnout maintained a positive and statistically significant connection with academic procrastination. Life satisfaction and fatigue partially account for the association between digital burnout and academic procrastination, indicating that digital burnout contributes to procrastination both directly and indirectly through these variables.

In this study, digital burnout emerging from the growing necessity of using digital systems and devices due to technological developments was found to increase academic procrastination among students, while simultaneously reducing life satisfaction and elevating fatigue.

Future research may explore strategies to reduce procrastination associated with digital burnout (e.g., social engagement, physical activity, and similar practices). Additionally, further studies could examine whether fatigue sequentially mediates the link between burnout and life satisfaction in university students, whether procrastination contributes to digital burnout within a reverse-causality framework, and how longitudinal designs, gender, and other demographic variables shape these mediating mechanisms. Cross-cultural research could also reveal potential cultural variations in these relationships.
