Rituximab-based Induction in Vascularized Composite Allotransplantation: Prolonged Rejection-free Survival and the Role of Donor-specific Antibodies
Authors: Alexis K. Gursky, Sachin R. Chinta, Hailey P. Wyatt, Alay R. Shah, Samantha Lu, Massimo Mangiola, Bruce E. Gelb, Daniel J. Ceradini, Eduardo D. Rodriguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Vascularized composite allotransplantation (VCA) restores form and function in patients with severe disfigurement, yet acute rejection remains common within the first year. In solid organ transplantation, rituximab, a B cell–depleting agent, has been shown to reduce rejection in highly sensitized recipients, and donor-specific antibodies (DSAs) are established biomarkers of rejection. The impact of rituximab and DSAs in VCA remains unclear. This study examined rejection-free survival following rituximab induction and the relationship between positive DSA levels and rejection in VCA recipients.Methods:This retrospective review analyzed 4 patients who underwent facial transplantation between 2015 and 2023 at our institution. All patients received rituximab and antithymocyte globulin as part of their induction immunosuppression. Rejection-free survival was the primary outcome, with DSA levels assessed at baseline and throughout follow-up. A generalized linear mixed-effects model was used to evaluate the relationship between DSA positivity and rejection.Results:The median rejection-free survival was 674.5 days (range 279–1465 d). The mixed-effects model found no significant association between DSA positivity and rejection (P = 0.693). Some patients were DSA-negative during clinical rejection, and some were DSA-positive without signs of rejection.Conclusions:This is the first study to evaluate rituximab-based induction in a cohort of VCA recipients, demonstrating median rejection-free survival exceeding one and a half years. Although some patients were DSA-positive during rejection episodes, no significant correlation was observed, challenging the predictive value of DSAs as seen in solid organ transplantation and highlighting the need to explore alternative biomarkers for rejection in VCA.

Takeaways

Question: Does rituximab-based induction prolong rejection-free survival in facial vascularized composite allotransplantation, and are positive donor-specific antibody (DSA) levels associated with rejection?

Findings: The median rejection-free survival in our institutional cohort was 674.5 days (range, 279–1465 d), exceeding 1.5 years. No significant association was found between positive DSA levels and rejection. Some patients were DSA-negative during clinical rejection, whereas others were DSA-positive without evidence of overt rejection.

Meaning: Rituximab-based induction in vascularized composite allotransplantation achieved prolonged median rejection-free survival exceeding 1.5 years. Unlike in solid organ transplantation, DSA positivity was not associated with rejection, underscoring the need for alternative biomarkers.

INTRODUCTION

Vascularized composite allotransplantation (VCA) is a major advancement in plastic and reconstructive surgery. It offers functional and aesthetic restoration for patients with severe disfigurements that conventional techniques cannot adequately address. In VCA, multiple tissue types, including skin, muscle, bone, nerves, and vasculature, are transplanted as a single functional unit.1,2 Since the first successful hand transplant over 25 years ago, VCA has expanded to include the face, lower extremities, abdominal wall, genitourinary organs, trachea, and larynx.3,4

Acute rejection remains one of the greatest challenges of VCA, limiting long-term graft survival and function. Similar to solid organ transplantation (SOT), VCA recipients require lifelong immunosuppression to maintain allograft viability.5 However, rejection rates are historically high, with 85% of VCA patients experiencing 1 or more episodes within the first year.6–10 Currently, the gold standard for diagnosing rejection in VCA is skin biopsy with dermatohistopathologic evaluation and grading via the Banff 2007 Working Classification.11 Rejection can also be diagnosed through clinical signs such as erythema, edema, or lichenoid papules on the allograft. If left untreated, acute rejection can progress to chronic rejection and ultimately may lead to graft failure.6–9

Donor-specific antibodies (DSAs) are recipient-derived antibodies formed against human leukocyte antigens (HLAs), expressed on cells in the donor allograft. In SOT, DSAs are widely considered a key component of antibody-mediated rejection (AMR), and their monitoring has become routine in rejection surveillance.12–14 Some patients present with preexisting DSAs due to prior exposure to foreign HLAs through blood transfusions, previous transplants, or pregnancies, causing immune sensitization. The degree of sensitization is quantified using the calculated panel reactive antibody (cPRA) score, which estimates the proportion of potential donors with whom the recipient is likely incompatible. A higher cPRA is associated with an increased risk of AMR.2,15,16 De novo DSAs can also develop posttransplant, increasing the risk of rejection and graft loss.17,18 Despite the clear role of DSAs in SOT, their relevance in VCA remains poorly understood.

Induction therapy is administered during transplantation to reduce sensitization and early immune activation while simultaneously improving graft acceptance. Most VCA induction immunosuppression protocols are adapted from SOT and primarily focus on T-cell depletion, commonly using the polyclonal antibody antithymocyte globulin (ATG).5,19–21 However, B cell–targeted strategies remain underexplored. Skin containing VCA grafts contains donor-derived resident mature B cells, which are highly immunogenic through direct antigen presentation to recipient lymphocyte populations, which likely contributes to the high rates of early acute rejection episodes seen in facial transplantation.1,22 Rituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, is a mature B cell–depleting induction agent that has been shown to reduce the number of rejection episodes in highly sensitized SOT recipients.23–25 Although rituximab has shown promise in SOT, its impact on VCA has not yet been studied.

This is the first longitudinal study to evaluate rituximab-based induction therapy in VCA and serves as an extension to our previously published case report, which demonstrated initial clinical success with this B cell–depleting agent.26 We aim to characterize the time to first rejection and assess whether positive DSA levels correlate with rejection. These outcomes may inform future immunosuppressive strategies and improve rejection surveillance in VCA.

METHODS

A retrospective review was conducted on 4 patients who underwent facial transplantation and received rituximab-based induction at our institution between August 2015 and May 2023. In addition to facial transplantation, 1 patient also underwent concurrent bilateral hand transplantation, and another underwent whole-eye transplantation. The primary outcomes assessed were the number of rejection-free days posttransplant and DSA positivity during each rejection episode. Collected data included recipient demographics, virologic serostatus, HLA mismatches, cPRA scores, immunosuppressive regimen details, and duration of posttransplant follow-up. Pretransplant and de novo DSAs were measured using mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). The institutional review board approved the retrospective review of patient data (no. i19-00621).

Induction and Immunosuppression Regimen

A total dose of 6 mg/kg of intravenous ATG was administered for induction. The first dose was given intraoperatively and then in divided doses over the first postoperative week with a methylprednisolone taper (1000 mg). Rituximab (1000 mg) was either given on postoperative day (POD) 0 or 1. Maintenance immunosuppression started on POD 0 and consisted of triple therapy with tacrolimus (0.15 mg/kg), mycophenolate mofetil (2 g/d), and an oral prednisone taper (to 5 mg/d).

Rejection Surveillance and Immunosuppression Monitoring

Routine follow-up was performed several times weekly for the first month, at least weekly through the second month, then eventually reduced when clinically appropriate to every 3–6 months. Routine monitoring included clinical examination, allograft biopsy with histopathology, serial DSA and non-HLA antibody testing, measurement of calcineurin inhibitor levels, and assessment of circulating lymphocyte subsets. Biopsies were obtained from the neck or forearm regions of the allograft, either as part of routine monitoring or in response to clinical suspicion of rejection. DSA levels were assessed at each visit or during suspected rejection episodes. Patients were considered DSA-positive if a class I or II antigen exceeded 2000 MFI. Tacrolimus trough levels, defined as the lowest drug concentration in the bloodstream measured before the next administered dose, were closely monitored and adjusted to maintain target levels. Additionally, renal function was routinely assessed by blood urea nitrogen and serum creatinine.

Diagnosis and Management of Acute Rejection

Rejection was defined as clinical signs, subjective patient experience, and allograft skin biopsy results consistent with Banff grade greater than or equal to 2.11,27 Clinical indicators of rejection included erythema, edema, ulceration, pruritus, or tenderness of the allograft. To evaluate for potential AMR, C4d immunohistochemistry was performed, and DSA and non-HLA antibody levels were measured. A final diagnosis was agreed upon through the consensus of a multidisciplinary team of plastic and transplant surgeons, immunologists, and dermatopathologists.28 Therapeutic interventions for rejection included pulse steroid tapering as first-line therapy, with increased dosage of maintenance immunosuppression (including the addition of belatacept in patient 3), additional lymphocyte-depleting therapy, and/or plasmapheresis for episodes that did not respond adequately to pulse steroids. Following the resolution of rejection, all patients were reevaluated in clinic, and tacrolimus trough levels were monitored and adjusted as needed.

Statistical Analysis

All data processing and analyses were performed with R (RStudio version 4.2.3; R Core Team, 2023). A generalized linear mixed-effects model was used to evaluate the association between DSA positivity and rejection episodes over time. The model incorporated longitudinal data across all PODs, with rejection status included as the fixed variable. Interpatient variability was accounted for as a random effect to control for repeated measures and individual differences. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare median rejection-free survival between our cohort and compiled data from outside institutions, stratified by induction therapy. Statistical significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05.

RESULTS

All 4 patients were White men with a median age of 33 years (range 21–46 y). The mechanisms of injury included 3 burns and 1 ballistic injury. Patients had limited comorbidities, including depression, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension. All patients received prior blood transfusions and had low-level pretransplant DSAs (<2000 MFI) against class I and II antigens. Patients 1, 2, and 4 had low cPRA scores, whereas patient 3 was highly sensitized, with a score of 94%. The median duration of follow-up was 43.4 months (range 24.3–120.1 mo). Characteristics of First Rejection Episode With Treatment

Patients 1 and 2 were DSA-negative before their first rejection episode, but were DSA-positive at the time of rejection. Patient 3 was DSA-negative during the first rejection and remained negative for all subsequent episodes. Patient 4 experienced no rejection and remained DSA-negative throughout the postoperative period. Patient 1 was DSA-positive in 66.7% of rejection episodes, patient 2 was DSA-positive in 50%, and patient 3 remained DSA-negative during all rejections. All elevated DSA levels returned to under 2000 MFI following treatment. Notably, during routine surveillance, patients were occasionally DSA-positive with no clinical signs or symptoms of acute rejection. Patient 1 had 8 instances of DSA positivity without evidence of rejection, and patients 2 and 3 each had 1. A generalized linear mixed-effects model found no significant association between positive DSA levels and rejection episodes when controlling for PODs and interpatient variability (P = 0.693).

DISCUSSION

In VCA, acute rejection is a major concern, with most recipients experiencing their first rejection within the first year of transplantation.8,9,29,30 Studies have demonstrated a pattern where recurrent rejection episodes may contribute to the development of chronic rejection and allograft loss, suggesting a cumulative effect of early immune activation on graft integrity.31–33 Despite this, there is no consensus on an optimal induction regimen. A major barrier lies in the skin component of VCA, which presents a unique immunologic challenge due to its high concentration of antigen-presenting cells, dense vascular network, and constant environmental exposure. These features make the skin highly susceptible to both acute cellular rejection, mediated by T cells, and AMR, driven by B cells.1,22 T cell–mediated rejection is the predominant mechanism for acute rejection in VCA. However, B cells also play a critical role by serving as antigen-presenting cells that activate additional T cells and by differentiating into plasma cells that produce DSAs.13,34,35 Highly immunogenic donor-derived mature B cells, which are transplanted with skin-containing allografts, may play a key role in face VCA rejection, particularly in the early postoperative period when recipient T-cell populations reconstitute, typically 30 days after receiving T cell–depleting induction therapy at the time of transplant.36,37 Early immune control remains extremely important in VCA, where graft viability is tied to both functionality and aesthetics.

Rituximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CD20-positive B cells, has been safely incorporated into ATG-based induction in SOT and serves a key role in modulating humoral immunity.38–41 This drug has a favorable pharmacological profile: it is well tolerated, provides durable mature B-cell depletion for at least 6 months, and can easily be monitored.42 In highly sensitized SOT recipients, rituximab has been shown to reduce preexisting DSAs and de novo DSA production, likely decreasing AMR.23–25 Given the high prevalence of sensitization among VCA candidates, rituximab represents a promising addition to induction protocols, with the goal of mitigating AMR.43 Building on its successful use in SOT, we investigated the role of rituximab in VCA by evaluating its effectiveness in prolonging rejection-free survival and modulating DSA levels, which can have major implications on graft monitoring and rejection risk management.

Given the dual roles of T and B cells in mediating VCA rejection, expanding induction strategies to add B-cell depletion may, therefore, offer more comprehensive immunomodulation than T-cell targeting alone. In our cohort, which received both rituximab and ATG, the median rejection-free survival exceeded 500 days–substantially longer than the timelines typically reported in VCA.44 When compared with compiled rejection-free survival data from published studies of face transplantation recipients,45,46 our cohort demonstrated a significantly longer median rejection-free survival with rituximab plus ATG compared with ATG alone (22.3 versus 2.0 mo, P = 0.04) (Fig. 1). Our cohort’s induction strategy emphasizes the potential of targeting the cellular and humoral pathways to prolong rejection-free survival and promote graft viability.47

Although DSAs are a well-established biomarker for predicting AMR, graft injury, and failure in SOT, their role in VCA is uncertain.48 Prior studies have failed to demonstrate a clear correlation between DSAs, C4d deposition (a marker for AMR), and acute rejection in VCA.49 Our cohort contributes additional evidence for this observation, as some patients with clinical rejection were DSA-negative, whereas others were DSA-positive without evidence of overt rejection. Moreover, DSA positivity was not significantly associated with rejection, further challenging its use in VCA. This discordance raises concerns over the utility of DSAs as a reliable biomarker for rejection monitoring in rituximab-induced VCA recipients. The absence of DSAs in patients undergoing acute rejection points to the possibility of alternative, potentially T cell–dominant, or non-HLA–mediated mechanisms of graft injury.50 Our extended rejection-free survival supports the notion that early B-cell depletion confers immunosuppressive benefits, regardless of DSA status.

Our induction protocol with rituximab and ATG may alter the early posttransplant immunologic course of VCA, offering extended rejection-free survival compared with the historically high rate of rejection within the first year of transplant, particularly in the first 6 months.6–10 The discordant relationship between DSAs and acute rejection underscores the need to reevaluate current surveillance strategies in VCA, as monitoring paradigms derived from SOT may not translate to this population. Therefore, more specific noninvasive biomarkers for rejection need to be developed and validated in VCA and for patients who receive rituximab-based induction therapy. Emerging biomarkers that are currently being studied include donor-derived cell-free DNA, lymphocyte subsets, and antibodies targeting endothelial cell antigens or angiotensin II type 1 receptors.50,51

This study is not without limitations. Given the novelty of VCA, specifically facial transplantation, our conclusions are drawn from a small institutional cohort, which inherently limits generalizability. The inclusion of patients who underwent bilateral hand or whole-eye transplantation also introduces a degree of heterogeneity; however, it broadens the relevance of our findings to other VCA types. Mixed-effects modeling was performed to account for repeated measures within patients, but given the limited cohort size and associated low statistical power, these findings should be considered exploratory in nature. Additionally, our results show prolonged rejection-free survival, though the absence of a control group limits direct attribution to rituximab. Importantly, rejection in VCA is a multifactorial process, and although our study describes trends in DSAs, additional immunologic and nonimmunologic factors likely play a role in acute rejection. Nonetheless, this study provides important preliminary data on rituximab’s effectiveness in VCA, as we are the first to present longitudinal outcomes.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study demonstrates that rituximab-based induction in VCA is well tolerated and associated with extended rejection-free survival, with the median time to rejection exceeding one and a half years posttransplant. Of the 4 patients in our cohort, only 1 experienced rejection within the first posttransplant year, 2 remained rejection-free for nearly 1.5 years and 4 years, and the fourth has remained rejection-free for more than 2 years. Diverging from established patterns in SOT, there was no significant association between DSA positivity and acute rejection. This challenges the utility of DSAs as a reliable biomarker in rituximab-induced VCA recipients. Together, these findings may indicate fundamental differences in rejection mechanisms between VCA and SOT, stressing the need for accurate, noninvasive biomarkers tailored to VCA’s unique immunologic profile. Overall, our data support the benefit of incorporating B-cell depletion into ATG induction protocols and highlight its potential to improve graft stability.
