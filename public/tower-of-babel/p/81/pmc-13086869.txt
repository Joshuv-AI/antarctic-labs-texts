Assessing the micromechanical architecture of joint tissues with speckle rheological microscopy
Authors: Nichaluk Leartprapun, Fernando P S Guastaldi, Mark A Randolph, Robert W Redmond, Seemantini K Nadkarni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractJoint tissues rely on a finely tuned interplay between solid (elastic) and fluid (viscous) constituents to support their mechanical function. Degradation of these constituents due to disease or trauma disrupts the elastic and viscous susceptibility of joint tissues, directly impairing joint function. We present Speckle rHEologicAl micRoscopy (SHEAR) to investigate the viscoelastic behavior of intact joint tissues without making contact or otherwise manipulating the sample, offering a promising non-destructive imaging tool for assessing mechanical function in degenerative joint disease. SHEAR demonstrated strong correlation to conventional rheometer in healthy swine knee joint tissues. In swine cartilage with focal lesions and surgically excised cartilage specimens from human patients undergoing knee replacement, SHEAR revealed transformation in both the elastic and viscous landscape of the tissue. SHEAR has the potential to provide a powerful tool for biomechanical analysis of joint structures and pave new paths for improved diagnosis and therapy of degenerative joint disease.Subject terms: Imaging and sensing, Cartilage, Biophotonics, Characterization and analytical techniques, Translational research

Introduction

Degenerative joint disease such as osteoarthritis (OA) is characterized by the breakdown of articular cartilage that causes pain and limits joint mobility. OA affects more than 500 million people and is the leading cause of disability worldwide1,2. There is no effective, disease-modifying cure to date3–6. A multitude of causes can trigger the breakdown of cartilage and progression of arthritis, including normal wear and tear of joint tissues, injuries from intensive physical activities, blunt traumas, as well as inflammatory disorders7–9. At the organ level, joint tissues perform important mechanical functions of load bearing, load distribution, and shock absorption during shearing movements of articulating joints. Degenerative transformations alter the mechanical properties of the tissues and directly compromise their ability to support these mechanical tasks10–12. At the cellular level, the altered mechanical microarchitecture of the extracellular matrix (ECM) further drives degenerative cellular processes via mechanotransduction, amplifying aberrant transformations11,13. At the onset of OA, early catabolic processes that disrupt the molecular constituents of the cartilage matrix can alter its mechanical properties well before any macroscopic or symptomatic signs can be observed10,14. Thus, investigating the micromechanical properties of joint tissues associated with degenerative transformations is critical for understanding the disease processes, holds important implications for tissue engineering and regenerative medicine approaches for tissue repair, and supports new avenues for clinical diagnosis and assessment of degenerative joint disease in patients.

Joint tissues are composed of specialized ECM that possesses a combination of elastic stiffness (load bearing) and viscous dissipative (shock absorption) mechanical behaviors via a finely tuned interplay between the solid and fluid tissue constituents11,12,15–17. Reduction in elastic modulus associated with the degradation of solid tissue constituents has long been investigated in orthopedic diseases. In cartilage, major matrix components, including collagen and aggrecans, consisting of proteoglycans and sulphated glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) chains, are disrupted and depleted in OA. However, these solid components only account for 20–30% of cartilage composition; the remaining majority is taken up by intracellular and interstitial water. An increase in water content has been observed in both OA cartilage and degenerative meniscus18,19. Importantly, the unique mechanical behaviors of cartilage are not derived simply from the presence of individual matrix constituents, but rather from the interactions of solid and fluid components within the tissue composite. Proteoglycans and GAGs retain interstitial water via charged interactions, producing varying degrees of hydrostatic pressure and fluid flow that modulate load resistance and transmission11,17. Thus, loss of proteoglycans and GAGs in OA affects not only the composition of the aggrecan structures, but also the fluid behavior and its contribution to load bearing and shock absorption. Therefore, the mechanical integrity of joint tissues cannot be adequately assessed by a single descriptor such as “stiffness” or modulus of elasticity, but additionally requires the comprehensive characterization of viscous traits.

The combination of solid-like and fluid-like mechanical traits can be described by the complex (viscoelastic) shear modulus, G*(ω) = G′(ω) + iG″(ω), composed of the elastic (energy storage), G′, and viscous (energy dissipation), G″, moduli that vary as a function of angular frequency, ω, at which the tissue is probed. Viscoelastic properties of joint tissues have been measured with conventional mechanical testing methods (compression testing, indentation, shear rheometry)20,21 as well as emerging optical approaches such as confocal strain mapping12,22,23. However, these approaches rely on subjecting the sample to precisely controlled quasi-static or dynamic mechanical deformation, which often involves complex testing instruments, invasive preparation to achieve precise sample geometry, and destruction of sample fidelity during testing, especially in the case of soft tissue specimens or fragile tissue-engineered constructs. These limitations restrict the utility of existing approaches in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, where non-destructive techniques applicable to a broad range of sample shapes and sizes are essential, and further hinder potential clinical translation for use as diagnostic tools in living patients.

Speckle rHEologicAl micRoscopy (SHEAR) is an emerging all-optical platform that enables high-resolution spatial mapping of viscoelastic properties in gross unprocessed tissue specimens in an entirely noncontact and non-destructive manner24–28. Unlike existing approaches, SHEAR probes the local viscoelastic properties of the tissue microenvironment in its natural state, without applying any mechanical deformation or otherwise manipulating the sample to make the measurement. We have previously validated and applied SHEAR to map the magnitude of the viscoelastic or shear modulus, |G*|, in biopolymer constructs24, biofluids29, and whole tissues, including breast tumors27, blood clots25,30, and atherosclerotic plaques26. Here, we demonstrate the capability of SHEAR to render 3D (2D+frequency) micromechanical maps of distinct elastic and viscous moduli and their dynamic behaviors as a function of frequency. The frequency-dependent SHEAR viscoelastic maps uncover the distinct contributions of solid-like elastic stiffness and fluid-like viscous dissipative behavior—critical to the load-bearing and shock-absorbing functions of joint tissues—in ways not achievable with a typical measurement of elasticity alone. We present the implementation of SHEAR in musculoskeletal tissues, with a focus on mapping the micromechanical transformation of joint tissues associated with degenerative disease and tissue repair. We describe the working principle of SHEAR for mapping frequency-dependent viscous and elastic behaviors in whole intact tissue at high spatial resolution, followed by a validation in healthy joint tissues from swine knees, and conclude with a demonstration in several scenarios of joint tissue lesions and degenerative disease in both swine and human knee joint specimens.

Results

Principle of SHEAR for noncontact mapping of tissue viscoelasticity

SHEAR exploits the natural thermally driven Brownian displacements of endogenous tissue constituents to derive the local viscoelastic properties of intact tissue composites using previously described methodologies24–28. These minute displacements are optically detected with exquisite sensitivity by illuminating the tissue with coherent light and capturing the granular pattern—speckle—that returns from the sample. Speckle arises from the interference of light that undergoes multiple scattering events with the internal tissue ultrastructure, the displacements of which alter the resulting interference pattern. Thus, speckle intensities dynamically fluctuate in time due to the Brownian displacements of the tissue ultrastructure, which are in turn governed by the viscoelastic susceptibility of the tissue microenvironment. For instance, in knee joints, microscopic scattering structures within a dense calcified cartilage matrix would undergo a lesser extent of Brownian displacements compared to those surrounded by interstitial fluid, and therefore, would produce a relatively more static speckle pattern. These fluctuating speckle patterns are captured with a simple upright microscope, consisting of a linearly polarized monochromatic light source that illuminates the sample and a high-speed CMOS camera that collects the orthogonally polarized backscattered light (Fig. 1a).

The raw speckle time series are processed in a (spatial) pixelwise manner to reconstruct a 2D depth-integrated map of viscoelastic properties (Fig. 1b). A detailed description of the SHEAR reconstruction procedure is provided in Methods. Briefly, speckle fluctuation is quantified by the speckle intensity autocorrelation function (Step 1), which provides a measure of the timescale-dependent mean square displacement (MSD) of the tissue ultrastructure via the diffusing wave spectroscopy formulation29,31 (Step 2). Then, the MSD and its local power scaling exponent, α (Step 3), enable the quantification of the frequency-dependent complex (viscoelastic) shear modulus via the Generalized Stokes-Einstein Relation (GSER)32–35 (Step 4). Specifically, the MSD is inversely proportional to the magnitude, |G*|, of the complex modulus, while α defines the phase angle which decomposes the overall modulus magnitude into its real (elastic modulus), G′, and imaginary (viscous modulus), G″, components (see illustration of the complex plane in Fig. 1b).

The frequency-dependent reconstruction of the full complex (viscoelastic) shear modulus provides additional insights on the micromechanical integrity of joint tissues that are not accessible with a single measurement of |G*| demonstrated in our prior work. Physically, while the modulus magnitude |G*| indicates the overall firmness (i.e., resistance to deformation) of the tissue, the parameter α provides a separate measure of its capacity for viscous energy dissipation (α = 0: purely elastic solid, all energy stored; α = 1: purely viscous fluid, all energy dissipates; α = 0.5: equal contributions from elastic energy storage and viscous energy dissipation). Both parameters are modulated as a function of ω, reflecting the dynamic viscoelastic behavior of the tissue microenvironment captured by the 3D frequency-dependent SHEAR maps. In the following sections, we implement SHEAR to map the viscoelastic microarchitecture of intra-articular joint tissue specimens from both healthy and compromised knee joints, demonstrating the ability to investigate the micromechanical transformation of joint tissues associated with degenerative disease and injury response. With entirely passive and noncontact operation, the simplicity of SHEAR implementation makes it compatible for future clinical translation as an imaging tool in orthopedic surgery and arthroscopy settings, as well as for applications in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

Mapping viscoelastic properties of soft tissues from healthy knee joints

We demonstrate SHEAR viscoelastic mapping in soft tissues of the healthy knee joint. Biopsy cores of cartilage, meniscus, tendon, and ligament were harvested from healthy swine knees and imaged fresh under the SHEAR microscope without additional sample manipulation. To capture the complex viscoelastic behaviors of joint tissues, we generate three types of visualization from the reconstructed G*(ω) (Fig. 2). Firstly, the |G*| map reveals the variation in the overall mechanical firmness, related to the combined contributions of both elastic and viscous behaviors, within the tissue. Secondly, the α map reveals microstructural features with distinct relative viscous (dissipative) contribution, providing an index of tissue fluidization and the loss ratio, G″/G′. Lastly, the composite map combines elasticity, viscosity, and the α dissipative index to highlight the contrast in different viscoelastic traits within the tissue in one visual representation. The composite map is generated by assigning the normalized values of G′, G″, and α to the blue, green, and red channels of the RGB color space, respectively. Thus, a relatively elastic tissue composite appears in a strong blue hue, whereas fluidized interstitial spaces are distinguishable in bright red.

In all samples, the α maps reveal underlying microarchitectural features that are distinct from those observed on the |G*| maps, suggesting that the ability to investigate the viscous dissipative behaviors separately from the overall stiffness can provide unique insights in joint tissues (Fig. 2). The cartilage matrix exhibits a rather uniform |G*| map, consistent with the smooth appearance of the ECM in histology slides. However, the α map displays heterogeneities with beehive-like structures (boxed areas), potentially reflecting morphological variation around the lacunae that house chondrocytes. This behavior is visible on the composite map as beehive patterns of higher-α orange-red hued structures on top of a relatively more elastic blue-hued background. The maps also show two cross-sections of blood vessels (marked with *) with distinctly low |G*| and high α, appearing as bright red circles on the composite map, indicative of a predominantly viscous fluid environment. In the meniscus, a clear contrast in |G*| is observed between the left and right side, coinciding with different collagen architecture and GAG content shown on the picrosirius red (PSR) and safranin O (Saf O) slides, respectively. In addition, the α map reveals micromechanical heterogeneities throughout, where locations of elevated α (marked with arrows) appear to coincide with regions of disrupted collagen matrix with less intense PSR staining. The tendon specimen exhibits sharp contrasts in both |G*| and α that coincide with the interface between the fibrous tendon matrix and adjacent adipose tissue (triangle and circle symbols, respectively). The composite map shows a relatively more elastic tendon matrix in blue hue and the interface to adipose tissue in bright red, likely reflecting a more fluidized interstitial space. The ligament matrix exhibits mostly uniform |G*| but displays distinct horizontal and diagonal striping patterns on the α map, which appear to coincide with the channels of cells between the striated fibrous bands clearly seen in PSR sections (marked with arrows).

Following mapping under the SHEAR microscope, the tissue samples were measured with a conventional oscillatory shear rheometer (TA Instruments, AR-G2) for comparison. Bovine synovial fluid was also measured to demonstrate the applicability of SHEAR for mechanical characterization across a broad range of specimens, from viscous fluid to stiff cartilage tissue. The values of |G*|, α, as well as G′ and G″ from the SHEAR maps of each tissue type are extracted at ω = 14π rad/s. Compared to SHEAR maps at ω = 6π rad/s in Fig. 2, there are distinct microstructures there are more or less apparent at one frequency over the other due to the frequency-dependent viscoelastic behaviors of the tissue (Supplementary Fig. 1). The frequency of 14π rad/s was chosen here because it roughly corresponds to the elastic plateau of the frequency-dependent G*(ω) spectra (Supplementary Fig. 2), which is where microrheological measurement by SHEAR is expected to bear the greatest resemblance to bulk measurement with a rheometer28,36–38. Among the four tissue types, cartilage exhibits the highest |G*| while meniscus exhibits the lowest α (Fig. 3a, b). Meanwhile, synovial fluid exhibits a low |G*| of only a few Pascals, with G″ being almost an order of magnitude greater than G′ (Fig. 3c), corresponding to a high α approaching that of a purely viscous fluid (i.e., α = 1). It is notable that cartilage exhibits the highest |G*| despite having the weakest PSR collagen signature, reinforcing that proteoglycans and GAGs (Saf O) are major contributors to the stiffness of hyaline cartilage11,17. The higher water content in cartilage compared to the meniscus likely contributes to the higher α39. The average values of |G* | , G′, and G″ obtained from the SHEAR maps of each sample demonstrate strong correlation to conventional measurements with a rheometer (Fig. 3d, f, g). Moduli values in the same range have previously been reported using atomic force microscopy (AFM)-based indentation10,40,41, confocal strain mapping22, shear rheometry42,43, and compression testing21,44 in cartilage and meniscus. Compared to the moduli values, α correlates less strongly to rheometry (Fig. 3e), likely due to the heterogeneities observed on the α maps in Fig. 2 that could not be captured in bulk testing, as well as the distinct flow-dependent viscous dissipative behaviors when probed at the micro versus macroscale. In the literature, macroscale testing based on bulk shearing or compression reported a relatively low and constant α of ~0.122,43,45, consistent with our rheometer measurements. In contrast, microscale testing with AFM reported a frequency-dependent α behavior spanning up to ~0.3–0.5 between 10–100 Hz10,40,41, in agreement with our SHEAR measurements.

These results serve to validate SHEAR as a viable tool for non-destructive mapping of viscoelastic mechanical properties in musculoskeletal tissues in a passive, noncontact manner without any invasive sample preparation or manipulation. The distinct information provided by the α dissipative index demonstrates the importance of investigating the full complex (viscoelastic) modulus in order to capture the mechanical contributions of both solid and fluid constituents of the specialized joint tissue ECM, further elucidated in the results in the following sections.

Mapping viscoelastic properties of cartilage with focal lesions

The ability to spatially resolve micromechanical heterogeneities within a tissue specimen makes SHEAR potentially suitable for biomechanical profiling of micro-lesions and injury response in joint tissues. We explore this application using a swine model of focal cartilage lesion repair, where laser-ablated micro-lesions with diameters of roughly 300–500 μm were inflicted to the articular cartilage of the femoral condyle in vivo in living swine46 before the animals were allowed to naturally heal. Osteochondral explants from the femoral condyle were harvested 4 months post-injury for viscoelastic mapping under the SHEAR microscope, followed by histological analysis (Fig. 4). The passive, noncontact measurement by SHEAR allows the cartilage lesions on the osteochondral unit (gross photo in Fig. 4b) to be mapped without any invasive procedure to isolate the cartilage tissue that may have damaged the specimen.

Three lesions are visible on the SHEAR maps acquired from the top-down view onto the articular surface (Fig. 4a). Notably, the lesions can be most clearly delineated from the variation in α, where a separation into the outer shell (~100-μm thick) with a lower α and the inner core with a higher α is observed in each lesion. Meanwhile, |G*| also exhibits a distinct pattern of stiffened hotspots (higher |G*| than the healthy cartilage in Fig. 3a) in the outer shell region of each lesion. Histology shows lower GAG (Saf O) but more fibrotic collagen morphology in the lesion, where the left-most lesion with the most prominent fibrous collagen structures (PSR) displays the highest |G*| (Fig. 4b). In addition, a magnified view of the right-most lesion reveals a rough outline with a more intense PSR collagen signature that coincides with the outer shell region on the SHEAR maps (Fig. 4c), which may potentially explain the elevated |G*| and reduced α in this region. Quantification of the average |G*| and α in the inner core versus outer shell of the lesions confirms that the former exhibits the highest α, whereas the latter exhibits the highest |G*| (Supplementary Fig. 3). The fluidized high-α signature at the inner core may stem from incomplete deposition of the solid cartilage matrix at the center of the lesion.

These results demonstrate that SHEAR can detect local transformation in the viscoelastic properties of cartilage due to focal micro-lesions or injuries that are not accessible by conventional bulk mechanical testing methods. Furthermore, SHEAR maps reveal micromechanical heterogeneities that are otherwise not apparent from histological analysis. This capability may be particularly relevant for early detection of micro-lesions at the onset of OA, where stiffness change in the cartilage matrix has been found to precede detectable histopathological and macroscopic changes10,14.

Mapping viscoelastic properties of osteoarthritic and degenerative cartilage

To explore the promise of SHEAR for clinical diagnostic applications to assess degenerative joint disease, we conducted viscoelastic mapping of degenerative knee cartilage specimens from clinical cases of patients undergoing knee replacement. Discarded osteochondral debris from knee replacement surgery were obtained from the MGH Surgical Pathology Suite in accordance with a protocol approved by the MGH Institutional Review Board. The first two cases are of knee replacement for OA (Figs. 5 and 6) whereas the third is of knee replacement surgery for unspecified cause (Fig. 7). These specimens represent advanced end-stage disease where knee replacement is the only viable treatment option. Specimens were collected at the time of surgery and mapped under the SHEAR microscope within 24 h.

In the first case, the osteochondral segment had been vertically cut during the surgery, and the debris was mapped side-on to show the cross-sectional view through the depth of the cartilage. The composite map reveals a clear division into the softer fluidized superficial zone (Zone I) in red and the firmer deep zone (Zone II) in cyan (Fig. 5a). The bright red signature in Zone I indicates an overall lower |G*| that predominantly composes of the viscous contribution, resulting in the highest viscous dissipative index (α, red channel). Meanwhile, the bright greenish-blue shade in Zone II indicates an overall higher |G*| that composes of comparable contributions from the elastic (G′, blue channel) and viscous (G″, green channel) components. The viscoelastic landscape is well corroborated by histological analysis (Fig. 5b), where Zone I shows clear evidence of osteoarthritic degradation, including reduced cellularity (H&E), depleted GAGs (Saf O), and abnormal collagen architecture (PSR)10–12,18 compared to Zone II. Notably, while collagen is still present (albeit with abnormal architecture) in Zone I, it is almost completely devoid of GAGs. The loss of GAG can be associated with a greater proportion of free water (versus water hydrostatically bound to GAGs), likely resulting in lower hydrostatic pressure (lower |G*|) and greater viscous fluid flow (higher α) within the cartilage matrix. Our observations are consistent with prior findings by confocal strain mapping in trypsin-treated (i.e., enzymatic depletion of GAG) cartilage23. However, our results also show that the altered viscoelastic behaviors are not merely static properties but evolve as a function of ω-frequency (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Movie 1). Notably, the GAG-rich Zone II maintains a relatively more elastic behavior with roughly constant α for most of the frequency range, whereas Zone I, with disrupted collagen and depleted GAG, exhibits increasing viscous dissipation with frequency. The plots of |G*| and α as a function of ω confirm that whereas |G*| follows a similar increasing trend in both Zones I and II, the α behavior is distinct, with Zone I rising to a peak at ω ~ 102 rad/s while Zone II remains relatively constant before decreasing at the same frequency (Fig. 5d).

In the second case, SHEAR maps were obtained from the top-down view of the osteochondral unit with intact but delaminated cartilage surface. The composite map reveals three different regions with distinct viscoelastic signatures (Fig. 6a). The top part appears mostly in dark blue hue (ROI I in Fig. 6c), indicating a lower |G*| with a higher contribution of G′ than G″, corresponding to a lower α. Histological analysis shows no obvious fibrous collagen structures (PSR) and reduced GAGs (Saf O) (Fig. 6b). The middle part stands out as a bright greenish-blue stripe, indicating similar contribution of both G′ than G″ to the relatively higher |G*|, coinciding with the strongest GAG staining (Saf O) and defined fibrotic collagen structures (PSR) (ROI II in Fig. 6c). The combination of GAGs and fibrous collagen likely contributes to the high |G*| and the fibrous heterogeneities observed on the composite map. The bottom part exhibits a bright red signature in the background, indicating the highest α and a relatively lower |G*|, with heterogeneous fibrous structures in blue-green hues. This region exhibits the weakest GAG staining (Saf O) and fibrillated collagen architecture (PSR) that appears disrupted compared to ROI II (ROI III in Fig. 6c). The viscoelastic behaviors in all three ROIs are modulated as a function of ω, with |G*| increasing and α decreasing with different slopes (Fig. 6d). The SHEAR maps show a particularly distinctive evolution of the α behavior with frequency (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Movie 2). The highly fluidized bright red signature in the bottom part gradually recedes to reveal an increasing contribution of the elastic matrix, with fibrous mesh-like structures becoming apparent as the frequency increases. Meanwhile, the initially elastic bright blue signature in the top part gradually exhibits a relatively more viscous dissipative behavior, eventually displaying a similar red-hued signature as the bottom part at the highest frequency.

In the third case, SHEAR maps were obtained from an intact section of a severely degenerative cartilage debris whose macroscopic appearance no longer resembled that of a healthy hyaline cartilage. Histological analysis shows heterogeneous tissue morphology with depleted cellularity (H&E), lack of GAGs (Saf O), and abnormal collagen architecture (PSR) (Fig. 7b). The composite map displays a highly heterogeneous viscoelastic landscape, spanning relatively more elastic to highly dissipative fluidized regions (Fig. 7a). For instance, the bright blue-cyan hues at the top of the image indicate a higher |G*| with greater contribution from G′ than G″, corresponding to a lower α. This region appears to have a denser fibrous matrix, especially in the top-right corner (ROI I in Fig. 7b). Surrounded by this relatively more elastic fibrous matrix, the composite map reveals a distinct red-hued fluidized tortuous structure (outlined with dashed line) that coincide with prominent gaps in the porous matrix (ROI II in Fig. 7b). Meanwhile, the bright red pockets (outlined with arrows) at the bottom indicate the highest α that approaches 1 and a lower |G*| that is predominantly composed of the contribution from G″. These pockets coincide with multiple voids in the matrix, lacking in both collagen and GAG signatures (ROI III in Fig. 7b). The evolution of SHEAR maps as a function of frequency shows that the bottom half is dominated by a bright red signature at low ω. This viscosity-dominant signature gradually gives way to the relatively more elastic behavior of the fibrous matrix at higher ω, unveiling the mesh-like micromechanical heterogeneities similar to those observed in the bottom region of Fig. 6 (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Movie 3). The distinct frequency-dependent viscoelastic behaviors in the three ROIs are clearly seen in the plots of |G*| and α as a function of ω (Fig. 7d). ROI III distinctly exhibits fluid-like behavior with α ~ 1 at ω < 20 rad/s, before α decreases as |G*| increases at higher frequencies. ROI I and II exhibit similar trends in both |G*| and α as a function of ω, but with the former being overall stiffer and more elastic.

These results show that SHEAR is sensitive to micromechanical transformation of the cartilage tissue associated with degenerative disease in humans. In all three cases, modulation in both the elastic and viscous properties of the tissue is observed with respect to varying extents of degenerative transformation, as corroborated by histological analysis. Notably, a decrease in |G*| and an increase in α seem to be associated with the severity of osteoarthritic degeneration, particularly GAG content. Furthermore, both |G*| and α vary dynamically as a function of frequency, with different collagen and GAG signatures exhibiting distinct frequency-dependent behaviors. As a result, distinct micromechanical features are often uncovered at different frequencies in the 3D frequency-dependent SHEAR maps. These results highlight the importance of inspecting the α signature separately from shear modulus magnitude in cartilage (and potentially other similar GAG-rich tissues), as well as show that investigating the frequency-dependent behaviors of both properties can provide unique insights not accessible by only looking at a ‘snapshot’ at a single frequency.

Discussion

Joints serve mechanical functions that rely largely on the intricate micromechanical traits of their constituent soft tissues. Investigating how the micromechanical architecture of joint tissues is disrupted in degenerative joint disease is therefore imperative for understanding disease mechanisms and developing new therapies. In this work, we present SHEAR as a non-destructive imaging tool that provides micromechanical contrast, via spatial mapping of the frequency-dependent complex (viscoelastic) shear modulus, for revealing the viscoelastic landscape of joint tissues with high spatial resolution. The advantage of SHEAR compared to existing mechanical characterization approaches (e.g., compression testing21, rheometry42,43, atomic force microscopy10, confocal strain mapping22,23) is the ability to map spatially varying viscoelastic properties within gross unprocessed tissue specimens noninvasively, in an entirely passive and noncontact manner without any sample preparation or manipulation. The major advance demonstrated in this study is the high-resolution mapping of distinct elastic and viscous traits that contribute to the emergent complex micromechanical architecture of joint tissues. Previously, we have validated (against conventional testing with a rheometer and atomic force microscopy-based indentation) and applied SHEAR mapping of the viscoelastic modulus magnitude, |G*|, to investigate the mechanical properties of tissue-mimicking phantoms24, biofluids29, and breast tumors27. Here, we expand the capabilities of SHEAR to map all components of the complex (viscoelastic) shear modulus, including the magnitude |G*|, the elasticity G′, the viscosity G″, and the α dissipative index, in musculoskeletal tissues. Furthermore, we also introduce an innovative way to integrate multiple viscoelastic signatures into a single composite map, revealing the various micromechanical contrasts that are present in the viscoelastic landscape of the tissue. These expanded capabilities provide unique opportunities to investigate various micromechanical factors relevant to the integrity of joint tissues that are otherwise not accessible in our prior implementations of SHEAR24–28 or by conventional mechanical testing techniques20,21.

SHEAR is sensitive to microarchitectural variations in the elastic and viscous mechanical properties in healthy knee joint tissues, focal cartilage lesions, as well as degenerative disease in human patients. Notably, the parameter α provides unique insights on the viscous dissipative behavior of the tissue independent from the overall stiffness of the samples. In all samples investigated in this work, the α maps display distinct structures or contrasts that are not observed on the |G*| maps. This is especially apparent in the healthy tissues where both the histology slides and the |G*| map display a relatively uniform matrix (Fig. 2), as well as in the healing of focal cartilage lesions where completely distinct patterns are revealed by the α and |G*| maps (Fig. 4). The frequency-dependent reconstruction of the SHEAR maps as a function of ω-frequency provides an additional dimension of dynamic contrast to uniquely characterize the complex mechanical behaviors of joint tissues. As joint tissues function in a dynamic environment, these distinct frequencies are associated with various mechanical stimuli that occur at both the cellular and organ levels within joint tissues during movements, from normal ambulation to impact loading10–12. Our results in OA and degenerative cartilage show that different micromechanical features are prominent on the SHEAR maps at different frequencies (Supplementary Movie 1–3), and regions with distinct histological characteristics exhibit different frequency-dependent behaviors, especially in the α parameter (Figs. 5d, 6d and 7d). Depending on the timescale at which the SHEAR maps are reconstructed, the features revealed by α may be associated with various microstructural dynamics of small cellular and ECM structures within the hydrated composite tissue47,48, and thus, may provide an additional source of contrast to distinguish the microarchitectural transformations of joint tissues due to injury and degenerative processes.

The high-resolution mapping capability of SHEAR (resolving features smaller than 50 μm) allows the micromechanical architecture observed on the SHEAR maps to be corroborated by histological analysis. In most cases, the presence of fibrous collagen matrix tends to be associated with a higher |G*| (e.g., in Fig. 4, the cartilage lesion with the most prominent fibrous collagen structures filling the defect exhibits the highest |G*|). The association of fibrous collagen matrix with higher |G*| was also previously observed in breast tumor specimens27. However, by separately extracting the α parameter, our results show that the disruption of the collagen matrix tends to also be associated with an increase in viscous dissipation. For instance, the inner core of the cartilage lesions (Fig. 4) and the bottom regions of the degenerative cartilage (Fig. 7) with sparse, disrupted collagen structures exhibit not only a lower |G*| but also a higher α. Furthermore, in GAG-rich tissues such as cartilage, the presence of GAGs—with its water binding capacity—appears to be prominently associated with the variations in both |G*| and α. Notably, the healthy cartilage sample exhibits higher |G*| than the other tissue types despite having the least prominent collagen structure, likely because the abundance of GAGs provides the hydrostatic pressure as a major load-bearing mechanism in hyaline cartilage11,17. Meanwhile, the depletion of GAGs in the OA cartilage specimens coincides with reduced |G*| and increased α (Figs. 5 and 6). Our results suggest that the loss of GAG in OA not only softens the cartilage as previously reported,10,12,49,50 but also enhances its viscous energy dissipation, possibly associated with the greater extent of frictional flow of water through the cartilage matrix (i.e., increased hydraulic permeability) due to the depletion of aggrecan structures that can retain water15. These preliminary observations motivate further investigation of |G*| and α metrics as potential mechano-biomarkers of OA progression in future studies.

As a research tool, SHEAR can enable non-destructive biomechanical analysis in a wide range of samples, from fresh tissue specimens to biomimetic scaffolds and tissue-engineered constructs, to track disease progression and regenerative processes. For instance, the experimental results presented in Fig. 4 can be extended to track the injury response over time and correlate the observed micromechanical transformation with other histopathological characteristics, biochemical content, and inflammatory markers. Similarly, SHEAR can be implemented to track micromechanical transformation associated with disease progression in animal models of arthritis51, extending our preliminary findings at the advanced stages of disease in Figs. 5 and 6 to the full course of disease progression. These studies will allow a correlative analysis of SHEAR signatures with histopathological/clinical measures of OA severity and help establish the clinical relevance of mechanical metrics derived from SHEAR. In addition to disease progression, SHEAR can also provide biomechanical outcome measures to evaluate the progress and quality of tissue repair for application to tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. An interesting avenue is to combine micromechanical analysis via SHEAR with similarly non-destructive biochemical analysis via optical approaches such as time-resolved fluorescence techniques49,52 and Raman spectroscopy53,54 in these settings. For instance, the association of GAG loss in cartilage with its micromechanical architecture can potentially be investigated non-destructively at a high-resolution by correlating fluorescence lifetime images52 and the SHEAR viscoelastic maps. The ability to conduct measurements without any invasive sample preparation or manipulation offers a major practical advantage of preserving the fidelity of the sample for a suite of other analyses (e.g., histological or biochemical analysis) on the same specimen. In addition, a fundamental uniqueness of SHEAR compared to other available tools20,21 is that it directly probes the nano- to microscale dynamics of structures that compose the tissue (e.g., ECM proteins, macromolecular structures, cell membranes, organelles, etc.) to infer the local viscoelastic susceptibility of the microenvironment. Thus, SHEAR provides a window into the mechanical cues that are felt by cells and govern their mechanotransduction. This is particularly relevant to understanding the catabolic cellular processes during the onset and progression of arthritis11,13, as well as the factors that regulate tissue repair processes, such as cell recruitment and matrix deposition in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine3. Furthermore, the SHEAR measurement itself does not rely on any mechanical loading, thus, permitting analysis of tissues in various states of mechanical stimuli, both loaded and unloaded. Beyond the analysis of ex vivo specimens presented here, the SHEAR microscope can be modified to enable in situ and in vivo measurements in the future.

The simplicity of SHEAR lends itself to future clinical translation as a diagnostic tool in the surgical or arthroscopic settings. Arthritis remains a major healthcare burden without an effective cure that orthopedic surgeons can definitively rely on3–6. Clinical assessment of joint tissue integrity is critical for diagnostic grading and treatment guidance, as well as for evaluating the status of tissue repair following interventions55,56. However, current diagnostic tools only provide structural information (e.g., joint space narrowing) and gross surface appearance of intra-articular tissues viewed through an arthroscope56–60, which are not only insensitive to the early stages of degenerative changes (when interventions are more likely to be effective), but also inadequate for evaluating the underlying functional (mechanical) integrity of the tissue. Existing mechanical testing approaches that require precise sample manipulation are not practical for in vivo clinical use in living patients. SHEAR has the potential to fill this critical gap through future development into a needle-sized rigid scope for arthroscopic assessment. Notably, a point-of-care device for comprehensive blood coagulation analysis25,61,62 and intravascular catheters for atherosclerotic plaque profiling26,63,64 have already been developed based on complementary laser speckle rheology technology. The results in Figs. 4–6 show that abnormal viscoelastic signatures due to isolated micro-lesions and degenerative transformation can be visualized in exquisite detail by SHEAR. These results motivate further studies to investigate the clinical utility of noncontact viscoelastic mapping by SHEAR, including the development of new mechano-biomarkers for early detection, prognostication, and as surrogate outcome measures for treatment effectiveness.

There are some limitations to the SHEAR approach as presented in this work. Firstly, SHEAR provides a depth-integrated measurement from all tissue structures that interact with the illumination light. Thus, SHEAR can detect micromechanical heterogeneities on the surface as well as those located deeper within the tissue, but it is not able to resolve variation in the viscoelastic properties as a function of depth. Nevertheless, the viscoelastic maps presented here show that SHEAR can uncover microstructural details that are corroborated by histology. Alternatively, depth information may be accessed by mapping the cross-sectional cut of the sample (for ex vivo specimens, such as in Fig. 4), or by conducting the analysis as a function of lateral distance from the illumination center combined with an inverse problem approach65. Secondly, to extract MSD from speckle intensity autocorrelation data and to further derive G*, information on tissue optical properties and scattering particle radius is required. We have assumed uniform optical properties and scattering particle radii for each tissue type in the reconstruction of G*(ω) (Supplementary Table 1). Previous study in highly heterogeneous breast tumor specimens suggests that a variability of up to 35% in |G*| may be incurred due to particle size variation. A lesser extent of morphological heterogeneity in scatterer sizes is expected within each joint tissue structure. Nevertheless, spatially varying optical properties and scattering particle size can be estimated by incorporating existing optical approaches27,66,67. We have recently demonstrated a speckle-based approach that can be implemented in conjunction with SHEAR to map variations in scattering particle radius across whole tissue samples to further improve the accuracy of |G*|68. Thirdly, there are a few considerations for the future implementation of SHEAR in vivo or as a clinical tool. Physiological factors such as motions and activity of live cells could confound SHEAR reconstruction of G*(ω), which assumes that the observed speckle fluctuation is driven under thermal equilibrium. However, the presence of any actively-driven, non-Brownian dynamics (e.g., cellular activity, bulk motions) dominating the measurement can be identified by an α value that exceeds 128,33,69. Furthermore, the impact of cellular activities69–71 or artifacts from physiological motions and hand tremors72,73 are only perceptible over a limited lower range of frequencies. Thus, the frequency-dependent measurement by SHEAR allows for selective reconstruction of G*(ω) at the optimal ω frequencies where these confounding factors are negligible.

Through exploration across diverse joint tissue types, our results show that SHEAR can detect micromechanical variations in both the elastic stiffness and the viscous dissipative behavior of tissues. The simplicity of SHEAR makes it applicable to a wide range of applications, including in vivo implementation in preclinical and clinical studies in the future. With these capabilities, SHEAR has the potential to provide a powerful tool for biomechanical analysis of joint structures in both basic research and clinical translational studies. Overall, this study shows that SHEAR may likely offer a powerful platform for advancing both basic research and translational studies to assess joint health and guide treatment strategies.

Methods

This research complies with all relevant ethical regulations in accordance with protocols approved by the MGH Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and the MGH Institutional Review Board.

Specimens

Cartilage, meniscus, tendon, and ligament tissues from healthy knee joints (Figs. 2 and 3) were obtained from a young Yorkshire swine (female, 33 kg, 4 months old, CBSET). Tissues were harvested with a biopsy punch (diameter 8 mm) after the knee was isolated. Three biopsy cores from one knee were included in the study for each tissue type. For cartilage, the biopsy cores were additionally sectioned horizontally to remove the attached subchondral bone. The final thickness of all specimens was approximately 1–2 mm. Synovial fluid from bovine knees (Fig. 3) was purchased from Animal Technologies (Species Bovine, Synovial fluid). Cartilage with focal lesions (Fig. 4) was obtained from the knee of Yucatan miniature swine (female, 51 kg, 5 months old, Sinclair Bio Resources) 4 months after an in vivo laser-ablation procedure46 to inflict isolated focal injuries on the articular cartilage of the femoral condyle. Specimens were harvested as osteochondral segments with intact cartilage and subchondral bone to preserve tissue architecture around the lesions. No other processing was performed on the specimens prior to the measurement. All animal specimens were obtained in accordance with protocol approved by the MGH Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol No. 2022N000196). Specimens were stored in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) at room temperature after collection and measured within 2–3 h.

Human cartilage specimens (Figs. 5–7) were obtained from patients undergoing knee replacement surgery at MGH. Only excess discarded tissue from the surgery were collected. All specimens were deidentified. No patient information, including sex and/or gender, was collected except for the diagnosis of OA for specimens in Figs. 5 and 6. Specimens were collected by the MGH Surgical Pathology Suite at the time of surgery and stored in an enclosed container in a refrigerator at 4 °C until the time of measurement (within 24 h of collection). All human specimens were obtained in accordance with protocol approved by the MGH Institutional Review Board (Protocol No. 2011P000301). In preparation for measurement, the specimens were removed from the refrigerator and covered with PBS-soaked gauze for 30 min to maintain hydration while equilibrating to room temperature.

Speckle rHEologicAl micRoscopy (SHEAR)

Schematic of the SHEAR microscope is shown in Fig. 1a. A Helium-Neon laser (Thorlabs, HNL210LB, 632 nm) was collimated, linearly polarized, expanded, and focused on the back focal plane of the objective lens (Olympus, LMPLFLN10X, 10X, 0.25 NA, Air). The collimated beam illuminates the sample over a wide area of approximately 2 mm in diameter with a total optical power of 3.3 mW. Backscattered light was collected by the objective lens in an epi-illumination configuration via a non-polarizing beam splitter. Only the backscattered light with orthogonal polarization to the illumination beam was focused on the CMOS camera sensor (Basler, acA2000-340km). The lateral optical resolution of the SHEAR microscope was 1.5 μm (supporting a spatial resolution of ~13 μm after spatial averaging over 9 × 9 pixels during the reconstruction procedure). SHEAR mapping was performed by acquiring speckle time series of 1000 frames with a pixel bit depth of 12 bit over a camera ROI of 1020 × 512 pixels, corresponding to a single-shot field-of-view (FOV) of 580 μm × 290 μm. This configuration supports an acquisition frame rate of 300–500 Hz for the exposure times used in this study. The exposure time was adjusted for each sample to ensure the speckle intensity filled the available dynamic range of the camera sensor while avoiding saturation. To obtain SHEAR maps over a larger area, a 2-axis motorized stage (Newport, MFA-CC) laterally translated the sample in steps that maintain an overlap of approximately 12% between adjacent single-shot FOVs. A custom-built acquisition software synchronized the camera trigger and the motion controller.

To reconstruct G*(ω) from the speckle time series data, the optical properties and scattering particle radius of the samples are required. A separate speckle acquisition was performed using a focused beam illumination to estimate these parameters for each tissue type. To estimate the optical properties, the diffuse reflectance profile (DRP) was obtained for each tissue type by acquiring a time-averaged speckle image over an FOV of approximately 1 mm × 1 mm and a duration of 1 s. The radial profile of the DRP provides an estimation of the optical properties via the photon diffusion theory74. To estimate the scattering particle radius, two separate speckle time series were acquired with orthogonal polarizations (parallel and perpendicular to the illumination beam) by adjusting the linear polarizer placed before the CMOS camera sensor (see Fig. 1a). The relative decay rate of the speckle intensity autocorrelation (Eq. (1)) acquired with parallel versus perpendicular polarization provides an estimation of the scattering particle size27. The full description of the SHEAR reconstruction procedure is provided in the next section.

Measurements were conducted at room temperature. Specimens were placed on and surrounded by PBS-soaked gauze on a petri dish. This approach is sufficient to maintain hydration of the tissue during the measurement set up and data acquisition process (typically 5–10 min and up to 30 min for mapping over a larger area). For specimens in Figs. 2 and 5–7, ink was applied with a needle to each specimen prior to SHEAR mapping to provide exact landmarks for the mapped area. These landmarks were used to co-register the SHEAR maps to the gross photo and subsequently the histology slides of each specimen. For the sample in Fig. 4, tissue marking dye was applied to the osteochondral segments to indicate the orientation of the grid of the lesions. This orientation was used to match the histology slides to the specific lesions on the SHEAR maps.

Mechanical rheometry

A parallel-plate oscillatory shear rheometer (TA Instruments, AR-G2, Rheology Advantage 5.5.22) was employed to establish the comparison between SHEAR and a conventional mechanical testing method (Fig. 3). Each of the biopsy cores from healthy swine knees were measured with the rheometer after SHEAR mapping was completed (SHEAR was conducted first due to the destructiveness of the rheometer in contrast to the passive noncontact mapping by SHEAR). The bottom plate was affixed with sandpaper (P320), and a custom parallel plate (diameter 8 mm) with a serrated surface was used to prevent sample slippage. Frequency sweep test over an angular frequency range of 1–100 rad/s was conducted at a temperature of 23 °C (room temperature). For cartilage, a normal force control of 1 N and oscillatory torque of 5 μNm (resulting in strain of ~0.01%) were used42. For the more compliant meniscus, tendon, and ligament, a normal force control of 0.2 N and a strain of 0.25% were used43. For bovine synovial fluid, a fixed gap of 450 μm and a strain of 1% were used with a larger parallel plate (diameter 40 mm) to increase the contact area for the testing of fluid. A solvent trap was installed over the test plates to maintain hydration during the measurement. Due to the tendency for artifacts (e.g., from sample slippage or inertial effect of the rheometer) at higher frequencies, results at ω = 2π rad/s were shown in Fig. 3 for all samples. We note that minimal variations in G*(ω) with frequency were observed over the applicable frequency range of 1–2π rad/s.

Histological analysis

Following SHEAR and rheometer measurements, tissue samples were fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin for at least 72 h. The fixed samples were embedded in paraffin, sectioned (thickness 7 μm), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), safranin O (Saf O), and picrosirius red (PSR). Slides stained with H&E and Saf O were digitized with a brightfield microscope (Hamamatsu, NanoZoomer 2.0 HT Digital Pathology, 40X, NDP.scan 3.4). Slides stained with PSR were digitized with a cross-circularly polarized light microscope (Olympus, BX43, 4X and 10X, cellSens 1.9). Digitized images are displayed in true color without manipulation. For the samples in Figs. 2, 5 and 6, the digitized histology slides were first manually co-registered to the gross photos of the sample based on the overall shape of the specimen and (when visible) the holes created by the ink-marking needle. Then, the SHEAR maps were also co-registered to the gross photo using the locations of the ink marks and the precise, known position of the mapped area relative to these landmarks. For the samples in Fig. 4, since the histology slides were not obtained from the same orientation as the SHEAR mapping, the images were manually co-aligned based on the locations of the lesions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted in MATLAB R2023b. Pearson correlation coefficients were evaluated for |G*|, G′, G″, and α measured by SHEAR versus rheometer in N = 12 samples (Fig. 3d–g). The 95% confidence intervals of the correlation coefficient R and P values are also reported. In cartilage with focal lesions, multiple comparison of |G*| and α measured in three different regions: inner core of lesion, outer shell of lesion, and uninjured distant sites was conducted with a Kruskal–Wallis test (one-way ANOVA on ranks, Bonferroni correction for multiple pairwise comparisons among groups). Mean values |G*| and α in each region from N = 6 lesions were used in the test; p values are reported (Supplementary Fig. 3).
