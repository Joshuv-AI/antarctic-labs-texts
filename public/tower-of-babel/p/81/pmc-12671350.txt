Thermal conductivity enhancement for multi-functional phase change materials: From random fillers to oriented networks in viscous systems
Authors: Wanwan Li, Tong Yang, Jiaxin Shi, Xiaohu Wu, Tao Zhang, Songzhen Tang, Liying Chen, Huankun Lu, Jian Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryEnhancing thermal conductivity in viscous multifunctional phase change materials (MPCMs) remains a critical challenge. Conventional filler dispersion or pre-built conductive networks often fail due to poor impregnation, resulting in discontinuous pathways. Here, we present a sequential assembly strategy to reconfigure the microstructure of a high-viscosity olefin block copolymer (OBC) system, constructing a hierarchical (PA@CA)/OBC composite. This design overcomes interfacial issues, enabling complete, oriented thermal-conductive networks. The optimized composite achieves an in-plane thermal conductivity of >10 W m−1 K−1 and enhanced vertical conduction. As an anisotropic phase change thermal manager for lithium-ion batteries, it reduces the peak temperature by 16.3°C during 3C-rate discharge, while the radial conductivity increases from 5.36 to 12.64 W m−1 K−1. This work resolves the long-standing challenge of viscosity-limited thermal enhancement and provides a generalizable strategy for constructing oriented networks in high-viscosity energy materials, advancing targeted thermal management or energy storage applications.Subject areas: condensed matter physics, applied sciences

Introduction

The global energy crisis and environmental degradation necessitate urgent advancements in sustainable energy technologies. Phase change materials (PCMs), which store and release latent heat during solid-liquid transitions, have emerged as pivotal solutions for thermal energy storage and management. Their near-isothermal operation enables efficient heat regulation in applications ranging from solar energy storage and building efficiency to thermal safety in lithium-ion batteries and 5G electronics (Figure 1).1

Application field of MPCM

In addition, with the escalating demands for advanced thermal management and energy efficiency, conventional single-functional PCM is increasingly inadequate for practical applications. Consequently, developing multifunctional PCM (MPCM) endowed with additional features—such as leakage-proof capability, superior flexibility, high specific heat capacity, or radiative cooling properties—has become crucial for addressing contemporary energy challenges.2

The preparation of MPCM typically involves incorporating functional polymer component (e.g., leak-proof polymers, flexible polymers, or high-specific heat polymers) into conventional single PCM to impart specific functionalities. However, the inherently low thermal conductivity of most single PCM (generally below 0.5 W m−1 K−1) significantly restricts their heat storage/release efficiency, posing a critical bottleneck for widespread applications.3 Compounding this issue, functional polymers themselves exhibit even lower thermal conductivity (typically <0.2 W m−1 K−1), which further reduces the overall thermal conductivity of the composite material system when blended with PCM, resulting in MPCM with substantially degraded heat conduction performance.4 This severely limited thermal conductivity profoundly constrains the practical implementation of MPCM in energy efficiency and high-power electronic thermal management applications.

From the above, developing effective strategies to enhance the thermal conductivity of MPCM with high viscosity have emerged as a pivotal scientific challenge with profound research significance and broad application potential.

Advances in thermal conductivity enhancement of PCM

In the field of thermal conductivity enhancement for PCM, research has evolved along a development pathway from single-component to composite systems, and from randomly isotropic filling to oriented conduction network constructing. Early studies focused on improving the thermal conductivity of single-component PCM by incorporating randomly isotropic fillers with high thermal conductivity.5 Subsequent research shifted toward constructing directional thermal conduction networks, such as graphene aerogel skeletons6 and carbon fiber frameworks,7 to effectively combine within the PCM matrix and further optimize heat transfer pathways. In recent years, with the diversification of energy application requirements, research emphasis has expanded to MPCM, aiming to synergistically optimize thermal conductivity alongside other functional properties.

Enhancement of single-component PCM

Strategy of randomly dispersed conductive fillers

Early studies predominantly adopted the strategy of randomly dispersing high-thermal-conductivity fillers (e.g., metal powders8 and carbon-based materials9) to enhance the thermal conductivity of single-component PCM,10 forming the structure of PCM/Filler, as summarized in Table 1. Jafarian et al.8 demonstrated that incorporating copper powders into paraffin (PA) increased its thermal conductivity from 0.25 to 0.8 W m−1 K−1. Similarly, Yang et al.9 achieved thermal conductivity enhancement from 0.33 to 0.43 W m−1 K−1 by randomly dispersing carbon nanotubes in stearic acid. However, comprehensive experimental surveys (Table 1) reveal that this approach shows limited effectiveness for single-component PCM, with thermal conductivity rarely exceeding 1.0 W m−1 K−1 when the filler content remains below 10 wt %.

The limited thermal conductivity enhancement primarily originates from phonon scattering effects in the randomly dispersed composite system. In such configurations of PCM/Filler, as shown in Figure 2A, the discontinuous distribution of conductive fillers creates numerous interfacial thermal barriers that strongly scatter heat-carrying phonons propagating through the filler’s crystal lattice. Since phonon mean-free paths become severely limited by the high density of filler-matrix interfaces, the intrinsic high thermal conductivity of the crystalline fillers cannot be effectively utilized. Moreover, the lack of percolating pathways for phonon transport leads to localized heat accumulation at interfaces, further reducing the composite’s overall thermal conduction efficiency. This interfacial-dominated thermal resistance fundamentally constrains the maximum achievable conductivity in randomly dispersed systems, regardless of the filler’s intrinsic thermal properties. However, exceeding 10 wt % filler loading improves thermal conductivity at the cost of significantly reducing PCM energy storage density,12 undermining its primary functionality.

Strategy of oriented thermal conductive networks

To address the limitations associated with randomly dispersed thermal conductive fillers, researchers have recently developed an innovative approach involving the construction of continuous and aligned thermal conductive networks. This strategy focuses on constructing highly interconnected conductive skeleton that can effectively infiltrate with low-viscosity molten PCM, hereby establishing continuous heat transfer pathways within the composite structure of PCM@Filler (Figure 2B) and achieving remarkable enhancement in thermal conductivity.

As summarized in Table 2, numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of integrating PCM with oriented conductive skeleton for thermal conductivity improvement. The most commonly employed skeleton are porous carbon aerogel (CA) structures derived from various carbon-based precursor materials with different dimensionalities (1D, 2D, and 3D, as illustrated in Figure 3), thus forming the structure PCM@CA. For instance, Zhao et al.19 successfully developed an interconnected network using 2D carbon scrolls as the skeleton material for phase change composites, forming PA@CA microstructure. Their results showed that with a carbon loading of 28.5 wt %, the PA@CA structure achieved directional thermal conductivities of 7.38 W m−1 K−1 along the axial direction and 4.04 W m−1 K−1 in the lateral direction. In another significant study, Hu et al.20 employed a directional freeze-casting technique to fabricate oriented graphene aerogel skeleton from 2D graphene precursors. When combined with polyethylene glycol (PEG), the resulting PCM structure of PEG@CA exhibited an exceptional in-plane thermal conductivity of 11.39 W m−1 K−1, representing a 35.6-fold enhancement compared to pure PEG (0.32 W m−1 K−1). Furthermore, Wu et al.21 and Dai et al.22 demonstrated the structure of SA@CA, PA@CA with 3D expanded graphite (EG)-based skeleton, where composites containing only 20 wt % EG achieved thermal conductivities exceeding 20 W m−1 K−1. These findings collectively highlight the tremendous potential of continuous conductive skeleton in significantly enhancing the thermal conductivity of PCM while maintaining reasonable filler loadings, offering promising solutions for advanced thermal storage and management applications.

Enhancement of MPCM

The preparation of MPCM typically requires the incorporation of polymeric functional component (FC) (e.g., leakage-proof polymers, flexible polymers, or optical-effect polymers), forming the morphology of PCM/FC, as shown in Figure 4, which often leads to further reduction in thermal conductivity and significant increase in viscosity during the molten state. For instance, Trigui et al.32 introduced polyethylene (PT) into PA to create leakage-proof PCM with microstructure of PA/PT, resulting in a decrease in thermal conductivity from 0.25 W m−1 K−1 (pure PA) to 0.15 W m−1 K−1, accompanied by a viscosity increase from 0.01 Pa s to over 10 Pa s in the molten state. Similarly, Potolinca et al.33 compounded polyurethane (PUR) with lauric acid (LA) to fabricate flexible PCM with microstructure of LA/PUR, observing a thermal conductivity reduction from 0.17 to 0.09 W m−1 K−1 and a viscosity rise to 50 Pa s. These studies demonstrate that the introduction of functional polymer component, forming the microstructure of PCM/FC, significantly compromises both thermal conductivity and molten-state viscosity, rendering the thermal conductive enhancement of MPCM substantially below practical application requirements.

Composition of the MPCM

Hybrid of polymer FCs integrated into a phase change matrix, forming the structure of PCM/FC.

To enhance the thermal conductivity of MPCM with microstructure of PCM/FC, researchers initially adopted isotropic blending methods by incorporating metal particles,34 carbon nanotubes,35 graphene,36 and EG powder,37 forming the structure of PCM/FC/Filler. However, the results revealed similar limitations in thermal conductivity enhancement as observed in single-component PCM, demonstrating constrained improvement effects.

To address these limitations, researchers have extended the oriented thermal conductive network strategy (Figure 5A), originally developed for single-component PCM, to MPCM systems. Taking the thermally conductive framework structure of CAs formed from different-dimensional carbon materials as an example, researchers tried to prepare the microstructure of (PCM/FC)@CA.38,39,40,41 In this approach, the MPCM melt with PCM/FC structure—a composite of phase change matrix and functional polymer carrier—replaces the pure PCM matrix and is impregnated into pre-fabricated carbon thermal conductive skeleton. However, the highly viscous nature of MPCM systems—PCM/FC significantly compromises their compatibility with skeleton structures, often preventing complete pore infiltration and sometimes even causing structural deformation or collapse of the skeleton (Figure 5B). Consequently, the actual thermal conductivity enhancement falls far below theoretical predictions.

Recent studies have demonstrated that while constructing oriented thermal conductive skeleton of varying dimensions can effectively enhance the thermal conductivity of MPCM, the high viscosity of FCs poses significant challenges to skeleton infiltration. As shown in Table 3, Bermany et al.42 revealed that graphene-based oriented skeleton achieved only 38% adsorption efficiency for high-viscosity (>20 Pa s) PEG-based PCM, with capillary forces below 10 kPa resulting in thermal conductivity improvements still substantially below the 10 W/(m·K) threshold. Similarly, Abdolmaleki et al.43 observed that 1D carbon nanotube aerogels suffered structural collapse during the infiltration of polypropylene (PP)-based MPCM melts with structure of (PA/PP)@CA, yielding merely 35% thermal conductivity enhancement. Although Yang44 reported improved anisotropic thermal conductivities of 2.52 and 1.86 W m−1 K−1 (at 5% enhancer content) in PA/styrene-butadiene-styrene (SBS) composites using 3D EG skeleton, these values remained well below the theoretical limit of 10 W m−1 K−1. More notably, Hu’s work45 showed that for the MPCM with structure of (PA/SEBS)@CA, even with 20% 1D carbon fiber CA content, the high viscosity of SEBS FCs severely compromised adsorption capacity, achieving 6.31 W m−1 K−1 thermal conductivity at the expense of significantly reduced latent heat storage capacity. Addressing this issue, Hosseini et al.46 developed a “pre-wetting/directional infiltration” strategy, preparing the MPCM with structure of (PA/ETMS)@CA, which reduced PA-based PCM viscosity (<5 Pa s), enabling 1.2 W m−1 K−1 with only 1% skeleton content, further confirming the decisive role of FC viscosity in determining thermal enhancement efficacy.

In summary, these collective findings underscore that high-viscosity FCs in MPCM systems not only dramatically impair thermal conductive network permeability but also adversely affect the overall thermal performance of composites, presenting a critical challenge that demands urgent resolution.

Critical challenges

Current research has identified three interrelated critical issues constraining the application of oriented thermal conductive skeleton strategies in MPCM systems with (PCM/FC)@CA microstructures (Figure 5B): (1) the high viscosity of MPCM melts significantly impedes their infiltration into oriented skeleton pores; (2) the infiltration pressure during forced filling induces skeleton structural instability; and (3) weak interfacial bonding between polymeric FC and carbon skeleton triggers phase separation. The synergistic effects of these issues severely compromise the continuity of thermal conduction networks, presenting a major bottleneck for thermal conductivity enhancement.

To overcome the constraints on thermal conductivity improvement in conventional (PCM/FC)@CA microarchitectures, this study proposes an innovative approach involving the reconstruction of microstructural arrangements in the “matrix-FC-skeleton” ternary PCM system. The proposed solution focuses on three key aspects: (1) designing hierarchically interconnected thermal conductive skeleton; (2) developing interface regulation methods for high-viscosity systems and constructing a highly compatible microstructure system “(PCM@CA)/FC” with significantly enhanced thermal conductivity; and (3) establishing a theoretical framework for thermal conductivity enhancement in various phase change composite materials. This research strategy aims to fundamentally address the challenges in constructing effective thermal conductive networks for highly viscous MPCM systems.

Strategies for constructing thermal conductive networks in MPCM proposed in this work

Herein, we propose a more universal strategy for constructing oriented thermal-conductive networks in MPCM, addressing the challenges posed by high-viscosity components and skeleton incompatibility. Our approach is demonstrated using flexible MPCM used for thermal management as a representative case, where the integration of a flexible polymer (e.g., olefin block copolymer, OBC) with a single-phase change matrix (e.g., PA) results in a high-viscosity blend that is incompatible with traditional oriented thermal-conductive CA. Unlike conventional methods that rely on infiltrating a high-viscosity blend into a CA with (PA/OBC)@CA microstructure, our approach involves the spatially decoupled and sequential assembly of the three component—PA, CA, and OBC—to form a hierarchical (PA@CA)/OBC structure. This design leverages the low viscosity of PA to enable its effective infiltration into the CA pores, forming a highly directional and continuous thermal-conductive network. The high-viscosity OBC is then used as an outer shell to encapsulate the PA@CA core, circumventing the challenges associated with infiltrating a high-viscosity blend into the CA. This innovative decoupling and hierarchical assembly strategy not only preserve the integrity of the thermal-conductive network but also imparts additional functionalities, such as flexibility and leak resistance, to the PCM.

Mechanism investigation

The feasibility of the hierarchical (PA@CA)/OBC structure is initially validated through a theoretical analysis of the molecular compatibility among its three components: PA, CA, and OBC, as illustrated in Figure 6A. Herein, CA is the product obtained by growing it from low-cost EG, a 3D carbon material with a specific porous structure, serving as the precursor. All three components dominated by C–H and C–C bonds are covalent compounds.51,52,53,54 This shared chemical bonding framework ensures intrinsic compatibility at the molecular level, thereby minimizing interfacial energy barriers and facilitating the achievement of a stable structural integration (as illustrated in Figure 6B).

Molecular structures in MPCM

(A) Schematic diagrams of molecular structures for the three components: OBC, CA, and PA.

To be specific, as shown in Figure 6A, CA is the biomaterial with vertically aligned directional pores (diameter: 10–50 μm) and high specific surface area (∼200 m2 g−1), providing a scaffold for directional thermal conduction. PA belongs to the linear alkane with low viscosity (0.01–0.1 Pa s) and high fluidity in the molten state, enabling effective infiltration into CA pores, and thus forming the core structure of PA@CA. OBC is a thermoplastic elastomer with high melt viscosity (∼50 Pa s) and mechanical elasticity, serving as a protective shell to prevent leakage. It encapsulates the PA@CA core, forming the (PA@CA)/OBC structure.

Based on the three-component molecular structure analysis, the interfacial stability of the proposed (PA@CA)/OBC structure is now analyzed from macroscale and microscale aspects.

Microscopically, as illustrated in Figure 6B, the CA skeleton maintains structural integrity due to lacking high-viscosity blend infiltration, providing stable skeletal support. This stability is augmented by capillary forces generated through adsorption interactions between PA and the vertically aligned CA pores, which enhance the (PA@CA) core’s cohesion. Furthermore, the high-viscosity molten OBC forms strong interfacial adhesion with the CA scaffold via the external facet, reinforcing the hierarchical architecture.

Microscale stability originates from intermolecular interactions, where London dispersion forces between polar C–H bonds in OBC and both nonpolar C–C bonds in CA/polar C–H bonds in PA enhance interfacial adhesion. These are complemented by van der Waals interactions between PA and CA surfaces that reinforce interfacial bonding. The combination of these macroscale mechanical stabilization and microscale interfacial interactions yields a comprehensively stable hierarchical structure capable of maintaining multifunctional performance under operational conditions. By combining these macroscale and microscale stability mechanisms, the hierarchical architecture achieves a comprehensive and robust structural and interfacial stability, ensuring effective multifunction in various applications.

Experimental strategy

To realize the aforementioned hierarchical (PA@CA)/OBC structure with oriented thermal conductive skeleton, which addresses the incompatibility between high-viscosity OBC and the CA framework, we propose an experimental preparation method involving a spatially decoupled and sequential assembly approach (Figure 7). We first employ ice-templated directional freezing to create a CA skeleton with aligned thermally conductive networks. Following vacuum impregnation for complete PA encapsulation to form a PA@CA core-shell unit, a high-viscosity OBC layer is melt-coated onto the surface, resulting in a stable (PA@CA)/OBC skin-core-shell structure. This innovative approach prevents structural collapse and functional failure caused by premature infiltration of OBC into the CA pores in conventional preparation strategies. After fabrication, the MPCM with an oriented thermally conductive network was characterized. Morphological evolution analysis

Directional freezing of CA

Macroscopic observation: ice-templated growth of cellulose (Figure 9A) reveals in-plane aligned ice crystals along the glass tube wall, mimicking the directional vascular networks in plant stems.

Microscopic features: SEM imaging confirms the formation of directional pore channels (diameter: 20 ± 5 μm) through unidirectional ice crystal growth, achieving a biomimetic scaffold with 92% porosity and 15 MPa compressive strength.

PA infiltration into CA framework

Core formation: after vacuum-assisted infiltration, molten PA fully occupies the directional CA pores (filling efficiency >98%), forming a continuous thermal-conductive pathway. The PA@CA core maintains structural alignment (orientation angle <5°).

OBC encapsulation for functional synergy

Shell integration: cross-sectional SEM demonstrates a distinct OBC encapsulation layer (thickness: 80 ± 10 μm) surrounding the PA@CA core. The molten OBC (viscosity: 48 Pa s) forms a conformal coating without penetrating the CA framework, preserving the directional pores.

This preparation strategy successfully resolves the conflict between structural integrity (0% collapse under 90% strain) and functional integration.

Comparison with morphologies prepared by traditional method

As a benchmark experiment, we used the conventional co-melt mixing method to prepare the three-component mixture of PA, EG, and OBC. It is worth noting that, due to lacking the directional freezing procedure to form a structured CA with an oriented skeleton in traditional methods, the benchmark experiment instead employs the precursor material EG as a thermal conductivity enhancer. The specific preparation process is described in our previous work.55 The micrograph of the experimental product is shown in Figure 9B, with the following observations.

From a macroscopic aspect, in the absence of orientation treatment, EG is randomly distributed within the melted PA. No directional macroscopic patterns are formed, and EG remains isotropically distributed. At the microscopic level, microscopic CA paragraph reveals that EG powder is randomly arranged in an isotropic manner at the microscopic level, lacking interconnected, highly directional pore structures.

From the perspective of adsorption (paragraph PA@CA core structure), direct adsorption of PA onto EG results in a composite where EG does not form a connected directional structure within the PA base. The EG sheet structure is randomly distributed within the PA, and no obvious structural boundaries or highly directional EG connectivity are present.

Regarding composite formation in conventional method, melting and blending of OBC, PA, and EG results in a homogeneous, isotropic, and fully mixed composite (paragraph [PA@CA]/OBC structure). The composite lacks discernible structural boundaries and highly directional EG connectivity, differing significantly from the (PA@CA)/OBC structure achieved by the decoupled and sequential assembly approach.

In comparison, the core structure of PA@CA prepared by the decoupled and sequential assembly approach differs markedly from that obtained by the conventional method. In the approach, EG as a precursor forms a connected, directional structure within the PA base, enhancing thermal conductivity. Besides, in contrast to the randomly distributed EG in the conventional method, the approach achieves highly directional EG connectivity within the PA@CA core, analogous to the directional networks.

Importantly, the encapsulation of the PA@CA core with OBC in the approach preserves the directional pores and enhances functional integration, achieving a balance between structural integrity and functional performance that is not possible with the conventional co-melt mixing method.

Through the comparison of Figures 9A and 9B, it is evident that the decoupled and sequential assembly strategy proposed in this work can regulate the spatial arrangement of the three components of MPCM at the microscopic level, emphasizing the advantages of the strategy in tailoring directional thermal conductive networks in MPCM.

Interface morphology and microstructural evolution analysis

The highly directional MPCM was sliced, and the interface morphology of the OBC coating and the core structure PA@CA is observed. The interface phenomena and distinct microstructural evolution patterns are observed. Subsequent sections elaborate on these findings.

Microscopic growth structures of PA

The interface morphology of five different mixing ratios of the composite is shown in Figure 9C. The lower part of each group of photos is a full view of the interface, and the upper part is a microscopic image.

It can be clearly seen in the figure that different microscopic growth structures of PA appear at the interface under different mixing contents. When the content of CA increases from 6% to 15%, the shape of PA changes from a short ellipsoid to a long columnar or rod-like shape, then becomes short again, and finally returns to a spherical shape. It seems that there is a rule that “with the increase of CA content, PA undergoes micro-nano growth, with the growth height first increasing and then decreasing, and finally disappearing.”

Statistical data on PA growth height and diameter

The statistical data on the growth height and diameter of PA also generally shows this rule. As shown in Figure 10A, when the content of CA increases from 6%, the growth height of PA increases from 0.46 μm. When the content of CA is 10%, the height of PA increases to a maximum of 10.19 μm, and then decreases to 5.00 μm until the content reaches approximately 15%. The growth diameter of PA follows an opposite trend, decreasing first and then increasing with the content of CA. When the CA content is 10%, the diameter of PA is the smallest, at 1.77 μm, while its height is the highest, indicating that the shape of PA is the most elongated in this case, consistent with the shape characteristics of PA in SEM image Figure 9C(iii), which is rod-shaped.

Exploration of the mechanism behind directional growth morphology

Herein, the directional growth morphology of PA at the interface between the PA@CA core and the OBC shell is a significant phenomenon discovered in this work. The reason for this phenomenon may be related to the molecular interactions and adsorption models as discussed later.

Molecular interactions and capillary-like effect

The reason for this phenomenon may be that when the CA content is appropriate, the PA molecules almost completely fill the directional pores of CA, as shown in Figure 10B. At the interface between CA and OBC, the OBC above the interface exerts an upward molecular attraction on the PA at the interface along the vertical direction of graphene layers, while the PA molecules inside the pores exert a downward attraction on the PA at the interface. Due to the much higher molecular weight of OBC than PA, the upward molecular attraction of OBC on PA is greater than the downward molecular attraction of PA inside the pores, resulting in a phenomenon similar to capillary action at the interface, where PA grows upward along the vertical direction.

Adsorption models and PA morphology

The growth morphology of PA is greatly affected by the content of CA, which can be largely explained by adsorption models. When the CA content varies, the adsorption type of PA changes accordingly.

1.Supersaturated adsorption (low CA content): when the CA content is low, as shown in Figure 10B, PA not only completely fills the pores of CA but also covers the outer layer of CA. The upward and downward molecular attraction experienced by PA mainly comes from other PA molecules, making it difficult for directional growth to occur.

Supersaturated adsorption (low CA content): when the CA content is low, as shown in Figure 10B, PA not only completely fills the pores of CA but also covers the outer layer of CA. The upward and downward molecular attraction experienced by PA mainly comes from other PA molecules, making it difficult for directional growth to occur.

2.Saturated adsorption (moderate CA content): as the CA content gradually increases, the adsorption of CA on PA approaches saturated adsorption. In this case, the internal CA pores are mainly occupied by PA molecules, while the interface is mainly occupied by OBC molecules. The molecular attraction of OBC to PA is greater than the internal PA attraction, resulting in PA growing along the in-plane direction toward the OBC molecules, similar to a capillary phenomenon.

Saturated adsorption (moderate CA content): as the CA content gradually increases, the adsorption of CA on PA approaches saturated adsorption. In this case, the internal CA pores are mainly occupied by PA molecules, while the interface is mainly occupied by OBC molecules. The molecular attraction of OBC to PA is greater than the internal PA attraction, resulting in PA growing along the in-plane direction toward the OBC molecules, similar to a capillary phenomenon.

3.Unsaturated adsorption (high CA content): when the CA content continues to increase, the adsorption of CA on PA becomes unsaturated. Some molten OBC flows into the directional pores of CA, causing PA at the interface to experience approximately equal upward and downward intermolecular attraction forces from both OBC and PA molecules. Therefore, the directional growth morphology of PA no longer exists.

Unsaturated adsorption (high CA content): when the CA content continues to increase, the adsorption of CA on PA becomes unsaturated. Some molten OBC flows into the directional pores of CA, causing PA at the interface to experience approximately equal upward and downward intermolecular attraction forces from both OBC and PA molecules. Therefore, the directional growth morphology of PA no longer exists.

In general, due to the highly directional pore structure of CA, when the CA content increases, the adsorption type of CA on PA changes from supersaturated adsorption to saturated one, and then becomes unsaturated adsorption. Under the three different adsorption conditions, the molecular attraction of PA at the interface varies, resulting in different microscopic morphologies of PA. Herein, the directional growth of PCMs in conductive skeletons is a universal phenomenon across adsorption models. Beyond PA, low-viscosity C–H bond-containing materials (e.g., PEG and fatty acids) would exhibit similar oriented growth when combined with oriented skeletons. Specifically, these materials form aligned structures through capillary and intermolecular forces when infiltrating carbon-bonded skeletons while being externally wrapped by non-adsorbed polymers. This discovered phenomenon can be extended to various ternary systems composed of low-viscosity small molecules, oriented skeletons, and high-viscosity polymers, offering insights for microstructure control in functional composite materials.

In summary, the innovative spatially decoupled and sequential assembly strategy utilized in this research not only realizes the highly directional microarrangement morphology tailored for MPCM but also modulates the intermolecular force interactions, resulting in microscopic phenomena that may profoundly influence the physical properties, particularly the vertical thermal conductivity along graphene carbon interlayer, of MPCM for advanced thermal energy storage and management applications.

Directional thermal conductivity enhancement

Previously, it has been observed that the experimental strategy has caused micro-nano structures, which may have a significant impact on thermophysical parameters, especially since the highly directional CA establishes a connected thermal conductivity network. Additionally, considering that at a CA content of 6%, there is no apparent directional growth of graphite lamellae at the microscopic level, and the effect of PA micro growth is insignificant, we have selected the remaining four composites for the analysis of thermal conductivity characteristics of the MPCM with the (PA@CA)/OBC structure.

Anisotropic thermal conductivity at different CA contents

Figure 11A shows the in-plane and vertical thermal conductivity of MPCM with different CA contents. The in-plane thermal conductivity of MPCM has been greatly improved along the graphene carbon layer direction. When the CA content is only 8%, the in-plane thermal conductivity of MPCM is about 25 times that of pure PA (0.21 W m−1 K−1). As the CA content increases, the in-plane thermal conductivity further increases. When the CA content reaches 15%, the in-plane thermal conductivity reaches up to 12.65 W m−1 K−1, exceeding the order of magnitude of 10 W m−1 K−1. In the vertical direction between graphene carbon interlayers, the thermal conductivity exhibits a similar trend as mentioned earlier, reaching its highest value when the PA growth height is maximized.

Comparing the in-plane and vertical thermal conductivity, it can be found that the thermal conductivity of MPCM exhibits significant anisotropy. When the CA content is 15%, the in-plane thermal conductivity reaches up to 12.65 W m−1 K−1, while the vertical thermal conductivity is only one-sixth of the in-plane value, at 2.15 W m−1 K−1. Combined with the SEM morphology photos of the samples mentioned earlier (Figure 9C), the lamellar structures of carbon are directional in the in-plane direction after directional freezing, so the heat transfer path in this direction is intact, and heat can be rapidly dissipated along the thermal conductive path. This also proves that the (PA@CA)/OBC preparation strategy designed in this work does not destroy the directional arrangement structure of CA, and the MPCM still has a highly directional heat conduction path.

Thermal conductivity enhancement comparison: Oriented networks vs. random fillers

To further verify whether the microstructure of MPCM affects their thermal conductivity, we compared MPCM prepared by conventional co-melt blending and strategy. Figure 11A(ii) shows the relationship between the in-plane thermal conductivity of MPCM prepared by the two different methods with different CA contents. It can be observed that the in-plane thermal conductivity of isotropic MPCM prepared by conventional co-melt blending is below 2.0 W m−1 K−1. This is because EG is randomly distributed in this case, failing to form a large-area continuous thermal conductive network at the microscopic level. In contrast, the anisotropic MPCM prepared with CA as the thermal conductivity enhancer exhibit a significant increase in in-plane thermal conductivity. When the content of the thermal conductivity enhancer is 15%, the in-plane thermal conductivity of anisotropic MPCM is 7.7 times that of isotropic MPCM. It can be dedicated that the micro-directional structure of the thermal conductivity enhancer can significantly improve the in-plane thermal conductivity of MPCM.

Figure 11A(iii) shows the relationship between the vertical thermal conductivity of MPCM prepared by the two different methods. As expected, the vertical thermal conductivity of isotropic MPCM increases with the content of the thermal conductivity enhancer. When the content of the thermal conductivity enhancer is 10%, the vertical thermal conductivity of anisotropic MPCM is 3.23 times that of isotropic MPCM. Notably, the thermal conductivity of anisotropic MPCM is higher in the vertical direction than that of isotropic MPCM with the same composition. When the content of the thermal conductivity enhancer reaches 15%, the vertical thermal conductivity of anisotropic MPCM reaches its lowest value, but it is still 2.15 W m−1 K−1 higher than that of isotropic MPCM. This indicates that there is also overlapping of carbon lamellae in anisotropic MPCM in the vertical direction, forming partial thermal conductivity channels, which improves the utilization efficiency of the thermal conductivity enhancer and facilitates efficient thermal management of electronic devices.

Phase change thermal storage properties

Figure 11B presents the phase change parameters of the prepared MPCM with different CA contents. It can be observed that there are distinct endothermic peaks during the heating process. By calculating these endothermic peaks, the phase change temperature and latent heat are obtained. As shown in Figure 11B(ii), the phase change temperature of MPCM remains generally close to the phase change temperature of PA (58°C). This indicates that the phase change temperature of the MPCM is almost unaffected by the CA content and remains at the stable level of that of the pure PA base. In addition, the latent heat of MPCM is around 122 J g−1, which is 59% of that of PA base, and close to the theoretical 60% level of PA during preparation. This indicates that changes in microstructure have not significantly adversely affected the phase change properties of MPCM, and their phase change parameters basically remain consistent with those of PA base.

Chemical stability

The phase structure of the MPCM can be determined by analyzing the X-ray diffraction patterns of CA, PA, OBC, and their composites. The obtained X-ray diffraction (XRD) curve is shown in Figure 11C. The diffraction peaks of the single component PA, CA, and OBC mainly appear near 2θ = 23.5°, 26.0°, and 21.4°, respectively, while their composites also show diffraction peaks at the same position. More importantly, no diffraction peaks appeared in their composites, confirming that there was only physical bonding between the three components, and no chemical reaction occurred to generate substances.

It can be observed from Figures 11D and 12A that at temperatures of 30°C, 60°C, and 90°C, the MPCM exhibited no significant leakage or shape deformation. This indicates that in the (PA@CA)/OBC structural form, the outer encapsulating of OBC can significantly improve the thermal stability of the MPCM. When the temperature reached 120°C and 150°C, exceeding the melting point of OBC, the encapsulating ability weakened, and oil stains of different sizes appeared on the filter paper in Figure 12A. By comparing the four groups of MPCM with different CA contents, it can be found that as the CA content increases, there is a clear trend of decreasing oil stain area. Furthermore, the final leakage rates of all the MPCM at 120°C and 150°C are both below 0.45%. This is because CA serves as a highly directional porous framework that can effectively adsorb the molten PA and OBC through capillary forces to further improve the thermal anti-leakage function.

In summary, at lower temperatures, the high-melting point OBC acts as an encapsulating agent for the overall MPCM. As the temperature continues to rise, the abundant pore structure between the carbon lamellae in CA begins to play a dominant role, ensuring that the MPCM exhibit good leakage resistance.

Flexibility function

Inadequate surface contact between the PCM and the controlled component can directly decrease the overall heat dissipation rate. Due to the MPCM with 15% CA content exhibiting favorable thermal properties in the above tests, this ratio of MPCM was selected for flexibility tests. The results are shown in Figure 12B. The MPCM can be pressed into thin sheets with a thickness of less than 1 mm using molds, making it suitable for narrow and complex application scenarios. The MPCM can be easily bent into a shape resembling the letter “X” at room temperature, and it can basically return to its original shape after removing the external force. Additionally, the MPCM can be twisted and knotted in any way without breaking, indicating that the flexible carrier OBC is not merely a simple outer coating for the internally brittle PA. Instead, there is good compatibility between OBC and PA, imparting excellent flexibility and mechanical properties to the prepared flexible MPCM in this work.

Application

Thermal management performance verification

To evaluate the thermal management performance of the prepared enhanced-thermal-conductivity MPCM, we developed a battery thermal management test system (Figures 13A and 13B). Figure 13C compares the temperature rise characteristics of the batteries: benchmark, managed by isotropic PCM (overall thermal conductivity: 0.85 W m−1 K−1), and anisotropic MPCM (in-plane thermal conductivity: 5.36 W m−1 K−1) under 1C discharge. Results reveal distinct phase-change endothermic stages in both PCM types, with the anisotropic MPCM exhibiting a 42% shorter phase-change duration compared to the isotropic system. During this phase-change stage, the anisotropic MPCM thermal management system achieved temperature reductions of 8.5°C and 5.1°C in battery temperature compared to unmanaged benchmark battery and isotropic thermal management systems, respectively. This regulation effect arises from the synergistic interplay between the in-plane dominant heat diffusion path of batteries and the vertical high-conductivity design of MPCM: in-plane thermal channels efficiently transfer the radial heat in battery to the MPCM core, accelerating latent heat utilization. After the finish of phase change, due to the higher thermal conductivity, isotropic PCM and anisotropic MPCM reduce thermal equilibrium temperatures by 31°C and 43°C, respectively, compared to the benchmark batteries, validating the improved heat dissipation capacity enabled by enhanced conductivity.

(C) Comparison of battery temperature rise curves under 1C discharge among the anisotropic thermal management system, isotropic mode, and non-thermal management mode.

Figure 13D illustrates the impact of increasing the in-plane thermal conductivity λ// (5.36 → 12.64 W m−1 K−1) of anisotropic MPCM on battery temperature evolution under a 1C discharge rate. Experimental data indicate that enhancing the in-plane thermal conductivity reduces the phase change duration by 23%, attributed to the rapid diffusion of radially accumulated heat in the battery through the MPCM’s high-conductivity network, effectively mitigating internal heat accumulation within the battery. When λ// is increased to 12.64 W m−1 K−1, the average temperature during the phase change stage decreases by 11.2°C, confirming that the enhanced thermal conductivity facilitates radial heat transfer to the MPCM.

Similarly, as shown in Figure 13E, under 3C discharge rate, higher in-plane thermal conductivity of MPCM leads to faster phase transition rates along the radial direction in battery and improved latent heat utilization efficiency. During the phase transition period, for instance, when the in-plane thermal conductivity rises from 5.36 to 12.64 W m−1 K−1, the battery temperature decreases by an average of 16.3°C, demonstrating that enhanced in-plane thermal conductivity significantly promotes rapid dissipation of radial accumulated heat, thereby better satisfying the anisotropic heat generation management requirements of cylindrical Li-ion batteries.

It is worth noting that the thermal management performance of MPCM is primarily evaluated by two key metrics: temperature reduction and effective thermal management duration. The above experimental results show that as thermal conductivity increases, the effective management duration shortens, but the temperature reduction effect becomes more obvious. This occurs because the total latent heat storage of the MPCM remains constant—higher thermal conductivity accelerates heat absorption, releasing stored energy more rapidly and thus reducing the thermal management time. However, this “shorter duration” actually indicates improved efficiency, as the enhanced thermal conductivity allows faster heat transfer from the battery, maintaining lower temperatures in a shorter time. Therefore, the increased thermal conductive rate and reduced management time strengthen thermal control effect by accelerating temperature reduction. In this context, the decrease in effective thermal management duration does not impair performance but rather enhances heat dissipation efficiency.

In summary, increasing thermal conductivity can significantly enhance the thermal management performance of MPCM for Li-ion batteries. By further optimizing the anisotropic thermal conductivity of MPCM, it is possible to achieve more precise and efficient directional thermal management tailored to the characteristics of cylindrical Li-ion batteries, including their high heat flux density and non-uniform thermal distribution, thereby meeting the stringent thermal management requirements of advanced electronic devices.

Discussion

This study resolves the long-standing challenge of constructing oriented thermal conductive networks in high-viscosity MPCM, overcoming the intrinsic limitations of random filler dispersion for thermal conductive enhancement. By innovatively designing a hierarchically structured (PA@CA)/OBC composite, we address the dual challenges of interfacial incompatibility and viscosity-driven impregnation barriers in MPCM systems, enabling a demonstration of oriented thermal networks in viscous, polymer-rich MPCM. The sequential assembly strategy achieves an “in-plane thermal conductivity of 12.65 W m−1 K−1” (15 wt % CA), a 600-fold enhancement over pure PA, while maintaining tunable vertical conductivity through correlated PA microstructural growth.

When deployed as an anisotropic thermal management material in Li-ion batteries, the MPCM composite significantly enhances in-plane heat dissipation. This effect reduces peak temperatures by 16.3°C during phase-change regulation under a 3C-rate discharge, underscoring the critical role of directional thermal-conductive engineering in high-power energy storage systems.

Beyond its immediate applications, this work establishes a generalizable framework for designing viscosity-immune, oriented thermal conductive networks within MPCM. This capability represents a pivotal advancement for renewable energy systems—such as concentrated solar power and grid-scale storage—that depend on precise thermal management. By systematically bridging microstructure control with macroscale thermal performance, our findings enable sustainable thermal energy storage solutions that are both highly efficient and readily scalable.

Limitations of the study

The thermal conductivity enhancement strategies proposed in this study are primarily developed for organic phase change materials (e.g., paraffins and alcohols), which can form composites through physical cross-linking with polymer matrices. However, their applicability may be limited to highly viscous inorganic phase change materials, which typically require stabilization via microencapsulation or macroscopic physical encapsulation. Fundamental differences in material fabrication methods and component properties between organic and inorganic systems pose challenges to the direct transfer of the proposed strategies. Future efforts should focus on developing thermally conductive network architectures tailored to the specific characteristics of inorganic materials.

Method details

Sample preparation

Firstly, EG was used as the precursor and dispersed in water, followed by ultrasonic vibration for 30 min to obtain a carbon-based suspension, as shown in Figure 7A. Then calcium chloride solution was added to enhance the cross-linking between the carbon-based layers. The highly directional and highly connected CA was prepared using the directional freezing method (freezing temperature ∼ −60°C, vacuum pressure ∼10 Pa). In a vacuum drying cabinet (vacuum degree ∼0.08 MPa, temperature ∼80°C), the vacuum adsorption method was used to achieve the adsorption of CA on the molten PA, preparing a phase transition core structure PA@CA with interconnected thermal conductivity networks, as shown in Figure 7B. Finally, using the melt adsorption method, the molten OBC with a melting point of 115°C was poured onto the outside of the phase transition core body PA@CA, and further blended in a vacuum chamber (vacuum degree of 0.06 MPa) to achieve stable encapsulating of the core body by OBC, as shown in Figure 7C. When cooled to room temperature, the three-component composite was appropriately pressurized and molded to achieve the designed spatial structure arrangement. The mass percentage of PA was 60%, with the combined mass percentage of CA and OBC being 40%. The mass ratio of CA to OBC was set at five levels, namely 34:6, 32:8, 30:10, 28:12, and 25:15, to investigate the influence of CA content on the composite structure of (PA@CA)/OBC. These were named CA-6%, CA-8%, CA-10%, CA-12%, and CA-15%, respectively.

Morphologies and structure characterization

Scanning electron microscopy characterization

The micro-morphologies and structures of CA, PA@CA, and MPCM were analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Zeiss Sigma 300). CAD software was utilized to calculate the dimensions of paraffin micro/nano-growth in the SEM images of MPCM.

X-Ray diffraction characterization

The chemical compositions of the constituent materials PA, CA, OBC, and MPCM were characterized using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, SmartLab SE).

Thermal properties characterization

The phase change temperature and latent heat of MPCM were investigated using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC, Q20), with each DSC test conducted within a scanning temperature range from room temperature to 100°C at a heating rate of 10°C min−1. The thermal conductivity of MPCM at room temperature was measured using a transient plane source thermal conductivity meter (Hot Disk, TPS 2500S, ±0.01 W m−1 K−1), with the sensor placed at both in-plane and vertical positions on two identical samples to measure their in-plane and vertical thermal conductivities, respectively.

Thermal management experiment

The prepared multifunctional MPCM was coated on the surface of Li-ion batteries to construct an anisotropic thermal management system, with performance testing conducted as shown in Figure 13A. The setup employs a cylindrical heating rod to simulate battery heat generation, featuring a uniformly wrapped 3 mm-thick MPCM layer on the simulated battery surface. Temperature data from four monitoring points were collected in real-time using a thermal profiler (spatial distribution of measurement points detailed in Figure 13B.
