Empowering communities: a case study of integrated knowledge translation in the A/C study
Authors: Agatha Nyambi, Shamara Baidoobonso, Winston Husbands, Pascal Djiadeu, Clémence Ongolo-Zogo, Muna Aden, Egbe Etowa, Wangari Tharao, Lawrence Mbuagbaw
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Ontario, Black people make up approximately 5% of the population and routinely account for over 20% of new HIV diagnoses. The A/C Study, a community-led cross-sectional study among Black Canadians, was designed to examine the factors that augment HIV risk and vulnerability for Black communities and inform policy and practice in Ontario, Canada, regarding HIV prevention and care for Black people. Knowledge translation (KT) was integrated throughout the research process as a core component of the study.MethodsAdhering to the principles of self-determination and autonomy in community-based participatory research, this study was led by Black researchers and was guided by an integrated knowledge translation (IKT) approach. The research team developed a KT plan including: (1) push activities to disseminate the study findings, (2) pull activities to help interested stakeholders “pull” information about the research and (3) linkage and exchange activities. Community members played an integral role in all aspects of the research, including KT activities, and contributed to the study's design and implementation and to the interpretation of findings. Significant efforts were made to build partnerships and trust with stakeholders and community members to support study implementation.OutputsThe KT outputs include a community report and fact sheets available in English and French. There were also numerous community presentations, six conference abstracts and several multimedia and digital products. Community members co-led a World Café to share research findings and generate qualitative data to better understand the survey results and a research summit to develop an action plan for moving forward. Seven peer-reviewed manuscripts have been published with several more manuscripts in progress. Study data are available upon request to interested stakeholders.ConclusionThrough active community involvement and engagement, the A/C Study generated a range of KT products. The information has been presented in various settings and formats to prioritize community audiences and support culturally appropriate and responsive knowledge sharing. These KT efforts underscore the importance of community involvement and partnership at all stages of the research process. The experiences, strengths and challenges of the IKT approach provide a case study of implementation in an equity-focused context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12961-026-01513-1.

Background

The Ontario HIV/AIDS Strategy identifies Black communities as a priority population, reflecting the disproportionate burden of HIV experienced by these communities in Canada [1]. In Ontario, Black communities make up approximately 5% of the population [2]. The Ontario HIV Epidemiology and Surveillance Initiative (OHESI) reported that in 2022, consistent with previous years, Black communities accounted for approximately 29.8% of new HIV diagnoses [3]. This disproportionate burden is driven by various systemic and social factors that act individually and collectively to increase HIV risk and vulnerability in Black communities, including limited access to the social determinants of health [4].

The Public Health Agency of Canada (PHAC) uses the Tracks Enhanced Surveillance System to monitor HIV, hepatitis C and other sexually transmitted and blood-borne infections (STBBIs) in communities with increased vulnerability to HIV infection in Canada [5]. As a result of advocacy by Black scholars (researchers, service providers, and advocates) working with Black communities, PHAC agreed to support the A/C (African/Caribbean) research track. The A/C Study was the result of a decade of advocacy and a concerted effort to ensure that this study met the ongoing needs of Black communities. This vision and community-led approach informed the study design and KT approach developed and implemented by the research team. The A/C Study was a community-driven, community-led cross-sectional study that examined the epidemiology of HIV and its related factors in first- and second-generation Black people aged 15–64 in Toronto and Ottawa [6]. This study was designed to develop an understanding of the factors that augment HIV risk and vulnerability in Black communities. The objectives of the study were to describe the prevalence of HIV, behaviours associated with HIV transmission, HIV testing behaviours, knowledge related to HIV transmission and acquisition, key individual and structural factors that affect vulnerability to HIV, care and treatment history for HIV and use of health services. As a community-led project generating new understanding of HIV in Black communities, knowledge translation was an integral component.

Knowledge translation is defined by the Canadian Institutes of Health Research (CIHR) as a “dynamic and iterative process that includes the synthesis, dissemination, exchange and ethically sound application of knowledge to improve the health of Canadians, provide more effective health services and products, and strengthen the health care system” [7]. In health research, two broad categories of knowledge translation (KT) are commonly described: end-of-grant KT and integrated KT (IKT). IKT is a health research model in which knowledge users are involved in the planning, implementation and dissemination of the research [8, 9]. This is done through partnership and collaboration with knowledge users at all stages of the research process. This means that knowledge users are included as partners in the creation of the research question, design of the study, development of data collection instruments, outcome selection, interpretation of results, and dissemination and implementation of the results [10]. End-of-grant KT refers to KT activities that occur after a project has been completed [10, 11]. End-of-grant KT activities span a spectrum ranging from typical activities such as specialist peer-reviewed publications and conference presentations to more tailored and interactive activities such as workshops [10]. The types of activities implemented are informed by the study’s KT goals, audience, strategies, expertise and resources [11]. End-of-grant KT and IKT are not mutually exclusive. Research studies that implement an IKT approach also require end-of-grant KT activities.

The A/C Study implemented an IKT approach, which informed the development of end-of-grant KT products directly relevant to knowledge users. The aim of this paper is to describe and document the processes, outputs and practice-based observations associated with the IKT approach implemented by the A/C Study, including its adaptation to Black communities in Toronto and Ottawa. This paper provides insights to inform approaches to KT in Black and other racialized communities.

Methods

The IKT approach implemented was guided by the principles of community-based participatory research (CBPR), which is a cyclical, iterative approach grounded in several principles. Our CBPR process is summarized in Fig. 1, illustrating the inputs and outputs of this project. The CBPR principles that informed the design and implementation of the A/C Study included the conscious engagement of researchers and community members, supporting the self-determination of communities (enabling communities to have control of information that influences decisions about their lives), the integration of capacity building into the research process, balancing generalizable knowledge with community benefits and adherence to collaboration [12]. A key feature of participatory research is the co-creation of research between researchers and community members [12]. The principles of CBPR were used to complement and inform our IKT approach, with the shared goal of broad community participation at all stages of the research process, aiming to increase the relevance and utility of the research to the community. Community members with varying roles and expertise participated in all aspects of the research, including KT efforts, and contributed to the study's design and implementation, as well as to deliberations on interpreting results and validating findings. The team prioritized building partnerships and trust with stakeholders to support study implementation. In Fig. 1, the Black community is positioned to reflect its foundational role in the study. The illustrated connections indicate that knowledge, expertise and influence flowed in both directions, such that community members actively shaped research processes while also benefiting from capacity-building and knowledge-sharing activities. This representation emphasizes that engagement was not linear or unidirectional, but instead involved continuous dialogue, co-learning and shared decision-making among all partners.Fig. 1A/C Study community-based participatory research process

A/C Study community-based participatory research process

In adherence with the CBPR principles, we developed a KT plan including (1) push activities to disseminate the study findings, (2) pull activities to help interested stakeholders “pull” information about the research and (3) linkage and exchange activities. Our KT plan included ongoing community engagement through our governance model. We included a variety of presentations prioritizing community presentations. We planned events as additional community engagement opportunities to facilitate knowledge co-production. Our plan also included developing multimedia and digital knowledge products for different audiences.

Our selection of KT strategies was informed by known and observed barriers to knowledge uptake among Black communities, including mistrust of health systems and research, stigma related to HIV, limited access to culturally appropriate health information, and structural barriers such as inequitable access [13–16]. To address these barriers, KT activities were tailored to enhance cultural relevance and accessibility. Community members and advisory groups played a central role in shaping messaging, tone and delivery formats to support cultural appropriateness. For example, community presentations were co-led by Black community members in partnership with members of the research team and were intended to foster trust and ensure culturally responsive knowledge sharing. Knowledge translation materials such as fact sheets and digital stories used culturally appropriate graphics, were co-written with community members and incorporated accessible language, community-informed messaging and formats intended to reflect lived experiences. The use of multiple formats (e.g., in-person presentations, digital stories, and podcasts) was intended to reach different audiences with varying preferences, literacy levels, and access to information. Recognizing the diversity within Black communities (e.g., differences in migration histories, language and cultural norms), the team worked with local partners at each site to adapt KT strategies to local contexts. Our approach was intended to support the perceived credibility of our findings, create opportunities for bidirectional dialogue and contribute to capacity building by positioning community members as active co-producers and disseminators of knowledge.

Outputs

Push activities

We implemented several “push” activities intended to disseminate information to venues where Black communities congregate, community-based organizations and policy-makers [17]. These included our capacity-building and knowledge-sharing activities.

Capacity building

The emphasis on community leadership extended to the inclusion of capacity-building opportunities in the research plan. These were academic and non-academic learning opportunities that the study team provided to community members and academic trainees, tailored to their interests, backgrounds and career paths. These activities were intended to help build a network of community partners, stakeholders and community members actively engaged in the development and implementation of this study. At least five peer interviewers and ten peer recruiters were trained in recruitment strategies and in phlebotomy, skills that can be transferred to other studies and lines of work. One postdoctoral fellow, one PhD candidate and one MSc candidate were supported by this grant and gained experience in evidence synthesis, data analysis and manuscript writing.

Knowledge sharing

The target audiences for our KT activities included Black community members, service providers who work with Black people, AIDS service organizations (ASOs) and policy-makers, researchers/academics and funders. We developed a data-sharing agreement and shared our data with our national health agency funder. We also presented our results back to all our funders. We presented our findings to several community groups and organizations, research and academic institutions, and local public health agencies. We have completed 18 community presentations to date. We have also shared our study findings on other media through digital stories and on a podcast [18, 19]. Our account on X (formerly known as Twitter) was used to promote recruitment and disseminate information about our study. Our study website remains a repository containing our study information, knowledge-sharing products, resources and contact information [20].

We published our study protocol detailing our aims, research methodology and a summary of our research process [6]. We have since published several manuscripts reporting findings from the A/C Study, including studies on racist experiences and health system access [21], community members’ experiences accessing HIV testing, PEP and PrEP in Ontario [22], the psychometric properties of the HIV Knowledge Questionnaire-18 (HIV-KQ-18) [23], factors associated with self-rated health among Black Canadians [24], healthcare utilization and HIV burden among Black communities in Ontario [25] and HIV prevalence and associated sociodemographic, socioeconomic and behavioural factors in HIV vulnerability [26]. We have also presented various aspects of the study findings at the Canadian Association for HIV/AIDS Research conference and the Canadian HIV and Aging Symposium through oral and poster presentations [27–32]. Additional manuscripts led by A/C Study investigators, trainees and external investigators are in progress.

We produced several end-of-grant KT products to fulfill our objectives. We developed these products in collaboration with community stakeholders and with input from the community from our World Café event. Our KT plan prioritized community presentations, and we created a master slide deck, allowing the team to respond to presentation requests quickly and efficiently. A community report with several embedded fact sheets summarizing the findings is available in English and French on the study website [33, 34]. Our KT activities are summarized in Table 1. Pull activities

We implemented several “pull” activities, which were designed to facilitate efforts by interested stakeholders to “reach in” to the research project to extract information by improving access to optimally packaged research that is of high quality and relevant [17]. These pull activities were facilitated through our community partnerships and data-sharing processes.

Community partnerships

Study implementation drew on established community partnerships to facilitate recruitment and inform data collection. We recruited participants through peer recruiters, and these community partnerships enabled recruitment at venues where Black communities congregate. Recruitment occurred at ASOs, community health centres, malls, libraries and festivals. These partnerships also extended to our knowledge-sharing activities by facilitating community presentations and the dissemination of our knowledge products. These partnerships also provided opportunities for stakeholders to reach into the project for relevant information through requests for presentations aligned with their specific needs.

Data sharing

The study’s data are available upon request to interested stakeholders. Figure 2 describes our data access and data-sharing process for A/C Study investigators and non-A/C Study investigators. Our data access and sharing process allows interested researchers who are not A/C Study investigators but meet the requirements of our Data Sharing Working Group (DSWG) to access our study data. Linkage and exchange activities

We implemented several “linkage and exchange” KT activities designed to support the development of shared understandings among stakeholders through partnerships with groups that could use research [16]. These activities included our community consultation, knowledge use, co-leadership and co-creation efforts.

Community consultation

We implemented this study after years of preparatory work to secure support and develop partnerships that facilitated access to the community, administrative and technical expertise needed to complete it. Various community organizations and stakeholders were involved as partners from the inception of this study, providing support at all stages of the research process. Community members with varying professional roles and expertise were also included through membership on community advisory committees and working groups.

Co-leadership

In adherence to the principles of self-determination and autonomy in CBPR, this study was led by Black scholars (researchers, service providers and advocates) who work with Black communities, strengthening the IKT approach. Our governance model combined several committees and working groups, as summarized in Fig. 3. The role of our Steering Committee was to provide leadership and oversight, facilitate the sharing of ideas, address implementation challenges and liaise with our external partners. Our Community Advisory Committee provided overall guidance to the Steering Committee and supported attention to GIPA (the Greater Involvement of People Living with HIV/AIDS) and MIPA (Meaningful Involvement of People Living with HIV/AIDS) principles. Our joint working groups included our Data Management Working Group with an ICES Data Linkage Sub-committee and our HIV Point-of-Care Testing Working Group. Each site had local working groups for recruitment and followed a shared KT plan with site-specific products. Our committees and working groups worked with and on our local teams in Toronto and Ottawa to implement this study.Fig. 3A/C Study governance model

A/C Study governance model

Co-creation of knowledge (World Café)

After collecting and analyzing survey data, we held a World Café event co-led by the research team and other community members with varying roles and professional expertise. The event consisted of a virtual workshop where information about the A/C Study was shared. Each presentation was co-led by a researcher and another community member. Focus group discussions were also held to gather additional qualitative information to better understand the survey results.

Provincial summit

The objective of the provincial summit was to develop action plans to address issues identified in the study and prioritized by stakeholders. Draft action plans were developed in collaboration with stakeholders and community members to drive change at individual, community, institutional and structural levels.

Impact

Black Expert Working Group

Our study experiences appear to have informed and aligned with the development of the Interim Committee on HIV among Black Canadian Communities (ICHBCC), including through the involvement of A/C Study investigators and community partners. This group led the development of the Black HIV Manifesto, in which A/C Study findings are cited [39]. The Black Expert Working Group (BEWG) was launched in summer 2023 as an initiative of the ICHBCC, and this group will provide advice to the national HIV/AIDS Surveillance System (HASS), maintained by the Public Health Agency of Canada, and contribute to the co-development and implementation of strategies to improve the completeness of race and/or ethnicity data and facilitate community leadership in HIV surveillance [40].

The HIV care acuity tool (H-CAT)

Our study findings were among the sources that contributed to the evidence base informing the development of the H-CAT, formerly the HIV Case Management Acuity Assessment Tool (HICMAAT). The development of H-CAT was facilitated by direct involvement of A/C Study investigators and community partners in its design and implementation. The purpose of the H-CAT is to support engagement in the HIV care cascade for Black people living with HIV in Ontario [41].

Recommendations from World Café/provincial summit

We generated several recommendations from the World Café and the Provincial Summit to address our the study findings [22]. These included calls to develop interventions addressing structural factors that impact HIV vulnerability among Black people and to build the capacity of those who provide care to Black people. Additional recommendations included improving race-based data collection and generating population-specific estimates, as well as increasing education about HIV vulnerability and prevention and community-led governance structures.

Tracks survey among Black people phase 2 (A/C 2.0)

The A/C Study appears to have informed the design and adaptation of A/C 2.0, the second iteration. Insights from the first iteration, including lessons learned from survey implementation and community engagement, contributed to revisions of the survey questionnaire and the continuation of community-engaged and participatory approaches to data collection and knowledge translation. Continuity in leadership across the two phases also facilitated the incorporation of lessons learned into the development of A/C 2.0.

Other projects

The relationships formed and capacity-building efforts of the A/C Study provided a foundation for continued collaboration in subsequent initiatives, including the development of the African, Caribbean and Black Community PHAC-funded COVID-19 Impact (APCI) study initiated by the Public Health Agency of Canada (PHAC) about the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on Black communities’ access to sexually transmitted and blood-borne infection (STBBI)-related services [42]. The BEWG provided guidance on the implementation of this study. That same working group has gone on to lead the APCI 2.0 project focused on strengthening capacity in ACB community organizations in Canada for health research, data management and knowledge mobilization [43].

Discussion

We present a detailed case study of an IKT approach implemented within a large, community-led study among Black communities in Ontario. We combined community leadership, co-creation of knowledge and multiple dissemination strategies to embed KT throughout the research process, with the aim of supporting relevance to stakeholder priorities, fostering trust through sustained engagement and facilitating the uptake of findings. Our case study contributes practice-based observations to the limited literature on KT strategies within Black communities in Canada. Our experience suggests that certain KT strategies may have created opportunities for engagement and knowledge exchange. Interactive and community co-led approaches, such as community presentations and the World Café event, were intended to facilitate bidirectional dialogue and contextual interpretation of findings and build trust between researchers and community members. These approaches aligned closely with the principles of community-based participatory research and were intended to foster meaningful involvement of community members as co-producers of knowledge. In contrast, more passive dissemination strategies, including static online platforms and social media, may have played a complementary role by broadening reach but may offer fewer opportunities for deep engagement or co-creation. While these approaches were useful for accessibility and dissemination, they may have offered fewer opportunities for relationship-building and collective interpretation.

The challenges encountered in implementing this IKT approach further highlight important considerations for practice. These challenges reflect a key trade-off inherent in IKT: while co-production may help support relevance, trust-building and cultural appropriateness, it requires sustained investment in relationship-building, flexible timelines and dedicated resources. As such, funding structures and project planning processes must adequately account for the iterative and collaborative nature of IKT approaches.

Our findings also highlight several transferable lessons for researchers and policy audiences implementing IKT approaches in similar contexts. First, early and sustained community co-leadership may be important for supporting trust-building and developing outputs that are responsive to community needs. Second, the use of multiple, complementary KT strategies may help reach diverse audiences; however, interactive and participatory approaches may be useful for supporting meaningful engagement and co-production. Third, cultural tailoring must extend beyond messaging to include co-design of delivery formats and the development of knowledge products intended to reflect lived experiences and community priorities. Fourth, integrated KT requires explicit planning for additional time, coordination, and resources, as well as flexibility to adapt to emerging challenges. Finally, embedding evaluation frameworks within IKT efforts is essential for assessing the effectiveness of different strategies and strengthening the evidence base for best practices.

Limitations

Despite the breadth of KT activities, some limitations should be considered. First, we developed KT products primarily in English and French, which may have limited accessibility for some community members who speak other languages. Second, although we document the number and types of KT activities delivered, we did not systematically evaluate their effectiveness in promoting knowledge uptake or influencing behaviours. As a result, we cannot determine which strategies were the most impactful across different audiences. Third, although we sought to reflect the diversity of Black communities through partnerships and engagement, some subpopulations may have been underrepresented in KT planning and dissemination.

Implications

Our findings highlight important opportunities to strengthen KT practice. Future research should incorporate formal evaluation frameworks to assess the effectiveness of different strategies and identify promising practices for engaging diverse Black communities. Expanding multilingual dissemination and tailoring approaches to reflect diversity may help improve reach and should be evaluated in future work. Funders should consider supporting longer timelines and flexible funding mechanisms that reflect the iterative and collaborative nature of IKT. Given the existing gap in the literature on KT strategies for Black communities in Canada, we provide practice-based insights that may inform future public health and knowledge mobilization efforts.

Building on these insights, lessons learned from the A/C Study, including the importance of community co-leadership, culturally tailored knowledge translation strategies and early integration of knowledge users, have informed the design and implementation of subsequent initiatives such as A/C 2.0, reinforcing the integration of community-engaged approaches to research and knowledge mobilization.

Conclusion

The IKT approach implemented was associated with the generation of a range of KT products. From the inception of the A/C Study, we prioritized the community and focused on delivering information in ways intended to be culturally appropriate and responsive to community needs. These efforts suggest the potential importance of community involvement and partnership at all stages of the research process. Knowledge translation initiatives that are intentionally designed, community-engaged and evaluated may help strengthen public health and health care systems and support the provision of responsive and effective health programmes and services.
