Differentiated impacts of climate physical risks on the Indian power sector
Authors: Abhinav Jindal, Ruben Kerkhofs, Gireesh Shrimali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAs India faces decarbonization and climate hazards, analyzing financial implications of climate-related risks for power sector is critical. This study presents a forward-looking, location focused, technology-wise analysis of physical climate risks for Indian power companies using novel asset-level methodologies. To assess physical risks, particularly flood exposure, we use spatially explicit modeling across 424 power-generating assets representing over 90% of India’s capacity. Monte Carlo simulations reveal that without protective infrastructure, average losses exceed 19%, with 99th percentile tail risks surpassing 24%. However, investment in 100-year flood protection reduces average losses to under 1% and tail risks to below 2%. Results show that the effectiveness of risk mitigation varies significantly by technology and geography, with coal and gas assets benefiting most from protective investments, and coastal regions like Kerala and Gujarat showing highest vulnerabilities. This study provides a comprehensive empirical quantification of physical climate risks for Indian power firms at asset levels.Subject areas: global change, energy engineering, energy sustainability, energy systems

Introduction

Climate change presents significant challenges for businesses globally, particularly in emerging economies like India. India ranks among the most climate-vulnerable globally, with over 80% of its districts exposed to extreme climate events according to recent national climate vulnerability assessments.1 India faces a diverse range of physical hazards, including floods, droughts, cyclones, and heatwaves.2 Notably, it ranks 13th globally in terms of flood exposure, with both riverine and coastal regions frequently impacted by intense rainfall and urban flooding events.3

The Indian power sector is increasingly vulnerable to acute climate hazards like extreme floods, cyclones, and heatwaves1 that produce not just operational disruptions but severe financial shocks. The Indian power sector forms part of the nation’s critical infrastructure, where disruptions can cascade into economic and social consequences. Addressing these risks is crucial for long-term business sustainability. These “tail risks,” characterized by low probability but high impact,4 are not well accounted for in current risk models or investment strategies. Evidence from international financial markets points to the materiality of such risks. In India, where power infrastructure is exposed to coastal flooding (e.g., Tamil Nadu and Odisha),5 glacial events (e.g., Uttarakhand),1 and inland droughts (e.g., Chhattisgarh),6 similar extreme event dynamics could lead to material mispricing of climate risk in corporate valuations, debt instruments, and infrastructure investment strategies.

As India simultaneously undergoes a rapid energy transition to expand its total power-generating capacity and integrate renewables, the physical resilience of energy assets becomes crucial. Strategic planning for such transitions must be grounded in reliable, asset-level physical risk assessments that can inform long-term capital allocation and operational strategies.7 While studies globally, have begun to incorporate climate risk into energy modeling, a specific focus on India’s physical risk landscape, tailored to local climatic, hydrological, and infrastructure contexts, remains limited. This research aims to bridge that gap by providing a systematic, data-driven understanding of how physical climate hazards affect the performance and reliability of India’s power sector, with implications for resilience planning, regulatory oversight, and sustainable investment.

Not only the power sector, the magnitude of these climate risks could also pose a significant threat to the stability of the Indian financial system. Not only the financial economy, the methodological literature on estimating climate risks in the real economy is also still evolving. While central banks in Europe and the United States have been increasingly using climate stress tests; in India, climate stress testing both at regulatory level and at firm level is in the initial stages. As India represents one of the largest developing economies in the world, it is important to assess the climate risks that firms in this country are exposed to.8 The impact toward its economy and society will have significant repercussions on the global economy. The integration of climate risk assessments into core infrastructure planning and public investment decisions remains fragmented and limited in scope. While India’s regulatory and policy landscape has made important strides in recognizing and disclosing climate-related risks, much remains to be done to translate that recognition into actionable risk management frameworks which need well-defined metrics and methods.8 Our research is an attempt in this direction.

As climate extremes intensify, the Indian power sector must confront a fundamental shift: from average-risk thinking to tail-risk preparedness. Financial markets are beginning to reflect these risks in select geographies, yet India’s energy finance, planning, and regulatory frameworks remain behind. Prior literature on climate-related physical risks has largely focused on three interrelated dimensions-how extreme climate events drive financial tail losses, the role of insurance in providing relief but not a substitute for resilience and the need for asset-level, spatial risk modeling to analyze these risks.

Empirical evidence from capital markets globally highlights how extreme climate events generate persistent financial shocks. Kruttli et al.9 examined how hurricanes create tail risks for US firms by analyzing stock options and returns of companies in projected landfall zones. Implied volatilities on these firms’ options spike sharply before a storm, reflecting huge uncertainty, yet investors initially underreact relative to subsequent realized volatility. After major hurricanes (e.g., post-Hurricane Sandy), exposed firms consistently have higher expected stock returns, suggesting extreme weather risk is being priced with a risk premium for firms vulnerable to such tail events. Lanfear et al.10 analyzed hurricanes in the United States during 1990–2017 and found that these disasters cause significant abnormal negative stock returns and spikes in illiquidity. Certain risk factors are especially sensitive: for example, high-momentum portfolios suffer outsized losses compared to other stocks, while only a small portion of the negative return can be explained by increased illiquidity. For Indian power firms, which are often capital-intensive and regionally concentrated, similar dynamics may exist. Yet, they remain understudied in domestic financial risk frameworks. Recognizing and pricing physical climate risk—particularly extreme events—remains a blind spot in energy sector finance and regulation.

Insurance plays a valuable role in accelerating post-disaster recovery, but its capacity to mitigate physical losses is inherently limited. This is particularly true in developing economies like India, where insurance penetration for energy assets remains low, and the effectiveness of post-event relief is inconsistent. Studies advocate for integrating resilience measures, such as flood defenses or nature-based solutions, to substantially reduce worst-case losses and even lower insurance costs over time. Kousky11 emphasized that while disaster insurance improves liquidity after events, it does little to incentivize risk reduction beforehand. In fact, most climate damages—especially in low-income and poorly insured areas, remain unrecovered. Pre-disaster resilience investments, therefore, offer a far greater return in terms of avoided losses. A synergistic model is outlined by Reguero et al.12 who showed that in a coral reef restoration case study, upfront investment in nature-based infrastructure reduced storm damage significantly, with insurance premiums dropping as a result. In their model, premium savings over 5 years covered nearly half the adaptation cost—demonstrating that resilience and insurance are not substitutes but complements. For India’s power sector, similar hybrid strategies could involve: elevation of substations and transformers in flood-prone zones, reinforced turbine housings for wind assets in cyclone corridors, and redundant cooling systems in thermal plants subject to heatwaves or drought.

Third, there is a consensus that fine-grained, asset-level, spatial risk modeling is essential to accurately assess and manage physical climate risks. Aggregate risk assessments frequently underestimate exposure and misidentify adaptation priorities. This is particularly problematic in infrastructure-heavy sectors like power, where asset location, configuration, and exposure to multiple hazards vary widely. Bressan et al.13 showed that failure to incorporate asset-level features leads to an underestimation of investor losses by up to 70%, and up to 82% when acute risks are excluded. This finding is particularly relevant for energy companies with geographically clustered assets, such as multiple coal plants along a single river basin or solar parks concentrated in desert zones.

Kerkhofs et al.14 explored spatial correlation of hazards, noting that assuming independence across assets ignores the possibility that a single extreme event—like a cyclone or riverine flood—can simultaneously impact multiple plants. This lack of spatial modeling can falsely suggest diversification benefits where none exist. Mandel et al.15 emphasized the importance of cross-border asset exposure, particularly for multinational utilities and power producers. Their work highlights how ignoring foreign operations leads to systematic underestimation of total firm-level climate exposure. In parallel, Hain et al.16 critiqued widely used commercial physical risk scores for showing low correlation across providers, largely due to black-box methodologies and reliance on coarse spatial data. For the Indian power sector—where granularity is essential given the diversity of terrain and climate—such tools are unlikely to support effective adaptation planning or capital allocation. Zscheischler et al.17 proposed a typology of compound events and suggested analytical and modeling approaches to aid in their investigation, as compounding climate events can be another source of extreme tail risks that is currently underdeveloped in the physical risk literature. Finally, Luo et al.18 developed such a framework in the context of power generation projects in which they provide a spatially explicit analysis of compounding impacts. They show how these compounding impacts can reveal vulnerabilities (and adaptation opportunities) that aggregated assessments would miss.

Empirical evidence highlights the growing cost of inaction. Research by Verschuur and Srijith19 demonstrated that on extreme weather days, the Indian power grid suffers disproportionately: outage durations spike by 80%–220% during intense precipitation events, and severe urban floods can amplify outages by factors ranging from two to five. Furthermore, chronic water stress has already led to material financial losses. Between 2013 and 2016, widespread water shortages forced 14 of India’s 20 largest thermal utilities to shut down plants, resulting in a cumulative loss of USD 1.4 billion and generation deficits equivalent to Sri Lanka’s annual electricity consumption.20 India’s dependency on water-intensive thermal and hydropower generation exacerbates these risks. A severe drought led to a 20% reduction in hydropower generation and a USD 350 million financial hit to coal-based power producers due to cooling water shortages.20 These episodes underscore the urgent need for integrating physical climate risk considerations into infrastructure planning, financing, and regulatory frameworks.

The evidence is clear—resilience investments can drastically reduce worst-case losses, while spatially explicit, asset-level modeling is critical to identifying both vulnerabilities and cost-effective interventions. This study is crucial for India as the country faces increasing climate vulnerabilities, particularly in its power sector, which is essential to its economic growth and development. Extreme weather events like cyclones, floods, and heatwaves are becoming more frequent and intense, threatening energy infrastructure and disrupting supply. With limited insurance penetration in the energy sector, resilience investments—such as flood defenses and robust infrastructure—become even more vital to reduce financial losses from such events. Moreover, India’s diverse geography means that climate risks vary significantly across regions, making fine-grained, asset-level, spatial risk modeling essential to identify specific vulnerabilities and guide targeted investments. By integrating these aspects, this research provides the insights needed for India to build a climate-resilient power sector, ensuring energy security and sustainable growth amid the challenges of climate change.

In this study, forward-looking methodologies have been chosen for assessing climate physical risks namely flood risk for Indian power companies whose assets are relatively young and long term in nature. The power infrastructure in India comprises of coal-fired plants, hydropower dams, and renewable energy installations typically have operational lifespans of 20–40 years or more. Without forward-looking analysis, companies risk underestimating future vulnerabilities that could impact the performance, cost, and viability of these assets. For example, a thermal plant built today without considering future flood scenario, water stress, or extreme heat may become inefficient or even inoperable within its expected lifetime. Therefore, forward-looking methodologies would enable Indian power companies to assess risks and opportunities over the long term, improve investment decisions, align with evolving policy and regulatory landscapes, and build resilience against the increasing physical impacts of climate change. It will explore how these risks will shape future business dynamics, offering a futuristic perspective on challenges and opportunities for companies and policymakers. The findings aim to contribute to the growing body of knowledge on climate risks, with specific focus on the Indian context, offering policy makers, investors, and companies’ valuable insights into navigating the complex intersection of climate change and financial stability.

The study adopts a framework proposed by Kerkhofs et al.14 which is based on the discounted cash flow model to translate asset-level damages into financial impacts for 424 power-generating assets representing over 90% of India’s fossil and non-fossil capacity. The framework performs a Monte Carlo simulation that generates synthetic realizations of flood events over a period of 25 years. The simulations are conducted under various scenarios of insurance coverage, including full coverage, partial coverage, and complete dropout after a predefined period. The model also supports comparative simulations using different copula structures to assess the sensitivity of tail risks to assumptions about spatial dependency and allows the comparison of flood protection levels. These flood events are subsequently translated into financial impacts by considering direct damages to assets, production disruptions, and impacts on the cost of insurance. Finally, these impacts are translated into a financial impact using the discounted cash flow model.

Our assessment reveals the material impact of climate-induced flooding on asset performance and firm valuation. The study demonstrates that physical climate risks are not only immediate and quantifiable but also vary significantly by geography and technology type. Average asset-level losses exceed 19% in the absence of flood protection, with 99th percentile tail risks surpassing 24%. Tail risks are especially high in flood-prone coastal and riverine regions such as Kerala, Gujarat, and Odisha. While insurance provides post-disaster relief, its effectiveness is significantly enhanced when combined with pre-disaster resilience measures like flood protection infrastructure. The synergy between insurance and adaptation investments emerges as a vital insight for comprehensive risk mitigation. Adaptation measures, such as infrastructure designed for 100-year flood protection, can reduce average losses to under 1% and extreme losses to below 2%, affirming the disproportionate benefits of even moderate investments in resilience.

Disaggregation by technology further reveals that coal and gas assets exhibit the greatest responsiveness to flood protection, with risk reduction multipliers dropping to nearly 0.03 under high protection scenarios. In contrast, bioenergy assets retain high residual risks, suggesting the need for technology-specific adaptation strategies. In summary, this paper contributes clear empirical evidence that climate risks are not only material and measurable but also vary considerably based on technology, geography, and adaptation strategies.

Results

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated that physical climate risks can result in substantial financial losses, particularly in the extreme tails of the loss distribution.13,14,15 Building on the framework developed by Kerkhofs et al.,14 this study extends the analysis by systematically examining how varying levels of flood protection influence financial outcomes. While Kerkhofs et al.14 offered a generalizable framework for asset-level tail risk analysis, this study advances the application specifically for India by integrating district-level flood protection estimates and technology-geographic disaggregation, offering actionable policy implications. Specifically, we introduce a series of scenarios in which power-generating assets are protected against flood events of increasing severity, corresponding to return periods of 5 years for a low protection scenario, 25 years for a medium protection scenario, and 100 years for a high protection scenario. These results are discussed for all power assets but also disaggregated results per region and technology type are presented with their policy implications. As outlined in earlier sections, insurance coverage and pre-disaster resilience measures, such as flood defenses are complementary in managing risks associated to extreme weather events. The model used in this analysis explicitly incorporates both mechanisms, allowing us to quantify their individual and joint contributions to risk reduction. We finally introduce a methodology for calibrating flood protection levels at lower granularities and provide policy implications of modeling results.

Table 1 demonstrates how both flood protection and insurance coverage independently reduce tail risks. However, the results also show an interaction effect between the two measures: the efficacy of insurance as a risk mitigation tool increases with higher levels of physical protection. In the baseline scenario with minimal protection, the inclusion of insurance reduces the 99th percentile tail risk by 9.34%. Under high (100-year) flood protection scenarios, this reduction increases to 22.78%. These values reflect a 25-year modeling horizon under hazard inputs centered on 2075 (SSP5-8.5) reflecting a late-century, high-risk climate regime.

This suggests that insurance becomes more effective when paired with pre-disaster resilience measures. The underlying mechanism likely reflects the compounding benefits of reduced expected damages and shorter recovery times, which improve the risk profile of insured assets and enhance the efficiency of insurance payouts. By investing in both insurance and adaptation, firms can significantly reduce the expected economic impact of damages and disruptions, leading to lower volatility in cash flows and a more favorable loss distribution. In practical terms, resilience investments also reduce the cost of insurance. These findings reinforce the argument that insurance and adaptation are not substitutes but components of a comprehensive risk management strategy. This complementarity implies that adaptation investments can generate indirect financial returns by lowering insurance costs and stabilizing long-term asset valuation, thereby improving the business case for resilience in capital-intensive sectors such as power.

To show these differences, we separate the analysis by technology type. Table 2 presents the estimated average losses and tail risks for each technology under different flood protection scenarios. Table 2 shows the average and tail impacts for power assets aggregated at the level of technology types. The protection multiplier compares the 99% tail risk for that technology type and flood protection with the 99% tail risk without any flood protection for that technology. For example, a protection multiplier of 0.5127 for coal plants with low flood would mean that the remaining flood risk is 51.27% of the flood risk for coal plants without any protection. In other words, by including a low level of protection, the coal plants have eliminated 48.73% of the tail risk for floods. The average impact and tail risk impacts are computed on a weighted portfolio.

Table 2 shows significant variation in both baseline risk and responsiveness to flood protection across different power generation technologies. Under low protection scenarios, bioenergy assets exhibit relatively lower tail risks compared to gas, oil, and nuclear plants. However, as protection levels increase, the relative effectiveness of flood defenses changes considerably. Technologies such as coal and gas demonstrate substantial reductions in 99th percentile tail risks, with protection multipliers falling below 0.04 under high protection, indicating significant benefits to resilience investments.

In contrast, bioenergy assets, which are a type of renewable power generation, do experience reductions in absolute tail risks under medium and high protection scenarios, confirming that protective measures are effective. However, compared to other technologies, bioenergy retains notably higher residual risks, as indicated by protection multipliers that remain more than twice those of coal or gas under high protection. This suggests that while protection works, it is relatively less effective for bioenergy, reflecting factors such as geographic locations in more hazard-prone locations.

These results have important economic implications. Technologies differ significantly in the financial value at risk under tail events. Coal and gas assets, for example, have some of the largest absolute risk reductions under high protection levels meaning that investments in adaptation can generate disproportionally large returns. Conversely, bioenergy assets retain high residual risks under 100-year protection. This shows that for some technologies, adaptation alone does not fully mitigate exposure.

Therefore, adaptation priorities should be technology specific. For coal and gas, traditional flood defenses offer large and cost-effective reductions in tail risk. For bioenergy and oil assets, however, additional measures such as siting adjustments, elevation of critical equipment, etc., may be required to achieve comparable reductions. Nuclear assets show low residual risks under medium and high protection but exhibit high sensitivity under low protection, suggesting that maintaining robust minimum protection standards is especially important for high-value, high-impact technologies.

The 99th percentile tail risks by state

Several states with significant coastal and riverine flood exposure show elevated tail risks, most notably Kerala, Puducherry, Gujarat, Karnataka, Odisha, and Andhra Pradesh. In these regions, the 99th percentile loss estimates exceed 35%, with Kerala reaching nearly 58%, highlighting the vulnerability of power infrastructure situated in flood-prone coastal zones. Elevated tail risks are also observed in inland states such as Chhattisgarh, Nagaland, and Arunachal Pradesh, emphasizing the importance of riverine flooding as a driver of financial risk beyond coastal areas. Additionally, the high-risk levels observed in Himachal Pradesh suggest that glacial melt and associated hydrological hazards may increasingly contribute to asset-level vulnerabilities in India’s northern regions. Conversely, states like Rajasthan, Delhi, and Haryana exhibit relatively low tail risks, with 99th percentile losses below 17%. This may reflect both the geographical positioning of assets and the benefits of spatial diversification within these larger regions.

Overall, the state-level results align partially with expectations based on known hydrological basins, such as the Ganges and Brahmaputra, but also reveal new at-risk areas driven by the specific siting of power infrastructure. This reinforces the need for high-resolution, asset-level geospatial assessments in evaluating climate-related financial risk. These findings imply that adaptation investments should be geographically prioritized: regions with clustered high-value assets in hazard-prone zones, such as Kerala, Gujarat, and coastal Andhra Pradesh, offer the highest marginal reduction in expected and tail losses. Conversely, in low-exposure regions, incremental protection may yield limited additional benefit.

Differentiated climate risks on power assets with varying district level flood protection levels

To refine the analysis further, we also consider variations in flood protection at the district level. We define flood protection assumptions at the asset level by combining empirical and policy-based sources. Scussolini et al.21 provided global flood protection standards through the FLOPROS database, estimating a median protection level in India corresponding to a 20-year return period flood, which we aligned with our medium protection scenario. Their policy-based assessment suggests that India’s stated infrastructure goals reach up to a 100-year return period, which we use to define our high protection scenario.

To complement these benchmarks, we incorporate district-level flood impact data from Saharia et al.22 who quantified historical flood severity using per capita flood fatalities per flood event. Districts with fatality rates significantly above the national average are classified as having low protection, while those with very low fatality rates are assumed to have high protection. This combined approach enables us to downscale protection assumptions to the asset level with greater contextual accuracy, and more realistically reflect the heterogeneity in resilience capacity across regions. Overall, these results show the value of incorporating granular, asset-level information when assessing physical climate risks in the Indian power sector. The analysis demonstrates that both flood protection measures and insurance significantly reduce average losses and tail risks, with their combined application resulting in the greatest benefits. Importantly, the effectiveness of insurance increases when paired with higher levels of physical resilience, highlighting that it is complementarity rather than substitutability.

Disaggregated results by technology show substantial differences in both risk and responsiveness to protection measures, coal and nuclear assets benefit disproportionately from resilience investments, while bioenergy and oil remain more vulnerable. Spatial analysis further shows that financial risks are not uniformly distributed across regions: high tail risks concentrate in states like Kerala, Puducherry, and Gujarat, while others, such as Delhi and Rajasthan, exhibit lower exposure, often due to specific asset locations or spatial diversification.

By calibrating protection levels using district-level flood fatality data, we confirm that many assets are located in moderately to highly protected areas, yet certain technologies and regions remain at risk. Together, these findings show that asset-level risk assessment, accounting for technology, geography, and local protection conditions, is essential for accurately quantifying and managing the physical climate risks facing India’s power assets.

To further investigate whether differentiated technology risks might simply reflect siting in safer districts, we report in the supplemental materials (Table S1) the distribution of assets by technology across low-, medium-, and high-protection districts, along with their average flood exposures. The results show a mixed pattern: coal plants, for example, are spread across all three protection categories, bioenergy and oil are concentrated in low-protection districts, and gas and nuclear assets are often sited in more exposed areas but with higher average protection. This confirms that while siting plays a role, it does not fully explain the differentiated risk profiles; rather, risks are shaped by exposure, protection, and siting decisions influenced by functional siting requirements.

Discussion

This study provides a systematic, asset-level assessment of flood-related physical climate risks in the Indian power sector and yields critical insights for infrastructure planning, financial risk management, and regulatory policy. The analysis demonstrates that flood protection infrastructure substantially reduces both average asset losses and financial tail risks, providing compelling evidence for integrating physical resilience into India’s energy transition strategy. Even small upgrades (e.g., 25-year protection) result in large reductions in tail risks.

In unprotected scenarios, i.e., the absence of flood protection, our findings show that average asset value losses exceed 25%, with tail risks, defined as losses in the most extreme scenarios, surpassing 50% for certain geographic regions. These figures underscore the acute vulnerability of India’s power infrastructure to flooding, particularly as climate change is expected to intensify the frequency and severity of extreme weather events. However, the potential for risk reduction through adaptation is equally striking. With 100-year flood protection in place, average losses decline to approximately 1%, and tail risks fall below 2%. Such dramatic improvements affirm the central role of physical protection measures in safeguarding national energy security.

Notably, the relationship between protection level and financial risk is highly non-linear. The largest risk reductions occur in transitioning from no protection to 25-year protection levels, suggesting that significant resilience gains can be achieved through relatively modest infrastructure investments. This finding is particularly relevant for public and private sector decision-makers seeking cost-effective adaptation strategies in resource-constrained settings. It also offers a pragmatic entry point for prioritizing near-term climate resilience actions without requiring immediate large-scale capital expenditures.

In this study, we focus exclusively on quantifying risk reductions. A full economic interpretation of marginal benefits would require combining risk reduction with detailed implementation cost data, which is beyond the scope of our analysis and not available at the necessary regional resolution. Future work should integrate protection costs into the analysis to enable comprehensive cost-effectiveness evaluations of adaptation strategies.

The study also reveals pronounced heterogeneity in risk across technologies. Gas and nuclear assets show heightened sensitivity to flood risk under low-protection conditions. Conversely, bioenergy assets retain relatively high tail risks even under high-protection scenarios. These differences imply that a differentiated approach is essential: resilience planning should be tailored not only to asset location but also to technology-specific vulnerability profiles, including design and operational dependencies.

A limitation of the study concerns the potential endogeneity of siting decisions, as firms may strategically locate assets in areas with stronger inherent protection. While our descriptive analysis (Table S4) highlights that protection and exposure distributions differ across technologies, confirming that siting is an important driver, the framework does not separate technology from location effects. Advanced methods such as instrumental variables or matching techniques could help address this, but are not feasible here given the absence of empirical loss data and richer asset-level covariates. We, therefore, emphasize that our results should be interpreted as quantifying risks for observed technology-geography combinations, rather than isolating pure technology effects.

An important additional limitation is the uncertainty associated to performing asset-level climate risk analyses. By performing a bootstrap analysis, we can already provide a first quantification of structural uncertainty in the financial model; however, structural uncertainty also arises from the hazard projections, damage functions, and copula estimations. Kerkhofs et al. performed robustness checks against these specifications and found that conclusions based on tail risk comparisons across parameter sets and scenarios are robust against different model specifications. In the present study, some of this risk is reduced by aggregating results across a large number of assets and reporting portfolio-, technology-, or geography-level outcomes rather than asset-specific loss distributions. However, a comprehensive treatment of structural uncertainty in climate-financial models remains an important direction for future research.

Geographic differences further reinforce the need for spatially targeted interventions. States such as Kerala, Puducherry, Gujarat, and Andhra Pradesh display the highest tail risks, reflecting a combination of high exposure and inadequate localized protection. Meanwhile, lower-risk regions like Delhi and Rajasthan benefit from either less hazardous geographies or more favorable locations of infrastructure. These patterns emphasize the importance of integrating regional climate vulnerability assessments into infrastructure development and regulatory oversight, particularly in high-density or systemically critical areas.

Beyond physical interventions, the findings highlight the limitations of relying solely on insurance mechanisms. While insurance can offer important financial relief post-disaster, it does not prevent the physical damages or service interruptions that can destabilize power systems. Moreover, the model shows that the effectiveness of insurance increases when paired with higher flood protection levels, reaffirming the need for a complementary strategy. In critical infrastructure sectors such as energy, where system continuity is essential to economic productivity and public welfare, physical risk reduction remains the most direct and durable pathway to resilience.

The differentiated climate risks identified in this study have direct implications for decision-making across the power sector. First, regulators could incorporate asset-level protection into climate-risk disclosures, enabling more accurate sector-wide assessments of resilience gaps. Alternatively, policymakers could provide guidance on proxy estimation similar to the district-level calibrations presented in this study. From a resilience point of view, this analysis also shows that incorporating protection-adjusted risk metrics into infrastructure approval processes could provide significant benefits in terms of resilience.

Second, investors can use tail-risk reduction multipliers to prioritize capital expenditure and resilience financing. Technologies such as coal and gas, which exhibit the largest marginal reductions in tail risk under higher protection levels, offer comparatively high returns on adaptation investments. Geographic hotspots, including Kerala, Gujarat, Odisha, and coastal Andhra Pradesh, represent priority regions where adaptation spending would result in the largest financial benefit.

Third, firms may use these findings to develop technology-specific resilience strategies. For technologies, such as bioenergy and oil, which retain higher residual risks even under strong protection, additional measures may be required. Firms with assets in high-risk districts can also integrate adaptation planning into maintenance schedules, insurance procurement, and long-term capital budgeting. Together, these implications demonstrate that adaptation investments must be tailored across technologies and regions to achieve cost-effective reductions in climate-related financial risk.

Finally, the study highlights the importance of improving transparency around asset-level resilience in Indian power sector. Given the materiality of physical risk, we advocate for the inclusion of asset-level protection status in corporate climate risk disclosures. Regulatory bodies should consider encouraging, or mandating, the disclosure of such information, especially for infrastructure categorized as critical. Doing so would enable investors, policymakers, and planners to make more informed decisions, enhance market discipline, and accelerate capital flows toward resilient infrastructure.

In conclusion, this assessment underscores the transformative impact of flood protection in reducing physical climate risks across India’s power sector. It calls for an integrated approach to resilience, one that combines targeted infrastructure investment, technology-specific adaptation, spatial prioritization, and transparent risk disclosure. As climate threats continue to escalate, embedding physical resilience into the energy sector is not only a climate imperative but also a strategic necessity for sustainable and secure power systems in India.

This study is complimentary to related work on impact of climate transition risks for Indian power companies by Jindal et al.8 wherein the financial impacts of transition risks have been examined based on the technology of the power asset. Jindal et al.8 concluded that the financial impacts of climate transition risks are well differentiated which vary as per technology type, a finding similar to this study for climate physical risks. For climate transition risks, Jindal et al.8 found minimal impact for hydropower, mixed for nuclear, moderate gains for renewables, and substantial losses for fossils-coal, gas, and oil assets. However, while our study also found that the impact of climate physical risks on power assets is heterogeneous across various technologies, the impact is seen to be moderated by the underlying protection levels which act as resilience measure. Even as power assets in general show high impact to flood risks under low-protection conditions with gas and nuclear assets in particular showing heightened sensitivity, the impact gets significantly reduced under protective investments with coal and gas power assets benefiting most.

Limitations of the study

This study provides a comprehensive asset-level assessment of flood-related physical climate risks in the Indian power sector; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the analysis focuses only on flood risk, while other relevant climate hazards such as heatwaves, droughts, and cyclones are not explicitly modeled. Given that power assets are exposed to multiple, and often compounding, hazards, the exclusion of these risks may lead to an underestimation of total climate-related financial exposure.

Second, the study relies on a single high-emission climate scenario (SSP5-8.5) and considers it a high-risk regime rather than modeling a time-evolving transition of hazards. While this approach is appropriate for tail-risk analysis, it does not capture the dynamic progression of climate risks over time or the uncertainty across alternative climate pathways.

Third, although the analysis is conducted at the asset level, it depends on publicly available datasets and proxy-based assumptions, particularly for flood protection levels and vulnerability functions. District-level protection proxies and global damage curves may not fully reflect local infrastructure conditions or firm-specific resilience, potentially introducing measurement error.

Fourth, the framework does not incorporate adaptation costs or cost-benefit analysis. While the study quantifies the risk reduction benefits of flood protection and insurance, it does not assess the economic feasibility or optimal allocation of such investments, limiting its ability to inform fully efficient decision-making.

Finally, the analysis abstracts from broader system-level considerations, such as interdependencies within the power grid, supply chain disruptions, and macroeconomic feedback effects, which could amplify or mitigate the financial impacts of physical climate risks.

Future research could address these limitations by incorporating multi-hazard frameworks, dynamic climate scenarios, cost-benefit analyses of adaptation, and more granular asset-level data, as well as by extending the modeling framework to capture system-wide and macro-financial impacts. Further studies on the impact of uncertainty in climate models on financial risk metrics are also encouraged.

Method details

The model is adopted from Kerkhofs et al.14 and uses a framework based on the discounted cash flow model to translate asset-level damages into financial impacts at the asset, firm, and sector-level. The framework performs a Monte Carlo simulation that generates synthetic realizations of flood events over a period of 25 years (For each set of parameters, the Monte Carlo simulation produces 10,000 synthetic sequences of flood events drawn from a stationary, late-century hazard regime [SSP5-8.5, centered on 2075]. The discounted cash-flow [DCF] model then evaluates the financial implications of these events over a 25-year horizon, which is a valuation choice rather than a climatological one. In principle, identical conclusions about financial tail risks would be obtained if we had valued cash flows over 5 years instead of 25 years, provided the simulation is adjusted accordingly [e.g., by increasing the number of Monte Carlo realizations]. The tail-risk estimates depend on the distribution of extreme events in the hazard regime, not on the specific length of the DCF horizon. The use of a 25-year horizon simply aligns the valuation window with typical operational and investment lifetimes of power-sector assets, rather than implying any mismatch between hazard timing and financial modeling). This horizon is applied only for extreme event generation and cash flow estimation, and is therefore exclusively used for the financial valuation. Importantly, the hazard inputs are derived from flood maps centered on the year 2075 under the SSP5-RCP8.5 scenario, meaning that the simulated flood events already reflect a late-century, high-risk climate regime. We focus on SSP5-8.5 as it captures a high-emission, worst-case scenario that is particularly informative for studying tail risks. Alternative scenarios would provide additional insights, but are beyond the scope of this study.

These hazard inputs represent a stationary high-risk climate scenario rather than a time-evolving hazard path. In other words, the analysis is carried out as if the current energy system would be transferred directly to this late-century, high-risk regime. Because India’s power assets are long-lived (20–40+ years), a late-century regime provides a conservative upper-bound estimate of physical risk relevant for tail-risk analysis. As such, the cash flow model does also not forecast the hazard evolution but instead evaluates financial performance as if assets are exposed to these high-risk hazards. - Adaptation measures (e.g., 5-, 25-, and 100-year flood protection) are applied consistently to these hazard maps; therefore the model quantifies marginal risk reduction, not literal 2075 economic forecasting.

These flood events are subsequently translated into financial impacts by considering direct damages to assets, production disruptions, and impacts on the cost of insurance. Finally, these impacts are translated into a financial impact using the discounted cash flow model. By performing a Monte Carlo simulation of many different realizations of flood events, the authors obtain a distribution of asset, firm, and sector-valuations from which they can derive tail risk estimates.

Flood events are modeled using a Poisson process for each basin and return period with associated flooding levels derived from the GIRI flood maps. To accurately capture spatial correlations between flooding events in different bases, the authors make use of copula functions calibrated on daily extreme discharge levels.

The flood events cause reductions in asset values through three distinct channels. First, direct damages to the assets are derived using flood damage curves from Nirandjan et al.22 Second, disruptions modeled via damage-duration functions from Luo et al.18 that estimate operational downtime. Disruptions affect both revenue and variable costs through reduced output. And third, short-term movements in insurance premiums modeled through dynamically priced premiums and payout structures.

Once flood events are simulated and their financial effects are estimated at the asset level, results are aggregated to the firm and sector levels using reported ownership structures. For each realization of the Monte Carlo simulation, a full portfolio valuation is computed. This repeated valuation across simulations results in an empirical distribution of outcomes, from which key financial risk metrics can be extracted, such as tail risk estimates and changes in default probabilities.

These simulations are conducted under two assumptions of insurance coverage: full coverage, and no coverage. The simulations take into account the benefits of insurance in case an extreme weather event occurs, as well as the payment of insurance premiums which is based on the expected total damages and short-term fluctuations in insurance premia (Kerkhofs et al., 2025).

Quantification and statistical analysis

The analysis of physical risks requires information on the exact geolocation of assets.14 As such, our analysis of physical risks is based on publicly available data provided by Global Energy Monitor (GEM) that contains asset-level information of production capacities, geolocation, and ownership for power-producing assets.23 The study covers operating bioenergy, coal, nuclear, oil, and gas plants and plants under construction. Our final dataset consists of 424 power generating assets, representing roughly 93% of the power production of these asset types in India. These 424 assets belong to 188 firms.

For the assessment of flood risk, we obtained flood maps from the Global Infrastructure Resilience Index (GIRI),24 which offers high-resolution flood extent and depth maps under various climate scenarios. These maps were obtained for multiple return periods (e.g., 1-in-10, 1-in-100, 1-in-500-year events) and for both a historical baseline and a high-emission climate scenario (SSP 5–8.5) centered around the year 2075. The maps are global in coverage and provide a resolution of approximately 90 m, which allows for the assessment of flood exposure at the asset level. To assign flood risk to individual assets, we applied a buffer zone of 0.01° around each geolocation and extracted the maximum flood depth within this zone for each scenario and return period.

To quantify the vulnerability of assets to flooding, we rely on sector-specific impact functions. Capital damages were estimated using depth-damage curves from Nirandjan et al.22 which relate inundation depth to the proportion of asset value lost. These curves are calibrated for infrastructure assets. Disruption impacts were modeled using the framework of Luo et al.,18 which links flood characteristics to days of operational disruption for power assets.

To calibrate the spatial correlation of extreme flood events across the Indian subcontinent, we used daily river discharge data from the Global Flood Awareness System (GloFAS) for the period 1979–2016.25 This data was aggregated to 126 distinct Level 6 river basins as defined by the HydroATLAS classification. Extreme discharge events were identified using the 99th percentile threshold, and a three-day temporal window was applied to account for the lagged nature of flooding across interconnected river systems.

While this study focuses specifically on flood-related damages to the power-producing assets, the approach can be extended to other risks by incorporating additional damage curves. For example, Shuai et al.26 provide functions that link water availability to power generation capacities in extreme drought scenarios, and Eberenz et al.27 provides these for extreme wind induced by tropical cyclones. The approach can also be extended from exclusively modeling power-producing assets to also include power-distribution networks. Even though this would require additional modeling, previous research has produced damage functions specifically for transmission infrastructure. An extensive review of these damage functions can be found in Kabre et al.28
