NKX2‐1 Restricts the Growth and Metastasis of Lung Squamous Cell Carcinoma Through Transcriptive Suppression of AKR1B10
Authors: Dan Yu, Ping Liu, Ruichen Gao, Ting Jiang, Caiwen Shi, Yan Wang, Ming Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) is associated with poor prognosis and limited treatment options. In this study, we investigate the role of NKX2‐1 as a tumor suppressor in LUSC. Our analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) revealed that NKX2‐1 expression is significantly lower in LUSC tissues compared to lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and normal lung tissues, and patients with low NKX2‐1 expression have poorer survival rates. The CCK8, colony formation, EdU incorporation, wound healing, and Transwell assays demonstrated that NKX2‐1 overexpression inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of the LUSC cell lines SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520. Moreover, in a subcutaneous xenograft model, NKX2‐1 overexpression reduced tumorigenic potential of the injected SK‐MES‐1 cells. The transcriptomic analysis highlighted the dysregulation of key genes associated with NKX2‐1 expression levels. AKR1B10 was expressed at higher levels in LUSC tissues and negatively correlated with NKX2‐1. Dual‐luciferase assays verified that NKX2‐1 suppressed the transcription of AKR1B10 by direct binding to its promoter. The tumor‐suppressive effects of NKX2‐1 diminished upon AKR1B10 overexpression, which indicated an AKR1B10‐dependent mechanism. In sum, our findings indicated that NKX2‐1 limited tumor growth and metastasis in LUSC by repressing AKR1B10 transcription, thereby revealing potential therapeutic targets to improve clinical outcomes in these patients.

Introduction

Non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is the most common type of lung cancer, and its subtypes include adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma [1]. Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), which originates from the squamous cells lining the airways, usually occurs in the central parts of the lungs, near the bronchi [2]. It is strongly associated with smoking, particularly in male smokers, and it often causes symptoms like chronic cough and hemoptysis. There are a few unique challenges in treating LUSC. It tends to be diagnosed only at an advanced stage due to its association with smoking and the presence of symptoms like chronic cough and hemoptysis. There is often resistance to conventional chemotherapy and radiotherapy, which decreases the response rate and shortens the duration of remission, and toxicity and side effects are frequently encountered [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Effective targeted therapies are few, and the patients often do not respond well to immunotherapy. Ongoing research is essential to improving the outcome for LUSC patients, and a deeper understanding of the molecular underpinnings is key to developing biomarker‐driven clinical trials toward novel treatment strategies.

NK2 homeobox 1 (NKX2‐1), also known as thyroid transcription factor‐1 (TTF‐1), is a critical transcription factor involved in lung development and function [9]. It is crucial for the early stages of lung development, guiding the formation of the lung buds and branching morphogenesis, and it maintains the expression of genes involved in surfactant production in adult lungs, which is vital for reducing surface tension in the alveoli and preventing lung collapse [10, 11]. In lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), NKX2‐1 is frequently expressed and can act as both an oncogene and a tumor suppressor depending on the context. In contrast, the aggressive nature of LUSC has been associated with the reduction or absence of NKX2‐1 expression [12, 13, 14, 15]. Recent studies have shown that NKX2‐1 can suppress the ERK signaling pathway by inducing the expression of DUSP6, which inhibits the tumor progression of LUAD [12]. NKX2‐1 has also been found to modulate the immune microenvironment in lung tumors, as its repression increases the recruitment of neutrophils and enhances the epithelial‐mesenchymal transition, which promotes tumor progression. In NSCLC, particularly in adenocarcinoma, NKX2‐1 has been shown to be frequently mutated or downregulated [10]. This loss of NKX2‐1 expression correlates with poor clinical outcomes, suggesting its potential role in tumor suppression [16]. Interestingly, the implications of NKX2‐1 in LUSC remain less well‐characterized, despite evidence indicating that its expression is significantly reduced in LUSC tissues compared to LUAD and normal lung tissues.

Aldo‐keto reductase family 1 member B10 (AKR1B10) is an enzyme involved in the reduction of aldehydes and ketones. It is a crucial player in various physiological and pathological conditions because it plays a significant role in lipid metabolism and detoxification processes. It is highly expressed in several types of cancers, including hepatocellular carcinoma, breast carcinoma, and lung cancer, and elevated levels of AKR1B10 are associated with poor prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [17]. It is worth noting that the AKR1B10 expression is linked to resistance to chemotherapy drugs (e.g., doxorubicin, taxol) as it detoxifies the reactive aldehydes produced during chemotherapy. In NSCLC, AKR1B10 is overexpressed and correlates with poor survival rates, and its upregulation is associated with increased malignancy, enhanced cell migration, invasion, and metastasis [18]. However, the direct interactions between AKR1B10 and NKX2‐1 are not well‐documented, despite the significant impact of both on the biology of lung cancer.

This study aims to elucidate the role of NKX2‐1 as a tumor suppressor in LUSC and investigate its regulatory relationship with AKR1B10. Understanding the molecular relationships and biological consequences of NKX2‐1 and AKR1B10 in LUSC may provide critical insights into the disease pathology and pave the way for innovative therapeutic strategies. Should NKX2‐1 significantly inhibit tumor growth and metastasis through the transcriptional repression of AKR1B10, it could serve as a promising therapeutic target. As current treatment modalities for LUSC are limited and often result in poor patient outcomes, identifying critical molecular drivers such as NKX2‐1 may lead to new strategies that enhance clinical efficacy and deliver precision medicine.

Materials and Methods

Cell Lines

Two LUSC cell lines, SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520, were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. They were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Bioinformatics

The expressions of NKX2‐1 (NM_001079668.3) and AKR1B10 (NM_020299.5) in LUSC, LUAD, and normal lung tissues were surveyed using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The differential expression was analyzed using the DESeq. 2 package in R, and the survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan−Meier method with the log‐rank test. The threshold of statistical significance was p < 0.05.

Cell Models

To create cell models overexpressing NKX2‐1 and AKR1B10, full‐length cDNAs were cloned into the pLVX‐IRES‐Puro lentiviral vector (Clontech). Lentiviral particles were produced in 293T cells using the Lenti‐X HTX Packaging System (Clontech), purified by ultracentrifugation, and titrated. SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520 cells were transduced with lentiviral particles (multiplicity of infection = 20) in the presence of 8 μg/mL polybrene and selected with 2 μg/mL puromycin for 7 days. The titer of purified lentiviral particles was 2.0 × 109 TU/mL. The empty pLVX‐IRES‐Puro vector served as a negative control.

Xenograft Tumor Models

Female 5‐week‐old BALB/c nude mice were obtained from Shanghai Laboratory Animal Research Center (Shanghai, China) and used to establish LUSC xenograft models. SK‐MES‐1 cells (5 × 106 cells/mouse) were suspended in a 1:1 Matrigel/PBS mixture (100 µL), and the suspension was subcutaneously injected into the right flank. Tumor dimensions were measured every 5 days, and the volume was calculated as (length × width2)/2. The mice were euthanized after 30 days, and the tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed. The animal experiments described herein were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University.

Characterizations

Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK8) Assay

Cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at a density of 2 × 103 cells/well, and their proliferation was assessed using CCK8 (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured at 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h using a microplate reader (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

EDU Incorporation Assay

Cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well. The cell proliferation was assessed using the Click‐iT EdU Imaging Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. EdU‐positive cells were counted using a fluorescence microscope (CFX53, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Colony Formation Assay

Cells were seeded in six‐well plates at a density of 5 × 102 cells/well and cultured at 37°C for 14 days. Colonies were fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet, and counted using ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Quantitative Real‐Time PCR (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan). The qRT‐PCR was conducted using SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) on a ViiA7 Fast Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Table 1 lists the primer sequences used for NKX2‐1 and AKR1B10. The internal control was GAPDH. The relative expression levels were calculated using the comparative C t method (2−ΔΔCt).

Western Blot Analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Sigma‐Aldrich) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk and incubated with primary antibodies against NKX2‐1 (ab76013, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), AKR1B10 (ab96417, Abcam), or GAPDH (sc‐32233, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) overnight at 4°C. After washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. The electrochemiluminescence signals of the protein bands were detected on a ChemiDoc XRS+ Imaging System (Bio‐Rad) using the ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA).

Dual‐Luciferase Assay

The functional activity of the AKR1B10 promoter region (2000 bp upstream of the transcription start site) was confirmed using a luciferase reporter assay. Potential transcription factor binding sites (TFBS) were predicted using the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.genereg.net/), and a high‐confidence NKX2‐1 binding motif (core: CACTTGA) was identified at −394 to −388 bp. The promoter region of AKR1B10 containing the predicted NKX2‐1 binding site was then cloned into the pGL3‐basic luciferase reporter vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The mutant sequence in the conserved domain was generated using site‐directed mutagenesis. The SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520 cells were cotransfected with the reporter vector and a Renilla luciferase control vector using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). After 48 h, the luciferase activity was measured using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Transwell Invasion Assay

Cells were seeded in the upper chamber of Matrigel‐coated Transwell inserts (Corning, NY, USA) in serum‐free medium. The lower chamber was filled with the culture medium containing 10% FBS. After culturing at 37°C for 24 h, cells that breached the Matrigel and invaded the lower chamber were fixed with methanol, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a light microscope (Olympus).

Wound Healing Assay

Cells were seeded in six‐well plates and grown to confluence, and a scratch was made with the tip of a 200 µL pipette. The cells were then cultured in serum‐free medium at 37°C, and after 24 h, the wound closure was observed using a light microscope (Olympus).

Analysis of Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs)

Analysis of DEGs was performed using publicly available RNA‐seq data from The Cancer Genome Atlas Lung Squamous Cell Carcinoma (TCGA‐LUSC) cohort. Samples were stratified into “High NKX2‐1” (top 25% expression) and “Low NKX2‐1” (bottom 25% expression) groups based on NKX2‐1 mRNA expression (FPKM‐UQ values). The hierarchical clustering of dysregulated genes was performed using Cluster 3.0, and the results were visualized using Java TreeView. A volcano plot was created to show the changes in gene expression in LUSC tissues with high and low levels of NKX2‐1. Genes were considered significantly downregulated if the fold change (FC) was ≤ −2 and the false discovery rate (FDR) was < 0.05. The gene ontology (GO) annotations and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways of dysregulated genes were identified using DAVID v6.8 (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/).

Statistical Analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. The differences between groups were analyzed using Student's t‐test or one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's range test, and they were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.

Results

NKX2‐1 Expression Was Elevated in LUSC Tissues and Correlated With Better Patient Survival

To assess the expression level of NKX2‐1 in LUSC, we analyzed RNA‐sequencing data from TCGA database. As shown in Figure 1A, NKX2‐1 expression was significantly lower in LUSC tissues compared to LUAD and normal lung tissues. This finding was somewhat surprising, as previous studies reported NKX2‐1 as a lineage‐specific oncogene in LUAD, whereas our analysis suggested that NKX2‐1 might play a different role in LUSC. Next, we investigated the clinical significance of NKX2‐1 expression in NSCLC patients. The Kaplan−Meier survival analysis revealed that NSCLC patients with higher NKX2‐1 expression had significantly better overall survival compared to those with lower NKX2‐1 expression (p < 0.001; Figure 1B). This result indicated that NKX2‐1 might act as a tumor suppressor in LUSC, contrary to its oncogenic role in LUAD.

NKX2‐1 is highly expressed in lung squamous cell carcinoma. (A) The level of NKX2‐1 expression in LUSC, LUAD, and normal lung tissues; *p < 0.05, ****p < 0.0001. (B) Survival of NSCLC patients in relation to NKX2‐1 expression (p < 0.001).

NKX2‐1 Overexpression Impaired the Proliferation of LUSC Cell Lines

To further explore the functional role of NKX2‐1 in LUSC, we overexpressed NKX2‐1 in two LUSC cell lines, SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520. The overexpression efficiency was confirmed by qRT‐PCR (Figure 2A) and western blot analysis (Figure 2B). We then assessed the impact of NKX2‐1 overexpression on cell proliferation using various assays. The CCK8 assay revealed that NKX2‐1 overexpression significantly inhibited the proliferation of SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520 cells compared to the control cells (Figure 2C). Similarly, the colony formation assay showed a marked reduction in the number and size of colonies formed by NKX2‐1‐overexpressing cells (Figure 2D). Furthermore, the EdU incorporation assay, which measures DNA synthesis during cell proliferation, demonstrated a significant decrease in the percentage of EdU‐positive cells upon NKX2‐1 overexpression (Figure 2E). These results collectively suggested that NKX2‐1 overexpression impaired the proliferation ability of LUSC cell lines.

NKX2‐1 overexpression impaired the proliferation of LUSC cells. Confirmation of the overexpression efficiency of NKX2‐1 by (A) qRT‐PCR and (B) western blot analysis, (C) CCK8 assay, (D) colony formation assay, and (E) EdU incorporation assay, scale bar = 20 μm. n = 3, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

NKX2‐1 Overexpression Inhibited the Migration and Invasion of LUSC Cell Lines

In addition to the effect on cell proliferation, we also investigated the impact of NKX2‐1 overexpression on the migration and invasion abilities of LUSC cell lines. The wound healing assay showed that NKX2‐1 overexpression significantly reduced the migration ability of SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520 cells compared to the control cells (Figure 3A). Similarly, the Transwell invasion assay demonstrated a marked decrease in the number of invading cells upon NKX2‐1 overexpression (Figure 3B). These findings indicated that NKX2‐1 overexpression inhibited the migration and invasion abilities of LUSC cell lines.

NKX2‐1 overexpression impaired the migration and invasion abilities of LUSC cells. (A) Wound healing assay, scale bar = 100 μm. (B) Transwell invasion assay, scale bar = 100 μm. n = 3, **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001.

To further validate the tumor‐suppressive role of NKX2‐1 in LUSC, we assessed its impact on tumorigenesis in a xenograft model. Nude mice received a subcutaneous injection of SK‐MES‐1 cells overexpressing NKX2‐1 or a control vector, and after 30 days, the resulting tumors were isolated and photographed (Figure 4A). The growth curve of tumors generated from NKX2‐1‐overexpressing cells was significantly slower compared to that of control cells (Figure 4B). The weight of tumors generated from NKX2‐1‐overexpressing cells was also significantly lower than that of control tumors (Figure 4C). These results suggested that NKX2‐1 overexpression impaired the tumorigenic ability of LUSC cells in vivo.

NKX2‐1 overexpression impaired the tumorigenic ability of LUSC cells in a xenograft model. (A) Photographs of isolated tumors. (B) Growth curve of tumors. (C) Weight of collected tumors. n = 6, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Various Genes and Signaling Pathways Were Dysregulated in LUSC Tissues With Low NKX2‐1 Expression

To gain insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the tumor‐suppressive role of NKX2‐1 in LUSC, we used the TCGA database to analyze the gene expression profiles of LUSC tissues with high and low NKX2‐1 expression. Hierarchical clustering analysis revealed distinct gene expression patterns between the two groups (Figure 5A), and a volcano plot showed that a lot of genes were expressed differentially (Figure 5B). According to the GO biological process enrichment analysis, the dysregulated genes were enriched in various processes related to cell proliferation, migration, and invasion (Figure 5C). Additionally, the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the dysregulated genes were involved in several signaling pathways, including the PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway, which is known to play a crucial role in cancer progression (Figure 5D). These findings suggested that NKX2‐1 might exert its tumor‐suppressive effects by modulating the expression of genes involved in these biological processes and signaling pathways.

FPKM values are log2‐transformed and median‐centered by each gene. (B) Volcano plot showing the expression change of genes. Dots are colored blue if FC ≤ − 2 and FDR < 0.05. (C) The GO biological processes of dysregulated genes. (D) The KEGG pathways of dysregulated genes.

NKX2‐1 Suppressed the Transcription of AKR1B10

To identify the downstream target genes of NKX2‐1 in LUSC, we focused on AKR1B10, a member of the aldo‐keto reductase family that has been reported to be overexpressed in various cancers (including LUSC) and to promote cancer progression. By analyzing TCGA database, we found that AKR1B10 expression was significantly higher in LUSC tissues compared to LUAD and normal lung tissues (Figure 6A). The Kaplan−Meier survival analysis revealed that NSCLC patients with lower AKR1B10 expression had significantly better overall survival compared to those with higher AKR1B10 expression (p < 0.001; Figure 6B). These results suggested that AKR1B10 might play an oncogenic role in LUSC. After further querying TCGA database, a negative correlation was found between NKX2‐1 and AKR1B10 expressions in both LUAD and LUSC tissues (Figure 6C), suggesting that NKX2‐1 might suppress the transcription of AKR1B10. To confirm this finding, we assessed the impact of NKX2‐1 overexpression on AKR1B10 expression in SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520 cell lines. The qRT‐PCR analysis showed that NKX2‐1 overexpression significantly inhibited the mRNA expression of AKR1B10 in both cell lines (Figure 6D). Consistently, western blot analysis demonstrated a marked reduction in AKR1B10 protein levels upon NKX2‐1 overexpression (Figure 6E). To further investigate the mechanism by which NKX2‐1 suppresses AKR1B10 transcription, we analyzed the promoter sequence of AKR1B10 using the JASPAR database. A conserved interaction domain (CACTTGA) of NKX2‐1 was predicted in the promoter sequence (−394 to −388 bp) of AKR1B10 (Figure 6F). To confirm the functional significance of this interaction domain, we performed a dual‐luciferase assay. The results showed that the suppressive effect of NKX2‐1 on AKR1B10 promoter activity diminished when the conserved interaction domain sequence was altered (Figure 6G). These findings suggested that NKX2‐1 suppressed the transcription of AKR1B10 by directly binding to its promoter sequence.

Inhibition of the (D) mRNA expression and (E) protein expression of AKR1B10 in LUSC cell lines by NKX2‐1 overexpression. (F) Predicted conserved interaction domain in the promoter sequence of AKR1B10. (G) Verification of the conserved interaction domain by dual‐luciferase assay. n = 3, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

NKX2‐1 Overexpression Impaired the Proliferation and Migration Abilities of LUSC Cell Lines in an AKR1B10‐Dependent Manner

To investigate whether the tumor‐suppressive effects of NKX2‐1 in LUSC were mediated by AKR1B10, we performed rescue experiments by overexpressing AKR1B10 in NKX2‐1‐overexpressing SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520 cells. The overexpression efficiency of NKX2‐1 and AKR1B10 was confirmed by qRT‐PCR and western blot analysis (Figure 7A,B). Next, we assessed the impact of NKX2‐1 overexpression on cell proliferation, with and without AKR1B10 overexpression. According to the CCK8 assay, colony formation assay, and EdU incorporation assay, NKX2‐1 overexpression significantly impaired the proliferation ability of SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520 cells (Figure 7C−E), but this inhibitory effect was partially reversed when AKR1B10 was co‐overexpressed. Hence, NKX2‐1's suppressive effect on proliferation must be at least partly dependent on AKR1B10.

NKX2‐1 overexpression impaired the proliferation and migration abilities of LUSC cells in an AKR1B10‐dependent manner. Verification of the overexpression efficiency of NKX2‐1 and AKR1B10 by (A) qRT‐PCR and (B) western blot analysis. Analysis of the cell proliferation ability by (C) CCK8 assay, (D) colony formation assay, and (E) EdU incorporation assay, scale bar = 20 μm. n = 3, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

We then examined the influence of NKX2‐1 overexpression on cell migration and invasion abilities. Wound healing assays demonstrated that NKX2‐1 overexpression markedly reduced the migration ability of SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520 cells (Figure 8A). Similarly, Transwell invasion assays showed that NKX2‐1 overexpression significantly impaired the invasion abilities of these cells (Figure 8B). Again, the inhibitory effects of NKX2‐1 on migration and invasion were partially reversed by AKR1B10 co‐overexpression, highlighting the AKR1B10‐dependent nature of NKX2‐1's actions. Finally, we evaluated the effect of NKX2‐1 overexpression on the tumorigenic potential of LUSC cells in vivo using a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model. SK‐MES‐1 cells overexpressing NKX2‐1, with or without AKR1B10 co‐overexpression, were injected subcutaneously into mice. The tumors generated from cells overexpressing NKX2‐1 had significantly smaller size and weight compared to control (Figure 9A−C). The reduction in tumor size and weight was partially reversed when AKR1B10 was co‐overexpressed, which implied that the tumor suppressive effects of NKX2‐1 took place via an AKR1B10‐dependent mechanism.

NKX2‐1 overexpression impaired the migration and invasion abilities in LUSC cells in an AKR1B10‐dependent manner. (A) Wound healing assay, scale bar = 100 μm. (B) Transwell invasion assay, scale bar = 100 μm. n = 3, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Collectively, these results demonstrated that NKX2‐1 restricted the proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumorigenic potential of LUSC cells in an AKR1B10‐dependent manner, and it could be a potential therapeutic target for LUSC.

Discussion

LUSC represents a significant challenge in oncology due to its aggressive nature, poor prognosis, and limited treatment options [6]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying LUSC progression is crucial for the development of novel therapeutic strategies. In this study, we investigated the role of NKX2‐1, a transcription factor previously implicated in lung cancer, in LUSC. Our findings reveal that NKX2‐1 acts as a tumor suppressor in LUSC, inhibiting cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumorigenicity through the transcriptional suppression of AKR1B10.

The role of NKX2‐1 in lung cancer has been controversial. Some studies suggest that it acts as an oncogene in LUAD, and others argue that it may have tumor‐suppressive functions [12]. Our analysis of TCGA data revealed that NKX2‐1 expression is significantly lower in LUSC tissues compared to LUAD and normal lung tissues. This finding is consistent with a previous report showing reduced NKX2‐1 expression in LUSC compared to other lung cancer subtypes. The lower expression of NKX2‐1 in LUSC correlated with decreased survival rates, suggesting that NKX2‐1 might function as a tumor suppressor in this context. The tumor‐suppressive role of NKX2‐1 in LUSC was further supported by functional assays. For both SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520 cells, NKX2‐1 overexpression significantly inhibited cell proliferation, as evidenced by CCK8, colony formation, and EdU incorporation assays. These findings are in line with previous studies showing that NKX2‐1 can inhibit cell growth in various cancer cell lines. Additionally, NKX2‐1 overexpression impaired the migration and invasion abilities of the LUSC cells, as demonstrated by wound healing and Transwell assays. These results indicate that NKX2‐1 not only affects cell proliferation but also plays a crucial role in regulating cell motility and invasiveness, which are key processes in cancer metastasis. The tumor‐suppressive effects of NKX2‐1 were also observed in vivo using a subcutaneous xenograft model. The tumors generated from NKX2‐1‐overexpressing SK‐MES‐1 cells grew significantly slower and were smaller in size and weight compared to the control tumors. These findings provide compelling evidence for the tumor‐suppressive role of NKX2‐1 in LUSC and suggest that restoring NKX2‐1 expression or activity could be a potential therapeutic strategy.

To gain insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the tumor‐suppressive effects of NKX2‐1 in LUSC, we used the TCGA database to analyze the gene expression profiles of LUSC tissues with high and low NKX2‐1 expression. The hierarchical clustering analysis revealed distinct gene expression patterns between the two groups, and a significant number of genes were expressed differentially. Hence, NKX2‐1 must play a crucial role in regulating gene expression in LUSC. The GO biological process enrichment analysis showed that the dysregulated genes were enriched in various processes related to cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. These results are consistent with the functional assays demonstrating that NKX2‐1 overexpression inhibited these processes in LUSC cells. Additionally, the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the dysregulated genes were involved in several signaling pathways, including the PI3K‐Akt signaling pathway. The PI3K‐Akt pathway is known to play a crucial role in cancer progression, regulating cell proliferation, survival, and motility [19]. Our findings suggested that NKX2‐1 might exert its tumor‐suppressive effects by modulating the expression of genes involved in this pathway.

One of the most significant findings of this study is the identification of AKR1B10 as a downstream target of NKX2‐1 in LUSC. AKR1B10 is a member of the aldo‐keto reductase family that has been reported to be overexpressed in various cancers, including LUSC, and to promote cancer progression [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. Our analysis of TCGA data revealed that AKR1B10 expression is significantly higher in LUSC tissues compared to LUAD and normal lung tissues, and patients with lower AKR1B10 expression have significantly better overall survival. These results suggest that AKR1B10 plays an oncogenic role in LUSC [26, 27, 28]. We observed a negative correlation between NKX2‐1 and AKR1B10 expression in both LUAD and LUSC tissues, suggesting that NKX2‐1 might suppress the transcription of AKR1B10. This finding was confirmed by qRT‐PCR and Western blot analyses, which showed that NKX2‐1 overexpression significantly inhibited the mRNA and protein expressions of AKR1B10 in LUSC cell lines. These results indicated that NKX2‐1 negatively regulated AKR1B10 expression at the transcriptional level. To further investigate the mechanism by which NKX2‐1 suppresses AKR1B10 transcription, we analyzed the promoter sequence of AKR1B10 using the JASPAR database. A conserved interaction domain (CACTTGA) of NKX2‐1 was predicted in the promoter sequence (−394 to −388 bp) of AKR1B10. Dual‐luciferase assays confirmed the functional significance of this interaction domain, as the mutant sequence significantly decreased the suppressive effect of NKX2‐1 on AKR1B10 promoter activity. These findings suggested that NKX2‐1 suppressed the transcription of AKR1B10 by directly binding to its promoter sequence.

To investigate whether the tumor‐suppressive effects of NKX2‐1 in LUSC are mediated by AKR1B10, we performed rescue experiments by overexpressing AKR1B10 in NKX2‐1‐overexpressing SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520 cells. The results showed that the inhibitory effects of NKX2‐1 on cell proliferation, migration, and invasion were partially reversed by AKR1B10 co‐overexpression. These findings indicated that NKX2‐1's suppressive effects on these processes were at least partly dependent on AKR1B10. The AKR1B10‐dependent mechanism of NKX2‐1's tumor‐suppressive effects was further supported by in vivo experiments. The tumors generated from SK‐MES‐1 cells overexpressing NKX2‐1 were significantly smaller and weighed less compared to control tumors. However, the reduction in tumor size and weight was partially reversed when AKR1B10 was co‐overexpressed. These results provide compelling evidence that NKX2‐1 restricted the tumorigenic potential of LUSC cells in an AKR1B10‐dependent manner. AKR1B10 has been implicated in various biological processes that contribute to cancer progression, including cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis. The enzymatic activity of AKR1B10 can catalyze the reduction of aldehydes and ketones, leading to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the activation of signaling pathways that promote cancer cell survival and proliferation. Therefore, the transcriptional suppression of AKR1B10 by NKX2‐1 could inhibit these processes, thereby exerting tumor‐suppressive effects.

Our findings have significant implications for novel LUSC therapeutic strategies. Restoring NKX2‐1 expression could inhibit cancer proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumorigenicity through approaches like gene therapy, epigenetic modulation, or AKR1B10 inhibition. Viral vector‐mediated gene delivery may reintroduce NKX2‐1, though its efficiency and safety require rigorous evaluation. Epigenetic modulation using FDA‐approved agents—such as DNA methyltransferase inhibitors (e.g., Decitabine) [29] or histone deacetylase inhibitors (e.g., Vorinostat) [30, 31] —could reactivate NKX2‐1 expression silenced in LUSC. For AKR1B10 inhibition, Epalrestat has shown promise in overcoming chemoresistance in NSCLC patient‐derived organoids, while other inhibitors like Fidarestat and Zopolrestat demonstrate preclinical efficacy. However, inhibitor selectivity and toxicity necessitate further validation. Beyond single targets, combination therapies (e.g., PI3K‐Akt inhibitors with NKX2‐1 reactivation or AKR1B10 blockade) could overcome resistance and enhance efficacy.

This study has several limitations. Functionally, findings were constrained to two LUSC cell lines (SK‐MES‐1 and NCI‐H520), and validation in additional models such as patient‐derived organoids or xenografts would help establish broader relevance. Mechanistically, the absence of ChIP‐seq/qPCR data precludes direct demonstration of NKX2‐1 binding at the AKR1B10 promoter. Clinically, while public data sets (TCGA) were leveraged, validation in local patient cohorts is pending. A study of NKX2‐1 expression in an institutional LUSC tissue cohort is underway.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the role of NKX2‐1 in LUSC and found that it acted as a tumor suppressor. Our analysis of TCGA data revealed that NKX2‐1 expression is significantly lower in LUSC tissues compared to LUAD and normal lung tissues, and its low expression correlates with poor patient survival. Functional assays demonstrated that NKX2‐1 overexpression inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of LUSC cells in vitro. In a xenograft model, NKX2‐1 overexpression reduced the tumorigenic potential of LUSC cells. The transcriptomic analysis identified AKR1B10 as a key gene dysregulated in LUSC tissues with low NKX2‐1 expression, and AKR1B10 was negatively correlated with NKX2‐1 expression. Mechanistic studies showed that NKX2‐1 suppressed AKR1B10 transcription through direct binding to its promoter. Since AKR1B10 overexpression partially reversed the inhibition of cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumorigenic potential by NKX2‐1, the tumor‐suppressive effects of NKX2‐1 might depend on AKR1B10. Collectively, our findings suggested that NKX2‐1 restricted tumor growth and metastasis in LUSC by transcriptionally repressing AKR1B10. These results provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying LUSC progression and highlight NKX2‐1 and AKR1B10 as potential therapeutic targets for improving clinical outcomes in LUSC patients.
