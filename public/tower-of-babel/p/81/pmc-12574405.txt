Mapping fine-scale spatial risk patterns of gestational diabetes over time in Australia: a nationwide geospatial study
Authors: Wubet Worku Takele, Siew Lim, Kiki Adhinugraha, David Taniar, Lachlan L Dalli, Jacqueline A Boyle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the geospatial distribution of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) over time in Australia.DesignAn ecological study was conducted using data from the National Diabetes Services Scheme (GDM cases). Data at Statistical Area Level 2 (SA2) level, a medium-sized spatial unit, on population denominators (women who gave birth) were obtained from the Australian Bureau of Statistics. The spatiotemporal distribution of GDM was explored at the SA2 level over three periods: 2016–2017, 2018–2019 and 2020–2021. Hotspot and cluster analyses were undertaken using Getis-Ord Gi* and local Moran’s I statistics.SettingA nationwide study in Australia was conducted between 2016 and 2021.ParticipantsWomen diagnosed with GDM and those who gave birth were included.Outcome measuresAge-standardised and crude incidence of GDM per SA2.ResultsDuring 2016–2021, 1 718 963 eligible women who gave birth in Australia were included. Hotspot areas of GDM were consistently observed in Victoria (Southwest and North Melbourne); Western Australia (South and Southwest Perth); Australian Capital Territory (ACT) (East and North Canberra); Queensland (North Brisbane) and New South Wales (West and Southwest Sydney and Southeast New South Wales). ACT (South Canberra), North Tasmania, Northern Territory (North Darwin) and Victoria (South East Melbourne) had new hotspot regions recorded in the last two consecutive study periods.ConclusionGDM incidence varies by geographical area over time, with hotspots in specific regions suggesting the need for geographically targeted policy interventions to curb the growing burden of GDM.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a metabolic disorder that occurs during pregnancy, increasing the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, such as pre-eclampsia,1 birth asphyxia2 and labour induction that leads to various obstetric complications, including caesarean delivery.3 Despite the significant efforts focused on research and clinical care, the global incidence of GDM is increasing rapidly. The International Diabetes Federation in 2021 reported that 20 million pregnant women had developed GDM.4 In Australia, in 2021/2022, 19.3% of pregnant women were diagnosed with GDM, up from 15.1% in 2016/2017, with differences noted across states and territories.5

GDM imposes significant long-term maternal and child health complications and economic burdens on the healthcare system.6 In Australia, the mean cost of managing GDM and its associated neonatal and obstetric complications was reported to be $3200 per individual in 2013.7 Women with a history of GDM have an increased risk of developing GDM in a subsequent pregnancy and type 2 diabetes.8 Furthermore, newborns of women are also more likely to develop metabolic syndrome later in their lives,9 setting up intergenerational metabolic disorders.

Multilevel socioecological factors contribute to the risk of GDM across areas. Studies in other countries show that the distribution of non-communicable chronic diseases, including diabetes in pregnancy, differs across areas, clustering in socioeconomically disadvantaged and less supportive neighbourhoods for healthy lifestyle (eg, less walkable areas).10 11 Other reasons for geographical variation in healthy lifestyle practices may relate to a lack of culturally responsive health promotion resources and health services for diverse population groups.12 Although the authors of previous studies have highlighted differences in the geographical distribution of diseases, including GDM,10 more contextual data are needed at finer geographical scales. Such an approach would enhance the identification of high-risk areas, enabling relevant policy to support the design and implementation of appropriate healthcare and prevention interventions equitably and efficiently.13 14 However, the distribution of GDM over time in Australia has not been examined beyond the state and territory level.

This study, therefore, aims to identify the geographical and temporal distribution of GDM in Australia at the granular geographic level, ‘Statistical Area Level 2 (SA2)’, from 2016 to 2021. This study aligns with the WHO’s recommendation to identify spatially clustered high-risk areas of non-communicable diseases to target interventions.15 The findings of the study may offer valuable insights into tailored, location-specific prevention interventions designed to mitigate the risk of GDM and subsequent cardiometabolic diseases.

Method and materials

Study design and context

We undertook a nationwide ecological study in Australia from 2016 to 2021. Australia has eight states/territories, each with a capital city (online supplemental figure S1). In Australia, geographical analysis is commonly conducted at structurally segmented administrative units defined by the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) called statistical areas (SAs).16 The first level, SA1, is the most granular, representing 200–800 people, whereas SA4 is the least granular level, with ≥100 000 people.16 This study was conducted at the SA2 level, a medium-sized area representing 3000–25 000 people, with the greatest population in regional towns and metropolitan cities.16 The number of SA2s is revised every 5 years (census year) to incorporate new areas, and there were no changes during our study period. The number of people in each SA varies according to the level of remoteness. Online supplemental figure S1 illustrates states/territories and major cities sectioned by SA2.

Data source and study population

Numerator data on GDM

Area-level data on women aged between 15 and 49 with GDM were obtained from the National Diabetes Services Scheme (NDSS) based on enrolments between 2016 and 2021. GDM is a form of diabetes first detected during pregnancy, typically at 24–28 weeks, with early screening undertaken in women having significant risk factors for GDM (women with one high risk factor, eg, body mass index (BMI)>35 kg/m2 or previous GDM or two moderate risk factors [eg, ethnicity and BMI 25–35 kg/m2]). GDM screening and diagnosis were based on the 2013 guidelines of the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Group, which was endorsed by the Australasian Diabetes in Pregnancy Society in 2014. This guideline recommends universal screening with a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)17 and fasting blood glucose (≥5.1 mmol/L) or 1-hour glucose (≥10.0 mmol/L) or 2-hour glucose (≥8.5 mmol/L) is diagnostic of GDM. This was implemented across most states/territories by mid-2016.18 Additionally, during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020), a temporary change was made to the screening and diagnostic guidelines for GDM, recommending a two-step approach based on the risk of infection transmission.19

Women who are diagnosed with GDM are referred to the NDSS programme with the help of health professionals. NDSS was launched in 1987 by Diabetes Australia to support people living with diabetes. The programme provides informational materials and subsidised medical equipment, including a glucometer. NDSS is Australia’s most comprehensive data source, capturing up to 90% of all types of diabetes in Australia.20 We chose NDSS as a data source because it enables us to explore the distribution of GDM at a granular geographic level (ie, SA2), unlike other available data sources.

Allocation of GDM cases to SA2

Women were aggregated by residential postcodes provided during enrolment to the NDSS programme. The GDM cases aggregated by postcodes were mapped to SA2 using the corresponding document developed by ABS.21 Online supplemental text S1 and figure S2 provide a detailed procedure and description of the data allocation process. Due to privacy, we excluded GDM cases under six per SA2. Additionally, 18 non-spatial special-purpose codes, comprising Migratory–Offshore–Shipping and SA2s with no usual address, were excluded.22

Denominator data on completed pregnancies

Denominator data on the number of women who gave birth in Australia in each SA2 from 2016 to 2021 were obtained from ABS.23 ABS reports annual births by usual residence (eg, SA2 and local government areas) and sociodemographic characteristics, such as mother’s age and country of birth, which are obtained from each state and territory.24 Each state and territory collects all births that occur in the respective hospitals and clinics.24

The 2016 Australian Statistical Geography Standard (ASGS) digital geographic boundary shapefile (geometric location file) by SA2 boundary was obtained from the ABS.25 The data were grouped into three periods: study period 1 (2016–2017), study period 2 (2018–2019), and study period 3 (2020–2021). The reason for aggregating the 6 years of data into three periods is to minimise bias (attributable to sparse and small counts per area) in estimating the incidence of GDM at the small geographical level, SA2.

Variables of the study

Outcome variable

The outcome variable was the crude and age-standardised incidence of GDM, estimated as the number of GDM cases at each SA2 level divided by the corresponding number of women who gave birth in the corresponding SA2s.

Measurement of sociodemographic characteristics

The crude and age-standardised rates of GDM across states/territories, socioeconomic status and remoteness were compared. The 2016 Australian census-based Index of Relative Socioeconomic Advantage and Disadvantage (IRSAD) data at the SA2 level was accessed from ABS.26 The scores were categorised into quintiles, with the increasing quintiles indicating greater relative socioeconomic advantage.26

Data on remoteness were sourced from the ASGS. The extent of remoteness is ascertained by the road distance from various populated locations to public centres and amenities, using the Accessibility/Remoteness Index of Australia Plus (ARIA+) and population as a proxy measure for service availability.22 There are five remoteness classes based on the ARIA scores, namely ‘major cities’ (ARIA=0–0.2), ‘inner region’, ‘outer region’, ‘remote’ and ‘very remote’ (ARIA≥10.53).22 ‘Remote’ and ‘very remote’ areas were excluded because of potential identifiability and data variability. This is because the participation of women residing in remote areas in the NDSS programme is limited.27 Including these areas would result in an underestimation of the incidence and misclassification of hot and cold spot areas, likely labelling those areas as cold spots falsely.

Furthermore, women’s ethnicity was classified based on their self-reported country of birth to the NDSS, in accordance with the Australian Standard Classification of Cultural and Ethnic Groups,28 as described in our previous work.29 ‘Migrant’ women in this study refer to the non-European women, mainly those who were born in Asia, Africa and the Middle East, who have an increased risk of GDM.18

Data analysis

Descriptive summary and geographic analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed, and differences in proportions across demographic characteristics were evaluated using χ2 tests. We used R (V.4.3.0) and ArcGIS (V.10.8) for the spatiotemporal analysis. The 2016 Environmental Systems Research Institute digital boundary shapefile (geometric location file) of Australia, formatted at the SA2 level, was used for mapping. The incidence of GDM was classified into five classes based on the natural breaks (Jenks) method.30 Jenks classification is preferred over others as it provides an accurate classification by minimising the deviation of values within a class while maximising differences between classes.31 Areas that fall in the fifth class (upper class) indicate ‘high risk’ of GDM.

Spatial cluster and hotspot analysis

Crude and age-standardised incidence of GDM per SA2 were estimated to identify hot and cold spot areas. Age standardisation was performed using 5-year age bands for GDM cases and population denominator data accessed from the ABS. We merged the age group above 45 years with the 40–45 age range due to the small population counts per SA2. The Moran’s I spatial statistics was applied to examine spatial clustering across areas. A significant Moran’s I statistic finding (p value<0.05) indicated that the distribution of GDM in Australia is not random.32 The ‘hotspot’ (significant clusters of high values) and ‘cold spot’ (significant clusters of low values) areas of GDM were identified based on the Getis-Ord Gi* statistics.33 The spatial weighting matrix was constructed using the contiguity edges corner spatial conceptualisation method.34 The contiguity edges corner method assumes that polygons (areas) sharing a border or a corner are considered neighbours. Thus, all neighbouring areas are included during the computation of a target polygon when determining spatial ‘hotspot’ and clusters.34 The ‘hotspot’ and ‘cold spot’ status of areas was determined using the z-score and the corresponding p values and confidence level (Gi-Bin, ranging from −3 to +3) determined using both crude and age-standardised incidence of GDM per SA2.35 A higher positive significant (p value<0.05) z-score with surrounding areas of high scores indicates a ‘hotspot’. Similarly, areas with lower negative significant z-scores surrounded by areas of similarly low values represent a ‘cold spot’.35 The confidence level ranged from 90% to 99%. For this study, significant ‘hotspot’ and ‘cold spot’ areas were reported if the confidence level was ≥95%, with p≤0.05.

The local pattern of GDM risk was further examined using Anselin’s Local Indicator of Spatial Autocorrelation (LISA) test.36 This method enables the identification of spatial clusters with areas of high values surrounded by high values (High–High), low values surrounded by low values (Low–Low) and spatial outliers surrounded by low values (High–Low) or low values surrounded by high values (Low–High). The LISA approach complements the evidence from global Moran’s I by identifying statistically significant specific locations of clusters and outliers at local level.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not directly involved in the research process, which involved deidentified and aggregated data from the NDSS, Diabetes Australia and the ABS. We acknowledge the contribution of patients and members of the public in these national data collection programmes.

Results

Description of the included women who gave birth over the study period

Excluding the GDM cases from remote/very remote areas and cases<6 per SA2, 1830, 1903 and 1954 SA2 were included in study periods 1, 2 and 3, respectively (online supplemental figure S3). A total of 1 718 963 women (n=570 231 in 2016–2017, n=579 137 in 2018–2019 and n=569 595 in 2020–2021) who gave birth across states/territories of Australia from 2016 to 2021 were included (online supplemental figure S3). The SA2s with≥6 cases of GDM ranged from 1830 in 2016–2017 to 1954 in 2020–2021. The highest number of SA2s and GDM cases included in the study was from New South Wales, followed by the state of Victoria (table 1). The highest percentage (36.1%) was among those aged 30–34 years old, while only 2.2% fell within the 15–19 age range.

Spatial pattern of socioeconomic status

Area-level socioeconomic status of women varied across regions. The most socioeconomically disadvantaged areas were in Melbourne (North, Southeast and Southwest), Adelaide (North and South), South and North Brisbane and South Perth. Conversely, inner locations of metropolitans, such as Melbourne, Sydney, Brisbane and significant areas of the Australian Capital Territory (ACT), were the least disadvantaged areas (online supplemental figure S4A–H).

Hotspot and clustering of GDM by study period

Study period 1: 2016–2017

Based on the Jenks classification method using the age-standardised incidence of GDM, areas categorised at ‘higher risk’ in the first study period ranged from 27.6% to 46.3%. Online supplemental figure S5 illustrates the geographical variation of GDM in major cities using age-standardised rates of GDM. Moran’s I test suggested a clustered GDM risk in 2016–2017 (Moran’s I: p<0.01; online supplemental table S1). Hotspot areas were identified in North and East Canberra (figure 1D), the majority of North Brisbane (figure 1G), Southwest and North Melbourne (figure 2E), South Perth (figure 2G), North Tasmania, vast areas of South and Southwest Sydney (figure 2J) and Northwest Sydney (figure 1J). In contrast, North and outer South Perth (figure 2G) and inner Southeast Melbourne (figure 2E) were cold spot areas.

Study period 2: 2018–2019

During this study period, areas with high incidence had an age-standardised incidence of GDM ranging from 25.8% to 50.5% (online supplemental figure S6), indicating that the cut-off point was higher than in the preceding study period. Maps and interpretations using the incidence of GDM across areas in this study period are provided in Online supplemental figure S5 and text S3. The distribution of GDM in this period was clustered in some areas, similar to 2016–2017 (Global Moran’s I, p<0.01; online supplemental table S1). The hotspot areas were identified in South, North and East Canberra (figure 1E), North Brisbane (figure 1H), Southwest and North Melbourne (figure 2E), South Perth (figure 2H), Southwest Sydney (figure 2K) and North Tasmania.

New hotspot areas were detected in almost every area of the ACT (figure 1E), Northeast Adelaide (figure 1B), South Brisbane (figure 1H), North Darwin (figure 1K) and Southeast Melbourne (figure 2E). Some areas of West and Southwest Sydney (figure 2K) were no longer significant hotspots in this study period. North Perth, outer South Perth (figure 2H) and inner Southeast Melbourne (figure 2E) remained cold spot areas.

Study period 3: 2020–2021

Areas categorised as higher risk in this study period had an age-standardised incidence of GDM ranging from 28.9% to 64.9% (online supplemental figure S7), reflecting an increase in average percentage changes of 1.4%–17.5% relative to the previous study period. The geographical distribution of GDM in this study period was also not random (Moran’s I, p value<0.01, online supplemental table S1). High-risk areas were detected in North and East Canberra (figure 1F), North Brisbane (figure 1I), Southwest, Southeast and North Melbourne (figure 2F), South and Southwest Perth (figure 2I) and West and Southwest Sydney (figure 2L). Additional hotspot areas emerged in North Darwin (figure 1L), North and Southeast Melbourne (figure 2F) and South Perth (figure 2I). Similar to the previous study period, North and outer South Perth (figure 2I) and inner Southeast Melbourne (figure 2F) remained cold spot areas.

Additional analysis using crude incidence of GDM indicates similar hotspot regions as identified using age-standardised incidence (online supplemental figures S8 and S9). Further cluster and outlier analysis using the LISA method (online supplemental figures S10 and S11) supports the findings of the hotspot analysis stated above. Consistent High–High clusters were identified in Canberra (North, South and East) (online supplemental figure S9), Melbourne (North, Southeast and West) and Southwest Sydney (online supplemental figure S10).

In a further analysis, a high percentage of non-European migrant women (those born in Southeast Asia, South and Central Asia and the Middle East and North Africa) with GDM were identified in particular regions of Australian major cities (online supplemental figure S12A–F). For example, a high percentage (up to 80%) of migrant women with GDM was observed in Southwest and Southeast Melbourne (online supplemental figure S12D).

Discussion

In our contemporary and multisource area-level data, we identified consistent hotspot areas over time in Australia, including in Victoria (Southwest and North Melbourne), Queensland (North Brisbane), Western Australia (Southwest and South Perth), NSW (West and Southwest Sydney) and significant parts of the ACT (North and East Canberra). Conversely, some regions of Victoria (inner Southeast Melbourne) and Western Australia (North and outer South Perth) were consistent cold spots over time. The observed variations across areas could be associated with a wide range of factors, including variations in social determinants of health (SDoH), geographical variations in the prevalence of women living with overweight or obesity and risk related to the country of birth.

Most areas identified in our study as persistent hotspots of GDM are predominantly classified as socioeconomically disadvantaged, whereas the consistent cold spots are mostly areas of lesser disadvantage.26 This disparity in the incidence of GDM by socioeconomic status has been reported previously in Australia and other high-income countries.12 37 38 A spatial report in Australia in 2017–2018 showed high prevalence of type 2 diabetes in women in areas identified as hotspots of GDM in the current study.39 The commonly used area-level socioeconomic measurement (IRSAD) in the Australian context encompasses broad and key components of SDoH, including educational status, income level, employment and access to community services and goods within specific geographic areas.26 These key components of SDoH may influence a population’s healthy lifestyle promotion behaviour differently.12 40 For example, individuals of lower socioeconomic status may not have the opportunity to maintain healthy lifestyle behaviour,40 41 possibly associated with financial insecurity affecting the ability to access health services, healthy food and organised physical activity.4244 Low education may impact health literacy and knowledge of preventive health behaviours. A combination of these factors can increase the risk of having a BMI in the overweight or obese range, further increasing the risk of GDM.45

Geographical variations in the prevalence of women living with overweight or obese (>25 kg/m²) may contribute to the observed spatial variation in GDM. A national area-level survey in 2017–2018 at the population health area level (comparable with SA2) demonstrated that the majority of persistent hotspot areas of GDM in this current study exhibited a high prevalence of women with a BMI of>30 kg/m2.39 For example, in hotspot areas in Queensland (North Brisbane) and Victoria (Southwest and Southeast Melbourne), approximately 70% of adult (>18 years) women had a BMI of>30 kg/m2. Conversely, the national spatial survey reported some locations with relatively low prevalence of obesity corresponding with cold spot areas of GDM (eg, inner Southeast Melbourne). The spatial variations in the prevalence of women living with overweight and obesity may be associated with the limited accessibility to physical activity environment (eg, walkability) and healthy food outlets.46 Overweight or obesity is a known risk factor for GDM, possibly through increasing insulin resistance linked to several mechanisms, including the high expression of adipokines and low-grade inflammation.47

The geographic variation in the risk of GDM may also be explained by differences noted by country of birth between areas. In most GDM hotspot areas, there was a greater percentage of migrant women born in countries from Southeast Asia, South and Central Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. This pattern was significantly observed in Victoria (Southwest, North and Southeast Melbourne), North Canberra and NSW (Southwest Sydney). This could reflect that the country of birth of residents overall in these areas are hotspots of GDM, while conversely cold spot areas of inner Southeast Melbourne, North and outer South Perth were predominantly born in low-risk countries, mainly Australia. It is well established that women born overseas, such as in South and Central Asia (eg, India), are at higher risk of developing GDM.18 Diabetes risk differs by ethnicity due to genetics, family history, physiological or metabolic responses to the ‘Western’ diet among migrants, chronic stress, lifestyle behaviours, exposure to environmental pollutants in the country of origin and others.4852 Further geospatial studies examining the association between high percentages of migrant women and GDM risk may be crucial.

Beyond the sociodemographic factors, it is also important to acknowledge the role of environment-related barriers contributing to GDM risk, such as limited access to sports and recreation centres and healthy food stores. There are still areas with socioeconomically least disadvantaged (eg, significant areas of the ACT) (online supplemental figure S4C), yet consistently hotspots of GDM. This suggests that other unmeasured environmental factors may affect the risk of GDM. Studies from other countries have shown that environmental neighbourhood factors, such as walkability and food environment, explain the geographical variation of GDM and diabetes in the general population, independent of area-level socioeconomic factors. However, the role of environment-related factors in the geographical variation of GDM in Australia is unknown and requires future investigation.

The findings of this study, particularly at the most granular geographic locations over time, have significant implications for health promotion services targeted at persistently high-risk areas. Most importantly, the findings facilitate policy implementations and provide evidence for tailored interventions. In this regard, the choropleth maps that show the risk of GDM over time and disadvantaged areas in this study could guide programmers in designing public health responses in the most affected areas. The study also provides opportunities for future research to understand the risks and needs of women in hotspot areas. For example, identifying cultural needs in health promotion and resources, culturally responsive healthcare and culturally safe spaces for physical activity are essential.53 Future spatial studies that involve ecological regression approaches at a granular geographic level are required to understand the role of environmental factors in the risk of GDM in persistently significant hotspot areas.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study to investigate the geospatial distribution of GDM over time in Australia at a more granular geographic level. Other strengths include the use of a large, up-to-date and representative sample of women with GDM across all states and territories of Australia. However, caution should be exercised when generalising our findings due to limitations. Data on GDM were sourced from a single registry (NDSS), which relies on voluntary participation, potentially underestimating the incidence of GDM and limiting its ability to reflect the risk in certain population groups. For example, First Nations people, particularly in remote communities, are less likely to be screened for GDM due to personal and logistical reasons and potentially less likely to be notified to the NDSS programme if there were alternative diabetes supplies through the Aboriginal Medical Services.27 However, NDSS is generally presumed to be the most comprehensive national data source, covering up to 90% of all diabetes forms.20 Similarly, there may be under-reporting of First Nations women who gave birth, which could impact the incidence of GDM by reducing the denominator population. However, this may have a negligible impact on the incidence of GDM per SA2, given the small percentage (3.8% in 2021) of First Nations people in the total Australian population, and most of them reside in remote areas, where the findings of our study are not inferred.54 In addition, the incidence may be minimally impacted if there is under-reporting in both numerator and denominator data. Furthermore, women with GDM are at increased risk of experiencing stillbirths and pregnancy losses, and these are not included in the denominator population. This may inflate the incidence, although fewer than 1% of pregnancies in Australia in 2021 were stillborn, regardless of maternal age or complications during pregnancy.55 Although in most cases, the cold spot areas in our study are located in inner areas of major cities where the representation of women in the NDSS programme is expected to be higher, cold spot determinations in some areas (eg, outer regions and areas of high percentage of non-European migrant women) may be impacted by the ‘false low’ GDM case reports to NDSS. The exclusion of remote and very remote areas, where the cases are poorly reported to the NDSS, may have minimised the bias. Conversely, excluding these areas would have impacted the overall incidence of GDM in Australia, which serves as a comparator in determining cold and hotspot areas. However, this may again have a minimal impact due to the low percentage of the population (2%) residing in remote and very remote areas in Australia in 2023.56 Another limitation is that, although there are guidelines for diagnosing pre-existing diabetes in pregnancy based on screening results for GDM,57 there may be misclassification during pregnancy as GDM that would lead to an over-reporting of GDM cases. However, the overall numbers at the SA2 level would be small to impact the incidence of GDM.

A one-step diagnostic approach for the diagnosis of GDM was introduced in 2014 and was mostly implemented in 2016 when this study commenced.18 However, there may still have been some variations in implementation across areas during the study period that could account for the observed GDM risk variations. There was also a temporary change to the GDM diagnosis guideline during the COVID-19 pandemic that recommended a two-step process, mostly with fasting glucose with or without a subsequent OGTT at 24–28 weeks.19 There were variations in the use of this updated guideline across health services nationally.58 For women with risk factors, screening in early pregnancy has changed from an OGTT to HbA1c.19 Studies comparing the differences in the incidence of GDM before and during/after the pandemic in Australia reported inconsistent changes, and the impact on GDM risk variation is difficult to ascertain.5961

Conclusions

The geographical distribution of GDM varies across small areas over time in Australia. Notably, persistent hotspot areas of GDM were observed in Southwest and North Melbourne, Southwest and South Perth, East and North Canberra, North Brisbane, West and Southwest Sydney and Southeast NSW. New and expanding hotspots were detected in Southeast Melbourne, South Canberra, North Tasmania and North Darwin in the last two consecutive study periods. Geographically targeted GDM policy prevention interventions in specific regions, such as Greater Melbourne (Southwest, North and Southeast) and West and Southwest Sydney, guided by choropleth maps, are warranted. This could benefit women’s health in pregnancy and later cardiometabolic health and have a positive intergenerational impact on diabetes risk. Future studies are recommended to investigate the socioecological risk factors associated with GDM risk across different areas.
