Global burden and between‐country inequalities in Turner syndrome from 1990 to 2021
Authors: Xicui Long, Yong Hu, Mingjie Wu, Lijuan Yang, Wenjiao Jin, Xuesong Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTurner syndrome (TS) seriously impacts women's health and quality of life, yet research on its global burden remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the burden and between‐country inequalities of TS using global population data.Material and MethodsData on prevalence, years lived with disability (YLDs), and incidence of TS were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database for comprehensive analysis. Geographic and socioeconomic disparities were assessed using decomposition analysis, cross‐country health inequality analysis, and frontier analysis. Future trends were projected using an autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model.ResultsIn 2021, TS accounted for an estimated 531 784 (95% UI: 420 532–697 786) prevalent cases, 9177 (95% UI: 4254–15 926) YLDs, and 23 929 (95% UI: 17 948–32 035) incident cases globally. From 1990 to 2021, the numbers of prevalence and YLDs increased by 27.7% and 29.2%, respectively, while their ASRs remained stable. Decomposition analysis indicated that population growth was the main driver of increases in prevalence and YLDs. Persistent between‐country inequalities were observed: prevalence and YLDs were predominantly concentrated in high Socio‐demographic Index (SDI) regions, whereas incidence remained higher in low‐SDI countries. Frontier analysis revealed notable efficiency gaps in high‐SDI countries, while low‐SDI countries were near optimal levels. The ARIMA model projected a steady rise in the absolute number of TS cases from 2021 to 2050, alongside slight declines in age‐standardized prevalence, YLDs, and incidence rates.ConclusionsThe global burden of TS has continued to rise, accompanied by persistent between‐country disparities. These findings highlight the need for improved disease management strategies and more equitable resource allocation to address the growing burden.

Key message

Turner syndrome poses a rising global health burden, largely driven by population growth, with marked disparities between high‐ and low‐SDI regions, underscoring the urgent need for equitable prevention, diagnosis, and care strategies worldwide.

INTRODUCTION

Turner syndrome (TS) is a condition in which a female is partly or completely missing an X chromosome, affecting approximately 25–50 per 100 000 females. 1 , 2 TS can lead to a range of medical and developmental problems, including short height, ovarian dysgenesis, delayed puberty and infertility, congenital anomalies, endocrine abnormalities, learning disabilities, and difficulty with social adjustment. 2 , 3 , 4 Consequently, TS affects multiple organs through all stages of life, profoundly influencing the patient's quality of life and overall well‐being. However, TS is frequently underdiagnosed or diagnosed late, with three peak periods: the prenatal/perinatal stage, ages 5–20, and ages 30–40. 4 Although prenatal screening has advanced, only about 42% of cases are detected prenatally, 5 and the median age at diagnosis remains 15, with some individuals never diagnosed. 6 Further, diagnosis after age 12 is associated with poorer outcomes and increased management challenges. 7 These findings highlight the urgent need to understand the global burden and epidemiological patterns of TS. This knowledge is crucial for clinicians to identify high‐risk age groups, address the challenges of delayed diagnosis, and optimize management strategies. It also informs targeted interventions and health policy planning.

In recent years, the prognosis for TS patients has improved due to most studies focusing on genetic mechanisms, clinical features, and associations, and treatments. 8 , 9 However, the epidemiological characteristics—including incidence, prevalence, and the global distribution and trends of the condition—remain unclear. Furthermore, inconsistencies in data reporting across countries hinder comprehensive understanding of the disease and complicate the accurate assessment of TS burden and the effective allocation of healthcare resources. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database provides a comprehensive framework for assessing disease burden, serving as a critical reference for evidence‐based public health decision‐making. 10 Using data from the GBD database, this study analyzes the global, regional, and national burden of TS from 1990 to 2021, and projects future trends to 2050 to inform healthcare planning and resource allocation.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Data sources and measurements

The GBD 2021 study estimated the burden of 371 diseases and injuries and 88 risk factors from 1990 to 2021 across 21 regions and 204 countries and territories. Data were synthesized from previous GBD iterations, international collaborators, major data sources (e.g., International Household Survey Network, WHO Central Data Catalog), and peer‐reviewed literature. 10 , 11 Using the Global Health Data Exchange (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd‐results/), we conducted a cross‐sectional analysis to extract data on prevalence, incidence, years lived with disability (YLDs), and their age‐standardized rates (ASRs) per 100 000 population at global, regional, and national levels.

Case definition

The GBD definition of TS encompasses International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes Q96.0, Q96.3, and Q96.9. 10

Statistical analysis

Decomposition analysis was used to quantify changes in prevalence, YLDs, and incidence attributable to population growth, population aging, and epidemiological shifts at the global level, across five Socio‐demographic Index (SDI) levels, and within 21 regions. 12 Socioeconomic inequalities in TS burden were assessed using the concentration index of inequality (CII) and slope index of inequality (SII), based on ASRs of prevalence, YLDs, and incidence. 13 Frontier analysis was used to evaluate health system efficiency in managing TS burden across 204 countries and territories; within each SDI category, the “efficiency gap” was defined as the difference between the observed burden and the theoretically minimal burden, representing unrealized health gains. 14 To forecast future trends in TS burden, we employed the Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model, a standard time series approach that accounts for autocorrelation through autoregressive, integrated, and moving average components. 15

All statistical analyses and visualizations were performed using R software (version 4.4.2) and JD_GBDR (V2.37, Jingding Medical Technology Co., Ltd.).

RESULTS

Global, five SDI, and regional burden trends

Globally in 2021, there were 531 783.59 (95% UI, 420 531.66–697 785.82) prevalent cases, 9176.79 (95% UI, 4254.19–15 926.38) YLDs, and 23 928.98 (95% UI, 17 948.41–32 035.34) incident cases of TS (Tables 1 and 2). From 1990 to 2021, the numbers of prevalent cases and YLDs increased by 27.65% and 29.17%, respectively (Tables 1 and 2, Figure 1A,C). In 2021, the age‐standardized prevalence rate was 14.47 (95% UI: 11.39–18.96) per 100 000, and the age‐standardized YLDs rate was 0.25 (95% UI: 0.11–0.43) per 100 000. From 1990 to 2021, the EAPC remained stable at −0.08% (95% CI: −0.09% to −0.06%) for prevalence and −0.09% (95% CI: −0.10% to −0.07%) for YLDs (Tables 1 and 2, Figure 1B,D). Additionally, incidence and its ASRs remained stable during this period (Figure S1).

From 1990 to 2021, the number of prevalent cases and YLDs increased across all SDI regions, except for a slight decline observed in the high‐middle SDI region. Low‐SDI regions experienced the sharpest increase in prevalence, rising from 48 686.42 (95% UI: 37 380.95–63 949.37) cases in 1990 to 99 710.09 (95% UI: 76 892.59–132 682.27) cases in 2021, a 104.80% increase (Table 1, Figure 1A). For YLDs, low‐SDI regions also recorded a 110.14% increase, from 773.51 (95% UI, 348.50–1393.89) to 1625.42 (95% UI: 733.76–2938.54) (Table 2, Figure 1C). During this period, ASRs for prevalence and YLDs remained stable across the five SDI regions (Figure 1B,D). While the number of incident cases increased in low‐SDI regions and decreased in other SDI regions, ASRs remained stable across all five SDI regions (Figure S1).

At the regional levels, South Asia had the highest prevalence in 2021, with 125 177.62 cases (95% UI: 97 525.55–167 397.41). Between 1990 and 2021, Western Sub‐Saharan Africa experienced the largest increase in prevalence (142.38%), from 18 991.04 (95% UI: 14 629.96–25 088.35) to 46 030.78 (95% UI: 35 441.76–61 242.54), whereas Eastern Europe saw the largest decrease (−21.17%), from 21 041.60 (95% UI: 16 706.66–27 563.44) to 16 586.75 (95% UI: 13 210.66–21 635.82) (Table 1). South Asia also had the highest YLDs in 2021 (2148.81; 95% UI: 995.46–3825.54). Over the same period, YLDs increased most in Western Sub‐Saharan Africa and declined most in Eastern Europe (Table 2). Additionally, 64% (16/21) of regions showed a decrease in both incidence numbers and ASRs.

National burden trends

In 2021, the highest absolute numbers of prevalent cases were observed in India (92 625.78; 95% UI: 72 019.93–123 526.82), China (57 261.99; 95% UI: 45 373.46–74 376.46), and the United States (42 081.27; 95% UI: 33 596.82–53 740.41), while the lowest were in Tokelau, Niue, and Tuvalu. The highest age‐standardized prevalence rates occurred in New Zealand (32.11 per 100 000; 95% UI: 25.27–42.04), the United States (30.54 per 100 000; 95% UI: 24.39–39.15), and Belgium (29.06 per 100 000; 95% UI: 22.45–37.65). Decomposition analysis

The decomposition analysis of prevalence revealed a net global increase of 116 683 cases between 1990 and 2021. The 161.12% increase in prevalence (188 005 additional cases) was primarily driven by population growth. In contrast, population aging and epidemiological changes reduced prevalence by 52.96% (−61 799 cases) and 8.16% (−9522 cases), respectively. Across all SDI regions, population growth was the dominant driver. Regional variation was pronounced: South Asia recorded the highest increase (34 236 cases), mainly attributable to population growth (174.08%). Conversely, regions such as Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and the High‐income Asia Pacific showed declines in prevalence, primarily due to the effects of population aging (Figure 5).

In terms of YLDs, a global increase of 2104 cases was observed, also driven mainly by population growth. For incidence, a modest global decrease of 565 cases was recorded. Across the five SDI regions, all but the low‐SDI region showed reductions. At the regional level, the largest increase in incidence occurred in Western Sub‐Saharan Africa, with an estimated rise of 1845 cases, primarily driven by population growth. In contrast, the most significant decline was observed in East Asia, with a reduction of approximately 1439 cases, attributable to both a declining population and improvements in epidemiological factors (Figure S3).

Cross‐country health inequality analysis

The CII of prevalence declined slightly from 0.07 (95% CI: 0.04–0.09) in 1990 to 0.06 (95% CI: 0.04–0.09) in 2021 (Figure 6A), indicating persistent concentration in higher‐income regions. Meanwhile, the SII for prevalence increased from 2.95 (95% CI: 1.19–4.72) in 1990 to 3.28 (95% CI: 1.49–5.07) in 2021 (Figure 6B), suggesting a widening absolute gap in prevalence between low‐ and high‐SDI regions. For YLDs, the CII showed a slight decrease from 0.08 (95% CI: 0.05–0.10) in 1990 to 0.07 (95% CI: 0.05–0.10) in 2021 (Figure 6C), while the SII increased from 0.06 (95% CI: 0.03–0.09) to 0.07 (95% CI: 0.04–0.10) over the same period (Figure 6D), reflecting persistent and slightly widening income‐related disparities in disability burden. By contrast, the CII of incidence remained negative, shifting from −0.07 (95% CI: −0.09 to −0.04) in 1990 to −0.08 (95% CI: −0.10 to −0.05) in 2021, indicating a greater incidence burden in lower‐SDI countries (Figure S4A). The corresponding SII was −0.36 (95% CI: −0.44 to −0.27) in 1990 and −0.35 (95% CI: −0.44 to −0.26) in 2021 (Figure S4B). All these results demonstrated a persistent between‐country health inequality.

Frontier analysis

For prevalence, New Zealand (SDI = 0.85) exhibited the largest efficiency gap (25.25), indicating its TS prevalence (32.24 per 100 000) substantially exceeded the optimal level (6.99 per 100 000), suggesting considerable room for improvement in disease management. Other countries within the top 15 efficiency gaps (14.37–21.95), such as Belgium, Greenland, and Ireland, also exhibited considerable gaps, indicating widespread inefficiencies in TS care. The five lowest efficiency gaps were observed in low‐SDI countries, including Niger, Somalia, Yemen, Papua New Guinea, and Afghanistan, with gaps ranging from 0 to 5.16, likely reflecting limited healthcare access or reporting biases (Figure 7A,B). For YLDs, the high‐SDI countries (SDI >0.85) with the top five efficiency gaps were the United States, Belgium, Ireland, Germany, and the Netherlands, with differences ranging from 0.33 to 0.41. In contrast, the smallest gaps (0.00–0.08) were observed in low‐SDI countries (SDI <0.50) such as Niger, Somalia, and Yemen (Figure 7C,D). For incidence, the top five high‐SDI countries (SDI >0.85) with the largest efficiency gaps were the United States, Andorra, Belgium, Germany, and Ireland, with differences ranging from 0.62 to 0.91. In contrast, the smallest gaps, ranging from 0.00 to 0.39, were observed in low‐SDI countries (SDI <0.50) such as Niger, Somalia, Afghanistan, Yemen, and Vanuatu (Figure S5).

Projected trends of Turner syndrome through 2050

The ARIMA model projects a steady rise in the absolute prevalence of TS from 1990 to 2050, with case numbers rising from 416 580 to an estimated 541 357 (Figure 8A). Despite this increase, the age‐standardized prevalence rate is expected to decline modestly from 14.79 per 100 000 in 1990 to approximately 13.96 per 100 000 by 2050 (Figure 8B). A similar pattern is observed in the YLDs, where the total number increases steadily from 7104 to 10 294 (Figure 8C), while the YLDs ASRs gradually decline from 14.79 per 100 000 to 13.96 per 100 000 over the same period (Figure 8D). The incidence shows an initial rise followed by a gradual decline, with case numbers decreasing from 23 930 in 1990 to 14 634 by 2050 (Figure S6A). The age‐standardized incidence rate increased slightly from 0.775 per 100 000 in 1990 to 0.805 per 100 000 in 2000, declined to 0.784 per 100 000 by 2015, rebounded modestly to 0.800 per 100 000 in 2020, and is projected to stabilize around 0.792 per 100 000 by 2050 (Figure S6B).

DISCUSSION

Our analysis shows that the global prevalence of TS and the associated YLDs increased steadily from 1990 to 2021 and are projected to continue rising through 2050. Significant disparities were observed across SDI levels, regions, and countries, highlighting pronounced global inequality in diagnosis, surveillance, and healthcare capacity. In five SDI regions, the most striking burden growth was observed in low‐SDI regions, where prevalence and YLDs increased by 104.80% and 110.14%, respectively. This finding echoes that underdiagnosis and limited access to early genetic testing in low‐resource settings may have led to underestimation of TS cases. 16 , 17 Regionally, South Asia emerged as the area with the highest TS burden in 2021, while Western Sub‐Saharan Africa showed the most rapid increases in both prevalence and YLDs. Conversely, Eastern Europe demonstrated a decline in both indicators. These divergent patterns may reflect variations in reproductive health policies, prenatal screening uptake, and health system maturity. 18 At the national level, TS burden varied widely, with India, China, and the United States reporting the highest case numbers, while countries like Niue, Tokelau, and the Cook Islands reported very few cases, likely reflecting small populations and limited diagnostic capacity. 19 Notably, the ASRs of prevalence and YLDs remained virtually flat, and 95% showed no significant incidence trend, consistent with its congenital nature. Our findings underscore the pressing need to strengthen TS surveillance systems, particularly in low‐SDI, rapidly growing regions.

Age‐specific analysis revealed that the global burden of TS was mainly concentrated in the <5 years age group. Notably, YLDs distribution showed region‐specific patterns. Globally and in low‐ to middle‐SDI regions, the YLDs burden peaked in the <5 years group, while in high‐SDI regions, peak YLDs were observed in young adulthood (20–34 years), possibly due to long‐term complications such as infertility, cardiovascular disease, and psychosocial challenges. A study evaluating the transition process in the West of Scotland revealed that a significant proportion of girls with TS are lost to follow‐up after transitioning to adult clinics, emphasizing the need for strategies to improve long‐term follow‐up. 20 In low‐SDI regions, the burden is likely exacerbated by limited access to pediatric endocrinology, growth hormone therapy, and cardiac care, which are essential for managing early‐onset complications of TS. 21 , 22 , 23 These findings collectively underscore the importance of early‐life diagnosis and lifelong, multidisciplinary care for individuals with TS. 24 Clinically, pediatricians should emphasize early diagnosis in infants and children and initiate growth hormone therapy as early as possible, along with timely endocrine management, whereas adult clinicians need to prioritize reproductive health, cardiovascular surveillance, and psychosocial support to improve long‐term outcomes. At the same time, strengthening early detection in low‐resource settings and optimizing adult‐focused interventions in high‐SDI regions remain critical priorities for healthcare systems.

The decomposition analysis underscores the critical role of population growth, aging, and epidemiological factors in shaping the global and regional burden of TS over the past three decades. Notably, the prevalence increase in low‐SDI regions such as South Asia was predominantly attributed to rapid population growth, while high‐SDI regions such as Western Europe and high‐income Asia Pacific experienced declines primarily due to population aging. The modest global decline in TS incidence, driven largely by epidemiological improvements, may be explained by advances in screening strategies, including the widespread application of prenatal genomics and transcriptomics technologies, 25 as well as the increasing rate of elective terminations, which partly reflects the rise in early diagnostic interventions. However, this trend was not observed in low‐SDI regions, where incidence continued to rise due to ongoing population growth and limited access to prenatal diagnosis and care. These findings highlight the persistent global burden of TS, emphasizing the need for prenatal and perinatal counseling in clinical practice to provide families with balanced information on prognosis, treatment options, and long‐term outcomes, while also underscoring the importance of region‐specific public health strategies to improve early detection and ensure equitable care.

The persistent positive CII and SII of prevalence and YLDs imply TS remains associated with higher socioeconomic status, possibly due to diagnostic bias or better healthcare access in affluent regions. Conversely, the consistently negative CII and SII of incidence underscore that TS burden disproportionately affects lower socioeconomic groups, likely due to disparities in diagnostic access or genetic counseling. 26 Despite global health advancements, the persistent health inequalities underscore the urgency of equitable healthcare policies, such as subsidized genetic screening and improved maternal health services in low‐income regions, to mitigate disparities. 27 For clinicians working in resource‐limited settings, awareness of these disparities is crucial, as patients may present later and require more intensive counseling, follow‐up, and support to ensure continuity of care.

We conducted a frontier analysis to evaluate the potential for improvement in the management of TS, considering national development levels as indicated by the SDI. In high‐SDI nations like Belgium and Ireland, which show large discrepancies in prevalence, YLDs, and incidence, there are indications of potential overestimation and inefficiencies in care. Conversely, low‐SDI countries such as Niger, Somalia, and Yemen have narrow gaps, likely due to limited healthcare access or reporting biases. High‐SDI countries need to optimize healthcare delivery through advanced technologies and comprehensive care models, 28 while low‐SDI regions require targeted international support to build healthcare infrastructure and improve diagnostic capabilities. 29 Addressing these disparities is crucial for enhancing TS management and achieving more equitable health outcomes globally.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis relies on the GBD database, which is constrained in accuracy by the availability and quality of national registry data. A substantial proportion of TS cases may be undiagnosed. Second, the cross‐sectional design of this study presents certain limitations, as interpreting historical data may lead to hypotheses that require further empirical validation. More precise and up‐to‐date data are needed to address these limitations and challenges related to data timeliness.

CONCLUSION

This study reveals a rising global burden of Turner syndrome from 1990 to 2021, a trend projected to continue through 2050. Persistent socioeconomic disparities highlight the need for tailored strategies: addressing efficiency gaps in high‐SDI regions by optimizing lifelong management and overcoming structural barriers in low‐SDI settings through improved early diagnosis and access to basic care.
