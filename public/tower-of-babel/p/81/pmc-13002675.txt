The therapeutic potential of Piezo1 channel-mediated ferroptosis and its inhibitor
Authors: Kai Nan, Lei Zhang, Yan Zhao, Su Yin, Yulong Peng, Jing Huang, Lin Liu, Zhaohui Yi, Hongchao Yu, Zhi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Piezo1 is a mechanically activated, non-selective cation channel characterized by its exquisite sensitivity to membrane tension and high permeability to calcium(Ca2+), enabling the conversion of mechanical stimuli into intracellular signaling events. Activation of Piezo1 leads to Ca2+ influx, which initiates iron metabolism reprogramming—including transferrin receptor 1(TfR1)-dependent iron uptake, divalent metal transporter 1(DMT1)-mediated iron transport, and Nuclear Receptor Coactivator 4(NCOA4)-regulated ferritinophagy—thereby promoting the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation. Ultimately, these events culminate in ferroptosis by suppressing glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) activity. The “mechanical force–Piezo1–Ca2+–iron/lipid metabolism” axis establishes mechanical stress as a pivotal upstream regulator of ferroptosis. This axis facilitates the functional integration of mechanotransduction into inflammatory mediator production, vascular and extracellular matrix(ECM) remodeling, and metabolic reprogramming. Furthermore, this signaling pathway exerts context-dependent pathogenic or protective effects across diverse pathological conditions, including musculoskeletal degeneration, ischemia-reperfusion injury, inflammatory bowel disease, neurovascular disorders, and cancer. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the molecular mechanisms and clinical evidence governing Piezo1-mediated ferroptosis. We summarize current pharmacological and genetic interventions for its inhibition—along with associated limitations such as selectivity and pharmacokinetic challenges—and explores interventions targeting the channel itself, Ca²⁺signaling, and downstream ferroptotic processes, including iron chelation, lipid peroxidation suppression, and preservation of the GPX4/coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) axis. Furthermore, the potential for integrating these interventions with established therapeutic modalities is also discussed. A profound understanding of the druggability and context-dependent dynamics of the Piezo1-ferroptosis axis is expected to facilitate the discovery of novel therapeutic targets and combinatorial regimens for the precision management of mechanosensitive diseases.

Introduction

Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death characterized by the accumulation of iron ions, excessive lipid peroxidation, and inactivation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) [1]. Piezo1 is a mechanically sensitive non-selective cation channel that senses mechanical stimuli and mediates the transmembrane flow of ions such as calcium(Ca2+) [2, 3]. Upon mechanical activation, Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ signaling reprograms iron metabolism, thereby fueling lipid peroxidation and ultimately culminating in ferroptosis. The “mechanical signal-ion flux-iron metabolism/lipid peroxidation-ferroptosis” axis plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of diverse conditions, including neurodegeneration, ischemia-reperfusion injury, inflammatory diseases, and tumors. In degenerative and ischemic diseases, Piezo1-mediated ferroptosis exacerbates cellular damage and functional decline. Conversely, within the tumor microenvironment(TME), the induction of ferroptosis can suppress the proliferation of drug-resistant neoplastic cells, representing a promising therapeutic strategy [4]. Lipid peroxidation not only alters membrane tension but also directly promotes Piezo1 gating and ion permeability, serving as a critical step in the execution of ferroptosis. Specifically, the inactivation of GPX4 leads to the accumulation of lipid peroxides, which amplifies the Piezo1-mediated ion imbalance and subsequent cell death through a positive feedback loop [2]. The regulation of ferroptosis involves multiple metabolic pathways, including iron homeostasis, glutathione metabolism, and lipid remodeling. While Piezo1 inhibitors such as Grammostola spatulata Mechanotoxin 4(GsMTx4) and ferroptosis inhibitors (such as ferrostatin-1(Fer-1), liproxstatin-1(Lip-1), and iron chelators) have shown promise in improving neurodegenerative, cardiovascular, and tumor diseases in animal models [5, 6], their clinical application remains hindered by challenges in targeted delivery, specific regulation, and side effects. A deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms of this axis is expected to provide new intervention strategies for various intractable diseases.

Molecular architecture and physiological functions of Piezo1

Piezo1 is a member of the evolutionarily conserved family of mechanically sensitive ion channels. Its protein monomer consists of 38 transmembrane helices, which assemble into a homotrimer to form a distinctive “three-bladed propeller” architecture [7–9]. Each blade is composed of multiple transmembrane helices symmetrically arranged around a central ion-conducting pore, which collectively induce a pronounced nanoscale bowl-shaped curvature in the plasma membrane. This unique structural organization not only confers exceptional mechanical sensitivity but also enables Piezo1 to directly gate in response to changes in membrane tension [7, 10, 11], effectively transducing diverse mechanical stimuli into biochemical signals through Ca2+ influx.

Piezo1 exhibits exquisite sensitivity to membrane tension with sub-second response capability, enabling the instantaneous transduction of minute physical mechanical stimuli, including membrane stretching, shear force, and external pressure. Its activation is primarily governed by the “force-from-lipids” mechanism, in which physical deformation of the lipid bilayer directly induces conformational changes in the channel. Additionally, mechanical coupling between the cytoskeleton and extracellular matrix(ECM) modulates Piezo1 gating via the “force-from-filaments” mechanism [12, 13]. Functionally, Piezo1 acts as a non-selective cation channel that facilitates the transmembrane flux of Ca2+, with particularly prominent Ca2+ permeability. Upon the channel opening, the rapid elevation of intracellular Ca2+ levels triggers diverse downstream signaling cascades, including cell volume regulation, migration, proliferation, differentiation, and programmed cell death. Moreover, its distinct activation and deactivation kinetics allow Piezo1 to respond and adapt rapidly to sustained mechanical stimulation [14–17]. It is important to note that Piezo1 undergoes rapid inactivation (desensitization) upon continuous mechanical stimulation, a mechanism that prevents excitotoxicity [18]. However, in pathological contexts such as tissue fibrosis or tumor progression, the mechanical microenvironment is dynamic rather than static. Repetitive mechanical loading, combined with altered cytoskeletal tension, allows Piezo1 to repeatedly cycle between active and resting states resulting in a repetitive oscillatory Ca2 + influx rather than a single transient spike. Furthermore, the transient Ca2+ signals generated by Piezo1 are integrated by downstream effectors, converting short-lived mechanical stimuli into long-lasting transcriptional changes that drive disease progression.

Piezo1 is widely distributed in various tissue cells, including vascular endothelial cells, erythrocytes, osteocytes, neurons, and tumor cells. It serves as a master regulator in a broad spectrum of physiological and pathological processes, such as fluid shear stress sensing, bone remodeling, erythrocyte volume homeostasis, sensory conduction, and mechanical adaptation of the TME [19–22]. Consequently, dysfunctional Piezo1 signaling is intimately linked to the pathogenesis of various genetic disorders, cardiovascular diseases, neurodegenerative changes, and tumorigenesis [17].

Overall, the three-bladed propeller structure of Piezo1 and its extreme sensitivity to membrane tension establish the molecular basis for its pivotal role in mechanotransduction. Its high permeability to Ca2+ makes it a key node in the regulation of intracellular calcium signals. The structural and functional characteristics of Piezo1 not only determine its versatility in various physiological processes but also provide a theoretical basis for the study of its mechanism of action and targeted intervention in pathological processes such as ferroptosis. Therefore, deepening our understanding of Piezo1’s gating regulation and its specific pathological contributions is essential for developing precise therapeutic strategies.

Piezo1-ferroptosis molecular axis

Mechanical stress-such as stretching, shear stress and osmotic pressure fluctuations- directly acts upon the cell membrane, leading to an elevated state of membrane tension. Piezo1, as a highly mechanically sensitive non-selective cation channel, rapidly responds to these physical stimuli and undergoes conformational changes to open the channel. The increase in membrane tension not only directly activates Piezo1 but also further enhances the gating sensitivity of Piezo1 through lipid peroxidation, thereby establishing a positive feedback loop [2, 23, 24].

Following Piezo1 activation, it mainly mediates the influx of Na+ and Ca2+, leading to a rapid elevation in intracellular calcium concentration that triggers downstream signaling cascades, most notably iron metabolism reprogramming and lipid peroxidation [25, 26]. During the process of ferroptosis, lipid peroxides preferentially accumulate within the plasma membrane, increasing membrane tension and further activating Piezo1. This creates a sustained imbalance in ion homeostasis that ultimately culminates in cell rupture. Additionally, the activation of Piezo1 can affect the expression of hepcidin, regulating iron uptake and storage, promoting the accumulation of iron ions, and providing a molecular basis for ferroptosis [24, 27, 28].

Under various pathological conditions-including nervous system trauma, ischemia-reperfusion injury, inflammatory diseases, and cancers-mechanical stress enhances susceptibility to ferroptosis through Piezo1 activation, ultimately leading to cellular dysfunction or death. For example, in ischemic brain injury, ECM remodeling and increased tissue stiffness trigger Piezo1-mediated ferroptosis, thereby compromising neuronal survival [24]. Conversely, within the TME, mechanical forces combined with upregulated Piezo1 expression synergistically facilitate ferroptosis, resulting in selective elimination of tumor cells [1, 16].

Lipid peroxidation is not only a core event of ferroptosis but also a critical regulatory factor for Piezo1 activation [3]. The inactivation of GPX4 leads to the accumulation of lipid peroxides, which further intensifies membrane tension and Piezo1 gating, forming a “mechanical stress - ion flow - iron metabolism/lipid peroxidation - ferroptosis” axis [29]. Furthermore, pharmacological or genetic blockade Piezo1 or related ion flows can significantly attenuate ferroptosis, suggesting that Piezo1 is a therapeutic target of this axis [30].

Current research indicates that Piezo1 serves as a pivotal bridge between mechanical signals and ferroptosis, particularly playing a core role in membrane tension regulation, ion homeostatic imbalance, and iron metabolism reprogramming. Upon activation by mechanical stress, Piezo1 facilitates lipid peroxidation and intracellular iron accumulation, thereby driving the ferroptotic process [16, 24, 29, 31, 32]. This molecular axis has a double-edged sword effect in various diseases, as it can both exacerbate tissue damage and be utilized for tumor treatment. In the future, precise regulation and targeted intervention of Piezo1 are expected to provide new therapeutic strategies for ferroptosis-related diseases (Fig. 1).

Piezo1 and diseases: from mechanical force to ferroptosis

Piezo1 and musculoskeletal system diseases

Mechanical overload is widely recognized as a primary pathological driver of degeneration in musculoskeletal tissues, particularly in articular cartilage and intervertebral discs. As a critical mechanosensor, Piezo1 mechanistically links physical pressure and ferroptosis within chondrocytes and nucleus pulposus cells [33]. In articular cartilage and intervertebral discs, excessive mechanical loading enhances both the expression and functional activity of Piezo1, thereby promoting Ca2+ influx and triggering the activation of downstream ferroptosis signaling pathways [25, 29, 31]. The molecular machinery downstream of Piezo1 activation in these tissues is well-characterized. The calcium influx triggers a reprogramming of iron metabolism: it facilitates iron uptake through the upregulation of transferrin receptor 1(TfR1) and divalent metal transporter 1(DMT1), while simultaneously mobilizing stored iron via Nuclear Receptor Coactivator 4(NCOA4)-mediated ferritinophagy. This synergistic action leads to a dangerous accumulation of LIP [34].

Concurrently, Piezo1-mediated calcium signaling activates crucial enzymes such as lipoxygenase (LOX) and NADPH oxidase (NOX). These enzymes catalyze the production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) and drive the peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids in cellular membranes. Crucially, the resultant oxidative stress compromises the antioxidant defense system by weakening GPX4 activity, rendering chondrocytes defenseless against lipid peroxidation [29, 31]. Beyond the direct induction of cell death, Piezo1 signaling in the musculoskeletal system is intricately linked to the homeostasis of the ECM. Calcium-dependent signaling axes, including Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II/Calcineurin-Nuclear Factor of Activated T-cells(CaMKII/calcineurin-NFAT) and Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2(Nrf2) pathways, are perturbed by mechanical overload, leading to an imbalance between matrix synthesis and degradation [29, 33].

Importantly, lipid peroxidation itself increases membrane tension, establishing a positive feedback loop that enhances Piezo1 sensitivity to mechanical stimuli and perpetuates ferroptosis signaling [12, 29]. Evidence demonstrates that pharmacological inhibition or genetic ablation of Piezo1 effectively attenuates iron overload, reduces oxidative stress, and suppresses ferroptosis in chondrocytes and nucleus pulposus cells, thereby mitigating tissue degeneration in preclinical models [25, 26, 31]. Furthermore, the targeted delivery of Piezo1 inhibitors—such as hydrogel-based systems—represents a promising localized therapeutic strategy, offering enhanced efficacy while minimizing systemic side effects [29, 35].

In summary, Piezo1 plays a pivotal role in mediating the detrimental effects of mechanical overload on musculoskeletal tissues through the induction of ferroptosis. The mechanotransduction axis encompassing mechanical stress, Piezo1 activation, Ca2+ influx, iron dysregulation, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis is well established in both chondrocytes and nucleus pulposus cells. While Piezo1 inhibition and antioxidant interventions hold therapeutic potential, significant challenges remain in achieving tissue-specific targeting and preserving Piezo1’s physiological functions in bone and other tissues (Fig. 2).

Piezo1 and spinal cord injury and central nervous system diseases

In CNS, particularly following the SCI, mechanical stress manifests as an acute event involving tissue compression, deformation, and shearing. Unlike the chronic overload seen in musculoskeletal diseases, the mechanical insult in SCI triggers an immediate and massive activation of Piezo1 in both neurons and glial cells (astrocytes and microglia) [35, 36]. Piezo1 is a mechanically sensitive cation channel that mediates an increase in calcium influx in response to elevated membrane tension, and lipid peroxidation during ferroptosis further exacerbates this elevation [2]. While the fundamental iron dysregulation mechanisms—involving TfR1, DMT1, and NCOA4—remain operative, the role of Piezo1 in the CNS extends into the complex “secondary injury” cascade that follows the initial trauma. Specifically, Piezo1-driven signaling expands the intracellular pool of labile iron, thereby heightening neuronal sensitivity to ferroptosis [3, 37, 38].

Similarly, within the injured spinal cord, Piezo1 acts via the LOX/NOX axis to drive mitochondrial ROS production. This creates a lethal oxidative environment where GPX4 impairment fuels a vicious cycle of lipid peroxidation and neuronal death [2, 38, 39]. Furthermore, Piezo1 serves as a nexus between ferroptosis and neuroinflammation. In the context of SCI, calcium-dependent signaling pathways (such as the CaMKII/calcineurin-NFAT and Nrf2 axes) do not merely regulate cell survival but also orchestrate the inflammatory phenotype of glial cells [19, 39]. The activation of these pathways contributes to a toxic microenvironment rich in ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokines, which further accelerates neuronal loss. Therapeutic interventions targeting this axis using broad-spectrum inhibitors like Ruthenium Red or GsMTx4 have demonstrated significant neuroprotective effects. By interrupting the Piezo1-ferroptosis axis, these treatments effectively alleviate iron overload and reduce oxidative stress, thereby preserving residual neural tissue and facilitating functional recovery [40].

Overall, Piezo1 is a central mediator of ferroptosis in SCI and CNS injuries, linking mechanical stress to iron-dependent cell death through calcium signaling and lipid peroxidation. Inhibiting Piezo1 or its downstream effectors can reduce neuronal loss and improve outcomes in animal models, supporting the neuroprotective potential of Piezo1-targeted therapies.However, challenges remain in achieving tissue-specific delivery, particularly in crossing the blood-brain barrier, and avoiding disruption of Piezo1’s physiological functions in neural networks. Future research should focus on improving targeted delivery systems and elucidating the long-term effects of Piezo1 modulation on CNS diseases.

Piezo1 and intestinal inflammation and mucosal immunity

Piezo1, a mechanosensitive cation channel, is highly expressed in intestinal epithelial cells and lamina propria immune cells and plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of IBD [41]. In a mouse model of Dextran Sulfate Sodium(DSS)-induced colitis, Piezo1 expression is significantly upregulated [42]. Pharmacological inhibition of Piezo1 (such as GsMTx4) can effectively alleviate intestinal inflammation, reduce weight loss, intestinal pathological damage, and the expression of pro-inflammatory factors (such as IL-1β, IL-6, Ptgs2). Piezo1 inhibitors not only diminish IL-6 secretion by macrophages and dendritic cells but also specifically regulate the proliferation of ILC3 cells and the production of IL-17 A, mainly through the PI3K-Akt-mTOR signaling pathway, while having limited effects on the distribution of T helper cell subsets [43–45].

At the level of epithelial cells, high expression of Piezo1 is closely related to ferroptosis. In clinical ulcerative colitis (UC) patients and DSS-induced mouse models, the upregulation of Piezo1 is accompanied by an increase in ferroptosis markers—specifically lipid peroxidation and the downregulation of GPX4-alongside the loss of tight junction proteins, leading to intestinal barrier dysfunction [46, 47]. Conversely, Piezo1 conditional knockout mice show lower lipid peroxidation and preserved tight junction protein expression, resulting in the significant alleviation of intestinal inflammation and barrier damage [42]. In vitro studies further demonstrate that Piezo1 deficiency inhibits LPS-induced ferroptosis in Caco-2 cells, and the AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway is involved in the molecular mechanism by which Piezo1 regulates ferroptosis. Ferroptosis activators (such as erastin) can reverse the protective effect of Piezo1 deficiency, further confirming the pathogenic role of the Piezo1-ferroptosis axis in enteritis [48, 49].

At the immunological level, Piezo1 is highly expressed in lamina propria macrophages, where it modulates the Nrf2, Nuclear Factor kappa-B(NF-κB), andNOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) signaling pathways, thereby promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and inducing oxidative stress, which collectively exacerbate intestinal inflammation [50, 51]. Inhibition of Piezo1 effectively downregulates NF-κB activation, reduces ROS and MDA levels, preserves intestinal barrier integrity, and attenuates DSS-induced tissue damage. Furthermore, by coordinately regulating ferroptosis in epithelial cells and the functional activity of immune cells, Piezo1 establishes a “mechanical stress–Piezo1–ferroptosis/immune activation–colitis” axis, highlighting its central role in driving inflammatory cascades in the gut [43, 48, 52].

Piezo1 and the tumor microenvironment

Piezo1 plays a dual role in the TME by promoting migration/invasion and regulating immune responses, while ferroptosis induction offers a promising approach for cancer treatment, especially for refractory tumors. Piezo1 is highly expressed in tumor cells as well as various stromal and immune cell populations within the TME [53, 54]. It is activated by mechanical stimuli such as increased matrix stiffness and interstitial pressure, promoting tumor cell migration and invasion, thereby facilitating metastatic dissemination [53, 55]. Furthermore, Piezo1 also regulates immune cell function by influencing the recruitment, activation, and polarization of macrophages, dendritic cells, and T cells, ultimately shaping the immunological landscape of the TME [53, 56–58].

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death driven by lipid peroxidation and serves as an intrinsic tumor suppressive mechanism, particularly in treatment-resistant and mesenchymal cancer cells [1, 55, 59, 60]. Inducing ferroptosis in tumor cells can enhance anti-tumor immunity by releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and promoting immunogenic cell death [56, 58]. However, the interaction between ferroptosis and immune regulation is complex: while ferroptosis can enhance immune-mediated tumor clearance, excessive lipid peroxidation and the release of oxidative products may impair the function of cytotoxic T cells and dendritic cells, potentially leading to immune suppression or treatment resistance [56, 58].

Piezo1 and ferroptosis are mechanistically linked within the TME. Piezo1-mediated calcium influx promotes iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation, thereby increasing tumor cell susceptibility to ferroptosis [53, 55, 61].Conversely, Piezo1 activation may also contribute to tumor cell survival and immune evasion by modulating redox homeostasis and enabling metabolic adaptation [53, 61]. The therapeutic implications are context-dependent: while inhibition of Piezo1 may attenuate tumor cell migration and invasion, it could simultaneously reduce ferroptosis sensitivity, potentially compromising the effectiveness of ferroptosis-targeted cancer treatments [58, 60, 61]. Therefore, targeting Piezo1 in oncology requires careful consideration of the balance between its pro-metastatic properties and its role in sensitizing cells to ferroptosis, as well as its broader effects on immune cell function and TME dynamics [58]. Future studies should aim to elucidate the molecular crosstalk among Piezo1 signaling, ferroptosis regulation, and immune modulation to inform the development of rational combination therapies in cancer treatment (Fig. 3).

Piezo1 and cerebrovascular and blood-brain barrier

After Cerebral Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury (CIRI), the integrity of the BBB is compromised, and endothelial cells are exposed to mechanical stress and oxidative stress environments. The expression of Piezo1 is significantly upregulated, making it a key sensor in the injury microenvironment. Once activated, Piezo1 mediates Ca2+ influx, initiates the CaMKII/Nrf2 signaling pathway, promotes oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, leading to endothelial barrier disruption and aggravated brain edema. In Piezo1 endothelial-specific knockout mice, brain injury, neurological dysfunction, and BBB disruption are significantly alleviated, suggesting that Piezo1 is a core molecular hub in CIRI [62, 63].

The Ca2+ signaling mediated by Piezo1 not only promotes oxidative stress but also highly intersects with the ferroptosis network. After CIRI, iron ion accumulation and lipid peroxidation increase, GPX4 activity decreases, and ferroptosis becomes an important mechanism for neuronal and endothelial cell injury. The activation of Piezo1 can exacerbate iron metabolism disorders and lipid peroxidation, further amplifying ferroptosis signals, forming a pathological axis of “mechanical stress - Piezo1 - oxidative stress/ferroptosis - BBB disruption" [62, 64–66].

During BBB injury, Piezo1 serves as a key modulator of the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway. The absence of Piezo1 enhances Nrf2 nuclear translocation, upregulates the expression of NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase 1(NQO-1) and Heme Oxygenase-1(HO-1). This activation effectively mitigates oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, ultimately preserving the structural integrity and microvascular function of the BBB. Additionally, Piezo1 cooperates with ferroptosis-related molecules (such as ACSL4, SLC7A11, NCOA4) to regulate lipid peroxidation and iron homeostasis. This molecular crosstalk significantly influences the survival of both neurons and endothelial cells under pathological conditions. [64, 67, 68].

Preclinical studies have shown that Piezo1 inhibitors (such as GsMTx4) or downstream antioxidant/ferroptosis interventions (such as Fer-1, Lip-1, iron chelators) can significantly improve neurological function and BBB integrity in brain ischemia models, suggesting that the Piezo1-ferroptosis axis provides a new target for the treatment of cerebrovascular diseases [62, 64, 69].

In conclusion, Piezo1 facilitates BBB disruption and neuronal injury following cerebral ischemia-reperfusion by orchestrating a complex network of endothelial barrier dysfunction, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis. Piezo1 inhibitors and ferroptosis interventions have shown neuroprotective and barrier repair potential in animal models, but clinical translation still faces challenges in delivery, specificity, and safety. Future research needs to further clarify the molecular mechanism of the Piezo1-ferroptosis axis and its precise intervention strategies in cerebrovascular diseases.

Mechanosensitive channels inhibitors: pharmacological characteristics and therapeutic prospects

Types of inhibitors and mechanisms of action

A variety of Piezo1 Broad-Spectrum inhibitors have been discovered, each with a unique mechanism of action that affects channel gating and calcium influx. GsMTx4, a well-characterized gating modulator from spider venom, binds to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane, alters local lipid tension, and shifts the Piezo1 pressure-activation curve to a higher threshold, thereby reducing mechanically-induced calcium influx without directly blocking the channel pore [70]. GsMTx4 is effective at nanomolar to micromolar concentrations and is reversible, making it a valuable tool for both in vitro and in vivo studies [40, 52, 71, 72].

Small molecule inhibitors include Dooku1, an analogue of Yoda1, which antagonizes the Yoda1 binding site or its allosteric pathway, selectively inhibiting Yoda1-induced Piezo1 activation without affecting basal mechanosensitivity or other cation channels. Dooku1 is specific for Piezo1 and does not interfere with TRPV4 or TRPC4, supporting its application in dissecting Piezo1-dependent signaling [73]. Other small molecules, such as ruthenium red and certain natural products (e.g., turbotropine I, salvianolic acid B, jatrorrhizine, aescin), have been reported to inhibit Piezo1, but their selectivity and clinical relevance require further validation [40, 52, 74].

Non-selective blockers, such as Gadolinium ions (Gd³⁺) and Ruthenium Red, act as broad-spectrum inhibitors of mechanosensitive cation channels, including Piezo1, by interfering with the gating or pore function of the channels. Nevertheless, their lack of specificity often leads to significant off-target effects, limiting their translational potential. [10, 52, 74].

Conversely, gene interventions, such as conditional gene knockout or RNA interference, can precisely inhibit the Piezo1 gene, providing mechanistic studies and proof-of-concept for targeted therapy in disease models. These methods have been shown to effectively reduce Piezo1-mediated calcium influx, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis in preclinical models of SCI and bone diseases [3, 29].

All these inhibitors are aimed at reducing Piezo1-mediated calcium influx, thereby weakening the downstream signaling cascades associated with ferroptosis and tissue damage. However, systemic inhibition of Piezo1 may disrupt physiological mechano-transduction in bones, blood vessels, and other tissues, thus necessitating targeted delivery strategies and further pharmacological improvements [71, 72].

In summary, the current pharmacological prospects for Piezo1 inhibition include peptide toxins, small molecules, non-selective cation blockers, and genetic tools, each with its own advantages and limitations in research and therapeutic applications. Continuous efforts to enhance selectivity, bioavailability, and tissue targeting are crucial for translating Piezo1 inhibitors into clinical practice.

Synergistic therapeutic strategies: combining Piezo1 inhibition with ferroptosis interventions

Piezo1 inhibitors and direct ferroptosis inhibitors-such as Fer-1, Lip-1, and Deferoxamine (DFO)-have shown synergistic effects across multiple disease models. Piezo1 inhibitors reduce mechanical stress-induced Ca2+ influx, thereby suppressing downstream iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and the ferroptosis signaling cascade, while Fer-1, Lip-1, and others directly block the lipid peroxidation chain reaction, and DFO reduces labile iron load through iron chelation [75]. In models of spinal cord injury, neurodegeneration, and joint degeneration, this dual-targeting strategy significantly reduces cell death and promotes superior structural and functional recovery compared to monotherapies [3, 76].

The integration of antioxidants and Nrf2 pathway activators with Piezo1 inhibitors significantly bolsters cellular defense against oxidative stress and ferroptosis [75]. Nrf2 activation can upregulate anti-ferroptosis molecules such as GPX4 and SLC7A11. However, the functional integrity of GPX4 relies heavily on the availability of Selenium (Se), an essential cofactor for selenocysteine biosynthesis. Supplementing Se directly counters Piezo1-mediated GPX4 inactivation by replenishing the enzymatic reserve [30]. Furthermore, coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) acts synergistically with GPX4 to suppress membrane lipid peroxidation via a glutathione-independent mechanism.These metabolic modulators act synergistically with Piezo1 inhibition to mitigate lipid peroxidation and restore iron homeostasis. Notably, certain natural products—including polyphenols and flavonoids—exhibit multifaceted therapeutic potential; they can directly scavenge ROS, modulate the Nrf2/GPX4 signaling axis, and in some instances, exert direct inhibitory effects on Piezo1 activity. These diverse pharmacological profiles render natural compounds ideal candidates for multi-targeted combination interventions [76–79].

Mechanical interventions (such as unloading strategies, orthopedic braces, and local hydrogel delivery) can reduce local mechanical stress, decrease Piezo1 activation, and work in synergy with pharmacological agents to preserve tissue architecture, particularly in regions subjected to high mechanical loading.Furthermore, the localized delivery of Piezo1 inhibitors enhances drug exposure at the lesion site while mitigating systemic toxicities, thereby optimizing the safety profile and precision of the combined therapeutic regimen [1, 29, 80, 81].

Nevertheless, within the TME, Piezo1 not only promotes tumor cell migration/invasion but also enhances ferroptosis sensitivity through the Ca2+-iron axis [53, 56]. While inhibiting Piezo1 may suppress metastatic dissemination, it may also inadvertently diminish the efficacy of ferroptosis-inducing therapies. The decision to inhibit Piezo1 should be made based on tumor type, treatment goals, and the biomechanical status of the microenvironment. Additionally, Piezo1 is widely distributed in tissues such as bone and blood vessels. Long-term systemic inhibition may affect physiological mechanical sensing and tissue homeostasis. Therefore, combined strategies should prioritize local delivery and individualized dose adjustment (Fig. 4).

Administration and delivery routes

Recently, local administration of Piezo1 inhibitors has gained increasing favor as it can achieve high local drug concentrations while minimizing systemic exposure and off-target effects, especially in tissues such as joints, intervertebral discs, and the intestine [29, 33, 45, 81]. Intra-articular injection has shown promise in targeting Piezo1-mediated inflammation and degeneration in osteoarthritis and other joint diseases, thereby directly modulating mechano-transduction and ferroptosis in chondrocytes [72, 82].Similarly, microendoscope-guided delivery of hydrogels to the vertebral growth plate can spatially restrict Piezo1 inhibition, effectively suppressing local ferroptosis and pathological ossification while avoiding systemic complications such as osteoporosis [33].

In models of intervertebral disc degeneration(IVDD), local delivery of Piezo1 inhibitors based on hydrogels has been shown to alleviate mechanical stress-induced cell death and matrix degradation, offering superior retention and bioactivity compared to systemic administration [83, 84]. Regarding intestinal inflammation, local delivery via enemas or direct mucosal application can target the regulation of Piezo1 in epithelial and immune cells, reducing inflammation and barrier dysfunction [45, 85].

Researchers are actively exploring advanced drug delivery systems, including hydrogels, nanocarriers, and stress or ROS-responsive platforms, to further enhance the tissue specificity and controlled release of Piezo1 inhibitors. Hydrogels can be engineered to respond to mechanical stimuli or oxidative stress, triggering drug release in direct response to the pathological microenvironment [81]. Nanocarriers (such as liposomes or polymer nanoparticles) have the potential for encapsulation and sustained release, and their surface modification enables targeting of inflamed or mechanically stressed tissues [36].

Mechanically and ROS-responsive delivery strategies leverage the unique disease microenvironment to trigger drug release, ensuring that Piezo1 inhibition occurs only under pathological conditions and preserving physiological mechanotransduction in healthy tissues [86]. These approaches are particularly suitable for diseases characterized by intermittent or local mechanical overload, such as joint degeneration, spinal cord injury, and intestinal inflammation [26, 81, 87].

Overall, the current literature supports a paradigm shift towards precise, localized, and responsive delivery of Piezo1 inhibitors, where hydrogels, nanocarriers, and microenvironment-sensitive platforms offer translational prospects for musculoskeletal, neurological, and gastrointestinal diseases.

The safety of Piezo1 inhibitors

Piezo1 is a mechanically sensitive cation channel widely expressed in endothelial cells, erythrocytes, and lymphatic vessels, which is crucial for maintaining vascular tone, endothelial integrity, and fluid homeostasis [12, 88–91]. Systemic or long-term inhibition of Piezo1 may disrupt endothelial mechanotransduction, leading to impaired vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and a heightened predisposition to edema or vascular inflammation [88, 89, 92, 93]. Furthermore, Piezo1 is indispensable for erythrocyte volume regulation; persistent inhibition can induce hydration imbalances, elevate the risk of hemolytic anemia, and cause significant alterations in hematocrit and other hematological indices [90, 94].

In the lymphatic system, Piezo1 is necessary for normal lymphangiogenesis and lymphatic vessel integrity; Its loss of function or continuous pharmacological blockade may result in lymphangiogenesis defects, tissue fluid imbalance, and increased susceptibility to lymphedema [90]. Toxicological concerns include off-target effects on non-vascular tissues, immune modulation, and potential interference with mechanosensation [12, 94, 95]. Clinically, acute administration of Piezo1 inhibitors may cause transient hypotension, bradycardia, or mild anemia. In contrast, chronic exposure increases the risk of vascular dysfunction, chronic anemia, and lymphatic system disorders [88, 90, 91, 94].

Limitations and future perspectives

Despite the promising therapeutic potential of the Piezo1-ferroptosis axis, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the precise molecular intermediaries linking Ca2+ influx to the transcriptional regulation of iron metabolism genes remain incompletely understood. Second, the “double-edged sword” nature of Piezo1—where it supports essential physiological processes like vascular tone and erythrocyte homeostasis—poses a significant challenge for systemic pharmacological inhibition. Current inhibitors, such as GsMTx4 and Ruthenium Red, are not specific to Piezo1. Therefore, there is a critical need to develop Piezo1-specific inhibitors and optimize their selectivity and pharmacokinetic profiles to enhance targeted therapeutic effects. Lastly, most evidence is currently derived from preclinical animal models, and extensive clinical validation is urgently needed to translate these mechanistic insights into safe and effective precision therapies.

Conclusion

Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death that plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disorders, cancers, cardiovascular diseases, and inflammatory conditions. As a mechanosensitive non-selective cation channel, Piezo1 transduces mechanical stimuli into intracellular Ca2+ signals and acts upstream of ferroptosis by modulating iron homeostasis, ferritinophagy, and lipid peroxidation. This positions Piezo1 as a promising therapeutic target for mitigating mechanical stress-associated pathologies and modulating the TME. A comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying Piezo1 gating, Ca2+ signaling dynamics, and their integration with iron metabolic reprogramming and lipid peroxidation—along with the physiological safety margins of Piezo1 activity across tissues—is essential for determining whether its inhibition or preservation is more beneficial in balancing tissue repair and antitumor responses. Despite emerging insights, systematic investigations are still required to elucidate the context-dependent role of Piezo1 in ferroptosis regulation, its contribution to disease progression in spinal cord injury, joint degeneration, and cerebrovascular disorders, and its dualistic functions in tumorigenesis. Future research should prioritize the development of highly selective and pharmacologically viable Piezo1 inhibitors, advance strategies for localized and stimulus-responsive (e.g., mechanical stress or ROS-activated) delivery systems, and explore potential synergies with ferroptosis inhibitors or agents targeting antioxidant pathways such as the Nrf2 signaling axis. These efforts hold promise for advancing the therapeutic management of ferroptosis-related diseases.
