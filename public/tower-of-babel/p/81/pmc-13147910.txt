Characteristics and Geographical Work Location of Early Career Nursing and Allied Health Practitioners Following a Rural Placement: Results From a Post‐Graduate Survey
Authors: Robyn McNeil, Trish Thorpe, Lisa Bourke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo describe the characteristics and geographical work locations of early career nursing and allied health practitioners who undertook a rural placement.DesignThis study used quantitative data from a longitudinal survey design with students who consented to be involved in the longitudinal study after completing their rural placement. These students are followed up every 2 years after their rural placement.ParticipantsNursing and allied health graduates working in their discipline of qualification (n = 706).Main Outcome MeasureThe main outcome measure was health professionals work location: rurally based, doing some rural work (e.g., outreach) or metropolitan based.ResultsMultinomial regression results indicated that rural origin, older age and nursing professionals were statistically significant predictors of being rurally based in recently graduated health professionals. Nurses were 1.7 times more likely to work rurally compared to allied health professionals. For some allied health disciplines, around one third of the respondents were rurally based up to four years after graduation, ranging from 17% for psychology to 52% for social work. Collectively, allied health professionals were more likely to be providing ‘some rural work’ compared to nursing professionals, and over 20% of occupational therapists, speech pathologists and psychologists responding to the survey were employed to ‘do some rural work’.ConclusionThis study provided preliminary insights into work locations of early career allied health and nursing workforces following a rural placement. Findings suggest there is complexity to understanding the impact of a rural placement given the various factors involved, including rural background, age at graduation, available rural work opportunities and options for providing outreach to rural areas. This study highlighted the importance of rural origin, the need to support mature age students and the variation in allied health disciplines in the extent they practice rurally or provide some rural outreach. Continued follow‐up of these professionals over time may help to identify key determinants of health professionals' workplace locations.Keywords: allied health, health workforce, nursing, prospective studies, rural health services, students

Introduction

Persistent health workforce shortages contribute to lower health outcomes for rural Australia [1, 2]. The Rural Health Multidisciplinary Training (RHMT) programme supports university nursing and allied students to undertake training in rural and remote communities, with the aim of improving recruitment and retention of health professionals in these regions [3]. The Going Rural Health (GRH) programme at the University of Melbourne organises students to undertake rural learning experiences by coordinating clinical and work‐integrated learning placements and providing accommodation, educational and financial support for students to experience a rural based clinical placement.

Until recently, there was limited evidence regarding the work destination of these students after graduation, the characteristics of these graduates and reasons underpinning their work migration patterns [4, 5, 6, 7]. Literature into early graduate nursing and allied health workplace locations has indicated that historically nursing is generally well spread geographically [8]. However, an ageing workforce and a decline in interest in a nursing career indicate that demand for nursing will exceed supply substantially by 2030, which is more likely to be experienced in rural and remote areas [8]. New government incentive schemes aimed at increasing nursing graduates have been implemented both federally and in some jurisdictions in Australia [9, 10]. For allied health professionals, a decline in allied health graduates staying rural after a few years of practice has been reported [8, 11]. Brown and colleagues found that 52% of their allied health graduates were working rural or remote 1 year after graduation, and this declined to 37.5% 3 years post‐graduation [12]. Further, a recent review of the literature by Cleland and colleagues reported that overall, retention of allied health professionals in rural and remote areas declined substantially after 2 years of practice [11].

Literature regarding indicators of early career work location identified that rural background [5, 6, 13], a quality rural placement experience [6, 14], longer placement [5, 6] and interest in rural practice [13, 14] were associated with rural work location in early career. Further, a rural location as a first job for nursing and allied health graduates has also been associated with long‐term rural practice [14]. This study aimed to understand geographical work locations and the characteristics associated with workforce geographical location of early‐career nursing and allied health practitioners who had previously undertaken a rural placement with the GRH programme.

Methods

Design

This study used quantitative data from a longitudinal survey design. Students who consented to be involved in the longitudinal study after completing their rural placement were followed up every 2 years after their rural placement.

Ethics Approval and Consent

Ethics approval was granted by the University of Melbourne Human Research Ethics Committee (Project ID: 24868).

Setting and Sample

This study used a purposive sampling strategy of nursing and allied health university students from more than 15 different metropolitan and regional universities who had completed a placement facilitated by the University of Melbourne Department of Rural Health. Potential participants were students who had completed both an online questionnaire following their clinical placement and a follow‐up online questionnaire 2 years later, using a rolling recruitment strategy. Data for the first follow‐up survey (Timepoint 1) were collected from 2017 to 2024 and included professionals that were up to 4 years post‐graduation. Inclusion criteria were students who had graduated and were working in their discipline of qualification.

Data Collection

Data from this survey were collected and managed using the online platform REDCap hosted by the University of Melbourne. Data from this study is from the first follow‐up survey (Timepoint 1) and includes students who completed a placement from 2014 and 2022.

Variables

Items from the survey included demographic and work location characteristics. Survey items explored drivers of workforce migration, underpinned by factors of rural health workforce retention [15] and the Whole of Person Retention Improvement Framework, that encompasses three domains: Workplace/Organisational, Role/Career, and Community/Place demonstrated to influence workforce retention [16].

The primary variable of interest for this study was the current location of work with categorical responses: ‘I am rurally based’, ‘I do some rural work (e.g., outreach)’ or ‘I am based in a metropolitan area’. Other variables of interest were rural/urban origin, geographical location (postcode), age, sex, years since graduated and discipline.

Data Analysis

Data analysis was limited to nursing and allied health students, with medical and dentistry students removed. Disciplines were coded into nursing (nursing and midwifery), and allied health that included audiology, chiropractic, exercise physiology, medical laboratory science, medical radiation, nutrition/dietetics, occupational therapy, optometry, oral health, orthotics and prosthetics, paramedicine, pharmacy, physiotherapy, podiatry, radiation science, social work, sonography and speech pathology [17].

Years since graduated was categorised into two groups: less than 2 years and 2–4 years post‐graduation. Due to the timing of questionnaires, some participants had been graduates for over 2 years. Postcodes were coded to Modified Monash Model (MMM) categories, MM 1 (metropolitan areas), MM 2 (regional centres), MM 3 (larger rural towns), MM 4–5 (medium and small rural towns respectively) and MM 6–7 (remote and very remote communities respectively) to describe the spread of geographical areas where health professionals were based.

To allow for regression analysis, discipline was collapsed into two categories, nursing and allied health. Missing data were less than 3% across all variables. Cases with missing data were excluded from regression analysis.

Multinomial Regression

Univariable multinomial regression was used to assess factors of interest associated with work location. The dependant variable was work location, where 1 = rurally based, 2 = I do some rural work (e.g., outreach) and 3 = metropolitan based. Independent variables of interest were student origin (metropolitan/rural), discipline (allied health/nursing), age (in years), and sex (male/female). A multivariable multinomial regression was conducted whereby all independent variables were included in the initial model to determine the association with the dependant variable. Variables were then removed in a backward stepwise manner to determine the best model fit for the data. Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) and Conditional Index (CI) post‐test diagnostics were performed to assess multicollinearity between variables in the regression models.

Results

Study Characteristics

A total of 1228 individuals were sent the questionnaire and 823 participants responded (67% response rate). Of these, 706 respondents met inclusion criteria. Forty‐two percent of respondents were working in regional, rural and remote areas. Respondents were mostly female (82%), 61% were of rural origin and around half studied nursing (Table 1).

Multivariable Regression Model

The full multivariable model was statistically significant X 2 60.5 [12] p < 0.001 and Goodness of Fit (GoF) indicated the model was a good fit for the data and explained 13% of the pseudo variance. Rural origin (Exp (B) 2.9 [1.9,4.3] p < 0.001) and age (Exp (B) 1.0 [1.0, 1.1] p < 0.001) were statistically significant in the full model. Years since graduated, sex and discipline were sequentially removed to improve the model (pseudo variance, 8%). The final model was statistically significant, X 2 = 39.7 [4] p < 0.001, with rural origin (Exp (B) 2.4 [1.7, 3.5] p < 0.001) and age (Exp (B) = 1.0 [1.0, 1.1] p < 0.001) being statistically significant. All variables were below 2 for VIF and under 15 for CI that indicated minimal multicollinearity. None of the variables for the outcome ‘I do some rural work’, were statistically significant in the multivariable models.

Location of Work—Disciplines

The geographical work location of nursing and allied health graduates varied across the disciplines. Nursing was similar between rural and metropolitan areas, with 47% of nursing graduates based rurally and 45% of nursing graduates based in metropolitan locations. Except for social work and occupational therapy graduates, more than 50% of other allied health respondents were working in metropolitan areas. For some allied health disciplines, around one third of the respondents were rurally based, which included 37% of physiotherapists, 36% of occupational therapists, 30% of medical radiation practitioners and 52% of social workers. A proportion (16%) of allied health graduates reported doing ‘some rural work’; being distinct from either rurally based or metropolitan based. Discussion

Findings from this study indicated that similar proportions of registered nurses were working rurally and in metropolitan areas up to 4 years after graduation. Limited evidence is available that tracks early nursing career graduates [18], however, these findings were consistent with a study that found approximately 50% of nurses that were trained in rural schools were practising rurally [19], and higher than Skinner et al.'s study that found 38% of nurses working rurally [20]. Further, these findings differed from the Nursing and Allied Health Graduate Outcome Tracking—(NAHGOT) study that found that 17% of all graduate nurses were practising in a rural or remote area [5]. A reason for the high proportion of nurses working rurally in this study may be the proportion of nurses being of rural origin (69%).

Consistent with other studies, over half of respondents in most allied health disciplines in this study were working in metropolitan areas [12, 18], however, 26% of speech pathologists, 30% of medical radiation practitioners, 36% of occupational therapists, 37% of physiotherapists, and 52% of social workers were working rurally up to 4 years after graduation. Allied health professionals were less likely to work rurally compared to nurses. This may be because nursing is more likely to be hospital‐based compared with allied health work, or a reflection that there are fewer opportunities for early career allied health professionals to practice in smaller rural sites [21], or due in part to a combination of push and pull factors identified in recruitment and retention literature [15, 22]. Further work is required at the individual discipline level to examine this relationship. Furthermore, a proportion of allied health professionals were found to be delivering services to rural areas while not being rurally based, with allied health being a predictor of ‘doing some rural work’ compared to nursing. The extent of providing some form of a rural service varied between graduate professions, with occupational therapists (24%), speech pathologists (21%), psychologists (22%), dieticians (19%), and medical radiation practitioners (17%) providing some rural work. This finding appears novel as much of the literature dichotomises working rurally or in metropolitan areas. Results did not differentiate how allied health practitioners provided ‘some rural work’, which may include regional public health service outreach [23], dedicated rural programmes [24] and telehealth and other digital service provision from metropolitan and regional organisations [25, 26]. Further research is required to fully characterise these types of service delivery.

Health graduates who were older were more likely to be working rurally than those who were younger, suggesting the importance of supporting mature‐aged students [27].

The final model used in this study accounted for less than 10% of the variance of those health professionals that indicated they practice rurally. Further research is required to determine what other factors may influence recent graduates working in rural areas.

Limitations

This study only utilised data from one University Department of Rural Health footprint. However, the GRH programme supports students from at least 15 different universities and multiple disciplines. Furthermore, because disciplines were collapsed into two categories for regression analysis purposes (nursing and allied health), detail was lost. Future work should consider each discipline individually.

Although the response rate was 67%, response bias, where respondents may have given favourable responses in the survey, and participation bias, where the cohort is not representative of the general graduate population, is likely to exist in the study sample.

Conclusion

This study provided preliminary insights into work locations of early career allied health and nursing workforces following a rural placement. Findings suggest there is complexity to understanding the impact of a rural placement given the various factors involved, including rural background, age at graduation, available rural work opportunities, and options for providing outreach to rural areas. This study highlighted the importance of rural origin, the need to support mature age students and variation between allied health disciplines in the extent to which they practice rurally or provide some rural outreach. Continued follow‐up of these professionals over time may help to identify key determinants of health professionals' workplace locations.
