Variations in Corticosteroids Used for Asthma Exacerbations in the Pediatric Emergency Department: A Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network Registry Study
Authors: Adjoa A Andoh, Melisa S Tanverdi, Daniel J Shapiro, Matthew J Lipshaw, Anna M Cushing, Cody S Olsen, Huong D Meeks, Kathryn Morris, Julie C Leonard, Joseph J Zorc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND:Systemic corticosteroids are a standard part of treatment for asthma exacerbations. Multiple options for administration exist without consensus recommendations, leading to variability in treatment regimens across sites.OBJECTIVE:To examine variations in corticosteroid regimens used to treat pediatric asthma exacerbations from 2012 to 2024 and identify sociodemographic and clinical characteristics associated with the type of corticosteroid administered.METHODS:We performed a retrospective cohort study of encounters of children aged 2 to 17 years with asthma exacerbations using the Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network Registry. Frequencies of corticosteroid types administered by site were summarized using median and interquartile ranges. Multivariable logistic regression compared treatment with enteral dexamethasone versus enteral prednisone by sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, adjusting for year and clinical site.RESULTS:The analysis included 206,388 encounters over 17 sites. Overall, 69.5% of encounters received enteral dexamethasone, 24.7% enteral prednisone or prednisolone, 4.8% intramuscular or intravenous methylprednisolone, and 1.0% intramuscular or intravenous dexamethasone. There was an increase in the proportion of patients receiving enteral dexamethasone at sites throughout the study period (P < .001). Patients with younger age, Hispanic race and ethnicity, Spanish language, and public insurance or self-pay were more likely to receive enteral dexamethasone. Encounters with emergency severity index triage level 1 or 2, moderate exacerbation severity, tachypnea, or who were overweight, were more likely to receive prednisone.CONCLUSIONS:Use of enteral dexamethasone in the management of asthma exacerbations has increased over time, and the type of corticosteroid administered varied by sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. Future studies should explore the comparative effectiveness of different corticosteroid regimens on clinical outcomes.Keywords: Asthma, Exacerbation, Corticosteroids, Pediatrics, Emergency, Trends

INTRODUCTION

Pediatric asthma exacerbations account for up to 791,000 emergency department (ED) visits and 64,000 hospital admissions per year.1 Systemic corticosteroids are a mainstay of treatment for asthma exacerbations. Corticosteroids suppress inflammation by broadly inhibiting multiple inflammatory mediators and cells in the airways, along with increasing the expression of β2-adrenergic receptors in the lungs.2 In clinical trials, early administration of systemic corticosteroids for asthma exacerbations in the ED has been shown to decrease the need for hospital admission for asthma exacerbations.3,4

Prior research has identified multiple corticosteroid regimens for pediatric asthma exacerbations. The corticosteroids most frequently used for asthma exacerbations include prednisone and prednisolone, methylprednisolone, and dexamethasone. Prednisone and prednisolone have a biological half-life of 12 to 36 hours, with a recommended treatment course of 5 to 10 days.5,6 However, the bitter taste of prednisone and prednisolone along with a longer treatment duration has led to concerns about patient nonadherence.7 Methylprednisolone can be given enterally but is traditionally reserved for use as the intravenous (IV) formulation for severe exacerbations. Oral dexamethasone, with a biological half-life of 36 to 54 hours, has recently become a favorable alternative, due to fewer doses required (a single dose or two doses given 24–48 hours apart)8 and multiple available forms with improved palatability.9,10

No consensus recommendations exist regarding corticosteroid selection for pediatric asthma exacerbations, leading to wide variability in types of corticosteroids used to manage asthma exacerbations in the pediatric ED. It is not yet known how corticosteroid selection has changed over time in pediatric EDs. Furthermore, there may be certain clinical and sociodemographic factors that influence corticosteroid choice in the management of pediatric asthma exacerbations. Physicians and health care professionals consider both previous asthma history and exacerbation severity in managing asthma exacerbations.5 The impact of sociodemographic factors on asthma outcomes is well documented in prior literature11 in terms of medication use,12 exacerbation frequency, and hospitalizations.13,14 It remains unclear how sociodemographic and clinical factors influence corticosteroid choice when multiple options are available for managing pediatric asthma exacerbations.

The objectives of this study were to (1) describe the temporal trends and site variation in types of corticosteroids administered for the treatment of pediatric asthma exacerbations over a 12-year period in pediatric EDs within the Pediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network (PECARN) Registry,15 and (2) describe associations between sociodemographic and clinical characteristics and types of corticosteroids administered in pediatric EDs for the management of asthma exacerbations.

METHODS

Data source and sample selection

We performed a multicenter retrospective cohort study using encounters from the PECARN Registry between January 1, 2012 and November 30, 2024. The PECARN Registry includes deidentified ED encounter data for 12 geographically diverse tertiary pediatric EDs and five affiliated community sites. The PECARN Registry contains comprehensive encounter data from the electronic health record of each participating ED, including physician and health care professional assessments, patient vital signs, names, routes, doses, and times of all medications administered or prescribed from the ED, and patient disposition. All data are transmitted and stored at the Emergency Medical Services for Children Data Center. This study was reviewed by Nationwide Children’s Hospital’s Institutional Review Board and determined to be non-human subject research (STUDY00004513).

Site survey

To assess whether site-level guidelines or protocols changed during the study period, we surveyed medical directors (pediatric emergency medicine physicians) at each PECARN site to characterize the corticosteroid prescribing guidelines at each ED (see Supplemental Material in this article’s Online Repository at www.jaci-inpractice.org.org). Survey questions related to this study included recommended corticosteroid per established protocols, the year each ED transitioned to using dexamethasone (if applicable), and potential situations when health care professionals or protocols may use one corticosteroid over another.

Study population

We included encounters for children ages 2 to 17 years with an asthma-related visit as previously defined in the PECARN Registry: an International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnosis of asthma (ICD, Ninth Revision: 493, and ICD, 10th Revision, J45), receipt of two or more β-agonist treatments or continuous albuterol in the ED,16 and one or more systemic corticosteroids administered or prescribed during the index visit. Visits were excluded if the patient had a complex chronic condition according to ICD, Ninth Revision or ICD, 10th Revision codes documented at the index visit;17 had a prior asthma-related visit to a PECARN Registry site within the preceding 14 days; if the patient did not receive dexamethasone, prednisone, prednisolone, or methylprednisolone; or if ED disposition did not include admitted, observation, or discharged to home.

Variable definitions

Outcomes included the number and percentage of each type of systemic corticosteroid administered. Corticosteroids were categorized as enteral prednisone (including enteral prednisolone and methylprednisolone), enteral dexamethasone, intramuscular (IM)/IV dexamethasone, or IM/IV methylprednisolone. For visits with multiple types of corticosteroids administered, we used the first type of corticosteroid administered.

Sociodemographic characteristics analyzed included age, sex, race and ethnicity, primary payer, zip code-level Child Opportunity Index (COI),18 and primary language. The COI is a composite measure of neighborhood conditions, characteristics, and quality of resources that impact children’s healthy development.19,20 Measurements of COI (scaled from 0 to 100) are broken down to quintiles from very low to very high, with lower COI associated with lower opportunities for health development.21 Race, ethnicity, and COI were included given the previously demonstrated associations between these factors and pediatric asthma health care use and outcomes.13,20,22,23 Clinical characteristics focused on markers and risks of exacerbation severity, including triage acuity level as defined by the Emergency Severity Index (ESI), tachypnea, and whether the child was overweight. The ESI is a five-level triage system based on severity of illness and perceived resource utilization, with ESI level 1 assigned to the highest acuity patients and level 5 to the lowest acuity.24 Tachypnea was defined as the first respiratory rate above age-based cutoffs included in Pediatric Advanced Life Support guidelines: greater than 40 if age less than 2 years, greater than 30 if age 2 to less than 6 years, greater than 29 if age 6 to less than 13 years, and greater than 20 if age 13 years or older.25 Overweight was defined as greater than the 95th percentile sex-specific weight-for-age according to Centers for Disease Control and Prevention growth charts for children aged 2 years and older.26 Previous studies demonstrated potential associations with overweight and obese status with higher severity of exacerbations.27–29 We also included asthma exacerbation severity, categorized as mild, moderate, or severe, based on validated mappings of the initial asthma severity score, using a previously validated approach to standardize the initial asthma severity score at each site on a single scale.16

Statistical analysis

Site-level survey responses were analyzed descriptively to summarize corticosteroid prescribing guidelines and trends between participating sites and over time. For the study cohort, we describe the overall proportion of encounters in which each corticosteroid type was administered overall and by site and year. We summarized demographic and clinical characteristics of the cohort by corticosteroid type using counts and percentages or means and standard deviations as appropriate. We calculated the proportion of asthma-related ED encounters for each corticosteroid per site per year. Because the choice of an IV or IM corticosteroid may reflect higher clinical severity that we were unable to account for, we focused the analysis on a comparison of enteral corticosteroid selection. We described variations in the proportion of enteral dexamethasone and enteral prednisone among all asthma-related ED encounters by participating clinical sites for each year during the study period using boxplots. We used a logistic regression model to test for a linear trend in the use of enteral dexamethasone over time and included the following variables: age, sex, ESI, race and ethnicity, site, and year.

Among children who received enteral corticosteroids, we used multivariable logistic regression to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) comparing sociodemographic and clinical characteristics between visits treated with enteral dexamethasone and those treated with enteral prednisone. Partially adjusted models were used to examine the association between the likelihood of receiving enteral dexamethasone and each of these variables separately: age, sex, race and ethnicity, primary payer, COI, preferred language, ESI, weight, initial asthma severity, and tachypnea. All partially adjusted models additionally include the year, clinical site, and interaction between the year and clinical site. We then constructed four fully adjusted multivariable logistic regression models to assess the relationships between corticosteroid type and each of the following sociodemographic measures: race and ethnicity, primary payer, COI, and language. Separate models were fit to address potential collinearity. Each model included age, sex, ESI, site, year, and a clinical site—year interaction, and adjustments for age, sex, and ESI. We then tested for a linear trend in the use of enteral dexamethasone over time using the race and ethnicity model with only main effects of the year and clinical site without the clinical site—year interaction. Analyses were performed in SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Survey responses

Table E1 (in this article’s Online Repository at www.jaci-inpractice.org) lists the survey responses. Of 12 pediatric EDs surveyed, 11 reported using dexamethasone as the primary recommended corticosteroid for asthma exacerbations. Of these, five pediatric EDs switched from recommending prednisone or prednisolone to dexamethasone between 2012 and 2016 and four sites switched between 2017 and 2023. Two pediatric EDs did not provide information on the timing on changes to the primarily recommended corticosteroid. Seven pediatric EDs have protocols that include both enteral dexamethasone and enteral prednisone, whereas five include only enteral dexamethasone. Nine protocols have options for IM dexamethasone, and all pediatric EDs have the option of using IV methylprednisolone. Three pediatric EDs reported that patients care for by pulmonary medicine and those with severe or difficult to treat asthma were more likely to be recommended to use prednisone or prednisolone compared to dexamethasone. Doses for enteral prednisone range from 1–2 mg/kg per dose to a maximum dose of 60 mg. The dose for enteral and IV dexamethasone is 0.6 mg/kg per dose, with maximum doses ranging from 8 to 16 mg. Doses for IV methylprednisolone range from 1 to 2 mg/kg per dose, with maximum doses ranging from 30 to 120 mg.

Study cohort

A total of 206,388 asthma-related ED encounters over 17 sites were eligible for analysis (Figure 1). The number of sites with available data increased from six in 2012 to 17 in 2024. During the entire study period, enteral dexamethasone was the most commonly administered corticosteroid (69.5% of encounters) followed by enteral prednisone (24.7%), IV/IM methylprednisolone (4.8%), and IV/IM dexamethasone (1.0%). Tables I and II list encounter-level demographic and clinical characteristics of each encounter by corticosteroid type. Regarding sociodemographic characteristics, patients were majority male and, of Black non-Hispanic origin, had public insurance, and most commonly resided in very low COI neighborhoods. A majority of encounters were assigned an ESI of 2 or 3, and most patients were normal or underweight, had a moderate initial asthma exacerbation severity score, and/or presented with tachypnea. During the study period, 35.6% of encounters required hospital admission.

Site-level variation and trends in corticosteroid type

Corticosteroid type varied by clinical site throughout the study period, as shown in Figure 2. There was a consistently increasing trend in the proportion of encounters receiving enteral dexamethasone compared with enteral prednisone in the entire cohort (P < .001) (Table III). In 2024, two sites used prednisone in over 60% of visits; the other 15 sites used enteral dexamethasone in over 80% of visits, a change from earlier years. Among the six sites submitting data to the PECARN registry in 2012, two used enteral dexamethasone exclusively whereas four used prednisone. There were only small increases in proportions of encounters receiving IV or IM dexamethasone and IV or IM methylprednisolone throughout the study period.

Sociodemographic and clinical factors associated with enteral corticosteroid type

Partially adjusted logistic regression models showed multiple significant associations between sociodemographic and clinical factors and the use of enteral dexamethasone (Figure 3; see Table E2 in this article’s Online Repository at www.jaci-inpractice.org). Children who were younger, of Hispanic ethnicity, had Spanish as the preferred language, and were enrolled in public insurance or self-pay were more likely to receive enteral dexamethasone compared with children who were older, non-Hispanic White, had English as the preferred language, or had commercial insurance. Children triaged as ESI 1, those who were overweight or had moderate asthma severity, and those presenting with tachypnea were less likely to receive enteral dexamethasone compared with children triaged as ESI 5, those of normal weight or underweight, those with mild asthma severity, and those without tachypnea. These associations remained statistically significant even after adjusting for other covariates in addition to site and year (Table E2). In the fully adjusted models, non-Hispanic Black children and children living in neighborhoods categorized as very low COI were also more likely to receive enteral dexamethasone compared with non-Hispanic White children (OR = 1.08; 95% CI, 1.01–1.16), and children from neighborhoods categorized as moderate COI (OR = 1.10; 95% CI, 1.01–1.19). Additionally, children triaged as ESI 2 were less likely to receive enteral dexamethasone compared with children triaged as ESI 5 (OR = 0.64; 95% CI, 0.42–0.99).

DISCUSSION

In this multicenter study of ED encounters for pediatric asthma exacerbations, corticosteroid types varied by clinical site, and the use of enteral dexamethasone increased substantially between 2012 and 2024. We identified multiple sociodemographic factors associated with higher odds use of enteral dexamethasone, including younger age, Hispanic and Black non-Hispanic race and ethnicity, Spanish language, very low COI, and enrollment in public insurance or self-pay. We found that encounters with multiple markers of higher asthma exacerbation severity were less likely to receive enteral dexamethasone. Our study is similar to prior studies demonstrating the increasing use of dexamethasone for pediatric asthma exacerbations; however, we add to the limited literature by exploring associations between sociodemographic and clinical factors with the type of corticosteroid administered.

When evaluating the management of asthma exacerbations, prior studies showed the increased use of dexamethasone over the past decade. Using a database of 40 US tertiary freestanding children’s hospitals, Tanverdi et al30 showed that 52.7% of pediatric asthma encounters received dexamethasone. Using the same database, a study by Ramgopal and Michelson31 showed similar rates of dexamethasone use, but also demonstrated increase adoption of dexamethasone over time, with 95.3% of children receiving dexamethasone and 4.7% receiving prednisone or prednisolone in 2024, the last year of the study period. When examining asthma exacerbation encounters in both general and pediatric EDs across the United States, Shapiro et al32 demonstrated that while prednisone remained the corticosteroid of choice for most encounters, there was an increase in proportion of visits using dexamethasone over the 11-year study period, particularly among children. A study by Best et al33 demonstrated both variability and an increasing trend in the use of dexamethasone for children hospitalized for asthma exacerbations among hospital sites. Our study further demonstrates the increasing acceptance of dexamethasone as an option for asthma exacerbation management in pediatric EDs.

The shift to dexamethasone during this study period may result from studies supporting the use of dexamethasone over prednisone in terms of clinical outcomes important to pediatric asthma exacerbation management in the ED, including early corticosteroid administration3,4,34 and revisit rates.35 Multiple studies demonstrated not only noninferiority between dexamethasone and prednisone regarding revisit and relapse rates, but also improved tolerability with dexamethasone, which may affect time to corticosteroid administration.36–39 However, these studies are limited by their single-site design, small sample size, and variable dosing regimens studied. Although there have been a few metanalyses showing similar or inconclusive results,40–44 larger multicenter studies are necessary to measure these outcomes more robustly. Additionally, future studies must examine potential differences in adverse effects on adrenal suppression and bone density by corticosteroid type and dose, and how potential side effects are considered by health care professionals treating asthma exacerbations.

Despite the potential advantages of using dexamethasone for pediatric asthma exacerbations, there may be clinical scenarios in which prednisone or prednisolone may be preferred. In our study, encounters with clinical findings indicative of more severe exacerbations were more likely to receive prednisone or prednisolone. A preference for using prednisone or prednisolone for potentially more severe exacerbations has been seen in multiple care settings and is supported by our site-survey. In the pediatric ED, DeLaroche45 showed that children assigned an ESI of 1 or 2 were 50% less likely to receive dexamethasone. In multiple studies surveying pediatric hospitalists, respondents were more likely to prescribe prednisone for children with more severe baseline asthma or current exacerbation.46,47 Although studies have shown the noninferiority of dexamethasone to prednisone, reasons for this preference for prednisone or prednisolone may be the lack of studies supporting the effectiveness of dexamethasone in moderate to severe exacerbations and the delay in physicians and health care professionals adopting new evidence for dexamethasone in exacerbation management.

Another novel finding of our study are the associations between younger age, Black non-Hispanic and Hispanic race and ethnicity, and markers associated with lower socioeconomic status (ie, very low COI and public insurance) with higher odds of dexamethasone use. This difference remained after adjusting for higher asthma exacerbation acuity (known to be more prevalent in this population)13 and for hospital site. One possible explanation for the association with younger age is the better tolerability of dexamethasone. A study by Gries et al48 reported that up to 41% of children refused additional doses of prednisone despite parental efforts. For all patients, but particularly in younger children, improved palatability of dexamethasone9 may make it easier for parents to administer to their children. Physicians and health care professionals may also have insights or observations about children from lower socioeconomic backgrounds that influence the choice of corticosteroid beyond clinical factors present during the ED encounter.49,50 Because most included study sites have standardized asthma clinical pathways available, our finding of variations in corticosteroid type across sociodemographic groups may reflect individual perceptions and past experiences when choosing which corticosteroid to use in this population.51 The known association between minority race and ethnicity and lower socioeconomic status with increased barriers to care and increased asthma exacerbation morbidity11–14 may lead physicians and health care professionals to choose dexamethasone over prednisone owing to its shorter course, leading to improved adherence. Additionally, dexamethasone’s cost-effectiveness compared to prednisone may lead health care professionals to select dexamethasone for children from families with fewer financial resources.52 Reasons for this cost savings include improved adherence with fewer revisits to the ED and fewer admissions, which might indicate that the use of dexamethasone may be more affordable for families and can help alleviate these potential barriers to care. Future work is needed to understand the reason for the differences observed in our study and to ensure that differential care across sociodemographic groups is not worsening disparities in health outcomes for children with asthma.

There are several limitations to our study. This is a retrospective study using a database limited to academic pediatric EDs and their affiliated community sites, so our findings may not be generalizable to other health care settings. Our criteria limiting inclusion to children with asthma exacerbations who received at least two doses of bronchodilators may have biased the patient population toward those with more moderate and severe exacerbations. However, current guidelines recommend corticosteroids for moderate to severe exacerbations, or those not responsive to initial bronchodilator treatments.5 Data provided to the PECARN Registry may be missing or incomplete. There are additional factors that may have influenced the choice of corticosteroid that were not accounted for in this study, including the proximity of COI neighborhoods from each study site, the available formulation at each site, baseline asthma severity, history of recurrent exacerbations, the duration of symptoms, subspecialist involvement,38 and concomitant illnesses. It is possible that some of our results reached statistical significance due to the large sample size of our study. Further studies are required to determine the clinical significance of our findings.

Conclusion

In a multisite study of pediatric EDs, we found an increasing trend in the use of enteral dexamethasone in the management of asthma exacerbations in the pediatric ED over a 12-year period. There is variation in type of corticosteroids administered in different populations. Among children who received oral corticosteroids, we identified multiple sociodemographic factors and measures of lower disease acuity that were associated with the use of dexamethasone compared with prednisone. Future studies are needed to compare the effectiveness of different corticosteroid regimens used in treating asthma exacerbations and to understand how physicians and health care professionals integrate clinical pathway guidelines, clinical severity, and social context when selecting corticosteroids for the treatment of pediatric asthma exacerbations.

This study highlights changes in practice patterns in systemic corticosteroids used for asthma exacerbations, and how sociodemographic and clinical factors may be associated with the choice of corticosteroid used for asthma exacerbation management.

How does this study impact current management guidelines?

This study provides clinicians with additional information on trends and patient factors that may influence corticosteroid administration for pediatric asthma exacerbations and how these factors may influence their own practice.
