A mild photothermal-mediated neuroimmunomodulatory composite hydrogel reverses the inflammatory osteoclast precursor phenotype to promote osteoporotic bone defect healing
Authors: Jiahao Yu, Qingcheng Song, Yiran Zhang, Shuo Zhang, Hongzhi Hu, Zhishang Wang, Xin Xing, Wei Chen, Yanbin Zhu, Yingze Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe peripheral sensory nervous system plays a crucial role in the repair of bone injuries. However, the excessive accumulation of oxidative stress and the inflammatory microenvironment lead to insufficient regeneration or dysfunction of sensory nerves, which is closely related to the impaired healing of osteoporotic bone defects (OBD). Therefore, specifically inducing sensory innervation and enhancing its regulatory function on the bone microenvironment has emerged as a highly promising therapeutic strategy. In this study, we developed a multifunctional composite hydrogel (MgAl-LDH@N/GP) by integrating nerve growth factor (NGF)-loaded MgAl-LDH nanosheets into gelatin methacrylamide (GelMA)/polydopamine (PDA) system. This hydrogel facilitates the sustained release of NGF at the bone defect site, which promotes sensory nerve growth. Furthermore, it leverages mild photothermal therapy (mPTT) to activate the transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) cation channels, thereby enhancing the secretion of the neuropeptide Substance P (SP). This combined therapy significantly reduces the proportion of inflammatory osteoclast precursors (iOCPs) at the defect site, thereby reshaping the local immune response to facilitate bone healing. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrate that the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel exhibits excellent biocompatibility. Furthermore, it effectively promotes sensory nerve growth, reverses the iOCP phenotype, and drives robust angiogenesis-osteogenesis coupling. Through this neuro-immune modulation, the system significantly accelerates high-quality bone regeneration.Keywords: Hydrogel, Sensory nerve, Photothermal, Substance P, Osteogenesis

Introduction

Against the backdrop of global population aging, the incidence of osteoporotic bone defects (OBD) has increased year by year. Due to its complex pathological microenvironment, impaired bone regeneration capacity, and prolonged healing cycle, it has become a severe challenge facing clinical orthopedics [1,2]. Traditional bone tissue engineering strategies mostly focus on inducing osteogenic differentiation or regulating various biological processes related to bone regeneration. However, as a highly integrated complex organ, the physiological homeostasis and injury repair of bone are highly dependent on a dense neurovascular network [3,4]. Nevertheless, solely regulating osteogenic differentiation cannot fully reconstruct the neural and vascular networks, making it difficult to achieve effective bone regeneration.

To date, the field of bone tissue engineering has developed some new strategies to reconstruct vascular networks to promote bone repair [5,6]. However, as a regulatory network deeply intertwined with the skeletal system, the critical promoting role of neural networks in bone regeneration is often overlooked, leading to delayed bone healing. Increasing evidence shows that sensory neural networks play an indispensable role in maintaining bone homeostasis [7,8]. Multiple studies on denervated fracture healing have confirmed that the loss of sensory nerves can lead to osteopenia and affect the fracture healing process [9,10]. Furthermore, sensory nerves can also positively regulate vascular network reconstruction and promote the proliferation and differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells by secreting various neurotrophic substances [11,12]. Therefore, elucidating the regulatory role and mechanisms of sensory nerves on the local bone microenvironment, and precisely screening and validating "nerve-bone" regulatory targets, can provide new therapeutic ideas for the clinical challenge of in situ bone regeneration of OBD, holding profound scientific significance and clinical application value.

Among numerous neuromodulation strategies, mild photothermal therapy (mPTT), as a non-invasive physical therapy approach, has demonstrated enormous application potential [[13], [14], [15]]. Studies have shown that maintaining the local temperature of the bone injury at approximately 43 °C can not only upregulate the expression of heat shock proteins to accelerate cell metabolism, thereby accelerating bone regeneration. More importantly, thermal stimulation can serve as a specific signal to activate the transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) ion channels on sensory neurons of the dorsal root ganglion (DRG) [[16], [17], [18]]. Emerging evidence indicates that following TRPV1 activation, sensory nerve endings can secrete various neuropeptides, such as calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), substance P (SP), and semaphorin 3A (Sema3A), which are widely involved in the regulation of cellular functions and the maintenance of tissue homeostasis, acting as potent "upstream regulators" to initiate the bone regeneration cascade [9,19]. Previous literature has reported that exogenous administration of SP treatment can improve mitochondrial function, induce macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype, and inhibit destructive inflammatory responses. It can also act as an effective wound healing agent to induce the proliferation and migration of epidermal cells and fibroblasts, promoting wound healing [[20], [21], [22]]. However, as a potential bone-targeted immunomodulator, SP is currently rarely reported in the field of OBD repair.

Bone tissue harbors abundant immune cells, and its repair process profoundly depends on the intimate crosstalk between the skeletal and immune systems [23]. Following bone injury, monocytes derived from bone marrow hematopoietic stem cells rapidly respond and are recruited to the defect area. They can not only polarize into classical macrophage subpopulations to finely regulate the initiation and resolution of inflammation, but also differentiate into osteoclasts upon signal stimulation, regulating the balance of bone homeostasis [24]. Osteoclast precursors (OCPs) are immature osteoclasts derived from the monocyte lineage. Under different microenvironmental conditions, osteoclast precursors can manifest as different subtypes. Homeostatic osteoclast precursors (hOCPs) can secrete platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB) to activate the angiogenesis-osteogenesis coupling process, thereby promoting bone tissue repair [25]. However, under pro-inflammatory stimulation, OCPs can exhibit an inflammatory phenotype (inflammatory OCP, iOCP), inhibiting sensory nerve regeneration and hindering nerve-bone signal transduction. They can also fuse into mature inflammatory osteoclasts with stronger osteolytic capacity, and can further activate local inflammatory responses, hindering the bone regeneration process [26,27]. Therefore, developing therapeutic strategies based on bone tissue engineering to precisely regulate monocyte lineage differentiation and reverse the inflammatory phenotype of OCPs has become a key strategy for achieving high-quality bone regeneration.

Inspired by the aforementioned mechanisms, to address the dual challenges of insufficient innervation and bone homeostasis imbalance in OBD repair, we constructed an injectable hydrogel system with a "thermo-chemical" dual neuromodulatory function (denoted as MgAl-LDH@N/GP). This hydrogel can achieve the purpose of synergistically promoting in situ bone regeneration of OBD by promoting sensory nerve growth and inducing neuropeptide release. As illustrated in Scheme 1, the MgAl-LDH@N/GP composite hydrogel can be precisely delivered to the bone defect site via simple injection, followed by in situ cross-linking to achieve minimally invasive filling, which facilitates local mechanical support. Subsequently, the mPTT effect generated under 808 nm near-infrared (NIR) irradiation activates the TRPV1 ion channels on the surface of sensory neurons, promoting the secretion of the neuropeptide SP. This process reverses the iOCPs phenotype and promotes the coupling of angiogenesis and osteogenesis. Concurrently, the embedded MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF nanosheets serve as intelligent "nanoreservoirs," enabling the long-term sustained release of nerve growth factor (NGF) and the continuous supply of bioactive metal ions (Mg2+), which synergistically improve the damaged neural and bone microenvironments. In summary, this study demonstrates that the hydrogel system can spatiotemporally coordinate multifaceted biological processes in bone healing, exhibiting remarkable repair effects in OBD. It not only provides a new strategy for multi-mechanism synergistic therapy in complex pathological environments but also highlights the immense potential of targeting nerve-bone crosstalk in tissue engineering.

Materials and methods

Materials

All chemical reagents in this study were used as received without further purification. Mg(NO3)2·6H2O (Mw: 256.415, 99%), Al(NO3)3·9H2O (Mw: 375.13, 99%), and chondroitin sulfate (ChS, >85%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. GelMA was acquired from Suzhou Yongqinquan Intelligent Equipment Co., Ltd. NGF was purchased from ABclonal Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and the Mouse SP ELISA Kit was obtained from Wuhan Fine Biotech Co., Ltd. The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), Calcein-AM/PI double staining kit, Actin-Tracker Green-488, and BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase color development kit were all supplied by Beyotime Biotechnology.

Preparation of MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF nanocomposites

MgAl-LDHs were synthesized according to previous literature [28]. Briefly, 2.735 g of Mg(NO3)2·6H2O and 2.007 g of Al(NO3)3·9H2O were dissolved in 20 mL of water. Subsequently, 80 mL of a mixed solution containing NaOH (0.5 mol/L) and NaHCO3 (0.5 mol/L) was added. The mixture was magnetically stirred until homogeneous, then transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave, and reacted in an oven at 150 °C for 24 h. The resulting samples were washed three times with deionized water and three times with water, and finally collected after drying in an oven at 60 °C.

To achieve tight binding between positively charged NGF and MgAl-LDH, negatively charged ChS was utilized to bridge MgAl-LDH and NGF via electrostatic adsorption. First, for the synthesis of the CS-NGF complex: NGF was dissolved in 50 μL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to prepare a 100 μg/mL NGF solution. Subsequently, a 0.1 mg/mL ChS solution was added, and the mixture was stirred at 4 °C for 0.5 h. In the second step, a 1 mg/mL MgAl-LDH solution was added to the aforementioned ChS/NGF complex solution and gently stirred at room temperature for 6 h. Finally, the precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 9000 rpm three times and lyophilized to yield MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF.

Preparation of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel

To prepare the gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA)/polydopamine (PDA) (GP) hydrogel, 15 mg of PDA was dissolved in 5 mL of deionized water under continuous stirring at 50 °C to obtain an aqueous PDA solution. Simultaneously, 100 mg of GelMA was dissolved in a photoinitiator lithium phenyl-2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) solution (0.25% w/v) at 60 °C. Subsequently, the two prepared hydrogel precursor solutions were mixed at a volume ratio of 1:1 and magnetically stirred for 30 min to yield the GP hydrogel precursor solution. An aqueous solution of MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF was then introduced into the GP hydrogel precursor solution. Finally, the mixture was crosslinked under 405 nm ultraviolet (UV) irradiation to form the MgAl-LDH@N/GP composite hydrogel.

Materials characterization

The morphologies and microstructures of different samples were observed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping. The thickness of the nanosheets was measured using atomic force microscopy (AFM). Zeta potentials and dynamic light scattering (DLS) were determined using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90 instrument. The chemical compositions and valence states of various samples were analyzed using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific ESCALAB 250XI).

Swelling ratio

Briefly, the initial dry weight of the lyophilized hydrogel (W0) was accurately weighed, followed by complete immersion in PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C. At predetermined time points, the hydrogel was removed, and the excess free water on the surface was gently blotted with filter paper before measuring its swollen wet weight (Wt). The swelling ratio of the hydrogel was calculated according to the following equation: Swelling ratio (%) = (Wt - W0)/W0 × 100% [29].

In vitro degradation

The in vitro degradation of the hydrogels was evaluated by monitoring their mass changes in PBS containing 2 U/mL collagenase at 37 °C. The lyophilized samples (W0) were immersed in a PBS solution containing 2 U/mL collagenase and incubated under constant shaking at 37 °C and 100 rpm. At predetermined time points, the samples were retrieved, gently rinsed with deionized water, lyophilized, and reweighed (Wt). The residual mass ratio was calculated as follows: Residual weight (%) = (Wt/W0) × 100%. Concurrently, the supernatants were harvested at each designated time point for the subsequent determination of their pH and Zeta potential values.

Photothermal performance evaluation of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel

To evaluate the in vitro photothermal performance of the hydrogel, the samples were placed in a 24-well plate and irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1.0 W/cm−2) for 5 min. Temperature variations and thermal images were recorded in real time using an infrared thermal imager (Testo). Furthermore, to assess the photothermal stability of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel, it was subjected to five cycles of 808 nm laser irradiation. Each cycle comprised a 5 min laser irradiation phase and a natural cooling phase, with the temperature recorded in real time every 30 s via the infrared thermal imager.

In vitro cytocompatibility assay

The cytocompatibility of the various hydrogels was evaluated using primary mouse bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), both of which are critical cell models intimately involved in bone regeneration. All hydrogels were subjected to dual sterilization with 75% ethanol and UV irradiation (1 h), and subsequently placed into 96-well plates. Cells were then seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well for co-culture. At 24, 48, and 72 h, the proliferation viability of the cells was evaluated using CCK-8 assay. Furthermore, at the 72 h time point, 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay and calcein-AM/PI double staining kit were utilized to assess the proliferative capacity and live/dead status of the cells, respectively. Furthermore, after 72 h of co-culture, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (T8200, Solarbio). Subsequently, the cytoskeleton and nuclei were stained with phalloidin and Hoechst 33342 for 30 min at room temperature, respectively. Finally, the cytoskeleton was observed via fluorescence microscopy.

Animal experiments

In this study, 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were utilized to establish an osteoporotic distal femoral defect model. The mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 0.3% sodium pentobarbital. The surgical site was shaved, routinely disinfected with iodophor, and draped under aseptic conditions. To perform the ovariectomy (OVX), a 1-cm longitudinal incision was made at the intersection approximately 1–1.5 cm below the costal margin and 1–1.5 cm lateral to the spinal column. The skin and dorsal muscles were incised, and the peritoneum was separated to expose the ovary, followed by blunt dissection of the peri-ovarian fat. The fallopian tube and supplying blood vessels were meticulously ligated, and the ovary was completely excised. The incision was then closed with layered sutures. The contralateral ovary was removed using the identical procedure. Postoperatively, the mice received an intraperitoneal injection of penicillin (200,000 IU/kg) to prevent infection. Twelve weeks post-surgery, the creation of the distal femoral defect model was initiated. Under continuous isoflurane anesthesia, a unicortical bone defect with a diameter of 1.0 mm was created above the distal femoral growth plate using a trephine. Subsequently, 20 μL of the hydrogel precursor solution was injected into the bone defect site, followed by UV irradiation for 30 s to achieve in situ crosslinking. For the mPTT, the implanted hydrogel was irradiated with an 808 nm NIR laser (1 W/cm2) for 5 min every 2 days post-operation, with the temperature monitored in real time using an infrared thermal imager. The mice were euthanized at 7 days post-operation, and the femur samples were harvested for subsequent micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis, histological and immunofluorescence staining. To evaluate the in vivo degradation performance, a subcutaneous implantation model was established in mice. After standard anesthesia, a longitudinal incision of approximately 5 mm was made on the midline of the mouse back. Subsequently, the sterilized hydrogel samples were implanted, and the incisions were sutured. In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed using In Vivo Imaging System. Fluorescence was excited by a 580 nm pulsed laser and captured using a 620 nm long-pass filter. The fluorescence intensity within the regions of interest (ROIs) was quantitatively analyzed using Phoenix 1.00 software.

Histology and immunofluorescence staining

For histological staining, the harvested femurs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 48 h and then decalcified in a 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution (Sigma-Aldrich) for 4 weeks, with the solution changed every 2 days. After complete decalcification, the samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4-μm-thick sections for subsequent hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome staining. Morphological changes were observed and photographed via optical microscopy.

For immunofluorescence staining, the decalcified samples were dehydrated in a graded sucrose solution series at 4 °C for 48 h and then embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT). Cryosections with a thickness of 20 μm were prepared using a cryostat at −30 °C. After rewarming, the sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 and blocking with 10% goat serum. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with corresponding primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. After staining the nuclei with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and mounting the slides, images were captured via fluorescence microscopy and quantified using ImageJ software.

Isolation of DRG

The DRGs were isolated and cultured using a standardized method. Briefly, neonatal mice were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium and disinfected by immersion in 75% ethanol. A longitudinal incision was made along the dorsal midline to retrieve the spinal column. Under a stereomicroscope, the spinal column was opened longitudinally to expose the spinal cord. The rounded DRGs located near the intervertebral foramina were extracted using microforceps and washed three times with PBS. After digestion with 0.25% trypsin for 5 min, the DRGs were transferred to confocal dishes and cultured in complete DRG medium (PRMC-G109, HyCyte) for subsequent experiments.

Isolation and culture of dorsal root ganglion cells (DRGCs)

The initial steps were consistent with the aforementioned DRG isolation procedure. After isolation, the DRGs were washed three times with PBS and digested with 0.2% collagenase type II at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by the addition of an equal volume of trypsin for an additional 15 min of digestion. Digestion was terminated using complete Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). After centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended in complete DMEM medium before being seeded onto poly-L-lysine-coated culture dishes. After 24 h, the medium was replaced with complete DRG medium supplemented with 5 μM cytosine arabinoside to inhibited the growth of non-neuronal cells. The medium was refreshed every 3 days until use for subsequent experiments.

Isolation and culture of OCPs

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from 4-week-old C57BL/6J mice. Briefly, the mice were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium followed by cervical dislocation. Under sterile conditions, the femurs and tibias were isolated, and the metaphyses were excised. The bone marrow cavity was repeatedly flushed with serum-free alpha-minimum essential medium (α-MEM) to collect the bone marrow cells, which were then placed in cell culture flasks and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After 24 h, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The old medium was replaced with complete DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 40 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and 40 ng/mL receptor activator of nuclear factor Kappa-B ligand (RANKL). OCPs were formed after 3 days of induction. After 7 days of induction, cells with three or more nuclei observed under optical microscopy were identified as osteoclasts.

Isolation and culture of BMSCs

The isolation method for BMSCs was consistent with the aforementioned procedure for OCPs. After the mice were euthanized, the femurs and tibias were isolated under sterile conditions. The bone marrow cavity was repeatedly flushed with serum-free α-MEM to collect the bone marrow cells. After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h, the medium was replaced with complete α-MEM. When the BMSCs reached 90% confluence, the cells were digested with trypsin and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. After the supernatant was discarded, the cell pellet was resuspended in complete medium and seeded into new culture dishes at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL to obtain P1 BMSCs. P3 BMSCs were used for subsequent experiments.

Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining

The OCPs obtained above were seeded into cell culture plates. Subsequently, the supernatants of DRGCs pre-treated with different hydrogel groups for 3 days were collected and mixed with complete DMEM/F12 medium containing 40 ng/mL RANKL and 20 ng/mL Interferon-γ (IFN-γ) for the in vitro inflammatory induction culture of OCPs. After 3 days, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. The substrate reaction solution was prepared according to the TRAP staining kit instructions (G1492, Solarbio). Then, the cells were fixed with a fixative for 30 min. After adding the prepared staining solution, the cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 1 h. Finally, the staining solution was discarded, and images were captured via optical microscopy.

Immunofluorescence staining

In vitro immunofluorescence staining was performed according to a standard protocol. Briefly, cells or tissue blocks were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h. After overnight incubation with primary antibodies, the samples were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies in the dark for 1 h, followed by DAPI incubation for 5 min. Staining results were imaged via an fluorescence microscopy and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using TRIzol reagent. The extracted RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA following the manufacturer's instructions of the reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan). The expression levels of target genes were detected using an ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA) in conjunction with a SYBR Green qPCR kit (Zhongshi Tongchuang, China). Three replicates were set for each sample, and the relative expression of genes was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blot

Western blot analysis was employed to assess changes in protein expression levels. After pre-treating DRGCs with different hydrogel groups for 3 days, the cells were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (G2002, Servicebio) containing protease inhibitors (G2008, Servicebio). The total protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (P0012, Beyotime, China). Protein samples (30 μg) were subjected to SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes under ice-bath conditions. The PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBST) buffer for 1 h to prevent non-specific protein binding. After washing three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C (GAPDH, 1:20,000, 10494-1-AP, Proteintech; SP, 1:500, HY-P81130, MCE). Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The target protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Biosharp, China). The optical density of the bands was measured using a Bio-Rad Gel Doc system, and the levels oaf the antigen-antibody complexes were further analyzed using ImageJ software.

Hemolysis assay

Briefly, 1.0 mL of anticoagulated blood was collected from healthy mice and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the red blood cell pellet was resuspended in 1.5 mL of PBS. Subsequently, 200 μL of the RBC suspension was added to 800 μL of different hydrogel samples. PBS and deionized water were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. All mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The absorbance of the supernatants was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. The hemolysis ratio was calculated according to the following formula: Hemolysis ratio (%)=(A1-A0)/(A2-A0)×100%. A0、A1 and A2 represent the absorbance of the negative control, hydrogel sample group, and positive control.

RNA-seq analysis

RNA-seq was employed to investigate the alterations in the gene expression profile of OCPs following SP treatment. Specifically, OCPs were cultured in a complete osteoclast medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL SP. After 24 h, the OCPs were collected, and total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer's protocol. Subsequently, sequencing and analysis were performed by CapitalBio technology (Beijing, China). Differential gene expression was visualized through heatmaps and volcano plots. Functional annotation of the transcriptomic changes was conducted via Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses to identify key signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms associated with the gene expression alterations.

In vitro evaluation of the effects of OCPs phenotypes on osteogenesis and angiogenesis

To further evaluate the impact of OCP phenotypic transition on osteogenic and angiogenic capacities, the supernatants from the co-culture of different hydrogel groups and DRGCs for 3 days were collected, centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min, and then filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. The filtered supernatants were mixed with OCP culture medium at a 1:1 ratio. After 24 h, the OCP suspension was collected. Firstly, the OCP suspension was seeded into the upper chambers of Transwell inserts at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well, while BMSCs were seeded into the lower chambers. 1 mL of the mixed cell culture medium was added to the upper chamber, and 2 mL of BMSC osteogenic induction medium (OriCell, MUXMT-90021) was added to the lower chamber, with the medium refreshed every 3 days. To evaluate alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity on day 7, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained using a BCIP/NBT ALP color development kit (Beyotime, C3206) according to the manufacturer's protocol. For Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining, cells cultured under the aforementioned conditions for 21 days were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with ARS solution to observe calcium nodules. For quantitative analysis, a 10% cetylpyridinium chloride (CPC) solution was added to each well and incubated for 30 min at room temperature to elute the bound dye. The absorbance of the resulting solution was then measured at 562 nm using a microplate reader.

Following the same co-culture method described above, the angiogenic potential of HUVECs was evaluated via a tube formation assay. Conditioned media were prepared by mixing serum-free high-glucose DMEM with the collected OCP suspension at a volume ratio of 1:1. Subsequently, using pre-cooled pipette tips, 150 μL of matrigel was carefully added to each well of a 48-well plate. After incubating the plate at 37 °C for 30 min until gelation, HUVECs were suspended in 200 μL of different conditioned media and seeded into each well. After 24 h of co-culture, the formation of tubular structures was observed via an inverted microscope. The total tube length, number of tubes, and number of junctions were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software. For the scratch healing assay, HUVECs were seeded into 6-well plates (3 × 105 cells/well) and cultured in a humidified environment at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until reaching 90% confluence. Then, a uniform scratch was created using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. After washing three times with PBS to remove cell debris, the HUVECs were incubated with different conditioned media, and images were captured at predetermined time points using an inverted microscope. The scratch healing rate was calculated using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post-hoc test was employed for multiple-group comparisons, or independent-sample t-tests were used to calculate significant differences between two groups. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and functional evaluation of MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF

Two-dimensional layered double hydroxides (LDHs), owing to their ultrahigh specific surface area, remarkable biocompatibility, and highly tunable metal composition, have demonstrated immense advantages in diverse biomedical fields such as drug delivery and tissue engineering [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. Utilizing the excellent sustained-release performance of LDHs and their abundant bioactive metal ions (such as Mg2+), anchoring growth factors onto the surface of two-dimensional LDHs is expected to simultaneously overcome the inherent challenges of in vivo inactivation and low local delivery efficiency of growth factors. Moreover, it can synergistically remodel the microenvironment of damaged tissues through the continuous release of bioactive metal ions, exhibiting tremendous application value in constructing high-performance bone tissue engineering scaffolds and reprogramming the damage microenvironment [34,35]. Therefore, MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF nanosheets were fabricated via a straightforward wet chemical synthesis approach. Initially, MgAl-LDH nanosheets were prepared by reacting a mixture of Mg(NO3)2 and Al(NO3)3 with a NaOH/NaHCO3 solution at 150 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, the positively charged NGF growth factors were functionalized onto the surface of the MgAl-LDH nanosheets through electrostatic interactions, utilizing negatively charged ChS as a linker to yield the MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF nanocomposites (Fig. 1A). The morphological features of the MgAl-LDH and MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF nanosheets were first characterized using TEM. As shown in Fig. 1B and C, all synthesized nanomaterials exhibited a uniform hexagonal morphology with a lateral dimension of approximately 2 μm, which was further corroborated by SEM analysis (Fig. 1D and E). EDS elemental mapping of the MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF revealed a homogeneous distribution of the constituent elements, including Mg, Al, C, and O (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, AFM images revealed that the thickness of the monolayer MgAl-LDH nanosheets was approximately 1.5 nm, which was not significantly altered following the successful loading of NGF via chondroitin sulfate (Fig. 1G and H). This is consistent with theoretical predictions, thereby confirming the precision of the monolayer MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF preparation process.

Additionally, the synthesis process of MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF was systematically monitored through zeta potential measurements and DLS analysis. As shown in Fig. 1I, upon functionalizing the NGF growth factors onto the MgAl-LDH surfaces, the zeta potential shifted from +11.03 ± 0.57 mV to −17.40 ± 0.76 mV, which can be attributed to the introduction of ChS. Meanwhile, DLS analysis confirmed that the size distribution of the MgAl-LDH nanosheets remained nearly unchanged before and after NGF loading (Fig. S1, Supporting Information), indicating that the functionalization process did not compromise the structural integrity of the nanosheets. To further evaluate the chemical properties of the MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF, its elemental composition and valence states were analyzed via XPS. The survey XPS spectrum displayed distinct signals for C, O, Mg, and Al (Fig. 1J). In the high-resolution XPS spectra, peaks at 1303.08 eV and 50.08 eV corresponded to Mg 1s and Mg 2p of Mg2+, respectively, while the characteristic peak for Al 2p of Al3+ was observed at 74.08 eV (Fig. S2, Supporting Information). These findings collectively confirm the successful fabrication of the MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF nanocomposites.

Fabrication and characterization of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel

As a highly promising biomedical material, GelMA retains various bioactive sequences of the natural extracellular matrix, possessing excellent biocompatibility, tunable physicochemical properties, and favorable in situ gelation capability, which render it an ideal scaffold for bone tissue engineering [[36], [37], [38]]. However, pure GelMA hydrogels lack photothermal conversion capacity and cannot provide thermal stimulation to further modulate the local neuro-osteogenic microenvironment, which to some extent constrains their therapeutic potential for promoting the repair of complex OBD. To overcome this inherent limitation, we introduced PDA into the GelMA network to construct a composite hydrogel system, endowing the hydrogel with excellent photothermal properties, and in situ loaded the MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF nanocomposites [39]. This was designed to achieve the purpose of in situ regulation of the bone regeneration microenvironment and promotion of in situ bone regeneration of OBD (Fig. 2A).

As shown in Fig. 2B, all hydrogel samples underwent rapid gelation within 30 s of UV irradiation. These hydrogels exhibited excellent shape-retention capability, maintaining their original structures without flowing or collapsing even in tilted vials, which confirms the formation of a stable three-dimensional (3D) crosslinked network. Furthermore, to evaluate the swelling properties, the hydrogels were immersed in PBS to monitor their dynamic swelling behavior. As illustrated in Fig. 2C, GelMA demonstrated a rapid initial fluid absorption capacity within 120 min, followed by a plateau in the subsequent period. Upon incorporating PDA to prepare the GP hydrogel and subsequently introducing MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF nanosheets to fabricate the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel, a slight decrease in the swelling ratio was observed compared to pure GelMA; however, no statistically significant differences were found among the groups. Concurrently, as a potential implantable material, the in vivo biodegradability of the hydrogel is of paramount importance. Therefore, the in vitro degradation behavior of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel was evaluated by monitoring the residual mass in a simulated physiological solution containing collagenase. As shown in Fig. 2D, GelMA degraded rapidly within 4 days, reaching a degradation rate of 50% by day 8. In contrast, the incorporation of PDA and the loading of MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF moderately reduced the degradation rate of the hydrogel. This may be attributed to the enhanced molecular interactions within the system. Concurrently, the pH values of the GelMA and GP groups remained consistently within the neutral baseline range during the degradation process, while their Zeta potentials were continuously maintained in a significantly negative regime. In contrast, the MgAl-LDH@N/GP composite system not only exhibited a gradual, mild alkalization trend, creating a favorable physiological microenvironment for osteogenic differentiation, but its Zeta potential also showed a significant positive upward shift over time (Fig. S3, Supporting Information). This confirmed the sustained release of cations, such as Mg2+, from the system during the degradation process. Such an appropriate degradation rate praovides sufficient time for the ingrowth of surrounding nerves while gradually releasing the encapsulated active ingredients, thereby creating a favorable microenvironment for bone tissue regeneration. Additionally, the release profiles of Mg2+ and Al3+ from the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel were characterized using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). The results revealed that the cumulative release concentrations of Mg2+ and Al3+ from the hydrogel over a 14-day period were 61.45 μg/mL and 1.23 μg/mL, respectively (Fig. S4, Supporting Information). Previous studies have demonstrated that when the local microenvironmental Mg2+ concentration is below 20 mM, it can be readily absorbed by osteogenic cells to facilitate excellent bone regeneration. Furthermore, multiple classic toxicological studies on biomaterials have confirmed that the trace amount of Al3+ released by this system is profoundly below the safety threshold for inducing cytotoxicity in endothelial cells or osteoblasts [40,41], a conclusion that aligns highly with the findings of Cheng et al. [42]. This indicates that the aluminum elements are highly and stably intercalated within the layered double hydroxide structure of the LDH, and their negligible potential dissolution remains far below the critical limit for triggering systemic damage.

The MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel was characterized using fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. As illustrated in Fig. 2E, the shift of the characteristic absorption peak at 500 cm−1 corresponds to the stretching vibration of M − O bonds. The peaks at 3289, 1630, and 1537 cm−1 were attributed to the Amide A band, Amide I band (C=O stretching), and Amide II band (N-H bending) of GelMA, respectively. Notably, the disappearance of the C-H absorption peak at 2930 cm−1 indicated that the polar groups of PDA were successfully introduced and formed a more robust hydrogen-bonding network with the amide groups of GelMA, which significantly restricted the segmental motion of the GelMA chains. These characteristic peaks and structural alterations were further corroborated by the XRD patterns of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel, collectively demonstrating the successful incorporation of PDA and MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF into the GelMA matrix and the successful fabrication of the composite hydrogel.

Subsequently, the micromorphological features of the prepared hydrogels were observed using SEM, as shown in Fig. 2F and G. Consistent with theoretical predictions, the incorporation of MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF nanosheets did not significantly alter the pore structure compared to the GelMA and GP hydrogels. All three hydrogel samples exhibited a uniform and highly interconnected three-dimensional porous structure with an average pore diameter of approximately 73.5 ± 29.3 μm, which facilitates cell infiltration into the scaffold interior. The elemental composition of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel was analyzed via SEM-EDX elemental mapping. The results revealed a homogeneous distribution of Mg and Al elements within the hydrogel matrix, demonstrating the successful loading of MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF nanosheets into the hydrogel network.

In recent years, composite hydrogels based on mPTT have demonstrated exceptional potential for clinical translation in the field of bone defect repair. These hydrogels not only modulate the osteoimmune microenvironment through mPTT effects, thereby promoting cellular metabolism and angiogenesis [18,43]. Emerging studies suggest that mild thermal stimulation under specific conditions can serve as a crucial exogenous physical signal, deeply regulating the complex neuro-osteogenic microenvironment by target-activating specific thermosensitive ion channels [44,45]. Therefore, we investigated the photothermal performance of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel under 808 nm NIR laser irradiation. As shown in Fig. 2H and I, the temperature increase of GelMA was negligible under 808 nm NIR laser irradiation. In contrast, the GP and MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogels rapidly heated up to approximately 43 °C within 5 min, which aligns with the optimal temperature range for mPTT. Subsequently, the photothermal stability of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel was evaluated through multiple laser irradiation cycles. After five consecutive cycles, MgAl-LDH@N/GP maintained a stable temperature-rise profile (Fig. 2J), indicating its outstanding photothermal stability. These results confirm the excellent suitability of the prepared MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel for continuous photothermal therapy in clinical applications.

Cytocompatibility of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel

Excellent cytocompatibility is a critical prerequisite for the in vivo application of biomaterials [46]. We investigated the biosafety of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel combined with mPTT using BMSCs and HUVECs, which are closely associated with osteogenesis and angiogenesis. As shown in Fig. 3Aand C, the CCK-8 assay results revealed that the absorbance of all four hydrogel groups increased with incubation time, indicating that the cells maintained favorable viability and proliferative capacity under different hydrogel treatments. Specifically, cell proliferation was similar across all groups at 24 h. After 48 h, the MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR group exhibited a slight enhancement in cell proliferation compared to the other three groups, although no significant differences were observed between groups. By 72 h of culture, the MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR group displayed the highest cell proliferation rate, suggesting that the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel combined with mPTT is more conducive to promoting cell proliferation.

In vitro evaluation of mPTT-promoted SP release from DRGCs

Numerous studies have demonstrated that nerve regeneration and functional recovery are crucial for bone repair [47]. As a key neuropeptide released by sensory nerve endings, SP has been proven to be abundantly secreted through the targeted activation of TRPV1 ion channels by mild thermal stimulation, which not only potently drives the reconstruction of local microvascular networks to accelerate tissue repair, but also significantly induces macrophage polarization toward the pro-reparative M2 phenotype, thereby playing a critical role in remodeling the local immune microenvironment [[48], [49], [50], [51]]. Therefore, to verify the activation effect of mPTT on the TRPV1 channel, the Fluo-4 AM calcium fluorescent probe was employed in this study to detect calcium influx levels in DRG neurons under various treatment conditions. Compared with the other three groups, the MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR group exhibited a significantly enhanced neuronal calcium fluorescence intensity under near-infrared photothermal stimulation, indicating that mild photothermal stimulation effectively activates the TRPV1 channel and promotes calcium influx in DRG neurons (Fig. S5, Supporting Information). Subsequently, to investigate whether the mPTT could promote SP release, DRGCs were cultured on different hydrogel substrates to evaluate their capacity for SP secretion (Fig. 4A). As shown in Fig. 4B and C, immunofluorescence staining revealed that under NIR irradiation, the expression of SP within neuronal nuclei (NeuN)-positive neurons in the MgAl-LDH@N/GP group was significantly increased. Consistent with the immunofluorescence results, WB analysis and quantitative data (Fig. 4D and Fig. S6, Supporting Information) demonstrated that the protein expression level of SP in the MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR group was significantly upregulated compared to the other three groups. Furthermore, we employed ELISA to precisely quantify the SP concentration in the cell culture supernatants. As shown in Fig. S7 (Supporting Information), under 808 nm NIR irradiation, the extracellular SP secretion in the MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR group was significantly elevated, indicating that mild photothermal stimulation effectively triggered the synthesis and secretion of SP in sensory neurons.

Following the occurrence of a bone defect, the growth capacity of peripheral nerve fibers is a prerequisite determining the high-quality repair of bone tissue. Therefore, in this study, a DRG explant culture model was employed to evaluate the nerve growth-promoting capability of the hydrogels. Various hydrogel formulations were plated in 35-mm culture dishes, followed by the addition of freshly isolated DRG explants. After 3 days of culture, the maximum neurite extension distance was measured using β3-Tubulin staining. As depicted in Fig. 4E, while all hydrogels supported axonal extension to some extent, the MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR group exhibited the most remarkable promoting effect. The maximum neurite outgrowth distance in this group reached up to 1508.90 ± 99.27 μm (p < 0.001). In comparison, the MgAl-LDH@N/GP group demonstrated a relatively moderate effect with an extension distance of 921.60 ± 143.73 μm, a difference that can likely be attributed to the accelerated release of NGF triggered by NIR irradiation (Fig. S8, Supporting Information). Conversely, the GelMA and GP matrices showed weaker activity in supporting axonal outgrowth. Taken together, these findings provide compelling evidence that the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel, when coupled with mPTT, not only enhances SP expression but also robustly promotes the axonal elongation of DRG.

SP reverses the iOCP phenotype via the ATF4-CHAC1 pathway

As crucial regulators of the local microenvironment, hOCPs not only secrete PDGF-BB to activate angiogenesis-osteogenesis coupling for promoting bone repair, but also secrete the neuroguidance factor Netrin-1 under specific conditions to induce sensory nerve ingrowth, thereby deeply participating in the synergistic reconstruction of neurovascular networks [25,52]. Conversely, iOCPs can inhibit the regeneration of CGRP-positive sensory nerves, impede nerve-bone signal transduction, and delay the bone repair process [53]. Therefore, reversing the iOCPs phenotype is beneficial for sensory nerve regeneration following bone injury. In this study, to evaluate the effect of SP on the OCP phenotype in an inflammatory microenvironment, we utilized IFN-γ and RANKL co-stimulation to induce the generation of iOCPs. Building upon this model, conditioned media from DRGCs treated with different hydrogels were introduced for targeted intervention. TRAP staining revealed that a large number of mature multinucleated osteoclasts were formed in the GelMA and GP groups. However, following intervention with the SP-rich conditioned medium from the MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR group, the number and spreading area of TRAP-positive cells were significantly reduced (Fig. 5A and Fig. S9, Supporting Information).

Furthermore, to investigate the effects of different treatment conditions on the iOCPs phenotype, immunofluorescence staining was performed for the iOCP markers TRAP and CX3CR1. As shown in Fig. 5B and Fig. S10 (Supporting Information), a significant decrease in the fluorescence intensity of CX3CR1 within TRAP + cells was observed in the MgAl-LDH@N/GP group, which was further reduced following m PTT. In addition, OCPs treated with MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR exhibited a pronounced morphological transition toward hOCPs, characterized by a substantial reduction in cell spreading area and a decreased degree of multinucleation.

This phenotypic transition was further verified at the transcriptional level via RT-qPCR. Following inflammatory induction, OCPs displayed typical inflammatory characteristics. Notably, treatment with the supernatant from the MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR group significantly downregulated the expression of the iOCPs marker Itgam and the pro-inflammatory markers Il-23a, Tnf, and Il-1b. Conversely, the expression levels of anti-inflammatory factors (Tgfb1 and Il-10) were significantly upregulated (Fig. 5C–H). These results demonstrate that the hydrogel-mediated release of SP effectively reversed the iOCP phenotype and ameliorated the inflammatory microenvironment.

More importantly, to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the SP-mediated phenotypic reversal in iOCPs, RNA-seq was employed to evaluate the alterations in mRNA expression profiles under different treatment conditions. The results revealed a total of 148 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the SP-treated and control groups, comprising 64 upregulated and 84 downregulated genes (Fig. 5I and J). Subsequent GO enrichment analysis of these DEGs demonstrated significant enrichment in terms such as "proton-transporting ATP synthase complex, coupling factor F(o)" and "glutathione specific gamma-glutamylcyclotransferase activity" (Fig. 5K). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) highlighted signaling pathways intimately associated with cellular energy metabolism and protein homeostasis, such as the "Oxidative Phosphorylation" and "Proteasome" pathways (Fig. 5L). These findings indicate that SP treatment can attenuate oxidative stress and restore the redox balance within iOCPs, which is highly consistent with the aforementioned phenotypic reversal of iOCPs driven by SP.

Furthermore, visualizing the differential gene expression changes via heatmaps revealed that SP treatment significantly downregulated the expression levels of cation transport regulator-like protein 1 (CHAC1) and its upstream transcription factor, activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), in OCPs (Fig. 5M). CHAC1 is a cytosolic glutathione-degrading enzyme; in macrophages, it can positively regulate inflammation-induced macrophage activation by triggering oxidative stress responses [54,55]. Hyperactivation of the ATF4-CHAC1 pathway can lead to the aberrant accumulation of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and induce endoplasmic reticulum stress [56]. Conversely, studies have demonstrated that the release of SP can significantly downregulate the expression of classical pro-inflammatory cytokines in local tissues, thereby mitigating oxidative stress-induced damage. Taken together, the SP-ATF4-CHAC1 axis likely influences the OCP phenotype by modulating ROS levels, subsequently impacting the local microenvironment of OBD.

In vitro evaluation of the effects of SP-mediated reversal of the iOCP phenotype on osteogenesis and angiogenesis

Bone regeneration is highly dependent on the dynamic crosstalk among osteoclasts, osteoblasts, and endothelial cells within the bone microenvironment [57]. In this study, conditioned media from OCPs subjected to different treatments were collected to evaluate the impact of reversing the iOCP phenotype on osteogenesis and angiogenesis. Initially, the effect of reversing the iOCP phenotype on osteogenesis was investigated via immunofluorescence staining for the osteogenic markers RUNX2 and OPN. As shown in Fig. 6A–C, with the introduction of MgAl-LDH@N/GP and mPTT treatment, the expression of RUNX2 and OPN gradually increased, accompanied by progressively enhanced green fluorescence. Furthermore, to evaluate the osteogenic activity of BMSCs, the activity of ALP was assessed. As shown in Fig. 6D and E, compared with the control group, ALP staining in the MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR group exhibited a deeper blue color with a substantially increased positive area ratio, indicating that the early osteogenic differentiation process was effectively promoted. Meanwhile, late-stage osteogenic mineralization was detected via ARS staining. The results revealed that a large number of dark red mineralized nodules were formed in the MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR group, accompanied by a significant increase in absorbance at 562 nm (Fig. 6D and F). This demonstrates that this therapeutic regimen possesses significant advantages in inducing osteoblast mineralization and promoting bone matrix formation.

In vivo evaluation of neurogenesis and SP-mediated reversal of the iOCP phenotype

Given that the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel combined with NIR-triggered mPTT demonstrated exceptional capabilities in remodeling the osteoimmune microenvironment and promoting angiogenesis-osteogenesis bidirectional coupling in vitro, we further constructed an osteoporotic bone defect model in C57BL/6J mice to comprehensively evaluate the in situ bone repair efficacy of this synergistic therapeutic strategy within a complex in vivo microenvironment. To assess the systemic toxicity and biosafety profile of the implanted hydrogel before conducting in vivo therapeutic evaluations, a standard hemolysis assay was performed. As presented in Fig. S12 (Supporting Information), the positive control group demonstrated a bright red supernatant, indicative of extensive erythrocyte lysis and hemoglobin liberation. Conversely, all experimental groups exhibited minimal hemolytic activity, confirming the favorable hemocompatibility of the hydrogel system. Subsequently, we harvested the major organs and the skin and muscle tissues at the irradiated site of the mice for H&E staining, alongside conducting serum biochemical assays. As expected, no obvious pathological abnormalities, inflammatory lesions, or structural damage were observed in any of the organs. Furthermore, the soft tissues exhibited no thermal ablation, coagulative necrosis, or massive inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. S13, Supporting Information). Hepatic and renal function markers, as well as serum Mg2+ concentrations, all remained within normal physiological ranges Fig. S14 (Supporting Information). Furthermore, the in vivo fluorescence tracing results demonstrated that the fluorescence intensity of the implanted MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel exhibited a progressive attenuation over time, with the signal being nearly quenched by day 20 (Fig. 7A and Fig. S15, Supporting Information). This confirms the excellent degradability and biosafety of the prepared material. Subsequently, The various hydrogels were implanted into the distal femoral bone defects of the mice, followed by 808 nm laser irradiation. The results indicated that the local temperature in the MgAl-LDH@N/GP group increased rapidly within 300 s of NIR irradiation and was maintained at approximately 43 °C (Fig. 7B and Fig. S16, Supporting information), confirming the excellent and controllable in vivo photothermal responsiveness of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP system. This not only effectively promotes bone regeneration but also prevents tissue thermal damage caused by excessive temperatures. Collectively, these findings substantiate that the MgAl-LDH@N/GP platform exhibits exceptional and precisely tunable in vivo photothermal responsiveness, coupled with robust biosafety. This optimized thermal regulation not only potently facilitates bone regeneration but also strictly safeguards surrounding tissues against hyperthermia-induced thermal injury.

MgAl-LDH@N/GP combined with mPTT accelerates vascularized bone regeneration in vivo

To comprehensively verify the in vivo translational potential of this multimodal synergistic osteogenic strategy, we further evaluated the in situ bone repair capacity of MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR via micro-CT, histological staining, and immunofluorescence staining. 3D reconstructed micro-CT images of the distal femoral bone defects following different hydrogel treatments revealed that, compared to the GelMA and GP groups, the MgAl-LDH@N/GP group exhibited partial bone formation at the defect margins. Notably, almost complete defect bridging accompanied by a massive amount of dense newly formed bone was achieved following the combination with mPTT (Fig. 8A). This demonstrates the pivotal role of MgAl-LDH@N/GP combined with mPTT in creating a microenvironment conducive to bone regeneration by promoting neurogenesis and releasing SP to reverse the iOCP phenotype. Furthermore, quantitative micro-CT analysis indicated that the MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR group exhibited superior repair outcomes across all bone morphometric parameters. Specifically, this group not only achieved the highest bone volume fraction (BV/TV, 54.7% ± 6.9%) but also displayed the optimal trabecular number (Tb.N), maximum trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), minimum trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), and highest bone mineral density (BMD) (Fig. 8B–E and Fig. S17, Supporting information). The aforementioned quantitative imaging data confirm the exceptional performance of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR therapeutic strategy in optimizing the osteoimmune microenvironment and inducing high-quality osteogenesis.

This indicates that the combination of MgAl-LDH@N/GP and mPTT exerts an ideal effect in enhancing angiogenesis-osteogenesis coupling and accelerating bone matrix deposition. Moreover, consistent with the aforementioned results, RT-qPCR revealed that the mRNA levels of key osteogenic markers, including COL1, RUNX2, and OCN, were significantly upregulated in the MgAl-LDH@N/GP + NIR group compared to the other treatment groups (Fig. 8G–I). In summary, the above in vivo results demonstrate that the combined application of the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel and mPTT can not only establish crucial nerve-bone crosstalk by promoting the early reconstruction of sensory neural networks, but also induce the targeted release of the neuropeptide SP from sensory neurons, thereby effectively reversing the inflammatory phenotype of OCPs, creating optimal conditions for the restoration of microenvironmental homeostasis, and ultimately achieving a significant enhancement of in situ osteogenic efficacy.

Traditional biomaterials for bone defect repair are largely confined to providing mere physical support or direct stimulation to osteoblastic lineages, typically overlooking the critical regulatory role of peripheral sensory nerves within the bone homeostatic microenvironment. Furthermore, existing neuromodulation strategies frequently encounter translational bottlenecks, including short in vivo half-lives, high reliance on passive matrix degradation, and an inability to achieve localized, on-demand release at the lesion site. To overcome these methodological limitations, the intelligent MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel combined with mild photothermal therapy developed in this study achieves a significant paradigm shift. On the one hand, this system utilizes mild photothermal therapy to efficiently translate physical optical signals into intracellular biological signals. This NIR-triggered, on-demand, and controllable pulsed release of SP overcomes the inherent flaws of passive release seen in traditional static materials, demonstrating exceptional physical-biological cascading intelligent responsiveness [58]. On the other hand, by advancing the intervention strategy, this system leverages MgAl-LDH nanosheets for the programmed loading of NGF to induce the early ingrowth of sensory nerve terminals at the defect site. Through spatiotemporal coupling, these two mechanisms successfully remodel the pathological inflammatory microenvironment within the bone defect area, ultimately achieving high-quality, accelerated in situ bone regeneration.

Despite the promising spatiotemporal cascade strategy proposed in this study, several limitations remain to be addressed. First, while our study establishes the causal link between mPTT-induced SP release and the reversal of the iOCP phenotype, the involvement of the ATF4-CHAC1 signaling pathway in iOCPs was not explicitly validated. Future investigations utilizing gene-specific knockout or overexpression models are warranted to elucidate this mechanism. Second, within the complex in vivo microenvironment, it remains challenging to completely decouple the direct tissue-repair effects of mPTT from the biological responses mediated by the triggered SP release. Nevertheless, our in vitro experiments have confirmed the pivotal role of SP in specifically reversing the inflammatory phenotype of iOCPs and its subsequent enhancement of osteogenic function. Third, relying on surface infrared thermography to approximate deep-tissue temperature imposes methodological constraints. Future translational studies will focus on integrating implantable microsensors to achieve precise in situ thermometric monitoring of the deep target regions.

Conclusion

In summary, this study designed a multifunctional hydrogel (MgAl-LDH@N/GP) with dual "thermo-chemical" neuromodulation to promote the healing of osteoporotic bone defects. Utilizing the photothermal conversion capability of PDA, the MgAl-LDH@CS-NGF-based therapeutic platform can achieve localized mild photothermal-enhanced secretion of the sensory neuropeptide SP under NIR irradiation, and reverse the iOCP phenotype to reduce abnormal intracellular ROS accumulation. By constructing this favorable microenvironment, the MgAl-LDH@N/GP hydrogel combined with mPTT can significantly promote the differentiation of BMSCs and angiogenesis, further advancing the bone regeneration process. This multimodal synergistic therapeutic strategy highlights the unique value of our system in orchestrating neuro-osteogenic crosstalk and achieving high-quality bone tissue regeneration. It fundamentally circumvents the severe secondary trauma and high infection risks associated with traditional open bone grafting surgeries, demonstrating immense application potential in addressing clinically challenging, irregular critical-sized bone defects. Furthermore, it provides a novel strategy for the field of bone tissue engineering to enhance bone regeneration outcomes through targeted neural intervention.
