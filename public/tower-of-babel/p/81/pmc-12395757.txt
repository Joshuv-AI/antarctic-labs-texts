Effects of sufentanil combined with remifentanil on hemodynamics during induction and postoperative recovery quality in preschool children undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy
Authors: Xinlei Lu, Siyue Li, Ye Xuan, Mengting Shen, Weiping Lei, Yaqin Huang, Yibin Tao, Yuan Cheng, Jianliang Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study was designed to assess the effects of sufentanil combined with remifentanil on hemodynamics during induction and postoperative recovery quality in preschool children undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy.MethodsA total of 90 pediatric patients undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy under general anesthesia from October 2024 to March 2025 were included and randomly stratified to receive either Group R or Group S with equal probability. In Group R, anesthesia induction used sufentanil (0.2 µg/kg) combined with remifentanil (2 µg/kg), while only sufentanil (0.4 µg/kg) was used for anesthesia induction in Group S. The rest of the induction drugs were the same in both groups. After drug administration, patient vital signs, such as heart rate (HR) and mean arterial pressure (MAP), were monitored and recorded before induction (T0), immediately before tracheal intubation (T1), 1 min after tracheal intubation (T2), immediately after mouth gag placement (T3), immediately after extubation (T4), and 5 min post-extubation (T5), respectively. Additional outcomes of interest included anesthesia recovery time, an intubation condition score (Copenhagen Consensus Scale), emergence agitation (Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium [PAED] score), a postoperative pain assessment score (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, and Consolability [FLACC] score), and adverse events.ResultsThe primary outcome is postoperative awakening time (the time from the end of surgery to tracheal tube removal), which was shorter in Group R than in Group S, but this difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). The secondary outcomes include intraoperative hemodynamic parameters, intubation condition score (Copenhagen Consensus Scale), emergence agitation (Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium [PAED] score), a postoperative pain assessment score (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, and Consolability [FLACC] score), and adverse events containing agitation during the awakening period, respiratory obstruction, nausea and vomiting, hypoxemia, and bradycardia. MAP was lower in Group R than in Group S at all time points, and was significantly lower at T3 (P < 0.05). HR was lower in Group R than in Group S at most time points, including being significantly lower at T2 (P < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups among the aforementioned secondary outcomes.ConclusionsAmong preschool children undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy, the combination of sufentanil and remifentanil in general anesthesia ensured stable hemodynamics during induction and surgery, reduced the total dose of opioid anesthetics, shortened recovery time without compromising quality, and led to no significant adverse reactions.Trial registrationChina Clinical Trial Center with registration number: ChiCTR2400090213,09/26/2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03315-4.Keywords: Sufentanil, Remifentanil, Preschool children, Tonsillectomy, Adenoidectomy

Introduction

Chronic tonsillitis and adenoidal hypertrophy are common conditions in pediatric otorhinolaryngology [1], being clinically characterized by a triad of obstructive symptoms: dyspnea, nocturnal snoring, and dysphagia [2]. Severe cases may impair the development of the neighboring organs or even lead to memory loss and intellectual disabilities [3, 4]. Despite the relatively brief procedural duration of the surgery (median, 25–40 min), the oropharyngeal region’s dense sensory innervation and intense surgical stimulation can elicit significant pediatric stress responses [5], precipitating profound hemodynamic fluctuations. The oral mucosal damage and pain that occur during the operation can also trigger agitation in children during the awakening period [6], such as involuntary limb movements, crying out, or moaning. These not only prolong the recovery time but also increase the rebleeding risk; as such, these surgeries are typically performed under general anesthesia with tracheal intubation, and anesthesia drugs with stable effects, mild associated intraoperative stress reactions, and rapid induction and awakening are usually selected.

Sufentanil is a highly selective µ-opioid receptor agonist commonly used in general anesthesia given its fast onset of action, good analgesic effect, quick recovery, mild respiratory depression, ability to suppress intubation-induced stress response, and maintenance of cardiovascular system stability [7]. However, sufentanil is characterized by a long duration of respiratory depression. Moreover, increasing the dosage of this medication can prolong the recovery time of spontaneous respiration, extubation time, and emergence. In contrast, remifentanil is an ultra-short-acting µ-opioid receptor agonist with unique pharmacokinetic characteristics; such as rapid onset of action, rapid clearance, and minimal accumulation. Similarly, it has minimal impacts on respiratory and circulatory functions in children, ensuring rapid emergence and recovery [8].

Sufentanil is significantly more lipophilic than remifentanil and can cross the blood–brain barrier easily; therefore, its analgesic effect is stronger and lasts longer than that of remifentanil [9]. Combining sufentanil and remifentanil is a logical approach: the ultra-short-acting analgesic effect of remifentanil can support a reduced dosage of sufentanil during the anesthesia induction, and more stable hemodynamics and earlier postoperative extubation can be achieved as a result.

This proof-of-concept study implemented a dual-phase analgesic strategy by synergizing the pharmacological merits of both agents: sufentanil could sustain baseline analgesia to prevent intraoperative injurious stimulation and prevent postoperative pain [10]. The present study explored the feasibility, safety, and clinical effects of combining these two drugs in anesthesia for preschool children, aiming to provide a theoretical basis for improving pediatric anesthesia.

Materials and methods

Participants

The present study was approved by the ethics committee of the First People’s Hospital of Hangzhou affiliated with Westlake University (ethics approval no.: ZN-2024309-02). It was registered in the China Clinical Trial Registry (registration no.: ChiCTR2400090213) and conducted in accordance with the Code of Practice for the Quality Management of Clinical Trials of Medicinal Products of the International Coordination for the Registration of Pharmaceuticals for Human Use and the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or guardians of all participants included in the study.

Subjects

It is a randomized, single-blind, controlled study. Patients 3–7 years of age with an American Association of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status of 1–2 and a weight of 10–30 kg undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy and whose parents or guardians provided their informed consent were enrolled. Exclusion criteria included family refusal to participate in the study, previous history of cardiac disease (including tetralogy of Fallot, patent ductus arteriosus, and ventricular septal defect), acute tonsillitis, recent respiratory infection or other system disorders. Study withdrawal criteria (criteria for exclusion, dropout, or termination) included the surgery duration exceeding 2 h, laryngospasm during induction of anesthesia or after removal of the endotracheal tube, voluntary subject withdrawal from the trial, serious adverse reactions, and the subject being lost to follow-up.

Intraoperative anesthesia management

All enrolled patients who met the study inclusion criteria underwent standardized preoperative fasting (≥ 8 h for solids, ≥ 4 h for clear liquids). Upon entering the operating room, continuous monitoring of non-invasive blood pressure, HR, and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) was performed, with facemask oxygen delivered consistently at 8 L/min. Atropine (0.01 mg/kg) was administered 3 min prior to induction to suppress secretory activity and stabilize cardiac rhythm. Anesthesia was induced by dexamethasone (0.1 mg/kg), midazolam (0.1 mg/kg), propofol (3 mg/kg), and cisatracurium (0.05 mg/kg). All children received general anesthesia with tracheal intubation. In Group R, anesthesia induction used sufentanil (0.2 µg/kg), followed by remifentanil (2 µg/kg, administered slowly over 30 s after 3 min of assisted ventilation), with intubation performed 90 s after remifentanil administration. In Group S, sufentanil was administered at 0.4 µg/kg for the induction of anesthesia, followed by tracheal intubation after 5 min of assisted ventilation.

Following successful intubation, mechanical ventilation was performed with a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg and a respiratory rate of 16–20 breaths/min, and respiratory parameters were adjusted according to the end-tidal CO2 concentration and maintained at 35–45 mmHg. In both groups, patients inhaled 2–4% sevoflurane. The inhalation of sevoflurane was stopped immediately after the end of surgery, and 15 mg of ketorolac tromethamine was given intravenously for postoperative analgesia. We monitored and recorded vital signs (HR, systolic blood pressure [SBP], diastolic blood pressure [DBP], mean arterial pressure [MAP], and SpO₂ at multiple time points, including before induction (T0), just before intubation (T1), 1 min after intubation (T2), just after placing the mouth gag (T3), just before extubation (T4), and 5 min after extubation (T5). Intubation conditions were also assessed using the Copenhagen Consensus Scale.

Upon arrival in the post-anesthesia care unit, patients’ SBP, DBP, MAP, HR, and SpO₂ were monitored. Additionally, the time to emergence, time to extubation, agitation score during emergence (Pediatric Anesthesia Emergence Delirium [PAED] score), and pain score (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, and Consolability [FLACC] score) were recorded. If the child showed restlessness, 20 mg of propofol was intravenously injected for sedation. If the postoperative pain (FLACC) score of the child was ≥ 4 points, 0.5 µg/kg of fentanyl was given for rescue analgesia. The child could only leave the recovery room when their Aldrete score reached 9 points.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of this study was the postoperative emergence time (defined as the interval from the end of the surgery to endotracheal tube removal).

Secondary outcomes

The intraoperative hemodynamic parameters included MAP, HR, SBP, DBP, and SpO₂ measured at six time points: before induction (T0), just before intubation (T1), 1 min after intubation (T2), immediately after mouth gag placement (T3), just before extubation (T4), and 5 min after extubation (T5). Perioperative indicators included children’s operation time and anesthesia duration. Other rating scales included anesthesiologist induction satisfaction (10-point visual analog score [VAS] score), T4 and T5 Ramsay sedation scores, PAED scale after T4 extubation, and FLACC scale after T4 extubation. The incidence of adverse events (respiratory obstruction, nausea and vomiting, and bradycardia) during the first 24 h postoperatively was also recorded.

Sample size

The target sample size was prospectively calculated with PASS 15 (NCSS LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA) according to data from previous pediatric anesthesia trials, employing a 1:1 parallel-group, non-inferiority design [11]. A one-sided significance level (α) of 0.05 was set, with a power (1 − β) of 80%. Additionally, the non-inferiority margin was set at 0.5 [12] and the true difference was set at 0.92. The calculation results indicated that 46 subjects were required in each group. Considering a 10% rate of loss to follow-up, it was decided that a total of 100 pediatric patients were required for this study (n = 50 per group).

Randomization and grouping

An assistant who was not involved in the surgical procedure prepared the study drugs. The anesthesiologist then administered anesthesia according to the protocol in the sealed envelope. The patient, anesthesiologist, nurses, data collectors, and statistical analysts were all blinded to the group assignments. The children were randomly divided into two groups at a ratio of 1:1; those in Group S received sufentanil 0.4 µg/kg, while those in Group R received sufentanil 0.2 µg/kg in combination with remifentanil 2 µg/kg for the induction of anesthesia. A random number table was generated through SPSS 27.0. A full-time researcher managed the anonymization process, including the random numbering system and the preparation of intraoperative study drugs. After completing a preoperative screening using the inclusion, exclusion, and withdrawal criteria, the anesthesia team determined the anesthesia protocol on the surgery day according to the random numbering scheme. An anesthesiologist who was aware of the group assignments administered anesthesia, while an assistant not involved in anesthesia management prepared the drugs. The children, nursing team, postoperative follow-up personnel, and statistical researchers remained blinded to the group information. In this single-blind study, independent researchers conducted data collection and statistical analysis without participating in clinical interventions.

Statistical processing

Data analysis was performed using the SPSS 27.0 software program (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Measurement data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation values (x ± s), and enumeration data are expressed as the number of cases or a rate. Repeated measures analysis of variance, the q test, a paired t test, and the chi-squared (χ²) test were conducted on the data of the two study groups. Among them, the results of the main indicators were tested using the non-inferiority study test, and a one-sided t test was further carried out with α = 0.025. If P < 0.025, there was a significant superiority in the difference; for the remaining tests, P < 0.05 denoted a statistically significant difference. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean, standard deviation and frequency.

Results

A total of 120 patients were screened. Of these, 20 patients were excluded, including 4 who did not meet the inclusion criteria, nine who refused to participate in the study, and seven who met the exclusion criteria, and the remaining 100 children were randomly assigned. Thereafter, five children in Group R withdrew from the study, and three children in Group S also withdrew. Subsequently, two children in Group S were also excluded from the analysis because the duration of their procedure was longer than 2 h. Finally, 45 children each from Groups R and S were included in the study (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The results of this study showed that the emergence time of the patients in Group R was shorter than that in Group S, albeit without a statistically significant difference. In addition, there was no significant difference in the intubation condition score or the VAS score between the two groups. These findings suggest that the combination of sufentanil and remifentanil can ensure stable hemodynamics during induction and surgery, reduce the total dose of opioid anesthetics, shorten recovery time without compromising quality, and lead to no significant adverse reactions.

Post-tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy procedures carry inherent risks of uvular edema and increased pharyngeal airway resistance, significantly increasing the potential for severe postoperative respiratory complications [15]. General anesthesia is often used for these short procedures in children, requiring stable intraoperative anesthesia, rapid postoperative awakening, and no delayed respiratory depression or metabolic residues of anesthetic drugs [16]. However, as children’s organ development is incomplete, anesthetic drug metabolism may be affected, so selecting appropriate agents is crucial [17].

Sufentanil is a commonly used intravenous anesthetic in clinical practice with high fat solubility and a strong analgesic effect. This medication can achieve rapid and stable analgesia during pediatric anesthesia induction, but the dosage should be calculated strictly according to the child’s body weight and age. Severe bradycardia, cardiac arrhythmias, or even an absence of spontaneous respiration may occur with sufentanil use in children, and thus, respiratory support must be prepared accordingly [18].

Remifentanil, an opioid receptor agonist, has rapid onset of action and half-life of less than 10 min, with minimal risk of accumulation. Its duration of action is relatively short, which is typically 5–10 min [19]. As such, its use enables children to wake up rapidly and recover quickly after their operation. However, its administration may also lead to adverse reactions such as hypotension, bradycardia, and respiratory depression [4, 20, 21]. Therefore, caution is necessary during the clinical application of remifentanil [22].

This study additionally demonstrates that, after using remifentanil to inhibit the effects of strong stimuli, such as tracheal intubation and mouth gag placement, the dosage of sufentanil during the perioperative period can be reduced without an increase in the degree of postoperative pain or adverse effects such as postoperative agitation. Huang et al. [23] previously investigated the effects of remifentanil (1 µg/kg) in combination with sufentanil (0.3 µg/kg given prior to pneumoperitoneum) versus sufentanil alone (0.3 µg/kg) on hemodynamics and oxidative stress of adult patients undergoing resection for rectal cancer. Their results showed that the combination of remifentanil and sufentanil could significantly reduce the stress response of these patients. Notably, the combined use of sufentanil and remifentanil may exhibit additive or synergistic effects, which helps stabilize the anesthesia process and thus reduces the occurrence of adverse reactions [24].

The mean difference in the emergence time between our two study groups was approximately 1.5 min, exceeding the preset non-inferiority margin of 0.5 min. Moreover, the difference favored the experimental group (i.e., faster emergence time), contrary to the preset non-inferiority direction. However, the results of the t test revealed that there was no statistical difference in the emergence time. This final result could be attributed to a non-inferiority study design that was adopted in this trial. Yu et al. [19] found in a retrospective study of pediatric patients undergoing transthoracic ventricular septal defect occlusion that remifentanil-based anesthesia ensured more stable hemodynamic indices than sufentanil at various surgical stages, such as skin incision, thoracotomy, suture of the incision, and extubation. Their findings align with our results, suggesting remifentanil can effectively suppress perioperative stress responses.

There were slight differences in the hemodynamic parameters between our two groups. At T3, the MAP of Group R was significantly lower than that of Group S, and the difference was statistically significant. In contrast, the HR of Group R was lower than that of Group S at all time points except T4, and there was a significant difference between the two groups at T2. This indicates that, in this study, stimulating procedures such as endotracheal intubation, skin incision, and mouth gag placement can trigger pronounced tachycardia in pediatric patients. This surge in heart rate exacerbates the imbalance between systemic oxygen delivery and consumption. Consequently, a prophylactic low-dose bolus of remifentanil can effectively blunts these explosive stress responses, offering a clinically advantageous strategy for this pediatric age group. Thus, Group R had less perioperative stress and better physiological stability than Group S. This is similar to the results of Xue et al. [25, 26], who compared the effects of pediatric application of remifentanil 2 µg/kg and fentanyl 2 µg/kg at the time of induction of anesthesia on cardiovascular and intubation responses; these authors noted that the percentage changes relative to baseline in blood pressure and HR during intubation, as well as the rate pressure product and its maximum value, were significantly increased at each observation point in the fentanyl group, whereas both in the remifentanil group were significantly decreased. Perioperative stress was also reduced in the remifentanil group.

In our study, there was no difference in SpO2 between the two groups at all time points (P > 0.05). This indicated that the two groups were comparable in terms of perioperative clinical safety.

There were also no significant differences between the two groups in terms of intubation condition scores and anesthesiologist induction satisfaction scores (VAS). This suggested that patients in both Groups R and S achieved satisfactory intubation conditions. According to Batra et al. [27], dosing remifentanil at 3 µg/kg along with 3 mg/kg of propofol for induction in children undergoing tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy surgery can ensure sufficient tracheal intubation conditions and adequately suppress the elevations in HR and MAP associated with tracheal intubation. Remifentanil can provide more satisfactory tracheal intubation conditions.

No significant differences were found in postoperative Ramsay scores, PAED scores for emergence agitation, or FLACC pain scores between our two groups, indicating comparable effects of drug administration on sedation depth, analgesia, and awakening quality. In this study, compared to the 0.4 µg/kg of sufentanil given in Group S, the sufentanil dose was reduced by half in Group R. There was no significant difference in the degree of pain experienced during emergence between the two groups, and the FLACC scores in both groups were < 4 points. This suggests that, after remifentanil effectively inhibits stimulation of the tracheal intubation and mouth gag placement, low-dose sufentanil during induction can also achieve similar intraoperative and postoperative analgesic effects. This implies that remifentanil can cut perioperative long-acting opioid use. However, previous studies have shown that using only remifentanil in pediatric tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy surgery may lead to frequent postoperative pain, suggesting that remifentanil should be used in combination with medium- or long-acting analgesic medications for better postoperative pain control to optimize remifentanil as an anesthetic agent in children [21].

Opioids are likely to induce postoperative hyperalgesia (opioid-induced hyperalgesia [OIH]) and acute tolerance, especially Remifentanil. To date, the pediatric literature lacks a clear description of the specific degree of hyperalgesia induced by remifentanil during clinical application. It is currently believed that patients receiving remifentanil are more susceptible to experiencing nociceptive hypersensitivity during prolonged or higher-dose (> 0.4 µg/kg/min) infusions [28]. Kim et al. [29] previously studied 60 children aged 1–5 years undergoing laparoscopic ureteral reimplantation and found that intraoperative remifentanil infusion given at 0.6–0.9 µg/kg/min increased postoperative opioid demand, while infusion at 0.3–0.9 µg/kg/min increased pain levels 1 h after surgery, which took an average of 3 h. However, our study recorded no cases of OIH from remifentanil used in induction, which could be because of the small dose of remifentanil used in the study protocol or the short duration of the infusion. Consequently, this hypothesis remains theoretically derived and necessitates empirical validation through further investigation.

In terms of adverse effects, the incidence rates of bradycardia, agitation during emergence, respiratory obstruction, postoperative nausea and vomiting, and postoperative hypoxemia did not differ significantly between the two study groups. This suggests that remifentanil combined with sufentanil is a safe and reliable regimen for the induction of anesthesia for pediatric tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy surgery.

However, this study has certain limitations. First, this trial was a non-inferiority study, and the primary research results were negative. Further expanding the sample size or conducting a superiority study may yield more positive results to verify the applicability and safety of the studied drug regimens in a wider range of clinical scenarios. Second, this study was conducted in preschool children aged 3–7 years, an age group with unique characteristics [30–32], and thus the results of this study may not be extrapolated to children < 3 years of age or pediatric patients aged 7–18 years. Third, the present study was a single-center study and intraoperative inhalation anesthesia with sevoflurane was used for maintenance, which could have led to limitations, such as a limited sample representation in the study, high risk of selection bias, and interference with outcome metrics [33].

Conclusion

In our trial, combined use of sufentanil and remifentanil for general anesthesia induction in preschool children undergoing Tonsillectomy and Adenoidectomy provided enhanced hemodynamic stability, optimized opioid utilization, favorable recovery profile and demonstrated safety. The combination of sufentanil of 0.2 µg/kg and remifentanil of 2 µg/kg can achieve satisfactory induction and maintenance effects.
