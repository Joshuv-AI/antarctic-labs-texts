Identifying sources of mixed groundwater contamination using PMF and stable isotopes: a case study of an industrial park
Authors: Rui Wang, Wenrui Fang, Xiaohan Li, Lijie Wu, Hengxing Zhang, Fenggang Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Groundwater contamination in industrial parks often involves overlapping inputs of nitrogen, chlorinated solvents, and dissolved salts, making source identification difficult. In this study, 45 shallow groundwater samples from a typical industrial park on the northern margin of the Hohhot Basin were investigated using hydrochemical analysis, PMF, and nitrate dual isotopes coupled with MixSIAR. The results showed pronounced salinity enrichment, high mineralization, elevated ammonium, and chlorinated hydrocarbon contamination. NH4+ exceeded the Chinese groundwater standard in 77.78% of samples, whereas vinyl chloride exceeded the WHO guideline value in all samples. Hydrochemical analysis indicated that groundwater chemistry still retained a water-rock interaction background, but had been significantly overprinted by anthropogenic inputs. PMF resolved six factors and showed that groundwater pollution was mainly controlled by high mineralization, high-ammonium wastewater input, and dissolved inorganic salt enrichment, whereas chlorinated-solvent industrial pollution had strong diagnostic significance. MixSIAR identified industrial wastewater as the largest nitrate contributor under both prior settings, followed by fertilizer-derived nitrification, whereas mountain-front soil background contributed comparatively less. These results indicate that the combined PMF-MixSIAR framework is effective for identifying mixed groundwater contamination in industrial parks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53795-8.

Introduction

Groundwater is an important freshwater resource that supports drinking water supply, agricultural irrigation, and industrial production1. With rapid urbanization and industrial development, groundwater contamination has become an increasingly serious environmental problem, especially in areas affected by intensive human activities2. In industrial parks, clustered enterprises, frequent wastewater discharge, and intensive material handling often lead to the coexistence of multiple pollutants and complex contaminant mixtures2,3. Among these, nitrogen species, chlorinated solvents, and dissolved inorganic salts are of particular concern because they can migrate through aquifers, alter the natural hydrochemical background, and threaten groundwater quality and human health4,5.

To support effective pollution control, source apportionment methods are increasingly used to identify contaminant origins and dominant controlling processes. Positive Matrix Factorization (PMF) is widely applied to hydrochemical datasets because it can resolve latent source signals from multicomponent concentration data without requiring predefined source profiles6,7. It is especially useful for identifying mixed source characteristics and distinguishing the relative importance of natural and anthropogenic controls7. However, PMF mainly relies on concentration patterns and is less effective in quantitatively distinguishing specific nitrate sources when multiple nitrogen inputs coexist8.

Stable isotopes provide complementary evidence for nitrate source tracing. Dual-isotope approaches based on nitrate δ15N and δ18O have been widely used to distinguish nitrate sources and interpret nitrogen transformation processes in groundwater systems9,10. When combined with Bayesian mixing models such as MixSIAR, they can further quantify the relative contributions of candidate nitrate sources11,12. This approach is particularly suitable for environments where nitrate originates from overlapping natural and anthropogenic inputs10,12. Nevertheless, isotope-based mixing models usually focus on nitrate alone and cannot adequately describe the broader hydrochemical context of mixed groundwater contamination.

Although PMF and isotope-based Bayesian mixing models have both been widely used in groundwater source apportionment, studies that combine these approaches to investigate mixed groundwater contamination remain limited, particularly in industrial park settings8,13. In such settings, groundwater chemistry is commonly controlled not only by water-rock interaction and background hydrochemical evolution, but also by superimposed inputs of wastewater, salts, and industrial pollutants14. Under these conditions, a single method is often insufficient to capture the full contamination pattern. Therefore, this study developed a joint PMF–MixSIAR framework and applied it to a typical industrial park. By integrating hydrochemical indicators with nitrate nitrogen and oxygen isotopes, this study aimed to identify the main source categories affecting groundwater chemistry, clarify the hydrochemical background and anthropogenic overprinting, and quantify the major sources of groundwater nitrate. The results are expected to improve the reliability of source apportionment and provide a scientific basis for groundwater pollution control and risk management in industrial parks.

Study area

Regional setting

The study area is located on the piedmont at the northern margin of the Hohhot Basin (Fig. 1a). Its climate is semi-arid and continental. The mean annual temperature is approximately 7.2 °C. The mean annual precipitation is approximately 388 mm. The pan evaporation rate is approximately 3260 mm. The topography is higher in the north and east and lower in the south and west. Several trunk streams flow east to west across the southern margin. The piedmont hosts alluvial–proluvial fans. Multiple outlet valleys form stacked fan systems that control surface–groundwater recharge pathways and groundwater flow. Under a “low-rainfall–high-evaporation” hydroclimatic setting, mountain-front infiltration and valley underflow are the main recharge mechanisms. The elevation typically ranges from 1000 to 1100 m. Micro-relief and fan gradients create a natural hydraulic gradient and a general northeast–-southwest discharge direction.

The Quaternary unconsolidated deposits are thick. Lacustrine expansion since the Late Pleistocene has produced thin silty interbeds in the shallow strata. The coarse piedmont clastics are interbedded with finer, more organic-, and low-energy deposits. The resulting “fan–lacustrine” coupling yields: (i) high infiltration capacity, large hydraulic gradients, and rapid flow in the upper aquifer near the fan apex, and (ii) localized retention and redox buffering due to fine layers. Thus, contamination may exhibit “fast migration–slow transformation” behavior.

Hydrogeologically, the system is a typical quaternary porous aquifer (Fig. 1b). A single unconfined aquifer prevails near the mountain front. Downstream, the system grades into a multilayer structure consisting of a shallow unconfined aquifer, a silty aquitard, and a deeper confined aquifer. At the aquitard margins, leakage may connect the shallow and deep units. Under natural conditions, mountain underflow first recharges the piedmont unconfined aquifer, then laterally recharges both shallow and confined aquifers, and finally discharges downstream. Permeability decreases from north to south across the fan segments. Representative hydraulic conductivity (K) is approximately 80–100 m d−1 at the apex, ~ 50 m d−1 in the transition zone, and 10–20 m d−1 farther south. Well yields are very high at the apex (> 5000 m³ d−1), but much lower in inter-fan depressions (≈ 500–1000 m3 d−1). Specific yield also decreases southward from ≈ 0.15–0.20 to 0.10–0.15. Shallow systems are highly sensitive to surface disturbances. The confined system exhibits partial natural shielding depending on aquitard continuity.

Industrial park

Most of the area is occupied by an industrial park that hosts chemical and new- material industries, along with supporting sectors such as machinery, building materials, logistics, food and light industry, and pharmaceuticals. Potential groundwater contamination pressures arise from (i) nitrogen-bearing wastewater and domestic sewage leakage or improper reuse, (ii) chlorinated solvents used in cleaning and processing that may spill and form persistent plumes, (iii) salt loading from material storage and accidental leaks leading to Na+, Cl−, and SO42− enrichment, and (iv) metals from surface finishing and electroplating. Overall, the park structure implies combined inputs of nitrogen, chlorinated solvents, and inorganic salts. Therefore, an integrated hydrochemistry–isotope approach is required.

Materials and methods

Field sampling

We sampled 45 shallow groundwater wells along the regional flow path (Fig. 1a). The sampling sites covered three settings: 11 in the outside buffer zone (OBZ), 19 in the key fenced factory zone (KFZ), and 15 in other in-park areas (OIA). We monitored pH, electrical conductivity, dissolved oxygen (DO), oxidation–reduction potential, and temperature (MENTA2, Eureka, USA). The samples were filtered on-site (0.45 μm). The cation and metal samples were preserved in ultrapure HNO3 to pH < 2. No preservatives were added to the anion or nutrient samples. Samples were stored at 4 °C in the dark and analyzed within the prescribed holding times. We recorded the coordinates, well depth, screened interval, purging time and flow rate, and stabilized field parameters.

Access to all field investigations and sampling was granted by the local ecological and environmental management authorities, in collaboration with the Institute of Hydrogeology and Environmental Geology, Chinese Academy of Geological Sciences. All field sampling and water testing were carried out in compliance with relevant protocols and guidelines, and the data used in this study were subsequently analyzed and written into this paper.

This study was based on a single sampling campaign, and the results therefore mainly represent groundwater conditions during the sampling period. Under the semi-arid setting of the study area, however, recharge is relatively limited and the major contamination pressures are associated with long-term industrial inputs. Thus, although individual concentrations may vary seasonally, the major hydrochemical facies and source framework identified here are expected to be more stable than short-term concentration fluctuations. Seasonal monitoring would still be needed to assess temporal variability more fully.

Laboratory analyses

All the analyses were performed using validated SOPs and QA/QC procedures at the Key Laboratory of Groundwater Science and Engineering, Ministry of Natural Resources. SO42− and NO3− were measured by ion chromatography (Thermo ICS-1500). Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, and Fe, Mn were measured by ICP-OES (Thermo Fisher iCAP 6300). Cl−, HCO3−, and CO32− were measured by titration. NH4+ and NO₂− were measured by UV–Vis spectrophotometry (UV-2550). The chlorinated VOCs were measured using purge-and-trap GC–MS; Shimadzu GCMS-QP2010 Plus). We used multi-point calibration. Method blanks, trip blanks, field blanks, and laboratory control samples were included. We analyzed duplicates to assess precision and matrix spikes for accuracy. Results are reported in mg L−1. Results were converted to meq L−1 for diagramming. Charge-balance errors were maintained within ± 5%.

For nitrate isotopes, we used the denitrifier method to convert NO3− to N₂O, followed by isotope-ratio mass spectrometry (Isoprime 100). International reference materials were used for calibration and drift correction. Typical analytical precision is better than ± 0.5‰ (1σ). Isotope results are reported in δ notation relative to international reference standards. The chemical and isotope data consistently followed appropriate significant figures and QC thresholds.

Given the shallow and near-isothermal conditions at our wells, we treat post-recharge isotope fractionation as limited; nevertheless, minor density/temperature-driven shifts cannot be fully excluded in confined or thermally stratified aquifers15.

Hydrochemical diagram methods

We used Piper and Gibbs diagrams to interpret the groundwater chemistry and establish a common framework for source apportionment and isotope interpretation. Major ions (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, K+, HCO3−, CO32−, SO42−, and Cl−) were converted from mg L−1 to meq L−1. Samples with charge balance error |CBE| ≤ 5% were plotted. Following convention, Na+ and K+ were merged as Na++K+ in the Piper diagram. The samples were plotted as equivalent percentages in cation and anion triangles and projected onto the central diamond to identify the hydrochemical facies and mixing trends16. In addition, to compare the spatial heterogeneity of key hydrochemical indicators among different sampling zones, boxplots were constructed for representative parameters in the key fenced factory zone (KFZ), other in-park areas (OIA), and the outside buffer zone (OBZ). Gibbs plots were used to diagnose dominant processes at the group scale: TDS versus Na+/(Na++Ca2+) and TDS versus Cl−/(Cl−+HCO3−)17. Ratios were calculated from meq L−1 concentrations. Because the Gibbs framework was developed for surface waters, groundwater interpretation is constrained by aquifer architecture, recharge–runoff dynamics, and ion-exchange processes14. Therefore, principal component analysis (PCA) was further applied after the Gibbs analysis to supplement the interpretation of hydrochemical variability and to better distinguish the dominant gradients associated with natural background and anthropogenic disturbance in the industrial park. The Piper facies, zonal boxplot comparison, PCA results, PMF factors, and isotope-mixing results were cross-checked to avoid over-extrapolation.

Positive matrix factorization (PMF)

Positive Matrix Factorization (PMF) originates from receptor modeling. Paatero and al. proposed and developed it in the 1990s into a framework with uncertainty weighting and robust solutions6,18. PMF decomposes the observed concentration matrix of samples × species into the product of factor contributions and factor profiles, with a residual term. We used two isotopes, δ15N–NO3− and δ18O–NO3−. Because two tracers can theoretically resolve no more than three sources, the Bayesian mixing model was configured with three potential sources of groundwater nitrate: mountain-front soil source (MFS), nitrate derived from nitrification of fertilizer N (NF), and industrial wastewater (IW).

The source endmembers were determined according to regional background characteristics, pollution types in the industrial park, and evidence from the dual-isotope analysis. The MFS endmember was defined using two mountain-front soil samples, which represent the background soil N signal in the recharge area with limited direct industrial disturbance. Using mountain-front samples provides a study-area-based constraint on the natural background endmember while minimizing the influence of direct industrial disturbance. The NF endmember was adopted from Tong et al. (2025)20, whose study was conducted in the Dahei River Basin, the same basin in which the present study area is located20. Because both areas are subject to similar climatic conditions and anthropogenic influences, the reported isotopic values provide a better-constrained regional reference for the fertilizer-related endmember in the absence of site-specific measurements. The IW endmember was defined using three industrial source samples collected from the study area, independent of the groundwater mixture samples, including circulating cooling water, leachate from waste calcium carbide slag, and raw industrial wastewater from a local chemical enterprise; the corresponding isotope data are provided in Table S1. Using multiple independent industrial source samples helps capture the isotopic heterogeneity of industrial effluents in the study area and provides a more representative constraint for the IW endmember. Results and discussion

Hydrochemical characteristics

We computed basic descriptive statistics for the major hydrochemical parameters. We compared the concentrations with the Standard for Groundwater Quality (GB/T 14848–2017)21 and the World Health Organization (WHO) Guidelines for Drinking Water Quality (4th edition)22. Table 2 presents the results of the study.

Overall, the groundwater was neutral to slightly alkaline, with pH values ranging from 6.82 to 8.24 (mean = 7.55). HCO3− showed a relatively stable distribution (157.1–637.3 mg/L, mean = 343.42 mg/L), indicating that the groundwater still retained a background hydrochemical signature related to water–rock interaction5. In contrast, Na+, Cl−, TDS, NH4+, and chlorinated hydrocarbons exhibited much larger variations. TDS ranged from 338 to 17,610 mg/L, with a mean of 3184.67 mg/L, and 62.22% of the samples exceeded the guideline value. Cl− showed a similarly high exceedance rate (62.22%), with a mean concentration of 1753.52 mg/L, while Na+ exceeded the guideline value in 46.67% of the samples, indicating widespread salinity enrichment and elevated mineralization23. Among the nitrogen species, NO3− had a mean concentration of 44.68 mg/L, with 31.11% of the samples exceeding the WHO guideline value, whereas NH4+ had a mean concentration of 199.46 mg/L and an exceedance rate of 77.78% based on the Chinese groundwater standard, which was substantially higher than that of NO3−, suggesting a stronger ammonium accumulation signal and possible wastewater-related influence3,24. In addition, the exceedance rates of TCE, VC, and 1,2-DCE were 80.00%, 100.00%, and 62.22%, respectively, indicating that the groundwater was affected not only by nitrogen pollution but also by pronounced chlorinated hydrocarbon contamination25.

To further examine the spatial variability of groundwater contamination, the samples were grouped into three zones according to their locations: the key fenced factory zone (KFZ), other in-park areas (OIA), and the outside buffer zone (OBZ). The spatial distribution of NO3− concentrations was first mapped to directly visualize the nitrate contamination pattern within and around the industrial park (Fig. 2). Samples with NO3− concentrations exceeding the WHO guideline value of 50 mg L−1 were mainly concentrated in KFZ, where major enterprises and historical solid-waste landfill areas are located, while a small number of such samples also occurred in OBZ. In contrast, most samples from OIA had NO3− concentrations below this guideline value. This pattern suggests that nitrate contamination was centered in the key factory zone and may have locally extended toward the peripheral areas of the industrial park. To further quantify the zonal differences in major hydrochemical indicators, boxplots combined with non-parametric tests were then used to compare representative parameters among KFZ, OIA, and OBZ.

Fig. 2Spatial distribution of NO3− concentrations in groundwater samples within and around the industrial park.

Spatial distribution of NO3− concentrations in groundwater samples within and around the industrial park.

As shown in Fig. 3, NO3−, Cl−, and TDS all exhibited obvious spatial differences, although their variation patterns were not identical. NO3− differed significantly among the three zones (p = 0.00027), with median concentrations of 56.61 mg/L in KFZ, 1.00 mg/L in OIA, and 22.60 mg/L in OBZ. Significant differences were observed between KFZ and OIA and between OBZ and OIA, whereas the difference between KFZ and OBZ was not significant. This pattern indicates that nitrate anomalies did not simply decrease monotonically from inside to outside the park; instead, some samples in OBZ still showed elevated NO3− concentrations, suggesting that the outside buffer zone cannot be regarded as a simple background area. In contrast, Cl− and TDS showed much clearer spatial gradients, both following the order KFZ > OBZ > OIA, with all pairwise differences being significant (p < 0.01). The median Cl− concentrations in KFZ, OIA, and OBZ were 1891, 77, and 502.4 mg/L, with exceedance rates of 84.21%, 20.00%, and 81.82%, respectively. The corresponding median TDS values were 3642, 581, and 1296 mg/L, with exceedance rates of 94.74%, 13.33%, and 72.73%, respectively. These results indicate that salinity and high-mineralization anomalies were concentrated in the key factory zone but remained pronounced in the outside buffer zone, suggesting clear spatial persistence of dissolved salt enrichment and high-mineralization effects.

Boxplots of Cl−, NO3−, and TDS concentrations in the key fenced factory zone (KFZ), other in-park areas (OIA), and outside buffer zone (OBZ).

As shown in Fig. 4, NH4+ and chlorinated hydrocarbons further reflected the complexity of the spatial contamination pattern. NH4+ differed significantly among the three zones (p = 0.00028), with median concentrations decreasing from 18.37 mg/L in KFZ to 2.42 mg/L in OIA and 0.53 mg/L in OBZ, and corresponding exceedance rates of 94.74%, 80.00%, and 45.45%, respectively. This indicates that ammonium contamination showed a strong core-zone concentration pattern, mainly controlled by high-strength wastewater input in and around the key factory zone, although its influence also extended, in weaker form, to other areas of the park and the periphery. Chlorinated hydrocarbons further showed both localized industrial hotspot characteristics and broader regional influence. TCE showed the highest median concentration in KFZ (0.87 mg/L), significantly higher than those in OIA and OBZ, indicating a stronger local industrial source control in the key fenced factory zone. In contrast, VC exceeded the guideline value in 100% of samples in all three zones, indicating a much broader regional distribution rather than a localized anomaly. Together with the co-occurrence of TCE, 1,2-DCE, and VC, this pattern suggests that reductive dechlorination of chlorinated ethenes may be occurring in the aquifer, following the sequence TCE → 1,2-DCE → VC25. Spatially, higher TCE in KFZ is more consistent with source-area hotspot characteristics, whereas the persistent and widespread VC exceedance across OIA and OBZ suggests that chlorinated hydrocarbon contamination has become more spatially extensive after migration and transformation. Although no formal quantitative health-risk assessment was conducted in this study, the universal exceedance of VC indicates that chlorinated ethene contamination in groundwater within and around the industrial park deserves particular environmental and public-health attention22.

In summary, the boxplot results indicate strong spatial heterogeneity in groundwater contamination across the study area. KFZ represents the core zone where high mineralization, salinity enrichment, elevated ammonium, and chlorinated hydrocarbons are most strongly concentrated, whereas OIA generally corresponds to a relatively low-value and weakly disturbed zone. Although contamination intensity in OBZ was overall lower than that in KFZ, Cl−, TDS, part of the NO3− signal, and chlorinated hydrocarbons remained elevated, and VC still exceeded the guideline value in all samples, indicating that the outside buffer zone is not a simple background area but has already shown clear anthropogenic response and transitional characteristics.

Hydrochemical facies

Piper diagrams were used to visualize the cation and anion compositions of the 45 groundwater samples and to identify the dominant hydrochemical facies and evolutionary trends16. In Fig. 5, the samples are grouped into three spatial zones according to their locations, namely the key fenced factory zone (KFZ), the other in-park areas (OIA), and the outside buffer zone (OBZ). According to the Shukarev classification, the 45 samples were divided into 19 hydrochemical types, indicating considerable complexity and marked facies variability26.

In the cation triangle, most samples fall in zone B, indicating a mixed cation composition with joint contributions from Ca, Mg, and Na, whereas only a few samples shift toward the Ca-type or Na-type fields. In the anion triangle, most samples are distributed in zone E and zone G, showing the coexistence of a background HCO3 facies and a Cl-enriched facies. OIA is mainly characterized by HCO3-type and HCO3-dominated mixed waters, KFZ shows a higher proportion of Cl-type and Cl-involved mixed facies, and OBZ lies between the two with transitional characteristics.

In the central diamond field, most samples are distributed in zones I, II, and IV, indicating that groundwater in the study area is not represented by a single background facies, but by multiple ionic combinations together with varying degrees of salinity enrichment. Overall, groundwater facies in KFZ show a more distinct shift toward Cl-enriched types, OIA is dominated by relatively stable background and weak mixed facies, and OBZ exhibits a transitional pattern23. These results indicate that groundwater facies in the study area are complex and spatially variable, with the most evident facies shift occurring in the key fenced factory zone.

Controls on hydrochemistry

To analyze the main factors affecting groundwater hydrochemistry, Fig. 6 presents zonal Gibbs diagrams showing the relationships of Na+/(Na++Ca2+) versus TDS in Fig. 6a and Cl−/(Cl−+HCO3−) versus TDS in Fig. 6b. Overall, most samples from the three spatial zones plot within the rock-weathering dominance field or its transition toward the evaporation field, indicating that groundwater in the study area still preserves a natural hydrochemical background primarily controlled by water-rock interactions17,14.

From the cation side (Fig. 6a), samples from KFZ generally show higher TDS values and tend to shift toward higher Na+/(Na++Ca2+) ratios, suggesting more pronounced solute enrichment superimposed on the natural weathering background in the key fenced factory zone. In contrast, OIA samples are more tightly clustered and mainly occur at relatively lower TDS levels, indicating comparatively weaker anthropogenic overprinting in the other in-park areas. OBZ samples occupy an intermediate position between KFZ and OIA, although several of them also shift toward the upper-right direction, suggesting that the outside buffer zone is not a simple background area but instead shows transitional characteristics.

From the anion side (Fig. 6b), the spatial differentiation is even clearer. Most KFZ and OBZ samples shift toward the evaporation-dominance field with increasing Cl−/(Cl−+HCO3−) ratios and higher TDS values, indicating a marked increase in the relative proportion of Cl−. Meanwhile, OIA samples are mainly distributed within the lower Cl−/(Cl−+HCO3−) and lower TDS range, remaining closer to the rock-weathering background field. However, most samples still plot below the ideal evaporation line. In other words, although Cl−/(Cl−+HCO3−) increases substantially, TDS does not reach the level expected under pure evaporation control. This suggests that groundwater hydrochemical evolution in the study area is not controlled by evaporation alone, but is more likely driven by the combined effects of external Cl− input and alkalinity-consuming processes, such as nitrification14,23. Overall, both the mean Cl−/(Cl−+HCO3−) ratio and TDS are higher in KFZ than in OBZ, indicating stronger chloride enrichment and solute accumulation in the key fenced factory zone, whereas OBZ, although weaker than KFZ, has already deviated clearly from the relatively background state represented by OIA. It should be noted that under contaminated industrial-park conditions, Gibbs diagrams are mainly useful for identifying the natural background and its deviation, but are limited in distinguishing the specific types and intensity of anthropogenic disturbance. Therefore, further analysis is still required to better discriminate the spatial heterogeneity of hydrochemical overprinting among the different zones.

Because the Gibbs diagrams mainly identify the first-order natural hydrochemical background and have limited ability to distinguish anthropogenic disturbance under contaminated industrial-park conditions, principal component analysis (PCA) was further applied to supplement the interpretation of hydrochemical variability27. As shown in Fig. 7, the first two principal components explained 80.78% of the total variance, with PC1 and PC2 accounting for 66.44% and 14.34%, respectively. In terms of loadings, PC1 showed positive loadings for most variables, with particularly high contributions from TDS, Ca2+, Cl−, Mg2+, K+, Na+, NH4+, and NO3−, indicating that this axis mainly represents a composite gradient of high mineralization, salinity enrichment, and wastewater-related anthropogenic disturbance. By contrast, PC2 was mainly controlled by HCO3− and SO42−, reflecting a secondary difference in hydrochemical composition superimposed on the dominant salinity–pollution gradient.

Fig. 7PCA biplot of hydrochemical variables in the key fenced factory zone (KFZ), other in-park areas (OIA), and outside buffer zone (OBZ).

PCA biplot of hydrochemical variables in the key fenced factory zone (KFZ), other in-park areas (OIA), and outside buffer zone (OBZ).

The distribution of samples in the PCA ordination space further reveals clear differences among the KFZ, OIA, and OBZ groups along the PC1 axis. Samples from KFZ are mainly located on the positive side of PC1 and are aligned with the directions of Cl−, TDS, Na+, NH4+, Ca2+, and Mg2+, indicating that the key fenced factory zone is subject to the strongest combined effects of high mineralization, salinity enrichment, and wastewater-related overprinting. In contrast, samples from OIA are mainly distributed on the negative side of PC1 and show a relatively compact pattern, suggesting comparatively weaker anthropogenic disturbance in the other in-park areas. OBZ occupies an intermediate position between KFZ and OIA and thus displays a transitional character; however, several OBZ samples shift toward the positive side of PC1, indicating that the outside buffer zone cannot be regarded as a purely natural background area and that some peripheral groundwater has also been affected by mixed anthropogenic activities. The significant zonal separation along PC1 further confirms the marked spatial heterogeneity of groundwater chemistry in the study area5,27.

Overall, the PCA results are consistent with the preceding boxplot-based zonal comparison and indicate that groundwater in the study area is not hydrochemically homogeneous, but instead varies along a dominant gradient of high mineralization-salinity enrichment-wastewater-related overprinting. KFZ shows the strongest anthropogenic overprinting, OIA is relatively less affected, and OBZ represents a transitional domain between background and impacted groundwater. These results provide additional multivariate evidence for the subsequent PMF-based identification of mixed contamination sources.

PMF results

Based on 45 shallow groundwater samples collected from the study area, PMF source apportionment was conducted to identify the major controls on groundwater chemistry and pollution sources. A total of 12 variables, including Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+, K+, Cl−, SO42−, HCO3−, NO3−, NH4+, TCE, VC, and 1,2-DCE, were selected for the analysis. These variables include major ions reflecting the natural hydrochemical background of groundwater, as well as typical chlorinated ethenes indicative of industrial solvent inputs. To determine the optimal factor solution, models with three to seven factors were systematically compared. The ratio of Qtrue to Qexpected, which is commonly used to evaluate the overall fit of the PMF model, indicated that the six-factor solution was within an acceptable range (Qtrue/Qexpected = 1.73)6,28. Combined with its clearer environmental interpretability relative to the other candidate solutions, the six-factor model was therefore selected as the final solution.

In the selected six-factor model, each factor represented a different source feature or hydrochemical process. The factor profiles and species-specific contribution rates of the full-sample model are summarized in Table 3, and the relative contribution of each factor is shown in Fig. 8a. In the full-sample model, F1 was dominated by TCE, VC, and 1,2-DCE and was interpreted as a chlorinated-solvent industrial pollution factor; VC and 1,2-DCE may also reflect the transformation of chlorinated ethenes in groundwater. F2 was characterized by enriched NH4+ and Cl− and represented a high-ammonium wastewater-related composite factor. F3 explained NO3− best and was interpreted as a nitrate input factor driven by agricultural and domestic activities. F4 was mainly characterized by elevated Na+, Cl−, and SO42−, indicating an increase in dissolved inorganic salts and thus representing a dissolved inorganic salt enrichment factor. F5 was characterized by elevated Ca2+, Mg2+, and Cl− and represented a Ca–Mg–Cl-type high-mineralization factor, likely related to anthropogenic salt input and local mixing effects. F6 was mainly characterized by HCO3− together with part of Ca2+ and Mg2+ and corresponded to an HCO3–Ca·Mg background hydrochemistry and water–rock interaction factor. Based on normalized profile totals, the mean contributions of F1–F6 were approximately 4.03%, 21.64%, 3.16%, 18.83%, 40.63%, and 11.72%, respectively. Overall, the full-sample model indicated that groundwater pollution in the study area was mainly controlled by the high-mineralization factor, the high-ammonium wastewater factor, and the dissolved inorganic salt enrichment factor, whereas the chlorinated-solvent industrial factor had a relatively low overall contribution but strong specificity.

To evaluate the reliability of the PMF results, Bootstrap, DISP, and Fpeak uncertainty analyses were further performed28. The results showed that, except for F2, all other factors had mapping rates higher than 85%, whereas the mapping rate of F2 was only 76.6%, indicating that the high-ammonium composite factor was relatively less stable. At the same time, the full-sample model did not achieve stable convergence in the DISP and Fpeak rotational analyses, suggesting that a few composite anomalous samples characterized by simultaneously high NH4+, high Cl−, and high mineralization enhanced the overlap among the high-ammonium wastewater factor, the dissolved inorganic salt enrichment factor, and the high-mineralization factor, thereby reducing model stability.

To further test the influence of anomalous samples on source apportionment, a sensitivity model was constructed after removing three composite anomalous samples with the highest NH4+ concentrations. The results showed that the Qtrue/Qexpected value of the sensitivity model remained 1.73, close to that of the full-sample model, indicating that the overall fit did not change substantially after removing these samples. In the sensitivity model, F1 represented the HCO3–Ca·Mg background hydrochemistry and water–rock interaction factor, F2 represented the high-ammonium chloride-rich wastewater factor, F3 represented the nitrate input factor, F4 represented the chlorinated-solvent industrial pollution factor, F5 represented the dissolved inorganic salt enrichment factor, and F6 represented the Ca–Mg–Cl high-mineralization factor, with mean contributions of 16.14%, 9.74%, 6.94%, 9.34%, 22.52%, and 35.32%, respectively. In addition, all 100 base runs of the sensitivity model converged successfully. The DISP analysis was completed successfully, with no factor swaps at any dQmax level, and the Fpeak results were also markedly improved compared with those of the full-sample model. These findings indicate that the anomalous samples mainly affected model stability and the separation of composite factors, but did not alter the basic interpretation of the major source categories28.

Taken together, the full-sample and sensitivity models identified broadly consistent source categories, indicating that the groundwater pollution source framework in the study area was reasonably robust. In terms of mean contribution, both models were dominated by the combined effects of dissolved inorganic salt enrichment and high mineralization, suggesting that groundwater in the study area has been subject to significant anthropogenic disturbance, with some local areas likely related to industrial discharge and wastewater input. This was followed by agricultural/domestic wastewater nitrogen sources and background hydrochemistry and water–rock interaction factors. In contrast, the chlorinated-solvent industrial pollution factor had a relatively low mean contribution, but strong specificity and diagnostic significance. The full-sample model can therefore be used to characterize the overall groundwater pollution pattern in the study area, while the sensitivity model further confirms the reliability of the identified major source categories. Given the evident spatial heterogeneity of groundwater chemistry in the study area, the PMF results are more appropriately interpreted as representing the overall source structure of mixed contamination at the study-area scale, rather than implying that all local zones share a fully homogeneous source mixture. Isotope results and source apportionment

Dual-isotope features and nitrogen cycling

The stable nitrogen and oxygen isotopic compositions of nitrate can provide important evidence for both its potential sources and nitrogen cycling processes in groundwater [46,47]. As shown in Fig. 9, only a weak positive relationship was observed between δ15N–NO3− and δ18O–NO3−, with a regression slope of 0.392, an R2 value of 0.060, and no significant correlation (P = 0.129). This indicates that the nitrate isotopic compositions in groundwater did not exhibit a clear coupled enrichment pattern, suggesting that denitrification was not the dominant process controlling nitrate isotope variation at the regional scale.

Fig. 9Relationship between δ15N–NO3− and δ18O–NO3− in groundwater samples.

Relationship between δ15N–NO3− and δ18O–NO3− in groundwater samples.

Further examination of the relationships between NO3− concentration and nitrate isotopes (Fig. 10a, b) showed that NO3− was weakly but significantly negatively correlated with δ15N–NO3−, with a regression slope of − 0.046, an R2 value of 0.163, and P = 0.0097. In contrast, the relationship between NO3− and δ18O–NO3− was weak, with a regression slope of − 0.032, a very low R2 value of 0.031, and no statistical significance (P = 0.279). Under strong and widespread denitrification, progressive nitrate consumption is generally accompanied by synchronous enrichment in both δ15N–NO3− and δ18O–NO3−, producing a relatively clear enrichment trend. However, such a typical pattern, namely decreasing NO3− accompanied by simultaneous increases in δ15N–NO3− and δ18O–NO3−, was not observed in this study. These results suggest that groundwater nitrate in the study area is more consistent with nitrification-related formation than with strong regional-scale denitrification10,29.

Combined with the hydrochemical characteristics described above, the generally elevated NH4+ concentrations in groundwater indicate that reduced nitrogen input was significant in some parts of the study area, and that part of the NO3− may have been produced through nitrification of ammonium-bearing inputs. During this process, isotope fractionation associated with NH4+ oxidation may partly affect the δ15N–NO3− composition, making the isotopic signatures of individual samples more complex24. Therefore, the interpretation of the dual-isotope results in this study focuses primarily on the overall regional trend rather than on exact transformation pathways for each individual sample. In summary, nitrification-related nitrate formation was likely an important process in the study area, whereas the isotopic evidence did not indicate strong denitrification control at the regional scale.

Dual-isotope source plot

Based on the above evidence for nitrification-related nitrate formation and the lack of a strong regional denitrification signal, potential nitrate sources were further examined using a δ15N–δ18O source plot (Fig. 11). We clarified the input pathways and spatial patterns. Most samples fell within fields for soil nitrogen and nitrified ammonium, indicating that the mineralization of soil organic nitrogen followed by nitrification forms the regional background. Some in-park sites plot toward the industrial wastewater field, with elevated δ15N and low-to-moderate δ18O. This reflects the key roles of industrial discharges and organic nitrogen decomposition30. Sites outside the park were clustered more within the soil-N and nitrified ammonium fields, indicating the dominance of natural processes. A few sites extended into fertilizer and manure/domestic-sewage fields, suggesting local agricultural activities or sewage inputs.

MixSIAR apportionment

To quantify the source contributions of groundwater NO3−, MixSIAR was implemented under the three-source framework described above. An equal-prior model was used as the main model, and an IW-weighted weak prior model was further introduced for sensitivity analysis. Under the equal-prior setting, the posterior mean contributions of IW, NF, and MFS were 58.1%, 28.1%, and 13.8%, respectively (Fig. 12). Under the IW-weighted weak prior setting, the corresponding contributions were 50.5%, 39.0%, and 10.4%, respectively. The DIC values of the two models were 237.02 and 236.97, indicating very similar overall model fit.

Convergence diagnostics showed that all monitored parameters in both models had R-hat values below 1.01. In the equal-prior model, only a small proportion of Geweke z-scores fell outside ± 1.96, whereas all z-scores in the IW-weighted weak prior model were within the acceptable range. These results indicate that both models achieved acceptable convergence and yielded interpretable posterior estimates. Considering the limited local endmember dataset, including two mountain-front soil samples for MFS, three independent industrial source samples for IW, and regional literature values for NF, the posterior estimates were interpreted mainly in terms of source ranking and plausible contribution ranges. Although the exact contributions of IW and NF varied to some extent between the two prior settings, the ranking of source importance remained unchanged, i.e., IW > NF > MFS. This suggests that industrial wastewater was consistently identified as the largest nitrate contributor under the tested prior settings. In addition, the posterior pairwise relationships showed a strong negative correlation between IW and NF, suggesting substantial allocation competition between these two sources during source apportionment. Therefore, the estimated contributions are more appropriately interpreted as plausible contribution ranges rather than exact fixed values. Overall, the MixSIAR results indicate that industrial wastewater likely represents the major nitrate source in the study area, followed by fertilizer-related nitrification input, whereas the contribution from mountain-front soil background input is comparatively minor.

Conclusions

This study investigated mixed groundwater contamination in a typical industrial park using hydrochemical analysis, PMF, and nitrate dual isotopes coupled with MixSIAR. The results showed that groundwater in the study area was characterized by marked hydrochemical complexity and contamination heterogeneity, with prominent salinity enrichment, high mineralization, elevated ammonium, and chlorinated hydrocarbon pollution. NH4+ exceeded the Chinese groundwater standard in 77.78% of samples, whereas vinyl chloride exceeded the WHO guideline value in all samples. Hydrochemical analysis further indicated that groundwater chemistry still retained a natural background controlled by water-rock interaction, but had been significantly overprinted by anthropogenic inputs. The key fenced factory zone showed the strongest deviation toward Cl-enriched, high-mineralization, and wastewater-related signatures, whereas the outside buffer zone also displayed clear transitional contamination characteristics. PMF resolved six factors, among which high mineralization, high-ammonium wastewater input, and dissolved inorganic salt enrichment were the dominant controls on groundwater chemistry, with mean contributions of 40.63%, 21.64%, and 18.83%, respectively. The other three factors were background hydrochemistry/water–rock interaction, chlorinated-solvent industrial pollution, and nitrate input, all with relatively lower mean contributions. Among them, chlorinated-solvent industrial pollution, although contributing less overall, showed strong source specificity. Sensitivity analysis further confirmed the robustness of the identified source categories. MixSIAR identified industrial wastewater as the largest nitrate contributor under the tested prior settings, followed by fertilizer-related nitrate input, whereas mountain-front soil background contributed the least. Under the equal-prior model, the mean contributions of the three sources were 58.1%, 28.1%, and 13.8%, respectively, while the source ranking remained unchanged under the industrial wastewater-weighted weak-prior setting. However, because allocation competition existed between industrial wastewater and fertilizer-related nitrate input, and because the endmember dataset was still limited, these values should be interpreted as plausible contribution ranges rather than exact fixed proportions. Overall, groundwater contamination in the study area is characterized by a natural hydrochemical background overprinted by anthropogenic disturbance, with high mineralization, dissolved salt input, ammonium-rich wastewater, chlorinated solvents, and industrially influenced nitrate input together defining the main contamination pattern.
