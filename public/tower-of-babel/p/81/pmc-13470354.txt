Influence of temperature and inoculum composition on standardized biodegradation tests of bioplastics in freshwater under aerobic conditions
Authors: David Gutiérrez-Rial, Aarón Lagoa, Iria Villar, Benedicto Soto, Josefina Garrido, Salustiano Mato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
During the last decades, the production of bioplastics has emerged as a promising alternative to mitigate the environmental impact generated by the accumulation of unmanaged plastic waste on natural ecosystems. Biodegradable bioplastics are expected to be less persistent than conventional oil-based polymers; however, the conditions under which biodegradation tests are conducted may lead to discrepancies between laboratory results and the real environmental conditions. For freshwater ecosystems, previous studies have highlighted important gaps, such as the variability caused by the inoculum. To assess if the use of commercial lyophilized bacteria as inoculum reduces the test variability, the biodegradation of three microplastics (MPs) with distinct polymer origins, poly-3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyvalerate (PHBV), polylactic acid (PLA), and polypropylene (PP), was studied in closed respirometers. Subsequently, following the reduction of the variability, the impact of the incubation temperature was evaluated at 10, 20 and 30 °C. Finally, the potential differences in the biodegradation process caused using different inocula were assessed with three different inoculum sources: the commercial freeze-dried bacteria, an activated sludge and digestate from biowaste. The results showed that freeze-dried bacteria could be used as an alternative inoculum to reduce inter-laboratory differences in the standardized test. Although no statistical differences were observed for PHB under identical incubation conditions with the freeze-dried inoculum, the results showed that the biodegradation of PHBV is highly influenced by temperature with higher values obtained at 35 °C than at 20 °C and 10 °C. Finally, the diversity and relative abundance of the inocula may significantly influence biodegradation outcomes, highlighting the inoculum source as a critical factor contributing to variability in this type of assay.

Introduction

There is a widespread misconception that bioplastics refer to polymers derived from renewable biological sources such as plants, bacteria and algae, and that they are a suitable alternative to reduce the impact caused by conventional petroleum-based polymers, due to the reduction in carbon footprint and because they are biodegradable1. Nevertheless, these polymers made from alternative organic sources are called bio-based polymers and they may be or not be biodegradable. Bioplastic is a broader and controversial term due to encompasses conventional oil-based polymers that are biodegradable and all bio-based polymers, independently on if they are biodegradable or not2. Thus, biodegradability does not only depend on the composition of the material, which has led to confusion among the public and in recent decades many scientific publications have underscored the urgency of clarifying this important issue, highlighting that the prefix “bio-“does not necessarily means that it is more environmentally friendly3–5. Global production of bioplastics has increased by 2 M.t. in recent years and is expected to growth to over 5.7 M.t. by 20296. In this sense, among the most relevant biodegradable bio-based polymers, polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) represent one of the most environmentally promising bioplastics to date due to their complete biodegradability, non-persistent microplastics, and lower emissions, with advancements in production further enhancing their viability7. Within this family, poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) (PHB) is the most common homopolymer, whereas poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyvalerate) (PHBV) is a copolymer composed of 3-hydroxybutyrate and 3-hydroxyvalerate monomeric units. Compared with PHB, PHBV typically exhibits reduced brittleness and improved mechanical flexibility, which enhances its suitability for applications such as packaging and diverse industrial applications8.

Given this context, issues such as clarifying the differences between bioplastic and biodegradable polymers or ensuring the biodegradability of materials classified as such should be considered as priority challenges for the coming years9.Biodegradability can be defined as the degradation of macromolecular chains by the action of microorganisms, and takes place in a two-step process; the first is the fragmentation of large fragments and the high molar mass macromolecular chain is broken down producing monomers, and depending on the chemical composition of the material this can take place due to different reactions such as hydrolysis or oxidation10. The second step consists of the mineralization of the monomers by the action of microorganisms resulting in the ultimately production of carbon dioxide (CO2), water, methane and biomass, with some variations depending on the availability of oxygen during the process11–13. So, the process of biodegradation is not exclusively conditioned by the polymer properties but also depends on the environmental factors such as the availability of oxygen, temperature, or the composition and activity of the microbial community and their enzymatic capabilities. Due to this, the biodegradability of classified biodegradable polymers determined under laboratory conditions cannot be taken as a direct indicator of their biodegradability under environmental conditions14. In fact, biodegradation tests usually do not reflect the reality and may overestimate the degradability of these materials since they are carried out under optimal conditions that usually do not occur in natural environments15. However, the standardization of these tests was a breakthrough in the field of plastics and since the publication of the first guidelines for the testing of chemicals by the OECD in 1981, several national and international standards have emerged based on it, which determine how the biodegradability of plastics should be carried out16. At this moment to ensure transparency, there are various standards and certifications that establish the criteria a product must meet before it can be labelled as biodegradable. These assessments evaluate the biodegradability of plastics in different environments such as soils (ISO 17556, ASTM D5988, NF U52-001, UNI 11462, DS/ISO 23517), or marine environment (ISO 18830, ISO 15314; ISO 16221, ISO 22403, UNE-EN ISO 23977-1:2022, DS/ISO 14852, ASTM D6691; ASTM D6692; ASTM D7081, DSF/ISO/FDIS 22766, BS EN ISO 22403, JIS K 6951), but does not exist an universal biodegradation procedure to assess the biodegradability of each type of bioplastic in all environment17. In the case of freshwater ecosystems such as rivers, there are no standardized protocols for assessing biodegradability in these media; alternatively, there are protocols to assess the biodegradability of these materials in aqueous medium by using activated sludge from Waste Water Treatment Plants (WWTP) as inoculum (ISO 14851:2004 and ISO 14852:2004) instead of collecting water directly from these ecosystems or instead of using bacterial strains isolated from the natural environment18.

In addition to the problem mentioned above, these tests, despite being performed under identical conditions in the laboratory, have been historically subjected to significant variability. This results in substantial differences not only between laboratories, but also within the same laboratory when attempting to reproduce the same test19–22. The standard ISO 14,851 proposes the use of activated sludge from a WWTP, but the diversity and composition of the microorganism community can be very variable depending on the WWTP selected to collect the sludge, so the enzyme activity will also be different, leading to differences in the final biodegradation23. This occurs because the relative amounts of enzymes produced by the microorganisms vary within the same species and even between strains of the same species24.

Based on this, the main objective of this study was to test an alternative inoculum, composed of freeze-dried bacteria, with greater stability, aiming to ensure consistent and reliable results across repeated biodegradability tests in aqueous media. Then, once this variability was reduced the specific objectives were to evaluate the effect of temperature on the biodegradation process and finally to assess the biodegradability of three different polymers by using three different inoculum sources, also determining the main microbial communities involved in the degradation process.

Methodology

Materials

Three different commercial bio-based polymers were employed to carry out the tests of this study (Table 1). The first material was a film plastic bag fully composed of polypropylene (PP), an oil-based catalogued as non-biodegradable polymer. The second one was a film plastic bag made from polylactic acid (PLA), bio-based and compostable biopolymer. The last material was a multipolymeric film foil composed of a polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA), specifically poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyvalerate) (PHBV). According to the manufacturer’s specifications, this PHBV material contains 92% 3-hydroxybutyrate (PHB) and 8% 3-hydroxyvalerate (PHV).

Before its use, all materials were previously micronized using a ZM200 ultracentrifuge mill (Restch, Verder Scientific) and sieved through a 250 μm metallic mesh. An elemental analysis was carried out on each material to determine the carbon (C), hydrogen (H), chlorine (Cl), nitrogen (N), sulfur (S), phosphorus (P), sodium (Na) and oxygen (O) content to subsequently calculate the Theoretical Oxygen Demand (ThOD) of each material.

Oxygen consumption within closed respirometers method

The biodegradability of the materials was assessed following the methodology proposed by the standard ISO 14,851, for the determination of the ultimate aerobic biodegradability of plastic materials in an aqueous medium, by measuring the oxygen demand in a closed respirometer25. The method is based on monitoring the oxygen consumed inside closed respirometers under aerobic conditions by using a standard solution as assay medium. To assess the influence of the temperature, inoculum source on the final degradation, different tests were designed with an incubation time of 28 days, as done by López-Ibáñez et al. (2022)26 who made some modifications of the standard to reduce the duration of the tests. The Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) was continuously recorded over the test duration.

The final degradation of each material was calculated as the relationship between the measured BOD at the end of the experiment (BOD28) and its Theoretical Oxygen Demand (ThOD), which is the total amount of oxygen necessary to fully biodegrade each test material.

Test solution and inoculum sources

A common assay medium was employed in all the prepared solutions according to the international standard ISO 14,851 (ISO, 2019) by using grade reagents: KH2PO4 (Labkem), K2HPO4 (Panreac), Na2HPO4·2H2O (Labkem), NH4Cl (Panreac), MgSO47H2O (Labkem), CaCl22H2O (Labkem), and FeCl36H2O (Labkem). As an inoculum three different sources were used. The first one was Bac Plus Evo ROTH, a biological product composed on freeze-dried bacteria specially selected for aerobic depurations of wastewater (LyoBact). The second was an activated sludge (ASG) collected from the Guillarei Wastewater Treatment Plant (WWTP) (Tui, Galicia, Spain), considered the standard inoculum source according to the guidelines And the third was a Liquid Fraction of Anaerobic Digestate (LFAD) provided by the technological Centre EnergyLab, included to increase microbial diversity and broaden the range of metabolic capabilities evaluated. Although originating from an anaerobic process, this inoculum may contain facultative microorganisms with metabolic versatility, allowing their activity under aerobic conditions and their use in oxygen consumption-based biodegradation assays. Before its use an elemental analysis of each inoculum source was carried out to determine the Total Carbon (TC), Total Nitrogen (TN) and Total Organic Carbon (TOC) via thermocatalytic decomposition (Analytik Jena multi-N/C 3100). Nutrient content (NO2–, NO3–, PO43-, −) and ammonium (NH4+) were analysed by ion chromatography (Metrohm 940 IC) and colorimetry (Spectroquant Nova 60). Besides, pH and Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD) were also measured. Independently of the inoculum used, the test medium was adjusted to a pH 7 and to a suspended solids range between 30 and 1000 mgL[-1 with a final CFU of 105 a. This parameter was determined following the procedure explained in the international standard UNE-EN ISO 6222:1999 (ISO, 1999).

Equipment

To determine the BOD, two different laboratory devices were employed, with a main difference in their stirring speed. Twelve OxiTop®-IDS manometric sensors were employed with their corresponding stirring system (440 rpm). In parallel, the same was developed using twelve Velp ® head sensors with the inductive stirring system from Velp ® with a stirring speed count of 60 rpm. Independently of the equipment used, amber glass bottles of 500 mL were employed as bioreactors, and a trap of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) was incorporated as a CO2 captor.

Batches and treatment preparation

A total of 9 batches were prepared, grouped in three different trials. Each of these trials was designed to give an answer to a different specific objective (Table 2). Each batch consisted of 12 reactors for each equipment type (Oxitop and VELP). For each system, three replicates were prepared for the blanks (test medium without plastic) and for each of the three materials tested (PHBV, PLA, and PP), resulting in a total of 12 reactors per equipment. Each reactor was filled with 164 mL, equivalent to one-third of the total volume, to guarantee the availability of O2 in the headspace and the analyzed materials were added at a concentration of 0.1 gL−1. The bottles were kept closed during the incubation time, so no additional oxygen was provided during the experiment.

Microbial community characterization

DNA was extracted from the samples following a protocol involving mechanical lysis, enzymatic digestion, and purification using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The V3–V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene and the ITS region were amplified using the primer pairs 515 F/806R and ITS3/ITS4 27, respectively, following a two-step barcoded PCR approach. Purified amplicons were pooled in equimolar concentrations and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform using 2 × 250 bp paired end reads (Microsynth AG, Balgach, Switzerland). Sequencing data were delivered demultiplexed and quality trimmed. Bioinformatic processing included primer removal, quality filtering, merging of paired end reads, denoising to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs)28, taxonomic assignment against the NCBI RefSeq Targeted Loci database, and diversity analyses performed using established pipelines in R and USEARCH29.

Statistical analysis

To analyze differences between the BOD curves, generalized linear mixed-effects models were applied. These models included treatment, inoculum, temperature, equipment, and time as fixed effects, and incorporated random effects to account for variability among experimental replicates and batches. Model selection was based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). To assess differences in the Dt reached by each material, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. This analysis was conducted separately for each equipment and batch to determine whether the treatment had a significant effect on the final degradation levels. All the tests were performed using the R Software30.

Results and discussion

Trial I: Searching for an alternative inoculum source to reduce variability

As shown in Fig. 1 no statistically significant differences were found in the BOD curves of each polymer between batches (P > 0.05) in none of the treatments, with BOD values above 100 mg O2 L− 1 with slight variations between replicates. In contrast, the oxygen consumption in the PLA and PP treatments did not differ from that of the blanks. Consequently, the BOD remained close to 0 mg O₂ L-¹ throughout the process. The minimal microbial activity in the treatments with PP also was expected, as it is one of the most studied polymers and it is known by its slow degradation rate under laboratory conditions27.This is mainly due to the length of the molecules that conform their chain, their highly ordered structure, the lack of available groups for oxidation and the presence of methyl side groups that impair biodegradation31. No doubt, PP can be biodegraded but the process generally implies a physicochemical pretreatment of the material in a first step and a then the oxidation carried out by the microbial degradation3233,. In the case of PLA, the results showed a similar trend to PP, despite being a bio-based polymer synthesized from natural sources like corn starch, sugarcane or casava roots and considered a compostable and biodegradable polymer34. It is widely acknowledged that PLA is biodegradable under composting conditions, mostly during the thermophilic phase characterized by high temperature3536,. Under these conditions the glass transition temperature of PLA (56–58 °C) is exceeded and the abiotic hydrolysis of ester bonds is accelerated37. This forms low-molecular-weight fragments which subsequently are assimilated and mineralized by microorganisms into end-products, generating carbon dioxide under aerobic conditions and methane under anaerobic conditions38. Nevertheless, it has been reported that microorganisms do not enhance PLA degradation, and that polymer cleavage proceeds solely by abiotic hydrolysis of ester linkages independently on the presence of microorganisms39. The lack of these specific conditions would explain why PLA based polymers do not suffer biodegradation in aquatic environments4041,, as shown in this study, where the kinetic of PLA treatments was exactly the same as for PP (Fig. 1). Indeed, the statistical model did not reveal statistically significant differences (p > 0.05) among the kinetic obtained for the BOD curves of PLA and PP treatments in none of the batches carried out as part of this first experiment.

The highest oxygen consumption was observed in the PHBV treatments (Fig. 1), reaching final BOD28 values above 100 mg O2 L− 1 with slight variations between replicates. This is explained by biodegradability of this material in aqueous medium, an already probed biodegradable polymer in freshwater that even has been proposed as a suitable material to be employed as positive control in this type of42–44. Nevertheless, despite having been proposed as a suitable positive control for these biodegradation tests, the high variability to which are subjected has been recently reported as an important gap on this area resulting on important differences even considering theoretically readily biodegradable polymers such as PHBV as found recently by Gutiérrez-Rial et al. (2025)45. One of the main causes of this variability has been attributed to the instability of the inoculum23, that according to the standard ISO 14,851, must be an activated sludge collected from a WWTP. Nevertheless, the microbial community may be highly variable on time making difficult the replicability of this assessments46. Standard inocula are normally prepared by diluting a source of microorganisms to obtain a suspension that contains an estimated amount of CFU per milliliter, and due to this variability, the inaccuracy of these tests increases the potential for not replicable results. Therefore, the development of standard bacterial communities as for example those preserved by free drying techniques could be an alternative to employ as bacterial reference inoculum for these tests47, increasing the replicability and reducing differences inter and intralaboratoies as proposed by Paixão et al. (2006)48.

In this study the BOD curves obtained for PHBV treatments as well as the final BOD28 measured at the end of the incubation period showed no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05), either among the different batches or between the two devices (OXITOP and VELP). Using activated sludge as inoculum, depending on the origin the bacterial diversity will be high variable and the potential to biodegrade a specific compound will therefore be different49. Previous investigations have shown that different activated sludges collected worldwide differ very much in their activity and microbial diversity, increasing the potential to obtain inaccuracy in the results5051,.

It is important to note that only one representative sample of each polymer type was evaluated in this study. Therefore, the results cannot be directly extrapolated to all materials within the same polymer class, particularly in the case of copolymers or materials with different structural arrangements (e.g., PHBV), which may exhibit different biodegradation behaviour. Nevertheless, as the primary objective of this study was to assess the potential of an alternative inoculum to reduce variability between experiments conducted at different times, the observed improvement in reproducibility is expected to be applicable across a broader range of materials. Indeed, the results not only prove that the utilization of a freeze-dried bacteria as inoculum reduces and even eliminates differences among tests, but also the differences among the different equipment; another important variability source recently revealed by Gutiérrez-Rial et al. (2025)45 and probably attributed to the different operational characteristics of each device. Consequently, the biodegradation rates obtained for PHBV28 were also consistent among the different batches ranging from 65 ± 11% to 72 ± 8% with no statistically significant differences between equipment or batches despite the slight variations (p > 0.05) (Fig. 2). In the case of PLA and PP treatments, no significant differences in biodegradation were observed, with biodegradation rates approaching or equaling 0%, indicating that these compounds are not biodegradable in water under the conditions of this experiment.

Trial II: Assessing the impact of temperature on the biodegradation process

The use of freeze-dried bacteria as inoculum reduces variability as shown in the results of the first trial of this study. Consequently, it can be considered a suitable alternative to evaluate the impact on the biodegradation process of other parameters, such as temperature. Under aerobic conditions, one of the main criticisms of test is that they are currently developed at temperatures higher than those found in nature52. In this context, the temperature experiment was performed exclusively using the freeze-dried inoculum in order to minimize variability associated with the inoculum source and to ensure that observed differences could be attributed solely to temperature effects, as demonstrated in Trial I. Indeed, the results of this study have demonstrated that the dynamic of the process was highly influenced by temperature, with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) between the BOD curves of the PHBV at 10, 20 and 35 °C. The oxygen consumption at 35 °C was higher than at the reference temperature indicated by the standard (20º). Meanwhile, at 10 °C, it was much lower, and, in this case, statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) were obtained between the equipment, with higher values measured in the OXITOP rather than in the VELP (Fig. 3). The activity in the treatments with PLA and PP was insignificant independently on the temperature at which each test was conducted and as a result the oxygen consumption within these treatments was practically the same as in the blanks, which is why the BOD28 curve was practically equal to 0 throughout the whole process.

It is well known that temperature plays an important role in the degradation of polymers and depending on the material composition at a given temperature irreversible physical and chemical modifications may occur. Heat may cause alteration on parameters such as molecular weight, molecular weight distribution or crystallinity53, but also leads to the breakage of the polymeric backbone, as well as changes in properties such as destruction of tensile strength, reduction of durability and cracks5455,. Thus, the exposure of plastics at high temperatures can be used as a pretreatment of the materials to improve the degradation efficiency of subsequent biodegradation carried out by microorganisms56. But temperature also plays an important role determining the abundance and the bacterial community composition in freshwater ecosystems since each bacterial phylotype has its own optimal temperature range5758,. So, temperature drives the microbial colonization of the plastic surface conditioning the biofilm formation, a process that affect the physiochemical properties of plastics and precedes the mineralization of them by the action of microorganisms59. This colonization of the plastic surface was called “plastisphere” and comprises to the microbial communities, including bacterial and fungal species embedded with a matrix of extracellular polymeric substances that results in the formation of biofilms60. Some studies have shown that the microbial community in the plastisphere varies depending on the polymer type, but also across different environments, with temperature being one of the most influential environmental factors6162,. Additionally, temperature also directly influences bacterial activity and generally, with some exceptions, the higher the temperature the higher the respiration rates and population growth63. This is one of the main gaps of these assessment tests, due to rivers as equal as oceans are not continuous bioreactors with a uniform temperature64. Indeed, it has been proved that plastisphere has distinct microbial community structure even across different river functional zones but also because of seasonal changes6566,. In rivers higher temperatures are associated with increased microbial growth, particularly in summer resulting to a greater diversity in surface waters67.

The effect of temperature on plastics degradation has already been demonstrated in soils68 and marine environments69. It is known that the impact is so important that this parameter may lead to an overestimation of the real biodegradability of bioplastics. For freshwater environments, as shown in Fig. 4, the results of this study revealed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) between the biodegradation rates reached by the PHBV on the different batches, carried out at 10 °C, 20 °C and 35 °C.The degradation at 10 °C also showed statistically significant differences between the equipment OXITOP and VELP, probably caused by the different stirring speed of the devices as previously found by Gutiérrez-Rial et al. (2025)45. For example, rivers from the Iberian Peninsula, located in the southernmost part of Europe, usually have an annual mean temperature of 15 °C, with values lower than 10 °C70. In this context, the certification or labelling of plastic products as biodegradable in aqueous media according to standard test conditions may lead to misuse and mismanagement of these materials. This could result, on the one hand, in the accumulation of large amounts of bioplastics in rivers, where they may not biodegrade or may require longer times than expected based on laboratory tests, increasing the risk of MPs formation. On the other hand, it may promote the accumulation of aged biopolymers that can act as vectors for organic and inorganic pollutants, representing a potential risk for biodiversity conservation and public health71..

Trial III: Variability on the biodegradation process caused by the variability of the inoculum

In this final trial, the BOD results for PLA and PP were consistent with those of the previous trials described above. No activity attributable to the biodegradation of these materials was detected, regardless of the inoculum used or the equipment employed. For PHBV, the oxygen consumption curves showed no differences between the three inoculum sources when incubated in the OXITOP equipment. However, treatments incubated in the VELP reactors showed statistically significant differences (p < 0.01) between the ASG and LFAD inocula (Fig. 5).

The absence of differences in the microbial activity dynamics, measured through oxygen consumption, for PHBV treatments incubated with ASG and LyoBact inocula would support the potential use of freeze-dried bacteria as an alternative inoculum source to the activated sludge recommended by current standards. Meanwhile, the differences observed for the LFAD inoculum support the initial hypothesis of this study, which proposed that variations in microbial community composition would lead to differences in biodegradation activity, which was previously considered as a potential variability source and thus an important gap of these techniques2447,. The characterization of the microbial community revealed differences between the three inocula employed in this experiment. As shown in Table 4, the diversity of bacterial (16 S) and fungal (ITS) OTUs identified in the Lyobact was significantly lower (p < 0.05) than in the ASG and LFAD inocula. Specifically, ASG exhibited the highest richness and diversity, with both the Shannon and Simpson indices indicating a more diverse and evenly distributed community compared to Lyobact in which the values obtained for Shannon and Simpson suggest a greater dominance of certain OTUs, resulting in a less balanced community7273,.

In this case, despite the differences, all the inoculum sources were characterized by a diverse bacterial and fungal community according to the Shannon diversity index. This diversity is fundamental and serves as the foundation of biodegradation tests, which rely on the assumption that within the range of taxa present in the sample selected as inoculum, exists a sufficient variety of microorganisms capable of degrading the compounds under investigation. So, the higher the diversity of microorganisms in the inoculum source the higher the probability of inoculating an assay with an adequate number of these specific cells which will be involved on the biodegradation process74. But the presence or absence of the specific taxa may not be the only relevant factor, in fact the relative abundance is also important, as found in this study. For instance, although the LFAD inoculum showed higher species richness and Shannon diversity than LyoBact, its community composition was much more like that found for ASG, with a comparable proportion of dominant microorganisms.

As shown in Fig. 6A, the LyoBact inoculum was dominated by the phylum Pseudomonadota, constituting 63.1% of the total. In the ASG inoculum, Pseudomonadota also emerged as the predominant phylum, accounting for 50.8% of the total. Furthermore, the presence of the phyla Bacteroidota and Chloroflexota was observed in both inocula, although their relative proportions differed. Bacteroidota was more abundant in ASG (14.5%) than in LyoBact (5.48%), whereas Chloroflexota was more prevalent in LyoBact (17.7%) compared to ASG (7.08%). In contrast, LFAD displayed a markedly distinct taxonomic profile, dominated by Bacillota (37.9%) and Synergistota (12.6%), with a lower proportion of Pseudomonadota (17.8%). These compositional differences suggest that LFAD harboured the most distinct microbial community among the inocula, which may have implications for functional redundancy and biodegradation potential. The prevalence of Pseudomonota in all three inocula is of particular significance, given that a substantial proportion of enzymes capable of degrading plastic have been affiliated with members of this phylum. Notably, Pseudomonas spp. alone has been reported to degrade up to 35 different plastic types, including most mass-produced polymers. Pseudomonads are highly adaptable, versatile, and ubiquitous in diverse environments; this underscores the likely importance of this taxonomic group in plastic biodegradation. Therefore, the observed dominance of Pseudomonadota in two of the tested inocula suggests a greater potential for effective plastic biodegradation within these microbial communities7576,.

In contrast to the bacterial communities, which exhibited marked compositional differences among the inocula, the fungal communities were relatively similar across all three (Fig. 7A and B). All the inocula were dominated by members of the phyla Ascomycota and Basidiomycota. The predominance of Ascomycota and, to a lesser extent, Basidiomycota is consistent with previous studies reporting these phyla as dominant in freshwater environments, both in the water column and on natural substrates77. More recently, these groups have also been identified as key components of the fungal plastisphere, colonizing plastic debris in aquatic systems. Culture-based and molecular studies have shown that fungal communities on plastic surfaces often consist of approximately two-thirds ascomycetes and one-third basidiomycetes, with taxonomic compositions differing from those found in surrounding sediments7879,. In addition, fungi are of special interest for potential plastic decomposition in the environment due to their vast metabolic potential and their ability to degrade highly recalcitrant structures, further supporting their relevance in the context of plastic biodegradation in aquatic ecosystems80.

Despite of the high diversity of the microbial communities, neither PLA nor PP suffered biodegradation during the incubation time despite of the presence of bacterial groups such as those included in the genus Bacillus (family Bacillaceae) (Fig. 6B) previously related with the biodegradation of PP and PLA (Jain et al., 2018) as well as other gram positive bacteria belonging to the order Aneurinibacillales which which includes known PLA-degraders81 or some species of the genus Pseudomonas included in the Family Pseudomonaceae wich were identified as potent PLA-degrading bacteria82. Nevertheless, the presence of bacterial groups with potential to biodegrade these materials is not enough to guarantee the biodegradation of these polymers, in part because the necessary conditions are not present, mainly due to the soft temperature selected for the development of these tests. Many of the most effective degraders, particularly obligate thermophiles, grow at temperatures between 50 and 70 °C, with an optimum of 55–65 °C. These organisms, including abundant members of the family Bacillaceae such as the genera Anoxybacillus, Brevibacillus, and Geobacillus, may not be active or even viable under the moderate temperature conditions used. Even facultative thermophiles, which thrive at 41–50 °C, or thermotolerant mesophiles that tolerate but do not prefer such temperatures, may not reach sufficient activity levels to cause effective biodegradation83.

On the contrary, for the PHBV treatments, higher microbial activity than in the blanks was observed, regardless of the specific inocula, as previously mentioned. This may be due to the utilization of PHBV as a carbon source by microorganisms, ultimately leading to polymer biodegradation. This was possible since all inocula contained microbial groups such as Bacillota or Pseudomonadota, which include taxa previously associated with PHA degradation processes8485.,. The first bacterial genera associated with the degradation of PHAs were members of the genus Bacillus, such as Bacillus megaterium, which has been extensively studied for its ability to produce and degrade PHB17. However, over time, the identification of PHA-degrading microorganisms has increased significantly. Other bacterial groups that have been identified are Alphaproteobacteria, particularly within the order Rhodobacterales, as well as Gammaproteobacteria, such as members of the family Rhodanobacteraceae, highlighting the broad phylogenetic diversity of microorganisms potentially involved in these processes86. The presence of these groups in the inocula used in this experiment enabled the assessment of PHBV biodegradation under the tested conditions, as the combination of an appropriate microbial community and favorable environmental conditions supported their enzymatic activity. Nevertheless, Further research focused specifically on the isolation and functional validation of microorganisms capable of degrading these compounds is therefore required to confirm their actual metabolic roles.

It is important to highlight the results obtained from the DCA analysis regarding the impact of PHBV on microbial community composition for both ASG and LFAD inocula. In the case of the ASG inoculum, the final microbial communities differed notably between the PHBV and Blank treatments in both reactors (Fig. 8). The 16 S rRNA analysis revealed a significant increase in the relative abundance of Betaproteobacteria in the PHBV treatments, suggesting that members of the order Burkholderiales were able to use PHBV as a carbon source, leading to enhanced growth. This was particularly evident in the high relative abundance of Comamonadaceae and Sphaerotilaceae, which exceeded the levels observed in the initial inoculum and in the blanks after incubation. In the case of the LFAD inoculum, the most notable shift was the increased abundance of the Caulobacteraceae family in response to PHBV. However, this response was more variable between replicates. In contrast, for the LyoBact inoculum, no statistically significant differences were observed between the PHBV and Blank treatments.

The results of this study revealed that a wide variety of bacterial species may contribute to PHBV biodegradation under suitable environmental conditions. For example, Brevundimonas haloterans was detected in high proportions in the PHBV treatments inoculated with LFAD, while Comamonas phosphati, Curvibacter delicatus, and Schlegelella koreensis exhibited marked growth in the PHB treatments inoculated with ASG. These species are known to thrive at temperatures around 20 °C87,88, supporting the conclusion that PHBV biodegradation can occur under mesophilic conditions, as illustrated in Fig. 9. In contrast, no degradation of PLA or PP was detected in this study, despite the presence of potential degrader taxa. This is likely because higher temperatures are required to activate the enzymatic systems responsible for the degradation of these polymers, which were not reached under the experimental conditions employed on this study (Fig. 9).

Conclusions

The use of standard or commercial freeze-dried inocula should be considered a suitable alternative to activated sludge from WWTPs, as it improves the replicability of these standard tests. The use of an inoculum with this characteristic would allow assessing the impact caused by other parameters, such as agitation, particle size or temperature, once the variability introduced by the inoculum has been reduced. In fact, slight variations in the microbial community composition of the inoculum lead to statistically significant differences in biodegradability rate between the same test material under identical conditions, as revealed in this study. In addition, the temperature range within which the standard test is conducted should be reviewed to ensure its correct use as an indicator of biodegradability in natural environments, given that lower temperatures can increase reliability. As demonstrated in this research, conducting biodegradability tests at temperatures higher than those found in natural ecosystems can lead to an overestimation of the material’s biodegradation potential. These findings highlight the need to carefully consider test conditions when using standardized methods as indicators of environmental behaviour. While standardized inocula improve comparability between studies, the development of environment-specific microbial consortia (e.g., soil, sediment, or marine “microbial cocktails”) could represent a complementary approach to provide greater ecological relevance and improve the interpretation of biodegradation assays under different environmental scenarios. Furthermore, this work extends current knowledge of PHB-degrading microorganisms by identifying potential degraders belonging to the orders Burkholderiales and Rhodobacterales, as well as the family Rhodanobacteraceae, highlighting the ecological diversity of bacteria capable of using PHAs as a carbon source.
