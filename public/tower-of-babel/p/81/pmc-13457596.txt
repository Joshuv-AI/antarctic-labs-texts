FET proteins and PARylation-dependent condensates promote replication fork reversal and genome stability
Authors: Celeste Giansanti, Jack C Schultz, Jessica Jackson, Alessandro Vindigni, David Cortez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTargeting replication-associated DNA repair mechanisms, including the control of ADP-ribosylation by PARP1/2 and PARG, is a powerful therapeutic approach for cancer. However, the mechanisms by which PARG inhibition impacts DNA replication remain unclear. Here, we combine isolation of proteins on nascent DNA (iPOND) with quantitative proteomics and functional assays to investigate replication fork dynamics upon acute PARG inhibition. We find that FET family proteins (FUS, EWS, and TAF15) are recruited to replication forks in a PAR-dependent manner, forming condensates that slow fork progression and promote fork reversal. FET proteins control fork dynamics in response to some, but not all, replication stresses. FUS inactivation leads to unrestrained fork progression via RECQ1 and PRIMPOL, increased single-stranded DNA gaps, genome instability, and synthetic lethality with BRCA1 deficiency. These findings reveal that FET protein assemblies modulate replication stress responses, influencing genome stability and the cellular response to cancer therapeutics targeting PARylation pathways.Subject terms: DNA damage and repair, Stalled forks

Introduction

Many chemotherapies and radiation treatments for cancer act by inducing DNA damage and disrupting DNA replication, but these approaches often cause significant collateral damage to normal tissues. Therefore, considerable effort has been put into studying the mechanisms of DNA repair and replication stress responses that control cancer cell survival with the goal of developing more cancer-specific therapies. One successful strategy is to target DNA damage repair pathways using synthetic lethality, particularly in tumors deficient in homology-directed repair (HR) due to inactivation of genes such as BRCA1 or BRCA21. In these HR-deficient tumors, agents that interfere with protein poly(ADP-ribosyl)ation (PARylation) by PARP enzymes exploit a large therapeutic window, selectively killing cancer cells while sparing normal cells2,3.

Protein ADP-ribosylation is a widespread posttranslational modification catalyzed by ADP-ribosyltransferases (ARTs). PARP1, PARP2 and PARP3 are ARTs involved in DNA replication and damage repair4–6. Poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR) is rapidly removed by PARG (Poly(ADP-ribose) glycohydrolase), which catalyzes the hydrolysis of the glycosidic bonds between ADP-ribose units of PAR chains7,8. During DNA replication, the processing of Okazaki fragments can activate PARP1 and PARP2, leading to the accumulation of PAR chains9. PARylation controls replication fork speed, and PARG activity is essential for maintaining normal replication dynamics9–14.

PARP inhibitors are now widely used to treat HR-deficient breast, ovarian, prostate, and pancreatic cancers, although both de novo and acquired resistance can limit their effectiveness. Pharmacological inhibitors targeting other DNA repair enzymes, including PARG, are being actively developed as additional cancer therapeutic tools15,16.

PARG inhibition preferentially kills cells with DNA strand breaks and those experiencing challenges to DNA replication, such as impaired joining of Okazaki fragments13,17–25. Several PARG inhibitors have been developed, including PDD00017272/3, IDE161, ETX-19477, XNW29016, JA2131, and COH3417,21,26–29. Notably, the FDA has granted fast-track designation to IDE161 for platinum-resistant, BRCA1/2-mutant, advanced or metastatic ovarian cancer, and HR + , HER2-negative breast cancers30,31. PARG inhibitors are also being considered for use in HR-deficient breast, endometrial, and gastrointestinal cancers32. However, the specific vulnerabilities that determine sensitivity to PARG inhibitors and their precise mechanisms of action remain incompletely understood, and further elucidation of these factors is crucial for optimizing the use of PARG inhibitors in precision oncology.

Multiple lines of evidence indicate that PARG inhibition exerts its cytotoxic effect by interfering with DNA replication. First, PARG inhibitor sensitivity is characterized by persistent DNA replication stress and an accumulation of PAR at sites of replication13,17,19,33. Second, interfering with replication confers PARG inhibitor sensitivity and cell lines intrinsically sensitive to PARG inhibition contain defects in genes involved in DNA replication34. Among these are components of the fork protection complex, TIMELESS, TIPIN, and CLASPIN, Okazaki processing factors EXO1 and FEN1, and the major replication stress response kinases ATR and CHK113,18,19,33. Third, PARG travels with the replication machinery, interacts with PCNA, and its localization is regulated by replication stress35–37. Fourth, PARG activity prevents the accumulation of abnormal replication structures, and PARG inactivation triggers replication fork stalling and the accumulation of reversed replication forks12,17.

To investigate how PAR accumulation interferes with DNA replication and to gain insights into the molecular determinants of PARG inhibitor sensitivity, we used Isolation of Proteins on Nascent DNA (iPOND)38 combined with quantitative mass spectrometry36,39 to analyze changes in the replication fork proteome following PARG inhibition. As expected, we found that acute PARG inhibition increases PAR and enhances the recruitment of PAR-binding proteins involved in the base excision repair and single-strand break repair pathways33. Importantly, we also observed that three related proteins, FUS (fused in sarcoma/translocated in sarcoma), EWS/EWSR1 (Ewing sarcoma protein), and TAF15 (TATA box-binding protein-associated factor 68 kDa), collectively abbreviated as FET proteins, are among the most enriched proteins on nascent DNA in the presence of the PARG inhibitor.

FET proteins form condensates using low complexity domains (LCDs), bind PAR, DNA, and RNA, and are involved in diverse cellular processes, including transcription regulation, RNA splicing, transport, and microRNA processing40–43. FET proteins also participate in the detection and repair of DNA damage. They localize to sites of laser-induced DNA damage and facilitate the recruitment of DNA damage response factors44–47. FUS promotes homologous DNA pairing and is required for efficient double-strand break repair by both homologous recombination and nonhomologous end joining46,48. Mutations in FET proteins are associated with pathological protein aggregation in neurodegenerative diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal lobar degeneration (FTLD)49–54. Furthermore, FET genes are also frequently involved in gene translocations resulting in fusion proteins that promote tumorigenesis55.

FET proteins contain two types of LCDs: a carboxyl-terminal, positively charged arginine–glycine–glycine (RGG)-rich LCD that acts as a PAR sensor, and an amino-terminal, prion-like SYQG-rich LCD that can form self-assemblies, undergo liquid-liquid phase separation, and form condensates44,56,57. In this study, we show that FET proteins and FUS-dependent assemblies regulate DNA replication fork progression and fork reversal in response to PARG inhibition and some replication stress-inducing agents. FET proteins are necessary to prevent the accumulation of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) gaps and to maintain genome stability and cell proliferation, particularly in the context of BRCA1 deficiency.

Results

FET proteins are enriched on nascent DNA at replication forks upon PARG inhibition

Previous studies of PARG function in DNA replication used RNA interference or prolonged treatment with PARG inhibitors. Since PARG inhibition can cause NAD+ depletion and metabolic changes that significantly impact replication dynamics58, we aimed to directly assess the immediate effects of acute PAR accumulation on DNA replication. We first investigated how 20 min of PARG inhibition impacts replisome composition in HEK293T cells utilizing iPOND combined with quantitative mass spectrometry (MS) using stable isotope labeling by amino acids in cell culture (SILAC)36,59 (Fig. 1a). The PAR-binding proteins XRCC1, POLβ and LIG3 are among the most enriched proteins on nascent DNA in the presence of the PARG inhibitor PDD00017273 (Fig. 1b; and Supplementary Data 1). These base excision repair and single-strand break repair proteins are recruited to sites of DNA damage by PARylation5. PARP2 is also enriched, while PARP1 recruitment is unchanged (Supplementary Data 1). Three related proteins, FUS, EWS, and TAF15, collectively abbreviated as FET proteins, were also highly enriched (Fig. 1b; and Supplementary Data 1). We confirmed that FUS localized to replication forks upon PARG inhibition using a proximity ligation assay (PLA) (Fig. 1c, d). We also confirmed that replication fork localization was PAR-dependent, as it was abolished by treatment with the PARP1/2 inhibitor Olaparib (Fig. 1e). These results are consistent with and extend previous observations that FUS is recruited to sites of DNA damage through an interaction with PAR44,45,47,57,60.

FET proteins undergo reversible liquid–liquid phase separation to form biomolecular condensates, which coordinate the localization of essential DNA Damage Response (DDR) factors44. We analyzed FUS distribution by immunofluorescent (IF) staining in U2OS and RPE1-hTERT cells. As expected, nuclear FUS is not uniformly distributed (Fig. 1f; Supplementary Fig. 1a). Instead, it forms nuclear compartments/foci. Importantly, in PARGi-treated cells, FUS foci increasingly co-localized with the replication protein PCNA, further corroborating that PAR accumulation triggers FUS recruitment to DNA replication forks (Fig. 1f, g; and Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Analysis of FUS signal within PCNA-positive replication sites revealed that FUS is present at ~40% of replication foci upon PARG inhibition, which often appeared to be internal to the nucleus rather than at the nuclear periphery (Fig. 1f, h). The increases in intensity and number of FUS-PCNA co-localized foci caused by PARG inhibition were largely abolished by the addition of Olaparib (Fig. 1f–h; and Supplementary Fig. 1a, b), establishing PARP1/2-mediated PARylation as the trigger for FUS re-localization. We conclude that FUS and the other FET proteins are recruited to replication forks in cells upon acute PARG inhibition and in a PAR-dependent manner.

Fork slowing upon PARG inhibition depends on FET proteins

Previous studies found that persistent PARG inactivation slows replication forks12,13,17,19,61. Since FUS inactivation has also been reported to restrain replication fork speed62, we wanted to assess whether FUS and the other FET proteins operate in the same pathway as PARG to control replication fork progression. Therefore, we utilized DNA combing with acute PARG inhibitor treatment during the nucleoside analog labeling period (Fig. 2a). PARG inhibition induced a significant reduction in fork speed that was dependent on PARP1/2 function, as Olaparib abolished this effect (Fig. 2b, c). However, we did not observe altered fork speeds when FUS or the other FET proteins were inactivated by themselves (Fig. 2c). Unexpectedly, silencing any of the FET proteins individually or all together restored fork speeds to normal rates in the PARG inhibitor-treated cells (Fig. 2b, c). The PARG inhibitor treatment increased PAR levels irrespective of FET protein knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Rescue of PARG inhibitor-dependent fork slowing was confirmed in cells where FUS was knocked out by CRISPR-mediated gene editing (Fig. 2d). We also confirmed the same effects using a second PARG inhibitor, COH3421 (Fig. 2e). Thus, FET proteins are required to restrain replication in response to acute PARG inhibition.

FUS PAR binding and self-assembly are required to modulate fork progression

Next, we examined whether the FET-mediated fork slowing involved the formation of FUS assemblies using the DisCo (Disassembly of Condensates) system, which provides inducible condensate disruption using rapamycin treatment63 (Fig. 2f; and Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). Indeed, FUS condensate disassembly completely abrogated fork slowing caused by PARG inhibition, indicating that restrained DNA replication fork progression requires the ability of FUS to assemble in biomolecular condensates (Fig. 2g).

To further determine which FUS functions are needed to slow replication forks, we examined three FUS truncation mutants in addition to full-length FUS (Supplementary Fig. 3a). FUS 1-373 lacks the two terminal PAR-binding RGG motifs. FUS 1-285 also lacks the RNA Recognition Motif (RRM) that binds RNA and has been shown to interact with PAR and control the auto-PARylation activity of PARP164. FUS 267-526 lacks the N-terminal SYQG-rich LCD, preventing it from undergoing phase separation and forming condensates44. All proteins were expressed at similar levels and localized similarly to the nucleus due to the addition of a nuclear localization signal (Supplemental Fig. 3b, c). Overexpression of full-length FUS was sufficient to induce the formation of FUS foci in the nucleus even in the absence of PARG inhibition (Fig. 3a, b; and Supplementary Fig. 3d), consistent with spontaneous assembly when intracellular concentrations exceed threshold levels. As previously reported, FUS 1-285 formed spherical droplets consistent with phase separation44. FUS 1-373 formed foci similar in shape to full-length FUS, but fewer in number, while FUS 267-526 was more diffusely localized throughout the nucleus (Fig. 3a, b; and Supplementary Fig. 3d). These mutants, except for FUS 267-526, showed reduced co-localization to PCNA foci, indicating the importance of the C-terminal PAR-interacting domains for recruitment to DNA replication forks (Fig. 3c; Supplementary Fig. 3e). Next, we examined whether these mutants regulate replication using DNA combing. Overexpression of full-length FUS caused fork slowing with or without PARG inhibition, consistent with its appearance in nuclear foci at replication forks irrespective of adding the PARGi (Fig. 3d; and Supplementary Fig. 3e). In contrast, none of the other FUS proteins slowed forks in the absence or presence of PARG inhibitor (Fig. 3d, and Supplementary Fig. 3f). Therefore, both FUS condensate-forming ability and PAR-binding domains are required to slow fork progression.

The FUS RGG domains are lost in gene translocations that generate FUS fusion proteins in sarcomas, suggesting they would be unable to regulate replication55. Indeed, a FUS-CHOP fusion protein is unable to localize to forks or slow replication when overexpressed in the absence of PARG inhibition, in contrast to the wild-type FUS protein (Fig. 3e–h; and Supplementary Fig. 3g, h). We utilized FUS wild-type cells for these experiments since the cancers with these fusion proteins typically retain one wild-type FUS gene. In these conditions, overexpression of the FUS-CHOP fusion also prevented fork slowing in the presence of PARG inhibition, suggesting it acts as a dominant negative protein since it can form assemblies with the endogenous FUS protein but is unable to localize to replication forks (Supplemental Fig. 3i–k).

FUS is required to slow replication forks in response to H2O2 and CPT, but not HU

We next asked whether the FET proteins are required to reduce replication fork elongation in other replication stress contexts. We treated cells with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to induce oxidative DNA damage, the topoisomerase I inhibitor camptothecin (CPT), which causes topological stress, or hydroxyurea (HU), which both depletes nucleotides and induces oxidative stress by inhibiting ribonucleotide reductase. In the presence of H2O2, FUS foci appeared smaller than those seen after PARG inhibitor treatment (Fig. 4a and Fig. 1f). However, as with PARG inhibitor treatment, these FUS foci often co-localized with PCNA. The intensity of FUS within PCNA foci was increased in H2O2-treated cells compared to the untreated control (Fig. 4a, b), and PCNA co-localization was dependent on PARP1/2 activity (Supplemental Fig. 4a). Similarly, CPT strongly enhanced the intensity of FUS within PCNA foci (Fig. 4a, b), indicating that FUS re-localizes to replication forks in response to topological stress. In contrast, HU did not affect the FUS signal distribution relative to PCNA (Fig. 4a, b), consistent with previous iPOND proteomics data that did not observe enrichment of FUS at forks in response to HU36. Notably, HU treatment did not significantly enhance chromatin-bound PAR levels (Supplementary Fig. 4b), suggesting that HU-induced replication stress does not induce as much PARylation as other stresses and, consequently, does not recruit FET proteins to DNA replication forks.

As expected, H2O2, CPT, or HU induced fork slowing (Fig. 4c–i). Importantly, silencing FUS, EWS or the FET proteins together by siRNA or knocking out FUS alone reduced fork slowing in response to H2O2 and CPT (Fig. 4d, e, g, h; and Supplementary Fig. 4c–g). TAF15 individual knockdown diminished fork slowing in response to CPT. TAF15 knockdown alone also reduced the slowing in response to H2O2, but it did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, replication fork speed remained slow in the presence of HU regardless of FET protein status (Fig. 4f, i). Similarly, aphidicolin-induced reduction in fork progression was largely unaffected by FUS inactivation (Supplementary Fig. 4h). These results indicate that FET proteins are required to slow replication in response to agents such as CPT, H2O2, and PARG inhibition, all of which increase PAR levels, but are not required for fork slowing induced by aphidicolin or HU, which directly inhibit DNA polymerases or ribonucleotide reductase, respectively.

FUS promotes fork reversal to slow DNA replication forks

Replication fork slowing in response to H2O2 and CPT is due to fork reversal (Fig. 5a)65. Previous studies have shown that genetic inactivation of PARG leads to the accumulation of reversed replication forks12. To determine if the FET proteins promote fork slowing in response to acute PARG inhibition by promoting fork reversal, we assessed the requirement for key fork reversal proteins, including RAD5165 and the DNA translocases SMARCAL166, ZRANB367, and HLTF68 using DNA combing (Fig. 5b). Depletion of RAD51 by siRNA or inactivation of the DNA translocases by CRISPR-mediated gene editing abolished the reduction in DNA synthesis in PARG inhibitor-treated cells (Fig. 5c, d; and Supplementary Fig. 5a, b), indicating that fork slowing caused by acute PARG inhibition depends on fork reversal. Notably, co-depletion of FET proteins did not further increase fork progression, suggesting that FET proteins function in the same pathway as fork reversal proteins to mediate fork slowing under replication stress.

To confirm that FUS is needed for fork reversal, we examined replication intermediates using electron microscopy in wild-type and FUS-silenced cells treated with the PARG inhibitor. As expected, we observed large numbers of reversed forks in the control cells in response to acute PARG inhibition (Fig. 5e, f). These were greatly reduced when FUS was inactivated. Instead, we saw the accumulation of fork structures with ssDNA gaps (Fig. 5e, f). Thus, FUS is required to observe reversed forks when PARG is inhibited.

Unrestrained DNA synthesis in FET-deficient cells depends on RECQ1 and PRIMPOL

The PRIMPOL polymerase can reprime DNA synthesis in response to replication stress, thereby increasing fork speeds but resulting in the formation of ssDNA gaps (Fig. 6a)69,70. To determine whether the unrestrained fork speeds and ssDNA gaps observed in FET-deficient cells depend on PRIMPOL-mediated repriming, we used PRIMPOL knockout cells. Indeed, loss of PRIMPOL prevented the increase in fork speeds that was otherwise seen upon FET depletion altogether or in the single knockdowns following PARG inhibition (Fig. 6b, c; and Supplementary Fig. 6a–c). Conversely, re-expression of PRIMPOL in these cells restored DNA synthesis rates to normal, indicating that PRIMPOL is responsible for the increased fork speeds when FET proteins are absent. Additionally, inclusion of the ssDNA gap-specific S1 nuclease in the DNA combing assay resulted in a significant reduction in the length of newly synthesized DNA tracks upon FET loss, consistent with the presence of ssDNA gaps due to PRIMPOL activity (Fig. 6b, d) and as observed in our electron microscopy analysis (Fig. 5e, f).

Previous reports identified a role for the helicase RECQ1 in restarting reversed DNA replication forks and showed that RECQ1-mediated fork restart is regulated by PARylation (Fig. 6a)71. Therefore, we used DNA combing to investigate whether the FET proteins acted antagonistically to RECQ1 activity, thereby reducing DNA replication fork progression (Fig. 6b). Knockdown of RECQ1 had no effect by itself but restored fork slowing in FET-deficient cells treated with PARG inhibitor (Fig. 6e; and Supplementary Fig. 6d), revealing that the unrestrained DNA synthesis observed in FET-deficient cells in the presence of PARG inhibitor depends on RECQ1.

FUS inactivation causes genome instability and decreased proliferation of BRCA1-deficient cancer cells

The accumulation of gaps on nascent DNA when FET proteins are inactivated prompted us to examine the effects of FUS on genome stability and cellular proliferation. FUS knockout did not confer a significant hypersensitivity to PARG inhibitors on its own (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). We also did not observe a significant increase in the DNA damage marker γH2AX in the FUS-deficient cells (Fig. 7a, b; and Supplementary Fig. 7c). We reasoned that the detrimental effects of the accumulation of replication-dependent ssDNA gaps on cellular fitness could be limited in HR-proficient cells due to their protection and repair by HR proteins72,73. If true, we would expect BRCA1 inactivation to sensitize cells to the loss of FUS and PARG inhibition. Indeed, depleting FUS exacerbated γH2AX accumulation in BRCA1-silenced U2OS cells and in the breast cancer cell line HCC1937, which lacks BRCA1 (Fig. 7a, b; and Supplementary Fig. 7c–f). Importantly, re-expression of BRCA1 in HCC1937 prevented the increase in γH2AX (Supplementary Fig. 5e–g). Inactivating FUS generated a large increase in micronuclei in BRCA1-deficient cells with little or no additional increase when PARG inhibitor was added (Fig. 7c; and Supplementary Fig. 7h, i).

We then asked how the combination of PARG inhibitor and FUS inactivation would affect cell survival and proliferation. BRCA1 silencing reduced the fitness of FUS knockout compared to wild-type U2OS cells in the presence of PARG inhibitors (Fig. 7d; Supplementary Fig. 8a). This effect was not observed in U2OS cells with an intact HR pathway (Supplemental Fig. 8b), and BRCA2 silencing yielded an intermediate phenotype (Fig. 7d). Hypersensitivity to PARG inhibition was also observed upon co-silencing of BRCA1 and FUS, EWS, or TAF15 (Fig. 7e). FUS silencing also reduced the fitness of BRCA1-deficient HCC1937 cells, but not HCC1937 cells complemented with wild-type BRCA1 (Fig. 7f). Silencing FUS in the BRCA1-deficient HCC1937 also reduced their ability to form colonies in the presence of PARG inhibitor compared to their wild-type BRCA1-complemented counterparts (Fig. 7g, h). We also confirmed that FUS inactivation reduced cell fitness in two additional BRCA1-mutant breast cancer cell lines, MDA-MB-436 and SUM149PT, and in BRCA1-deficient RPE1-hTERT cells (Fig. 7i; and Supplementary Fig. 8c–f). Thus, FUS is important for the continued growth and viability of BRCA1-deficient cells when treated with PARG inhibitor.

Discussion

Replication stress can trigger multiple tolerance pathways, including fork reversal and repriming74,75. The balance between these pathways is tightly regulated, as each can be beneficial or deleterious to genome stability and cell survival, depending on the context75,76. Here, we discovered that condensate-forming FET proteins (FUS1, EWS, and TAF15) shift the balance towards replication fork reversal-dependent fork slowing and away from repriming in response to PARG inhibition, oxidative stress, or topoisomerase poisoning. This activity promotes genome stability by reducing ssDNA gaps. When FET proteins are inactivated or FUS is prevented from assembling into condensates, the balance shifts towards repriming, leading to increased genome instability and cell death in BRCA1-deficient cell backgrounds.

FET protein recruitment to forks is dependent on PARP1/2-dependent PARylation. This is consistent with previous studies showing recruitment of FET proteins to DNA damage sites44–47, and with a study showing that FET proteins localize to broken telomeres in a PAR-dependent fashion in PARGi-treated cells that use the alternative lengthening of telomeres pathway77. Whether there is a specific PARylation target that is important in these circumstances is unknown. FUS itself can be ADP-ribosylated64,78, and PARP1 auto-modification is sufficient to trigger condensate formation in vitro79, suggesting two possible targets. However, the localized presence of PAR chains may be sufficient to trigger FET recruitment and condensation, regardless of the target of modification.

Our data indicate that FET proteins control replication dynamics by promoting fork reversal-dependent slowing and inhibiting RECQ1- and PRIMPOL-dependent unrestrained DNA synthesis. Exactly how the FET-dependent condensate modulates these effects is unclear. PAR can directly inhibit RECQ1, thereby favoring fork reversal71. It is possible that the FET proteins stabilize or organize PAR at the fork to inhibit RECQ1. However, this seems unlikely to fully explain the results since we found that PAR accumulates to similar levels even when the FET proteins are absent. The FET protein assembly could directly contribute to RECQ1 regulation, although there are no reports of an interaction between these proteins. PARylation of the TIMELESS protein promotes its degradation and alters fork dynamics80, so it is also possible that the FET proteins participate in that regulation. The FET-PAR assembly could recruit critical fork reversal proteins or exclude negative regulators to control the balance of reversal and other replication stress tolerance pathways. It could also act as a physical impediment to the replisome due to the numerous interactions among FET proteins, PAR, and nucleic acids within a FET-protein compartment. Finally, it could facilitate the RAD51 strand-invasion step required for reversal65,81,82, similar to the reported activity of FUS in promoting strand pairing during DSB repair46,48. Further studies will be needed to determine which, if any, of these mechanisms operate. In addition, while several of our observations indicate that FUS condensates are important for fork reversal (FUS protein localizes to nuclear compartments at stressed forks, disrupting FUS assemblies using the DisCo system causes unrestrained synthesis rates, and the FUS condensate-forming LCD is required for fork slowing), it is possible that FET protein functions other than condensation are involved in fork reversal.

Fork reversal is a universal response to replication stress-inducing agents, including H2O2, CPT, aphidicolin, and HU65. In each of these cases, reversal slows the rate of DNA synthesis. Our data indicated that acute PARG inhibition also causes fork-reversal-dependent slowing. Surprisingly, the FET proteins are required only to slow synthesis in response to the PARG inhibitor, H2O2, and CPT, but not to aphidicolin or HU. This difference may reflect the amount of PAR generated by each of these agents. Another difference is that HU and aphidicolin slow the replisome more directly than the other agents, either by inhibiting ribonucleotide reductase or by inhibiting polymerase. In contrast, the base damage caused by H2O2, the torsional stress and strand breaks caused by CPT, and the elevated PAR levels caused by PARG inhibitors act differently to uncouple replisome activities needed to drive fork reversal81,82. Perhaps the FET-dependent condensates are required to make the uncoupling persistent enough to cause reversal in these cases. In any case, our study indicates that different types of replication stress engage different mechanisms to control fork dynamics.

FET-mediated fork slowing is required to prevent the accumulation of ssDNA gaps caused by PRIMPOL repriming. This was surprising, since a previous study found that PARG inhibition caused ssDNA gaps independent of PRIMPOL33. Differences in methodology and cell type could be important. We examined the effect of an acute 30-min treatment with the PARG inhibitor in contrast to the more prolonged treatment times in the other study. In addition, the other study used DNA fiber analysis rather than DNA combing. Observing ssDNA gaps using the fiber method should require gaps on both the leading and lagging template strands. The gaps on the lagging strand could result from defects in Okazaki fragment processing; however, it is unclear where the gaps on the leading-strand template would come from without PRIMPOL repriming. In any case, the gaps we observe in PARG inhibitor-treated, FET protein-deficient cells do not appear to pose a major problem, except when BRCA1 is also inactivated, in which case they are associated with higher levels of γH2AX and reduced cell growth consistent with previous studies indicating the sensitivity of BRCA1-deficient cells to ssDNA gaps72.

The FUS-CHOP fusion protein is unable to slow replication forks and may act as a dominant negative to reduce the fork slowing activity of wild-type FUS in response to PARG inhibition, suggesting that cancers with FUS protein fusions could have altered responses to PARG inhibitors. In addition, the FUS gene on chromosome 3p21.3 is often deleted, and the FUS protein is absent in most lung cancers, a pattern that correlates with worse overall patient survival83,84. Our findings that FUS is critical for mediating the replication stress response to DNA-damaging chemotherapeutic agents such as topoisomerase poisons and PARG inhibitors, and that its inactivation yields higher levels of DNA damage and increased sensitivity to PARG inhibitors in BRCA1-deficient cancer cells, suggest that FUS status could be an important factor to consider when testing cancer treatment strategies. In addition to the FET proteins, we found that many additional proteins are enriched or depleted from nascent DNA upon PARG inhibition. Some, such as those linked to chromatin regulation and DNA end resection, deserve further investigation and could potentially yield insights into genetic vulnerabilities that could be exploited to improve the clinical application of PARG inhibitors.

Methods

Cell culture

U2OS (ATCC, HTB-96; osteosarcoma, human female origin), HEK293T cells (ATCC, CRL-3216; epithelial, human female origin), hTERT-RPE (ATCC, CLR-4000; epithelial, human female origin), and MDA-MB-436 (ATCC, CRM-HTB-26; breast; mammary gland) were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, 11965092, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (GeminiBio, S11550). HCC1937 cells (CRL-2336; breast; duct; mammary gland) were grown in ATCC-formulated RPMI-1640 Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A1049101) supplemented with 10% FBS. SUM149PT cells (CVCL_3422, human female triple-negative breast cancer origin) were cultured using Ham’s F-12 Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific,11765054) supplemented with EGM-2 SingleQuots (Lonza, CC-4176), 10% FBS and 10 mM HEPES (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 15630080). All cell lines were purchased from ATCC, cultured under standard conditions at 37 °C and 5% CO2, and regularly tested for mycoplasma using the Universal Mycoplasma Detection kit (ATCC, 30-1012 K).

Cell transfection and transduction

U2OS, MDA-MB-436, hTERT-RPE and HCC1937 cells were transiently transfected with 40 nM siRNA using DharmaFECT 1 transfection reagent (Horizon Discovery Biosciences, 30-1012 K), and HEK293T and SUM149PT cells were transiently transfected with 20–25 nM siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 13778030) for 48 h, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. siRNAs were obtained from Horizon Discovery Biosciences or Qiagen (FUS, L-009497-00-0005; EWSR1, L-005119-02-0005; TAF15, L-008930-00-0005; RECQ1, L-013597-00-0005; BRCA1, L-003461-00-0005; BRCA2, SI02653434; RAD51, J-003530-11-0005, J-003530-12-0005; AllStars negative control, 1027281). Target protein depletion was confirmed by immunoblotting. GFP-tagged FUS-FRB and C-BLOCK overexpression plasmids were obtained from Dr. Chandra Tucker’s laboratory and used as a template to clone FUS full-length along with FUS truncation mutants into pEGFP-Flag-NLS (EV control). FUS-CHOP fusion overexpressing plasmid and the wild-type control were obtained from Addgene (21831 and 21828, respectively). Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, L3000015) was used for overexpression according to the manufacturer’s instructions. pEGFP-Flag-NLS and the derived pmCherry-Flag-NLS were used to make retroviral vectors to generate stable cell lines expressing mCherry or GFP, used in the two-color competition assay. Retroviral particles were produced using GP2-293 packaging cells (Takara Bio, 631458). GP2-293 cells were co-transfected with the retroviral transfer vector (pEGFP-Flag-NLS or pmCherry-Flag-NLS) and the envelope plasmid pVSV-G using polyethylenimine (PEI; 919012, Millipore Sigma). For lentiviral production, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with the lentiviral transfer vector, the packaging plasmid psPAX2, and the envelope plasmid pMD2.G using PEI. Viral supernatants were collected 72 h post-transfection and filtered through a 0.45 µm PVDF membrane. Target cells were transduced with viral supernatant supplemented with polybrene (8 µg/mL; TR-1003, Millipore Sigma). Stably transduced cells were selected with puromycin dihydrochloride (2 µg/mL; A1113803, Thermo Fisher Scientific). pDC20585 was used to induce BRCA1 re-expression in HCC1937 cells.

iPOND

iPOND, isolation of proteins on nascent DNA, combined with stable isotope labeling of amino acids in cell culture (SILAC) mass spectrometry was performed and analyzed as previously described36,38,59,86. Briefly, iPOND applies click chemistry to link biotin to the nucleoside analog 5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU), which is incorporated into newly synthesized DNA. Light and heavy isotope-labeled samples were mixed prior to purification of biotin-coupled DNA and associated proteins using streptavidin-coupled C1 magna beads (Dynabeads™ MyOne™ Streptavidin C1 Thermo Fisher Scientific, 65001).

Purified proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, and gel regions above and below the streptavidin band were excised. Gel regions were washed with 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate, and proteins were reduced with 4.5 mM DTT and treated with 10 mM iodoacetamide. Gel pieces were destained, and proteins were in-gel digested with trypsin as described previously36. After gel extracts were dried, peptides were reconstituted in 0.2% formic acid for analysis by LC-coupled tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS).

Peptides were loaded onto a C18 reverse phase analytical column using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 nanoLC and autosampler and were gradient-eluted at 350 nL/min using a 100 min gradient. The gradient was as follows: 1–77 min, 2–28 %B; 77–87 min, 28–40 %B; 87–92 min, 40-90 %B; 92–93 min, 90 %B; 93–94 min, 90–2 %B; 94–100 min, 2 %B. Peptides were analyzed using a data-dependent (top 20) method on an Orbitrap Exploris 240 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), equipped with a nanoelectrospray ionization source. The acquisition method consisted of MS1 with an AGC target of 3 × 106, followed by 20 MS/MS scan events with an AGC target of 1 × 105. The intensity threshold for MS/MS was 1 × 104. Dynamic exclusion was enabled with a 20-sec exclusion duration, and the HCD collision energy was 30 nce.

For peptide and protein identification, data were analyzed using MaxQuant, version 2.1.4.087. MS/MS spectra were searched against a human database created from the UniprotKB protein database. Precursor mass tolerance was 20 ppm for the first search and 4.5 ppm for the main search. Variable modifications included oxidation of methionine and acetylation of protein N-termini, and carbamidomethylation of cysteines was selected as a fixed modification. Enzyme specificity was set to Trypsin/P, and a maximum of 2 missed cleavages were allowed. The target-decoy false discovery rate (FDR) for peptide and protein identification was set to 1%. A multiplicity of 2 was selected, and Arg10 and Lys8 were selected as heavy labels. The requantify option was enabled. MaxQuant results were filtered to include proteins identified with at least 2 unique peptides. Protein groups identified as reverse hits were removed from the datasets. All reported protein groups were identified with two or more distinct peptides and were quantified with one or more ratio counts.

DNA combing assay

Cells were plated 24 h before treatment to reach 50-70% confluence. To label newly synthesized DNA, cells were first treated with 5-chloro-2’-deoxyuridine (CldU, 25 μM, 30 min) (Sigma-Aldrich, C6891) and then 5-iodo-2’-deoxyuridine (IdU, 100 μM, 30 min) (Sigma-Aldrich, I7125). As indicated, treatments with PARG inhibitor (PDD 00017273, Biotechne, 5952) and PARP inhibitor (Olaparib, Selleckchem, S1060), H2O2 (hydrogen peroxide, Millipore Sigma, H1009), HU (hydroxyurea, Millipore Sigma, H8627), and CPT (camptothecin, Millipore Sigma, C9911) were added. ~400,000 cells were embedded in 1.5% low-melting agarose plugs in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and digested overnight in 0.1% sarkosyl, proteinase K (2 mg/ml), and 50 mM EDTA (pH 8.0) at 50 °C. Plugs were washed in TE (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA), transferred to 100 mM MES (pH 5.7), melted at 68 °C, and digested with 1.5 U of β-Agarase I (New England Biolabs, M0392S) overnight at 42 °C. DNA was combed on silanized coverslips using a GenomicVision combing apparatus. The DNA was stained with antibodies recognizing IdU (BD, 347580) and CldU (Abcam, ab6326) for 1 h, washed in PBS, and probed with fluorescent secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488, A28175; Alexa Fluor 555, A21434) for 45 min. Images were obtained using the Axio Scan.Z1 (ZEN 3.1 blue edition, Zeiss). Images were manually analyzed for fiber length using Fiji.

DNA molecular combing with S1 nuclease

The DNA molecular combing assay was performed as described above. S1 nuclease (10 U, 2 h at 37 °C) was added to the agarose plugs after TE washes and transfer into 1 × S1 nuclease buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, EN0321), in the presence of protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 05892970001) to inhibit residual proteinase K. Plugs were washed in TE buffer once and then transferred to 100 mM MES (pH 5.7) before continuing the DNA molecular combing protocol.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were plated in a 96-well clear-bottom plate (Cellvis, P96-0-N) and treated as described, before pre-extraction with 0.4% Triton X-100 in cold PBS on ice for four min, followed by fixation with 4% formaldehyde solution (prepared from Millipore Sigma, F1635) for 15 min at room temperature, incubation for 1 h at room temperature with blocking buffer (5% goat serum, 0.1% triton X-100 in 1xPBS) and incubations with the primary antibodies for γH2AX (1:1000; JBW301, Millipore Sigma, 05-636) or Poly/Mono(ADP-ribose) (1:500, E6F6A, Cell Signaling Technology, 89190), which were performed at 4 °C overnight in blocking buffer. After incubation with fluorescently labeled secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 488, A28175, or A11008), nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma, D9542). Plates were imaged on a Molecular Devices ImageXpress Micro, and the nuclear integrated intensity was quantified using the MetaXpress Multi-Wavelength Cell Scoring software module or In Carta 2.8 (Molecular Devices). For immunofluorescent staining of FUS and PCNA, cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with primary antibodies: FUS (Bio-Techne, NBP2-52874 or Proteintech, 11570-1-AP) and PCNA (PC10, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, SC-56). After incubation, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488, A28175 or A11008; Alexa Fluor 594, A11012 or A11005; Alexa Fluor 647, A31571) were used, and nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma, D9542). Plates were imaged on an Operetta CLS Revvity using a 40x water-immersion objective, capturing 25 fields of view per well, with 5 Z-slices and a Z-step of 0.1 µm. Image analysis was performed using the Harmony 5.3 software. Maximum projection was used to merge Z-slices into a single image, and nuclei were identified using DAPI staining.

Proximity ligation assay (PLA)

PLA was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the Duolink PLA kit (Millipore Sigma, DUO92008), in situ PLA probe anti-mouse MINUS (Millipore Sigma, DUO92004), and in situ PLA probe anti-rabbit PLUS (Millipore Sigma, DUO92002). The protocol was used with the following additions. Cells were plated, pre-extracted and fixed as stated in the immunofluorescence method. Cells were labeled with 10 μM EdU for 30 min and, when indicated, treated with the drug of interest. Then the cells were blocked with 3% BSA and processed via click chemistry with biotin-azide. Biotin antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 5597) and FUS antibody (Bio-Techne, NBP2-52874) were used. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma, D9542). Plates were imaged on a Molecular Devices ImageXpress Micro, and the nuclear integrated intensity was quantified using the MetaXpress Multi-Wavelength Cell Scoring software module.

Immunoblotting

Whole-cell lysates were generated using lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 200 mM NaCl, 1% IGEPAL CA-630 (Millipore Sigma, I8896), 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.5), Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche, 05892970001), 25 U of Pierce Universal Nuclease (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88700), 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and 1 mM MgCl2. Whole-cell lysates were collected by scraping cells and incubating them on ice for 10 min in lysis buffer. Equal protein weights were calculated using the DC protein assay kit I (Bio-Rad, 5000111), separated using SDS-PAGE gel and immunoblotted on Amersham Hybond PVDF membrane (10600023). The membrane was blocked in 5% (w/v) non-fat milk in TBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (Millipore Sigma, P1379) for 1 h and incubated with the primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. For protein detection, the primary antibodies targeting the following proteins were used: FUS (Bio-Techne, NBP2-52874); EWSR1 (5H7, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-24791); TAF15 (7D71, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-47431); PRIMPOL (1:50, J. Mendez70); RAD51 (Abcam, ab213); GAPDH (1E6D9, Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig); γH2AX (JBW301, EMD Millipore, 05-636); ZRANB3 (Proteintech, 23111-1-AP); HLTF (EPR14761, Abcam, ab183042); SMARCAL1 (Bethyl Laboratories, A301-616A); BRCA1 (MS110, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA1-23164); BRCA2 (PSH07-55, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA5-60184); Poly/Mono(ADP-ribose) (E6F6A, Cell Signaling Technology, 89190); RECQ1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-27100); GFP (Abcam, ab13970). Membranes were then incubated with peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, anti-mouse, 31457, anti-rabbit, 31460) or StarBright Blue 700 Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (Bio-Rad, 12004158). The signal from the HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies was detected by SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34580). ChemiDoc MP (Bio-Rad) was used for detection and image acquisition with the Image Lab Software (version 3.0.14, Bio-Rad).

Two-color competition assay

Cells were transduced with retroviral vectors to generate stable cell lines expressing mCherry or GFP. These cells were mixed in a 1:1 ratio to seed on 96-well clear-bottom plates (Cellvis, P96-0-N) at a density of 3,000 cells per well for experiments. 48 h after transfection (Day or Time 0), cells were treated with PARG inhibitor or left untreated. The medium with and without the PARG inhibitor was refreshed every 2 days. Imaging, segmentation analyses, and counting of GFP- and mCherry-positive cells were performed using a Molecular Devices ImageXpress Micro and the MetaXpress Multi Wavelength Cell Scoring software application module. Alternatively, imaging and analysis were completed with an Agilent Cytation 5 instrument and Gen5 3.16 software. Each experiment was conducted in at least two biological replicates (independent siRNA transfections), and each condition was assessed in at least three technical replicates.

Viability assays

Viability assays were completed with alamarBlue HS Cell Viability Reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific, DAL1025). Readings were taken at 590 nm 1 h after adding the reagent to the cells. For long-term clonogenic survival, cells were seeded at low density and allowed to grow for two weeks prior to scoring with methylene blue staining (48% methanol, 2% methylene blue, 50% water). All clonogenic survival assays were completed in triplicate.

Electron microscopy

Electron microscopy experiments were performed as previously described88. DNA was cross-linked by incubating with 10 μg/mL 4,5′,8-trimethylpsoralen (MedChemExpress, HY-B1157), followed by a 3-min exposure to 366 nm UV light on a precooled metal block, for a total of three rounds. Cells were lysed, and genomic DNA was isolated from the nuclei by proteinase K digestion and chloroform-isoamyl alcohol extraction, followed by genomic DNA purification by isopropanol precipitation. The purified DNA was digested with PvuII HF in the appropriate buffer for 4 h at 37 °C. Samples were spread on a carbon-coated grid in the presence of benzyl-dimethyl-alkylammonium chloride, then subjected to platinum rotary shadowing. For imaging, a JEOL JEM-1400 electron microscope using a bottom-mounted AMT XR401 camera was used. Analysis was performed using ImageJ software (National Institute of Health). DNA secondary structures were scored according to the following analysis settings: fibers with a minimum thickness of 10 nm were counted as duplex DNA, fibers with reduced thickness (5-7 nm), as single-stranded DNA (ssDNA). The joining of three DNA fibers into a single junction, with two symmetrical daughter strands and a single parental strand, was counted as a canonical replication fork (three-way junction). Structures with four DNA fibers joined at a single junction, consisting of two symmetrical daughter strands, one parental strand and the addition of a typically shorter fourth strand, representative of the reversed arm, were counted as reversed replication forks (four-way junction). The length of the two daughter strands corresponding to the newly replicated duplex must be equal (b = c), whereas the length of the parental and regressed arms can vary (a ≠ b = c ≠ d). This allows us to distinguish reversed replication forks from canonical Holliday junction structures, as the latter will be characterized by arms of equal length (a = b, c = d). Moreover, the junction of the reversed replication fork must present a bubble structure, indicating that the junction is open and isn’t the result of the occasional crossover of two DNA molecules.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis for all data was completed using Prism 10 (GraphPad). The details of the individual tests are indicated in the respective figure legends. Statistical methods were not used to estimate sample size or to exclude/include data points or samples. Multiple approaches were used to verify the results of each experiment and ensure that off-target effects or clonal variation did not affect them. All experiments were performed at least three times unless otherwise noted in the figure legend. Graphs display all data points from all replicate experiments, along with the mean of each replicate. Statistical analyses were performed using experiment means, not individual data points, unless otherwise indicated in the figure legend.
