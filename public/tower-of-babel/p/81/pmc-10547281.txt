Recognizing the Struggles of Caregivers of In‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest Survivors and Paving the Way for Solutions
Authors: Elvira Dsouza
Published: 2023

Having played the role of a caregiver to a parent who experienced in‐hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA), the article titled “Disrupted lives: Caregivers' experiences of in‐hospital cardiac arrest survivors' recovery five years later” by Harrod et al. struck a chord. 1 As with any significant medical event, the survivor and their needs take precedence and caregivers usually only exist in the shadows despite the very critical role they play in influencing the health outcomes of the survivor. This study describes the experiences of caregivers for IHCA survivors and highlights the need for increased support and resources.

Becoming a caregiver, particularly in the case of IHCA, is usually not a planned event. My personal experience was more like suddenly being thrown into the deep end of the pool and figuring out how to survive. The experiences of the caregivers described in the study are truly disruptive to one's life, even though there is little time to think of them as such as one is going through the experience.

Post discharge, IHCA survivors often face ongoing physical and cognitive impairments and may require assistance in day‐to‐day activities. The study findings describe the increased responsibilities shouldered by caregivers, including driving, attending health care appointments, administering medications, and monitoring diet and exercise. In addition to these responsibilities, caregivers also act as emotional pillars, helping survivors navigate and manage their emotions as they go through the experience.

Caregiving for IHCA survivors comes with several challenges that disrupt not just the caregivers' daily activities but also their mental health and well‐being. Post‐traumatic stress disorder associated with witnessing the event, a constant fear of recurrence, anxiety, and depression are common experiences. As I think through my personal experience, the feeling of being alone through the experience predominated and as with most caregivers, I wished the experience came with a training manual that started with a “how to” on navigating the health care system.

Although the study has not specifically touched upon this, not all caregivers are equally impacted and social determinants of health influence the extent of the impact. Caregivers from households grappling with economic instability struggle with providing optimal levels of care as they must go back to work to make ends meet and cannot afford paid caregiving support. Employment opportunities offering flexibility to navigate caregiving responsibilities may also be limited. Consequently, caregivers are often mentally and physically overwhelmed. Caregivers without access to education find interactions with health care providers even more intimidating and may struggle with understanding care protocols or accessing resources that could benefit them. From my personal experience in patient advocacy, I have experienced first‐hand the helplessness experienced by caregivers in these situations. When access to quality health care is a challenge due to economic conditions, both the patient and the caregiver are impacted. Any solutions to address the unmet needs of caregivers should take into account the impact of various social determinants of health.

The findings of this study throw light on the disruption experienced by caregivers across 3 domains: their personal lives, the caregiver–patient relationship, and their well‐being. The study describes the strategies used by caregivers to manage disruptions across these domains. Resources that could help have also been described. Caregivers of IHCA survivors need support on an ongoing basis because it is difficult to put a time stamp on the recovery period.

The findings of this study call for the recognition of caregivers as important stakeholders and the development and implementation of comprehensive caregiver care programs targeting their physical, emotional, and informational needs. It is important for not just health care providers but also policy makers to participate in the development of these programs. Receiving the necessary support, resources, and training will not only improve the caregivers’ physical and emotional well‐being but will also help them provide better care to survivors. While health care systems are grappling with several challenges and caregivers may not be a top priority, engaging volunteer patient advocates in health care settings may be a good initiative to start to provide caregivers with some of the support they seek. Additional research on caregiving should aim to better understand and quantify the impact of social determinants of health. Research that aims to understand the unique caregiving challenges related to race, ethnicity, or cultural differences will help with developing more inclusive solutions.
