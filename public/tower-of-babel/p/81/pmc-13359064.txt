Bow hunter’s syndrome (BHS), also known as rotational vertebral artery (VA) occlusion, is a rare cause of vertebrobasilar insufficiency characterized by compression of the VA during movement of the head and neck. Although BHS was classically characterized as occurring during head rotation,1 a number of subsequent reports have described an atypical BHS that occurs during neck flexion or extension.2,3 This can lead to transient symptoms of vertebrobasilar insufficiency that are relieved with returning one’s head back to a neutral position.1 Although BHS is classically described at the atlanto-occipital level with head rotation, subaxial variants and nonrotational variants are also seen and may remain undiagnosed, as in our case. The pathology of BHS spans from compression from osteophytes, intervertebral disc, and fibrous bands to noncompressive vascular anomalies.4,5 Diagnosis of BHS can be achieved with CT or MR angiography; however, digital subtraction angiography (DSA) is considered the gold standard.6 This case illustrates a diagnostically challenging presentation of subaxial BHS, characterized by paradoxical VA reopening on extension in a patient with recurrent posterior circulation ischemic strokes. The unusual hemodynamic pattern made recognition difficult, but the case underscores the importance for neurosurgeons to consider dynamic VA compression when evaluating unexplained or recurrent posterior circulation ischemia.

Illustrative Case

The patient is a 54-year-old male with no relevant past medical history who initially presented to an outside hospital with the chief ­complaint of dizziness. On imaging the patient was found to have a right cerebellar stroke (Fig. 1A). The patient was started on dual antiplatelet therapy and discharged 3 days after admission. Two days after discharge the patient re-presented with a new visual field cut and left arm numbness. On follow-up imaging the patient was found to have a new right posterior cortical artery (PCA) stroke (Fig. 1B). Two days into this admission, the patient experienced a new right visual field cut and right arm weakness. Additional imaging showed a new left PCA stroke (Fig. 1C). The patient was then transferred to our center for care. Initial imaging at our center demonstrated acute areas of infarction within the left occipital, parietal, and mesial temporal lobes (Fig. 1D). Transthoracic echocardiography, malignancy screening, an anticoagulation panel, and transcranial Doppler evaluation for high-intensity signals in the PCAs were all negative. A dissection was mentioned as a possible diagnosis; however, no clear anatomical correlate was found. At this point, we went forward with obtaining a diagnostic angiogram (DSA), which demonstrated left VA origin occlusion with reconstitution proximally from the thyrocervical trunk and distally from the deep cervical artery (Fig. 2). The patient then underwent provocative testing, and on neck extension the left VA was reopened, notably not seen with axial rotation (Fig. 3). CTA of the neck also demonstrated a large ventrolaterally projecting osteophyte at the level of the left C6–7 transverse foramen with compression along the expected path of the left VA (Fig. 4A). At this point, BHS was deemed the diagnosis at the level of C6–7. The patient was placed in a cervical collar until surgery.

Three days after the DSA study, we performed direct decompression of the left VA at C6–7 and cervical fusion. Under general anesthesia, neuromonitoring was used throughout the procedure and a standard exposure for an anterior cervical discectomy and fusion was performed. Under the microscope the annulus was opened, and the osteophyte was drilled away to decompress the left VA. Intraoperative cone-beam CT was performed, demonstrating satisfactory decompression of the offending osteophyte (Fig. 4B). The head was then extended and the VA was heard on Doppler evaluation above the dissection point. The C6–7 vertebra was then fused with a titanium cage in a standard fashion due to preoperative listhesis and concern for possible postoperative instability (Fig. 5). At the 6-month follow-up, the patient remained clinically stable, with no further strokes, moderate resolution of his preoperative symptoms, and a modified Rankin Scale score of 1 due to memory difficulty. Discussion

Observations

The diagnosis of BHS remains a significant clinical challenge due to its rarity, heterogeneous etiologies, and the dynamic nature of VA compromise. The syndrome is characterized by position-dependent vertebrobasilar insufficiency, most commonly triggered by head rotation, yet clinical manifestations can be subtle, variable, and sometimes unrelated to neck movement, especially in cases with underlying vascular anomalies or dissection.5,7,8 In the presented case, posterior circulation infarctions prompted a standard vascular imaging workup, which at first failed to reveal BHS. A diagnosis of dissection was initially based on clinical and imaging findings; however, subsequent consideration of alternative mechanisms led to identification of an atypical, mechanical etiology for the patient’s recurrent strokes. In retrospect, careful review of the outside CT angiogram revealed subtle but appreciable VA compression that had initially gone unrecognized. It is important to note that in this case BHS led to recurrent mechanical compression and injury of the left VA, resulting in artery-to-artery embolic events rather than the more typical symptoms of vertebrobasilar insufficiency by flow reduction.

Dynamic imaging modalities, such as DSA, dynamic CTA, and Doppler ultrasonography with provocative maneuvers are essential for diagnosis, as they can directly demonstrate rotational VA occlusion and correlate with VA compromise.1,9–12 Current literature emphasizes that neutral position imaging may be misleading, as VA patency at neutral position does not exclude significant compromise during head movement.11,12 In this patient, the identification of an osseous complex as the culprit for dynamic VA compression was only possible through targeted dynamic studies, underscoring the necessity of such approaches in cases of repeat posterior circulation strokes in an otherwise healthy patient without typical risk factors.1,7,10,13,14

Medical management alone for recurrent strokes secondary to BHS is rarely effective7,9 Definitive treatment typically requires targeted surgical intervention to directly decompress the VA and stabilize the cervical spine, thereby preventing further ischemic events.1,9,10,15

The primary goal of surgical management in BHS is to alleviate dynamic mechanical compression of the VA. While a posterior approach may be preferred in cases of atlantoaxial compression,16 anterior approaches are more commonly utilized, particularly for subaxial lesions.17 Direct decompression of the VA is commonly cited as a preferred method.18–21 Some authors, however, advocate for cervical fusion alone to limit pathological motion and reduce surgical risk, accepting the associated loss of cervical mobility.14,22,23

Intraoperative DSA has been used to confirm VA patency following decompression, including during dynamic head movements.24,25 Concerns regarding the safety of intraoperative DSA in BHS have been largely refuted in recent reports.2 Indocyanine green angiography has also been described as a useful adjunct for intraoperative assessment of VA flow.25

In the present case, an anterior approach was selected to facilitate direct access to the uncinate process and associated osteophyte complex contributing to VA compression.26,27 Consistent with the existing literature, we believe that in cases of osseous compression, direct decompression provides the most durable long-term outcome.20 Given the patient’s preexisting cervical listhesis, and movement detected at the level intraoperatively, anterior cervical fusion was performed concurrently to maintain stability following decompression.

Lessons

We report a case of recurrent posterior territory infarcts in a patient who was otherwise healthy and had a negative hypercoagulability workup. BHS should be considered in patients with recurrent posterior circulation ischemic events when conventional vascular imaging fails to identify a clear embolic or hemodynamic source. This case demonstrates an underrecognized presentation of BHS where static imaging created a diagnostic pitfall, suggesting a fixed VA occlusion. The nonaxial location in additional to unexpected VA reopening with neck extension demonstrates an atypical presentation of BHS that may delay diagnosis.

This case emphasizes the danger of diagnostic anchoring in patients with recurrent posterior circulation infarctions. When conventional vascular imaging and laboratory evaluations fail to identify a clear embolic or dissection-related source, clinicians must maintain a broad differential and consider dynamic, mechanical causes of ischemia.

Additionally, surgical strategy should be individualized. Although direct decompression of the VA may be sufficient in select cases, the presence of segmental instability or listhesis may compromise the durability of decompression alone. In such situations, concomitant cervical fusion may be warranted to provide long-term protection against recurrent dynamic VA compression, while recognizing that fusion is not universally required in the management of BHS. Ultimately, a high index of suspicion, comprehensive dynamic evaluation, and prompt surgical management are essential to achieving favorable outcomes in this rare but potentially devastating cause of vertebrobasilar insufficiency.
