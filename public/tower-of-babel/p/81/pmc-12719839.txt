Advancing cardio‐obstetric care through digital health technologies: A narrative review
Authors: Toluwalase Awoyemi, Oluwaremilekun Zeth Tolu‐Akinnawo, Arun Mahtani, Inderbir Padda, Kayode Emmanuel Ogunniyi, Ikeoluwapo Kendra Bolakale‐Rufai, Abiola Olusanya, Adeleke Oluwaseun Dorcas, Chituru Believe Amarachi, Olumide Damilola Akinmoju, Christabel Ijeoma Uche‐Orji, Mina Fattah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular disease is a leading cause of maternal morbidity and mortality. As cardio‐obstetric care evolves, digital health technologies including telehealth, wearable devices and remote monitoring are playing an increasingly critical role. These innovations have the potential to enhance cardiovascular screening, risk assessment and disease management across the perinatal continuum. This article explores the role of digital health technologies in advancing cardio‐obstetrics care. It focuses on the impact of telehealth, wearable sensors and emerging digital tools on improving maternal cardiovascular outcomes and reducing disparities in care delivery. A narrative review approach was used to synthesize existing literature and clinical insights related to telecardiology, wearable monitoring and digital innovations. Emphasis was placed on applications in pregnancy and post‐partum care, as well as on evaluating implementation challenges and equity concerns. Digital health tools improve access to care and facilitate early diagnosis of cardiovascular conditions. Telehealth increases care continuity and patient satisfaction, especially in underserved populations. Wearable devices equipped with photoplethysmography and electrocardiogram sensors enable intermittent, non‐invasive monitoring, aiding early detection of arrhythmias and hypertensive disorders. Furthermore, novel technologies such as digital twins, natural language processing and virtual reality show potential to personalize care and support medical education. Despite these advancements, key barriers persist, including data privacy concerns, unequal access to technology and algorithmic bias. Digital health technologies are transforming cardio‐obstetrics care by enabling proactive management and expanding access. However, for these tools to deliver equitable benefits, targeted efforts are needed to address privacy, infrastructure and literacy challenges. Future efforts should focus on integrating digital health into routine maternal care, promoting digital literacy, ensuring equitable technology access and improving interoperability with electronic health records. Additionally, ongoing evaluation through clinical trials in high‐risk pregnancies and ethical safeguards to mitigate algorithmic bias will be essential to ensure safe, scalable and inclusive implementation of these innovations in maternal cardiovascular care.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease remains a leading cause of maternal morbidity and mortality globally, with many women still underdiagnosed, undertreated and underrepresented in clinical trials. 1 The field of cardio‐obstetrics has made strides in addressing the unique cardiovascular challenges women face during pregnancy and post‐partum, yet disparities, particularly among racial and ethnic minority groups, persist. 1 Advancements in digital health technologies and remote monitoring offer promising solutions to transform healthcare delivery and improve outcomes, with potential to address these disparities. However, the digital divide must be addressed to ensure equitable access across diverse populations. 1 Moreover, the cost‐effectiveness and real‐world impact of telehealth and digital health interventions in cardio‐obstetrics require further evaluation. 2

This article provides a detailed analysis of digital health technologies in cardio‐obstetrics, including remote monitoring, emerging technologies and future directions in leveraging digital health to enhance care. We will explore the potential of these innovations to improve cardiovascular screening, risk assessment and care for pregnant and post‐partum women, while addressing ethical considerations and implementation challenges.

Methods

This narrative review synthesizes current literature on digital health technologies in cardio‐obstetrics, with a focus on telehealth and remote monitoring to support cardiovascular health during pregnancy and the post‐partum period. The aim is to evaluate clinical outcomes and identify implementation challenges related to these interventions.

A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed and Scopus using combinations of the following keywords: “cardio‐obstetrics,” “telehealth,” “remote monitoring,” “digital health,” “pregnancy,” “cardiovascular health,” and “women's health.” The search was restricted to English‐language articles published between 2014 and 2025. Studies were eligible if they addressed digital health applications for cardiovascular care during pregnancy or the post‐partum period. Randomized clinical trials (RCTs) were prioritized, although observational studies and pilot interventions were also considered. Inclusion criteria were studies utilizing telehealth or remote monitoring technologies to manage cardiovascular health during pregnancy or post‐partum, reporting clinical outcomes, feasibility or implementation. Exclusion criteria included studies on general cardiovascular diseases not related to pregnancy or post‐partum care and those not involving digital health technologies or remote monitoring technologies.

The search yielded approximately 320 articles; 35 were screened in full text, and 11 studies met the inclusion criteria based on relevance, population and intervention type. A formal quality appraisal was not conducted due to the narrative synthesis approach and substantial heterogeneity in study design, intervention types, outcome measures and follow‐up periods. Instead, studies were included based on clinical relevance, methodological soundness and applicability to maternal cardiovascular care.

Data extraction was collectively performed by the reviewers, who read the eligible articles, collected information on study design, sample size, patient characteristics (e.g., gestational age, cardiovascular risk factors), types of digital health interventions (e.g., digital health consultations, remote monitoring devices) and outcomes (e.g., maternal and foetal health, patient satisfaction, healthcare access). Reviewers also identified key findings, limitations and future research recommendations.

The extracted data were synthesized qualitatively into thematic areas such as clinical outcomes, patient and healthcare provider perspectives and implementation barriers. A narrative synthesis approach was used to summarize trends and gaps in the literature, based on study rigour, sample size and relevance to the research question. No formal quality assessment tool was applied due to the narrative nature of the review. Digital health technologies in cardio‐obstetrics

Telehealth and remote monitoring in cardio‐obstetrics

Telehealth has emerged as a transformative tool in prenatal and post‐partum care, effectively expanding access, improving care efficiency and promoting continuity across diverse patient populations. It supports a wide range of services from routine monitoring and specialist consultations to behavioural interventions and remote patient management, helping bridge both geographic and socio‐economic disparities in maternal health. Particularly in prenatal care, these virtual care models have been associated with enhanced patient satisfaction and improved health outcomes, especially when designed to personalize care delivery and reduce the burden of frequent in‐person visits.

The Mayo Clinic's OB Nest model exemplifies this integration by offering a hybrid approach for low‐risk pregnancies that combines eight in‐person obstetrician visits with six virtual nurse‐led check‐ins. 14 , 15 This model empowers patients through home‐based monitoring tools, such as automated blood pressure (BP) monitors and foetal Dopplers, allowing them to collect and transmit health data from the comfort of their homes. Central to the OB Nest model is its emphasis on proactive communication and patient engagement, which was reflected in reports of higher patient satisfaction, reduced anxiety and increased confidence in self‐monitoring, without compromising maternal or foetal outcomes. 14 However, its utility remains confined to low‐risk populations, limiting its broader application in managing high‐risk pregnancies that necessitate more intensive clinical oversight and frequent in‐person evaluations.

Virtual care has proven effective in supporting long‐term maternal health in the post‐partum period. Albright et al. (2014) demonstrated that a year‐long intervention combining personalized telephone calls and an interactive website significantly increased moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity (MVPA) from 44 to 246 min/week, compared to a modest increase in the control group (46–156 min, P = 0.027). 3 The intervention was particularly effective for mothers with two or more children (P = 0.016), but accelerometer‐measured MVPA did not show significant differences between the standard and intervention groups. 3 Notably, the intervention was more effective among women with low baseline physical activity. However, interpretation of these findings should consider several limitations, including the high educational attainment of the sample, potential bias due to differential attrition and the absence of long‐term follow‐up to assess sustained behaviour change.

Bijlholtlh et al. evaluated the INTER‐ACT intervention, a combination of a smartphone app and four virtual coaching sessions, which led to improvements in dietary behaviours such as better control over eating habits and reduced energy intake (−69 kcal, P = 0.01). 4 However, these changes were not sustained at follow‐up, and no significant effects on physical activity or sedentary behaviour were observed. Engagement with the app varied, with greater usage observed among participants with higher educational attainment and those from single‐parent households. This pattern suggests that increased engagement may be associated with improved outcomes. However, variability in adherence and potential biases, including underreporting of energy intake, may limit the generalizability of these findings.

In addition to lifestyle interventions, remote monitoring technologies like home BP monitors and foetal Dopplers have become crucial tools in reshaping prenatal care. These tools enable regular, accurate tracking of maternal and foetal health, supporting timely detection of complications, which is particularly vital for managing high‐risk pregnancies and hypertensive disorders. They are especially valuable for underserved or geographically isolated populations, where frequent in‐person care is limited, thus enhancing access to essential healthcare. 14

Remote care platforms have also been pivotal in supporting post‐partum weight management, a key factor in long‐term maternal cardiovascular health. One culturally tailored programme targeting low‐income, overweight and obese mothers utilized a 16‐week curriculum delivered via theory‐based, culturally sensitive videos. These videos addressed stress, nutrition and physical activity and were supplemented with virtual group discussions and motivational coaching sessions. 16 Participants in this programme demonstrated meaningful improvements in lifestyle behaviours, highlighting the potential of virtual interventions in reducing cardiovascular risk during the post‐partum period. 5 , 16

Herring et al. further examined the effectiveness of technology‐supported behavioural weight loss programmes in socio‐economically disadvantaged, urban, ethnic minority mothers during the first post‐partum year. 6 In their study, 18 women were randomized to receive either standard care or a tech‐enabled intervention incorporating personalized feedback through daily self‐monitoring texts, biweekly coaching calls and support via a private Facebook group. The intervention group achieved significantly greater weight loss after 14 weeks, demonstrating the capacity of remote health strategies to deliver impactful, scalable solutions for post‐partum obesity and cardiovascular risk reduction in high‐need populations. 6

Expanding on earlier work, Herring et al. tested a digital intervention among Black women from early pregnancy to 6 months post‐partum. 7 At 6 months, women in the intervention group were significantly more likely to return to or remain below their early pregnancy weight compared to those receiving usual care (56% vs. 29%). 7 However, this difference was not sustained at 12 months (41% vs. 38%), highlighting the importance of ongoing engagement, self‐monitoring and evidence‐based behavioural goals supported through phone coaching and Facebook‐based peer support in the early post‐partum period. 7 Importantly, the continuity of remote contact with health coaches helped reinforce accountability and motivation, ultimately reducing obesity‐related complications and enhancing maternal cardiovascular health. 7

Structured remote interventions, particularly those combining telephone support with text messaging, have emerged as vital components of virtual cardio‐obstetrics care. Holmes et al. evaluated a programme in which 29 women received a 1‐h virtual education session followed by ongoing support via phone and text messages, compared to 31 control patients. 8 After 6 months, participants in the intervention group achieved significantly greater weight loss, underscoring the scalability of remote behavioural strategies to prevent type 2 diabetes mellitus, especially among women with a history of gestational diabetes mellitus. 8

Similarly, telephone‐based health coaching has become a central pillar of post‐partum remote care models. Horwitz et al. assessed a 5‐month coaching intervention delivered to low‐income Latina women at elevated risk for diabetes, with sessions offered in both English and Spanish. 9 While the intervention was culturally inclusive in design, results indicated that Spanish‐speaking participants did not experience the same weight loss benefits as their English‐speaking peers. These findings point to the need for deeper cultural and linguistic tailoring of virtual interventions to avoid reinforcing disparities in health outcomes across diverse populations. 9

In parallel, web‐based platforms have also played a prominent role in promoting post‐partum health behaviours. Kim et al. studied a 13‐week online intervention in which post‐partum women received educational content, daily motivational messages and pedometers, along with access to an interactive forum. 17 Weekly step data uploads allowed for personalized goal‐setting and visual feedback on progress. While the intervention group showed modest improvements in weight and insulin resistance relative to controls, these changes were not statistically significant. 17 Nonetheless, the findings illustrate the potential of digital platforms to promote physical activity and behavioural engagement during a critical period for long‐term maternal health.

Digital health interventions in cardio‐obstetrics have been associated with improved greater patient engagement and high levels of satisfaction. Beyond facilitating healthier lifestyle behaviours, these platforms have significantly improved post‐partum follow‐up, particularly in the management of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. For example, remote follow‐up visits achieved a 70% completion rate compared to just 32% for traditional in‐person appointments, with the most notable improvements observed among Black women. 1 This highlights the capacity of virtual care models to address structural barriers, reduce racial disparities in post‐partum outcomes and advance equity in maternal healthcare delivery.

From the perspective of patient experience, remote care has consistently demonstrated high acceptability and satisfaction. In prenatal settings, patients report that virtual consultations offer comparable benefits to in‐person visits, particularly in terms of convenience and accessibility. 14 Contributing factors such as decreased travel burden, increased frequency of contact for timely monitoring and more adaptable scheduling have made these models not only effective but, in many cases, the preferred option for prenatal and post‐partum care. 15 These findings collectively reinforce the potential of virtual care to transform cardio‐obstetrics practice by enhancing access, continuity and equity across diverse populations.

Remote monitoring using digital and mobile devices

Wearable devices with photoplethysmography (PPG) and electrocardiogram (ECG) sensors enable continuous, non‐invasive cardiovascular monitoring during pregnancy, when physiological changes elevate cardiac risk. 18 ECG‐based wearables, chest straps, patches and smartwatches offer clinically accurate arrhythmia detection in user‐friendly formats. In non‐pregnant populations, smartwatch algorithms have demonstrated high positive predictive values (84%–99%) for atrial fibrillation, underscoring their diagnostic potential in ambulatory care. 18 While many pregnancy‐related arrhythmias are benign, some may unmask or exacerbate underlying conditions, increasing the risk of heart failure, thromboembolism and foetal compromise through impaired placental perfusion. 19

In the post‐partum period, remote monitoring is equally vital, particularly for managing hypertension, a leading cause of maternal morbidity. Home BP monitoring enables superior risk stratification compared to office‐based readings and is more closely linked to target organ damage. 20 , 21 When paired with antihypertensive self‐titration, home monitoring significantly improves BP control and reduces cardiovascular risk. 22 , 23 These strategies are especially beneficial following pre‐eclampsia or gestational hypertension 24 , 25 , 26 and reduce the need for frequent clinic visits during early recovery.

Cairns et al. assessed a telemonitoring protocol adapted from the TASMIN trials for post‐partum hypertension. Participants used home BP monitors with medication titration every 5 days under virtual clinician oversight. At 6 weeks, the intervention group showed significantly lower BP (mean systolic/diastolic: 121.6/80.5 mmHg) compared to controls (126.6/86.0 mmHg), with adjusted differences of −5.2/−5.8 mmHg. Diastolic BP remained significantly lower at 6 months (−4.5 mmHg), demonstrating the effectiveness of remote self‐management for post‐partum hypertension. 10

Remote monitoring plays a crucial role in chronic disease prevention among post‐partum women. In a study by Davenport et al., sedentary participants with elevated body mass index (BMI) or post‐partum weight retention enrolled in a supervised walking and nutrition programme. The intervention, which included heart rate monitors, pedometers and dietary support, led to greater weight loss in both the low‐ and moderate‐intensity groups (−4.2 and −5.0 kg, respectively) compared to controls (−0.1 kg, P < 0.01). Additionally, participants in the intervention groups experienced reductions in low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol (−0.29 and −0.28 vs. +0.03 mmol/L, P = 0.015), improvements in glucose levels and increased adiponectin, while the control group showed no such changes. 11

Similarly, mobile‐based behaviour change strategies offer scalable solutions for post‐partum health management. Fjeldsoe et al. evaluated the ‘Mobile Mums’ intervention, a 13‐week programme using tailored text messages to encourage physical activity among mothers of young children. The study found that the intervention was feasible, acceptable and initially effective, with participants reporting significant increases in MVPA duration (48.5 min/week, 95% CI [13.4, 82.9]) and frequency (1.6 days/week, 95% CI [0.6, 2.6]). However, the benefits were not sustained at 6 months, and no improvements were observed in accelerometer‐based MVPA. This pattern reflects a common trend in digital health interventions: initial gains that diminish without continued reinforcement, highlighting the need for long‐term engagement strategies to maintain impact. 12

Digital tools show promise in managing post‐partum weight retention, a significant cardiometabolic risk factor. In the E‐Mom trial, 13 a smartphone‐based intervention with personalized coaching and remote monitoring led to significant increases in self‐reported MVPA (48.5 min/week, 95% CI [13.4, 82.9]; 1.6 days/week, 95% CI [0.6, 2.6]). However, no significant changes in body weight, fat percentage or waist‐to‐hip ratio were observed compared to the usual‐care group. Post hoc analyses revealed a dose–response effect, with participants showing high adherence (>70%) achieving significant reductions in weight (−3.6 ± 1.6 vs. +1.8 ± 0.9 kg; P = 0.005) and body fat (−2.5% ± 1.0% vs. +1.7% ± 0.6%; P = 0.001) compared to controls. These findings highlight the importance of sustained engagement in digital health interventions to fully realize their benefits in the post‐partum period. 13

The integration of AI into wearable technology continues to advance, particularly in the development of AI‐enabled photoplethysmogram (PPG)‐based devices. These devices estimate BP non‐invasively by measuring pulse transit or arrival time, offering continuous, cuff‐less monitoring that may enhance early detection and management of hypertensive disorders like pre‐eclampsia, ultimately improving maternal–foetal outcomes. 18 , 27 In addition to their clinical utility, wearables provide patients with real‐time feedback on physical activity, heart rate variability, sleep and cardiovascular trends, encouraging behaviour change and adherence to health goals. 27

Despite these promising benefits, the uptake of wearable devices remains limited. Fewer than 25% of individuals at risk for cardiovascular disease report using wearable devices, and only half of users engage with them daily. 27 Disparities in access, affordability and health literacy contribute to lower usage among low‐income, older and minority populations. Furthermore, most current devices offer only intermittent rather than continuous monitoring. To fully maximize the potential of wearable technologies in cardio‐obstetrics, equity‐focused strategies and policy interventions will be necessary to ensure broader and more consistent utilization. VR training in cardio‐obstetrics enables providers to practice rare, high‐risk pregnancy emergencies in immersive simulations. Institutions like the University of Alabama at Birmingham and NYC Health + Hospitals use VR modules to train OB/GYN residents in managing scenarios such as maternal cardiac arrest and hypertensive crises. 35 , 36

Emerging digital health technologies and future directions

Digital twins and personalized simulations

Digital twins and personalized simulations are rapidly transforming the landscape of cardio‐obstetrics by providing dynamic, individualized models of cardiovascular physiology during pregnancy. 39 A digital twin is a high‐fidelity virtual representation of a specific patient's anatomical and physiological profile. In pregnancy, this technology enables real‐time modelling of maternal cardiovascular adaptations such as increased blood volume, cardiac output and heart rate, as well as vascular and myocardial remodelling, thereby offering a deeper understanding of gestational haemodynamics.

These models are not static; they evolve alongside the patient, continuously updated with data from wearable biosensors, electronic health records (EHRs) and routine clinical assessments. 40 As living models, digital twins can simulate clinical scenarios to anticipate individual risks. For instance, they may predict the haemodynamic impact of pre‐eclampsia, assess decompensation risk in congenital heart disease or visualize ventricular strain due to volume overload, providing early insight into potential adverse events.

Beyond prediction, digital twins offer precision in therapeutic planning. Simulations allow clinicians to test the maternal–foetal impact of specific medications, delivery methods or anaesthetic choices before implementation. 40 This is particularly valuable in high‐risk pregnancies such as those complicated by pulmonary hypertension or prior peripartum cardiomyopathy where physiological reserve is limited. Digital twins can forecast responses to labour and delivery stressors, aiding in multidisciplinary decisions on delivery timing and mode. In the post‐partum period, they may help model the acute cardiovascular shifts that predispose patients to decompensation, supporting early identification and intervention for conditions like heart failure or thromboembolism. 40

The integration of digital twins with AI‐powered wearable technologies and remote monitoring tools represents a frontier in individualized cardio‐obstetrics care. Together, these innovations may enable continuous, proactive risk assessment and tailored intervention, marking a paradigm shift towards predictive and preventative maternal cardiovascular medicine.

Augmented reality (AR) and VR in medical education

In parallel with the clinical advances offered by digital twin technology, AR and VR are revolutionizing education and training in cardio‐obstetrics. VR platforms create immersive, three‐dimensional environments that allow trainees to explore intricate cardiovascular anatomy and simulate dynamic physiological adaptations of pregnancy, including plasma volume expansion, increased stroke volume and reduced systemic vascular resistance. 41 These experiential tools offer a deeper understanding of maternal cardiovascular physiology than traditional two‐dimensional resources.

Beyond static visualization, VR‐based simulations allow clinicians to manage virtual patients in complex cardio‐obstetrics scenarios, including high‐risk events such as peripartum cardiomyopathy, myocardial infarction or acute aortic dissection. 42 These simulations strengthen diagnostic reasoning and decision‐making in a low‐risk environment and permit repetitive practice of time‐sensitive interventions. Importantly, VR systems can tailor scenario difficulty to individual learners, supporting stepwise competency development across training levels.

AR complements these platforms by integrating instructional overlays into real‐world clinical tasks. For example, AR can enhance procedural training by guiding probe positioning and identifying optimal echocardiographic windows during cardiac assessments in pregnant patients. 41 This real‐time feedback improves precision and confidence. Moreover, both VR and AR support team‐based training by simulating cardio‐obstetric emergencies requiring interdisciplinary collaboration among obstetricians, cardiologists, anaesthesiologists and nurses. These coordinated simulations foster clear communication, define team roles and enhance readiness for high‐stakes maternal cardiovascular events, ultimately advancing both provider preparedness and patient safety.

Ethical considerations and challenges

Data privacy and security

As digital innovations such as digital twins, wearable monitors and mobile health platforms become increasingly integrated into cardio‐obstetrics care, ensuring the security and privacy of patient data is paramount. Pregnancy‐related cardiovascular information is particularly sensitive, often encompassing reproductive history, biometric trends, genetic profiles and behavioural data. The potential for misuse or discrimination underscores the critical need for stringent data governance and privacy safeguards. 43 , 44

Best practices begin with data minimization, collecting only clinically necessary information and extend to the deployment of robust encryption protocols for data both at rest and in transit. Role‐based access control systems should ensure that only authorized personnel can access or modify patient records, and compliance with established regulatory frameworks, such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), remains foundational to legal and ethical use. 45 , 46

However, the integration of cloud‐based platforms and remote diagnostics introduces added complexity, including concerns around data residency, cross‐border transfer and cybersecurity vulnerabilities. Advanced safeguards such as multi‐factor authentication, secure APIs and blockchain‐enabled audit trails can strengthen system transparency and resilience. 47 , 48 , 49 Given the high stakes involved, tailored data protection guidelines specific to cardio‐obstetrics are warranted to address the unique privacy risks in this domain.

In parallel, investment in advanced cybersecurity infrastructure such as real‐time threat detection systems and encrypted communication networks is essential to protecting the integrity of digital health systems. 48 Just as crucial is fostering patient awareness regarding data consent, access rights and the implications of digital health engagement. Transparent communication empowers patients to make informed decisions about their care and builds trust in digital tools that aim to improve maternal cardiovascular outcomes.

Equity and access

Integration of digital technologies risks amplifying existing healthcare disparities if equitable access is not prioritized. Socio‐economically disadvantaged, rural and minority populations often face structural barriers, including limited access to smartphones, broadband internet or healthcare facilities equipped with digital infrastructure that impede their engagement with digital health tools. 50 These challenges are compounded by disparities in digital literacy, particularly among older adults, individuals with lower educational attainment and non‐English‐speaking patients, many of whom may struggle to navigate telehealth platforms or interpret health data from remote monitoring devices. 50

Such inequities undermine the promise of digital innovation to improve maternal cardiovascular outcomes and may disproportionately affect communities already burdened by higher rates of maternal morbidity and mortality. To counteract these risks, healthcare systems and policymakers must adopt a multipronged approach that extends beyond technology deployment. Infrastructure investments should support broadband expansion and the provision of mobile devices to underserved populations, while digital literacy initiatives can empower patients to participate more fully in their care.

Crucially, the design of digital tools must prioritize inclusivity, featuring multilingual functionality, culturally tailored content and user‐friendly interfaces that accommodate a range of literacy and health literacy levels. 51 Partnerships between academic centres and community hospitals can further democratize access by facilitating knowledge exchange and resource sharing. Additionally, ensuring that datasets used to develop predictive algorithms are demographically representative will help avoid reinforcing systemic biases and improve the relevance of clinical decision‐making tools across diverse populations. 52 , 53

To ensure that no patient is left behind, offering low‐tech or hybrid solutions such as telephone‐based follow‐up or paper‐based monitoring options remains essential. By embedding equity into the fabric of digital health adoption, cardio‐obstetrics care can advance in ways that are both technologically sophisticated and socially just, narrowing gaps in maternal cardiovascular outcomes for historically marginalized communities. 54

Integration into clinical workflows

The integration of digital health tools into clinical practice is contingent on overcoming a host of operational and systemic barriers. One major challenge lies in the uneven availability of technological infrastructure. Resource‐limited and rural healthcare settings often lack reliable high‐speed internet, modern EHR systems and device interoperability elements that are essential for real‐time data transmission, remote monitoring and coordinated care. 55 Without these foundational capabilities, the benefits of digital innovation risk being inequitably distributed, further entrenching geographic disparities in maternal cardiovascular outcomes.

Even in well‐resourced settings, the lack of seamless integration between digital tools and existing EHR systems can lead to fragmented care, redundant documentation and workflow inefficiencies. Clinicians may face additional administrative burdens or difficulty accessing consolidated patient data, diminishing the very efficiencies that digital platforms aim to deliver. Moreover, clinical inertia, whether due to unfamiliarity with emerging technologies, perceived disruptions to workflow or scepticism about the clinical utility of digital interventions, remains a formidable barrier to widespread adoption. 55 , 56 , 57

Regulatory uncertainty and the high upfront costs associated with procurement, cybersecurity and ongoing maintenance further complicate implementation. To mitigate these barriers, healthcare systems must invest in structured training programmes that equip cardio‐obstetrics care teams with not only the technical skills to operate digital platforms but also the ethical, communicative and clinical frameworks necessary for effective virtual care delivery. These programmes should also address the nuances of remote diagnostics, digital documentation and patient engagement in the context of cardio‐obstetrics care. 58

Additionally, digital integration should not be an afterthought but a deliberate redesign of clinical workflows. Conducting comprehensive pathway analyses can identify optimal entry points for digital augmentation. Pilot initiatives and iterative co‐design processes incorporating feedback from clinicians, nurses and patients can ensure that new technologies align with real‐world practice and address frontline needs. Such collaborative approaches promote user ownership, enhance tool usability and ultimately support the sustained and meaningful integration of digital health into cardio‐obstetrics care.

Quality assurance and validation

Ensuring the safe, effective and equitable implementation of digital health technologies in cardio‐obstetrics requires robust‐quality assurance protocols and evidence‐based validation frameworks. As these tools become more embedded in maternal cardiovascular care, patient safety, clinical efficacy and ethical use must be upheld through clear regulatory standards that address device performance, interoperability, privacy and transparency. 59 , 60 These standards serve not only as a safeguard for patients but also as a foundation for long‐term clinical trust and sustainability.

Clinical validation efforts must move beyond early‐phase feasibility studies to include large‐scale, multicentre trials that assess digital health tools against established clinical benchmarks. Such trials should reflect the diversity of real‐world patient populations, including those historically underrepresented in research to ensure generalizability and to address known disparities in cardio‐obstetrics outcomes. 61 , 62 Furthermore, validation frameworks must incorporate post‐market surveillance mechanisms to detect rare or long‐term adverse events and ensure continued alignment with evolving clinical practice.

User experience is also a central determinant of sustained adoption. Technologies must be intuitive for both patients and providers, with user‐centred design principles that prioritize accessibility, ease of navigation and integration into clinical workflows. Features such as automated data capture, real‐time alerts and customizable dashboards can enhance usability and minimize provider burden, increasing the likelihood of routine use in care delivery. 63

Continuous monitoring of tool performance across metrics like clinical outcomes, adherence rates and satisfaction is essential. Built‐in feedback loops that engage developers, clinicians and patients allow for iterative refinement and adaptation. This ensures that algorithms remain accurate and relevant and that interfaces evolve to meet changing clinical and patient needs. Equally important is the inclusion of robust cybersecurity and ethical data governance frameworks to mitigate risks related to data breaches, bias in predictive models and algorithmic opacity.

Finally, the creation of collaborative, multidisciplinary networks spanning academia, healthcare institutions and technology developers can support knowledge exchange, foster innovation and harmonize standards across the field. By embedding quality, safety and equity into the design and deployment of digital health tools, the cardio‐obstetrics field can fully realize the potential of digital transformation while preserving patient trust and clinical integrity.

Cost‐effectiveness and scalability

Evaluating the economic impact of digital health interventions is critical for informing policy decisions and justifying widespread integration into cardio‐obstetrics care. Rigorous cost‐effectiveness analysis, return on investment (ROI) models and utilization studies provide essential data on the financial sustainability of digital tools compared to traditional care pathways. These evaluations help quantify potential savings from reduced maternal complications, hospitalizations and emergency visits, particularly through the early detection and management of cardiovascular risk. For example, remote patient monitoring programmes may prevent cardiovascular decompensation, while telehealth services can reduce travel burdens and administrative overhead, expanding access to specialized care in resource‐limited settings. 64 , 65

To achieve scalability, digital health solutions must be interoperable with existing EHR systems and adaptable to diverse infrastructure levels. This technological flexibility must be matched by organizational readiness, including robust training programmes and continuous technical support for healthcare providers to ensure confident and competent adoption. In parallel, adaptive regulatory frameworks are needed to streamline technology approval, clarify reimbursement policies and address legal considerations without stifling innovation.

Strategic collaboration across stakeholders' healthcare institutions, technology developers, payers and community organizations is vital for building supportive ecosystems that can sustain and expand digital health initiatives. Patient engagement is equally important; co‐designing platforms with end‐users, providing ongoing support and establishing patient advisory councils can foster trust, improve usability and ensure that interventions are responsive to the lived experiences of pregnant and post‐partum patients.

As digital tools scale, maintaining quality assurance is paramount. Regular audits of performance metrics, real‐world outcome evaluations and continuous improvement mechanisms guided by both clinician input and patient‐reported outcomes can drive optimization and foster long‐term value. By aligning economic viability with clinical impact and equitable design, digital health technologies can catalyse a paradigm shift in cardio‐obstetrics, offering scalable, cost‐effective solutions that advance maternal cardiovascular care across diverse populations.

The studies included in this review exhibit substantial heterogeneity in design, sample characteristics and outcome measures, which limit the strength and generalizability of their findings. Many relied on self‐reported data, small sample sizes and single‐centre recruitment, introducing potential biases and reducing external validity. Additionally, the absence of standardized outcome measures and limited longitudinal follow‐up in several studies hinders the ability to assess the long‐term efficacy, safety and sustainability of digital interventions in cardio‐obstetrics. These methodological limitations contribute to variability in reported outcomes, particularly across diverse populations. There is also a potential for publication bias, as studies with favourable digital health outcomes are more likely to be published, potentially skewing the available evidence. Moreover, heterogeneity in study populations, intervention modalities (e.g., mobile health applications vs. remote physiologic monitoring) and clinical endpoints complicate the synthesis of findings and precludes the feasibility of formal meta‐analytic approaches.

Digital health technologies hold significant promise in transforming cardio‐obstetrics care by facilitating proactive management and improving access, particularly in resource‐constrained settings. However, to ensure these tools deliver equitable and sustainable benefits, future research must prioritize key areas. Well‐designed clinical trials are needed to evaluate the safety, efficacy and cost‐effectiveness of digital interventions in high‐risk pregnancies and post‐partum populations. Strategies to promote digital equity such as improving broadband access, addressing device affordability and advancing culturally tailored interventions are essential to avoid widening existing disparities. In parallel, efforts should focus on seamless integration of digital tools with EHR systems to enhance clinical workflows and enable real‐time data sharing. Addressing challenges related to data privacy, infrastructure and digital literacy will be critical. With thoughtful implementation and a strong evidence base, digital innovation can serve as a catalyst for improving maternal cardiovascular outcomes on a population level.
