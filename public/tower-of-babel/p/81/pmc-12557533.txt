Broadband topological transitions in twisted elastodynamic metasurfaces
Authors: Simon Yves, Yu-Gui Peng, Andrea Alù
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceWe extend the concept of twistronics to elastodynamic metasurfaces with broken symmetries, unlocking exquisite control over phonon fields. Notably, we predict and experimentally demonstrate broadband, reconfigurable, and robust topological transitions for flexural waves, modifying their dispersion topology from elliptic to hyperbolic by twisting a pair of identical elastic metasurfaces. These effects result in broadband and robust control over mechanical fields, opening a pathway from our macroscopic implementations to their transposition to chip scales. The generality of our approach, which allows to probe twistronics phenomena with classical waves, finds practical applications in domains where the precise engineering of phonon fields is crucial, such as for surface acoustic wave technology, ultrasound sensing, and microfluidics.

Symmetries play a fundamental role in the broad realm of physics. This is particularly relevant in the context of wave propagation, for which the underlying symmetries of the background medium are inherently linked to the wave features. This interplay is described by effective material constitutive tensors, whose elements account for the medium response to wave excitation along different directions in space. When plugged into the dispersion relation, these tensor components dictate the shape of the isofrequency contours (in 2D, surfaces in 3D), which predict the spatial properties of wave motion in the medium, at the given frequency. Rooted in their symmetries, these contours can exhibit different topologies as a function of the relative sign of the tensor components: The more common closed topology arises in materials for which the constitutive tensor elements have the same sign along all directions, while an open topology can emerge when some tensor elements have an opposite sign (1). These two categories correspond to strongly different wave behaviors in terms of directionality and localization features, which can be directly probed in real space. For instance, an open isofrequency contour implies that wave propagation is forbidden in some regions of space. Therefore, the ability to precisely tailor the isofrequency topology of a medium opens opportunities for advanced wave manipulation, which is pertinent for emerging, multiscale technologies in the fields of telecommunications, imaging, and sensing. In this context, artificial metamaterials and metasurfaces have become a powerful and versatile platform to achieve control over these broken symmetries, the resulting constitutive parameters and hence the dispersion topology, both in photonics (2) and phononics (3–5).

Recent advances in twistronics (6) have highlighted the major role played by the rotation degree-of-freedom in achieving reconfigurable control over a material response, leading to a plethora of phenomena, such as magic-angle superconductivity (7), moiré valley-excitons (8), and topological domain walls (9). This twist-driven symmetry engineering within multilayer structures has been rapidly transposed to the photonic and phononic wave domains, leading to tunable features linked to flat bands (10–18) and moiré patterns (19–21), topological pumps in synthetic dimensions (22) and higher-order topological modes (23), as well as artificial gauge fields for anomalous refraction (24, 25).

Here, we combine these concepts and demonstrate total control over isofrequency topological classes in the elastodynamic domain through rotations. We harness the interplay of topology and the twist degree-of-freedom to achieve extreme elastic wave manipulation. Notably, we demonstrate isotropic, elliptic, flat, and hyperbolic contours over broad frequency ranges within a 2D elastodynamic metasurface. Furthermore, working in the hyperbolic regime, we engineer an isofrequency topological transition in a twisted metasurface bilayer, leading to the elastodynamic analog of photonic magic-angles (26, 27). This topological transition, analogous to a Lifshitz transition in electronics (28), occurs over a wide spectral range, hereby providing tunable broadband control of the directionality and enhancement of the displacement field, which we showcase both with frequency-domain and time-domain experiments. Finally, we harness the interplay between the excitation chirality and the twist-reconfigurable isofrequency topology of the metasurface bilayer to demonstrate the control over directionality due to spin-momentum locking. These results show that twistelastics, namely leveraging rotations to manipulate the symmetries of a phononic medium, provides an exquisite control over elastic waves, which, upon miniaturization, may find applications in the design of robust, reconfigurable phononic devices, of relevance for microelectronics, haptics, ultrasound sensors, and microfluidics.

Results

The field of metamaterials has popularized the use of resonances as a powerful tool to control the way a medium responds to an impinging wave field (29). Confined resonances in tailored arrays, due to their enhanced and frequency-dependent response, enable effective constitutive parameters very different from the constituent materials, which may be strongly positive or negative depending on whether the operating frequency is below or above the resonance frequency, respectively. These features, combined with spatial asymmetries, have been used to implement hyperbolic metasurfaces for sound (30, 31) and elastic waves (32–37). Here, we study flexural waves propagating within a thin plate, decorated with a square lattice of pillars with rectangular cross-section, entirely made of the same PLA-based plastic (Fig. 1A, see Methods for geometrical and material details). Similar pillared metasurfaces are ubiquitous in phononics and make an interesting tabletop platform to investigate wave phenomena across a variety of scales, ranging from seismic waves to on-chip surface acoustic waves (SAW) (38). In our geometry, due to the asymmetry in the plane of the plate linked to the rectangular cross-section of the pillars, the bending resonance along the x and y directions is detuned, hereby yielding broadband strongly anisotropic effective material properties. where uz is the out-of-plane displacement, D is the plate flexibility, h the plate thickness, ρ the plate mass density, and ω the operating pulsation. The quantities τ1,2 encompass the resonant behavior of the two detuned bending modes of the pillars, as shown in Fig. 1B. A first look at the dispersion relation shows that the specific properties of flexural waves forbid the existence of purely open contours due to the bi-Laplacian term that acts as a nonlocal effect (SI Appendix, section II and Fig. S1 for more details). It also prevents a direct classification of the isofrequency topology solely based on the relative signs of the real parts of the material tensor components, as it is done in optics (39). However, even for wave domains that are free of this bi-Laplacian term, such as acoustic or electromagnetic waves, the asymptotic behavior of open contours is always limited by either spatial nonlocality or dissipation (39), hence ensuring the physical relevance of the model. As a matter of fact, dissipation within our pillared metasurface overdamps the large wavevectors for which nonlocal effects dominate, effectively resulting in a hyperbolic behavior. This is clearly demonstrated by simulations and experiments consisting of the monochromatic excitation of the metasurface by a point source set at the center of the plate (Fig. 1 C and D and see Methods for more details and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 for experimental protocols). Depending on the operating frequency (colored lines in Fig. 1B), the medium is either in the closed (Fig. 1C) or open (Fig. 1D) topological phase, which have remarkably different consequences on the wave propagation. Indeed, the closed topology showcases an omnidirectional, widespread wave field with little degree of anisotropy (isotropic and elliptic contours), while the open topology corresponds to strongly directional displacement fields with a frequency-dependent aperture (flat and hyperbolic contours). These ray-like, diffraction-less wave propagation features, along with negative refraction and enhanced wave–matter interaction, are rooted in the open nature of the hyperbolic contours (40), highlighting the relevance of this type of pillared metasurface for phononic applications in sensing and communications.

The sharp contrast in the wave behavior associated with the isofrequency topological phase motivates the search for an efficient and tunable way to modify the contour topology at a given frequency. In this context, twisted hyperbolic metasurfaces in photonics (26, 27, 41, 42) and acoustics (43, 44) have recently highlighted the potential of the rotation degree-of-freedom to tailor the global spatial symmetries of multilayered systems. Inspired by these works, we implemented a twisted bilayer version of our pillared metasurface in the hyperbolic frequency regime (Fig. 2A), where the twist angle θ describes the rotation-induced misalignment between the two layers’ symmetrical axes. The interaction between the two identical layers (black arrows on Fig. 2A) is modeled using a spatially homogeneous constant interlayer coupling β (18) (see SI Appendix, section III for more details, and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 for a parametric simulation study on the thickness of a generic interlayer model). This results in the hybridization of the isofrequency contours of each metasurface shown in Fig. 2B, as a function of the twist angle θ. For twisted geometries (θ>0), single-layer contours (gray lines on Fig. 2B) overlap, and their hybridization yields anticrossing points (ACP, red disks on Fig. 2B) whose number, in the small wavevector local limit, is either 2 or 4 depending on the twist angle. Remarkably, we can directly relate the number of ACPs to different isofrequency topologies within the twisted bilayer system: 2 ACPs correspond to an open hyperbolic contour, and 4 ACPs correspond to a closed topological phase. In this way, the number of ACPs acts as a topological invariant which permits to track the topological transition induced by the twist angle. At the transition point, which we name elastic magic angle θ∗ in reference to photonic magic angles (26), the contour is flat, leading to a strongly canalized regime (45). In our experiments, the two metasurfaces are coupled using double-sided tape, which allows a straightforward reconfigurability of the sample without adding debilitating loss (see Methods for more details), as evidenced by the good agreement between theoretical predictions, simulated and experimental displacement field maps (Fig. 2 C and D, respectively). Indeed, for twist angles smaller than θ∗≈45∘, the strong localization and directionality of the wave propagation are evidence of a hyperbolic phase, with an aperture and anisotropy that depend on the two layers’ respective orientation. Above θ∗≈45∘, the contours progressively close, till the medium becomes isotropic at θ=90∘. This twist-driven topological transition is also apparent in real space, thanks to the curvature inversion of the wavefronts, going from hyperbolic (open), to flat (magic angle), and then elliptic (closed), which is manifested in both simulation and experiments. This directly acts as a signature of the experimental critical twist angle, complementary to the number of ACPs in reciprocal space (see SI Appendix, section III for more details). Moreover, the topological nature of the process accounts for its robustness: Provided that the twist angle is smaller than the magic angle, the bilayer system is able to guide and steer a strongly localized displacement field. It is also worth noting that, unlike moiré-related effects studied in condensed matter (6–9), photonics (10–12, 16), and phononics (13–15, 17, 18), this twist-driven effect is not tied to superlattice periodicity. In turn, this feature relaxes the constraints on the magic angle value, which opens the door to designs whose functionality is significantly less sensitive to errors and sample fabrication, making them amenable to on-chip miniaturization operating at higher frequencies (38).

The significance of our twistelastics approach extends to a wide range of frequencies due to the large bandwidth of each metasurface hyperbolic band (Fig. 1B). We note that this bandwidth can be further increased using optimization protocols (46). This broadband behavior is evidenced by the experimental polar plots of Fig. 3A, which represents the average angular distribution of wave energy in the plate, in the case of a central point source excitation at three different frequencies. These plots show that the displacement field remains strongly localized and directional for the twist angles θ≤45∘, with little dependence on the frequency. For θ>45∘, however, the signal spreads within the medium as a function of frequency. For θ=90∘, the field ends up covering the entire sample at all frequencies. These results are further corroborated by the displacement field maps in reciprocal space (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), which show the occurrence of a topological transition for a significant frequency range Δf/fC=50 %, where fC=6 kHz is the central frequency. The broadband nature of this transition, tightly linked to its inherent topological robustness, is particularly relevant in the context of telecommunications and microelectronics (47), where dynamical routing of short pulses is important for signal processing. In this context, we showcase energy propagation of a broadband Gaussian pulse sent from a point source at the center of the twisted bilayer sample (central frequency fcP=6 kHz, Gaussian width ΔfP=1.3 kHz) on Fig. 3B (see also Movie S1). The experimental snapshots, reconstructed in data processing (see Methods for more details), taken at three different times after the excitation of the system, highlight the ability of our twisted hyperbolic metasurface for adaptable wave control, both in terms of field enhancement and guiding. Albeit at the proof-of-concept stage, these results establish the potential of our twistelastics platform, whose robustness and versatility opens opportunities for next-generation phononic devices.

Finally, motivated by the recent works on orbital angular momentum and inherent spin degrees-of-freedom of elastodynamic waves (48, 49), we investigate how the interplay between the angular momentum of the source and the isofrequency topology may be affected by the twist-driven tunability of the multilayer. This effect can be obtained by combining multiple sources, with a proper phase shift, which results in the preferential excitation of specific portions of the contour (50) (see Methods for more details). In the case of a flat contour, for instance, a source dipole aligned along the y axis leads to the excitation of the ky>0 part of the contour (Fig. 4A). This directly translates into a canalized beam toward y>0, which rotates as a function of the twist angle with a noticeable stability. In the case of two different contour topologies, controlled by the twist configuration, the resulting wave behavior can be extremely anisotropic as it combines strong spatial asymmetry from the source with high contrast in field localization from the medium (Fig. 4B). This is especially true if the orientation of the dipole source specifically excites portions of the contours that have opposite curvature depending on their topology, as in the configuration shown in Fig. 4C. In this case, the spatial distribution of the wave propagation is entirely transformed simply by rotating the two layers. Such reconfigurable spin-momentum locking shares similarities with the valley degree of freedom within twisted bilayer transition metal dichalcogenides, which stems from electron spin–orbit interaction and rotation angle (8). This source-induced wave directionality is complementary to the recent demonstrations of unidirectional canalization obtained by further breaking the rotation symmetries within multilayer media, both in optics (51) and acoustics (52). Overall, it generally shows the potential of twistelastics for applications based on wave angular momentum such as multiplexing, filtering, sensing, and particle manipulation.

Conclusions.

To conclude, in this work, we demonstrated theoretically and experimentally extreme control over elastic waves by tailoring the isofrequency topology of pillared metasurfaces through the twist degree-of-freedom. We showed broadband and reconfigurable topological transitions which we directly probed in real space both in frequency- and time-domain. The topological nature of the transition points, namely elastic magic-angles, accounts for a large robustness, opening the door to smaller-scale designs whose functionality is significantly less sensitive to errors and sample fabrication. By tailoring the source symmetry, additional directionality and beam steering were achieved. Overall, our work evidences the relevance of the twistelastics platform for precise, reconfigurable, and robust phononic wave manipulation, which may benefit further from additional layers (44, 51). Beyond the macroscopic proof-of-concept results shown here, our approach, upon miniaturization, may provide opportunities for analog signal processing in microelectronics, as well as complex particle manipulation and sensing in the context of ultrasound imaging and microfluidics.

Methods

Sample Fabrication and Experimental Setup.

Mechanical samples depicted in SI Appendix, Fig. S2 are 3D printed with polylactic acid (PLA, E=2.5 GPa, ν=0.3, ρ=1,300 kg-3) using the Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) technique (3D printer model is Raise3D Pro2 Plus). The cross-section of the pillar resonators is cx=1.225mm by cy=2.625 mm, their height is H=7.5 mm, the lattice step is A=3.5 mm, and the thickness of the plate is h=1.3 mm. These parameters define the frequency range of the pillar resonances and the dispersion of the modes of the nude plate, which hybridize to form the bands discussed in the Main Text. Further details on the physics of pillar resonators, especially related to bending resonances, may be found in ref. 38. The out-of-plane displacement field maps of the metasurface are measured every 20 Hz between 2 kHz and 12 kHz thanks to a 3D laser vibrometer (Polytec PSV-500-3D in spectrum mode), as presented in SI Appendix, Fig. S2. The medium is excited close to its center thanks to a mechanical shaker (Brüel & Kjær model 4809), with a broadband signal covering the frequency range of interest. Due to the linearity of the system, different spatial excitation schemes can be implemented in postprocessing by virtue of the superposition principle in linear media. Bidimensional spatial Fourier transformations of the field maps, as well as relevant spatial filtering, are carried out directly in MATLAB to extract the isofrequency contours. In particular, the spatial filtering of wavevectors far larger than the first Brillouin zone allows to get rid of measurement noise. Despite a negligible frequency mismatch of ≈50 Hz due to sample fabrication inaccuracies, the corresponding experimental hyperbolic contours are in good agreement with the pillar system simulations.

In the practical case of the bilayer sample, the interlayer coupling is obtained using double-sided tape, which, albeit being an additional but not debilitating source of dissipation, allows for the straightforward implementation of multiple twist configurations. These proof-of-concept experiments may be further extended to more realistic devices with the addition of an extra layer whose thickness, structure, and composition can be designed to achieve an accurate control of the interlayer coupling properties. Moreover, the clear qualitative agreement between theoretical predictions, simulations, and experiments, allows us to envision the extension of our approach to smaller scales with materials with reduced dissipation, such as metals for instance. Besides, the time-domain results presented in Fig. 3B are reconstructed by applying the inverse temporal Fourier transform of the experimental frequency field maps, summed with a Gaussian weight in frequency to mimic the time-domain propagation of a pulse. Such data processing is allowed in the absence of nonlinearities which ensures the superposition theorem. In the same manner, the multisource results shown in Fig. 4 are obtained with the linear combination with a phase delay of two independent successive measurements corresponding to different source positions.

Simulation Protocol.

The simulation results of pillared metasurface presented in this paper were obtained with both the eigenfrequency module and the frequency response module of COMSOL Multiphysics Solid Mechanics for the band structure results (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) and source excitation of the finite-size cavity (Figs. 1 and 2), respectively. The PLA material parameters used are E=2.5 GPa, ν=0.3, ρ=1,300 kg-3. The dissipation is modeled in COMSOL Multiphysics as a built-in isotropic loss factor ηLoss=0.05 such that the lossy elasticity tensor CLoss=(1+iηLoss)∗C. The simulated system consists of two identical plates with a thickness h=1.3 mm decorated with a square lattice of pillars on each side with geometrical parameters similar to the ones of Methods A, separated by a homogeneous layer of thickness d made of a generic soft material (E=100, ν=0.49 kPa, ρ=1,000 kg-3). The results presented in Fig. 2C correspond to d=h/1,000, thereby establishing a strong coupling between the two twisted lattices. In that regard, it slightly differs from the experimental implementation where this strong coupling is practically obtained with double-sided tape which, albeit making the experiment straightforward and reconfigurable, induces some additional dissipation in the system. For these finite sample point-source simulations, perfectly matched layers (PML) are displayed around the bilayer metasurface, that has a diameter of 51 unit cells, to mitigate the reflections. A point load with an out-of-plane total force is set at the center of the system, on the bottom side only, in order to efficiently excite the flexural waves in the plate. At a given operating frequency, the corresponding out-of-plane displacement 2D field maps on the bottom side of the plate are recorded and using a 2D spatial Fourier transformation in the MATLAB environment, we extract the corresponding contours in reciprocal space. These results show good qualitative agreement with the experiments, albeit with negligible frequency mismatches due to fabrication inaccuracies. Besides, a parametric simulation study on the influence of the thickness of the coupling layer on the contour topology and canalization properties is presented in SI Appendix, Fig. S6.
