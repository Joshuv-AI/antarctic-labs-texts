Antibacterial and cytotoxicity properties of a polyherbal mouthwash containing Achyranthes aspera and Trachyspermum ammi against selected periodontal pathogens
Authors: Ram Surath Kumar, Anil V. Ankola, Mahantesh B. Nagamoti, Roopali M. Sankeshwari, Kishori P. Sutar, Shushant I. Jigan, Atrey J. Pai Khot
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChlorhexidine (CHX) is considered as a gold standard for its antibacterial efficacy and substantivity in chemical plaque control. However, some adverse effects are associated with its prolonged use. Herbal medicines like Achyranthes aspera and Trachyspermum ammi have been used in many clinical conditions, and they appear to be a valuable substitute next to CHX in the management of periodontal diseases.ObjectiveThis in vitro study was designed to assess and compare the antibacterial potential and cytotoxic effects of novel polyherbal mouthwash containing A. aspera and T. ammi with 0.2% CHX mouthwash against Porphyromonas gingivalis, Tannerella forsythia, and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans.MethodsEthanolic extracts of A. aspera and T. ammi were prepared by the Soxhlet apparatus method and were subjected to preliminary phytochemical screening. The individual plant extracts and the plant extract mixture (PEM) of A. aspera and T. ammi in the ratio of 1:1, 2:1, 1:2 (w/v) were assessed for minimum inhibitory concentration (resazurin microtitre assay) and minimum bactericidal concentration (spread plating method) against selected periodontal pathogens in comparison to CHX. The polyherbal mouthwash was assessed for zone of inhibition (well diffusion method) and cytotoxicity (MTT assay) on adult human gingival fibroblasts. All the experiments were performed in triplicate.ResultsThe antibacterial activity was evident in the PEMs, and polyherbal mouthwash against tested periodontal pathogens and was comparable to CHX. The cytotoxicity assay findings confirmed that polyherbal mouthwash exhibited 82.1% of surviving cells which proved good biocompatibility.ConclusionA. aspera and T. ammi based mouthwash possess comparable antibacterial activity against periodontal pathogens when compared to CHX.

Introduction

Periodontitis, a bacterially induced, localized, chronic inflammatory disease, destroys connective tissue and bone that support the teeth [1]. The burden of periodontal disease is continuously rising globally, therefore potent treatment modalities to manage periodontal inflammation as well as its accompanying systemic complications are essential [2].

Porphyromonas gingivalis and Tannerella forsythia, members of red complex pathogens are strongly associated with the onset and progression of periodontal diseases. Moreover, there is a strong positive correlation between the red complex pathogens and the bleeding on probing/periodontal pocket depth [3]. Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg), Tannerella forsythia (Tf) and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (Ac) are the principal pathogens that have been first identified and classified as the primary causative agents of periodontal disease [4].

Mechanical plaque control is the common treatment modality in the management of periodontal disease in addition to chemical plaque control. The antibacterial efficacy and substantivity of chlorhexidine (CHX) make it stand out as the gold standard since 1940s [5]. According to a recent systematic review, 60% of the included studies demonstrated that herbal-based mouthwashes were as effective as CHX. On the other hand, 40% of the included studies proved that CHX mouthwash is superior to herbal mouthwashes [6]. However, some documented adverse effects associated with its use for an extended period of time include staining of tooth and restoration, burning sensation, parotid swelling, change in taste, and increased formation of supragingival calculus, making it mandatory to search for effective substitutes [7]. Traditional medicine has gained popularity, due to its affordability, therapeutic value, and fewer side effects as compared to synthetic drugs [8].

Achyranthes aspera Linn (A. aspera) commonly referred to as Apamarga, belongs to the family Amaranthaceae. Its versatile applications in diverse traditional healthcare systems encompass the effective management of conditions such as fever, toothache, tonsillitis, arthritis, pneumonia, urinary and gynaecological disorders, insect and snake bites, wound healing, stomach pain and abdominal tumour. The active ingredients of A. aspera are utilized as an antibacterial, antifungal, antiviral, antimalarial, antiarthritic, antileprotic, antispasmodic, purgative, diuretic, oestrogenic, and cardiotonic agent. In ancient times, fresh roots of A. aspera were used as a toothbrush in routine oral hygiene practices [9]. Trachyspermum ammi Linn (T. ammi), commonly referred to as Ajwain, belongs to the family Apiaceae. Ajwain seeds have been ingrained in traditional practices for the management of bronchial complications, amenorrhea, asthma, loss of appetite, abdominal tumours, diarrhoea, stomach aches, and piles [10]. It has been associated with a variety of biological activities including aphrodisiac properties, analgesic, antibacterial, antiviral, antifungal, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory activity [10].

There is an impending need to bring to light the medicinal properties of such herbal products. Using herbal mouthwash as a suitable alternative could be a breakthrough for these problems, thereby acting as a valuable substitute for the management of gingivitis and periodontitis, an alternative to CHX as an adjunct to mechanical plaque control. The present study aims to assess and compare the antibacterial potential and cytotoxic effects of extracts and novel polyherbal mouthwash containing A. aspera and T. ammi with CHX mouthwash against Pg, Tf, and Aa.

Materials and methods

Collection and authentication of plant specimen

The current study was an experimental in vitro study and was in accordance with Good Laboratory Practice standards [11]. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Research and Ethics Committee (IRB number: EC/NEW/2021/2435) with reference number 1454, dated May 05, 2021. Roots of A. aspera and Seeds of T. ammi were obtained from the Ayurveda pharmacy of a recognized institute, in Belagavi, India. Authentication of the plant specimen was conducted by a taxonomist from the ICMR-National Institute of Traditional Medicine (accession number RMRC-1618).

Preparation of plant extracts

Fresh roots of A. aspera and seeds of T. ammi were dried under shade and ground into a coarse powder. Ethanolic extracts of A. aspera and T. ammi were prepared by the Soxhlet apparatus method in 600 mL of 70% Ethanol based solvent (Changshu Hongsheng Fine Chemicals. Ltd, China) at 50 °C. A. aspera and T. ammi extracts were prepared after the cyclic procedure with a duration of 8 h and 5.5 h, respectively. The cycles were repeated until the solvent transformed from a coloured to a colourless one. A total of 150 gms of coarse powders were utilized in 600 mL of solvent each in a 1:4 ratio to produce an extraction yield of 13.9 gms of A. aspera (9.3%) and 30.2 gms of T. ammi (20.1%) crude extracts. The extracts were removed from the apparatus and dried in the IKA RV 10 rotary flash evaporator. The prepared extracts were subjected to preliminary phytochemical screening using the standard procedures as suggested by Evans et al. [12]. The sterile extracts were stored at −20 °C for further use.

Source and preparation of bacterial suspension

The fresh standard strains of Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg): ATCC 33277, Tannerella forsythia (Tf): ATCC 43037 and Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans (Aa): ATCC 29523, were procured from LGC Promochem, Pvt. Ltd., India. Direct colony suspension of each bacterial isolate was prepared anaerobically in 5 mL Brain–Heart Infusion (BHI) broth (HiMedia Laboratories Pvt. Ltd., India) and inoculated in an anaerobic chamber (N2 80%, CO2 10%, H2 10%) at 37 °C for 48 h. The turbidity was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland Standard (1 × 108 Colony Forming Unit (CFU)/mL).

Preparation of stock solutions, chlorhexidine solution and culture media

Each of the sterile extracts (w/v) weighing 50 mg was reconstituted in 1000 μL of 10% Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Qualigens, Thermo Fisher Scientific Pvt. Ltd, India). A MixMate® Vortex agitator (Eppendorf, Australia) was used to agitate the mixture for 3 min at 1000 rpm and then sonicated using Branson bath sonicator 1800 (Branson Ultrasonics, Danbury, CT) for 15 min. A solution of 0.12% of CHX which was used as a positive control prepared using CHX hydrochloride salt BP grade (ICPA Health Products Ltd., India) (1.2 mg/mL). The culture media and plates were pre-incubated in the anaerobic chamber at 37 °C for 24 h prior to culture to remove residual oxygen.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and Fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC)

A Resazurin microtitre assay as per the standard protocol of Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) and the modified method of Pai Khot et al. [13] were employed to determine the MIC of individual component plant extracts, PEM (plant extract mixture) in the ratio of 1:1, 2:1, 1:2 and 0.12% CHX, against Pg, Tf and Aa. A volume of 100 μL of the test sample (A. aspera) was introduced to the first well of the 96-well microtitre plate (NEST-Biotechnology, China) followed by 100 μL of sterile BHI broth was introduced to each well. The various concentrations (50–0.19 mg/mL) of extract were prepared by a serial doubling dilution method. Finally, 10 μL of standardized bacterial suspension (1 × 108 CFU/mL) was added to the respective wells. In addition to these, one well filled with 200 μL of BHI broth acted as vehicle control to assure the broth sterility. In a separate well containing a BHI broth of 200 μL, 10 μL of bacterial suspension was added that acted as a growth control. The plates were sealed and incubated in anaerobic jars (A-35, Don Whitley, Yorkshire, UK) using the microaerophilic atmosphere generation system at 37 °C for 48 h. After the period of incubation, 10 μL of resazurin solution (Hi-Cert™ HiMedia® Laboratories, Pvt. Ltd, India) (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well and further incubated for 4 h in anaerobic condition at 37 °C. The resulting change in colour to pink/red was inferred as an indication of bacterial growth and no change in resazurin blue/purple indicates inhibition of bacterial growth. The MIC was taken as the lowest concentration with no change of resazurin colour [14]. The FIC was determined to assess the synergy of the plant extracts, and it was calculated as follows:FICI(FICofA.aspera)=MICofPEMMICofA.asperaaloneFICII(FICofT.ammi)=MICofPEMMICofT.ammialone

The FIC index (Σ FIC) was calculated according to the formula: ΣFIC=12(FICI+FICII).

FIC index determines the interaction between the various plant extracts in the PEM wherein it is evaluated using the following range of value; value < 0.5 as synergistic, value > 0.5–1 as an additive, value > 1–4 as no interaction and value > 4 as antagonistic [15].

Minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

MBC was determined by the spread plating method. About 20 μL of the bacterial suspension from the wells with a concentration higher than the MIC value was subjected to inoculation on plates containing BHI agar (HiMedia Pvt. Ltd., India) and were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The lowest concentration of the extract (A. aspera) that showed no growth was taken as MBC [16]. Formulation of mouthwash

The formulation of the mouthwash was based on antibacterial efficacy, cytotoxicity, and palatability. At room temperature, the best formulation derived with a weighed proportion of ethanolic extracts comprising 2% A. aspera and 4% T. ammi in the ratio of 1:2 (w/v) was mixed with 15% of glycerine (humectant). Methylparaben (0.5%), propylparaben (0.01%) as preservatives, 3% xylitol (sweetening agent), and 0.5% sodium benzoate (antioxidant) were dissolved in 20 mL of deionized distilled water. Finally, 0.01% peppermint oil (flavouring agent) and 2% Tween 80 (surfactant) were added. The obtained mixture was filtered and the volume was made up to 100 mL (q.s) using distilled water as a vehicle. Finally, the prepared mouthwash was stored in a sterile amber-coloured glass container.

Antibacterial susceptibility testing

Two types of mouthwash namely, prepared polyherbal mouthwash and 0.2% CHX gluconate mouthwash (Hexidine, ICPA Health Products Ltd., India) were tested using an agar well diffusion assay [17]. Colonies of microorganisms were inoculated on BHI agar plates with a sterile cotton swab, swabbed thrice and the inoculum was adjusted to a McFarland 0.5 turbidity. A sterile borer was used to create a 6 mm diameter and 4 mm depth well in the inoculated plates. Three wells on each plate were prepared, one each for polyherbal mouthwash, positive control (0.2% CHX), and negative control (Nucleus-free water, Qiagen, Germany). Each well received 100 μL of the respective test samples. After 48 h of incubation period at 37 °C, the zone of inhibition (ZOI) was measured with a vernier calliper (Scienceware, Pequannock, NJ). The experiments were performed in triplicate and were repeated for other periodontal pathogens.

Cytotoxicity assay

The MTT (3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5 diphenyl tetrazolium bromide) (HiMedia® Laboratories, Pvt. Ltd, India) cytotoxicity assay was carried out on adult human gingival fibroblasts (HGF) [18] that were harvested from healthy gingival tissue of a mandibular premolar region. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Research and Ethics Committee (Reference number: 1454, dated May 05, 2021).

The in vitro growth inhibition effect of the test sample was calculated using ELISA (Epoch, BioTek® Instruments, Inc., USA) by conversion determination of MTT into Formazan blue by living cells. Each well was seeded with 50 μL of a 4000 cells/mL cell suspension, with the addition of complete Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco™ Life Technologies, India) including antibiotics, Fetal bovine serum (FBS), and antimycotic to reach a final volume of 150 μL. In the presence of 5 % CO2, the respective test sample 100 μL each [6% PEM in the ratio of 1:2 (w/v); prepared 6% herbal mouthwash containing 2% A. aspera and 4% T. ammi in the ratio of 1:2 (w/v); 0.2% CHX mouthwash (Positive control); nucleus-free water (Negative control)] was introduced to the wells and incubated in a CO2 incubator (New Brunswick™ Galaxy® 170 R, Eppendorf, Germany) for 24 h at 37 °C. About 20 μL of 5 mg/mL MTT reagent was introduced to the wells after 24 h. The supernatant was removed without disrupting the precipitated Formazan crystals. DMSO (100 μL) was added to the crystals to dissolve them. The optical density (OD) was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm and the experiments were performed in triplicate [19,20].

Statistical analysis

The collected data were input into Microsoft Excel (2020) and analyzed with SPSS® (IBM Corp. Released 2012 IBM SPSS, Version 21.0. Armonk, NY). The descriptive data were given in the form of mean ± standard deviation. To compare the difference in the antibacterial and cytotoxic properties of extracts, polyherbal mouthwash and CHX, Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn's post hoc tests were used. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Discussion

The overall findings confirmed that A. aspera and T. ammi based mouthwash is effective in controlling and inhibiting the growth of Aa, Pg and Tf. Aa exhibited heightened sensitivity, inhibiting at concentrations ranging from 6.3 to 12.5 mg/mL, while Pg and Tf were inhibited at concentrations between 12.5 and 16.7 mg/mL. The potential inhibitory effects highlight their role in managing gingivitis and periodontitis. These findings suggest that such a mouthwash could serve as an effective anti-plaque agent, preventing the colonization and maturation of dental plaque. Furthermore, the application of A. aspera gel demonstrated its efficacy as a non-surgical local drug delivery system for periodontitis management, with no reported adverse effects [21].

Previous studies reported that preliminary phytochemical analysis of both extracts indicated the presence of flavonoids, alkaloids, tannins, phenolic compounds and steroids [22,23]. These phytochemicals exhibit significant antibacterial activity and have been proven to possess antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties and serve as an alternative to synthetic drugs. The specific mechanism of action (MOA) and biochemical components responsible for the antibacterial activity against periodontal pathogens are unclear in A. aspera and T. ammi. In general, the antimicrobial MOA of flavonoids can be categorized as inhibiting cytoplasmic membrane function, synthesis of nucleic acid, and energy metabolism [24]. Flavonoids present in A. aspera are responsible for antibacterial activity according to Mishra et al. [25]. The total phenolic content of A. aspera ranged from 0.39 to 5.26 mg/g for tannic acid equivalent according to Upadhya et al. [26]. Similarly, Modareskia et al. reported that the major constituents in T. ammi were phenolic compounds (Thymol: 59.9–96.4%, p-cymene: 0.6–21.2%, γ-terpinene: 0.2–17.8%, and carvacrol: 0.4–2.8%) exhibit strong antibacterial activity against various pathogens [27].

The active compound of any natural product must be extracted using a solvent that dissolves the chemicals of interest. Ethanol can dissolve both non-polar and polar compounds, including a range of plant-derived chemicals and is safe for humans. Hence, ethanol was chosen as the solvent in the present study. The Soxhlet extraction technique was employed to prepare extracts, as it is widely used, cost-effective and has been proven to be more efficient [28].

CHX, a widely used antimicrobial agent in oral care products, is known to be effective against a spectrum of oral microorganisms, including periodontal pathogens. However, studies reveal that Pg can acquire resistance to CHX, potentially through binding of CHX by outer membrane vesicles. This resistance mechanism suggests that bacterial interactions within the oral cavity may influence microbial sensitivity [29]. The existence of multidrug resistance in dental plaque bacteria further complicates this resistance [30]. Moreover, CHX being a chemical carries various adverse effects when used for a longer period. To overcome such adverse effects, extensive research is being conducted in the field of alternative medicine. There has been a huge turnaround toward herbal remedies in recent times [31]. Herbal medicines are frequently employed owing to various benefits such as easy availability, acceptance, simplicity of cultivation and processing, cost-effectiveness, low toxicity, and lack of microbial resistance [32].

A. aspera grows as a weed across India, Baluchistan, Africa, Sri Lanka, Australia, Tropical Asia, and America. T. ammi is an Egyptian native plant. However, it is extensively spread and cultivated all around the world, including Iran, Iraq, Afghanistan, Pakistan, Afghanistan, India, and Europe [33].

The FIC index is viewed as the gold standard to determine the interaction between various natural products [15]. Research focusing on antimicrobial combinations can lead to a breakthrough that addresses the growing potential problem concerning antimicrobial resistance. Resistance to crude extracts is less common than resistance to single actives [15,34]. As a result, focusing on single targeted molecules does not generate long-term alternatives to antimicrobial resistance.

The reason to choose these herbal extracts was their long-standing history as effective antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory agents. Several studies reported that A. aspera [35,36] and T. ammi [37,38] exhibited antibacterial and antifungal activity against various oral microorganisms like Streptococcus mutans, Lactobacillus acidophilus, Enterococcus faecalis and Candida albicans. Soorgani et al. observed a significant reduction in the subgingival microflora count of P.gingivalis when used as a non-surgical local drug delivery system for the management of chronic periodontitis [39]. A study conducted by Kumar et al. reported the antibacterial activity of mouthwash containing A. aspera and T. ammi against P.gingivalis and T. forsythia [40].

The cytotoxicity assay revealed that CHX mouthwash allowed 76% of surviving cells. The cell viability between CHX and the herbal mouthwashes was insignificant and comparable. These findings align with those of the study conducted by Shah et al. [41]. Herbal mouthwash exhibited 82.13% of surviving cells which proved good biocompatibility. Furthermore, low concentrations of A. aspera (2%) and T. ammi (4%) extracts were used in the formulation of polyherbal mouthwash, thus considering the safety concern of using these extracts in humans. Moreover, they are native to the country with strong religious significance leading to a higher likelihood of acceptance.

Surfactants aid in the solubilization of oils (extract) present in mouthwash formulations and impart homogeneity to the preparation. Furthermore, it functions as a foaming agent and assists in the elimination of oral debris [42]. Tween 80 (Polysorbate) was used as a surfactant in the present study for the formulation of mouthwash. The herbal mouthwash exhibited a marginally lower cell viability (82%) compared to PEM (89%), possibly attributed to the presence of methylparaben, a preservative used in the formulation. Recent studies suggest the potential ill effects associated with methylparaben exposure [43].

The study's primary limitation lies in its restricted scope, which focused on a qualitative evaluation of the phytochemical constituents present in the extracts, precluding a quantitative analysis of the formulation of mouthwash. Periodontal disease is a multifactorial illness due to the interplay of the host, agent, and environmental variables. It is crucial to remember that laboratory investigations do not reflect the real condition that exists in the gingival sulcus.

Future research

Further investigation is warranted as follows:1.Quantitative analysis of the formulation of mouthwash.2.Identification of the specific MOA underlying the antibacterial efficacy of A. aspera and T,ammi against Aa, Pg and Tf.3.Establishment of a robust in vivo experimental model is crucial to evaluate the long-term antibacterial effectiveness of this combination. Moreover, the impact on gingival health and plaque control and potential adverse effects, such as tooth staining following the administration of A. aspera and T. ammi mouthwash should be investigated.

Conclusion

A. aspera and T. ammi based polyherbal mouthwash is a promising therapeutic agent with comparable antibacterial activity against periodontal pathogens and with excellent biocompatibility over CHX. In addition to being efficacious, it is widely available and cost-effective as well. Hence, it could be a valuable alternative to CHX for the management of gingivitis, as an adjunct to mechanical plaque control.
