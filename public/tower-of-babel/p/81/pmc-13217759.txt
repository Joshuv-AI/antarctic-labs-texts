Simulation-based learning enhances interprofessional competencies in undergraduate health students: a retrospective pre-post study
Authors: Howard Gomwe, Lesego Phiri, Chioneso Show Marange, Thokozile Harriet Kgongwana, Lindi Zikalala-Mabope, Rahab Mothapo, Xolelwa Dyani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSimulation-Based Learning (SBL) is an interactive teaching method that replicates real-world clinical settings to train healthcare professionals. Interprofessional Education and Collaborative Practice (IPECP) brings together students from various health disciplines to learn collaboratively, promoting teamwork and effective communication. Although international guidelines support SBL in IPECP and its benefits are well-documented, there is a lack of research on how undergraduate students across different disciplines perceive this approach.ObjectivesThis study aimed to assess undergraduate health professions students’ perceptions of SBL as a tool for promoting IPECP across multiple disciplines.MethodA quantitative, retrospective pre-post design was used involving N = 540 undergraduate students from various health disciplines. The Interprofessional Collaborative Competency Attainment Survey (ICCAS) was administered after the SBL intervention. Paired and independent samples t-tests were used to analyze pre- and post-test differences and gender comparisons.ResultsSignificant improvements (all p < 0.001) were found across all the six interprofessional competency domains. Female students showed significantly higher post-test scores in several domains compared to males, although no pre-test gender differences were observed.ConclusionSBL significantly enhances students’ perceptions about their interprofessional competencies among first-year undergraduate health students and fosters the development of collaborative practice early in their training. The observed gender differences in post-intervention outcomes highlight the need for further investigation into engagement dynamics within SBL. This study contributes to the limited body of research on undergraduate students' perceptions of SBL in IPECP across multiple disciplines. It offers empirical support for integrating simulation into interprofessional curricula.

Background

Interprofessional education (IPE) plays a crucial role in equipping healthcare students with the skills required to work collaboratively within diverse healthcare teams to enhance patient care outcomes [1–4]. SBL offers a dynamic and authentic platform that allows students from various health disciplines to engage in realistic clinical scenarios, fostering the development of teamwork, communication, and critical thinking abilities in a risk-free environment [5]. Although the significance of integrating IPE with SBL is increasingly acknowledged, there is still limited evidence of research exploring students’ perceptions of such interprofessional learning experiences and how these perceptions contribute to the development of essential collaborative competencies [6–8]. Understanding students’ perceptions of IPE through SBL is crucial because positive perceptions are often associated with greater engagement, motivation, and confidence in applying collaborative skills in real-world healthcare contexts [3, 9, 10]. Conversely, negative or neutral perceptions may hinder the development of interprofessional competencies such as teamwork, communication, and role clarification [3, 5].

SBL is a comprehensive, multifaceted educational method that can be integrated into a healthcare professional training curriculum [11, 12]. SBL is defined as a technique used to replace or amplify real experiences with guided experiences that mimic specific aspects of the real world in a fully interactive manner [13–15]. SBL may use people, devices, or conditions to present problems authentically [10, 16]. The World Health Organization [17] recommends the use of SBL for educational training purposes in both developed and developing settings, but it may be that in a low-resource setting, low-fidelity simulation is more appropriate and available [18].

The use of simulation in the interprofessional education and collaborative practice (IPECP) began decades ago but is rapidly growing in health professions education [12, 13, 19]. IPECP provides students with opportunities to learn together, learn from one another, and gain a deeper understanding of each other’s roles and perspectives [20]. Another benefit is that IPECP is reported to improve patient health care and job satisfaction [19, 21, 22]. It is vital to cultivate the culture of IPECP for effective communication [23] and teamwork [24], which sets the tone for clinical practice [19]. One may wonder how clinicians can be expected to work together effectively in clinical practice when they have not learned together during their education and training. IPECP addresses this gap by offering unique, structured opportunities for collaborative learning [25].

This article is grounded by two theories, namely the Social Learning Theory developed by Bandura [26] and the Social Constructivist Learning Theory by Vygotsky [27]. According to Bandura [26], students learn through interaction with other students, or they learn through observation. When students interact during interprofessional education learn from each other when performing their roles, and this involves cognitive factors like perceptions, expectations, attitudes, beliefs, knowledge, and intelligence. The Social Constructivist Learning Theory emphasizes that learning occurs through active participation, reflection, and social interaction [27], SBL offers a safe, hands-on environment where students from different disciplines can work together to address clinical challenges. This process promotes essential interprofessional skills such as teamwork, communication, and role clarification. Nevertheless, it remains uncertain how learners’ perceptions of IPE experience using SBL contribute to measurable gains in collaborative competence [28].

A systematic review on the effectiveness of debriefing in relation to SBL in interprofessional education (IPE) was conducted [13]. Methods of debriefing such as post simulation debriefing, in-simulation debriefing, instructor facilitated were used in the studies identified [13]. The results showed a statistically significant improvement of the participants in the pre-test and post-test performance regardless of the type of debriefing conducted in the study. A study conducted by Labrague et al. [29] looking at students participating in interprofessional simulation showed that the benefit of using simulation-based IPE is that it nurtures communication skills between professions, enhances understanding of other occupations’ roles, enables collaboration, and improves students’ self-confidence in collaborative situations. Also, Nawagi et al. [30] evaluated the IPECP competencies of student participants before and after their involvement in the IPECP Program. The findings showed a great improvement in the participants in the pre-test and post-test. A systematic review on attitudes towards interprofessional education and associated factors among faculty at the College of Health Sciences in a public university in Kenya was conducted by Kithuci et al. [31]. The findings indicated no significant differences in gender and age demographics. Overall, students had positive attitudes towards interprofessional education.

The use of SBL in health professionals’ training is advantageous, and it is widely recommended by bodies such as the WHO [18]. However, empirical studies across multiple undergraduate disciplines are scarce, as it is a relatively developing field [32]. Thus, the authors of the current study argue that the perceptions of health professions students are not adequately documented in the literature. Most studies have focused on a particular profession, such as nursing and medical students [29]. It is for this reason that the purpose of this study is to explore the perceptions of students at the first-year level across various disciplines on the use of simulation-based learning in IPECP.

Methods

Design

This study assessed retrospective pre- and post-quantitative surveys completed by first-year students from nursing, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech and language pathology and audiology, radiography, Medicine (MBChB), pharmacy, Emergency Medical Care (Certificate), Bachelor of Dental Surgery (BDS), Bachelor of Oral Hygiene, and Bachelor of Dental Therapy who participated in SBL for IPECP. The study was conducted in Gauteng, South Africa.

Setting and case scenario

There were training sessions for SBL in all disciplines before the intervention was implemented. So, a simulation-based interprofessional program was conducted on the 7th and 14th, of February 2025. This comprised of two sessions of a female simulation case scenario. The case scenario was on a 19-year-old single-parent patient who lives in a shack, with no running water and electricity, and in a mountainous area near Ga-Rankuwa Township (Gauteng, South Africa). She had complained of a dry cough for six weeks, experienced night sweats, and extreme tiredness/lack of energy lately. She has visibly lost weight over the past four weeks and was diagnosed with HIV before the birth of her second child a year ago. At the age of 15 years, she could not continue with her studies because of her first pregnancy. In order to attend high school that is in the township, she required a financed transport.

She is a sessional domestic worker twice a week in Ga-Rankuwa township. She has two surviving children from different fathers and receives a social grant for both children. She walks four kilometres to her nearest clinic as she has no money for a taxi. At the clinic, the doctor is only available once per week; there are no dentists/dental therapists, physiotherapists, podiatrists, radiographers and nutrition, and dietetics services. The emergency medical services take longer than 6 h to pick up patients from the clinic to the nearest hospital. There is rotational load shedding, where scheduled power outages are implemented to reduce pressure on the electricity generation system. This occurs every third day of the week and directly affects the clinic’s operating hours, as well as the cold chain required for the proper storage of medicines.

The scenario aimed to evaluate the health care team’s ability to employ the necessary medical treatment skills and to collaborate and communicate among themselves. The intervention was guided by six co-values, namely communication, collaboration, roles and responsibilities, patient-centred approach, conflict management, and team functioning.

Procedures

Initially, the study was designed to include 27 groups, each comprising 30 students, with two facilitators assigned to each group. The facilitators are lecturers who were trained in workshops to act as group facilitators from all departments. They assisted by organizing students into their groups during scenario activities and leading debriefing sessions. However, not all students attended as planned. Ultimately, a total of N = 540 students participated in the study, consisting of 366 females and 174 males. Participants were drawn from several health sciences streams, namely Medical and Surgical, Rehabilitation Sciences, Oral Health Sciences, Allied Health and Diagnostics, Emergency Medical Care, and Nursing Sciences. These groups included students from programmes such as MBChB, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech-language pathology and audiology, radiography, pharmacy, emergency medical services (certificate and diploma levels), as well as Bachelor of Dental Surgery, Bachelor of Oral Hygiene, and Bachelor of Dental Therapy. This diverse representation reflects the interprofessional nature of the simulation. Before each simulation session, facilitators explained the aims and procedures of the program to the students. Each group then participated in structured pre-scenario activities, followed by the simulation exercise, and concluded with a post-scenario debriefing. After completing the sessions, students were required to complete a performance reflection survey. To ensure data quality, responses from students who did not attend the sessions or failed to respond sincerely to the survey were excluded from the analysis. The number of students who were excluded was 270.

Measures

In this study, perceptions toward interprofessional learning were measured using the Interprofessional Collaborative Competencies Attainment Scale (ICCAS), developed by MacDonald et al. [33]. The ICCAS consists of 21 items grouped into five sub-factors, including the relevance of IPE. A total of 20 items measured participants’ abilities across six interprofessional competencies: communication, collaboration, roles and responsibilities, patient‑centred approach, conflict management, and team functioning. Each item was rated on a 5‑point Likert scale (1 = Poor, 2 = Fair, 3 = Good, 4 = Very good, 5 = Excellent). Sample items include: “Before participating in the learning activities, I was able to understand the meaning of IPE and interprofessional collaboration (IPC) as related to my aspired profession”, “I was able to actively listen to interprofessional team members’ ideas and concerns”, “I was able to express my ideas and concerns without hesitation,” and “I was able to provide constructive feedback to interprofessional team members.” Other items assessed the ability to express ideas with confidence, recognize challenges in under-resourced settings, and interact effectively with team members to enhance care. Each item was rated both before and after the IPE simulation, enabling a comparison of students’ perceived competencies over time. To assess the global perceived impact of the learning activities, a single retrospective item was administered at the end of the programme. Participants were asked: “Compared to the time before the learning activities, would you say your ability to collaborate interprofessional is…” Responses were recorded on a 5‑point scale: 1 = Much better now, 2 = Somewhat better now, 3 = About the same, 4 = Somewhat worse now, 5 = Much worse now. Lower scores indicate greater perceived improvement. The ICCAS demonstrated strong internal consistency in this study, supporting its reliability as a measure of interprofessional collaborative competence.

Previous research in healthcare education has provided strong evidence for the excellent psychometric properties of the Interprofessional Collaborative Competencies Attainment Scale (ICCAS). In its initial validation, MacDonald et al. [33] reported high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.92 to 0.96 across the five domains of interprofessional collaboration. Follow-up validation studies have further confirmed its construct validity, supporting either a unidimensional or five-factor structure that reflects key interprofessional skills such as communication, teamwork, understanding of roles and responsibilities, patient-centred care, and conflict management [34, 35]. Additionally, the ICCAS has shown strong test–retest reliability and responsiveness to change, making it especially useful for assessing outcomes in pre–post interprofessional education interventions. Overall, these findings establish the ICCAS as a trustworthy and valid tool for measuring self-assessed interprofessional collaborative competence in healthcare training settings.

Sampling

The study employed a convenience sampling method to recruit students. This approach was chosen due to the accessibility of participants within the university setting during the study period. While convenience sampling facilitated data collection, it is acknowledged that this method may introduce potential sampling bias and limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader student population. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, and all participants provided informed consent prior to taking part. There were no benefits given to participants.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29. Prior to inferential analysis, the reliability of the ICCAS was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient to evaluate internal consistency. Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize the demographic characteristics of participants using frequencies and percentages. A paired samples t-test was performed to examine differences between pre- and post-test scores on the ICCAS. This test assessed whether the intervention led to significant improvements in interprofessional competencies. To further explore gender differences, an independent samples t-test was conducted to compare ICCAS scores between male and female participants at both pre- and post-test phases. Levene’s test for equality of variances was used to assess the assumption of homoscedasticity, and for cases where the assumption was violated, the adjusted t-test results were reported accordingly. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and significance was set at alpha = 0.05. Results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Retrospective pre-test comparisons

No significant differences were observed between male and female participants in any competency domain during the retrospective pre-test phase. Communication scores were similar between males (mean = 2.68, SD = 0.85) and females (mean = 2.63, SD = 0.83), (t = 0.657, p = 0.512). Collaboration scores also showed no significant difference (t = 0.843, p = 0.400). Similarly, roles and responsibilities (t = 1.101, p = 0.271), patient-centred approach (t = −0.078, p = 0.938), conflict management (t = 0.462, p = 0.644), and team functioning (t = −1.745, p = 0.082) all yielded non-significant differences between genders. The total ICCAS score was comparable for males (mean = 2.86, SD = 0.74) and females (mean = 2.84, SD = 0.69), (t = 0.293, p = 0.770).

Post-test comparisons

In the post-test, significant differences emerged in several competency domains. Female participants scored significantly higher in communication (t = −2.035, p = 0.042), collaboration (t = −2.625, p = 0.009), patient-centred approach (t = −2.922, p = 0.004), conflict management (t = −2.077, p = 0.039), and team functioning (t = −3.630, p < 0.001). Roles and responsibilities did not show a statistically significant difference (t = −1.908, p = 0.057), though the trend suggests a possible difference. The total post-test score was significantly higher for females (mean = 4.42, SD = 0.40) compared to males (mean = 4.27, SD = 0.47), (t = −3.685, p < 0.001).

Overall, while pre-test competency levels were similar across genders, female participants exhibited significantly higher post-test scores in multiple domains, indicating a stronger improvement in competencies among females following the intervention. Discussion

The present study examined health professions students’ perceptions of IPE through SBL in a South African university context. The findings of this study suggest the significant role that SBL plays in enhancing students’ perceptions on interprofessional education and collaborative practice among undergraduate health professions students. These results resonate with prior literature emphasizing the multifaceted advantages of SBL in health education [11, 12, 14]. The substantial improvements observed across all interprofessional competency domains, communication, collaboration, roles and responsibilities, patient-centred approach, conflict management, and team functioning, support the effectiveness of simulation as a teaching strategy [13, 15]. The statistical significance (p < 0.001 across domains) of these changes highlights the capacity of SBL to provide experiential learning opportunities that mimic real-world healthcare settings, thereby fostering a deeper understanding of interprofessional collaboration.

The improvement in communication and collaboration aligns with Labrague et al. [29], who identified that SBL within IPE settings enhances communication skills and mutual respect between professions. Furthermore, students demonstrated better clarity in their understanding of roles and responsibilities, an outcome that directly supports the goals of IPE to prepare future professionals to "learn with, from and about each other" [20].

The gender analysis provided an additional layer of insight, revealing that female participants reported significantly higher post-test scores in most competency domains. However, the absence of pre-test differences across gender suggests that the intervention itself likely contributed to the observed divergence in outcomes. Despite the encouraging findings, the study also noted a slight decline in internal consistency from pre-test to post-test in some domains (e.g., collaboration and roles/responsibilities), although reliability remained acceptable. This could be attributed to the greater variability in student reflections and interpretations after the simulation experience. As Levett-Jones and Lapkin [13] emphasized, the quality and structure of debriefing processes significantly influence learning outcomes in SBL, suggesting a need for continuous refinement of facilitation methods.

The observed improvements across domains are consistent with the principles of Social Learning Theory [26] and Social Constructivist Theory [27], which underpin this study. These theories emphasize that learning is fostered through observation, interaction, and collaborative problem-solving. The simulation-based environment provided authentic, experiential opportunities for students to engage in teamwork, communication, and role clarification within realistic patient care scenarios. Through active participation and peer collaboration, students internalized professional values and behaviours fundamental to effective interprofessional practice. Furthermore, the debriefing sessions likely consolidated these experiences by facilitating reflection, dialogue, and feedback processes central to experiential and constructivist approaches to learning [13].

The strength of this study lies in its inclusive approach, examining the perceptions of students across multiple disciplines, an area underrepresented in existing literature [18]. Most previous studies have focused on specific professional groups, such as nursing or medical students [12], thereby limiting the generalizability of their findings. By contrast, this research provides broader insights into how SBL is perceived and experienced across the interprofessional student spectrum.

This study supports global recommendations by the WHO for integrating SBL into health professional training programs [18], especially as a strategy to cultivate interprofessional collaboration. It also highlights the importance of structured learning activities that actively engage all participants, play a vital role in developing essential professional skills like teamwork, communication, and collaborative decision-making. Inclusive educational practices to build competency in teamwork, communication, and shared decision-making. Future research should continue to explore the long-term impact of SBL on clinical performance and patient outcomes.

Limitations of the study

While this study provides valuable insights into undergraduate students’ perceptions of SBL in promoting interprofessional education and collaborative practice, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, the use of a convenience sampling method may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings beyond the sampled population. Second, the study was conducted at a single institution, potentially reducing the external validity and transferability to other educational or cultural contexts. Data were collected using self-administered questionnaires, which relied on students’ self-reported perceptions of their competencies. As such, the potential for self-reporting bias cannot be excluded, as participants may have overestimated or underestimated their competencies. These limitations were acknowledged to enhance methodological transparency.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that SBL significantly enhances students’ perceptions of their competencies among undergraduate health professions students across multiple disciplines. Notable improvements were observed in all key domains, suggesting that the intervention effectively fostered students’ readiness for interprofessional collaboration, a critical competency in modern healthcare delivery. Additionally, the post-intervention gender differences, with females generally showing higher gains, highlight the need for further research into how demographic factors influence engagement and learning outcomes in SBL environments. These findings support the integration of SBL into interprofessional curricula and suggest its potential as a scalable strategy in resource-constrained educational settings to prepare students for collaborative clinical practice.
