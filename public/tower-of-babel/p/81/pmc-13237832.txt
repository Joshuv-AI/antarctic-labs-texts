Maximum utilization of all elements in biomass waste
Authors: Yiping Luo, Tianjie Ao, Fang Deng, Zhijie Xie, Dong Li
Published: 2026

A new paradigm: Maximum potential of biomass elements

The transition from a fossil-dependent economy to a renewable bio-economy is not merely a strategic choice but a necessity for global sustainable development. Biomass waste, abundant and renewable, stands as a cornerstone of this transition. However, current strategies of biomass waste utilization have hit a critical bottleneck. Despite technological advances, comprehensive utilization rates remain limited, leaving a staggering portion of resources underutilized or discarded. The core of this inefficiency lies in a limited approach that focuses on carbon utilization while largely neglecting the complex elemental matrix of biomass waste. Energy elements (C, H, O) are predominantly converted into fuels or heat, ending their life cycle as CO2 emission. Without coupling technologies for CO2 capture or conversion, the potential for true carbon negativity is lost. Nutrient elements (e.g., N, P, K, and S), essential for fertilizers and feeds, suffer from significant losses during processing and lack precision applications, often devolving from valuable resources into environmental pollutants. Meanwhile, trace elements (e.g., Fe, Mg, Mn, Zn, and Cu), which play pivotal roles in bioactivities, are often overlooked, representing a significant “hidden loss.” The forthcoming era of biomass waste utilization requires a paradigm shift, which demands a strategy that transcends the recalcitrant nature of biomass waste toward comprehensive utilization of all elements. Herein, we outline the technological roadmaps essential to unlock the optimized potential of all elements in biomass waste, ensuring that every element finds its rightful place to achieve enhanced circularity.

Maximum mining of feedstock elements

The comprehensive utilization of biomass waste should focus on its elemental composition. For energy elements (i.e., primarily carbon), the paradigm is shifting from a traditional energy-focused model to the creation of long-term carbon reservoirs. The conversion of biomass waste into biochar through carbonization creates stable carbon forms, acting as a “carbon sink.” Deng et al. recently reported that converting biomass waste into biochar could achieve a negative emission potential of 0.92 billion tons of CO2 annually in China, playing a crucial role in achieving carbon neutrality by 2060.1

Nutrient elements like N, P, and K in biomass are equally critical but frequently underutilized. In China, the 3.8 billion tons of annual livestock manure contain 35 million tons of N, P, and K, an amount equivalent to over 50% of the chemical fertilizers used yearly.2 Biomass waste also contains other trace elements such as Fe, Mg, Mn, Zn, and Cu, which are typically overlooked due to their quite low concentrations. Nevertheless, emerging studies, such as the work by Basak et al., highlight their critical functions in enhancing process performance when properly leveraged.3 Serving as cofactors for key enzymes in anaerobic digestion, these trace elements stabilize microbial activity and suppress volatile fatty acid accumulation, thereby improving methane production.3 Additionally, trace elements are essential for the growth of animals and plants, so maximizing their recovery is fundamental to the comprehensive utilization of biomass waste.

Maximum conversion efficiency of elements

Efficient valorization of biomass waste also hinges on conversion processes that maximize element retention. Biomass waste utilization via aerobic composting suffers from significant C and N losses via greenhouse (CO2, CH4, and N2O) and odorous (NH3) gas emissions. While parameters optimization (e.g., oxygen supply, pH) and microbial additives can mitigate these losses, the theoretical efficiency remains limited. Alternatively, hydrothermal humification offers a promising way to achieve the maximal element retention. Yang et al. demonstrated that the hydrothermal humification of biomass in a closed aqueous system with a base to synthesize artificial humic substances prevents gaseous carbon losses to achieve nearly 100% carbon retention.4 Such chemical approaches offer superior process controllability compared with biological systems, effectively locking carbon, nutrients, and trace elements into stable humic acid fertilizers with high atom economy. Although the thermochemical processes are energy-intensive, their economic and environmental viability can be secured by coupling them with low-cost or industrial waste heat sources. When applied for soil remediation, humic substances serve as a stable form of carbon and also act as a “biological lever”, stimulating the soil microbiome to generate recalcitrant bacterial necromass for amplified sequestration. However, further investigation is required to assess their long-term stability and monitor elemental fate in complex soil environments.

Maximum end-use of elements

The inevitable production of by-products remains a significant obstacle to the comprehensive utilization of biomass elements. However, establishing an integrated system-level framework enables multi-element circularity. Taking anaerobic digestion as a model, the production of methane as a bio-natural gas for energy, while by-products such as H2S contained in biogas and element-enriched biogas slurry are repurposed into valuable resources. Microbial bioconversion shows a great potential for the effective uptake of sulfur recovered from biogas purification, along with nutrients and trace elements from biogas slurry to produce single cell protein, defined as edible, protein-rich microbial biomass that serves as a sustainable protein source. Simultaneously, solid digestate is converted into high-value artificial humic acids, which can be further enhanced by integrating recovered sulfur to produce sulfur-enriched fertilizers. These strategies offer new avenues to recycle and valorize the elements derived from the by-products of anaerobic digestion. Moreover, CO2 is regarded as the final output of biomass waste utilization. The utilization of CO2 to produce bio-materials and chemicals can support a truly optimized system for biomass element utilization.

A way forward

The maximum utilization of biomass waste requires a paradigm shift from component conversion to element utilization, recognizing that its valorization involves complex elemental cycles extending from atmosphere to soil.

Theoretical insights

A fundamental shift in conceptual thinking is required from waste disposal to element mining. Strengthening the awareness of element utilization provides the theoretical foundation for redesigning sustainable routes, feed, and fertilizer strategies, through recognizing, designing, and applying the Earth’s elemental cycles (Figure 1A).

Technological innovation

The forthcoming bio-industry should be designed around holistic element utilization of biomass wastes, capturing all elements through integrated advances in feedstock element mining, process loss elimination, and by-product utilization (Figure 1B). Our recent work on corn stover utilization demonstrated a cascade process combining ethanol fermentation, anaerobic digestion, and microalgae cultivation, boosting carbon utilization from 48% to 62%.5 Future investigation should focus on technological innovation to address specific bottlenecks in elemental utilization. For example, microfluidic sorting for functional strain screening, adaptive evolution and gene editing for metabolic pathway construction, directly maximizing the carbon flux toward target products. The intelligent control systems precisely regulate the reaction environment in real time to minimize unintended by-product generation. Together, these technologies will steer elements from waste into targeted products with minimal losses.

Future breakthroughs

A critical frontier lies in managing the co-valorization of macro-elements and trace elements. Considering the precision utilization of nutrient elements, it is essential to develop a synchronized spatiotemporal nutrient supply system that align nutrient release with crop demand (Figure 1C1). The application dosage and method must be precisely determined based on soil nutrient characteristics and real-time crop nutrient requirements to ensure on-demand release and maximize uptake efficiency. Trace elements can be recovered from biomass waste through biological enrichment (using hyperaccumulating plants or microorganisms) or chemical extraction methods (e.g., adsorption, membrane separation, and precipitation. Rather than treating trace elements as impurities, future research should move toward unlocking their industrial potential (Figure 1C2). In the future, more efforts should be placed on the valorization of CO2 derived from biomass waste utilization. We should construct a CO2-fixing biological or chemical system instead of treating it as an unavoidable loss or greenhouse gas emission (Figure 1C3). Achieving these breakthroughs will require close collaboration across synthetic biology, chemocatalysis, process engineering, and data science.

Management paradigm

The industrial supply-demand balance is the driving force behind biomass waste utilization (Figure 1D). In a practical industry chain, systematic evaluation of feedstock availability and market demand is essential for determining the optimal pathway and operational scale. Life cycle assessment and techno-economic analysis should be integrated to evaluate both environmental performance and economic feasibility, and to identify factors that constrain element utilization. These analyses should also be tailored to specific practical scenarios to ensure a comprehensive assessment. Further in-depth analyses of feedstock characteristics and conversion pathways are required to develop optimal element allocation frameworks that ensure efficient cascading utilization. Additionally, policy guidance is crucial for informed technology selection. Such systematic assessments will facilitate cascading utilization strategies tailored to all elements of biomass waste, paving the way for sustainable development.
