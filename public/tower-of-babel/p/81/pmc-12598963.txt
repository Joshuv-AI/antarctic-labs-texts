A modified Delphi consensus to reframe and prioritise the management of chronic insomnia in UK primary care
Authors: Alanna Hare, Jasvinder Singh Kaler, Lavan Baskaran, Caitlin Chasser, Gail Fortes-Mayer, Kevin Fernando, Danny Bartlett
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesChronic insomnia is a prevalent but under-recognised sleep disorder in the UK, affecting 6.8%–14.9% of adults. It is associated with significant health and economic burdens, yet remains under-diagnosed and poorly managed in primary care. This study aimed to address key gaps in the understanding, diagnosis and management of chronic insomnia and to develop consensus-based recommendations to optimise care pathways across the National Health Service.DesignA modified Delphi consensus study.SettingUK primary care, involving general practitioners, pharmacists and sleep specialists.ParticipantsA multidisciplinary steering group of seven UK healthcare professionals (HCPs) with expertise in chronic insomnia developed 39 statements. These were tested in a two-round Delphi survey distributed to a panel of 201 HCPs. Consensus was defined as ≥75% agreement on a 4-point Likert scale.ResultsConsensus was reached for 37 of the 39 statements. Key findings included the recognition of chronic insomnia as a distinct chronic condition, the recommendation to include routine sleep-related screening in primary care and the need for increased HCP training in cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia and pharmacological treatment. Respondents highlighted the necessity for greater awareness and public engagement regarding insomnia and its treatment options. In spite of mitigating biases, responses may have been subject to acquiescence bias.ConclusionsThe study identifies systemic barriers to effective insomnia care and calls for chronic insomnia to be prioritised in UK primary care. The resulting consensus recommendations aim to reduce the burden of untreated insomnia, improve patient outcomes, enhance healthcare delivery and increase economic productivity.Keywords: Insomnia, SLEEP MEDICINE, Primary Care

Introduction

Insomnia is a sleep disorder characterised by difficulties in initiating sleep, maintaining sleep and early waking, either in combination or alone, despite adequate opportunity and circumstances.1 It is increasingly recognised as a 24-hour disorder, with symptoms that impact patients across the whole day. Individuals with insomnia often experience a range of daytime impairments, including extreme fatigue, mood disturbances and cognitive impairment, all of which can result in substantial social and occupational dysfunction.1 These symptoms contribute to a reduced overall quality of life, productivity and a significant burden on healthcare services.2 3 The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) characterises short-term insomnia as symptoms that last less than 3 months. Symptoms that impair daytime function, occur at least three nights per week and persist for more than 3 months constitute chronic insomnia.4

In the UK, insomnia affects approximately two times more females than males, and the prevalence of nocturnal awakenings increases with age.5 6 Other risk factors include mental health disorders, age, environment, stress, lifestyle and occupation.7 Inequalities have been identified within socioeconomic groups, with the incidence of sleeplessness increasing with social deprivation; incidence also varies between ethnicities.8 In the UK, it is estimated that 6.8%–14.8% of the adult population suffer from chronic insomnia.9 The nationwide economic burden is substantial, with estimated costs to the UK economy of up to £34 billion per year.9 Given its widespread impact, chronic insomnia is considered a significant public health concern.

The active management of chronic insomnia is critical. Untreated cases can lead to significant health outcomes which include an increased risk of mortality and cardiovascular disease.10,12 Cognitive-behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is recommended as the first-line treatment in adults.1 CBT-I is a multimodal intervention typically comprising sleep hygiene, relaxation therapy, sleep restriction therapy, stimulus control therapy and cognitive therapeutics.13 It can be delivered in individual or group settings, and either face-to-face or via digital platforms. Where CBT-I is inaccessible, unsuitable or refused, or fails to provide desired improvements, then pharmacological interventions may be considered in accordance with NICE guidelines.4

Primary care services are often the first point of contact for individuals with chronic insomnia and optimal assessment and management is imperative for patient outcomes. Despite this, primary care requires more support in the diagnosis of chronic insomnia, with approximately 58% of cases undiagnosed.14 Barriers to diagnosis have been identified as a lack of knowledge, awareness and CBT-I trained practitioners, leading to undertreatment.15 There is a need to support primary care providers in diagnosing and managing chronic insomnia and provide recommendations to help supplement areas that are inadequately covered.1 16

This modified Delphi consensus aimed to provide insights and guidance to optimise the care pathway for patients with chronic insomnia. The recommendations from this Delphi study are intended to improve clinical outcomes in chronic insomnia across primary care in the UK, by improving awareness, diagnosis and minimising inequalities in access to high quality, evidence-based treatment for chronic insomnia. A modified Delphi was chosen as a formal way to generate consensus between a group of healthcare practitioners (HCPs).

Methods

The process followed a modified Delphi methodology (figure 1) and was managed by an independent Delphi facilitator (Triducive Partners Limited). The article follows the ACCORD guidelines for consensus reporting (online supplemental information). The study was not registered.

Modified Delphi study design flow.

In April 2024, a literature review on chronic insomnia was undertaken on PubMed and Embase with additional free text searches for additional guidelines using Google. Search terms included, but were not limited to, ‘insomnia’, ‘chronic insomnia’, ‘disorders of initiating and maintaining sleep’. Literature was filtered for open access and UK publications, and 55 relevant papers published between 2009 and 2024 were identified. The search provided context for the study and was used to prompt discussions at the first steering group meeting.

In July 2024, a steering group of seven UK specialists convened to discuss the current management and treatment of chronic insomnia in the UK and how it can be improved. The group was composed of two senior consultants in sleep disorders, two general practitioners (GPs), a sleep practitioner, a senior strategic commissioner (including mental health) and a clinical pharmacist. Through discussion, the steering group agreed on the following domains for the focus of statement development:

These were then used to generate consensus statements for testing with a wider Delphi panel. The statements aimed to define the situation, goals and expectations of chronic insomnia and to guide action to support optimal practice. Following the first steering group meeting, the drafted statements were independently ratified, and the facilitator actioned required changes. The resulting statements were developed into a survey with a four-point Likert scale (‘strongly disagree’, ‘tend to disagree’, ‘tend to agree’, and ‘strongly agree’) to allow respondents to indicate their corresponding level of agreement for each statement. For further analyses, some demographic data, including role, country and time in the role, were also captured.

To minimise responder selection bias, the survey was distributed by a third party (M3 Global) in August-October 2024. All responses were completely anonymous. The survey was completed on a first come, first served basis, with emails sent by M3 Global to relevant clinicians registered to their panel and the response rate not being monitored. Instead, stopping criteria were defined a priori as a target of 100 responses, with 90% of statements achieving consensus, and a threshold for agreement of 75% (a widely accepted threshold17). The survey was distributed to HCPs from community care settings, pharmacists, primary care professionals and secondary care experts in sleep disorders. The ratio of responders was designed to be reflective of the steering group and the types of HCPs involved in treating insomnia within the UK. The number of responses was to ensure a comprehensive range of perspectives was considered, while taking into consideration the pressures of the healthcare system. Respondents received a small fee for completing the survey.

A statement of consent was included at the start of the survey. Ethical approval was not sought as this study only collected the anonymous opinions of HCPs, and no patient-specific data were captured. Completed surveys were analysed to produce an arithmetic agreement score for each statement. The responses were aggregated to provide an overall agreement level. The steering group then reviewed the results at a second meeting in December 2024 to agree if the stopping criteria were met or if more rounds of survey were required.

Patient and public involvement

This study was designed to seek opinions on treatment strategies and NHS services. Therefore, it only focused on gathering consensus from HCPs and did not seek patient input.

Results

Initially, 44 statements were generated by the steering group. Of these, 5 were removed, and the remaining 39 all underwent minor linguistic editing. The finalised 39 statements were approved by all steering group members and used within the survey.

A total of 201 responses were received. Most respondents were based in England (n=183), with a minority from Scotland (n=13), Wales (n=2) and Northern Ireland (n=2). Respondent time in the role varied (≥5 years, n=23; 6–10 years, n=68; 11–19 years, n=76; ≤20 years, n=34). Consensus was achieved in 37/39 of statements. All stopping criteria were met and the steering group agreed to stop after one round of survey. Of the 39 statements tested, 27 statements achieved consensus agreement of ≥90%, and 2 statements (5%) failed to achieve consensus (table 1, figure 2). Overall, there was generally good agreement across sub-groups (figure 3 online supplemental information). Respondents from Northern Ireland and Wales were less agreeable to some statements. However, due to low response rates from these countries, no solid conclusions could be drawn.

Domain A. Burden of chronic insomnia on patients, primary care and the economy

Responses highlighted that a significant lack of recognition and management of chronic insomnia may exist in the NHS. Strong agreement was established regarding identifying chronic insomnia as a modifiable risk factor for cardiometabolic health conditions (S3, 92%) and mental health conditions (S4, 95%). Respondents also agreed that chronic insomnia is associated with an increased risk of accidents in the workplace (S7, 97%), and on the burden that chronic insomnia poses to the UK economy (S8, 94%).

Domain B. Optimal future role of community care services (assessment)

Respondents failed to reach consensus regarding whether there is stigma in community care surrounding discussions about chronic insomnia (S13, 65%), and only 64% agreed that they ask at least 50% of their patients about their sleep quality. All other statements in domain B reached consensus, with statements encouraging HCPs to ask questions such as ‘How do you feel in the daytime?’ (S11, 95%) and ‘How is your sleep quality?’ (S14, 93%) reaching strong agreement levels.

Domain C. Role of treatment strategies

Agreement with statement 16 (76%) suggests that there is a reticence to ask, “How is your sleep quality?” due to a lack of locally available treatment options. Statement 17 (91%) identifies the disparity between access to digital vs face-to-face CBT-I. There was a clear consensus that CBT-I is an effective first-line treatment option for chronic insomnia (S19, 91%).

Domain D. Value of education and guidance

Results here emphasise the value of education and guidance in chronic insomnia management in the UK. Respondents strongly agreed that there is a knowledge gap in the identification, diagnosis and management of chronic insomnia in the UK (S25, 92%). The necessity of addressing this knowledge gap with a multi-disciplinary approach using patient groups, charities, professional bodies and industry partners was also recognised (S28, 93%).

Domain E. Value of engaging individuals

Agreement highlighted the importance of a holistic understanding of chronic insomnia patients and empowering them to seek help for their sleep problems (S33, S34; 98%, 99%, respectively), supporting the active engagement of patients in the management of their chronic insomnia.

Domain F. Optimal future roles for policymakers and existing comorbid policies

All statements in Domain F were strongly supported by respondents. Almost all (S35, 96%) agreed that there is a perceived need for chronic insomnia optimisation in the NHS, which may require investment of resources. Respondents also supported the view that sleep health should be given equal priority to other long-term conditions (S38, 90%).

Discussion

Chronic insomnia should be recognised as a stand-alone chronic condition and as a significant driver of associated cardiometabolic and mental health co-morbidities.

Despite its well-documented consequences, the consensus revealed that chronic insomnia remains under-recognised and under-treated in both primary and secondary care settings. The results suggest HCPs are aware of the gap present between patients diagnosed and treated, and those still suffering while undiagnosed (S2, 89%). The substantial under-treatment of chronic insomnia in the UK clearly requires greater actionable change driven by HCPs in chronic insomnia diagnosis and management.

Evidence suggests sleep disorders, including insomnia, are associated with adverse cardiometabolic risk profiles and outcomes.11 18 The evidence linking chronic insomnia and mental health conditions is robust. A meta-analysis of 34 cohorts involving 172 077 participants, which found a significant association between insomnia and an increased risk of depression.19 Similarly, a 2016 meta-analysis identified insomnia as an important predictor for the onset of depression, anxiety, alcohol abuse and psychosis.20 21 These findings align with the consensus findings (S3, 92%; S4, 95%) on the indirect health implications of untreated chronic insomnia. Regarding the increased risk of accidents associated with chronic insomnia, a 2014 population-based prospective study reported that individuals with sleep difficulties were associated with an increased risk of unintentional fatal injuries and fatal motor vehicle injuries compared with those who never reported insomnia symptoms.22

The economic burden of chronic insomnia is equally substantial. A 2023 RAND Europe study reported that if chronic insomnia were treated effectively across the working-age population, it would increase the UK Gross Domestic Product by as much as £34 billion per year.9 This significant impact on productivity should be recognised by policymakers, and investment is required to optimise the management of chronic insomnia within primary and secondary care. Overall, these data support the recommendation that chronic insomnia should be treated as a stand-alone chronic condition.23

Chronic insomnia should be prioritised accordingly within primary and secondary care services.

Evidence suggests that HCPs do not routinely discuss chronic insomnia. A study conducted in 2021 reported that around 30% of people with trouble sleeping indicated that their primary care physicians (PCPs) never asked about sleep.24 Further research suggests that only 43% of PCPs routinely inquire about sleep.25 This aligns with findings from the current consensus. While practice may differ in the UK, it suggests that chronic insomnia is seen globally as a lower priority than other chronic conditions.

Respondents supported the need for a standardised diagnostic and treatment processes for chronic insomnia (S9, 95%; S12, 79%), suggesting recognition of the gap in care and the urgency for streamlined management approaches. Previous studies highlight the significance of proactive screening to discover undiagnosed chronic insomnia.26 The current consensus reinforces this, with respondents in agreement that incorporating sleep-related screening questions during routine healthcare appointments is critical. Current results also suggest a disparity between awareness and practice, with respondents disclosing that they do not consistently screen for sleep issues. Literature reports that 52% of HCPs do not regularly screen for insomnia, and 51% cite time constraints as a barrier to addressing sleep-related concerns.15 These findings corroborate the current consensus and indicate that primary care services have a key role to play in improving detection and treatment of chronic insomnia and that capacity needs to be improved to allow this to take place.

Respondents did not agree that stigma surrounding chronic insomnia exists in community care settings, which suggests an increasing normalisation of help-seeking behaviour among patients regarding sleep issues. This aligns with recent research, which found positive attitudes towards psychological interventions for insomnia, with reports of a societal shift in attitudes towards seeking psychological support.27 Combined, these results suggest that patients are seemingly willing to discuss their sleeping difficulties and, consequently, HCPs should feel empowered to ask patients questions about sleep.

Updated guidelines and further HCP education on chronic insomnia are required.

The study results suggest that guidance on chronic insomnia could be improved. The 2019 British Association for Psychopharmacology (BAP) recommendations, the 2023 European Insomnia Guidelines, and the current NICE clinical knowledge summary all support the use of CBT-I as first-line treatment, followed by pharmacological intervention if CBT-I is ineffective.6 13 28 This guidance may not be specific to primary care and may be too generalised to address the specific patient needs. For example, according to existing guidelines, many individuals may present with classical symptoms but fail to meet the formal diagnostic criteria for chronic insomnia, which require the presence of impaired daytime functioning or well-being.29 30 The DSM-5 classification defines insomnia as sleep disturbances occurring on three or more nights per week, potentially excluding those with more sporadic sleep loss who may still experience a significant burden.31 32 Furthermore, treatment options for chronic insomnia in primary care have evolved significantly since guidelines were published. This highlights the need for updated primary care guidelines in the UK, which are tailored to the needs of HCPs and address the gap in management of chronic insomnia.

All chronic insomnia patients should have access to CBT-I and pharmacological treatment, which should be administered according to an individualised plan.

Statements 17, 18 and 19 highlight barriers to access to CBT-I in the UK. Respondents agreed that there is a general lack of understanding about the role of CBT-I, underscoring the need for wider HCP education on effectively administering CBT-I. Unfortunately, CBT-I is routinely unavailable to NHS patients, and there are no current plans to train therapists to deliver this treatment.33 According to a freedom of information request made by The Sleep Charity, CBT-I is made available to patients at just 12% of the 132 NHS Trusts that responded.34 Based on the current consensus, it is recommended that training for HCPs, particularly GPs, in this area could be beneficial. Furthermore, the authors suggest that research comparing the effectiveness of digital CBT-I and face-to-face CBT-I is warranted, and if necessary, a plan should be created to ensure consistent and effective delivery of CBT-I across the UK.

Where CBT-I is ineffective or not suitable (eg, in patients that do not have a good command of English or have learning difficulties, patients with a comorbid illness or patients that struggle to engage with digital CBT-I methods), pharmacological treatment is recommended.4 35 Current results indicate strong agreement that pharmacotherapy should be accessible for patients and that care should be administered in a stepwise approach as outlined within NICE guidance (S21, 93%).4 28 33 Respondents believed that further education and increased resource is needed to improve the management of chronic insomnia. Integrated care systems and health boards should prioritise raising awareness of treatment options, to enable patients to make informed decisions about their care. Additionally, increased investment in chronic insomnia management is needed to support HCPs in delivering personalised treatment.

Education/training on chronic insomnia should be included in all clinical schedules and policymakers should invest in its management within primary and secondary care.

There was very strong consensus (S35, 96%) from respondents that the management of chronic insomnia within the NHS needs to be optimised. Improved training addressing gaps in identification, diagnosis and management is required.36 Experts in the current consensus highlighted that NICE guidelines are complex and that HCPs require more straightforward tools to diagnose patients. Easy-to-interpret flowcharts have been suggested in the literature and received feedback that they would positively impact patient management.15 Such research could provide the basis for local guidance aimed at PCPs, to help bridge the gap which exists between primary care management and guidance from NICE and further international bodies.

Respondents agreed (S29, 96%) that educational curricula should include chronic insomnia and other sleep disorders and believe that chronic insomnia should be treated with the same level of importance (S38, 90%). Incorporating chronic insomnia into medical education programmes would mark a significant step towards recognising its importance compared with other long-term health conditions. It should be noted that any training must be recognised by the NHS and implemented by local integrated care and health boards (S36, 90%; S37, 91%).

Strengths and limitations

This consensus project achieved consensus from a large sample size of 201 respondents with a diverse range of roles. It included responses from primary, secondary and community care settings to ensure representation across all HCPs who manage patients with insomnia. The use of a third party to manage anonymous questionnaire distribution and subsequent collection of results reduced responder selection bias. Consensus was achieved for 37/39 statements, providing a strong evidence base for the given recommendations. Despite a large sample, the study only included HCPs from the UK, which may limit the application of these findings to other countries. When comparing across the UK, results were predominantly from England, which limited comparisons with the devolved nations and may limit generalisability across the UK. Self-reported data can also be subject to recall bias and subjectivity. The survey was only completed by HCPs, and the patient perspective may be helpful in strengthening the utility of these findings and driving patient-centred care. As with all Delphi processes, participants may have adjusted their responses to align with perceived group consensus, which could lead to bias and impact the accuracy of the results. However, differences noted in the sub-analyses by region, role and years of experience support the validity of the consensus.

Conclusions

This consensus study highlights the urgent need to reframe the management of chronic insomnia within UK healthcare. Despite its significant health and economic burden, chronic insomnia remains under-recognised and under-treated across primary and secondary care. These results support the need for chronic insomnia to be recognised as a stand-alone condition and for ongoing investment to help improve clinician education on appropriate diagnosis and management strategies. Routine screening for sleep disorders, particularly in patients with long-term conditions, is critical for addressing underdiagnosis and improving treatment outcomes.

Updated clinical guidelines on the management of chronic insomnia are needed alongside concerted efforts to raise public awareness about chronic insomnia and available management options, to empower patients to make informed treatment decisions. Lastly, optimisation of chronic insomnia management within the NHS is central to addressing the current gap between evidence-based recommendations and clinical practice. By improving access to both CBT-I and pharmacological intervention, healthcare services can better meet the needs of patients, reduce the economic burden of untreated insomnia by improving productivity and, critically, improve population health outcomes.
