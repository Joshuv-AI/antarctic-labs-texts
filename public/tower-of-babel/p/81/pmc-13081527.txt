Pre-trigger risk scoring for moderate-to-severe ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome in in vitro fertilization and intracytoplasmic sperm injection cycles
Authors: Lina He, Wei Chen, Jie Lin, Li Hao, Fuming Wan, Yanhong Fu, Qiongyu Wu, Xu Ran, Zhengwen Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop a practical, pre-trigger risk scoring system using routinely available clinical parameters to predict moderate-to-severe ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) in in vitro fertilization and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (IVF/ICSI) cycles.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed women undergoing their first fresh IVF/ICSI cycle between January 2018 and July 2025. Only pre-trigger variables were included. Univariable logistic regression identified candidate predictors, which were entered into multivariable logistic regression with backward selection. Regression coefficients were converted into an integer-based risk score. Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, calibration, and internal bootstrap validation.ResultsA total of 1,641 women were included, of whom 125 (7.6%) developed moderate-to-severe OHSS. Younger age, higher AMH, lower FSH and E2, elevated LH/FSH ratio, and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) were independently associated with OHSS. These six predictors were incorporated into a 0–13 point scoring system. OHSS incidence increased progressively with higher scores, enabling clinically meaningful stratification into low-, moderate-, high-, and very high-risk categories. The model demonstrated good discrimination (AUC 0.84; 95% CI 0.81–0.87) and excellent calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.61). Bootstrap validation confirmed robust performance with minimal optimism. The score retained predictive utility in both PCOS and non-PCOS subgroups.ConclusionWe developed a simple, pre-trigger risk scoring system that reliably predicts moderate-to-severe OHSS prior to final oocyte maturation, supporting proactive prevention and individualized stimulation strategies. Introduction

Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) remains one of the most serious iatrogenic complications of assisted reproductive technology (ART), associated with considerable morbidity and, although rarely, mortality. Despite substantial advances in stimulation protocols and trigger strategies, moderate-to-severe OHSS still occurs in approximately 3–8% of in vitro fertilization/intracytoplasmic sperm injection (IVF/ICSI) cycles, with severe disease reported in 0.5–2% of women undergoing controlled ovarian hyperstimulation (COH) [1]. Its clinical manifestations range from abdominal discomfort to life-threatening complications such as massive ascites, pleural effusion, thromboembolism, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and renal failure [2, 3].

The syndrome is driven primarily by increased vascular permeability mediated by vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) released from hyperstimulated ovaries, with human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) acting as a key precipitating trigger, either exogenously administered for final oocyte maturation or endogenously produced after implantation [4, 5]. These pathophysiological insights have led to preventive approaches including cycle cancellation, coasting, gonadotropin dose adjustment, GnRH agonist triggering, and elective embryo cryopreservation [6]. However, their effectiveness critically depends on timely identification of women truly at risk.

Multiple patient- and treatment-related predictors of OHSS have been identified, including young age, low body mass index (BMI), polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), high antral follicle count (AFC), elevated anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), rapid estradiol rise, and extensive follicular development [7–9]. Among them, PCOS consistently represents one of the strongest determinants due to exaggerated ovarian response and heightened VEGF-mediated vascular permeability. Although the Rotterdam criteria provide standardized diagnostic definitions, their systematic integration into structured OHSS risk stratification remains limited in routine practice [6, 10].

Several prediction models have been developed; however, clinical uptake has been constrained by key methodological limitations. Many rely on trigger-day parameters such as estradiol levels, progesterone, or mature follicle counts, which—despite strong predictive performance—are available too late to meaningfully modify stimulation strategy [11, 12]. Other models are overly complex, require sophisticated computation, or lack external validation, restricting generalizability and bedside applicability [13, 14].

Contemporary guidelines from ESHRE and ASRM emphasize individualized stimulation strategies with risk assessment as a central component of clinical decision-making. An optimal prediction tool should therefore rely on pre-trigger parameters, offer robust discrimination and calibration, remain simple enough for routine clinical use, and provide actionable risk stratification supporting preventive intervention planning [15].

The present study aimed to develop a practical, integer-based risk scoring system using exclusively pre-trigger clinical variables to predict moderate-to-severe OHSS in IVF/ICSI cycles. By enabling early recognition of high-risk patients, this tool is intended to support proactive prevention strategies, enhancing patient safety without compromising treatment efficacy.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective study at Zigong Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital. The study population comprised all women who underwent their first fresh IVF or ICSI cycle between January 1, 2018, and July 31, 2025. Eligible participants were women aged 20 to 45 years undergoing COH for autologous oocyte retrieval with planned fresh embryo transfer or elective embryo cryopreservation. Cases with cycle cancellation before oocyte retrieval were excluded. Patients with missing OHSS severity data or incomplete information on key predictor variables were also excluded from the analysis. This study was conducted after receiving approval from the Institution’s Ethics Committee of Zigong Maternal and Child Health Care Hospital (Ethics number: 2024IECA05).

Clinical protocols and procedures

All patients underwent comprehensive baseline evaluation including transvaginal ultrasound for AFC measurement on cycle day 2–3 and hormonal assessment comprising follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), estradiol (E2), testosterone, and AMH. Physical examination included measurement of height and weight for body mass index calculation. Ovarian stimulation protocols were selected according to institutional guidelines based on patient characteristics, including age, ovarian reserve markers, and diagnosis. The protocols employed included GnRH antagonist protocol, long GnRH agonist protocol with either luteal phase or follicular phase initiation, mild stimulation protocol, and progestin-primed ovarian stimulation protocol. Recombinant FSH, urinary FSH, or human menopausal gonadotropin was used for controlled ovarian hyperstimulation, with starting doses and subsequent adjustments determined by the treating physician based on ovarian response.

Follicular development was monitored through serial transvaginal ultrasound examinations and serum hormone measurements according to standard practice. When at least three follicles reached 17 mm or greater in mean diameter, final oocyte maturation was triggered using hCG (5,000–10,000 IU), GnRH agonist (triptorelin 0.2 mg), or dual trigger combining both agents. The choice of trigger method was based on ovarian response characteristics and perceived OHSS risk. Transvaginal ultrasound-guided oocyte retrieval was performed 36 to 38 h after trigger under conscious sedation or general anesthesia. Retrieved oocytes underwent conventional IVF or ICSI according to semen parameters and clinical indication. Embryo culture was performed using sequential media, and embryo quality was assessed according to standard morphological criteria.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome of interest was moderate-to-severe OHSS occurring within 14 days following oocyte retrieval, representing early OHSS. We classified OHSS severity as Grade 1 to 4 according to modified Golan criteria [16], which stratify severity based on clinical symptoms, ovarian enlargement, presence of ascites, and systemic complications such as hemoconcentration and pleural effusion. We combined Grade 2 and above as moderate-to-severe OHSS to identify clinically significant cases requiring medical intervention or hospitalization. Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) was diagnosed according to the Rotterdam diagnostic criteria [17].

Predictor variable selection and measurement

To ensure clinical applicability and enable truly preventive interventions, we deliberately restricted our analysis to predictor variables available before trigger administration. The candidate predictors evaluated included age, body mass index (BMI), duration of infertility, baseline FSH, LH, E2, and testosterone concentrations, serum AMH level, AFC counted during baseline ultrasound, LH/FSH ratio calculated by dividing LH by FSH, and PCOS diagnosis status.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Intergroup comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or chi-square test as appropriate. Variables associated with the outcome (p < 0.05) in univariable logistic regression were included in a multivariable model. The final model was constructed using backward stepwise selection based on the Akaike Information Criterion, chosen to achieve a parsimonious and clinically interpretable model suitable for transformation into an integer-based risk scoring system, while retaining predictors supported by both statistical significance and biological plausibility. Multicollinearity was assessed. An integer-based risk scoring system was developed by transforming the regression coefficients from the final model. Patients were stratified into risk categories based on their total score. Model discrimination was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Performance metrics, calibration, and clinical utility were assessed. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations. Subgroup analysis was conducted for patients with and without PCOS, and sensitivity analyses were performed to test robustness. In addition, to address potential heterogeneity introduced by different stimulation protocols, a sensitivity analysis restricted to GnRH antagonist cycles was conducted. All analyses and clinical risk calculator development were conducted using R software (version 4.3.0), with statistical significance set at a two-sided α of 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics and study population

Among 1,652 eligible patients, 11 (0.7%) were excluded due to missing OHSS severity information, leaving 1,641 patients for analysis. Table 1 presents baseline characteristics. Moderate-to-severe OHSS occurred in 125 patients (7.6%). Of these, 102 patients (6.2%) experienced moderate OHSS and 23 patients (1.4%) developed severe OHSS. No OHSS-related deaths occurred during the study period. All patients with moderate-to-severe OHSS received appropriate medical management, with 18 patients (14.4% of OHSS cases) requiring hospitalization for intravenous fluid resuscitation, electrolyte monitoring, or paracentesis. The mean age was 29.69 ± 3.57 years old in moderate-to-severe OHSS group, and 32.68 ± 4.93 in No/mild OHSS group, mean BMI was 21.5 ± 2.45 kg/m² and 21.88 ± 2.92 kg/m². Regarding ovarian reserve, the OHSS group demonstrated lower baseline FSH (6.6 vs. 8.3 mIU/ml, p < 0.01), higher AMH (7.03 vs. 3.24 ng/ml, p < 0.01), and higher AFC (28.63 vs. 14.78 follicles, p < 0.01). The LH/FSH ratio was significantly elevated in the OHSS group (0.93 vs. 0.58, p < 0.01). Notably, patients who developed OHSS received significantly lower cumulative gonadotropin doses (1,998.40 vs. 2,250.85 IU, p < 0.01). PCOS was substantially more prevalent among patients with OHSS (75.2% vs. 24.20%, p < 0.01).

Multivariable analysis and independent predictors

Univariable logistic regression analysis showed that younger age, lower baseline FSH, higher baseline LH, higher AMH, elevated LH/FSH ratio, and PCOS diagnosis were all significantly associated with moderate-to-severe OHSS (all p < 0.05). In the multivariable logistic regression model, several predictors remained independently associated with OHSS risk (Table 2). Increasing age remained strongly protective, with an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of 0.92 per year (95% CI, 0.87–0.97; p = 0.004). Higher AMH continued to demonstrate a robust positive association with OHSS (aOR, 1.11 per ng/ml; 95% CI, 1.04–1.20; p = 0.003). An elevated LH/FSH ratio was also independently associated with increased risk (aOR, 1.90; 95% CI, 1.19–3.04; p = 0.008), and PCOS diagnosis emerged as one of the strongest predictors (aOR, 2.81; 95% CI, 1.55–5.10; p = 0.001). Lower baseline FSH levels were independently protective (aOR, 0.86; 95% CI, 0.76–0.97; p = 0.015), whereas serum estradiol demonstrated a borderline association toward reduced risk (aOR, 0.98 per pg/ml; 95% CI, 0.97–1.00; p = 0.004). BMI did not retain statistical significance in the adjusted model (p = 0.094).

Risk scoring system and risk stratification

To enhance clinical interpretability and applicability, continuous predictors were categorized before conversion into integer scores. Cut-off values were not only determined according to the relative magnitude of regression coefficients to preserve predictor weighting, but also the clinically meaningful thresholds commonly used in reproductive endocrinology practice. Moreover, the clinical applicability also one of the reasons for the determination. We tried to avoided purely data-driven or overly complex cut-points to improve generalizability and practical implementation.

We translated the multivariable logistic regression coefficients into a simple integer-based risk scoring system ranging from 0 to 13 points as presented in Table 3. OHSS rates increased progressively across the score range, demonstrating a clear dose-response relationship (p for trend < 0.01 by Cochran-Armitage test). Based on score distribution, clinical interpretability, and performance characteristics, we stratified patients into four risk categories (Table 4; Fig. 1A). The low-risk category comprised 707 patients (52.2%) with OHSS rate of 1.7%. The moderate-risk category included 353 patients (26.1%) with OHSS rate of 7.6%. The high-risk category encompassed 273 patients (20.2%) with OHSS rate of 23.4%. The very high-risk category contained 21 patients (1.6%) with OHSS rate of 38.1%. This stratification revealed a tenfold gradient in OHSS risk between the lowest and highest risk categories, providing clinically meaningful separation for guiding management decisions.

Model performance assessment and validation

As shown in Fig. 1B, the risk scoring system demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.84 (95% CI, 0.81–0.87). Performance evaluation across different thresholds confirmed a clear balance between sensitivity and specificity (Fig. 1C). A score of ≥ 7 points was selected as the optimal cut-off, yielding a sensitivity of 72.1%, specificity of 77.2%, positive predictive value of 22.0%, negative predictive value of 96.9%, and an overall accuracy of 76.8%. Notably, the high negative predictive value indicates strong ability of the model to reliably exclude moderate-to-severe OHSS in patients classified as low risk.

Model calibration was shown in Fig. 1D. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test yielded a chi-square statistic of 7.15 with a non-significant p value of 0.61, indicating no evidence of lack of fit. The calibration plot comparing predicted and observed OHSS probabilities across deciles of predicted risk demonstrated close agreement throughout the risk range, with observed event rates closely following the 45-degree line of perfect calibration. These findings indicate that predicted probabilities derived from the model accurately reflect true OHSS risk in this population.

The validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples demonstrated robust model performance. The bootstrap-corrected AUC was 0.85 with a 95% percentile confidence interval of 0.81–0.88, very close to the apparent AUC (0.84), with a minimal optimism estimate of 0.005. These results indicate negligible overfitting and suggest that the discriminative performance of the model is likely to be preserved in similar clinical populations.

Subgroup analysis by PCOS status

Among 461 (28.1%) patients with PCOS, 94 (75.2%) developed moderate-to-severe OHSS, compared with 367 of 1,516 non-PCOS patients (24.2%), yielding a risk ratio of 7.85. This confirms PCOS as a major risk determinant for OHSS. The risk scoring system retained discriminative ability within both subgroups, although performance was modestly attenuated in PCOS patients, with an AUC of 0.64 compared with 0.77 in non-PCOS patients.

Sensitivity analysis restricted to GnRH antagonist cycles

To minimize potential confounding by stimulation protocol heterogeneity, we performed a sensitivity analysis limited to women undergoing GnRH antagonist cycles only. Multivariable logistic regression demonstrated results highly consistent with the primary analysis. Younger age, lower E2 and FSH, higher AMH, elevated LH/FSH ratio, and PCOS remained independently associated with moderate-to-severe OHSS, with comparable effect sizes (Supplementary Table s1).

Clinical implication

Based on the graded risk stratification and its clear correspondence with OHSS incidence, we propose a practical management framework to assist clinicians in tailoring ovarian stimulation strategies according to individualized risk levels. Suggested management approaches for each risk category are summarized in Table 5. In addition, we developed a user-friendly clinical risk calculator based on this scoring system, the interface of which is shown in Fig. 2, and the web-based tool for research and educational purposes only is available at cweimed.shinyapps.io/shiny. No patient-identifiable information is collected or retained by the system.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a clinically pragmatic, pre-trigger risk scoring system capable of identifying women at elevated risk for moderate-to-severe OHSS during IVF/ICSI cycles. Our findings suggest that OHSS is predictable once key biological and clinical signals available early during stimulation are appropriately synthesized. Importantly, by restricting model inputs to variables measurable prior to final oocyte maturation, this tool addresses a long-recognized gap in OHSS prevention—namely that many existing models rely on trigger-day estradiol or follicular metrics, which, although highly predictive, become available too late to meaningfully influence stimulation planning or trigger strategy. This aligns with evolving preventive paradigms advocated by both ESHRE and ASRM, which emphasize early identification of high-risk women to facilitate individualized mitigation strategies rather than reacting once risk has already materialized [15, 18, 19].

The identification of age, AMH, LH/FSH ratio, and PCOS as independent predictors is biologically plausible and concordant with prior literature. Younger age has consistently been associated with heightened ovarian responsiveness and OHSS susceptibility due to greater follicular sensitivity to gonadotropins and more robust angiogenic signaling [1]. AMH, reflecting the growing follicular cohort, has emerged as perhaps the single most powerful ovarian reserve marker linked to OHSS risk, with multiple studies demonstrating strong associations between elevated AMH and both excessive ovarian response and moderate-to-severe OHSS [8, 20, 21]. Our results corroborate these observations and underscore AMH as a cornerstone parameter in preventive risk assessment. The LH/FSH ratio has traditionally been regarded as a surrogate marker of underlying ovarian endocrine milieu, particularly in women with polycystic ovarian morphology, and elevated ratios have been associated with exaggerated folliculogenesis and VEGF activation, thereby contributing to OHSS development [10]. Moreover, PCOS has been shown to be one of the strongest clinical determinants of OHSS, mediated through intrinsic follicular hypersensitivity, chronic hyperandrogenic and hyperinsulinemic environments, and amplified VEGF signaling [22–24]. Our subgroup observations further highlight that even within PCOS populations, risk is not homogenous, supporting refined stratification rather than categorical labeling of “high-risk” solely based on diagnosis.

In our final multivariable model, baseline E2 showed a statistically significant inverse association with OHSS risk, a finding that warrants careful interpretation. It is essential to distinguish baseline E2, which reflects the background endocrine status, from peak E2, which reflects the acute follicular mass at trigger. While higher baseline E2 often signals premature follicle recruitment or diminished reserve, a lower baseline E2 identifies a quiescent but highly primed ovarian state. When integrated with high AMH and an elevated LH/FSH ratio, this low E2 phenotype, often seen in PCOS patients, represents a highly reactive state prone to synchronized and exaggerated responses to stimulation, thereby increasing OHSS risk.

Beyond the statistical performance, the clinical relevance of this scoring system lies in its interpretability and actionable granularity. The clear gradient of OHSS incidence across score categories offer meaningful discrimination that can feasibly inform decision-making in daily practice. In particular, the very high negative predictive values at clinically relevant thresholds suggest the tool may be used not only to identify candidates for intensified preventive interventions but also to reassure and avoid unnecessary overtreatment among women at genuinely low risk. This is particularly important as the field continues to emphasize safety while preserving treatment efficacy and patient experience. The proposed management suggestions are based on clinical experience and existing practice patterns. They are intended to provide practical guidance and were not formally tested or validated within the present analysis. Meanwhile, women classified in high or very high-risk strata may benefit from early counseling, tailored gonadotropin dosing, preference for antagonist protocols, judicious consideration of GnRH-agonist triggering, or elective cryopreservation strategies, as supported by prior interventional evidence [19, 25–27]. Thus, the score has the potential to shift OHSS prevention from reactive rescue toward rational, proactive planning.

However, several limitations warrant careful consideration. First, this study is retrospective in design, and although rigorous statistical methodology and validation were applied, residual confounding and selection bias cannot be completely excluded. In particular, the number of women who developed moderate-to-severe OHSS, although comparable to real-world prevalence data, remained relatively limited, which may introduce statistical instability for extreme risk categories; this is especially relevant for the very high-risk group, where case counts were small, resulting in wider uncertainty around risk estimates and limited precision. Because some cycles may have been cancelled due to excessive response or early OHSS symptoms, selection bias cannot be excluded and the model may underestimate risk in settings with different cancellation practices. Second, although bootstrap internal validation demonstrated stable performance with minimal optimism, the lack of external validation remains a major limitation. Prediction models often exhibit performance degradation when applied to independent populations with different clinical practices or patient characteristics. Therefore, the generalizability of our model cannot be assumed, and external validation in prospective, multicenter cohorts is essential before routine clinical implementation. In addition, recalibration of the model coefficients or intercept may be necessary when applied to populations with different baseline risk profiles, stimulation protocols, or clinical practices to ensure accurate risk estimation. Third, this was a single-center study conducted within a defined institutional framework, and stimulation strategies, clinician judgment, and laboratory conditions may differ from those in other regions or healthcare systems. Generalizability therefore requires cautious interpretation until multicenter or prospective studies can corroborate performance. Finally, although the model purposefully prioritized ease of use, additional biologically relevant parameters such as dynamic estradiol kinetics or detailed follicular development markers were not incorporated, and whether their inclusion could further refine prediction deserves exploration in future research.

Despite these limitations, this study provides meaningful contributions to contemporary OHSS prevention strategies. By integrating readily available clinical and biochemical markers into a simple integer-based risk score, we offer a practical tool that may enhance patient counseling, optimize stimulation planning, and meaningfully reduce preventable morbidity. Future priority directions include prospective multicenter validation, integration into electronic clinical decision platforms, and evaluation of whether implementing this score alters clinical outcomes in real-world practice.

In conclusion, this pre-trigger OHSS risk scoring system represents a clinically practical and biologically grounded approach to early risk identification. While further validation is required, it provides a promising framework to support individualized stimulation strategies and potentially improve the safety of IVF/ICSI treatment.
