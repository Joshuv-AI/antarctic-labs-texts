Mineralized sclerites in the gorgonian coral Leptogorgia chilensis as a natural jamming system
Authors: Chenhao Hu, Ravi Tutika, Zhifei Deng, Zian Jia, Liuni Chen, Hongshun Chen, Yang Geng, Xianghui Xiao, Pavel D. Shevchenko, Christoph Pierre, James C. Weaver, Daniel Baum, Michael D. Bartlett, Ling Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceUsing external pressure control, granular systems based on particle jamming can transform quickly from flexible to rigid states, leading to diverse applications in fields such as soft robotics. While particle geometry plays an important role in the mechanics of granular jamming, as it influences both the packing configuration and contact network, optimizing a granular jamming system in terms of weight, robustness, and mechanical tunability remains challenging due to the large available design space of particle geometries. The present study investigates sclerite-based gorgonian skeletons as a natural jammable material system, which offers insights for designing particle geometries for granular jamming with improved mechanical strength and structural efficiency.

Granular materials, such as sands and grains, can undergo a transition from a fluid-like to a solid-like state (1, 2). This phenomenon, known as granular jamming, occurs when the particles in the granular assembly, arranged in either random or periodic patterns, reach a critical density, and become locked in a stable configuration (3–5). Recently, researchers have combined this concept with precise pressure control to create systems with high mechanical tunability (6, 7). In this context, loose grains are typically sealed into a closely spaced bag attached to a vacuum actuation system. In the unjammed state, the particle assembly is soft and malleable. However, upon applying a confining vacuum, the assembly undergoes the transition to granular jamming, becoming stiffened and strengthened. This simple and versatile mechanically tunable approach has found applications in soft robotics designs, including soft grippers (8–10), minimally invasive surgical tools (11–13), and wearable joint supports (14, 15). The ability to effectively tune the stiffness and strength in these systems is thus critical to their adaptability, functionality, and performance.

The mechanical properties, including stiffness and strength, of a jammed granular assembly are influenced by several factors, including particle geometry and arrangement, interparticle friction, external conditions (i.e., confining pressure and applied shear stress), and the intrinsic mechanical properties of the constituent particles (16). Among these factors, particle geometry plays a critical role as it relates to packing fraction (i.e., the volume occupied by particles in the assembly) and contact network formation (i.e., the intricate network formed based on how the particles interact with one another) (17). Traditionally, sand particles and coffee grounds (8, 9) have been used in granular jamming studies due to their fine sizes and ease of accessibility. However, due to their irregular and variable shapes, it is often challenging to conduct mechanistic analyses of their jamming behaviors. Spherical particles, on the other hand, exhibit identical and highly controlled geometries, but they produce interparticle sliding and rotation (18), often leading to insufficient jamming performance. Recently, research efforts have been directed to investigate granular assemblies based on more complex particle geometries that aim to improve contact stability and suppress particle sliding. Such shape-induced interparticle contact enhancement is known as geometric cohesion, a concept first proposed in 1996 by using rod-shaped particle assemblies (19), and subsequently being extended to a variety of convex and nonconvex geometries. For example, convex particles such as cubes (20), tetrahedra (21), dodecahedra (22, 23), and other highly faceted polyhedral shapes (20, 22, 23) have been shown to exhibit enhanced resistance to interparticle slipping by replacing point-to-point interactions with face-to-face contacts, thus significantly enhancing the compressive modulus and strength of the granular assembly (22). Nonconvex geometries, including staples (24–26), Z-shaped particles (27), star-shaped particles (28, 29), and poly-pods (20, 30), achieve enhanced strength via strong particle interlocking and entanglement (20, 24, 25, 29). Beyond gross particle geometry, recent studies also highlight the influence of fine surface features. For example, the presence of microasperities on particle surfaces has been shown to increase the average number of particles in contact, thus enhancing the stiffness and strength of the jammed assembly (31).

Unlike classical phase transformations, in which the material structure is determined by thermodynamically favored “ground states”, a jammed granular system depends on local aggregation and “architecture” of the contact network of particles, representing an athermal system far from classical thermodynamic equilibrium (16). Therefore, a general relationship between the mechanical response of a jammed granular system and the constituent particles’ shapes is largely unknown, and the design space of granular materials with a desired mechanical response is vast (16). Consequently, the “inverse design” of granular particle geometries that meets specific jamming performance criteria has proved to be a rather difficult endeavor. Recently, Miskin and Jaeger proposed an effective artificial evolution algorithm to conduct a high-throughput exploration of granular designs, although the particle geometries were limited to equal-size rigidly bonded spheres (17).

To expand the realm of rationally designed jammable particle geometries for engineering applications, biological systems based on discrete element-based skeletal systems with tunable stiffness may offer important design inspirations. Particularly attractive for such efforts are the alcyonaceas, commonly known as soft corals, which are a group of colonial marine invertebrates that are distantly related to stony corals. As the name implies, soft corals lack a fully consolidated skeleton and hence do not build reefs; instead, their skeletons are flexible, which are either based on a homogeneous fleshy tissue (32) (true soft corals) or a core-shell-like structure consisting of a proteinaceous axial skeleton, surrounded by a soft cortex called the coenenchyme (sea fans or gorgonians) (33–36). For both groups of soft corals, the soft tissues can contain millions of small (ranging from sub-100 µm to several mm), hard, calcareous skeletal elements called sclerites (33, 37–39). When exposed to external stimuli, soft corals can rapidly expel water, leading to a denser packing of their sclerites (40). This process increases the stiffness of the colony, similar to the concept of granular jamming-induced mechanotunability.

Although sclerites are composed of calcite, they do not adopt the thermodynamically stable rhombohedral geometry of calcite; instead, they exhibit diverse geometrical variations, which have been grossly categorized into a wide range of morphological categories such as scaphoids, spindles, clubs, radiates, and double-heads (33). Due to their species-specific geometries, sclerites have previously been used as a taxonomic indicator, and in terms of biological functions, the sclerites have been identified to contribute to structural support (33, 34, 39) and predator defense (39, 41, 42). While the mechanical support and stiffness tunability of the sclerite-based skeletal systems have long been recognized, the structural organization of this granular system and how its mechanical performance is associated with specified sclerite geometries remain unclear. In this work, using the red gorgonian coral, Leptogorgia chilensis, as a model system, we elucidate the structure-property relationship of this sclerite-based biological granular jamming system. We combine quantitative multiscale 3D structural analyses, parametric geometrical modeling, 3D printing, mechanical testing, and discrete element simulations to uncover the strategies by which sclerites can achieve lightweight, mechanically robust, and dynamically tunable skeletal systems. Beyond providing insights into the structural complexities of gorgonian skeletal architectures, this work also presents an alternative, bioinspired perspective for the development of innovative synthetic granular jamming-based mechanotunable systems.

Results

Sclerite Morphology and Geometrical Characteristics.

The red gorgonian L. chilensis is found in the subtropical and temperate waters of the western coasts of North and South America. L. chilensis colonies can reach heights of up to ~1 m and are characterized by long, red-colored, and flexible branches (43, 44) (Fig. 1 A and B). Similar to other gorgonians, their skeletons are composed of a pliable proteinaceous axial skeleton (36) surrounded by a sclerite-containing outer coenenchyme layer (Fig. 1 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The axial skeletons of gorgonians primarily consist of a collagen-like (and sometimes mineralized) protein called gorgonin (33), which provides the internal structural integrity of the branched colony (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). The coenenchyme layer primarily consists of two material components: a gelatinous, hydrated organic matrix (the mesogleal gel), and millions of calcitic sclerites embedded within (Fig. 1 C and D). Inside the coenenchyme, there also exists a delicate network of gastrovascular canals and cavities (Fig. 1 C and D), which facilitate communication among the polyps to regulate colony movement and nutrient and gas transport (45). As is the case for other soft corals, gorgonian polyps are characterized by their distinctive eightfold symmetry, and as such, are commonly referred to by their alternative name, the octocorals (Fig. 1 B and C).

In order to perform a detailed analysis of the skeletal system of L. chilensis, we first isolated the individual sclerites and investigated their morphology using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and synchrotron X-ray nano-computed tomography (nano-CT) (Materials and Methods). The mineralized sclerites (40 to 100 µm in size) exhibit a complex yet highly consistent geometry, which is dramatically different from the calcite’s thermodynamically stable rhombohedral geometry (Figs. 1 E–H and 2 A–F). From the side view, each sclerite can be considered a branched cylinder, which is characterized by a central shaft (CS), two sets of side branches (SB), and two axial branches (AB) at the ends of the central shaft (Figs. 1F and 2 A and B). For consistency, here we adopt a cylindrical coordinate system, where the longitudinal (L) and radial (R) directions are aligned with the sclerite’s axial and lateral directions, respectively. When viewed from the L direction, the two sets of side branches exhibit a triradiate pattern with an opposite trihedral arrangement (Figs. 1G and 2 C–E). While the connecting regions between branches are smooth, the tips of the side and axial branches are decorated with protrusions, which are further divided into microscopic spike-like features (Fig. 1H). The base and height of these spikes each measure 0.61 ± 0.07 µm and 0.92 ± 0.16 µm (number of measurements, N = 10), respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). The spikes have sharp tip ends with tip radii of 0.28 ± 0.09 µm (N = 10), which may represent an overestimated value due to the potential slight etching during the removal of the surrounding organic matrix.

The high-resolution nano-CT measurements (voxel size, 20 nm) allow for detailed quantification of the 3D geometry of individual sclerites. Here, we further define and measure the following morphometric parameters (N = 18): h (total length of sclerite, 76.5 ± 8.6 µm), h1 (height of axial branches, 26.2 ± 3.7 µm), h2 (height between two sets of side branches, 23.9 ± 3.0 µm), and b (length of the side branches, 18.7 ± 2.2 µm) (Fig. 2 B and E). The normalized values reveal that the axial branches (AB) (h1/h, 0.34 ± 0.02) are comparable in dimensions to those of the central shaft (CS) (h2/h, 0.31 ± 0.03), but slightly longer than the side branches (SB) (b/h, 0.25 ± 0.03) (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, the interbranch angle between the adjacent side branches within the same side branch set, θ (119.9 ± 10.3°), exhibits a nearly perfect threefold symmetry.

Composition, Crystallography, and Nanomechanical Properties.

To gain additional insights into the constituent mineral of sclerites, we conducted compositional, crystallographic, and nanomechanical measurements on embedded and polished sclerite samples. First, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) revealed four major elements of carbon, oxygen, calcium, and magnesium (Fig. 2G), suggesting that the constituent material of the sclerites is magnesium-enriched calcium carbonate. The magnesium-to-calcium ratio was approximately 12.5 at% (atomic percentage), consistent with the results found in other octocoral species (46–48). This magnesium concentration is comparable to or slightly higher than that observed in other calcite-based invertebrate skeletal structures, such as sea urchin spines (ranging from 4.0 to 5.2 at%) and sea urchin test plates (12.4 at%) (49). No significant elemental compositional variations within the sclerites were observed.

The crystallographic information of the sclerites was obtained by examining the orientation of epitaxially overgrown calcite crystals on the polished sample surface (Fig. 2 H and I). This method has been successfully used in studying other calcium carbonate-based biological materials to examine their polymorphs and crystallographic texture (50). By acquiring SEM images from the selected cross-sections, we found that the overgrown calcite crystals retained the rhombohedral geometry and were coaligned, demonstrating that the underlying substrate material is consistent with predominantly single-crystalline calcite. In addition, for longitudinally polished sclerite surfaces, the longitudinal direction of the sclerites was aligned with [0001], the c-axis of the calcite crystals (Fig. 2H). For transversely polished sclerite surfaces, we observed that the threefold symmetry of calcite is aligned with the threefold side branches (Fig. 2I). More specifically, the three exposed side branches are oriented antiparallel to the basal plane projections of the three ridges formed by the adjacent 101¯4 faces of the crystal (along the negative a*-axes direction), which also reveals that the other set of the side branches are oriented along the positive a*-axes directions. Previous work based on another octocoral species Corallium rubrum suggests that its sclerite side branches follow the 404¯1 directions of calcite, which are the directions along the three ridges formed by adjacent 101¯4 faces (51, 52). Based on the 3D nano-CT analysis, here we treat the side branches from this species as perpendicular extensions from the central shaft, i.e., the side branches are within the basal plane of calcite. As such, the corresponding crystallographic directions of the sclerite side branches in L. chilensis are oriented along 101¯0, i.e., the a*-axes of calcite, similar to the triradiate spicules in the calcareous marine sponges (Porifera, Calcarea) (53, 54).

As denoted by the orange color shaded regions in Fig. 2H, we also observed that the overgrown crystallites at the periphery were not perfectly oriented in the same direction as the ones grown in the interior. Such misalignment is consistent with previous findings of crystallographic misorientations for sclerite spike development in other octocoral species (51, 52). Additionally, after subjecting the sclerite to a mild chemical etching process, we observed that the basic building blocks within the sclerite’s interior displayed a fiber-like texture. These constituent mineral fibers appear to be aligned along the longitudinal direction of sclerites (Fig. 2 J and K), and as such, sclerites can be considered as mesocrystalline calcite composed of fiber-shaped, crystallographically coaligned subunits, consistent with prior studies (55–57).

With regard to mechanical properties, our nanoindentation measurements revealed that the mineralized sclerites exhibited an average reduced modulus (Er) of 56.8 ± 3.5 GPa and a hardness (H) of 3.49 ± 0.23 GPa (Fig. 2L). These values are comparable to those observed in other calcite-based biominerals found in echinoderms (58) and mollusk shells (59, 60). The slightly reduced mechanical properties in the periphery of sclerites were potentially due to artifacts resulting from the reduced mineral thickness below the polished sample surface and were thus removed from the statistical analysis.

3D Organization of Sclerites in L. chilensis.

We next utilized synchrotron X-ray microcomputed tomography (µ-CT) with a voxel size of 0.92 µm to investigate the 3D packing and spatial organization of the sclerites in L. chilensis (Materials and Methods). 3D tomography renderings of a representative branch (diameter, ca. 1.4 mm) revealed the organic axial skeleton (diameter, ca. 0.2 mm), and the outer coenenchyme cortex with densely packed sclerites (Fig. 3 A–E). Within the coenenchyme layer, elliptically shaped cavities can be observed, each measuring 1.07 ± 0.30 mm in length and 0.59 ± 0.94 mm in width. These cavities, together with a canal (diameter, 0.12 to 0.30 mm), were interconnected to form the gastrovascular network, which was further connected to the polyps located at the branch surface (Fig. 3B).

While segmentation of the entire sclerite assembly from the skeleton could be achieved using a simple grayscale thresholding algorithm due to its higher mineral content compared to the surrounding soft tissue, separating individual sclerites from each other was challenging as the sclerites were densely packed and were often in contact with neighboring ones. To address this problem, we utilized a high-throughput, high-fidelity segmentation method based on random-walk distance transform (61) (Materials and Methods and SI Appendix, Fig. S4). This method allowed us to successfully segment, identify, label, and analyze the sclerite assembly at the individual particle level, as shown in some representative volumes where individual sclerites are highlighted in different colors (Fig. 3 A and E).

We first utilized this method to compare the colony branches preserved in “relaxed” (expanded) and “stimulated” (contracted) states (Materials and Methods). We found that the packing fraction of the contracted specimens was slightly higher than that of the expanded specimens (0.33 vs. 0.31 and 0.31 vs. 0.27 for the colony holdfast and branch tip, respectively) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). It is likely that in the living gorgonians, the change in packing fractions is greater than the measured values reported here since the expanded specimens (despite the employment of our MgCl2-based sample preparation protocol (62) and care in specimen handling) might have undergone some contraction before being preserved. In addition, the final sample preservation in 70% ethanol likely also led to some additional dehydration, which might further reduce the spacing among sclerites since the mesogleal gel was originally hydrated (33). Nevertheless, our results (and observations of the living animals by the coauthors) suggest that the sclerite assembly in gorgonian octocorals do indeed undergo changes in the sclerite packing fraction, thus contributing to the tunability of skeletal stiffness.

Using a representative volume (3.34 mm3) taken from the tip of a colony branch in the relaxed state, we next conducted a quantitative analysis of the sclerite assembly in terms of the sclerite morphology, distribution, and organization (Fig. 3 F–K). Within this volume (packing fraction, ca. 0.27), 18,000 sclerites were identified, corresponding to ca. 5000 sclerites per mm3. 77.4% of sclerites had volumes ranging between 5,000 and 45,000 µm3, and the majority of sclerites had an aspect ratio (h/2b) between 1.9 to 2.3 (Fig. 3F). Fig. 3G displays µ-CT reconstructions and corresponding SEM images of sclerite morphologies that represent different combinations of skeletal element volumes and aspect ratios. Assuming all these sclerites undergo the same formation pathway, we hypothesize the following growth stages of sclerites in L. chilensis: the small sclerite seeds (volume <10,000 µm3) first form with a high aspect ratio (2.5 to 2.8), and exhibit underdeveloped side branches and a rough surface throughout the entire sclerite (Stage 1, Fig. 3 F and G). As the sclerite grows, the volume increases to 30,000 µm3 where the aspect ratio reaches a stable magnitude between 2.0 to 2.2 (Stage 2 to 6, Fig. 3 F and G). In addition, the tips of the side and axial branches become more developed and decorated with sharp spikes, while the central shaft becomes smoother. Therefore, it appears that the growth of sclerites first starts by forming the overall architecture and symmetry and then gradually detailing the fine surface features. Last, in addition to sclerites with two sets of side branches, a small proportion exhibits more than two sets of side branches and more-slender shapes (Stage 7, Fig. 3 F and G).

We next investigated the spatial distribution and arrangement of sclerites within L. chilensis. Some representative results include: 1) Smaller (volume < 30,000 µm3) and shorter (h < 60 µm) sclerites were more abundantly found near the surface opening of gastrovascular network, corresponding to where the polyps are located (Fig. 3 H and I). Conversely, the larger (volume > 30,000 µm3) and longer (h > 60 µm) sclerites were distributed away from the polyps. 2) It appears that the longer and more-slender sclerites (aspect ratio > 2.8, h > 100 µm) were concentrated more on the outer surface of the coenenchyme, whereas the shorter and less slender ones were more frequently found at the interior region (Fig. 3 I and J). 3) The number of neighboring sclerites in contact, despite an overall wide distribution ranging from 0 to 20, exhibited a correlation to the distance from the gastrovascular openings. More specifically, the further away the sclerites were from the polyps, the higher the number of neighboring sclerites they tended to have (Fig. 3K). 4) Although the nematic order parameter (63) (a scalar value between 0 to 1 that describes the orientational order of a system, where 0 indicates no preferred orientation and 1 indicates perfect ordering) of 0.17 suggests that the overall sclerite assembly is a disordered system, local coalignment among nearby sclerites was observed, with ca. 64.1% of sclerites having a misorientation angle of less than 60°. Additionally, the sclerites close to the polyps tended to align their longitudinal directions along the tangential periphery of the gastrovascular openings (SI Appendix, Figs. S6–S8).

Parametric Modeling of Sclerites.

To analyze the jamming performance of the sclerite assembly as a potential granular media, we developed a parametric geometrical modeling scheme for sclerite-inspired particles and fabricated mm-sized particles via 3D printing for experimental evaluations. For the geometrical modeling, we chose the most representative sclerite morphology, i.e., a cylindrical shaft with two sets of triradiate side branches (Fig. 2A), as the baseline geometry. With the measured dimensional parameters incorporated, the sclerite geometry was further simplified by using a ball-and-stick model with smoothly interpolated surfaces at the conjunctions. As shown in Fig. 4A, the modeled sclerite geometry (denoted as SG) closely approximates that of the native sclerites when viewed in different orthogonal slices.

(B) Bioinspired sclerite models with geometrical modifications on the number of side branch sets, the side branch length, and the spike coverage, where SG is marked in red and other geometries are marked in blue. (C) 3D-printed granular samples based on the parametrically modeled forms and a set of spherical particles.

With the parametric model, we were able to go beyond the original sclerite geometry by varying structural parameters and subsequently studied the performance efficiency and design strategies of sclerites as granular media. To do so, we focused on three geometrical factors: the number of side branch sets, the side branch length, and the surface spike coverage (Fig. 4B). For the number of side branch sets, we designed models SB-S1 and SB-S3 with one and three sets of side branches, respectively. To investigate the effects of the side branch length, we designed models without side branches (SB-L0) and with side branches two times (SB-L2) and three times (SB-L3) that of the native sclerite geometry. Finally, we investigated the effects of the surface spike coverage and designed three model types based on the original sclerite geometry: no spike coverage (SG), spike coverage on branch tips only (S-BT), and full spike coverage (S-FC). For simplification purposes, the branch tip spikes were modeled as spherical asperities. These parametrically designed sclerite-inspired geometries, along with one set of spherical particles, were fabricated using stereolithography-based 3D printing with a typical size of 10 mm and a volume of 80 mm3 (Fig. 4C). The material used here was a hard plastic (Rigid 4000, Formlabs) with an elastic modulus, strength, and fracture strain of 4.1 GPa, 69 MPa, and 5.3%, respectively (64).

Jamming Performance of the Sclerite-Inspired Particle Assemblies.

We first utilized a triaxial compression test to evaluate the mechanical performance of the 3D-printed particle assemblies, which has been widely used to study the mechanics of granular media (65–67). In a typical test, we placed the particles in a soft, cylindrical sheath and then applied a confining vacuum pressure (σr, 85 kPa) to induce jamming (Fig. 5A). The jammed particle assembly was then quasi-statically compressed uniaxially (Materials and Methods and SI Appendix, Fig. S9), from which the engineering compressive stress (σzz) and strain (εzz) were measured. In triaxial compression tests, the relation between the deviatoric stress (i.e., σzz – σr) and strain (εzz) is routinely used to assess the mechanical performance of the particle assemblies (68). To improve the reproducibility of triaxial compression across repeated trials, we conditioned the particle assembly before testing, following the protocol established by prior work (20) (Fig. 5A and Materials and Methods and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). For all tested particle assemblies, the deviatoric stress-strain curves exhibited a linear elastic regime (εzz < ca. 0.01) and a plastic regime with a reduced slope after reaching the yield stress [σyield, determined by εzz at the intersection of two linear fits at elastic and plastic regimes (20)] (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S11). The same particle assembly showed consistent stress-strain responses across repeated tests (see representative curves for the SG and spherical particle assemblies in Fig. 5B).

To quantitatively compare the mechanical performance of different particle assemblies, we calculated the effective elastic moduli (Eeff) in the elastic regime (20) (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). The calculated Eeff and the packing fractions (ϕ) are summarized in Fig. 5C, from which several observations can be made: 1) In comparison to the spherical geometry, the original sclerite geometry (SG), while exhibiting a lower packing fraction (ϕSG = 0.494, ϕsphere = 0.601), achieved a significantly higher effective modulus (ESG = 32.45 ± 0.24 MPa, Esphere = 28.00 ± 2.20 MPa). In fact, most of the sclerite-inspired systems exhibited higher moduli with reduced packing fractions compared to the sphere assembly, suggesting the advantage of sclerite-like geometries in achieving stiff jammed states. 2) As the number of the side branch sets was increased from one (SB-S1) to three (SB-S3), the elastic moduli reduced from 38.47 ± 1.22 MPa to 30.73 ± 1.24 MPa, accompanied by a decrease in packing fraction from 0.565 to 0.451 (green highlight in Fig. 5C). 3) Regarding the effects of the branch length, the SG assembly was the stiffest in comparison to other configurations (ESB-L0 = 30.73 ± 2.03 MPa, ESB-L2 = 28.88 ± 0.43 MPa, and ESB-L3 = 24.33 ± 0.38 MPa). Moreover, the packing fraction continuously reduced from 0.535 to 0.359 from SB-L0 to SB-L3 (yellow highlight in Fig. 5C). 4) In terms of the effects of surface asperities, while the particles with asperities on branch tips only (S-BT) and with full coverage of asperities (S-FC) exhibited similar stiffness (ES-BT = 33.32 ± 0.43 MPa and ES-FC = 33.34 ± 0.28 MPa), they were both stiffer than the particles without asperities (ESG = 32.45 ± 0.24 MPa), and their packing fractions were similar (~0.5, blue highlight in Fig. 5C). The yield stress (σyield) of various particle assemblies follows a similar trend observed for Eeff (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). This observation that the stiffness and strength of granular assemblies under triaxial compression are generally correlated is consistent with prior studies (20).

In addition to the triaxial compression studies, we performed an indentation-based measurement to further evaluate the jamming properties of the particle assemblies under concentrated loading conditions. This testing scheme was motivated by simulating more biologically relevant loading conditions that the L. chilensis skeleton may experience. Carnivorous fishes, such as some angelfish, butterflyfish, wrasses, and triggerfish, represent common predators of soft corals and often graze on their polyps (42). It has been recognized that sclerites can act as feeding deterrents, and their effectiveness increases with the concentration of sclerites (41). As illustrated in Fig. 5D, we utilized a hemispheric tip (radius, 20 mm) to indent into the compacted particle assemblies inside a rigid cylindrical container (Materials and Methods). As the tip indented into the particle assembly, the indentation force gradually increased, and upon reaching a peak value, the force dropped rapidly (Fig. 5E). This behavior was observed multiple times throughout the entire 10 mm indentation depth, where the number of force cycles was highly dependent on the particle system. As this test lacked confinement and the indentation generated high local shear stresses, the stochastic, sudden force drop was likely to be due to the local failure of the jammed state, in which excessive loads triggered the interlocking breakage and particle rearrangement (flow) (4).

To facilitate more quantitative comparisons, we further calculated two parameters: maximum peak force (Fmax, the highest force value during indentation) and the critical displacement for the initial peak force (dint, the indentation depth where the first jamming breakage occurred). The results regarding various geometrical effects are summarized as follows: 1) The spherical assembly exhibited a significantly smaller Fmax, measuring two orders of magnitude lower than that observed in the SG assembly, and did not show a distinguishable dini due to the constant flow of particles and the lack of geometrical interlocking (Fig. 5 E, i). 2) For the number of branch sets, the particle assembly with more branch sets showed increased Fmax (284.9 ± 58.3 N, 441.5 ± 76.2 N, and 633.7 ± 186.2 N for SB-S1, SG, and SB-S3, respectively) but reduced dint (6.46 ± 0.28 mm, 4.49 ± 0.76 mm, and 4.17 ± 1.14 mm for SB-S1, SG, and SB-S3, respectively) (Fig. 5 E, ii and F). 3) For branch length, increasing branch length (i.e., from SB-L0 to SB-L2) dramatically increased Fmax from 90.2 ± 69.7 N to 887.3 ± 180.9 N but reduced dint from 6.49 ± 1.99 mm to 4.75 ± 0.74 mm (Fig. 5 E, iii and F). 4) For asperity coverage, no significant differences in Fmax or dint were observed; however, for the particles with asperity coverages, the surface bumpiness of these particles produced small force drops during compression force buildup, which is likely due to interlocking breakage at the asperity level (red arrows in Fig. 5 E, iv and F). 5) Post-indentation examinations revealed that many sclerites fractured in the SB-S3 and SB-L2 assemblies (SI Appendix, Fig. S13), and the SB-L2 particles also exhibited a prominent self-interlocking phenomenon even after the indentation load was removed (SI Appendix, Fig. S14).

Particle Interaction Analysis.

We further utilized discrete element modeling (DEM) to identify, quantify, and evaluate the contact modes and networks of the parametrically modeled, sclerite-inspired particles. Following previous studies (69–71), we represented each particle using a simplified geometry composed of connected spherical subunits (multisphere method) to reduce computational cost (Fig. 6A). With the three regions, i.e., central shaft (CS), axial branches (AB), and side branches (SB), identified in a sclerite geometry, six contact types can be classified depending on which two regions from adjacent sclerites are in contact, and they include AB–AB, AB–SB, CS–AB, CS–CS, SB–CS, and SB–SB contacts (Fig. 6 B and C). The number of each contact type (denoted as Nct, e.g., NAB–AB, NAB–SB, etc.) was estimated by counting all individual contacts of that contact type within the particle assembly. Given the complex, nonconvex geometries of the particles, a single pair of particles may form multiple contacts, either of the same or different types. Each of these contacts was counted toward the corresponding Nct, as they all contribute to the overall contact network and mechanical stabilization of the system. The coordination number Cct for each contact type was then calculated using the formula Cct = 2Nct/Np, where Np is the total number of particles in the assembly. The total coordination number Ctotal was defined as the sum across all contact types. This analysis was applied to all particle assemblies, although the particles with surface spikes were not included here due to the high computational costs associated with their complex geometries.

Following the triaxial compression experiments, particles were first randomly packed in a cylindrical space, subjected to a confining hydrostatic pressure of up to 85 kPa, and then compressed in the axial direction (Materials and Methods and SI Appendix, Supplementary Notes). During the increased isotropic confinement from 10 to 85 kPa (σr), we observed an increase in the magnitude of the particle contact force in the SG particle assembly (Fig. 6D), as well as in other parametric modeled particles (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). We observed that the particles with prolonged side branches and multiple branch sets (SB-L2, SB-L3, and SB-S3) experienced more large-magnitude contact forces compared to particles with shorter side branches or fewer branch sets (SG and SB-S1) under the same loading conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). The combination of large contact forces and longer branch lengths likely generated higher moments on the branch-like structures, which could lead to particle fracture (as observed in the indentation experiments). Similar to previous studies, the stress-strain responses of jammed particle assemblies obtained from our DEM simulations were not perfectly aligned with our experimental results at high strain values (SI Appendix, Supplementary Notes) (31, 72). This result is partly due to the fact that parameters such as friction and stiffness at the particle-wall and particle-particle interfaces are difficult to calibrate accurately. Therefore, the focus of these DEM simulations here was to compare the trend of their mechanical responses and to investigate particle-to-particle contact characteristics. As shown in the SI Appendix, Fig. S23, the simulated deviatoric stress-strain curves for all sclerite-inspired particles exhibited the two-regime response, and the trends of moduli calculated from the elastic regime are consistent with our experimental observations. In addition, the effective elastic moduli, Eeff, across all investigated particle assemblies show an overall linear dependence on the product of the total coordination number and packing fraction, Ctotalϕ, in line with Rumpf’s framework (73–76), though with a slope over an order of magnitude smaller than predicted, possibly due to the effects of particle nonconvexity and interlocking (SI Appendix, Fig. S16).

To identify which contact type plays a dominant role in contributing to the jamming state, we evaluated the probability density function of the contact normal forces (excluding tangential force from interparticle contact forces), P(F), for different particle assemblies at different confining pressures. For frictional granular systems, P(F) typically exhibits an exponentially decaying function in an unjammed state; however, upon jamming, a peak or a plateau tends to appear at F/<F> (<F>, average contact normal force) smaller than 1, which is considered a signature of granular jamming (77, 78). As shown in Fig. 6E (for other particle geometries, see SI Appendix, Fig. S17), the as-calculated F/<F> profiles for the SB–SB and AB–SB contacts exhibit prominent peaks when F/<F> <1 under the high confining pressure (σr = 85 kPa) while others do not. This result suggests that these two contact types contribute substantially to the granular jamming in the sclerite assemblies (79).

We monitored the evolution of coordination numbers (Cct and Ctotal) and volumetric strain (εvol) during triaxial compression to investigate their relationship with the mechanical response of the sclerite-inspired particle systems. First, SB–SB and AB–SB contacts consistently dominated (60 to 85%) for all modeled geometries within the elastic regime (Fig. 6F). As these contact types also exhibit prominent peaks or plateaus at F/<F> <1, they likely play a central role in stabilizing the jammed granular assemblies. Second, for most particle geometries (SG, SB-S1, SB-S3, SB-L2, and SB-L3), Ctotal increased with εzz up to ~0.01 to 0.02 before decreasing (Fig. 6G). Correspondingly, εvol first decreased (volume shrinkage) and then increased (volume dilation) (Fig. 6H). The initial rise in Ctotal, coupled with volume contraction suggests enhanced interlocking and force transmission as the assembly became more densely packed during early compression. Beyond this critical strain threshold, however, the increasing shear stress disrupted the contact network, leading to volumetric dilation and a reduction in interparticle contacts (4, 31, 72). The onset of Ctotal reduction also coincided with the yield point in triaxial compression, suggesting a direct link between coordination number evolution and macroscopic mechanical response (SI Appendix, Fig. S18).

Discussion and Conclusions

Biological systems produce a wide range of structural materials to achieve mechanical functions such as skeletal support and protection (80, 81). These composite materials are often static and highly mineralized, with examples including mollusk shells (82), vertebrate tooth enamel and bones (83, 84), and crustacean exoskeletons (85). In contrast, other examples, such as echinoderms (86), armadillos (87), pangolins (88), and chitons (89, 90), have evolved flexible skeletons or protective armors consisting of well-organized and discrete skeletal elements (ossicles, osteoderms, scales, and shell plates, respectively) with specific geometries and arrangements. As these skeletal elements are often interconnected with muscular tissues, they allow the organisms to change their body configurations by adjusting individual elements’ relative positions and orientations, which simultaneously facilitates locomotion and protection. While the intrinsic mechanical properties of the materials in both aforementioned examples remain constant, other species have evolved structural materials with true mechanical tunability. One notable example is the collagen-based mutable connective tissue (MCT) found in echinoderms. The MCT can undergo rapid stiffening by a factor of 10 within seconds (91). This is achieved via reversible cross-linking between adjacent collagen fibrils, which enhances the interfibrillar stress transfer and results in a stiffening effect (92). The sclerite-containing soft coral skeletons, which include L. chilensis studied here, represent another class of dynamic biological structural materials that are based on granular jamming. The jamming of these sclerites is achieved through the contraction of the surrounding gel-like coenenchyme matrix, which enables on-demand skeletal mechanical modulation (40). In the case of the natural jamming system of L. chilensis, the sclerites, acting as a granular media, possess a characteristic branched shaft geometry and much smaller sizes (ca. 0.1 mm) compared to the skeletal units in other biological systems mentioned above. They are densely packed (over 5,000 particles/mm3) and arranged in an overall disorganized configuration without interconnecting muscular tissues.

Our combined analyses revealed that the multiscale geometrical features of sclerites in L. chilensis play a critical role in their jamming behavior. In this species, the sclerite geometry consists of a cylindrical shaft with two sets of triradiate side branches and two axial branches with their tips covered with small spike-like protrusions. From our parametric modeling and mechanical testing studies, we found that nearly all our sclerite-inspired particle variants exhibited improved jamming performance compared to their spherical controls. We also observed that increasing the number of side branches leads to reduced jamming performance, and notably, the geometry with a single set of side branches exhibited the highest modulus. This geometry resembles a triangular bipyramid, which has been previously identified to give the most rigid assembly among the granular geometries formed by five rigidly connected spheres (17). In addition, in terms of the effects of branch lengths, the original sclerite geometry exhibits the highest effective modulus. A similar trend has been observed in artificial designs using a series of “jacks” with varying arm lengths: shorter arms, functioning as “geometric friction”, significantly enhance the stiffness of the particle assembly, whereas doubling or tripling the arm length leads to soft initial loading response due to a concomitant reduction in packing fraction (20). The small spike-like protrusions (asperities) on the tips of the side and axial branches improve the rigidity of the jammed granular assembly, following the mechanism that these surface asperities can facilitate interlocking between particles, restrict particle rotation, and ultimately enhance jamming performance (31). Our DEM simulations further revealed that the contacts between the side and axial branches are the dominant types for achieving the jamming state, an observation that might help explain why the small asperities are only present at the tips of side and axial branches instead of covering the entire sclerite surface.

These observations suggest that the sclerite geometry of L. chilensis collectively integrates several effective strategies to enhance their jamming performance. While there is generally no large-scale (whole colony-level) global ordering of the sclerites, geometrical variations and localized alignment patterns do exist (Fig. 3 H–K), suggesting that this species may exhibit a degree of sclerite morphological plasticity and assembly to achieve specific functional requirements at different locations. For example, we observed a tangential alignment of slightly smaller sclerites around the gastrovascular openings, which may impart increased, directionally dependent flexibility (93), in order to accommodate large localized soft tissue deformations as the colony polyps expand and contract.

The analyses described in the present work also raise additional questions regarding the specific selection pressures that ultimately influenced sclerite geometry in L. chilensis. For example, why are the geometries with a single branch set or longer side branch lengths not preferred even though SB-S1 provides the highest Eeff under triaxial compression, and SB-L2 produces the highest Fmax under indentation? Other biological functions (e.g., directional flexibility or resistance to predatory grazing) could thus also play a role in the selection of the sclerite geometry (e.g., the aspect ratio of SB-S1 is nearly 1, which makes it challenging to achieve an aligned packing configuration for desired mechanical anisotropy, and our indentation experiments revealed that increasing the side branch length increases the risk of sclerite fracture when jammed). Our previous work on the dual-functional (photonic coloration and mechanical protection) exoskeleton of flower beetles suggests that the relative importance of each functional requirement, or functional hierarchy, plays an important role in the evolution of biological materials at the microstructural level (94). Beyond the topic of potential multifunctional requirements, another important consideration is related to the growth of sclerites. Both previous studies (51, 52) and our present work show that the triradiate symmetry of the side branches is associated with the crystallographic symmetry of the constituent calcite. Such morphology-crystallography correlation is a common phenomenon observed in many biomineralized structures (53, 54, 95, 96), and may thus impart an energetic constraint on the potentially exploitable sclerite geometrical design space during sclerite formation.

Finally, it is important to note that a vast diversity in sclerite morphology exists across different soft coral groups: in addition to the branched shaft design described in L. chilensis here [also denoted as dumbbell, double-club, or capstan according to Floquet et al. (52) and Bayer (97)], other morphologies include the scaphoids, spindles, clubs, radiates, scales, and double-heads (33, 97). This diversity in sclerite geometry is further paralleled by an equally impressive diversity in octocoral colony symmetry, three-dimensionality, branching fractality, sclerite numerical density, water volume fraction of the soft tissue, and habitat (32, 42–44), thus providing a vast morphospace for the further investigation of structure-function relations in these dynamically tunable skeletal systems. The integrated workflow described here, which combines quantitative multiscale 3D analysis, 3D printing, and mechanical testing and modeling, represents a powerful approach to address these underlying questions in the contexts of multifunctionality, phylogenetic constraints, and formation pathways. Such large-scale, cross-species studies on octocoral sclerites will not only provide further insights into the design of synthetic granular jamming materials, but will also offer a deeper understanding of how members of this group of colonial marine invertebrates leverage this unique strategy of mechanotunability for their survival.

Materials and Methods

Colonies of L. chilensis were collected from Santa Barbara, CA, and prepared by bleaching to isolate individual sclerites. The sclerite morphology was characterized by synchrotron-based micro- and nanocomputed tomography (µ- and nano-CT) and SEM. Crystallographic orientation was examined via calcite epitaxial overgrowth on polished sclerite sections. Nanoindentation mapping was performed on embedded sclerites to obtain reduced modulus and hardness. Jamming performance was evaluated using triaxial compression and particle bed indentation on stereolithography (SLA) 3D-printed sclerite-inspired particles. DEM simulations (linear contact/frictional model) were conducted with experimentally calibrated parameters to analyze particle interactions under jamming conditions. Detailed protocols, imaging parameters, and simulation conditions are provided in SI Appendix, Methods.
