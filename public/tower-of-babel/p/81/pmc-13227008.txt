Mkrn3, Dlk1, and Mecp2 have distinct hypothalamic expression patterns across pubertal maturation in male and female mice
Authors: Flavia R Tinano, Cinthia G Meireles, Delanie B Macedo, Natalia Boris, Sidney Pereira, Melissa Magnuson, Ana Paula Abreu, Rona S Carroll, Ana Claudia Latronico, Ursula B Kaiser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPubertal onset is driven by the reactivation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis, a process orchestrated by a complex interplay of stimulatory and inhibitory factors and influenced strongly by genetic regulation. In this study, we assessed the expression patterns of 3 genes associated with central precocious puberty (CPP) in humans—Mkrn3, Dlk1, and Mecp2—across pubertal maturation in male and female mice. Hypothalamic regions were collected for mRNA quantification by reverse transcription and real time quantitative polymerase chain reaction and protein measurement by Western blot for all 3 genes; in addition, serum samples were used for Dlk1 quantification by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. Hypothalamic levels of Mkrn3 mRNA and protein, a well-established inhibitor of pubertal onset, were high in early postnatal life and declined prior to puberty onset, consistent with its role in suppressing HPG axis activation. In contrast, Dlk1 mRNA and protein levels were higher in adults than younger mice—an unexpected finding since loss of DLK1 expression advances puberty onset. Conversely, serum Dlk1 decreased progressively with age, suggesting a role for peripheral Dlk1 in pubertal regulation. Mecp2, a key epigenetic regulator implicated recently in puberty regulation, had lower hypothalamic mRNA levels after PND10, whereas protein levels remained unchanged, possibly reflecting splice variant–specific expression or post-transcriptional regulation. These distinct age-dependent expression patterns suggest that Mkrn3 and Mecp2 likely contribute primarily to central HPG axis regulation, whereas Dlk1 originating peripherally may act as a central regulator of puberty. Defining these trajectories offers insights into the molecular control of pubertal timing and potential therapeutic targets for pubertal disorders.

Puberty marks the transition from childhood to adulthood, encompassing the development of secondary sexual characteristics, gonadal maturation, and attainment of reproductive capacity. Central precocious puberty (CPP) is a consequence of premature re-activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis, which leads to an increase in the amplitude and frequency of hypothalamic gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) secretion prior to the expected age [1]. GnRH neurons, located in the preoptic area (POA) of the hypothalamus, are regulated by a complex network of upstream stimulatory and inhibitory neurons, among which 2 populations of kisspeptin neurons are particularly notable. The first population, in the hypothalamic arcuate nucleus (ARC), co-express kisspeptin, neurokinin B (NKB), and dynorphin, collectively referred to as KNDy neurons. These neurons coordinate the pulsatile release of GnRH through the stimulatory actions of NKB and the inhibitory effects of dynorphin [2, 3]. A second population of kisspeptin neurons, located in the hypothalamic anteroventral periventricular nucleus/periventricular nucleus continuum (AVPV), induces the mid-cycle luteinizing hormone (LH) surge and ovulation in female rodents [4].

Over the past 2 decades, the identification of monogenic causes of CPP has unveiled critical pathways involved in normal and abnormal pubertal timing [5]. Among these, loss-of-function mutations in Makorin ring finger 3 (MKRN3) are the most common genetic cause of CPP, while mutations in Delta-like 1 homolog (DLK1) are less prevalent [5, 6]. Recent advances have elucidated the roles and mechanisms of action of MKRN3 in pubertal onset through in vitro and animal studies [7-11]. Studies have demonstrated a decline in hypothalamic Mkrn3 expression in the ARC and AVPV nuclei preceding pubertal onset in male and female mice, consistent with an inhibitory role in HPG axis activation [7, 8]. In contrast, the mechanisms by which DLK1 influences HPG axis maturation are not as well understood. Villanueva et al [12] first demonstrated increased Dlk1 expression in several hypothalamic nuclei, including the ARC, between infantile (PND6) and juvenile/adult male mice (PND20 and PND60). Conversely, in our previous study, we found a decline in circulating serum DLK1/Dlk1 protein concentrations during pubertal maturation in humans—specifically, in girls between Tanner stages III and V and in boys between Tanner stages II and V—and in female mice between weaning and first estrus [13]. These findings support a potential role for peripherally derived DLK1 in the regulation of pubertal onset.

Methyl CpG binding protein 2 (MECP2) mutations and deletions are associated with the neurodevelopmental disorder, Rett syndrome, and with reports of early thelarche and/or CPP among affected girls [14-17]. Recently, loss-of-function mutations in MECP2 were identified in children with CPP, with or without neurologic abnormalities [18]. Expression of Mecp2 has been identified in some regions of the hypothalamus in mice, including the ARC [18], although its expression pattern across pubertal development remains to be investigated.

The aims of this study were to characterize Mkrn3, Dlk1 and Mecp2 expression patterns in the hypothalamus of WT male and female mice at different ages across pubertal maturation. Additionally, because Dlk1, unlike Mkrn3 and Mecp2, is a transmembrane protein whose biologically active form is soluble and detectable in serum, we also measured its circulating levels in these animals at the same developmental stages.

Materials and methods

Animals

Adult WT C57BL/6 male and female mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, MA) and mated to produce offspring. Dlk1 KO mice [19], Mkrn3 KO mice [9], and Mecp2 KO mice [20], available in our laboratory, were also bred and mated. All animal studies were approved by the Brigham and Women's Hospital Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Mice were maintained in a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle and were fed a standard rodent chow diet and water ad libitum. Genotyping was conducted by PCR analyses on genomic DNA isolated from ear biopsies.

Mouse tissue collection

Male and female WT mice at varying stages of postnatal development, corresponding to infantile (PND10 and PND15), juvenile (PND22), peripubertal (PND30) and adult/postpubertal (PND60) stages, were sacrificed by CO2 inhalation. After decapitation, approximately 1 mL of trunk blood was collected at the decapitation site and allowed to coagulate before centrifugation at 1000 × g for 10 minutes. Brains were removed and the hypothalami were isolated by 2 bilateral parasagittal cuts, each 0.5 mm lateral to the midline, taking as limits the posterior margin of the optic chiasm (rostrally) and the anterior margin of the mammillary bodies (caudally), with a dissection depth of approximately 2 mm. Each hypothalamic sample was dissected and divided into 2 parts—ie, the POA and the mediobasal hypothalamus (MBH), including the AVPV and ARC nuclei, respectively. All animals were weighed prior to euthanasia. All tissues and fragments were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored at −80 °C until further processing.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was isolated from the MBH and POA of WT male and female mice using TRIzol (Invitrogen), according to the manufacturer's protocols. Total RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Total RNA (500 ng) was reverse transcribed using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). RT-qPCR assays were performed in duplicate for each sample using iQ™ SYBR® Green Supermix (Bio-Rad), along with corresponding primers (10 pmol/μL), using a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, US). Relative mRNA levels were normalized using hypoxanthine guanine phosphoribosyl transferase (Hprt) as a housekeeping gene and calculated by the comparative threshold cycle (Ct) method, according to the formula (2−ΔΔCt) [21]. A sample from a male PND10 mouse was used as the control for normalization and included in all PCR reactions. Primer sequences used included: Mkrn3 (5′–3′ AAGCGCATACTGGCATCAAG and 3′–5′ AGCCAACGGTCATCAGAGAA), Dlk1 (5′–3′ AGTGCGAAACCTGGGTGTC and 3′–5′ GCCTCCTTGTTGAAAGTGGTCA), Mecp2 (5′–3′ CCACTGAAGTTTAAGAAGGCGA and 3′–5′ GTCCCCGGTCACGGATAAT), and Hprt (5′–3′ CCTGCTGGATTACATTAAAGCGCTG and 3′–5′ GTCAAGGGCATATCCAACAACAAAC).

Western blot analysis

Total protein was isolated from the MBH and POA of WT male and female mice using RIPA mammalian cell lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After quantification, 10 µg of soluble proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE on 10% polyacrylamide gels under reducing conditions at 4 °C and constant voltage (100 V). Proteins were then transferred for 90 minutes at 4 °C and 170 mA onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore Corp., Bedford, Ma, USA). Nonspecific binding was blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk diluted in Tris-buffered saline with 0.05% of Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 hour at room temperature on a platform rocker. Membranes were then incubated with a mouse anti-Mkrn3 antibody (1:1000; RRID:AB_10603873), anti-Pref1 antibody (1:1000; RRID: AB_3659225) and anti-Mecp2 antibody (1:1000; RRID:AB_2144004) in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C with gentle shaking. Blots were washed with TBST and then incubated for 1 hour with anti-rabbit IgG horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibody (1:1000; RRID:AB_3083796). After washing with TBST, the blots were developed with an enhanced chemiluminescence system and visualized by autoradiography. For normalization purposes, blots were stripped using stripping buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and probed with anti–β-actin antibody (1:10 000; RRID:AB_262011). The molecular mass was approximated using a Kaleidoscope 250 kD (Bio-rad 1610375) molecular weight ladder. The specificity of the bands obtained were confirmed using protein samples from Dlk1 KO mice [19], Mkrn3 KO mice [9], and Mecp2 KO mice [20].

Measurement of serum Dlk1

Serum was obtained after centrifugation of whole blood samples isolated from WT mice at 1000 × g for 10 minutes. Serum samples from male and female PND60 Dlk1 KO mice frozen at −80 °C available from previous studies [19] were used as a negative control. Serum Dlk1 was then measured by enzyme immunoassay (ELISA; RRID:AB_3095182), following the manufacturer's instructions. The sensitivity of the assay was 0.0045 ng/mL, inter-assay coefficient of variability was <12% and intra-assay variability <10%, according to manufacturer's information.

Statistical analyses

All data are expressed as the mean ± SEM for each experimental group. For experiments with 2 comparisons, a 2-tailed unpaired Student t test was used for statistical analysis. For experiments with multiple comparisons, 1-way or 2-way ANOVA tests, followed by Tukey's post hoc test, were used to assess variation among experimental groups. Significance level was set at P < .05. All analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism Software, Inc.

Results

Hypothalamic Mkrn3, Dlk1, and Mecp2 mRNA levels throughout pubertal development

Mkrn3 mRNA levels in both the MBH and POA were highest at PND10, the earliest age studied, with a significant decline observed by PND15 and a further decrease by PND22 in both male and female mice. From PND22 onward (ie, at PND30 and PND60), levels continued to decrease, although no significant differences were observed among these later ages. At all ages studied, Mkrn3 mRNA levels were similar between males and females (Fig. 1A and 1B).

In male mice, Dlk1 mRNA levels in the MBH were significantly higher at PND60 compared to PND22, while female mice exhibited significantly higher Dlk1 mRNA levels in the MBH at PND22 and PND30 compared to PND10 and PND15. In the POA, male mice had significantly higher Dlk1 mRNA levels at PND60 compared to PND10, PND15, and PND22, whereas no significant differences were observed across ages in female mice. Additionally, Dlk1 mRNA levels were higher in the MBH of females compared to males at PND22, and higher in the POA of males compared to females at PND60 (Fig. 1C and 1D).

In male mice, Mecp2 mRNA levels were significantly higher at PND10 compared to PND15, PND22, and PND30, and higher at PND60 than at PND15 and PND22 in the MBH. In female mice, Mecp2 mRNA levels were significantly higher at PND10 than at all other evaluated ages in the MBH. In the POA of both male and female mice, Mecp2 mRNA levels were significantly higher at PND10 compared to all other ages. Mecp2 mRNA levels were higher in the MBH of males compared to females at PND60, and higher in the POA of females compared to males at PND22 (Fig. 1E and 1F).

For these analyses, 5 to 8 mice of each age and sex group were used. There were no significant differences in weights between male and female mice of the same age (P > .05; data not shown).

Hypothalamic levels of Mkrn3, Dlk1, and Mecp2 protein throughout pubertal development

Mkrn3 protein was undetectable by Western blot analysis in the MBH or POA of Mkrn3 KO male mice, as expected; in contrast, it was easily detectable to Mkrn3 WT male mice at PND10 (Fig. 2A). Mkrn3 protein levels decreased with increasing age in the MBH and POA of male and female mice, with the highest levels in the MBH at PND15 of male mice, and at PND10-PND22 in the MBH of female mice (Fig. 2B and 2C). Mkrn3 protein levels were highest at PND10-PND15 in the POA of male mice; and at PND10 in the POA of female mice, followed by progressive declines with increasing age in both sexes (Fig. 2D and 2E). By age PND60, Mkrn3 protein levels were undetectable in both the MBH and POA of male and female mice (Fig. 2B-2E).

Serum Dlk1 levels were undetectable in both male and female Dlk1 KO mice at PND60. In both male and female WT mice, serum Dlk1 levels were highest at PND10, decreasing progressively with increasing age until PND60. Serum Dlk1 levels were similar between WT male and female mice, except at PND15, when females had higher levels than males (Fig. 3). Discussion

Recent advances in understanding the genetic underpinnings of CPP have highlighted critical pathways that govern the onset of puberty. Loss-of-function mutations in MKRN3 are currently the most prevalent known monogenic cause of CPP, with an overall frequency of approximately 10%, and a higher prevalence of about 22% to 46% in cases of familial CPP [6, 7, 22, 23]. Although the precise mechanisms by which MKRN3 regulates puberty are not fully elucidated, experimental evidence indicates that Mkrn3 is expressed in kisspeptin neurons of the ARC and may modulate neurokinin B (NKB), thereby contributing to the inhibitory control of the HPG axis before pubertal onset [8, 9]. Consistent with this proposed role, the decrease in Mkrn3 expression observed in the ARC and AVPV of both male and female mice prior to reproductive maturation supports its involvement in the network that suppresses the HPG axis before puberty initiation [7-9, 24]. This is aligned with findings linking loss-of-function mutations in MKRN3 to precocious pubertal onset in humans [7]. Our findings confirm the decrease in Mkrn3 mRNA and protein levels in the MBH and POA (containing the ARC and AVPV, respectively) of male and female mice before the onset of pubertal development and, by expanding the range of ages examined, further validate this pattern. The highest levels of hypothalamic Mkrn3 mRNA were observed at PND10, followed by progressive decreases at PND15 and PND22, with very low levels after PND22. These findings suggest that Mkrn3 plays a more prominent role early in life, potentially becoming less necessary in adulthood. Consistent with this, hypothalamic Mkrn3 protein levels were generally highest at PND10 and PND15, with no detectable Mkrn3 protein in the hypothalamus by PND60. No significant differences in Mkrn3 expression were found between male and female mice, which is consistent with the observation that loss-of-function MKRN3 mutations cause CPP in both boys and girls [7, 25, 26].

Loss-of-function mutations in DLK1 are a less common cause of monogenic CPP. Despite their rarity, a growing number of publications have recently reported novel mutations associated with CPP [13, 27-34]. DLK1-related CPP is of particular interest due to the increased risk of adverse metabolic outcomes in adulthood, such as obesity/overweight, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and dyslipidemia [27, 28]. Notably, Temple syndrome, a rare imprinting disorder resulting in loss of expression of the paternal copy of DLK1, is associated with CPP, overweight and/or truncal obesity later in life, dyslipidemia, and early onset T2DM [35-37]. The human metabolic alterations are remarkably similar to the previously described Dlk1 KO mouse phenotype, which was reported to have intrauterine growth restriction and growth retardation followed by subsequent accelerated weight gain with increased adipose tissue, associated with enlarged fatty liver and hyperlipidemia [38]. Unlike Mkrn3, higher Dlk1 expression in the ARC of WT male mice at PND20 (juvenile stage) and PND60 (adult stage), compared to PND6 (infantile stage), was reported previously by Villanueva et al [12]. Our findings corroborate and extend these observations by demonstrating a consistent age-related increase in hypothalamic Dlk1 mRNA and protein levels across a broader developmental window (PND10, 15, 22, 30, and 60) in both sexes, and by showing that this pattern occurs in both the ARC and the POA, with adult mice generally exhibiting higher levels than younger animals. However, we observed considerable variability in Dlk1 expression levels within all age groups, which may explain the lack of statistically significant differences between some age groups. While we controlled for differences in body weight, it is possible that other metabolic or physiological factors not assessed in this study contributed to the observed variability in Dlk1 levels. Moreover, because dissections of the MBH and POA inherently encompass multiple hypothalamic nuclei and diverse neuronal populations, this broader sampling could have introduced variability by capturing region- or cell-specific differences in gene expression.

DLK1 is a transmembrane protein for which the biologically active form is soluble, generated by tumor necrosis factor α–converting enzyme-mediated cleavage of its extracellular domain [12, 39]. This circulating, soluble form of DLK1 can be measured in serum, and its levels are very low or undetectable in patients with loss-of-function mutations in this gene [13, 27-29, 31]. Previous studies have reported a decline in circulating DLK1 levels in humans both during pubertal maturation [13] and with increasing age [40], suggesting a potential role for peripherally derived DLK1 in the initiation of puberty. In a prior study, we reported a progressive decline in circulating Dlk1 levels in female mice from weaning to the onset of puberty (vaginal opening) and pubertal maturity (first estrus) [13]. The present study demonstrates that serum Dlk1 levels decrease progressively from PND10 to PND60 in both male and female mice, providing additional support for the hypothesis that a decline in circulating Dlk1 levels may be important for HPG axis reactivation. This decline may be also linked to the restricted postnatal tissue expression of DLK1, which is expressed broadly during embryonic development but limited to endocrine and neuroendocrine tissues, such as adipose tissue and certain hypothalamic nuclei, in postnatal life [12, 41].

The role of peripherally generated DLK1 in regulating the HPG axis is supported by a study in Dlk1-null mice, in which pituitary development and function was investigated [42]. Despite these mice being shorter (ie, reduced body length) and lighter (ie, reduced weight) than their wild-type littermates, the loss of Dlk1 led to only minor pituitary defects, with hormone-producing cell populations preserved. These findings suggest that the growth and weight differences observed in mutant animals are likely due to Dlk1 expression in non-pituitary tissues [42]. In addition, detailed neuroanatomical studies demonstrate that DLK1 is expressed in multiple hypothalamic nuclei, predominantly as a cleaved, soluble form [12]. Together with the progressive decline in circulating DLK1 levels observed during postnatal development, these findings raise the possibility that the soluble DLK1 that influences pubertal timing through actions in the hypothalamus may be derived from peripheral, extra-CNS sources rather than that produced locally within hypothalamic neurons. This hypothesis provides a conceptual framework that can help to direct future experimental directions.

MECP2 is a key mediator of DNA methylation in mammals, involved in chromatin remodeling, RNA splicing, and other processes that ultimately result in epigenetic regulation of transcription [43]. Recent studies have linked loss-of-function mutations in MECP2 to CPP with or without mild neurodevelopmental abnormalities, further highlighting the role of epigenetic mechanisms in the hypothalamic regulation of puberty [18]. MECP2 is expressed widely in the brain, including the hypothalamus, in both humans and mice [44]. Canton et al [18] showed high Mecp2 expression in key hypothalamic regions regulating GnRH function in female mice, such as the arcuate, suprachiasmatic, and paraventricular nuclei, as well as in the median eminence, with co-localization in most GnRH neurons. However, Mecp2 expression patterns across pubertal stages of maturation had not been investigated previously. Herein, we demonstrated a decrease in Mecp2 mRNA levels after PND10 in both the MBH and POA of male and female mice, suggesting an inhibitory role in pubertal onset. These findings align with cases of CPP caused by inactivating mutations in MECP2 in humans [18]. However, no decrease was observed in hypothalamic Mecp2 protein levels, suggesting that Mecp2 protein levels were not limited by the moderate reduction in Mecp2 mRNA levels. The absence of significant developmental changes in Mecp2 protein levels is itself informative, as it suggests that overall Mecp2 abundance in the hypothalamus remains stable throughout postnatal development. This stability narrows future hypotheses to more subtle regulatory mechanisms, such as enhanced translation efficiency, increased protein stability, reduced degradation, or cell type–restricted roles that are not detectable in bulk hypothalamic tissue. Isoform-specific expression may also contribute to the observed discrepancy between mRNA and protein levels, since the MECP2 gene generates 2 main transcripts (MECP2-E1 and MECP2-E2) through alternative splicing [45]. Dragich et al [44] showed that both transcripts are distributed widely in newborn mice, but by PND21, differences in their distribution emerge, with Mecp2-E1 being predominant in the hypothalamus, including the ARC. While the specific functions of these isoforms are not fully understood, proteomic and genomic studies suggest each isoform interacts with different protein partners and regulates distinct sets of genes [45]. In our experiments, the RT-PCR primers amplified Mecp2-E1, the most abundant isoform of Mecp2 in the adult hypothalamus [44], while the antibody used in Western blot analysis was not isoform-specific. Thus, the protein measured likely represents both isoforms, with stable expression across postnatal development. This stability may reflect the continued involvement of MECP2 in regulating transcription of multiple gene sets, beyond those specifically related to pubertal timing. In contrast, the mRNA levels quantified reflect Mecp2-E1 levels specifically, which may be more closely linked to pubertal development, in line with its decrease prior to puberty onset. Further studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis.

In summary, Mkrn3, Dlk1, and Mecp2 exhibit distinct hypothalamic expression patterns across pubertal maturation in male and female mice. Both Mkrn3 and Mecp2 showed a decline in hypothalamic mRNA levels preceding puberty onset, supporting their roles as inhibitory regulators of the HPG axis. In contrast, Dlk1 expression increased in the hypothalamus, while its circulating levels decreased with age, suggesting a potential inhibitory role arising in the periphery.

Importantly, limitations of the present study need to be considered in the interpretation of these findings. First, our analyses were performed using bulk dissections of the MBH and POA, regions that encompass multiple hypothalamic nuclei and heterogeneous neuronal and non-neuronal populations. As such, the gene expression levels reported here represent signals that are averaged across diverse cell types and may mask nucleus-specific or cell-type–specific changes, particularly within key pubertal regulators such as kisspeptin or GnRH neurons. Future studies employing single-cell transcriptomic or epigenomic approaches will be important to further delineate the precise cellular sources and regulatory networks associated with these genes and proteins during pubertal maturation.

Second, while quantitative RT-PCR and Western blotting remain well-established and highly sensitive methods for assessing transcript and protein abundance, they do not provide spatial resolution. Emerging technologies such as single-nucleus RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and epigenomic profiling would enable more precise identification of cell-type–specific transcriptional and regulatory dynamics during pubertal maturation in future studies.

Despite these limitations, the use of bulk MBH and POA dissections does provide some biologically relevant insights, as these regions contain the principal nuclei governing GnRH secretion and HPG axis activation (including the ARC and AVPV). Therefore, the observed developmental patterns reflect integrated changes occurring within the broader hypothalamic network that regulates pubertal timing. By characterizing temporal expression systematically at 5 ages across postnatal development in both sexes and at both transcript and protein levels, our study provides a comprehensive developmental framework that complements prior gene-specific or single time point analyses.

These findings underscore the need for future mechanistic studies at higher spatial and cellular resolution to identify nucleus-specific and cell-type–restricted roles of Mkrn3, Dlk1, and Mecp2. Such studies will be essential to clarify how these genes and proteins interact within the neuroendocrine circuitry controlling pubertal onset and how their dysregulation leads to disorders of pubertal timing. Ultimately, integration of developmental profiling with emerging cell-specific and epigenomic approaches will advance our understanding of the molecular architecture governing reproductive maturation.
