Mapping the landscape of geriatric medicine education, training and practice in Europe
Authors: Anna Marie Herghelegiu, Karolina Piotrowicz, Anne Wissendorff Ekdahl, Tahir Masud, Mirko Petrovic, Athanase Benetos, Sofia Duque, Matteo Cesari, Giulia Ogliari, Jurate Macijauskiene, Gabriel Ioan Prada, Mohamed Salem, Ana Gabriela Prada, Marina Kotsani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGeriatric Medicine (GM) has evolved in Europe over the past few decades, although variably across countries. The aim of this paper is to explore the GM specialty status, postgraduate and undergraduate education for medical students, nurses and Allied Health Professionals (AHPs), academic development and GM clinical services in Europe.MethodsWe collected cross-sectional data from 38 European countries as listed by the World Health Organization with an online quantitative survey. This study is part of the PROGRAMMING COST Action, which stands for PROmoting GeRiAtric Medicine in countries where it is still eMergING and is funded by the European Cooperation for Science and Technology.ResultsGM is recognised as a distinct specialty in 24 countries (63.15%), while specialty training is available in 21 countries (55.26%). Principles of GM are included in the undergraduate curricula for medical students in all or most medical universities in 24 countries (63.15%), for nurses in 26 countries (68.42%) and for AHPs in 19 countries (50%). Geriatric hospital wards are present in 28 countries (73.68%), and in eight countries, there are no GM clinical services available, especially in Southern Europe. GM is a standalone academic discipline in 20 countries (52.63%), and advanced academic doctoral study is available in 18 countries (47.36%). GM is more established in Northern and Western Europe.ConclusionOur study highlights the inconsistent development of GM across European countries. It provides a foundation for policy development and educational reform to meet the needs of Europe’s ageing population.

Introduction

Geriatric medicine (GM) serves as a pivotal force in managing healthcare of older adults, in combating ageism and promoting a multidisciplinary and holistic approach in clinical care, yet the trained workforce is in short supply. There are encouraging GM developments in Europe [1], albeit recent research describes many differences in GM specialty recognition and training across Europe [2]. Older patients are treated in various settings by almost all medical specialties and, even in countries like the UK where geriatrics is well developed, less than one in five patients aged 80 years and older receive GM clinical services [2]. In some countries, even though there are services dedicated to the care of older people, the involvement of geriatricians is limited, while education in the principles of GM is scarcely offered at the undergraduate level [3, 4]. Other countries share the same challenges with regard to specialty recognition, expertise and implementation of GM tools in clinical practice [5]. In the last decades, there have been many initiatives to raise awareness of the importance of ensuring formal education and training in GM for healthcare workers [6, 7]. The European Union of Medical Specialists issued recommendations for GM postgraduate training [European Training Requirements in Geriatric Medicine (ETRGM)] [8], but it is unclear how many countries are currently following these recommendations. Furthermore, the extent of GM inclusion in undergraduate curricula of European universities is unknown.

Existing literature has identified only some pieces of the puzzle in several European countries, and despite the challenging and complex landscape of GM development, we need a clearer overall picture before advancing solutions for further development. In this paper, we describe the current state of GM, identify differences across regions and provide a comprehensive attempt to accurately present the GM situation across Europe.

Materials and methods

Four main facets are explored: postgraduate training (specialty status, structure of specialty training and related aspects, other postgraduate training opportunities for physicians and other healthcare professionals), undergraduate education in GM for medical students, nurses and Allied Health Professionals (AHPs), academic development (academic discipline status and development, advanced academic study prospects, presence of professional organisations in the field of GM) and GM clinical services (presence of geriatrics wards and clinical services for older adults) (Annex I).

This study is part of the PROGRAMMING COST Action, which stands for PROmoting GeRiAtric Medicine in countries where it is still eMergING and is funded by the European Cooperation for Science and Technology (COST) [9, 10]. The European Geriatric Medicine Society (EuGMS), as the grant holder of the Action, plays a vital role in coordinating PROGRAMMING activities, supporting national efforts and advocating for equitable access to GM clinical services across Europe [11]. By October 2025, PROGRAMMING included around 425 participants from 45 countries (from Europe and beyond), providing a powerful European network of professionals involved in the care of older people [9].

This survey was developed as a result of a four-stage expert collaboration within the PROGRAMMING Action. A first version of the questionnaire was proposed by a small group of experts and subsequently adjusted for the last version after receiving feedback during three online meetings with leaders and other active participants in the Action. The questionnaire includes 25 single and multiple-choice questions and four open-ended questions. To ensure consistency across countries, predefined response options were used, and answering all questions was mandatory. The four open-ended questions allowed additional comments and were optional. All respondents provided informed consent and voluntarily included their names to support the reliability and validity of the data.

We collected cross-sectional quantitative national-level data from European countries listed by the World Health Organization (WHO) [12], excluding the Russian Federation and Belarus due to COST regulations [13]. Even though Kosovo is not yet part of the WHO, it is a COST Near Neighbour Country [13] and a partner in the PROGRAMMING Action, and was therefore included in the survey. The EuGMS Secretariat distributed the invitation to complete the online questionnaire via email to a convenience sample of national representatives in the Action, representatives of the relevant national professional organisations, experts and prominent figures (one or two respondents per country). Data were collected between February and April 2024 and validated by two researchers. For accuracy, responses were re-checked with all the initial and additional experts where needed (in eight countries) between May and July 2025. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, mean, mode) are reported. The Dunn–Bonferroni test was used for post hoc pairwise comparisons following a significant Kruskal–Wallis test (P < .05; CI 95%) to identify differences between European regions. We were able to contact 45 countries, responses were received from 42 countries and 4 countries were excluded (the respondents did not give their consent to have their names published as collaborators, or the provided answers were inconsistent). We present data from 38 European countries.

Results

Geriatric medicine postgraduate training

GM is recognised as a distinct medical speciality in 24 of the 38 European countries (63.15%) and in 10 of 38 countries (26.31%); it is available as a subspecialty of other medical specialties, mostly internal medicine (in all 10 countries) but also other specialties [e.g. general or family medicine (4 countries), physical or rehabilitation medicine (4 countries)] (Supplemental Material Table S1, Figure 1). In three of the mentioned countries, GM is both a separate specialty and a subspecialty, while in Germany, the situation differs among counties. The distribution of countries where GM is a distinct specialty is highest in Northern Europe (8 of 9 countries; 88.88%), followed by Eastern Europe (4 of 5 countries; 80%) and Western Europe (5 of 7 countries; 71.42%), while in Southern Europe, less than half the countries recognise GM (5 of 12 countries; 41.66%).

In Andorra and Luxembourg, GM is a distinct specialty but the postgraduate training is provided by neighbouring countries, while in Cyprus, the legislation for formal GM specialty recognition has recently been adopted and training is not yet available. The total duration of specialty training since graduating from medical school takes an average of 5.71 years, most frequently 5 years (nine countries) and varies from 4 to 9 years across the European countries. Clinical rotation on geriatrics settings is mandatory in 20 of 21 countries (95.23%) for a duration of 18–60 months (average of 35.05 months, most frequently 36 months). In Croatia, rotation on geriatrics wards is not required for specialty training. The training includes theoretical courses (19 of 21 countries; 90.47%), rotations on various medical wards (19 of 21 countries; 90.47%), practical skills teaching (19 of 21 countries; 90.47%), research (13 of 21 countries; 61.90%), leadership (Finland and the UK) and Quality Improvement and Audit Management (the UK). In Eastern Europe, research is a part of the specialty training in only one of four countries (25%), compared to all other European regions (75%). There are no other notable differences in the structure of specialty training programmes among European regions. The postgraduate training curriculum is national in all countries where GM is a distinct specialty, but the curriculum is aligned with the ETRGM [14] in only 6 of 21 countries (28.57%). In Portugal, there is a national curriculum for GM formal competency training, aligned to the ETRGM. Details about the postgraduate GM training in each country are available in Supplemental Material Table S1.

Training is provided exclusively by specialists in GM in only nine countries (33.33%), and it is shared among geriatricians, internal medicine and other specialists in 16 countries (59.25%). In Croatia where GM is a distinct specialty and in North Macedonia where GM is a subspecialty, postgraduate education is delivered by internal medicine and other specialists. Advanced nurse practitioners and AHPs teach principles of GM to postgraduate trainees in nine countries (Supplemental Material Table S2). Details about which institutions develop the postgraduate curricula can be found in the Supplemental Material Table S3. Postgraduate training courses in GM, either Continuing Medical Education (CME) or non-CME accredited, are currently available in 23 of 38 (60.52%) countries, and the distribution is similar among European regions. Scientific communications (e.g. conferences, congresses) are organised in 24 of 38 (63.15%) countries (Supplemental Material Table S2). In many European countries, training in the principles of GM is part of the postgraduate curricula of other medical specialties, mostly internal medicine (20 of 38 countries; 52.63%), as well as in the postgraduate curricula for nurses (27 of 38 countries; 71.05%) and physical therapists (16 of 38 countries; 42.10%) (Supplemental Material Table S4).

GM undergraduate training

Out of the 38 participating countries, GM is included in the undergraduate curricula for medical students in 36 (94.73%) countries, for nurses in 37 countries (97.36%) and for AHPs in 31 countries (81.57%). The availability of undergraduate education in GM for medical students varies significantly across European regions [H(3) = 8.41; P = .038] (Figure 2). GM is present in the undergraduate curricula significantly more widely across universities in Northern Europe compared to Eastern (z = −26.48; P = .013) and Southern Europe (z = 25.99; P = .001). For nurses and AHPs, we see the same trend in favour of Northern and Western Europe but the situation is more balanced across regions [H(3) = 3.47; P = 0.32, respectively, H(3) = 6.61; P = .085] (Figure 2). More details about GM undergraduate training in each country are available in Supplemental Material Table S5.

GM academic development

GM is a standalone academic discipline in 20 of 38 countries (52.63%) and, in 14 of 38 countries (36.84%), is associated with other medical disciplines such as internal medicine, psychiatry and nursing. The distribution of countries where GM is an independent academic discipline varies significantly across European regions [H(3) = 10.46; P = .015] and is significantly lower in Southern compared to Northern Europe (z = 11.01; P = .021) (Figure 3). In 14 of 38 countries (36.84%), there are no standalone GM academic chairs, while in 16 of 38 countries (42.10%), there are several (Figure 3). The presence of standalone GM academic chairs varies significantly across European regions [H(3) = 13.26; P = .004] with a significantly larger presence in Northern and Western Europe compared to the Southern region (z = 11.7; P = .01, respectively, z = −11.97; P = .03). Doctoral studies in GM are available in 18 of 38 countries (47.36%), mostly in the Northern and Eastern regions, while master courses are available in 21 of 38 European countries (55.26%), especially in Northern and Western Europe (Figure 3). Differences in availability of advanced academic study across Europe do not reach statistical significance [H(3) = 4.12; P = .24]. Details about the academic situation of GM in each country can be found in Supplemental Material Table S6. National GM Societies and other professional organisations in the field of GM are present in 30 of 38 European countries (78.94%), and they support GM education and training, while in 21 of 38 countries (55.26%), geriatrics specialists are active at governmental level in a position of decision-making involved in medical education/training, public health policies or national health programmes.

GM clinical practices

Geriatric hospital wards exist in 28 of 38 countries (73.64%) and are less present in Southern Europe (Figure 4). Healthcare for older adults is offered in different settings and facilities across Europe: on internal medicine wards (19 countries), orthopaedics wards (18 countries), in long-term care facilities (24 countries) and in rehabilitation care settings (21 countries). Palliative care for older people is practiced in 18 countries. Healthcare services for older adults are available in primary care/community settings in 21 countries and in ambulatory settings in 24 countries. There are no specific GM clinical services available in eight countries, especially in Southern Europe (Figure 4). Details about the settings where GM/healthcare for older people is being delivered across Europe can be found in Supplemental Material Table S7. Geriatricians can subspecialise in various domains in a few countries, mainly in Northern and Southern Europe (Supplemental Material Table S8).

Discussion and conclusions

Access to GM clinical services depends on where in Europe they are located. Most countries with no GM clinical services available are in Southern Europe where the specialty is seldom recognised, in contrast with the other European regions where GM is more available. This study reveals substantial heterogeneity in GM education and training across Europe, underscoring the fragmented nature of GM clinical services provision. Harmonisation is necessary to ensure equitable, high-quality care and strengthen research and collaboration.

Considerations on the current situation

Although principles of GM are included, to some extent, in the undergraduate curricula for medical students in most of Europe, it is a common practice in just over half of the countries. Moreover, undergraduate training for AHPs varies widely, frequently seen among physiotherapists and occupational therapists, leaving a gap in GM education for most AHPs in many countries. The structure of postgraduate training is heterogeneous across Europe, while the curricula are aligned with the ETRGM [14] in a few countries. As a subspecialty, GM is mainly available for internal medicine specialists, followed by a restricted list of other specialists. Similarly, principles of GM are included in the postgraduate curricula mostly for internal medicine. These findings are consistent with Pearson et al.’s observations that there is a mismatch between demographic needs and formal training structures globally [15]. Teodorczuk et al. emphasise the importance of how geriatric principles are taught, embedded and valued by upskilling professionals outside geriatrics through interprofessional education and shared learning environments [16]. Our results extend this evidence by showing that educational fragmentation translates directly into limited preparedness among the wider health workforce and suggests that variability in training standards may contribute to unequal professional standards and competency profiles across Europe.

The limited availability of specialised healthcare services for older adults in primary care, community and ambulatory settings, reported in only about half of the participating countries, highlights the challenges many countries face in adapting to evolving healthcare management models [17]. Even in countries where geriatrics wards exist, the healthcare for older people is delivered in a wide range of other settings, further highlighting the necessity of basic geriatrics skills. The uneven distribution of GM infrastructures risks exacerbating inequalities in access to quality care for older adults across Europe.

Challenges and pathways for change

Emphasis on multimorbidity, functional ability and continuity of care closely aligns GM with the needs of a generalist workforce and positions geriatrics as a form of ‘expert generalism’ for older people rather than a traditional organ-based discipline. Training and service models that embed interprofessional collaboration, shared decision-making and the lived experience of older people and their caregivers are essential to ensuring that GM contributes to integrated, person-centred care.

Improving the attractiveness of GM specialty among graduates is not an easy mission due to factors such as the popularity of GM among other medical specialties, training and research opportunities. A strong GM department presence in universities, research and advanced academic study opportunities, as well as harmonisation of training requirements across European countries, could contribute to specialty appeal. This aligns with the work of Andersen et al., who demonstrate that career choice in geriatrics is strongly influenced not only by exposure to geriatric competencies but also by role models, visibility within medical school curricula and perceptions of work–life balance [18]. Our data suggest that in many European countries, these enabling conditions are structurally absent due to limited academic presence of GM and lack of formalised training pathways, which may further perpetuate recruitment challenges identified in the Danish context.

International collaboration and scientific mobility, strongly encouraged by the COST organisation and other EU opportunities, may provide a facilitator for networking and lobbying [19]. Embedding GM experts in EU-level health and education advisory bodies could foster and accelerate a stronger political commitment. This need for stronger academic and policy presence echoes Waddell et al.’s argument that clearer conceptual and policy alignment around what it means to ‘age well’ is required to guide service development [20]. Without a stronger academic voice in policy arenas, geriatric medicine risks remaining peripheral to broader ageing strategies despite its central relevance.

The present paper has several limitations. Conflicting responses and limited data availability led to the exclusion of certain questions and some of the developing countries from detailed analysis. Importantly, the data collection process revealed a need to standardise geriatric-related concepts, as varying national interpretations may affect the assessment of system maturity. The absence of data from all European countries might skew the overall picture of GM education and training across the continent. Future work should triangulate survey responses with national curricula and health system data to validate reported structures and avoid reliance solely on expert self-report.

However, our findings are consistent with previous reports from several European countries, which have highlighted variation in specialty status; duration and content of training; and the extent to which geriatricians are embedded across acute, community and long-term care settings [4, 21]. Our mapping confirms these patterns at a pan-European level while also demonstrating that such variation persists even among countries with comparable demographic pressures and health system structures. Moreover, our findings provide systems-level confirmation of concerns raised in recent geriatric education and workforce literature. Reviews by Teodorczuk, Pearson and colleagues have highlighted pedagogical, curricular and recruitment challenges in preparing a workforce for ageing societies [15, 16]. Our study demonstrates how these educational and professional issues manifest structurally across European health systems. Addressing them therefore requires not only curricular reform but also coordinated policy, academic and service-level action across countries. Coordinated investment in harmonised education, stronger academic presence and integrated service models will be essential to ensure equitable, high-quality care for all older Europeans.
