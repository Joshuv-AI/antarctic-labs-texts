MELAS syndrome complicated by anti-GFAP autoantibody positivity: a case report and literature review
Authors: Jun Shu, Yiping Zhou, Ziwei Xu, Wenshi Wei, Lirong Zhao, Jie Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAutoimmune abnormalities have been reported in mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes (MELAS) syndrome, but anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) antibody positivity in both serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) has not been previously described. We report such a case and review the literature on MELAS-associated autoimmune abnormalities.Case presentationA 46-year-old man presented with hearing loss, aphasia, and psychiatric symptoms. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed bilateral temporo-parietal lesions. Anti-GFAP antibodies were detected in both serum and CSF. Despite multiple immunotherapies including immunoglobulin, methylprednisolone, and cyclophosphamide, the patient showed minimal clinical improvement. Genetic testing revealed m.3243 A > G mutation (11.91% heteroplasmy), confirming MELAS syndrome. The patient died within three months of onset.ConclusionsTo our knowledge, this is the first report of MELAS syndrome with concurrent serum and CSF anti-GFAP antibody positivity. This case expands the spectrum of autoimmune abnormalities observed in MELAS syndrome. Together with our literature review, this case suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction may trigger various secondary autoimmune responses. The clinical significance and pathogenic role of these autoantibodies in MELAS syndrome warrant further investigation.

Introduction

Mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes (MELAS) syndrome is a maternally inherited mitochondrial disorder caused by mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). The m.3243 A > G mutation in the MT-TL1 gene is the most common pathogenic variant [1]. Clinical manifestations include stroke-like episodes, seizures, hearing loss, cognitive impairment, and myopathy, typically presenting in childhood or early adulthood with variable severity [2].

Recent evidence has revealed an intriguing connection between MELAS syndrome and autoimmune phenomena. Several case reports have documented the presence of various autoantibodies in MELAS patients, including antiphospholipid antibodies and anti-Caspr2 antibodies, often associated with more severe clinical courses [3, 4]. These observations suggest that mitochondrial dysfunction may trigger autoimmune responses, though the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood.

Autoimmune glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) astrocytopathy is a recently recognized autoimmune encephalitis characterized by anti-GFAP antibodies in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and/or serum [5]. Patients typically present with meningoencephalitis features including headache, altered consciousness, and seizures, often with characteristic linear radial gadolinium enhancement on brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [6]. While anti-GFAP antibodies have been detected in various neurological conditions, their presence in mitochondrial disorders has not been explored.

Here, we report the first case of MELAS syndrome with concurrent anti-GFAP antibody positivity in both serum and CSF. Additionally, we conducted a systematic literature review to summarize current knowledge about autoimmune abnormalities in MELAS syndrome, aiming to enhance understanding of the potential interplay between mitochondrial dysfunction and autoimmunity.

Case presentation

A 46-year-old man was admitted to a local hospital on March 10, 2024, with a one-week history of progressive hearing loss and expressive aphasia. He had experienced upper respiratory symptoms one month before the neurological symptoms began. Initial brain MRI revealed bilateral temporal lobe hyperintensities suggestive of viral encephalitis. Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) showed no vascular abnormalities. Despite empirical treatment with antiviral agents, antibiotics, and neurotrophic medications, his condition deteriorated with the emergence of psychiatric symptoms. He was transferred to our tertiary care center on March 15, 2024. On March 20 (hospital day 5), he developed convulsive status epilepticus requiring intubation and mechanical ventilation. Seizures were managed with intravenous diazepam for acute control followed by levetiracetam for maintenance therapy. Valproate was avoided due to its potential mitochondrial toxicity.

The patient had been previously healthy with no significant past medical history except for chronic headaches that had never been evaluated. His height was 170 cm and weight 50 kg (BMI 17.3 kg/m²). Family history was notable for early childhood deaths: his sister died at age three, and his son also died at age three, both from unknown causes. His mother suffered from chronic headaches similar to the patient. There was no other family history of neurological or metabolic disorders.

On admission to our hospital, the patient was apathetic but able to ambulate independently. Neurological examination revealed equal, round, and reactive pupils bilaterally. Further detailed neurological examination was limited due to poor cooperation. Laboratory investigations showed neutrophilia (78.3%; reference range: 40–75%), lymphopenia (13.6%; reference range: 20–50%), and mild anemia (hemoglobin: 119 g/L; reference range: 130–175 g/L). C-reactive protein was mildly elevated at 16.57 mg/L (reference: <10 mg/L). Liver and kidney function tests, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, glycated hemoglobin, creatine kinase, serum ammonia, and serum lactate were all within normal limits. CSF lactate was not measured. Infectious disease screening for syphilis and HIV was negative.

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis showed normal cell count, glucose, and protein levels. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) of CSF for bacterial, viral, and fungal pathogens was negative. Comprehensive autoimmune encephalitis panel testing was performed by Dian Diagnostics Group Co., Ltd. using cell-based assay (CBA) method. The panel included antibodies against: N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor, leucine-rich glioma-inactivated 1, gamma-aminobutyric acid B receptor, contactin-associated protein-like 2, α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptor 1 and 2, IgLON family member 5, dipeptidyl-peptidase-like protein 6, glycine receptor 1, dopamine 2 receptor, glutamic acid decarboxylase 65, metabotropic glutamate receptor 5, myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein, and GFAP. Among these, only anti-GFAP antibodies were positive at a titer of 1:32 in both serum and CSF (Fig. 1). All other tested autoantibodies were negative.

Chest and abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed incidental findings of emphysema, left renal calculi, cholelithiasis, and prostatic calcification, with no evidence of malignancy or other significant abnormalities.

Follow-up brain MRI on March 18, 2024 (hospital day 3) showed progression of the neuroimaging findings. There was bilateral parieto-temporal-insular cortical swelling with T1 hypointensity, T2/fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintensity, and restricted diffusion on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) with corresponding reduced apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values (Fig. 2). Contrast-enhanced brain MRI demonstrated focal patchy mild enhancement in the affected regions. Electroencephalography (EEG) revealed diffuse slowing with predominant frontal theta activity but no epileptiform discharges.

Given the positive anti-GFAP antibodies and MRI findings, autoimmune encephalitis was the initial working diagnosis, though mitochondrial disorders including MELAS syndrome remained in the differential diagnosis. While awaiting genetic testing results, empirical immunotherapy was initiated with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG; 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days) and high-dose methylprednisolone (500 mg/day for 5 days) followed by a tapering regimen. After treatment, the patient was successfully extubated but remained somnolent with minimal responsiveness.

Follow-up brain MRI on March 29, 2024 (hospital day 14) revealed disease progression despite immunotherapy. Compared to the prior study, there was extension of the T2/FLAIR hyperintense lesions with new involvement of the left occipital lobe and left thalamus, along with increased leptomeningeal enhancement (Fig. 3). Given the lack of response to first-line immunotherapy, cyclophosphamide (300 mg/day for 5 days) was administered as second-line treatment. Mitochondrial supplements including coenzyme Q10 and L-arginine were also initiated.

Genetic testing performed by Dian Diagnostics Group Co., Ltd. revealed the m.3243 A > G mutation in the MT-TL1 gene with 11.91% heteroplasmy in peripheral blood, confirming the diagnosis of MELAS syndrome.Despite aggressive management including continued supportive care, the patient’s neurological status progressively declined. He died approximately three months after initial symptom onset.

Discussion

We present the first reported case of MELAS syndrome with concurrent anti-GFAP antibody positivity in both serum and CSF. The diagnosis of MELAS syndrome was established based on characteristic clinical features (stroke-like episodes, seizures, and hearing loss), neuroimaging findings of cortical lesions not conforming to vascular territories, suggestive family history with possible maternal inheritance, and genetic confirmation of the pathogenic m.3243 A > G mutation. The unexpected finding of anti-GFAP antibodies presented a diagnostic challenge and raised important questions about the relationship between mitochondrial dysfunction and autoimmune phenomena in MELAS syndrome.

To contextualize our findings, we conducted a systematic literature review of MELAS syndrome with autoimmune manifestations (Fig. 4). Our search identified nine previous cases with documented autoimmune abnormalities [3, 4, 7–13] (Table 1). These cases demonstrated a diverse spectrum of autoimmune phenomena, ranging from organ-specific autoantibodies to systemic autoimmune conditions. The onset age ranged from 20 to 59 years, with several adult-onset cases comparable to our patient. Available clinical data suggest that the presence of autoimmune abnormalities may be associated with complex clinical presentations, though long-term outcomes were not consistently reported across cases. Our case extends this spectrum as the first to demonstrate anti-GFAP antibody positivity, suggesting that mitochondrial dysfunction in MELAS may trigger diverse autoimmune responses rather than a single specific pathway.

Our patient’s presentation at age 46 represents a relatively late onset for MELAS syndrome, though adult-onset cases are increasingly recognized [2]. The heteroplasmy level of 11.91% in peripheral blood was relatively low, which might partially explain the delayed presentation, although the relationship between heteroplasmy levels and clinical phenotype remains complex [14]. The neuroimaging findings were characteristic of MELAS [15], showing bilateral temporo-parieto-occipital cortical lesions that crossed vascular territories. However, the presence of anti-GFAP antibodies created a diagnostic dilemma. Typical GFAP astrocytopathy presents with distinct features including marked CSF pleocytosis, elevated protein levels, and characteristic periventricular radial enhancement on MRI [16, 17]—none of which were present in our case. The normal CSF cell count and protein levels, along with the absence of radial enhancement pattern, argued against primary GFAP astrocytopathy and supported MELAS as the primary diagnosis.

The poor response to immunotherapy in our patient warrants careful consideration. Despite aggressive treatment with IVIG, high-dose methylprednisolone, and cyclophosphamide, the patient showed minimal clinical improvement and continued neurological deterioration. This contrasts with typical GFAP astrocytopathy, where immunotherapy often leads to significant improvement [18]. The treatment failure might be explained by several factors. First, the underlying mitochondrial dysfunction may have rendered neural tissue particularly vulnerable to metabolic stress, limiting any potential benefit from immunosuppression. Second, if the anti-GFAP antibodies were secondary to mitochondrial disease-induced tissue damage rather than primary pathogenic factors, targeting the immune response alone would be insufficient. The progressive neuroimaging changes despite immunotherapy further support that the primary disease process was metabolic rather than autoimmune in nature.

The significance of anti-GFAP antibodies in our patient remains uncertain but intriguing. Anti-GFAP antibodies have been detected in various neurological conditions beyond primary GFAP astrocytopathy, including viral encephalitis, traumatic brain injury, and brain tumors [19–21], where they appear to correlate with tissue damage severity. In the context of MELAS syndrome, we hypothesize that mitochondrial dysfunction may lead to progressive astrocytic injury and blood-brain barrier disruption, potentially exposing GFAP antigens to the immune system. This could trigger a secondary autoimmune response that, while not the primary disease driver, might amplify tissue damage and contribute to disease severity. The concurrent presence of anti-GFAP antibodies might may reflect extensive astrocytic involvement rather than indicating a primary autoimmune process. Further studies with serial antibody measurements would be needed to determine whether these antibodies have biomarker potential.

Beyond tissue damage-induced autoimmunity, mitochondrial dysfunction in MELAS may directly modulate immune responses through multiple pathways. Mitochondria serve as critical immune signaling platforms [22], releasing damage-associated molecular patterns such as mitochondrial DNA and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species when dysfunctional, which can activate innate immune pathways including Toll-like receptor 9, nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 inflammasome, and cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes [23, 24]. Furthermore, impaired mitochondrial metabolism may disrupt the immunometabolic reprogramming essential for immune cell function, potentially promoting pro-inflammatory states [25]. The m.3243 A > G mutation-induced respiratory chain deficiency in MELAS could amplify these processes, potentially establishing a self-perpetuating cycle of mitochondrial dysfunction and immune activation. While this bidirectional relationship might contribute to the diverse autoimmune manifestations observed in MELAS syndrome, whether such immune responses represent epiphenomena or actively contribute to disease progression remains to be elucidated.

In conclusion, we report the first case of MELAS syndrome with concurrent anti-GFAP antibody positivity in both serum and CSF. This case, together with our literature review, demonstrates that diverse autoimmune abnormalities can occur in MELAS syndrome, suggesting a complex relationship between mitochondrial dysfunction and immune activation. Further studies are needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying this relationship and determine whether autoimmune markers have prognostic significance in MELAS patients.
