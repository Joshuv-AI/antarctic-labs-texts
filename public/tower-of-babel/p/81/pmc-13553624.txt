Demographic determinants of REMS-derived BMD and fragility score
Authors: Dennis Chan, Weiwen Chen, Elizabeth Yabsley, Nicholas Pocock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryREMS estimates bone mineral density and fracture risk. This study found that its output is driven mainly by demographic factors (age and weight) rather than direct skeletal measurement. This affects interpretation of outliers, discordance with DXA, and monitoring. Using REMS within FRAX, which already includes demographic variables, may introduce confounding effects. Algorithm transparency is essential to reassess REMS’ clinical role.PurposeRecent findings have questioned the dependency of radiofrequency echographic multi-spectrometry (REMS) outputs on demographic inputs. This study investigates the extent to which REMS-derived fragility score (FS) and REMS-derived bone mineral density (REMS-BMD) values are influenced by demographic inputs (e.g., age, weight, and height) through the analysis of clinical data and controlled experimental manipulation.MethodsA clinical cohort (178 females, 31 males) underwent REMS scans of the lumbar spine (LS) and femoral neck (FN). Multiple linear regression was used to determine the variance in REMS-BMD and FS explained by demographics. Additionally, five healthy volunteers underwent multiple REMS scans where age and weight inputs were artificially varied to measure the direct impact on the REMS-BMD output, assuming stable underlying bone.ResultsIn the clinical cohort, regression models based on demographic variables of input age and input weight (as well as input height for LS) explained the majority of the variance for REMS-BMD (R2 > 0.90), FN FS (R2 > 0.95), and LS FS (R2 > 0.80) in both males and females. In the experimental cohort, artificially increasing the input age caused a FN REMS-BMD decline of approximately 6.3% per decade, while an artificial 5-kg increase in weight input caused a FN REMS-BMD increase of approximately 4.3%.ConclusionsREMS-BMD and fragility scores are strongly influenced by demographic inputs, particularly age and weight. The REMS output therefore reflects an assessment of BMD and fracture risk strongly based on demographic parameters, rather than an ultrasound-based direct skeletal measurement. The results indicate the need for caution in individual patient assessment (particularly outliers), its application with FRAX calculations, and its use for longitudinal monitoring.

Introduction

Radiofrequency echographic multi-spectrometry (REMS) has emerged as an alternative, radiation-free approach to bone health assessment, providing bone mineral density (REMS-BMD) values, T-scores, Z-scores, and a derived fragility score (FS) [1–3]. Unlike dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), REMS derives these metrics through a two-stage algorithmic process: first, comparing backscattered ultrasound spectra against a demographically selected set of reference models, and second, converting the resulting “osteoporosis score” using a separate, demographically dependent transformation equation [4].

While REMS shows promising reproducibility and correlation with DXA [5–8], as well as utility in fracture prediction [3, 9], its outputs remain the results of a “black box.” The manufacturer has stated that their proprietary reference database is stratified by age, sex, ethnicity, site, and BMI [4], but the relative contribution of this stratification versus the ultrasound signal in the final REMS-BMD output has not been clinically quantified. In contrast to DXA, where the physics are well understood [10], incomplete understanding of the determinants of REMS outputs has led to concerns about the influence of operator-entered demographic parameters. These concerns were supported by Bobelyak and colleagues [11], who recently demonstrated that a model incorporating only age, sex, and BMI could account for approximately 90% of the variability in femoral neck REMS-BMD. Importantly, their work also reported that the REMS output could be significantly altered by artificially changing the entered age and body mass index (BMI) values within individual subjects. Provocatively, they also reported that the REMS software at the time of their study showed only minimal change in hip REMS-BMD values following the insertion of a metallic hip prosthesis. These observations suggest that while DXA measurements (g/cm2) do not alter changes in the entered demographics [10], REMS’s reliance on a reference database, with subsequent algorithmic refinement that is dependent on age, sex, and weight inputs, raises the hypothesis that the algorithm’s prior weighting of demographics might overwhelm any posterior input from the ultrasound signal.

Building on the recent findings of Bobelyak et al. [11], and noting that no previous study has interrogated the FS in a similar way, the present study was designed to test the hypothesis that age and weight inputs are dominant determinants of REMS FS and REMS-BMD independent of bone mineral status (density and structure) or pathology. FS is a parameter purported to reflect skeletal fragility independent of BMD (2). It is intended to provide additional information about bone quality and fracture risk. FS is unitless ranging from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating greater skeletal fragility. The FS is aimed to complement BMD by capturing structural or qualitative bone features not reflected in density alone. We used a real-world clinical dataset as well as controlled experimental manipulation of a small sample of volunteers to quantify the precise contribution of input age, weight, and height to REMS-BMD and FS outputs across a heterogeneous patient population.

Methods

Two cohorts of participants were recruited: a clinical cohort of 209 subjects referred for fracture risk assessment, and an experimental cohort of five healthy volunteers spanning an age range of 37 to 70 years. All REMS scans were performed by a single Echo S® Plus device from Echolight® S.p.A, running EchoStudio® version 2.2.0–0, with Fragility Score version 1.0.0 and Quality Check version 4.6.1. REMS scans of the lumbar spine (LS) and both femoral necks (FN) were performed by operators trained by the local Echolight® REMS distributor, using standard manufacturer-recommended protocols [12]. Two quality checks were completed without issue over the 7-month study period, in line with manufacturer recommendations.

Retrospective clinical cohort analysis

In the clinical cohort, a retrospective study was performed on all patients who attended a dedicated REMS clinic between 1 March 2025 and 1 October 2025. Most patients were self-referred. Inclusion criteria for this analysis were all adults over 20 years old with at least one valid REMS scan (FN or LS). Exclusion criteria were scans that did not meet quality control (e.g., inadequate view or positioning of the neck of femur). If a single patient had multiple scans performed over the 7-month period, only their earliest set of scans was used. There was no missing demographic data in the entire cohort. The sample size for each analysis varies slightly, reflecting cases where patients did not have scans performed at all three anatomical sites (e.g., due to hip joint replacement(s) or pregnancy). The study utilized only summary data in accordance with national guidelines on the use of deidentified information. All patients who declined to have their deidentified information included were excluded from the analysis.

Experimental cohort and parameter sweep protocol

To provide a mechanistic proof-of-concept for the findings in the clinical cohort, an experimental protocol was conducted. Five healthy volunteers underwent up to 20 REMS FN scans. Acknowledging the small sample size, this component was designed to be illustrative rather than for formal statistical inference. For each volunteer, the operator first performed a scan with their true demographic data. All scans were performed on the same hip by the same operator. By using large variation in input parameters, these sweep studies test the stability of the outputs against demographic data input variation, a critical parameter of any measurement device.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Retrospective analysis of previously collected de-identified imaging data was performed in accordance with the Australian National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research. Ethical approval was obtained for the volunteer scanning component of the study, and written informed consent was obtained from all five participants prior to participation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS (v9.4). In the clinical cohort, multiple linear regression analysis was performed to quantify the contribution of operator-entered demographic data to REMS-derived outputs. For FN REMS-BMD, the predictor variables were age and weight. For LS REMS-BMD, the predictor variables were age, weight, and height. Models were run independently for females and males.

To investigate predictors of REMS-derived fragility score at the hip and lumbar spine, separate multiple regression models were conducted for females and males, incorporating age and age2 as predictors.

All statistical analyses were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Assumptions for linear regression, including linearity, normality of residuals, and homoscedasticity, were assessed and met for all models.

Discussion

Using a clinical cohort and controlled experimental manipulations, this study demonstrates the profound effect of operator-entered demographics on REMS-BMD and FS. In the clinical cohort, regression models based on age and weight explained over 90% of the variance in REMS-BMD (R2 > 0.9). Similarly, the fragility score is strongly influenced by age, with a quadratic function of age predicting 80–98% of its variance. These findings are in contrast with previous large clinical cohorts using DXA which report that age and body composition only account for 20–50% of the variance in measured BMD [13, 14]. This discordance suggests the influence of input demographic parameters within the REMS algorithm may extend beyond expected natural biological correlations. While this stratification is an intentional design feature, the extent of this dependence has not been previously quantified and is a central finding of this paper. This raises concerns about clinical interpretation and casts doubt on the potential role of REMS-BMD to guide treatment decisions or to monitor therapy over time. The strong dependence of REMS on input demographic data is in direct contrast to DXA, where the BMD (g/cm2) value is independent of changes in inputs for age or weight [10]. Our baseline data also demonstrate an almost perfect linear relationship between left and right FN REMS-BMD (r = 0.99). Such near-identity between contralateral sites is biologically unlikely given side-to-side variability is expected. Previous studies of DXA femoral neck BMD have indicated strong correlations (typically 0.90 to 0.93) between left and right femoral neck DXA-BMD [15, 16], but while strong these were consistently lower than observed in the current study. The near-perfect bilateral concordance observed with FN REMS-BMD is consistent with algorithmic dependence on shared demographic inputs rather than independent skeletal measurements at each hip.

These findings support and extend the recent observations by Bobelyak et al. [11]. While potential methodological concerns regarding scan acquisition quality have been raised about their specific pre-/post-arthroplasty comparison [17], their key observation that artificially altering demographic inputs significantly changed REMS outputs is directly supported and quantified by the current study. In combination, these studies do not completely negate the potential utility of REMS as a diagnostic tool, but the likelihood of clinical utility of REMS-BMD for estimating DXA BMD is not strengthened by the findings of this study. Rather, they highlight the urgent need for clarification from device manufacturers. The manufacturers have previously stated that the backscattered spectral models are stratified by age, sex, and BMI [1–4]. They have also indicated that the algorithms to convert the derived “osteoporosis score” are themselves stratified by age and weight. However, the precise weighting of the ultrasound signal versus demographic modeling in the REMS-BMD calculation remains unclear, and while the exact parameters of this two-layer system are proprietary, this study demonstrates that its final output is almost entirely tethered to the demographic inputs, regardless of the underlying ultrasound signal. Therefore, independent validation studies, ideally conducted with full access to the raw radiofrequency data, should be prioritized.

Algorithmic interpretation and clinical implications

REMS has shown good correlation with DXA in population-level studies [3, 5, 8, 9], and this study is not a dismissal of these findings but rather a caution regarding its application to individual patient care. This distinction is fundamental, as the technology’s current algorithmic opacity, specifically regarding the precise weighting between the demographic inputs and the ultrasound signal, poses challenges for clinical interpretation and independent validation. While REMS may provide advantages in its ability to assess patients with spinal artifacts like osteoarthritis where DXA can be falsely elevated [18], this benefit must be weighed against its heavy reliance on demographic inputs. Additional data is required to assess if the strong impact of demographic data, which leads to low sensitivity to the impact of osteoarthritis, comes at the expense of insensitivity to the impact of osteoporosis of the spine.

Study limitations

We acknowledge the limitations of our study, including the modest sample size, especially in the male cohort, and a potential selection bias, as many participants were self-referred. Furthermore, our findings are based on a single REMS device, and while the manufacturer has quality assurance procedures to ensure consistency across devices, multi-center studies using this experimental design would be valuable to confirm our findings are not machine specific. However, this study’s primary focus is on the direction and magnitude of demographic influence, for which our experimental design provides statistically compelling evidence, despite the small cohort. Furthermore, the five-person experimental cohort was designed to be illustrative of the algorithm’s mechanism, not definitive for longitudinal monitoring. Nevertheless, the profound sensitivity to demographic inputs observed strongly indicates the need for larger, prospective studies to validate its use in this context.

The absence of a direct comparator, such as DXA, in our dataset also prevents the empirical determination of clinical misclassification rates due to REMS demographic weighting. Future studies with larger, multi-site, and multi-operator experimental cohorts are needed to robustly confirm the generalizability of these findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that REMS-BMD and fragility scores are strongly driven by operator-entered demographics. This suggests the technology functions primarily as a sophisticated, demographically weighted algorithm to predict DXA BMD. The algorithm’s weighting of demographics might overwhelm any potential adjustment from the ultrasound signal. Further data is required, but in the interim clinicians should be wary when REMS results are discordant with the overall clinical picture, especially when assessing biological outliers. Similarly, caution is needed when using REMS output with FRAX or monitoring patients over time, where the profound influence of demographics may obscure true skeletal changes. Clarifying these issues, ideally with the input of the manufacturer, will determine the role of REMS in osteoporosis diagnosis and longitudinal patient monitoring.
