Behavioral and cognitive gains from digital game-based psychological intervention for at-risk Chinese university EFL learners with internet addiction
Authors: Yaoyao Zhu, Chunlin Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Internet addiction among university students has become a growing concern, yet effective interventions that reduce it without conflicting with academic demands remain limited. This quasi-experimental study examined the effects of a digital game-based psychological intervention on producing behavioral and cognitive gains, specifically reducing internet addiction and enhancing English reading, writing, and translation proficiency, among at-risk Chinese university EFL learners with internet addiction. A total of 67 participants from two intact classes were randomly designated into either an experimental group (n = 34) or a control group (n = 33). The experimental group engaged with a digital game-based psychological intervention, whereas the control group received conventional instruction without game-based elements. Behavioral and cognitive gains, specifically improvements in English reading, writing, and translation proficiency, were assessed at the 17th week (post-test) and the 42nd week (delayed post-test) using self-developed English examination papers. In addition, changes in internet addiction were measured using the Gaming Disorder Screening Scale at the same time points. Independent samples t-tests and 2 × 3 mixed-design ANOVAs were used for data analysis. Results revealed that, compared with the control group, the experimental group achieved significantly higher gains in terms of English reading, writing, and translation skills, as well as a marked reduction in internet addiction levels. These findings suggest that a digital game-based psychological intervention may be effective in yielding dual benefits, alleviating internet addiction while enhancing English reading, writing, and translation proficiency, among at-risk Chinese university EFL learners. Practical recommendations include selecting language-rich games, regulating gameplay time, and integrating structured tasks for educators, as well as allocating resources for program development and teacher training for policymakers.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of digital technologies has established the Internet as an integral part of modern life. Among university students, Internet Addiction (IA) has emerged as a major concern, affecting approximately 11% of Chinese college students [48]. While non-pharmacological interventions exist, many require restricting Internet use, an approach that is impractical given the increasing necessity of online time for academic purposes [59]. Thus, there is a pressing need for interventions that reduce IA without conflicting with educational demands.

In the Chinese context, EFL learners represent a particularly pertinent subgroup for such an intervention. Unlike students in many other disciplines, EFL learners depend heavily on the Internet as an indispensable academic resource, for accessing authentic reading materials such as online news and academic articles, consulting digital dictionaries and corpora, and engaging with multimedia content for listening and speaking practice. This essential reliance on online resources means that restriction-based interventions, which require limiting Internet use, are not merely impractical but could actively hinder students’ language development [59]. Consequently, any viable intervention for this population must reduce IA without compromising their access to the digital tools and materials that underpin their academic work. These considerations establish EFL learners as a compelling population for investigating non-restrictive intervention strategies.

Digital game-based language teaching has been recognized as a promising pedagogical approach, fostering motivation and reducing anxiety [24]. Notably, recent evidence suggests that digital gaming can serve as an effective tool for language acquisition even among students with gaming addiction [58]. However, existing studies have primarily focused on linguistic outcomes among general student populations, leaving it unclear whether such an approach can simultaneously reduce IA while improving language skills. The mechanisms through which game-based instruction might alleviate IA symptoms also remain unexplored.

To address these gaps, the present study aimed to investigate the effects of a digital game-based psychological intervention in producing dual behavioral and cognitive gains among at-risk Chinese university EFL learners with IA. By examining both the outcomes and the underlying mechanisms, this research provides a foundation for integrating psychological and pedagogical approaches to support at-risk student populations.

Literature review

Previous studies on internet addiction

Internet addiction (IA) has prompted progress in identifying specific Internet-related disorders, although it is not formally recognized as a distinct clinical diagnosis [63]. The International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11), for instance, classifies “Gaming Disorder” as an addictive disorder associated with Internet use [15, 57]. While IA remains a broader, non-clinical umbrella term commonly used in educational research [51], Gaming Disorder is among its most frequently studied manifestations, particularly in young adult populations.

IA is not a categorical condition but a continuum, ranging from mild to severe. To assess where individuals fall on this continuum, researchers have developed several instruments, including the Internet Addiction Test [64], the Generalized Problematic Internet Use Scale [7], and the Compulsive Internet Use Scale [38]. While these tools do not provide clinical diagnoses, they offer valuable reference points for evaluating IA severity.

Based on such measures, global IA prevalence has been estimated to range from 0.8% to 22.2% across countries [42]. Prevalence also varies by age, with adolescents displaying the highest rates (13.1%), followed by adults (5.8%) and children (5%) [10]. These differences are largely attributed to situational and contextual factors, as adolescents have grown up in a digital era where Internet access is ubiquitous, particularly for educational purposes during and after the COVID-19 pandemic [62]. Thus, monitoring and addressing IA among university students remains a priority for psychologists and educators.

IA levels have risen in recent years [32], prompting extensive research into its consequences. Empirical evidence indicates that IA is positively associated with aggression [2, 11], anxiety [19, 36], depression [19, 54], impulsivity [23, 66], and low self-esteem [28, 67]. Conversely, IA is negatively correlated with subjective well-being [40, 52, 61], mental health [8, 43], and academic performance [9, 21]. These findings underscore the need for effective IA interventions.

IA is widely recognized as a condition requiring professional intervention [44, 53, 65]. Identifying contributing factors, such as individual traits, family dynamics, peer relationships, social context, and online platform features, is essential for developing effective mitigation strategies [55, 63, 67]. Existing prevention and intervention efforts include promoting alternative activities, enhancing parental control, implementing structured routines, and equipping educators with pertinent resources [1]. However, the shift to digital game-based learning has complicated screen time management, as limiting Internet use may conflict with academic demands [59]. Thus, there is a need for interventions that reduce IA without restricting necessary online time for educational and occupational purposes.

In China, a substantial population is affected by IA. In the Chinese EFL context, recent studies have documented the detrimental effects of digital media overuse on language learning. Shu [49] surveyed 480 Chinese EFL learners and found that problematic use of social media was significantly associated with low language achievement, high foreign language anxiety, high academic burnout, and negative aspects of perfectionism. Additionally, Yao et al. [63] demonstrated that an emotional intelligence training program could effectively alleviate IA symptoms among at-risk Chinese college students, though their study focused on psychological outcomes rather than language learning. Extending this line of inquiry, Guan et al. [20] examined 1,448 students across nine Chinese public universities and revealed that emotional intelligence negatively predicted problematic social media use, with social exclusion and experiential avoidance serving as mediating mechanisms. The gravity of these issues is not confined to individual samples but is of national scale. Approximately 11% of Chinese college students exhibit IA symptoms, with higher prevalence among males (16%) than females (8%) [48]. Based on official statistics reporting 48.46 million college students in 2024, an estimated 5.33 million students in China may be identified with IA. Given this staggering number and the documented negative effects of IA on academic performance, there is an urgent need for interventions that are both effective and compatible with students’ academic routines.

Pharmacological treatments for IA have shown limited efficacy and are often accompanied by side effects [29, 39]. In contrast, non-pharmacological interventions, particularly psychological interventions, are favored due to their safety profile and minimal adverse effects [14]. Psychological interventions for IA include several approaches. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), for instance, has been evaluated in student populations [60, 69]. However, CBT often requires restricting Internet use, an impractical demand for students whose academic work depends on online resources. The present study proposes a digital game-based psychological intervention as an alternative that does not require Internet restriction. By embedding language learning within gaming activities, this approach redirects rather than restricts online time, offering an alternative approach to IA reduction.

Digital game-based teaching

Digital technologies have fundamentally transformed language education by improving learner engagement [56] and educational outcomes [27, 68]. Digital game-based teaching was conceptualized by Erhel and Jamet [18] as a competitive activity wherein students pursue educational objectives aimed at facilitating knowledge acquisition. They further emphasized that such games may be designed to enhance learning or cognitive skill development by providing a virtual environment for learners to practice their abilities. Digital game-based teaching is widely regarded as a promising pedagogical approach, capitalizing on the intrinsic appeal of games to foster interactive and immersive learning experiences [17].

Extensive scholarly inquiry has investigated the impact of digital game-based teaching on language acquisition. Numerous studies [5, 24, 25, 35, 41, 47] have documented its benefits in foreign language acquisition, including increased motivation, reduced anxiety, the cultivation of positive attitudes toward the language learning process, and the promotion of cultural awareness, all of which contribute to effective language acquisition.

For example, Hong et al. [24] found that gamified grammar exercises enhanced epistemic curiosity and reduced language anxiety among EFL learners, while Li et al. [35] demonstrated that out-of-school digital gaming provided rich opportunities for authentic language use and socialization. These studies, together with others [5, 25, 41, 47], have established the pedagogical potential of digital games for foreign language learning.

The mechanisms through which digital game-based teaching facilitates language learning can be summarized as follows. First, gamified elements inherent in such teaching, such as dynamism, unpredictability, immediate feedback, achievement, and interactivity, render the acquisition process enjoyable and motivating [47]. Second, digital games provide platforms where learners are compelled to use the target language and engage with online communities, thereby facilitating interaction and socialization [35]. Third, digital games support learner-centered pedagogies by enabling learners to exercise active control over their learning processes [5].

Beyond these pedagogical mechanisms, however, digital game-based teaching may also operate through a psychological pathway that is central to understanding its potential for addressing IA. Drawing on Self-Determination Theory (SDT; [16], digital games have been shown to satisfy three basic psychological needs: autonomy (the sense of control over one’s actions), competence (the experience of mastery and efficacy), and relatedness (the feeling of connection with others). These needs, when fulfilled, enhance intrinsic motivation and psychological well-being [45, 46]. When learners satisfy these needs through constructive, goal-oriented gaming activities, they may experience reduced urges to seek compensatory gratification from problematic Internet use, as addictive behaviors often arise from unmet psychological needs [6, 31]. In this sense, a digital game-based approach serves a dual function. It is simultaneously a pedagogical tool for language acquisition and a psychological strategy for behavioral substitution, replacing maladaptive online behaviors with rewarding, academically meaningful engagement. This dual conceptualization is central to understanding how the intervention might generate benefits that extend beyond language learning alone.

Despite these advances, the field has predominantly focused on vocabulary acquisition and affective outcomes, leaving the effects on integrated language skills, such as reading, writing, and translation, largely underexplored. Moreover, existing studies have employed small-sample qualitative designs or focused narrowly on discrete skills, limiting the generalizability of their findings. More importantly, none have examined whether this dual function can be intentionally leveraged to simultaneously reduce Internet addiction symptoms while promoting integrated language skills among at-risk learners.

The current study

The extant literature indicates that IA adversely influences learners’ physical and psychological well-being as well as their academic achievement [2, 9, 19]. What remains unclear, however, is whether the psychological mechanisms inherent in digital game-based instruction can be intentionally leveraged to serve this dual purpose, namely reducing IA symptoms while simultaneously improving integrated language skills among at-risk learners.

In light of these considerations, the present study seeks to conduct an experimental investigation to examine the effects of instructional digital games on IA levels and English reading, writing, and translation proficiency (English RWT proficiency) among at-risk Chinese university EFL learners of IA. Methodology

Design

This study employed a quasi-experimental pre-test, post-test, and delayed post-test design. The independent variable was the type of after-class assignment, with two levels: digital game-based activities (experimental group, EG) versus conventional homework exercises (control group, CG). The in-class instruction, however, was identical for both groups. The dependent variables were (a) IA severity, measured by the Gaming Disorder Screening Scale, and (b) English RWT proficiency, measured by three researcher-developed examination papers assessing reading, writing, and translation skills.

Participants

In China, university students receive training from both formal academic institutions and specialized organizations. To enhance specific skills, many enroll in such programs. Tangshan Siyue Education and Technology Institute (TSETI), a training provider, offers programs across 10 disciplines for university students, attracting over 3,000 participants annually.

Participants in this study were recruited from TSETI based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria required: (1) a score of 47 or above on the IA assessment, and (2) willingness to participate in a digital game-based psychological intervention. Exclusion criteria included: (1) diagnosis of other psychological disorders; (2) current involvement in alternative IA treatments; (3) refusal to participate in the digital game-based psychological intervention; and (4) cognitive impairments hindering cooperation.

Classes with more than 30 students scoring above the cut-off were considered eligible. Two classes that showed no significant differences in either IA or English RWT proficiency at pre-test (both ps > 0.05) were selected. One class was randomly designated as the EG (n = 34) and the other as the CG (n = 33) using a coin toss. Both classes were taught by the same instructor and followed the same curriculum.

The EG comprised 34 undergraduates (19 males, 15 females; aged 19–25, M = 22.6), and the CG comprised 33 undergraduates (18 males, 15 females; aged 19–25, M = 22.4). Instruments

Data were collected via a questionnaire and three English RWT proficiency tests. Although multiple instruments existed for assessing IA, such as Chinese versions of Young’s [64] 8-item Diagnostic Questionnaire [4], the Revised Chinese Internet Addiction Scale [22], and the Gaming Disorder Screening Scale [37], the Gaming Disorder Screening Scale was selected for its suitability. Developed specifically for Chinese students, the Gaming Disorder Screening Scale aligns with diagnostic frameworks such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5, [3], the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11, [57], and the cognitive model of Internet Gaming Disorder [34]. The scale consists of 18 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = never to 4 = always), yielding total scores from 18 to 72, with scores ≥ 47 indicating high IA risk [37]. Participants were given approximately 10 min to complete the scale.

In the original validation study of the Gaming Disorder Screening Scale, criterion validity was established through significant correlations between the Gaming Disorder Screening Scale scores and the Internet Addiction Test. For structural validity, exploratory factor analysis identified a three-factor structure (behavior, functioning, cognition and emotion) accounting for 62.4% of the variance, and confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated good model fit (χ2/df = 5.581; RMSEA = 0.074; TLI = 0.916; CFI = 0.928). The scale also demonstrated strong reliability, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.93 and a 15-day test–retest reliability of 0.79. Although the original validation study did not report a formal content validity index (CVI) based on expert ratings, its robust structural and criterion-related validity evidence, combined with the scale’s strong reliability, supports its appropriateness for use in the present study. In our sample, the scale demonstrated similarly high reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.88; test–retest = 0.89).

English language proficiency was evaluated using three researcher-developed examination papers, each with a maximum score of 100. These assessments were modelled on the format of the Chinese National Postgraduate Entrance Examination (Shuoshi Yanjiusheng Ruxue Kaoshi) and included four sections: Cloze Test (10% of total score with 20 items), Reading Comprehension (40% of total score with 25 items), Writing (25% of total score with 2 writing tasks), and Chinese-English Translation (15% of total score with one text). The cloze test and reading comprehension sections were scored objectively using standard answer keys. The writing and translation sections were scored independently by two experienced English instructors who were blind to the group assignment. For the writing section, the two raters used an analytic rubric assessing content, organization, grammar, and vocabulary. For the translation section, the raters used a rubric evaluating accuracy, fluency, and completeness. When the two raters’ scores differed by more than 3 points, they discussed the item and reached a consensus. Inter-rater reliability was assessed on a random sample of 20% of the papers (n = 13). The intraclass correlation coefficient was 0.89 for the writing section and 0.88 for the translation section, indicating excellent agreement. The three examination papers were reviewed and validated by three experts in English education, who confirmed their clarity, relevance, and appropriateness for measuring the target language skills, thus establishing face validity. Although a formal content validity index (CVI) was not calculated for the examination papers, the expert panel’s unanimous endorsement of the content coverage and item representativeness supports their content validity. The internal consistency of the three examination papers was examined using Cronbach’s alpha, which yielded coefficients of 0.85 for Paper 1, 0.86 for Paper 2, and 0.86 for Paper 3, indicating good reliability [13].

The digital game used in the intervention was Phasmophobia (available at https://store.steampowered.com/app/739630/Phasmophobia). Phasmophobia is a cooperative online game in which players work together to identify ghosts in haunted environments. The game offers extensive opportunities for linguistic input and output, as players must read in-game clues and equipment descriptions, view mission objectives, and communicate with one another in English to solve the cases. This game has been recognized as an effective tool for English language learning among Chinese university students [35].

The core English curriculum used for both groups was developed by English instructors at TSETI based on its in-house teaching materials, which were aligned with the National Postgraduate Entrance Examination English Syllabus (Shuoshi Yanjiusheng Ruxue Kaoshi Yingyu Dagang). The curriculum was structured around 16 thematic units, each covering vocabulary, reading comprehension, writing, and translation skills. Each unit introduced approximately 30–40 target vocabulary items drawn from past examination papers and high-frequency academic word lists. Reading passages were selected from English-language newspapers, academic journals, and examination archives, with texts ranging from 400 to 600 words in length to mirror the difficulty level of the entrance examination. Writing tasks were designed to reflect common examination prompts, including argumentative essays and letter writing. Translation exercises focused on English-Chinese translation of paragraphs on social, cultural, and scientific topics, consistent with the examination format. The three examination papers used in this study were all designed with reference to the question types, difficulty levels, and content scope of the National Postgraduate Entrance Examination, sharing the same underlying syllabus as the curriculum. Consequently, the instructional materials and the assessment instruments were intrinsically aligned in terms of topic areas, text difficulty, and skill coverage. This alignment ensured that any observed improvements in test performance could be attributed to the instructional approach itself rather than to coincidental matches between teaching content and test content.

Procedure and materials

In July 2022, IA levels were assessed using the Gaming Disorder Screening Scale across multiple intact classes. Classes with more than 30 students scoring ≥ 47 were considered eligible. After obtaining written informed consent from all participants, two researchers administered the English RWT proficiency test and the Gaming Disorder Screening Scale to all participants as the pre-test. The intervention lasted for 16 weeks, with both the EG and the CG attending one 90-min session per week, resulting in a total of 16 sessions for each group. During this period, one English instructor taught both groups. To minimize instructor-related bias, the same instructor received a thorough briefing on the study protocol and was instructed to deliver identical content and maintain consistent teaching demeanor across both groups. All teaching sessions were audio-recorded, and 20% of the recordings were randomly selected and reviewed by an independent researcher to verify fidelity to the planned curriculum (audit trail).

In terms of in-class instruction, both groups covered identical English content based on the core English curriculum, including vocabulary, reading comprehension, writing, and translation skills, as measured by the English proficiency tests. The EG was not exposed to any gameplay during class time; their in-class instruction followed the same format as the CG. In each 90-min session for both groups, the first 20 min were devoted to teacher-led explanation of vocabulary and grammar rules based on the course textbook. The next 30 min involved students completing individual and pair-based textbook exercises, during which the instructor circulated and provided assistance. Students were required to complete these exercises independently before discussing their answers with their partners. The following 25 min were allocated to student presentations and group discussions, during which students presented their answers to the class, shared any difficulties they encountered, and engaged in peer feedback. The instructor facilitated these discussions, corrected common errors, and highlighted key points. The final 15 min were used by the instructor to summarize the main content of the session, review the most important vocabulary and grammar rules, and assign homework.

The only difference between the two groups lay in their after-class assignments. The CG was required to complete conventional homework assignments (textbook exercises) after each session and review the material covered in class before the next session. No digital game elements were used in the CG sessions. In contrast, the EG replaced these written homework assignments with post-class gameplay, drawing upon the frameworks proposed by Li et al. [35] and Zhou [68]. Instead of completing conventional homework, students in the EG were required to play Phasmophobia for approximately 2 h outside of class each week. The game-based activities were designed to reinforce the same vocabulary and grammar points that the CG practiced through their textbook exercises.

Weekly structure of the digital game-based intervention for the EG (Post-Class Activities)

During the familiarization phase (Week 1), students explored the game environment, learned basic controls, and familiarized themselves with the English interface; no formal language tasks were assigned. During the vocabulary and reading phase (Weeks 2–4), students were given a list of target words drawn from the game interface; they played the game to encounter these words in context, recorded each word with its in-game meaning and usage in a vocabulary log, and submitted the log at the start of the following session. During the reading comprehension phase (Weeks 5–8), students played the game, read in-game equipment descriptions and mission objectives, and wrote a 100–150 word case summary in English identifying the key clues and their connections. During the writing phase (Weeks 9–12), students played the game and wrote a 150-word game summary describing their gameplay strategies, the challenges they encountered, and the outcomes, using a structured template provided by the instructor. During the integrated skills phase (Weeks 13–15), students played the game and completed an English-to-Chinese translation task based on in-game texts, followed by a brief written reflection linking their gaming experience to the vocabulary and structures learned in previous weeks. Week 16 served as a review phase, during which students revisited all vocabulary logs and written summaries in preparation for the post-test. All written outputs were submitted weekly, and the instructor provided written feedback at the beginning of each subsequent session.

To ensure that the intervention was implemented as intended, compliance was monitored through multiple channels. First, students’ gameplay time was tracked via Steam’s built-in playtime tracker. The average weekly gameplay time for the EG was 2.06 h (SD = 0.32), closely approximating the required 2-h target. Second, task completion was monitored through weekly submission records. The submission rate for all assigned tasks (vocabulary logs, case summaries, game summaries, translation tasks, and reflections) was 97.24% across the 16-week intervention period. Third, students’ engagement quality was assessed through brief weekly online feedback forms. Over 91.35% of respondents reported that they found the tasks “engaging” or “very engaging.” Collectively, these data indicate that the intervention was delivered with a high degree of fidelity and that participants in the EG actively complied with the assigned tasks.

Follow-up assessments of English RWT proficiency and IA (using the same Gaming Disorder Screening Scale) were conducted at the 17th week (post-test, immediately after the 16-week intervention) and again at the 42nd week (delayed post-test, 26 weeks post-intervention). Upon completion of data collection, the researchers proceeded with data analysis.

Data analysis

Participant responses to questionnaires and test papers were evaluated and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 27.0. Descriptive statistics were reported as means with corresponding standard deviations (SD). An independent samples t-test was employed to examine the differences of IA and English RWT proficiency between CG and EG in the pre-test. A 2 (Group: EG vs. CG) × 3 (Time: pre-test, post-test, delayed post-test) mixed-design analysis of variance (mixed ANOVA) was used for between-group comparisons in the post-test and delayed post-test. This method was chosen because it allowed for the simultaneous examination of between-subjects effects (group differences), within-subjects effects (changes over time), and, most importantly, the Group × Time interaction effect, which indicated whether the two groups changed differently over the course of the intervention. Statistical significance was determined based on p values and effect sizes (partial η2), with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. Following Cohen’s [12] guidelines, partial η2 values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14, or r values of 0.1, 0.3, and 0.5, were interpreted as small, medium, and large effect sizes, respectively.

Ethical consideration

This study adhered to strict ethical standards. Participation was voluntary, with no monetary compensation or gifts provided. However, all participants were granted complimentary access to the digital game-based psychological intervention. The research team provided a comprehensive explanation of the program to participants in both the EG and CG. Participants’ personal data were anonymized using unique codes, stored on a password-protected computer, and accessed only by the research team. All responses were treated as confidential and used exclusively for research purposes. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the start of the training, authorizing the publication of this report, including all related data and images. The CG served as a comparator for data analysis, with the assurance that its members would have the opportunity to participate in future digital game-based psychological intervention programs if they so wished.

Regarding IA, the EG showed a decrease from pre-test to post-test, whereas the CG remained relatively stable. The following subsections report the inferential statistical analyses examining these patterns.

Research question one: effect of the intervention on English RWT proficiency

To determine whether the observed differences in English RWT proficiency were statistically significant, an independent samples t-test was first conducted on the pre-test scores. The results revealed no significant difference between the EG (M = 59.47, SD = 8.31) and CG (M = 58.61, SD = 3.10) at pre-test, t(42.25) = −0.57, p = 0.574.

A 2 × 3 mixed-design ANOVA was then conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention. Mauchly’s test of sphericity was not violated (W = 0.92, p = 0.081); however, Greenhouse–Geisser corrected values were reported for consistency. The results revealed a significant main effect of group, F(1, 65) = 25.21, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.28, indicating that overall English RWT proficiency scores differed significantly between the two groups. A significant main effect of time was also found, F(1.86, 120.86) = 34.29, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.35, suggesting that performance changed across the three time points. Crucially, this was qualified by a significant Group × Time interaction, F(1.86, 120.86) = 4.72, p = 0.012, partial η2 = 0.07, indicating that the pattern of change over time differed between the two groups.

To decompose the significant interaction, simple main effects analyses were conducted. To control for Type I error due to multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied. For the EG, the effect of time was significant, F(2, 64) = 23.64, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.43. Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons showed a significant increase from pre-test (M = 59.47, SD = 8.31) to post-test (M = 67.91, SD = 5.37), p < 0.001, and from pre-test (M = 59.47, SD = 8.31) to delayed post-test (M = 68.38, SD = 4.05), p < 0.001, while the change from post-test (M = 67.91, SD = 5.37) to delayed post-test (M = 68.38, SD = 4.05) was not significant (p = 0.990). For the CG, there was also a significant effect of time, F(2, 64) = 8.21, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.20. Pairwise comparisons with a Bonferroni adjustment revealed that performance significantly increased from pre-test (M = 58.61, SD = 3.10) to post-test (M = 63.27, SD = 4.26), p < 0.001, and from pre-test (M = 58.61, SD = 3.10) to delayed post-test (M = 62.18, SD = 4.95), p = 0.009. However, the change from post-test (M = 63.27, SD = 4.26) to delayed post-test (M = 62.18, SD = 4.95) was not significant (p = 0.442).

Research question two: effect of the intervention on IA levels

An independent samples t-test on the pre-test IA scores revealed no significant difference between the EG (M = 54.65, SD = 4.97) and CG (M = 55.82, SD = 5.32), t(65) = 0.93, p = 0.355.

A 2 × 3 mixed-design ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention on IA reduction. Mauchly’s test indicated that the assumption of sphericity was violated (W = 0.63, p < 0.001); therefore, Greenhouse–Geisser corrected values were reported (ϵ = 0.73). The results revealed a significant main effect of Group, F(1, 65) = 17.77, p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.22, indicating that overall IA scores differed significantly between the two groups. A significant main effect of time was also found, F(1.46, 95.06) = 5.75, p = 0.009, partial η2 = 0.08, suggesting that IA scores changed significantly across the three time points. Importantly, this was qualified by a significant Group × Time interaction, F(1.46, 95.06) = 8.01, p = 0.002, partial η2 = 0.11, indicating that the pattern of change over time differed between the two groups.

To decompose the significant interaction, pairwise comparisons were conducted to examine the simple main effects of time within each group. To adjust for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied. For the EG, IA scores decreased significantly from pre-test (M = 54.65, SD = 4.97) to post-test (M = 50.62, SD = 4.97), p < 0.001, and from pre-test (M = 54.65, SD = 4.97) to delayed post-test (M = 50.59, SD = 4.80), p < 0.001, while the change from post-test (M = 50.62, SD = 4.97) to delayed post-test (M = 50.59, SD = 4.80) was not significant (p > 0.999). In contrast, for the CG, no significant differences were found between any of the time points (M = 55.82 and SD = 5.32 in the pre-test, M = 56.58 and SD = 4.82 in the post-test, M = 55.76 and SD = 5.12 in the delayed post-test; all ps > 0.05).

Discussion

This study assessed the impact of digital game-based after-class assignments within a 16-week English program. Two cohorts of at-risk Chinese university EFL learners with IA, with comparable baseline English RWT proficiency and IA, received the same in-class instruction (vocabulary, reading, writing, and translation) but different after-class assignments. The EG participated in a digital game-based psychological intervention program, whereas the CG completed conventional textbook exercises.

Research question one: effect of the intervention on English RWT proficiency

These findings align with prior research [5, 24, 58, 68], which has documented the benefits of game-based instruction for language learning. In particular, Wu et al. [58] reported improved vocabulary acquisition through digital gaming in a self-identified gaming enthusiast, offering initial evidence that even at-risk populations may benefit linguistically from gaming. However, their study was limited to a single participant, focused narrowly on vocabulary, and examined informal learning contexts outside the classroom. The present study extends this evidence by providing further support for the efficacy of such an approach among a broader cohort of at-risk Chinese university EFL learners with IA. Specifically, our study employed a quasi-experimental design with 67 participants, targeted integrated language skills (reading, writing, and translation) rather than vocabulary alone, and embedded the intervention within a formal educational setting. Moreover, beyond linguistic outcomes, our study simultaneously examined the effects of the intervention on IA symptoms, a dimension not addressed in prior work. This broader scope offers more robust evidence that game-based approaches may benefit at-risk student populations across multiple domains.

The observed improvements in English RWT proficiency among the EG can be meaningfully interpreted through the lens of Self-Determination Theory (SDT; [16]. According to SDT, intrinsic motivation is fostered when three basic psychological needs are satisfied: autonomy, competence, and relatedness [45].The digital game-based psychological intervention in this study satisfied these needs through features that distinguish it from conventional instruction. First, its enjoyment-oriented nature, characterized by challenges, immediate feedback, and rewards, enhanced learner engagement and cultivated positive attitudes toward language acquisition [26]. Within the SDT framework, this enjoyment can be attributed to the satisfaction of autonomy (through voluntary engagement with game tasks) and competence (through progressive challenges and performance feedback). Second, the intervention adopted a learner-centered paradigm, positioning students as active explorers who acquire language through experimentation and problem-solving [33]. This approach further supported autonomy by granting learners a sense of control over their learning processes. Third, the intervention was utilization-focused, creating environments that require learners to produce the target language and participate in interest-based online communities, thereby facilitating interaction and socialization [35]. This feature directly addressed the need for relatedness, as students collaborated and communicated in meaningful contexts. These interrelated characteristics, when understood collectively as need-supportive features, appear to have contributed to enhanced intrinsic motivation for English learning, which may have led to more sustained engagement with reading, writing, and translation tasks and, consequently, improved test performance. This interpretation aligns with prior research demonstrating that SDT-based game designs effectively promote language learning outcomes [24, 68].

However, our results run counter to those of Shu [49], who found that problematic use of digital media among Chinese EFL learners was negatively associated with overall language attainment. The probable reasons for this discrepancy may be attributed to two key differences in the nature of digital engagement and the presence of pedagogical scaffolding. First, Shu [49] examined social media usage in a recreational, self-directed context, where learners engaged with digital platforms without structured linguistic objectives, often leading to distraction and academic burnout. In contrast, our intervention directed learners’ digital engagement toward a curriculum-aligned, goal-oriented gaming activity that systematically reinforced reading, writing, and translation skills through weekly tasks and instructor feedback. This reframing of digital time from passive consumption to active, productive language use may have transformed the academic impact from negative to positive. Second, the pedagogical scaffolding embedded in our program, including vocabulary logs, case summaries, and written reflections, likely played a crucial role in facilitating transfer from gameplay to measurable skill gains. Without such scaffolding, as in the contexts examined by Shu [49], the linguistic benefits of digital engagement may remain incidental and insufficient to counteract the negative effects of excessive screen time. These differences appear to suggest that the academic outcomes of digital media use may depend critically on how such engagement is structured and supported, rather than on the mere presence or absence of digital activities.

Research question two: effect of the intervention on IA levels

The significant reduction in IA levels observed in the EG, sustained over 26 weeks post-intervention, can also be understood within the SDT framework. Several factors contribute to elevated IA among university students, including individual traits, family dynamics, peer relationships, social environment, and online platform features [55, 67]. In the Chinese sociocultural context, loneliness and low self-esteem are particularly salient [30, 50]. Chinese society places strong emphasis on academic achievement, which serves as a key source of recognition and self-worth. Students who perform well receive affirmation from parents, teachers, and peers, fostering high self-esteem. In contrast, those with poor academic performance often face criticism, potentially leading to diminished self-esteem and social isolation. In response, some individuals turn to virtual environments for affirmation, thereby increasing their risk of IA.

From an SDT perspective, this compensatory dynamic reflects a failure to satisfy basic psychological needs in offline academic contexts, specifically, the need for competence (feeling capable and recognized) and relatedness (feeling connected to others) [45]. Within the proposed digital game-based intervention program, these needs were addressed in ways that repositioned gaming as a legitimate and effective means of language acquisition rather than a distraction from academic work. The need for competence was satisfied as students who excelled in game-based tasks received recognition from instructors and peers, enhancing their self-esteem through both gameplay and language learning. The need for relatedness was fulfilled as integrating gaming into the learning process encouraged open discussion of gaming strategies, fostering peer interaction and reducing loneliness. Students also exercised choice and control over their gaming strategies and learning approaches, thereby supporting their need for autonomy. Together, these mechanisms may have helped reduce IA levels among at-risk Chinese university EFL learners by redirecting need-seeking behavior from problematic Internet use toward constructive, academically meaningful activities.

However, the results diverge from those of the previous studies [49, 58] probably because the nature and purpose of gaming in our intervention differed fundamentally from the contexts examined in prior research. Previous studies have primarily focused on the negative consequences of recreational gaming, such as increased screen time, social withdrawal, and exacerbated IA symptoms. In contrast, our intervention repositioned gaming as a structured, goal-oriented language learning activity embedded within the formal curriculum. The integration of pedagogical scaffolding, including vocabulary logs, writing tasks, and instructor feedback, appears to have transformed gaming from a purely entertainment-driven activity into a purposeful academic exercise. This reframing may have redirected participants’ gaming engagement toward productive language use, potentially mitigating the adverse effects typically associated with gaming. Additionally, the collaborative nature of the game tasks may have fostered social connectedness and peer support, counteracting the social isolation often linked to IA.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the relationship between digital gaming and IA is not inherently negative; rather, its outcomes depend critically on how gaming is structured and integrated into educational contexts. When gaming is purposefully designed with clear pedagogical objectives, linguistic tasks, and social interaction, it may serve as a protective factor that reduces IA symptoms while simultaneously fostering language development. The mechanisms underlying this dual benefit can be understood through Self-Determination Theory, which posits that intrinsic motivation and psychological well-being are fostered when basic needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness are satisfied [16, 45]. In the present intervention, the game-based activities appear to have provided a context in which these psychological needs could be met, potentially enhancing motivation for English learning while reducing the drive to seek compensatory gratification from problematic Internet use. This theoretical lens helps to explain why the intervention yielded improvements across both behavioral and cognitive domains.

Conclusion

This study implemented a digital game-based psychological intervention program targeting at-risk Chinese university EFL learners with IA. The intervention aimed at reducing IA levels while simultaneously improving English RWT proficiency. Results provide preliminary evidence that the program may have enhanced participants’ English RWT proficiency and mitigated IA symptoms, with both effects sustained at the 26-week follow-up (42 weeks after pre-test). These findings suggested that addictive behaviors could be redirected into opportunities for academic engagement. Overall, the study concluded that integrating psychological and pedagogical approaches offers a promising strategy to address IA while supporting language learning in at-risk populations.

The findings have implications for educators, policymakers, and researchers across pedagogical, theoretical, and methodological dimensions. Pedagogically, for educators seeking to implement digital game-based instruction with students at risk of IA, game selection matters. Educators should choose games that require substantial language use (e.g., games involving reading comprehension and verbal interaction) rather than action-heavy games with minimal linguistic input. In addition, task integration enhances learning. Teachers should design structured language tasks tied to game content, such as vocabulary logs, strategy discussions, or post-game summaries, to ensure that gaming translates into measurable language gains. Furthermore, monitoring and support should be embedded. Instructors can periodically assess students’ IA symptoms, providing early feedback to those showing signs of increased dependence. For policymakers, it is recommended to allocate resources for developing localized digital game-based language programs and training teachers to implement them safely and effectively. Theoretically, this study contributes to the literature by providing initial evidence that digital game-based psychological interventions may be able to serve a dual function: reducing IA while simultaneously promoting English RWT proficiency. This finding offers a counterpoint to the conventional view of gaming as solely a risk factor for IA and instead suggests it as a potential protective factor when structured appropriately. Methodologically, the 16-week intervention with a 26-week follow-up offers evidence of sustained effects, suggesting the feasibility of implementing preventive interventions in educational settings without requiring formal clinical referral.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the quasi-experimental design relied on two intact classes for group assignment rather than random assignment of individual participants. While this approach minimized contamination between groups, it may have introduced cluster effects and unmeasured pre-existing differences between the two classes, such as variations in class dynamics, peer influence, or collective learning motivation, which could potentially confound the observed intervention effects. Second, participants were identified as at risk of IA based on self-report questionnaire scores rather than clinical diagnostic interviews, which may affect the accuracy of group classification. Third, although the intervention significantly reduced IA levels in the EG, post-test and follow-up scores remained above the established cut-off, indicating that symptoms were not fully ameliorated. Fourth, due to the lack of reliable epidemiological data on at-risk Chinese university EFL learners, the target sample size could not be precisely estimated. Consequently, the present sample of 67 students with IA indicators may not be fully representative of the broader at-risk population. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the study’s findings.

Building on the findings and limitations of this study, several directions for future research warrant consideration. First, to improve the accuracy of participant classification, future studies should incorporate clinical diagnostic interviews in addition to self-report measures when identifying at-risk individuals. Second, given that IA symptoms were reduced but not fully ameliorated, researchers should investigate complementary interventions (e.g., cognitive-behavioral therapy components, parental involvement modules, or mindfulness training) that might be integrated with the digital game-based approach to achieve greater symptom reduction. Third, to enhance generalizability, large-scale multi-site studies with more representative samples are needed to confirm the effectiveness of this intervention across different educational and cultural contexts. Fourth, future research should explore the long-term sustainability of intervention effects beyond 26 weeks, as well as potential mechanisms (e.g., changes in self-esteem, loneliness, or academic self-efficacy) that mediate the observed outcomes. Given the continued rise in Internet use among youth, the study underscores the urgent need for developing and implementing similar interventions across diverse educational settings, with particular attention to adapting game content to local languages and cultural norms.
