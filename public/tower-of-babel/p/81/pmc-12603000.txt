Acceptability and Uptake of Long‐Acting Reversible Contraceptives in Ethiopia: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Tariku Shimels, Zewdneh Shewamene, Ashish Bajracharya, Getachew Teshome
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimDespite their high effectiveness, the acceptability and utilization of long‐acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) in Ethiopia remain low and vary across population groups and regions. This systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to estimate the pooled levels of LARC acceptability and utilization by method type, population subgroup, and study context to inform reproductive health programming and policy.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and EBSCOhost (CINAHL), along with grey literature sources including Google Scholar and Google. Studies published between 1st January 2021 and 30th June 2024 that focused on adolescents, reproductive‐age women, female healthcare providers, and communities in both rural and urban settings in Ethiopia were included. Pooled estimates were calculated using a random‐effects model, and heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic. The review protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024594288). Findings were presented as proportions with corresponding 95% confidence intervals.ResultsOut of 527 records identified, 50 studies comprising a total of 83,082 participants met the inclusion criteria. The pooled acceptability of LARCs (implants or IUCDs) was estimated at 24% (95% CI: 18%–31%), with implants alone showing higher acceptability at 26% (95% CI: 12%–49%) compared to IUCDs at 19% (95% CI: 12%–30%). The overall utilization of LARCs was 20% (95% CI: 15%–27%), with the highest uptake observed among women in the immediate postpartum or postabortion period at 30% (95% CI: 21%–39%). Utilization of implants alone was 15% (95% CI: 11%–20%), while IUCD use remained lower at 5% (95% CI: 4%–7%), with slightly higher usage in urban areas (9%). Considerable heterogeneity was detected across studies, with I² values exceeding 90%.ConclusionLARC acceptability and utilization remain suboptimal in Ethiopia, particularly among adolescents and rural populations. Implants are more widely accepted and used than IUCDs. These findings indicate the need for targeted, context‐specific interventions to address demographic, cultural, and health system‐related barriers to LARC uptake.Keywords: acceptability, contraceptives, Ethiopia, implants, IUCDs, LARCs, meta‐analysis, systematic review, uptake, utilization

Background

Long‐acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) including intrauterine contraceptive devices (IUCDs), as also known as intrauterine devices (IUDs), and birth control implants, are family planning methods with over 99% effectiveness in preventing unintended pregnancy [1]. Several global health initiatives strongly advocate for LARCs due to their efficacy and benefits for reproductive health. The World Health Organization (WHO) endorses LARCs as safe and effective options for diverse populations, including adolescents and postpartum women [2, 3], and Family Planning 2030 (FP2030) partners with global stakeholders to improve LARC access and reduce unmet contraceptive needs [4]. The United Nations Population Fund Agency (UNFPA) firmly advocates for the integration of (LARCs) into healthcare systems in low and middle‐income countries, providing crucial support and encouragement towards achieving the health and gender equality outlined in the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [5]. Similarly, the Global Financing Facility (GFF) supports maternal and child health by incorporating family planning services, including LARCs, into routine services [6], and the International Planned Parenthood Federation (IPPF) collaborates with governments to promote LARC accessibility, reproductive autonomy, and lower unintended pregnancy rates [7].

Despite ongoing efforts, the acceptability and utilization of LARCs in low and middle‐income countries are still challenged by factors such as unnecessary restrictions by healthcare providers [8], women's knowledge and misconceptions [9] regarding these methods and sociocultural norms surrounding fertility and male disapproval [10] among others. Increasing healthcare provider training may enhance but uptake addressing women's understanding is also crucial, as evidence from a systematic review indicates [11].

In Ethiopia, there have been fragmented systematic reviews and meta‐analyses examining specific aspects of family planning, such as immediate postpartum IUCD use [12], factors affecting long‐acting family planning method use [13], and reasons for implant discontinuation [14]. However, no comprehensive reports address the overall acceptability and utilization of these methods, especially during the COVID‐19 pandemic period or under the Ethiopian Health Sector Transformation Plan II (HSTPII), which spans from 2020/21 to 2024/25 [15]. Before the pandemic, national data from the 2019 Ethiopia Mini Demographic and Health Survey (EMDHS) reported a modern contraceptive prevalence rate of approximately 41%, with implants comprising the majority of LARC use and IUCD uptake remaining consistently low [16]. However, the onset of COVID‐19 disrupted reproductive health services across the country. Studies have documented supply chain interruptions, peoples' mobility restrictions and reduced facility‐based service delivery [17, 18], all of which may have significantly constrained both access to and use of LARCs, especially among adolescents and women in rural settings.

Notably, the acceptability and use of LARCs vary significantly by region and population subgroup. For instance, in the Gamo Zone, IUCD acceptability rates reached 35.6% among immediate postpartum women, with positive influences from antenatal counseling and healthcare support [19]. Acceptability of IUCDs among extended postpartum women is low, at just 2.3%, due to social and religious barriers and limited knowledge, such as religious beliefs and husband's influence [20]. A study conducted in 2020 in southwestern Ethiopia found that IUCD acceptability among non‐postpartum women was 30% [21], compared to 11% in an urban study of a similar population group [22]. Utilization rates for implants also vary significantly; only 3.5% of non‐postpartum women reported implant use in a national study [23], while postpartum uptake in urban settings, such as Harar, can reach as high as 58% [24]. Among adolescents, however, utilization of either IUCDs or implants remains consistently low, as reported in a study from the Tigray region [25].

This review focuses specifically on IUCDs and implants, as they are the only WHO‐recommended LARC methods currently available majorly within Ethiopia's public health system. Their broader adoption is essential to achieving the country's reproductive health goals under the HSTP II and global commitments such as FP2030. Despite these policy efforts, national evidence continues to show disproportionately low uptake, particularly for IUCDs, highlighting a disconnect between availability and utilization.

The review seeks to address critical gaps in understanding the acceptability and utilization of LARCs in Ethiopia. While national surveys such as the demographic health survey (DHS) provide valuable aggregate data, they often lack disaggregated insights into how LARC uses vary across demographic groups and geographic contexts. By focusing on adolescents, immediate and extended postpartum women, rural versus urban populations, and method‐specific differences (implants vs. IUCDs), this review offers a more detailed and contextualized understanding of the factors influencing LARC uptake. These findings align with and support the Ministry of Health's ongoing efforts to expand contraceptive access and scale up LARC use under national strategies. By synthesizing recent and representative evidence, the review highlights actionable opportunities to tailor interventions that are both population‐ and context‐specific. This evidence base is intended to guide policymakers and program implementers in refining strategies that promote equitable access, improve acceptability, and increase the utilization of LARCs—ultimately contributing to improved reproductive health outcomes across Ethiopia.

Methods

Eligibility Criteria

This review included studies focusing on women in the reproductive age group at different phases and roles, such as adolescents, women in the postpartum period, healthcare providers, and communities within Ethiopia. The condition of interest was the acceptability and utilization of LARCs across diverse populations. Contexts spanned health facility‐based settings, rural and urban environments, and varied cultural backgrounds in Ethiopia. Outcomes considered were related to the extent of acceptability or intention to use and utilization or uptake of LARCs, specifically implants and IUCDs. We followed the below definitions for certain terminologies in this review:

Acceptability: Often referred to as ‘intention to use’ of LARCs, reflects women's favourable perception or approval toward using IUCDs, implants, or both. Studies examining this concept were treated under this category.

Utilization: Also described as ‘uptake’ of LARCs, this term encompasses both immediate and long‐term use of IUCDs, implants, or LARCs in general. While there may be a subtle difference between utilization and uptake related to timing, all primary studies on this topic were analyzed as a combined measure of utilization or uptake.

Immediate Postpartum Period: Refers to the acceptance, intention to use or uptake of IUCDs, implants, or LARCs within the first 48 h following childbirth.

Extended Postpartum Period: Refers to the acceptance, intention to use or uptake of IUCDs, implants, or LARCs during the first 12 months after childbirth.

Sources and Search Strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across multiple bibliographic databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and EBSCOhost (CINAHL). Grey literature sources such as Google Scholar, and Google were also explored. Search terms included ‘acceptability,’ ‘utilization,’ ‘intention to use,’ ‘uptake,’ ‘LARCs,’ ‘Implant*,”IUCD*,’ ‘contraception,’ ‘acceptability,’ and ‘Ethiopia,’ using Boolean operators (AND, OR) (See Supplementary file 1). The review included studies published between January 1, 2021, and June 30, 2024, in English.

Screening and Data Extraction Process

This review followed the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) guidelines to ensure a transparent and systematic approach in identifying, screening, and including relevant studies [26]. After studies were located from various sources and imported into EndNote 9, duplicates were removed manually before proceeding with title and abstract screening. Each item was manually screened based on titles, abstracts, and full‐text readings. Data were then extracted using a standardized template that included study identification, publication year, design, setting, population, objectives, and outcomes (acceptability and utilization). Two reviewers (TS and ZS) performed the screening and data extraction, with the third or fourth reviewers (AB or GT) available to resolve any disagreements.

Quality Assessment

The quality of the included studies was assessed based on their design. For observational studies, the Newcastle‐Ottawa Scale (NOS) [27] was used, and mixed‐methods studies were evaluated with the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [28].

Outcome Measure

The outcomes assessed in this review focused on the acceptability and utilization of LARCs, specifically, implants and IUCDs, among diverse Ethiopian populations. The outcomes included reported acceptability and utilization rates, which were analyzed by demographic and geographical subgroups to understand variation across different population segments, such as postpartum women (immediate and extended), adolescents, and healthcare providers.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2 [29], with the ‘metafor’ package [30] used to conduct meta‐analyses following the SAMPL (Statistical Analyses and Methods in the Published Literature) guidelines [31]. Pooled proportions for LARC acceptability and utilization were estimated using a random‐effects model with the logit (PLOGIT) transformation to stabilize variance. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochrane's Q‐test, I2 (I‐squared) statistics to quantify the percentage of total variation due to heterogeneity rather than chance, and τ² (tau‐squared) for estimating between‐study variance. Forest plots and Egger's regression tests were used to visualize publication bias. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using the ‘leave‐one‐out’ method to assess the influence of individual studies. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and p‐values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Pre‐specified analyses included pooled estimates and subgroup analyses by population (postpartum or postabortion women, non‐postpartum women, healthcare providers, adolescents and/or young adults), LARC type (implant vs. IUCD), study setting (urban, rural, national), and publication year (2021–2024). Exploratory analyses involved evaluating patterns in utilization over time and examining differences across regions and subgroups. All proportions are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Abbreviations and statistical terms such as LARC, IUCD, CI, I², and τ² were defined upon first use in the manuscript.

Ethics Considerations

Since this study is a systematic review of secondary data, no ethical approval was required. However, the review adhered to established guidelines for conducting systematic reviews.

Results

Study Screening and Selection

The PRISMA flowchart for this review illustrates studies' identification, screening, and selection process across multiple databases. Initially, 527 records were identified from databases, including PubMed (148 records), Embase (253 records), Scopus (69 records), and Google Scholar (57 records), with no relevant records from EBSCOhost (CINAHL) and populated into EndNote 9 reference manager. Before the screening, 177 duplicate records were removed, resulting in 350 records for initial manual review. During the title and abstract screening phase, 267 records were excluded, leaving 83 records for a more detailed assessment. Of these, two records were removed as they did not report the outcome of interest, resulting in 81 reports sought for retrieval. One report could not be retrieved, leading to 80 reports assessed for eligibility. After full‐text assessment, 22 reports were excluded for reasons such as being outside the scope (13 reports), studies do not address the required quantitative estimates on acceptability and/or utilization (8 reports), or publication in an earlier year than required (9 reports). Finally, 50 studies were included in the systematic review, represented by 107 reports comprising a total of 83,082 participants. Study Characteristics

The studies included in this analysis primarily consist of cross‐sectional and mixed‐method designs conducted across Ethiopia from January 1, 2021 to June 30, 2024. They assessed the acceptability and utilization levels of LARCs, particularly implants and intrauterine contraceptive devices (IUCDs). Populations vary widely, including reproductive‐age women, postpartum and postabortion women, adolescents, and healthcare providers, with unique subgroups, including people living with HIV/AIDS [32], women with disabilities [33], and residents in pastoral [34] and rural areas [19]. Sample sizes range from small in a mixed‐methods study with 195 participants [35] to large‐scale national surveys involving over 14,593 individuals, reflecting both localized and nationwide perspectives on LARC‐related assessments [36]. The geographic coverage includes urban centers like Addis Ababa and underserved pastoral regions Boerena in Oromia region, Dasenech in Southern region and Afar region, providing insights into regional disparities in LARCs. Reported acceptability and utilization rates vary by subgroup and location. For instance, postpartum IUCD acceptability ranged from 12.5% [37] to 30% [21], while implant utilization among reproductive‐age women averaged around 24.7% [38], with some variation across regions and populations [39, 40] (Supplementary file 2).

Quality Assessment of Included Studies

All studies fulfilled the methodological quality assessment of both methods, ‘NOS’ for observational studies and, and ‘MMAT’ for mixed‐method designs. In one study, the crude odds ratio calculation was re‐estimated as the interpretation drawn and earlier estimates of a factor were not aligned [28].

Acceptability or Intention to Use LARCs

The acceptability of LARCs (either of implants or IUCDs) in Ethiopia shows considerable variation across different subgroups based on product type, publication year, and study context. The overall pooled estimate for acceptability is 24% (95% CI: 18%–31%), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 98.9%) based on x studies. About 28% (95% CI:20%–39%) of the women in the postpartum period accepted any of the LARC methods while those only 19% (95% CI: 11%–31%) of the women in the immediate postpartum or postabortion period accepted or had intention to use LARCs. When analyzed by product type, the highest acceptability was reported for implants or IUCDs, with a rate of 33% (95% CI: 19%–51%) based on data from four reports. Acceptability of implants alone was 26% (95% CI: 12%–49%) based on 3 reports, while IUCD alone had a lower rate of 19% (95% CI: 12%–30%) across 8 reports. Over time, acceptability remained relatively stable. In 2021, it was 19% (95% CI: 7%–42%) (based on how many reports), increasing to 26% (95% CI: 14%–42%) in 2022 and maintaining this level in 2023/24 at 26% (95% CI: 16%–41%). Contextual differences also influenced acceptability. In urban areas, the rate was 23% (95% CI: 16%–34%) across 8 reports, while rural areas had a higher acceptability rate of 34% (95% CI: 21%–49%) based on 4 reports. National or subnational studies showed the lowest acceptability at 15% (95% CI: 4%–44%) from 3 reports (Table 1).

Utilization of LARCs by Population

The overall utilization level of LARCs, including implants and IUCDs, in Ethiopia, was 20% (95% CI: 15%–27%) based on data from 25 studies. Among specific subgroups, utilization was highest among women in the immediate postpartum or postabortion period, with a rate of 30% (95% CI: 21%–39%) based on 8 studies, whilst the uptake in those in the extended postpartum period was 22% (95%CI: 15%–32%). Healthcare providers (HCPs) also showed a high uptake at 31% (95% CI: 13%–57%) across two studies. In contrast, utilization among women in the non‐postpartum period was 13% (95% CI: 5%–29%) from 8 studies, and among adolescents, the rate was lower at 12% (95% CI: 3%–40%) based on three studies. A high degree of heterogeneity was observed across the studies (I² = 99%), reflecting substantial variability in LARC utilization across these subgroups (Figure 2).

Utilization of LARCs by Year of Publication

Considering the year of publication, the extent of LARC uptake in Ethiopia was 25% (95% CI: 18%–34%) in 2021, based on 6 reports. The uptake remained the same in 2022, with a utilization rate of 25% (95% CI: 11%–48%) from 5 studies. In 2023, the uptake decreased to 17% (95% CI: 9%–30%) according to 9 studies, and in 2024, it was reported at 18% (95% CI: 8%–36%) from 5 studies (Figure 3).

Utilization of LARCs by Study Context

The utilization level of LARCs was also assessed using the context of the study across Ethiopia. It was found that 23% (95% CI: 17%–30%) of LARC users were in rural areas, based on 11 studies. Urban areas accounted for a slightly lower utilization rate of 22% (95% CI: 13%–36%) according to nine reports. National or subnational studies reported the lowest utilization level at 14% (95% CI: 4%–39%) from five reports (Figure 4).

Utilization of Implant‐Only Contraceptive

The utilization of implant contraceptives in Ethiopia varies considerably across different population subgroups, periods, and study contexts. Overall, the pooled estimate for implant use is 15% (95% CI: 11%–20%), with high heterogeneity (I² = 99.3%). Among population groups, utilization is highest among healthcare professionals with a level of 27% (95% CI: 12%–50%), followed by women in the extended postpartum period reported in 25% (95% CI: 17%–35%), those in the immediate postpartum or postabortion period, at 23% (95% CI: 15%–32%) and those in the non‐postpartum period, at 12% (95% CI: 7%–18%). Usage is significantly lower among adolescents and young women, at just 6% (95% CI: 0%–53%). Over time, implant use has shown a declining trend. In 2021, the utilization rate was 25% (95% CI: 15%–38%), but it dropped to 17% in 2022 and to 16% in 2023, and further decreased to 8% by 2024. In terms of study context, urban areas have the highest utilization rate at 24% (95% CI: 14%–40%), while the rate in rural settings is 17% (95% CI: 13%–22%). National or subnational studies report even lower rates of 9% (95% CI: 4%–17%) (Table 2).

Discussion

This review highlights considerable variation in the acceptability and utilization of LARCs across Ethiopian populations, with substantial heterogeneity among the included studies. The pooled acceptability rate for any of the LARC methods was 24%, with implants having higher acceptability (26%) than IUCDs (19%). Among postpartum women, acceptability was higher, at 28%, suggesting a greater openness to LARCs due to the need for effective contraception during this period. In contrast, acceptability was notably lower at 19% among women in the immediate postpartum or postabortion period, indicating that timing and the context of counselling play essential roles in influencing LARC acceptance. The generally low acceptance rate of LARCs can largely be explained by contextual factors such as cultural beliefs, religious considerations, and perceptions about the benefits and side effects of these methods. For example, the overall acceptability rate of implants in this context aligns with figures reported in the United States [44], while acceptance rates for IUCDs specifically, or for either method individually, were significantly lower. Low IUCD uptake, particularly in low‐income settings, has also been reported in other studies, likely due to limited promotional efforts and insufficient government commitment [45].

Utilization rates varied across subgroups. The overall utilization of LARCs in Ethiopia was 20%, with the highest uptake found among women in the immediate postpartum or postabortion period (30%). This prevalence is close to the 2019 national demographic survey estimate for 26 Sub‐Saharan African countries (21.7%) but significantly lower than the rate reported in Benin (55%) [46]. Several factors may contribute to this observed difference, including variations in healthcare infrastructure, accessibility of family planning services, and cultural and socioeconomic influences. Women in the extended postpartum period had a slightly lower utilization rate of 22%, which may reflect differences in counselling practices and access to contraceptives after initial postpartum care. Although this rate is higher than the Sub‐Saharan African average for postpartum women, it remains significantly lower than that reported in Rwanda [47]. One possible reason for the observed differences is that earlier national studies relied on gross postpartum data. In contrast, our review provides a more precise, disaggregated estimate for immediate and extended postpartum periods. Considering the uptake of specific product types, implants showed a remarkably higher figure with an overall utilization rate of 15%, while IUCD utilization remained low at 5%. This low uptake of IUCDs suggests that these methods generally face poor acceptability across diverse populations, likely influenced by a range of socio‐cultural, accessibility, and informational factors. A study reported that factors, such as education level, age, religion and income were the primary influencers of acceptability [48]. Other reasons included partner disapproval and low information about IUCDs [21], misconceptions [49] and fear and myths [50].

Notably, the review indicated low utilization of LARCs among adolescents (12%) and a lower uptake of implants among rural populations (17%), in contrast to significantly higher use among postpartum women and healthcare providers. Multiple factors likely contribute to these disparities. Among adolescents, barriers include limited availability of youth‐friendly reproductive health services, stigma surrounding premarital sexual activity, inadequate or non‐tailored counselling, and widespread misconceptions regarding side effects and potential infertility. A low rate of LARC utilization among adolescent girls has been consistently observed in many high‐fertility countries across Sub‐Saharan Africa [51]. In rural settings, structural challenges such as geographic inaccessibility, shortage of trained providers, irregular supply of contraceptive methods, and prevailing cultural or religious norms may limit both access and acceptability. These findings are consistent with evidence from previous scoping reviews and mixed‐methods studies [52, 53], which identified a broad range of determinants—including regional disparities, entrenched social norms, limited male partner involvement, and health system or provider‐level barriers—as key contributors to low contraceptive use among women in underserved settings.

The IUCD‐only utilization figure among women in the immediate postpartum or postabortion women was relatively higher (8%), which is similar to the finding documented in a previous systematic review and meta‐analysis (8.4%) [12]. While the lower IUCD‐only figure of overall use in the current review suggests a reduced level of uptake among other segments of the population, utilization among the immediate postpartum or postabortion women has been noted to be unchanged in the past couple of years. Among the factors reported to have an association with a lower rate of use across individual studies were misconceptions about IUCDs [54], such as fears around insertion, discomfort, and perceived side effects, which are more frequently cited barriers than those associated with implants, as well as user and supply side factors, including low knowledge of clients, insufficient counselling service and lack of trained provider [55]. On the other side, the discontinuation rate in some districts is significantly high [56], and many associated the reason for discontinuation of LARCs, in general, as side‐effects [49].

The review also highlighted the impact of urban versus rural settings on IUCD utilization, with urban areas showing a higher rate of 9% compared to the national rate of only two percent. Similarly, the extent of implant utilization in urban and rural areas showed differences with 24% and 17% rates respectively. Sample sizes and selection techniques may influence this urban, national or rural difference in individual studies, as well as the urban classification used in this review, where only studies from regional towns and Addis Ababa were designated as urban settings. While urban populations generally have better access to family planning resources, including trained providers who can address IUCD‐related concerns, the utilization rate remains low compared to similar figures reported in Burkina Faso [57]. Of the reasons often mentioned in the current review include poor knowledge, lack of supply and insufficient counselling services provided [55], fear of side‐effects or misperception and religious prohibition [21]. Overall, the extent of utilization across all settings remains suboptimal, which shows the need for improved awareness and education on LARCs, particularly IUCDs, to increase acceptance and reduce misconceptions.

This review found substantial heterogeneity across included studies (I² range: 89.1% to 99.6%). While demographic and contextual factors such as age, geographic location, and postpartum status were explored, other potential sources of heterogeneity warrant further consideration. These include differences in study design (e.g., facility‐based vs. community‐based surveys), sampling techniques, measurement tools for acceptability and utilization, and the definitions or timeframes used for postpartum periods. Variability in data collection periods, especially in relation to policy shifts, COVID‐19 disruptions, or service delivery models—may also have influenced outcomes. Furthermore, inconsistencies in how LARC methods were introduced or promoted in different regions could have contributed to divergent findings.

This review has several strengths and limitations. A key strength is its comprehensive scope, systematically synthesizing quantitative data on LARC acceptability and utilization across diverse Ethiopian populations, which provides valuable insights into demographic and regional differences. The review follows PRISMA and SAMPL guidelines and uses rigorous quality appraisal tools (NOS and MMAT). By calculating pooled acceptability and utilization rates, the review offers a clearer overall picture of LARC trends in Ethiopia and enables comparisons with national and regional benchmarks. Including subgroup analyses, such as postpartum women and adolescents, allows for targeted recommendations and highlights specific areas where interventions are needed most. Another strength of this review is its focus on recent studies, which offers valuable insights into current trends in LARC use in Ethiopia. Additionally, the use of robust statistical methods and sensitivity analyses strengthens the reliability of findings. This approach provides policymakers and practitioners with the most up‐to‐date and reliable information for decision‐making. However, limitations exist. High heterogeneity was observed across studies due to differences in study design, populations, and data collection methods. Some regions and hard‐to‐reach groups (e.g., pastoralist or conflict‐affected areas) were underrepresented. Also, despite an extensive search strategy, some grey literature or unpublished data may have been missing. We also acknowledge potential biases in primary studies. Lastly, acceptability was often measured as intention to use, which may not always translate to actual uptake, possibly overestimating real‐world acceptability.

Conclusion and Recommendation

This review reveals substantial variability in the acceptability and utilization of LARCs across different demographic and geographic groups in Ethiopia. The overall acceptability result shows such that implants were more widely accepted and utilized than IUCDs, especially among postpartum women, likely due to increased counseling during maternal health visits. However, utilization remains low among adolescents and rural populations due to sociocultural barriers, limited awareness, and restricted healthcare access.

To address these gaps, Ethiopia should implement context‐specific, multi‐level interventions. First, integrate comprehensive LARC counselling into routine antenatal, postnatal, and adolescent health services at both primary and referral‐level facilities. This counselling should be tailored by age group and parity status and delivered by providers trained in youth‐friendly and right‐based approaches. Second, expand community‐based distribution by leveraging Health Extension Workers (HEWs)—a cornerstone of Ethiopia's primary healthcare system—as part of a task‐sharing approach. HEWs are well‐positioned to deliver LARC education, raise awareness, dispel misconceptions, and refer clients to trained providers for method insertion. Strengthening their capacity through focused training, supportive supervision, and supply chain improvements is particularly critical in rural and pastoralist areas, where access to facility‐based services is limited and LARC uptake remains low. Third, engage male partners and local leaders through culturally sensitive community dialogues and media campaigns to challenge misconceptions and foster supportive environments for contraceptive decision‐making. Fourth, prioritize provider training on IUCD insertion and removal, ensuring sufficient commodity availability and supportive supervision in low‐performing regions. Lastly, strengthen monitoring mechanisms within the national health information system (DHIS2) to track LARC uptake by age, geography, and method type, enabling data‐driven program adjustments. These recommendations would help to improve LARC access and utilization equitably, ultimately contributing to better reproductive health outcomes and progress toward Ethiopia's HSTP II and the FP2030 commitments. Additionally, further research on regional and demographic barriers can help refine these strategies to support informed contraceptive choices and improve reproductive health outcomes across Ethiopia.
