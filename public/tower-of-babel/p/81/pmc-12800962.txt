Arthroscopic Reduction and Internal Fixation of Detached Osteochondritis Dissecans Lesions Using Bioabsorbable Screws and Fibrin Glue
Authors: Zirong Huang, Wenhan Yang, Hanyu Yao, Wenzhe Feng, Gen gen Huang, Yankan Ou, Weimin Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteochondritis dissecans is a disease that may lead to disability in adolescents and young adults, with a potentially multifactorial etiology. Currently, there are numerous treatment options for osteochondritis dissecans. A variety of implants can be used for fragment fixation. Each type of implant has potential advantages and disadvantages. Our Technical Note describes the arthroscopic preparation of the bone bed, followed by microfracture, repositioning of the detached cartilage with fixation using absorbable screws, and simultaneous edge sealing with biological protein glue at the margins.

Osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) is an acquired, idiopathic disorder affecting the subchondral bone and overlying articular cartilage, primarily in adolescents and young adults, with a higher prevalence in males and those engaged in high-impact sports.1,2 It most commonly involves the knee, particularly the lateral aspect of the medial femoral condyle, and can lead to joint pain, dysfunction, and premature osteoarthritis if untreated.1 The incidence of knee OCD is estimated at 9.5 to 29 per 100,000 in children and adolescents, with lesions classified as juvenile (open physes) or adult forms based on skeletal maturity.2,3

OCD formation is generally considered the result of multiple factors, including repetitive microtrauma, ischemia, and developmental abnormalities, leading to separation of osteochondral fragments ranging from mild subchondral instability with intact overlying cartilage to complete detachment and loose body formation.4

Clinical presentation varies by lesion stability: stable lesions often manifest as vague knee pain and altered gait, while unstable lesions may cause mechanical symptoms such as locking, effusion, and crepitus.2 Diagnosis relies on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to assess lesion stability, size, and subchondral involvement, with unstable lesions typically requiring surgical intervention to restore articular congruity and prevent long-term degeneration.5

Treatment strategies for unstable OCD lesions emphasize fragment preservation and fixation to promote healing. Nonoperative management succeeds in 50% to 90% of stable juvenile cases but fails for unstable ones, necessitating arthroscopic or open procedures.6 Current surgical options include drilling (transarticular or retroarticular) for stable lesions and fixation with metallic screws, bioabsorbable implants, or osteochondral autografts for unstable, salvageable fragments.2 While effective, these techniques have limitations: metallic screws often require secondary removal and cause MRI artifacts, bioabsorbable screws may risk implant failure or uneven compression, and autografts introduce donor site morbidity.7 Outcomes show healing rates of 76% to 85% with fixation, but complications like fragment nonunion or osteoarthritis persist in 10% to 40% of cases.1,8

We describe a technique using bioabsorbable screws combined with fibrin glue edge-sealing fixation for OCD, which may offer advantages over existing methods. This fully arthroscopic approach combines bioabsorbable screws for stable compression with fibrin glue to seal the fragment edges, enhancing biological integration, minimizing implant-related complications, and preserving cartilage integrity without secondary surgery.1,2 By addressing both mechanical stability and biologic healing, this method may improve outcomes in unstable OCD lesions, particularly in skeletally immature patients.

Indications

Arthroscopic fixation of osteochondral lesions is indicated for patients with unstable or detached but reducible OCD fragments, where the lesion is accessible to arthroscopy and operative instruments. This technique is particularly suitable for skeletally immature adolescents to promote biologic healing and minimize complications, specifically postoperative stiffness, infection, and pain; compared with open approaches, arthroscopy may also reduce soft tissue morbidity and wound-related complications.

Step 1: Patient Positioning and Preoperative Planning

Preoperatively, evaluate the involved knee with standard radiographs (anteroposterior and lateral views), computed tomography with 3-dimensional reconstruction, and MRI to assess the OCD lesion. Assessments include the integrity, size, and location of the detached cartilage, as well as the presence of small loose bodies (Fig 1). In patients with open physes, measure the distance from the cartilage defect surface to the physeal line to guide bioabsorbable screw length selection.

Proton density fat saturation magnetic resonance images of the right knee. Sagittal (A) and axial (B) images show a lateral femoral condyle osteochondritis dissecans fragment (arrows) with intact cartilage. ∗Cartilage defect area.

Position the patient supine with a tourniquet placed proximally on the thigh to minimize intraoperative bleeding and enhance visualization. Drape the affected limb over the edge of the operating table with the knee flexed at 90°. Administer spinal or general anesthesia based on patient preference.

Step 2: Arthroscopic Examination

To minimize incisions, select an initial portal distant from the lesion for a stable, widefield view. For example, preoperative MRI indicates cartilage detachment on the lateral femoral condyle, so the anteromedial portal is established first. Perform a thorough arthroscopic inspection of the entire knee joint to identify the site of cartilage damage, position of the detached fragment, and any small loose bodies (Fig 2). Use a spinal needle to localize the anterolateral portal, ensuring optimal instrument access to the target area. Gently extract the detached fragment extracorporeally for later use.

Lateral femoral condyle in the right knee visualized through the anteromedial portal. The patient is in the supine position with the knee flexed to 30°, and a complete osteochondritis dissecans can be visualized in the lateral femoral groove.

Step 3: Preparation of Bony Bed

Debride the subchondral bone using a motorized shaver and curette to remove overlying fibrous tissue. Smooth the edges of the defect without reshaping to a regular form, ensuring stable initial fixation upon fragment reduction. Perform microfracture on the bony bed with a depth of 4 mm and spacing of 2 to 4 mm using a microfracture awl, until bleeding or fat droplets are observed, promoting bone marrow stem cell release and healing (Fig 3).

This figure shows the lateral femoral condyle of the patient’s right knee. Arthroscopic view from the anterolateral portal. After clearing the proliferative fibrous tissue from the bone bed, microfracture is performed by drilling holes spaced 2 to 4 mm apart.

Step 4: Reduction and Fixation of Fragment

Preorient the detached fragment based on the defect morphology to minimize intra-articular adjustments. Insert a spinal needle through the skin for temporary fixation, then use straight forceps to gently reduce and press the fragment into the bony bed (Fig 4). Add 1 to 2 additional spinal needles for provisional fixation to prevent rotation during screw placement. Drill through the cartilage fragment and subchondral bone using a 2.0-mm drill bit. Insert 1 bioabsorbable screw (SinoBiom), ensuring the screw head is countersunk into the cartilage to avoid abrasion of opposing surfaces (Fig 5). Place an additional 2 bioabsorbable screws to provide compression and rotational stability. After all screws are secured, aspirate intra-articular saline with a suction device, and inject fibrin glue along the fragment edges to localize growth factors from microfracture within the defect, enhancing biologic integration (Fig 6). Postoperative Rehabilitation

Immobilize the knee in extension with a brace, and advise nonweightbearing for the first 2 weeks postoperatively. Initiate muscle control exercises, including patellar mobilization and straight-leg raises, on the first postoperative day. Begin range-of-motion exercises and partial weightbearing in week 3, progressing to full weightbearing by week 5. Recommend return to sports at 4 to 6 months postoperatively, contingent on MRI confirmation of lesion healing.

Discussion

In situ fixation is the preferred method for managing unstable OCD lesions, particularly in the early stages of the lesion when the osteochondral fragment retains biological viability. This strategy aims to create an optimal biomechanical environment for reintegrating the fragment with the bone bed through anatomic reduction and stable fixation, thereby restoring the smoothness and continuity of the articular surface and avoiding more invasive reconstructive surgeries.9 However, when the fragment is too small, is severely fragmented, or has undergone degeneration and loss of biological activity due to prolonged separation, in situ fixation becomes infeasible. In such cases, the clinical approach shifts to repair or regenerative techniques, including marrow stimulation methods that induce migration of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (e.g., microfracture or retrograde drilling), autologous chondrocyte implantation, osteochondral autograft transplantation (also known as mosaicplasty), and osteochondral allograft transplantation for large defects.1

When implementing in situ fixation, different fixation methods each have unique advantages and limitations. Metal screws, especially compression screws, are widely favored in clinical practice due to their superior initial fixation strength and compressive effects, ensuring tight apposition of the fragment to the bone bed and effectively promoting bony healing. However, their drawbacks are notable: the cartilage layer at OCD lesions is often thin, making screw insertion prone to damaging fragile cartilage tissue; additionally, protruding screw heads may cause mechanical abrasion to adjacent menisci or opposing articular cartilage, leading to secondary damage.10 More critically, metallic implants typically require a secondary surgery for removal after bone healing, which increases the risk of infection, postoperative joint stiffness, and additional medical burdens. Despite these shortcomings, for giant osteochondral fragments or patients with high athletic demands, metal screws remain a key option due to their reliable mechanical performance, but postoperative imaging monitoring is essential to track healing progress.

As an alternative, the suture anchor fixation technique involves implanting anchors into the bone bed and using high-strength sutures to secure the fragment, effectively avoiding wear issues caused by protruding implants.11 This method is particularly suitable for minimally invasive arthroscopic procedures, but it has a high technical threshold, requiring surgeons to precisely position anchors and adjust suture tension.12,13 Additionally, the risk of anchor pullout persists, potentially leading to fixation failure, especially in cases with suboptimal bone quality.

In recent years, the use of bioabsorbable screws has become increasingly common. These implants provide effective compression fixation while gradually degrading and being absorbed in vivo, eliminating the need for secondary surgery and significantly reducing the risk of long-term complications. Their slow degradation rate ensures that healing is complete before mechanical strength diminishes. However, different designs require careful consideration: headed designs offer reliable compression but retain abrasion risks similar to metal screws; headless countersunk designs avoid protrusion but may result in inadequate fragment–bone bed apposition—particularly in cases with concomitant microfracture, where exuded blood can create local hydraulic effects that exacerbate fragment instability. Therefore, applying appropriate and uniform pressure to the cartilage fragment during surgery is crucial. To further optimize fixation and potentially reduce the number of screws used, biological adhesives (e.g., fibrin glue) have emerged as an effective adjunct. Applied at the junction between the detached cartilage and normal cartilage, it seals gaps, prevents synovial fluid infiltration, and enhances the accumulation of growth factors from microfracture sites at the bone bed–fragment interface, thereby improving initial stability and promoting biological healing. A case report of solely using fibrin glue to successfully treat pediatric knee OCD showed complete fragment integration and no recurrence during follow-up,14 providing greater confidence for simplifying surgery to fibrin glue–only fixation in cases of small cartilage detachments.

Furthermore, arthroscopic surgery offers substantial advantages due to its minimally invasive nature. Compared to traditional open surgery, arthroscopy significantly reduces tissue trauma, postoperative pain, and recovery time, while lowering the risks of joint stiffness, infection, and long-term osteoarthritis. At the same time, arthroscopy enables comprehensive intra-articular exploration, allowing for 1-stage management of concomitant issues such as meniscal tears, ligament injuries, or loose bodies, thereby comprehensively enhancing overall therapeutic outcomes.

In summary, treatment decisions for unstable OCD must be highly individualized, comprehensively evaluating lesion size and location, fragment biological activity, patient age and functional demands, and surgeon expertise. Under contemporary treatment paradigms, arthroscopic bioabsorbable screw fixation combined with biological adhesive represents a highly promising approach. This method integrates the benefits of minimally invasive surgery with biological repair mechanisms, poised to maximize fixation efficacy while minimizing trauma and the risk of osteoarthritis progression. In the future, more rigorously designed, long-term follow-up randomized controlled trials are needed to clearly compare their long-term efficacy with other alternatives.

Video 1

This video shows the arthroscopic technique for preparing and fixing unstable osteochondritis dissecans (OCD) lesions in the knee using bioabsorbable screws and fibrin glue. The surgical video shows an OCD lesion of the patient’s right knee, with the patient positioned supine on the operating table. After anesthesia, arthroscopic exploration of the knee joint is performed through an anteromedial portal to identify the OCD lesion. The detached OCD fragment is removed and set aside for later use. The bone bed is then debrided, with a shaver used to remove superficial fibrocartilage, followed by microfracture. The detached cartilage fragment is repositioned, and a lumbar puncture needle is used to assist in temporary fixation. Bioabsorbable screws are inserted, with the screw caps embedded into the cartilage as much as possible to minimize secondary joint surface irregularities and wear. Finally, fibrin glue is injected into the boundary zone between the detached and normal cartilage, and a thin layer is applied over the surface of the detached cartilage for enhanced stability.
