Early versus delayed laparoscopic cholecystectomy following endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography for concomitant gallstones and common bile duct stones: a retrospective study
Authors: Jinhuan Liu, Hong Chen, Song Liu, Zhilin Sha, Yakai Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal timing for laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) following endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) in patients with both gallbladder and common bile duct stones remains debated. This study aimed to evaluate clinical outcomes based on the timing interval of sequential LC performed within two weeks post-ERCP.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 241 patients with concomitant cholecystolithiasis and choledocholithiasis who underwent ERCP followed by LC at our institution between January 2018 and December 2023. Patients were stratified into three cohorts based on the ERCP-to-LC interval: Group A (≤ 24 h, n = 78), Group B (24–72 h, n = 83), and Group C (72 h–2 weeks, n = 80). Comparative analyses were performed regarding baseline demographics, perioperative laboratory and imaging data, surgical findings, and postoperative recovery. Logistic regression was employed to determine risk factors associated with delayed surgery (72 h–2 weeks).ResultsCompared to Groups A and B, Group C exhibited a significantly prolonged total hospital stay (P < 0.001) and increased overall medical expenses (P < 0.001). Furthermore, Group C demonstrated higher incidences of post-ERCP acute cholecystitis (P = 0.005) and total postoperative adverse events (P = 0.042). Multivariate analysis identified preoperative total bilirubin exceeding three times the upper limit of normal, post-ERCP pancreatitis, and post-ERCP bleeding as independent predictors for delayed LC.ConclusionIn the absence of serious complications or high-risk factors, performing LC within 72 h after ERCP is recommended to reduce hospital stay, lower healthcare costs, and minimize complication rates. Introduction

Gallstone disease is one of the most common disorders of the digestive system worldwide, and the clinical incidence of concomitant choledocholithiasis and cholecystolithiasis is notably high. Studies have shown that approximately 3%–10% of patients with gallbladder stones also harbor common bile duct (CBD) stones, with an even higher proportion observed in older adults [1]. Such patients frequently present with abdominal pain, jaundice, and fever, and in severe cases may develop life-threatening complications such as acute cholangitis or acute pancreatitis [2]. Therefore, the prompt and effective clearance of CBD stones combined with cholecystectomy is essential to prevent recurrence and avoid serious complications.

With the ongoing advancement of minimally invasive surgical techniques, treatment strategies for patients with concomitant choledocholithiasis and cholecystolithiasis have become increasingly diverse. Currently, two primary approaches are employed: (1) one-stage laparoscopic common bile duct exploration combined with cholecystectomy (LCBDE + LC), and (2) a two-stage strategy consisting of endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) for stone extraction followed by sequential laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) [3]. Among these, the ERCP + LC strategy has gained widespread clinical acceptance owing to its minimal invasiveness, rapid postoperative recovery, and particular suitability for elderly patients or those with multiple comorbidities [4].

Despite its advantages, the optimal timing for performing LC after ERCP remains a matter of debate. Early LC may increase operative difficulty due to local edema, gastrointestinal distension, or the possibility that complications such as post-ERCP pancreatitis or bleeding have not yet fully manifested. Conversely, excessive delays may increase the risk of recurrent CBD stones, secondary choledocholithiasis, or acute cholecystitis, thereby prolonging hospital stay and escalating medical costs [5]. Research suggests that elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy should be performed within two weeks after endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography to balance surgical risk and stone recurrence [6]. However, several studies have suggested that performing LC within 72 h—or even within 24 h—after ERCP can significantly shorten hospital stay, reduce overall costs, and lower the incidence of acute cholecystitis [7, 8]. For instance, Wild et al. reported that same-day LC following ERCP reduced hospital stay by approximately two days and significantly decreased overall costs [9].

Most existing studies on the influence of the ERCP-to-LC interval on clinical outcomes—both within China and internationally—are limited by small sample sizes or single-center designs, resulting in inconsistent conclusions. In China, disparities in technical expertise, patient characteristics, and multidisciplinary team collaboration across medical centers further complicate the determination of optimal surgical timing. Therefore, systematic research evaluating the impact of different ERCP-to-LC intervals on perioperative outcomes, complication rates, and overall prognosis is of substantial clinical importance.

Materials and methods

General information

Clinical data of patients diagnosed with choledocholithiasis and cholecystolithiasis who underwent sequential ERCP and LC at the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University between January 2018 and December 2023 were retrospectively collected. Based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 241 patients were finally enrolled (Fig. 1), including 121 males (50.2%) and 120 females (49.8%). Patients were categorized into three groups according to the interval between ERCP and LC: Group A (≤ 24 h, n = 78), Group B (24–72 h, n = 83), and Group C (72 h–2 weeks, n = 80). All patients completed both ERCP and LC during a single hospital admission without interim discharge; no patient was readmitted for LC after being discharged following ERCP. The rationale for this three-group stratification was as follows: based on previous clinical practice, the 72-hour threshold is frequently used to define “early” versus “delayed” surgery, as it represents a clinically meaningful cutoff point for assessing the impact of surgical timing on postoperative outcomes. Meanwhile, the 24-hour cutoff was chosen to explore whether ultra-early intervention (within 24 h) confers additional benefits over surgery performed between 24 and 72 h.This study has passed the ethical review of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University. All ERCP procedures were performed by two experienced endoscopists (each with > 10 years and > 500 ERCP cases annually) using a standardized technique under conscious sedation. All LC procedures were performed by three senior laparoscopic surgeons (each with > 8 years and > 200 LC cases annually). The same team of endoscopists and surgeons was involved throughout the study period to ensure technical consistency.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Age 18–80 years; (2) Diagnosis of choledocholithiasis and cholecystolithiasis confirmed by abdominal ultrasonography, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography; (3) Treatment completed via ERCP combined with LC.

Exclusion criteria: (1) History of previous ERCP; (2) LC performed more than 2 weeks after ERCP; (3) Laparoscopic choledocholithotomy performed due to unsuccessful ERCP cannulation, incomplete stone removal, or LC performed only after achieving complete stone clearance via two or more ERCP sessions; (4) Conversion to another surgical procedure during LC (e.g., partial hepatectomy); (5) Postoperative pathological diagnosis of gallbladder malignancy; (6) History of upper gastrointestinal reconstruction surgery (e.g., Billroth II anastomosis, Roux-en-Y choledochojejunostomy) that increased the difficulty of ERCP; (7) Preoperative conditions requiring emergency intervention, such as acute suppurative obstructive cholangitis, septic shock, acute pancreatitis, or acute cholecystitis; (8) Preoperative biliary tract tumor; (9) Severe hepatic or renal disease; (10) Pregnancy; (11) Incomplete or missing clinical data.

Observation indicators and evaluation criteria

It should be noted that in our institution, ERCP and laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) are performed as separate procedures; even when scheduled within 24 h, they are not conducted under the same anesthesia session.

Operator consistency

All ERCP procedures were performed by two experienced endoscopists (each with > 10 years of experience and an annual caseload of > 500 ERCPs). All LC procedures were performed by three senior laparoscopic surgeons (each with > 8 years of experience and an annual caseload of > 200 LC cases). The same team of endoscopists and surgeons was maintained throughout the study period (2018–2023) to ensure technical consistency.

Surgical protocols

ERCP procedure: ERCP was performed using a duodenoscope (TJF-260V, Olympus, Japan) under fluoroscopic guidance and conscious sedation. After selective bile duct cannulation, endoscopic sphincterotomy (EST) was performed using a standard pull-type sphincterotome. Stone extraction was carried out using a retrieval balloon or basket. Mechanical lithotripsy was applied for stones >15 mm in diameter or those not extractable intact. Complete stone clearance was confirmed by occlusion cholangiography showing no filling defects. Nasobiliary drainage (NBD) was routinely placed after stone extraction.

LC procedure: LC was performed under general anesthesia using a standard four-trocar technique. Pneumoperitoneum was established at 12–14 mmHg. Stone clearance criteria

Complete stone clearance was defined as: (1) absence of filling defects on the completion cholangiogram during ERCP; and (2) no residual CBD stones detected on imaging (ultrasound or magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography) within 48 h post-ERCP. Patients with residual stones who required repeat ERCP were excluded from this study.

Definition of time groups

The interval between ERCP and LC was defined as the time from completion of ERCP (endoscope withdrawal) to skin incision of LC. Patients were categorized as follows:

Group A (≤ 24 h): LC performed within 24 hours after ERCP (usually the same calendar day or next morning), but not under the same anesthesia session.

The decision for early (Group A/B) versus delayed (Group C) LC was based on the attending surgeon’s assessment of patient stability, absence of post-ERCP complications (pancreatitis, bleeding, perforation), and resolution of cholestasis. Delayed surgery was typically chosen for patients with post-ERCP pancreatitis (mild to moderate), bleeding requiring transfusion, or severe hyperbilirubinemia (> 3× upper limit of normal) with ongoing cholestasis.

Definitions of procedural complexity

To ensure that intergroup outcome differences reflect surgical timing rather than baseline procedural difficulty, we defined and compared ERCP complexity and LC difficulty using the following parameters. ERCP complexity was assessed by difficult biliary cannulation (> 5 attempts or > 5 min or need for advanced techniques), periampullary diverticulum, pancreatic duct cannulation, stone burden (CBD stone > 15 mm or ≥ 3 stones or need for mechanical lithotripsy), and bile duct diameter > 12 mm. LC difficulty was assessed by gallbladder wall thickness > 4 mm on preoperative imaging, history of acute cholecystitis within 30 days before surgery, severe adhesions or fibrotic Calot’s triangle (surgeon-reported intraoperatively), operative time > 90 min, intraoperative blood loss > 50 mL, and conversion to open surgery. These parameters were recorded prospectively. Comparisons among the three groups are presented in Supplemental Tables 1, and no significant differences were observed (all P > 0.05), confirming that outcomes are attributable to surgical timing rather than baseline complexity.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s) and compared among groups using one-way ANOVA. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables were presented as number (percentage) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. To identify factors associated with delayed LC, patients were dichotomized into early (≤ 72 h, combining groups A and B) and delayed (72 h–2 weeks, group C) groups. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed with delayed LC as the dependent variable. Variables with P < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. A two-sided α level of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General information, preoperative laboratory indicators, and imaging data

Of note, all patients remained hospitalized between ERCP and LC; no patient was discharged and readmitted for the subsequent cholecystectomy. A comparative analysis of the general information, preoperative laboratory indicators, and imaging data of patients in groups A, B, and C revealed statistically significant differences among the three groups in the levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil (NEUT) count, and albumin (P < 0.05). Further multiple comparisons indicated that Group C had significantly higher levels of AST, TBIL, DBIL, WBC, and NEUT, and significantly lower albumin levels, compared to Group A (P < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed among the three groups in other variables, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, coronary heart disease, respiratory diseases, viral hepatitis, cirrhosis), additional laboratory indicators (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], platelet count, hemoglobin, alkaline phosphatase [ALP], gamma-glutamyl transferase [GGT], prothrombin time [PT], activated partial thromboplastin time [APTT]), imaging findings (number of gallstones, gallbladder wall thickness, gallbladder long-axis diameter, number of common bile duct stones, common bile duct diameter), and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification (P > 0.05, Table 1).

Intraoperative ERCP and LC

Intraoperative parameters were compared across the three patient groups. No statistically significant differences were observed among the groups in any of the key ERCP- and LC-related intraoperative measures. These included the method of cannulation, cannulation time, pancreatic duct access, presence of periampullary diverticula, utilization of stone extraction techniques (endoscopic sphincterotomy [EST], balloon dilation, basket retrieval, mechanical lithotripsy), stent placement (biliary or pancreatic), nasobiliary drainage tube placement, as well as LC operative time, intraoperative blood loss, rate of conversion to open surgery, and rate of abdominal drain placement (P > 0.05; Table 2). These findings indicate that performing sequential LC at varying time intervals does not differ significantly in terms of procedural complexity or immediate technical success.

Postoperative outcomes of ERCP and LC

Analysis of postoperative outcomes revealed significant differences among the three groups based on the timing of surgery. Compared with Groups A and B, Group C demonstrated a longer total hospital stay, higher hospitalization costs, and a greater incidence of postoperative cholecystitis (P < 0.001). The overall postoperative complication rate was also significantly higher in Group C than in Groups A and B (P = 0.005). In addition, the postoperative length of stay specifically after LC was longer in Group C compared with Groups A and B (P = 0.012).

No statistically significant differences were found among the groups in terms of postoperative pancreatitis, cholangitis, bleeding rate, retained common bile duct stones, rate of repeat ERCP, duration of abdominal drainage tube placement, postoperative antibiotic use, abdominal infection, surgical site infection, bile leakage, hemorrhage, overall complication profile, or pathological type of the resected gallbladder (P > 0.05; Table 3).

Risk factors for delayed laparoscopic cholecystectomy

Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified three independent risk factors for delayed laparoscopic cholecystectomy (performed between 72 h and 2 weeks after ERCP): preoperative total bilirubin > 3 times the upper limit of normal (OR = 2.56, 95% CI: 1.38–4.75, P = 0.003), post-ERCP pancreatitis (OR = 3.12, 95% CI: 1.45–6.71, P = 0.004), and post-ERCP bleeding (OR = 3.98, 95% CI: 1.70–9.32, P = 0.001; Table 4). These factors remained significant after adjusting for potential confounders, indicating that patients with marked preoperative hyperbilirubinemia or who develop post-ERCP complications such as pancreatitis or bleeding are at significantly higher risk of undergoing delayed cholecystectomy. Clinicians should take these factors into consideration when planning the optimal timing of sequential LC after ERCP.

Discussion

Concurrent common bile duct stones and gallbladder stones represent a prevalent biliary pathology in clinical practice. Among various therapeutic strategies, the sequential approach combining endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with laparoscopic cholecystectomy (LC) is widely adopted due to its minimally invasive nature and facilitation of rapid recovery [14]. However, the optimal timing for LC following ERCP remains controversial. This retrospective study analyzed clinical data from patients undergoing sequential LC at different intervals (≤ 24 h, 24–72 h, 72 h–2 weeks) to investigate the impact of surgical timing on patient prognosis.

The findings demonstrated that compared to Group A (≤ 24 h) and Group B (24–72 h), patients in Group C (72 h–2 weeks) exhibited significantly longer hospital stays, higher hospitalization costs, increased incidence of post-ERCP cholecystitis, and higher overall complication rates. These results align with previous studies. Ben-Gal T et al. [15] reported that performing LC on the same day as ERCP can shorten hospitalization by approximately 2 days and significantly reduce total costs. Hu et al. [16] also emphasized that early LC improves patient outcomes, particularly in reducing cholecystitis and overall complications. Paik et al. [17] further corroborated that concurrent ERCP and LC resulted in shorter hospital stays and eliminated the risk of recurrent acute cholecystitis compared to staged procedures.

From a pathophysiological perspective, early LC post-ERCP may be advantageous as edema in Calot’s triangle and the hepatoduodenal ligament is minimal, preserving clear surgical anatomy and facilitating LC [18]. However, delayed intervention may exacerbate local inflammatory responses and edema, particularly beyond 72 h, potentially increasing LC difficulty and the likelihood of conversion to open surgery [19]. Although this study did not observe statistically significant differences in conversion rates, Group C demonstrated a higher numerical trend compared to Groups A and B, consistent with findings by Yang et al. [20], suggesting potential risks associated with delayed surgery.

Regarding procedural safety, no significant differences were observed among the three groups in intraoperative parameters such as cannulation time, stent placement rates, operative duration, or blood loss, indicating technical feasibility across different surgical timings. This aligns with conclusions by Manasseh et al. [21], whose study reported comparable stone clearance rates and intraoperative outcomes between concurrent and staged ERCP + LC. A meta-analysis by Lv S et al. [22] further supports this view, indicating no substantial differences in technical success rates based on surgical timing.

Notably, multivariate logistic regression analysis identified preoperative total bilirubin > 3 times the upper limit of normal, post-ERCP pancreatitis, and post-ERCP bleeding as independent predictors of delayed LC (72 h–2 weeks). This suggests that for patients with these high-risk factors, premature surgical intervention may be detrimental. For instance, early LC in patients with post-ERCP pancreatitis could exacerbate systemic inflammatory responses and increase surgical risk [23]. Thus, individualized patient assessment and careful balancing of surgical timing with safety considerations are paramount.

This study also revealed significantly higher preoperative ALT, total bilirubin (TBIL), and direct bilirubin (DBIL) levels in Group C compared to Group A, indicating more severe cholestasis or hepatic impairment in the delayed surgery group. A prospective study by Shrestha et al. [24] demonstrated a close association between liver function status and disease severity in patients with common bile duct stones. A prospective study by Jendresen et al. [25] further emphasized that preoperative liver function is a critical factor influencing therapeutic decision-making. Regarding surgical timing, a study recommend elective LC within two weeks after ERCP [26]. However, growing evidence supports earlier intervention. A retrospective study by Zhou et al. [27] confirmed the safety and feasibility of early LC post-ERCP. Vithayathil et al. [28] further indicated that LC within 72 h after ERCP reduces postoperative complication rates, consistent with the present findings.

In terms of complication prevention, this study demonstrated that early surgery significantly reduces the incidence of post-ERCP cholecystitis. The Tokyo Guidelines established by Bouassida et al. [29] provide diagnostic criteria for acute cholecystitis, and early LC effectively mitigates its occurrence. For post-ERCP pancreatitis prevention, consensus recommendations by Cotton et al. [10] and ESGE guidelines by Dumonceau et al. [13] emphasize standardized procedural techniques and preoperative risk stratification.

From a health economics perspective, early surgery substantially reduces medical costs. A meta-analysis by Katwal et al. [30] confirmed that early LC following ERCP lowers hospitalization expenses, aligning with this study’s results. Furthermore, research by Büyükkasap et al. [31] highlighted that optimizing surgical timing is crucial for enhancing healthcare resource utilization efficiency.

We acknowledge that the delayed LC group (72 h–2 weeks) was clinically heterogeneous. In our cohort, the reasons for postponing LC beyond 72 h were not uniform. Some patients experienced post-ERCP complications (pancreatitis in 5 cases, bleeding in 2 cases) that required medical stabilization before surgery. Others presented with severe preoperative hyperbilirubinemia (> 3× ULN) and ongoing cholestasis, for which surgeons preferred to wait for liver function improvement. A substantial proportion (55/80, 68.8%) of patients in Group C, however, underwent delayed surgery primarily due to logistical factors (operating room availability or surgeon preference) rather than clinical necessity. Notably, the adverse outcomes observed in Group C—longer hospital stay, higher costs, and increased cholecystitis—were predominantly driven by patients with post-ERCP complications or severe cholestasis. Of note, among the 55 patients in Group C who were delayed mainly for logistical reasons (e.g., operating room availability or surgeon preference), the outcomes did not appear to be substantially worse than those in the early groups in our descriptive observation. However, this finding is preliminary and hypothesis‑generating, as the sample size was insufficient for formal statistical subgroup analysis, and no specific data are presented here. This heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting our findings. Future prospective studies should stratify delayed patients according to the specific reason for postponement to better delineate the impact of surgical timing from that of intercurrent complications.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. As a single-center retrospective analysis, inherent selection bias may have influenced the results; As shown in Table 1, significant baseline differences in liver function and inflammatory markers existed among the three groups: Group C had higher levels of AST, total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, WBC, and NEUT, as well as lower albumin levels, compared to Group A. These imbalances indicate that patients with more severe cholestasis and inflammation were more likely to undergo delayed surgery, potentially introducing selection bias and influencing the comparability of postoperative outcomes. Although multivariate regression was used to adjust for certain confounders, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled given the retrospective design. Future prospective studies with matched or stratified designs are warranted to validate these findings. Additionally, all data originated from a single institution where procedures were performed by the same surgical team, which limits the generalizability of our findings to other centers with varying technical expertise and healthcare environments. The study also focused primarily on short-term perioperative outcomes, and the absence of long-term follow-up data precludes assessment of the impact of surgical timing on long-term prognosis, such as rates of bile duct stone recurrence or biliary function. Another concern relates to the heterogeneity within Group C (72 h–2 weeks). In this group, we were unable to distinguish between patients whose surgery was delayed due to post-ERCP complications and those who were discharged after uneventful ERCP and readmitted for LC after 72 h. This distinction is clinically important, as the latter subgroup might not experience the same increases in hospital stay and costs as the former. Future studies should stratify delayed cases based on the presence or absence of intercurrent complications to better assess the true impact of surgical timing. Furthermore, the delayed LC group (72 h–2 weeks) was heterogeneous with respect to reasons for postponement, and we were unable to perform subgroup analyses due to limited sample sizes in certain categories (e.g., post-ERCP bleeding). Future studies with larger cohorts should stratify delayed patients by the specific indication for delay. Furthermore, patient-reported outcomes including pain scores, quality of life, and satisfaction were not evaluated, thereby hindering a comprehensive assessment of how different treatment timings influence overall recovery. Lastly, although the sample size met statistical requirements for our primary analyses, it remains inadequate for robust assessment of certain low-incidence complications. Given these limitations, future research should involve multicenter, large-scale prospective randomized controlled trials that incorporate a broader range of outcome measures to further validate and refine these findings.

In conclusion, the timing of sequential LC after ERCP significantly influences short-term clinical outcomes. In the absence of serious complications or high-risk factors, performing LC within 72 h-particularly within 24 h-after ERCP is recommended to reduce hospital stay, lower healthcare costs, and minimize complication rates. For patients with preoperative hyperbilirubinemia, post-ERCP pancreatitis, or bleeding, careful evaluation and appropriately delayed surgery should be considered. This study offers valuable insights to support individualized surgical planning for patients with concurrent common bile duct stones and gallbladder stones.
