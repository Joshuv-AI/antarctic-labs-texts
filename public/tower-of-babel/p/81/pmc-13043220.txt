Psychologically informed reminder messages for promoting BRCA1/2 carrier screening: evidence from a large-scale population-based study
Authors: Tom Mushkat, Rotem Greenberg, Ofer Isakov, Samah Hayek, Hadassah Raskas, Shay Ben-Shachar, Adi Berliner-Senderey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPsychologically Informed Reminder Messages (PIRMs) may promote health-behavior change, but their impact on preventive genetic screening is unclear. Due to low adherence to a screening program, Clalit Health Services initiated an intervention to encourage BRCA1/2 carrier screening in July 2022. This observational study assesses the effectiveness of the intervention in encouraging screening and its spillover effect on related healthcare utilization, focusing on Ashkenazi Jewish females aged 25–50 with no relevant cancer history. Patients were randomly assigned to receive one of five PIRMs. Four PIRMs utilized different psychological strategies, while the fifth served as a control. The effectiveness of the intervention on screening adherence was assessed using a regression discontinuity analysis. A Cox regression assessed the effectiveness of the four PIRMs compared to the control. Spillover effects on healthcare utilization were evaluated using logistic regressions. The intervention showed a positive correlation with increased adherence to screening (β = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.01–0.72). The “Barrier Remover” PIRM emphasized ease (hazards ratio = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.02–1.16), while the “Health Control” PIRM focused on autonomy (hazards ratio = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.01–1.14), both indicating greater effectiveness than the control. The findings suggest a positive short-term spillover effect of the “Health Control” PIRM on healthcare utilization compared to the control (odds ratio = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.04–1.29). PIRMs effectively increased BRCA1/2 carrier screening adherence, demonstrating a scalable, low-cost intervention to improve preventive healthcare uptake. The effect varied by the psychological strategies, highlighting the potential of tailored behavioral interventions to enhance public health.

Introduction

Pathogenic variants in the Breast Cancer 1 and 2 (BRCA1/2) genes are the leading cause of hereditary breast and ovarian cancers (HBOC) among females of all ethnic and geographic populations [1–4]. Unlike population-wide cancer screening modalities such as mammography, BRCA1/2 testing is typically offered through a risk-based approach, informed by an individual’s personal and/or family history of cancer [5–7]. As a result, many carriers remain unidentified until cancer diagnosis, limiting opportunities for prevention and early intervention [8, 9].

Identifying an asymptomatic BRCA1/2 carrier initiates a structured, lifelong follow-up for early detection and prevention, usually starting at age 25, following worldwide guidelines [5, 7, 10]. Standard follow-up includes genetic counseling, intensified surveillance such as earlier and frequent breast imaging, and risk-reduction surgeries. These follow-ups offer clinical benefits and are cost-effective, supporting early identification, reduced cancer incidence, earlier-stage diagnoses, and improved survival [11–16].

Within the Ashkenazi Jewish (AJ) population, there is a relatively high prevalence of three founder pathogenic variants in the BRCA1/2 genes, which together account for the majority of HBOC cases in this group. Approximately 1% AJ individuals are carriers of one of these variants [6]. In 2020, the Israeli Ministry of Health initiated a funded BRCA1/2 carrier-screening program for asymptomatic AJ females. However, a recent study evaluated the outcomes of the screening initiative and found that merely 3.2% of eligible females underwent the screening test within 2 years [6]. This low uptake highlighted the urgent need for interventions to increase adherence to the screening program, particularly given the availability of effective prevention and risk-reduction strategies for BRCA1/2 carriers.

In response, Clalit Health Services (CHS), the largest Israeli health fund, which insures 53% of Israel’s population, initiated a nudge intervention by sending Psychologically Informed Reminder Messages (PIRMs) to its female members aged 25–50 to encourage them to undergo BRCA1/2 carrier screening. Consistent with clinical guidelines for BRCA1/2 carrier follow-up and age-based mammography screening, the target population was designed to include younger females [5, 7].

This approach aligns with existing research suggesting that leveraging behavioral insights as PIRMs holds promise for enhancing policymaking, including policies related to adherence to medical guidelines. PIRMs have effectively increased healthier lifestyle [17] prompted medical appointment attendance [18] and improved adherence to annual flu vaccine uptake and COVID-19 vaccination [19–21]. Adherence to BRCA1/2 carrier screening differs from other healthcare behaviors. Although BRCA1/2 carrier screening might appear to be a routine medical procedure, a positive result can confer a high lifelong risk of cancer and, therefore, the test may have profound life-changing implications for the carrier and their family. Unlike conventional screening tests that assess risk at a single time point, genetic testing reveals a persistent inherited predisposition.

The primary objectives of the current study are twofold: first, to determine the effectiveness of a PIRMs intervention on BRCA1/2 carrier screening adherence, and second, to assess the effectiveness of the various psychological strategies embedded within these PIRMs.

The secondary objective is to evaluate the existence of a spillover effect, defined as an engagement in one behavior affecting the likelihood of engaging in or disengaging from a second behavior [22]. Specifically, it examines whether the intervention is associated with related healthcare utilization. The intervention targeting one aspect of females’ health (i.e. BRCA1/2 carrier screening) may unintentionally highlight other aspects of females’ health [23]. This could, in turn, lead to increased utilization of related healthcare services. Alternatively, the spillover effect could be harmful, distracting patients from non-reminded actions or depleting their willpower to perform similar related behaviors [24, 25]. Moreover, the spillover effect may be limited or absent, resulting in no impact on other aspects of health service utilization.

Methods

Study population

The study includes female members of CHS who were eligible for screening from June 2021 (when the screening program began) to June 2023 (when the study commenced). CHS is Israel’s largest integrated healthcare service provider and payer system, serving over 4.7 million members. Eligibility criteria included at least 1 year of CHS membership, no personal history of BRCA1/2-associated cancers (breast, ovarian, pancreatic, and fallopian tube), no prior screenings before June 2021, and an age range of 25–50 years. This age range was chosen because 25 is the age at which follow-up programs are typically recommended, and many BRCA1/2 carriers over the age of 50 have already been diagnosed with cancer. Females were required to be fully or partially (with at least one grandparent) of AJ origin, as self-reported. The origin of the screened females was determined based on their self-reports during the screening process. The origin of eligible females who have yet to undergo testing was identified based on the recorded ethnicity in their electronic health records (EHR), which primarily relies on self-reporting.

Relevant participant information was extracted from CHS’s EHR, including administrative and clinical data, socio-demographic details, diagnoses from both community and hospital settings, recorded chronic diseases, clinical biomarkers, and information related to appointments.

Study design and settings

In July 2022, the study population received one of five randomly assigned messages, using Short Message Service (SMS) to encourage BRCA1/2 carrier screening. These messages were carefully designed to convey information pertinent to the BRCA1/2 carrier screening test. Four messages served as PIRMs, addressing various psychological motives (strategies), while one served as a control, as outlined in Supplementary Appendix 2 Table S.1. A randomized program sent one of the messages to each female concerning the BRCA1/2 carrier screening. To prevent clinic overload, messages were sent 1 month after a woman’s birthday.

Four PIRMs types were implemented, each highlighting a distinct screening benefit. These PIRMs were grounded in established behavioral principles: (i) The “Social Norm” PIRM leveraged the influence of social identity and descriptive norms by highlighting that CHS members get screened [26, 27]. (ii) The “Health Control” PIRM emphasized autonomy and competence in line with the Self-Determination Theory [28]. (iii) The “Family Responsibility” PIRM appealed to intrinsic motivation by highlighting responsibility toward oneself and one’s family [28]. (iv) The “Barrier Remover” PIRM addressed commonly reported obstacles to preventive healthcare utilization by emphasizing that the screening test is simple, free of charge, and logistically accessible. The selection of these barriers was informed by prior evidence demonstrating that perceived cost, time constraints, and procedural complexity are key determinants of non-utilization of preventive health services [29, 30]. The four PIRMs were selected to represent distinct, theory-informed behavioral mechanisms commonly used in large-scale health interventions, balancing theoretical relevance with feasibility of implementation in a national healthcare setting. The fifth message, designated as the control, provided information about the screening test and its purpose.

For the first primary objective, a pre-post interventional study was conducted from June 2021 to June 2023 to determine the adherence rate of BRCA1/2 carrier screening before and after the intervention initiation. There was no distinction between the PIRMs, and the population included all eligible CHS females within the BRCA1/2 carrier screening timeframe.

To address the second primary objective and the secondary objective, a retrospective cohort design was employed to evaluate the unique effectiveness of each PIRM method compared to the control message in enhancing adherence to the BRCA1/2 carrier screening test and related healthcare utilization. The population included all eligible CHS females for BRCA1/2 carrier screening during the specified timeframe who received a PIRM and had not previously undergone the screening.

The follow-up period for the second primary objective and the secondary objective began on the PIRM date and concluded in June 2023. The population included in this analysis consisted of eligible CHS females for BRCA1/2 carrier screening who received a PIRM before undergoing the screening, as well as those who received a PIRM but did not undergo screening.

The primary outcome was the uptake of BRCA1/2 carrier screening. As a secondary outcome, we examined healthcare utilization based on the hypothesis that Psychologically Informed Reminder Messages may increase the salience of related female health services through a spreading activation mechanism [23]. In consultation with clinical domain experts, we selected gynecology (GYN) and breast surgery specialists (BSS) as the services most directly related to female reproductive and breast health, making them most likely to show spillover effects. Utilization was measured by scheduling a physician visit within 30 days of the PIRM. Visits to ear, nose, and throat (ENT) physicians were included as a negative control, as these are not conceptually related to female-specific health services.

For the first analysis, the index date was defined for each month from June 2021 to June 2023. For the second and third analyses, the index date is the PIRMs’ date. All CHS members’ information was extracted from CHS’s EHR at the index date.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study population, stratified by BRCA1/2 carrier screening status. To determine the BRCA1/2 carrier screening uptake rates before and after the intervention implementation, a Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD) was employed. This methodology allows for assessing the impact of an intervention by comparing an outcome measure around a specific threshold [31, 32]. This entails analyzing the monthly BRCA1/2 carrier screening adherence rates pre- and post-implementation.

Adherence, defined as the monthly ratio of females who underwent screening to those eligible, was analyzed over a 24-month period from June 2022 to June 2023, with time serving as the running variable. Given that, in each month, only females who had a birthday in the previous month received a PIRM, and it required time to schedule an appointment for the screening, no immediate effect was expected. Therefore, the discontinuity threshold, indicating the expected timing of the PIRMs’ effect, was set for October 2022.

Equation (1) describes the model’s specification. In this equation, ym represents the monthly adherence to the screening (during month m). Effectm represents a binary variable adjusting for the incline in the BRCA1/2 carrier screening ratio due to the intervention. f(datem) represents the date measured in months from the start of the follow-up, with datem normalized to zero 3 months after the intervention began (October 22). Clinicsm represents the number of clinics that provided the BRCA1/2 carrier screening in month m. f(Clinicsm), which represents the squared number of clinics, was also controlled to allow the number of clinics available to explain a discontinuity in the monthly screening ratio.

A Cox proportional hazards model was employed to evaluate the impact of the different PIRMs on the BRCA1/2 carrier screening adherence. This model estimated the likelihood of screening completion following each specific PIRM compared to the control message, while accounting for the duration from PIRM to the end of follow-up (undergoing the screening or the study’s end). The analysis was adjusted for age, age squared, body mass index (BMI), immigration status (yes, no), socioeconomic status (SES; low, moderate, high), smoking status (non-smoker, past-smoker, current-smoker), sector (general-Jewish, ultra-orthodox, other non-Arab), residential location (districts of Israel), urbanicity (urban, non-urban), supplementary insurance status (non, Gold, Platinum), and the number of COVID-19 vaccines taken before the index date (0, 1, 2, +3).

Three logistic regression models, one for each type of medical service—GYN, BSS, and ENT, assessed the association between the PIRM and healthcare utilization. The models compared the different PIRM strategies to the control message and evaluated the likelihood of booking an appointment within 30 days post-PIRM. Two models focused on females’ health: GYN and BSS bookings. A third model served as a negative control assessing the utilization of ENT bookings, which is unrelated to females’ health. The covariates utilized in the Cox regression model were included, with an adjustment for prior service utilization three years before the index date (0, 1, +2). Females with fewer than 30 days of follow-up were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analyses were conducted using the R program (version 4.2.1) [33]. All tests were two-tailed, with a 5% significance threshold. The large datasets prevented sole reliance on the P values from confirming the proportional hazards assumption (using cox.ph). Instead, Schoenfeld residual plots were visually inspected for each covariate, and the assumption was met for all models.

Results

The study includes 233 677 CHS females who were eligible for BRCA1/2 carrier screening between June 2021 and June 2023. Of these, 28 453 (12.2%) chose to undergo the screening test, while 205 224 did not (Supplementary Appendix 3 Fig. S.1). Most of the screened females were aged 35–39 and belonged to the general sector. The majority were urban non-smokers with a normal BMI and high SES. Likewise, most of the females who were not screened were urban, non-smokers with a normal BMI, but were younger (ages 25–29 or 35–39) and predominantly of medium SES (Supplementary Appendix 4 Table S.2). To assess the reliability of self-reported ancestry, we evaluated the concordance between self-reported AJ origin and ancestry information recorded in the EHR, observing a high level of agreement, with less than 4% of females without AJ ancestry showing missing data in the EHR.

The BRCA1/2 carrier screening ratio gradually increased by approximately 0.1% monthly from June 2021 until the study’s end. However, a notable discontinuity occurred in the fourth month after the intervention onset. The discontinuity threshold, set to 3 months after the onset, was statistically significant (β = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.01–0.72, P = .045). Figure 1 visually represents the monthly screening ratios. Each data point illustrates the ratio of females who underwent screening to those eligible that month. A dotted vertical line in Fig. 1 denotes the discontinuity threshold, signifying the anticipated increase in screening adherence.

The effect of different types of PIRMs compared to the control message on BRCA1/2 carrier screening uptake is illustrated in Fig. 2. The “Barrier Remover” message demonstrated an 8.1% adherence rate (95% CI: 7.82%–8.38%), which was significantly higher compared to the control message (5.8%, 95% CI: 5.55%–6.05%), HR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.02–1.16, P < .001. Similarly, the adherence rate of the females who received the “Health Control” PIRM (6.3%, 95% CI: 6.05%–6.55%) was significantly greater compared to those who received the control message, HR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.01–1.14, P = .033. The females who received the “Family Responsibility” PIRM (4.7%, 95% CI: 4.47%–4.93%) had no difference in adherence to the screening compared to the control message, HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.92–1.05, P = .69. However, the females who received the “Social Norm” PIRM (5%, 95% CI: 4.77%–5.23%) were less inclined to undergo screening than those exposed to the control message, HR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.83–0.96, P < .001.

Discussion

This study uses real-world data to assess the effectiveness of PIRMs interventions in promoting adherence to BRCA1/2 carrier screening. Three key analyses evaluated PIRMs’ immediate and extended impacts on adherence to BRCA1/2 carrier screening and healthcare utilization. The findings suggest that PIRMs were positively correlated with females’ likelihood of undergoing BRCA1/2 carrier screening and that PIRMs vary in efficiency. Furthermore, PIRMs may have a spillover effect on related healthcare utilization.

Employing an RDD facilitates the assessment of a causal effect around the intervention onset in a quasi-experimental design [31, 32]. Consequently, the observed significant incline in adherence around the discontinuity threshold can be attributed to the intervention. Additionally, it is possible that some females who underwent screening were motivated by a PIRM their family member or friend received. Therefore, this analysis evaluates the monthly ratio of screened females, irrespective of PIRM reception.

The “Health Control” PIRM outperformed the control message, promoting the BRCA1/2 carrier screening. Furthermore, it was also the only PIRM to show a spillover effect, as females who received it were more likely to utilize related healthcare services than females in the control group. According to the Self-Determination Theory, feeling in control of one’s choices and believing in one’s ability to succeed can enhance motivation for action and behavioral change [34]. These factors likely contributed to females adhering to the PIRM, undergoing the BRCA1/2 carrier screening, and engaging with other aspects of their healthcare by highlighting similar aspects of females’ health [23].

Furthermore, previous research has indicated that perceived cost is a significant barrier to genetic testing and that participation is higher among those with greater socio-economic status, while interest tends to increase when tests are offered free or at low cost, especially among lower socio-economic groups [30, 35, 36]. Therefore, it is understandable that the “Barrier Remover” PIRM also outperformed the control message in encouraging BRCA1/2 carrier screening. However, in the Israeli healthcare system, which functions both as an insurer and a healthcare provider, patients usually expect to receive most medical procedures at little to no cost. In this context, the success of the “Barrier Remover” PIRM may be due to a continuing misconception that genetic testing is costly or not fully covered.

Furthermore, BRCA1/2 carrier screening was offered only at specific clinics, making it logistically more challenging than other tests available at the local clinic. By highlighting the convenience of booking the screening close to home, this PIRM likely reduced the concerns regarding the perceived difficulty of undergoing the screening. This targeted focus on the specific advantages of the BRCA1/2 carrier screening may also explain why the “Barrier Remover” PIRM did not influence adherence to other medical services, as it did not generalize to broader health-related behaviors.

Future research will need to examine the hypothesis regarding the power of PIRMs, in particular, and Nudges, in general, emphasizing the ease of screening procedures and seeking to explain why some psychological constructs may be less efficient in this context. Furthermore, further studies should test whether personalizing which patient received which PIRM (rather than randomly assigning, as in this study) might have an even greater effect than the “Barrier Remover” and the “Health Control” PIRM [18].

Limitations

Utilizing regression discontinuity with time as a running variable presents several constraints. Testing unit sorting across the threshold is irrelevant due to the running variable’s uniformity [37]. Moreover, varying bandwidths around the threshold pose challenges: narrow bandwidths reduce observations, while wider ones risk attributing effects to time-related confounders [37]. Given the limited observations, this study utilized maximum bandwidth, assuming minimal value in adjusting time-driven confounders beyond the controlled clinic availability due to the relatively short follow-up. Additionally, the Sharp RDD employed a discontinuity threshold set 3 months after the intervention, reflecting the time needed to receive and act on PIRMs. Figure 1 illustrates that adherence increased four months post-intervention, slightly later than anticipated. Nevertheless, a Fuzzy model presented in Supplementary Appendix 6 accounts for the pre-threshold period.

The increase in BSS and GNY booking utilization may not result from PIRMs but rather from BRCA1/2 carrier screening. The “Health Control” PIRMs encouraged screening, potentially increasing healthcare service utilization. However, this theory weakens because the “Barrier Remover” PIRM had no similar effect. Moreover, considering the 3-month wait for screening results and the 30-day appointment window, it is likely that screening outcomes did not cause this utilization. Mediation analyses (Supplementary Appendix 7) evaluated direct PIRM effects on healthcare use, excluding indirect BRCA1/2 carrier screening effects.

Figure 1 illustrates an average monthly response of 0.65% (95% CI: 0.52%–0.79%). The small effect size might limit the intervention’s impact. However, even small effects are valuable. With the costs of diagnosing ovarian and breast cancers at USD 133 265 (ILS 495 353) and USD 46 929 (ILS 174 438), respectively [38], a 0.25% increase in screening adherence among 10 000 females could identify 6.25 additional carriers. Early cancer detection could avert ovarian cancer costs of USD 832 906 and breast cancer costs of USD 312 555, excluding treatment prevention costs.

Conclusions

Behavioral methods for adherence should be integral to evaluating and expanding healthcare services. Sending motivational PIRMs is one strategy to improve public health. Our findings highlight that the “Health Control” strategy was the most effective, although results may vary among individuals and health domains. Therefore, we advocate for a tailored strategy that personalizes interventions to meet each patient’s specific needs.
