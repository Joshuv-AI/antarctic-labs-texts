Satellite tracking reveals the home range and movement ecology across the annual cycle of a 32 g songbird
Authors: Zishi Wu, Noah G. Perlut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUnderstanding movement ecology across life-history stages is critical for conserving declining migratory species. The Bobolink (Dolichonyx oryzivorus), a grassland songbird, exhibits complex spatial behaviors shaped by annual cycle stage, landscape context, and environmental conditions. We investigated the movement ecology of adult male Bobolinks across three stages of the annual cycle using satellite tracking.MethodsWe deployed Sunbird Argos satellite tags on 26 adult male Bobolinks from two breeding sites in the northeastern United States: one large, continuous grassland and one small, isolated site. Using continuous-time movement models, we estimated home range size and composition, analyzed movement patterns during nesting, post-fledging, and fall migration periods, and compared these parameters between sites. We documented nocturnal pre-migratory flights and used multiple linear regression to assess environmental and geographic factors associated with stopover movement.ResultsDuring nesting, males at the large site maintained larger home ranges with greater grassland cover than males at the isolated small site. By the post-fledging period, home range sizes no longer differed between sites, although home ranges at the large site still contained a higher proportion of grassland. We recorded temporary movements outside of established home ranges (forays) of up to 17 km during the nesting period, potentially linked to extra-pair mating, and provided evidence of nocturnal pre-migratory flights in most individuals. During migration, stopover home-range size was only moderately associated with temporal and environmental variables and was not influenced by landscape context.ConclusionsThis study represents one of the first applications of fine-scale satellite tags on small migratory passerines across multiple annual cycle stages. It extends evidence of breeding-season forays and nocturnal pre-migratory flights to a grassland specialist, highlighting the behavioral flexibility of Bobolinks across seasons. Bobolinks adapted their space use and movement strategies to meet varying ecological demands. Our study emphasized the need to maintain connected grassland networks to support breeding populations and protect key Caribbean stopover sites to sustain Bobolinks along their long-distance migration route.

Background

Birds exhibit a diverse array of movement behaviors ranging from short foraging flights within local territories to transcontinental migratory journeys. The mechanisms that drive bird movements are shaped by both evolutionary processes and environmental factors [1]. Understanding movement and space use in birds is key to conservation efforts, as it provides important insights into their resource use and habitat preference. This knowledge is particularly important given the international decline in bird populations [2].

Early studies of songbird movement were limited in scope: breeding territory size was typically estimated using flush-mapping or radio telemetry [3, 4], while migration pathways were primarily inferred from banding recoveries and recaptures [5]. However, modern tools now enable us to track bird movements with unprecedented precision in both space and time [6, 7].

Despite these advances, challenges remain in the trade-off between the quality of position data and the weight of tracking devices. High-resolution transmitters, such as those using global positioning system (GPS) technology, are often heavier and therefore limited in their application to larger species like raptors, waterbirds and shorebirds [8]. Small passerines weighing < 40 are typically tracked with light-level geolocators and radio transmitters, devices that weigh as little as 0.3 g [9, 10]. However, these lightweight options come with significant compromises in spatial precision, with spatial errors that average 140 km latitude and 50 km longitude for geolocators [9]. Similarly, radio transmitters, such as NanoTags™, rely on the presence of Motus Towers within ~15 km to detect signals, leaving birds undetected in regions without tower coverage [10].

More recently, high-accuracy archival GPS tags weighing ~1 g have become available, providing 10 m precision [11, 12]. But these are archival tags and require recapturing of the bird to retrieve data [13]. As a result, datasets are often biased toward surviving birds and are prone to data loss when individuals die or are not recaptured. In contrast, recent technological advancements have reduced non-archival satellite tags to just 2 g, enabling remote tracking of medium-sized songbirds without the need for recapture [8]. These tags combine moderate spatial accuracy (up to 250 m) with high temporal resolution (location fix intervals of less than an hour) [14], allowing researchers to identify habitat selection not only at single snapshot in time but also dynamically across life-history stages. This combination of continuous temporal coverage and fine-scale spatial resolution remains uniquely powerful for addressing the habitat requirements and conservation needs of songbirds across their full annual cycle. Therefore, we employed this satellite-tracking technology in the present study to investigate space use and movement strategies in Bobolinks (Dolichonyx oryzivorus).

Bobolinks are grassland-obligate songbirds that travel ~20,000 km annually between their North American breeding and South American wintering grounds [15]. They rely on diverse native and agricultural grassland habitats, putting them in conflict both with farmers who mow and graze their fields during the breeding season, and with rice crop farmers on their wintering and stopover grounds who see them as pests [15]. With such a complicated annual cycle, both in terms of habitat selection and distance moved, conservation of this declining species is dependent on understanding their year-round movement ecology.

While geolocator data has expanded our knowledge of Bobolink migration ecology [16]—including their fall transoceanic flight from North to South America and stopovers in Venezuela or Colombia before wintering in Bolivia and Argentina [17]—the coarse spatial resolution of light-level geolocators limits our understanding of fine-scale movements at specific annual stage. Point count studies have shown that Bobolinks prefer large, open grasslands with greater vegetation height and density during the breeding season [18–20]. However, these approaches capture only static snapshots of habitat use at the population level. After selecting a grass field for nesting, it remains unclear whether or how individuals use adjacent landscape, whether suitable or unsuitable, for foraging or social interactions, including extra-pair mating. Additionally, how movement decisions vary across different landscape contexts and annual cycle stages remain poorly understood.

In this study, we documented movement patterns across nesting, post-fledging, and fall migration periods in two geographically distinct but genetically similar populations of male Bobolinks using 2 g satellite tags. Our goal was to describe the spatial and temporal characteristics of movement and contribute to the growing body of research on migratory songbirds’ movement ecology.

One population bred in a large agricultural landscape characterized by extensive hayfields and pastures, whereas the second population occupied a heterogeneous landscape where small, isolated hayfield patches were embedded within a matrix of forest, row crops, and urban development. These contrasting landscape configurations provided an opportunity to describe movement patterns across different habitat contexts. However, given the limited number of study sites, our aim was to provide detailed descriptions of site-level movement patterns rather than to test specific mechanisms.

The space use of an animal can be determined by many factors cush as seasonality [21–23], landscape, population dynamic, and other factors [24–26]. Accordingly, we structured our analyses into three levels: first, we compared nesting and post-fledging home range sizes between the two populations to document site-level differences; second, we examined within-individual changes in home range size between nesting and post-fledging stages; and third, we analyzed migratory movement by describing nocturnal pre-migration movement and modelling temporary home ranges during stopover with parameters such as date, weather, and landscape features. By examining these factors, we aimed to identify the environmental and temporal drivers of Bobolink movement during stopovers, an essential stage of their annual cycle when birds must balance energy replenishment, time minimization, and predation risk within rapidly changing habitats [27].

Methods

Study area

We conducted the breeding portion of our study at two agricultural sites in the northeastern United States: Shelburne Farms in Shelburne, Vermont (44.39°N, 73.26°W) and the Hudson Valley Farm Hub in Hurley, New York (41.91°N, 74.09°W) (Fig. 1). In this region, Bobolinks almost exclusively nest in agricultural hayfields and pastures. The larger Vermont site (hereafter VT) comprised approximately 800 ha of continuous, actively managed hayfields supporting a well-established population of > 100 breeding Bobolink pairs; hayfield management occurred on most fields during the breeding season, with mowing occurring between mid-May and early-July. These fields, studied since 2002, were largely planted with alfalfa (Medicago sativa), red clover (Trifolium pratense), white clover (Trifolium repens), dandelion (Taraxacum officinale), orchard grass (Dactylis glomerata), timothy (Phleum pretense), bluegrass (Poa sp.), reed canary grass (Phalaris arundinacea) and vetch (Vicia sp.). Satellite transmitter Deployment

During 2021–2024, we captured 26 male Bobolinks on their breeding grounds using mist nets and audio play-back. Each bird was weighed and fitted with a Sunbird Solar Argos Transmitter (Lotek Wireless Inc., Model S1H 310, 2 g) using 1.0 mm elastic thread and the leg-loop harness method [28] (Total harness weight 2.1 g, Fig. 2). We conducted an initial pilot experiment on a single male in VT in 2019 and found no tag-caused mortality. The mean mass of male Bobolinks in our study was 32.5 g, with no significant difference between sites (t = −0.71, p = 0.49), resulting in a transmitter-to-body-mass ratio of approximately 6%. While this exceeded the conventional 3–5% preferable tag load on birds [8], our pilot studies indicated no observable impact on flight performance, and we restricted our tracking to males because they were heavier than females. Bobolinks have a unique molting strategy, being one of few bird species that can undergo two complete molts annually [15]. From July to October, adults perform a complete definitive pre-basic molt on or near the breeding grounds following reproduction, while juveniles undergo a partial performative molt. From January to March, individuals in both their first and definitive cycles undergo a complete prealternate molt on the non-breeding grounds [30]. As a result, second-year and after-second-year individuals become indistinguishable in plumage during the breeding season, so we could not reliably age adult Bobolinks in hand. Therefore, all adult Bobolinks captured were categorized as after-hatch-year (AHY) individuals.

Annual cycle stage definition

We analyzed male Bobolink movement ecology across three stages of their annual life-cycle, beginning with the nesting period. For each individual, the start of the nesting stage was defined as the date we tagged the bird on the breeding ground (Fig. 1). If not disturbed, Bobolinks complete a breeding event in about six to eight weeks, including nest-building (1–3 days, by females), egg laying (5–6 days, by females), incubation (10 days, by females), nestling development (10–11 days, by females and males), and post-fledging dependence which both parents continue feeding fledglings for up to four additional weeks [15]. During breeding, males extensively defend territories, attend females, and engage in mate-seeking behavior [15].

For several tagged males, we observed a distinct shift in behavior as individuals left their established breeding fields and settled in new fields outside their defended territory. When these movements occurred between 1 July and 1 August, we designated the date of movement as the transition between the nesting and post-fledging stages. For males that did not exhibit a clear change in field use, we assigned July 15 as the end of the nesting stage. This date was selected to reflect the end of male mate-seeking behavior, as it corresponds to the approximate timing of the last nest-building events for the second brood at our study sites [31] and falls within the nesting end date for the broader region [32]. After this date, opportunities for extra-pair copulations (EPCs) were expected to cease, and males’ movement patterns may have changed accordingly [33]. We also confirmed this breeding phenology through ongoing nest monitoring and daily field observations. By early July, we observed a decline in territorial displays, almost none of the female fertile call, and an increase in flocking behavior across our study fields. The post-fledging period extended from July 15th until the onset of fall migration, during which time most fledglings had become independent and both young and adults began gathering in flocks to prepare for migration.

We identified migration initiation as the first significant movement (>50 km) across unsuitable habitat (e.g., urban areas, forests or water bodies). Prior to migration, some individuals exhibited pre-migration movements, including by either a large looping movement that returned to the post-breeding area or short directional flights after which migration began from the new location. During these pre-migration periods, birds continuously moved across the landscape, making it inappropriate to estimate a home range. We defined a stopover and the corresponding stopover site where individuals remained in a localized area (e.g., within a single field or habitat patch) for at least two consecutive nights [34], determined through visual inspection of movement trajectories.

Home range calculation

Argos tag locations are obtained when polar-orbiting satellites calculate a transmitter’s position using Doppler shifts in transmission frequency during each satellite pass [14]. In our data, the median interval between consecutive location fixes was 49 minutes. Argos assigned a location class (LC) for each location fix based on its estimated error radius: LC 3 (<250 m), LC 2 (<500 m), LC 1 (<1,500 m), and LC 0 (>1,500 m). Locations derived from fewer than 4 messages during a satellite pass (LC A and LC B) lacked error estimates and were considered as the least accurate [14]. We estimated home ranges for each tracking period using only individuals with ≥20 high-quality fixes (LC 3, 2, and 1). When restricting to high-quality data, the median interval between fixes was 80 minutes.

All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1 [35]. To address the inherent temporal autocorrelation in animal tracking data, where current locations influenced subsequent position fixes, we employed Continuous Time Movement Models (CTMM) [36]. This approach accounted for temporally autocorrelated positions, irregular sampling intervals and location errors. For each life-history stage (breeding, post-breeding, and stopover periods), we used the CTMM package in R to estimate space use with two utilization distribution (UD) metrics: home range (95% UD) and core area (50% UD) following [36]. For birds that used multiple stopover sites, we calculated separate home ranges for each stopover period.

For breeding and post-breeding periods, we compared home range and core area sizes between the two sites using two-sample t-tests when data met parametric assumptions (normality and homogeneity of variances). Because of our small sample size and large variation in home range sizes, we primarily employed bootstrap resampling (10,000 iterations) to calculate the difference in means between sites using the ‘boot’ package in R [37]. This non-parametric approach is particularly appropriate for our small dataset, providing more reliable inference without requiring distributional assumptions. For bootstrapped comparisons, we report bias-corrected and accelerated (Bca) 95% confidence intervals, which adjust for both bias and skewness in the bootstrap distribution. For individuals with both breeding and post-breeding data, we conducted paired t-tests within each population to assess temporal changes in home range size at their breeding grounds.

Breeding season forays

Tracked male Bobolinks may make temporary forays to distant areas outside their typical range during the breeding season. To systematically identify these forays, we examined location fixes falling outside the preliminary home range boundaries calculated using all high-quality location fixes (LC3, 2 or 1). To distinguish true forays from potential outliers, we defined an foray as multiple consecutive high-quality location fixes showing a bird visiting open agricultural habitat beyond its preliminary home range, and each foray ended when the individual returned to it. While most birds returned to their home range within the same day, some foray extended over several days. Following Burt’s definition of home range [38], which excludes “occasional sallies outside the area, perhaps exploratory in nature,” we recalculated final home ranges after removing these foray points. We then analyzed these forays through a two-step approach. First, we compared the probability of making forays between populations using a chi-square goodness-of-fit test. Second, we conducted a conditional analysis on birds that made forays, examining population-level differences in foray distances (from the centroid of the home range) and frequencies through the same bootstrap resampling technique (10,000 iterations) to calculate differences in means between sites and report bias-corrected and accelerated (Bca) 95% confidence intervals [37].

Breeding home range landscape composition

We exported breeding season home ranges and core areas as shapefiles and analyzed their landcover composition using QGIS 3.38.1 [39]. Landcover data were obtained from Sentinel-2 Land Cover Explorer [40] and were subsequently refined based on our visual inspection and field observations. Each 10 m × 10 m grid cell was classified into one of five categories: grassland, forest, row crop, urban, or water. Of these classes, Bobolinks exclusively nest in grassland habitat. We used QGIS to calculate the percentage of each land cover type within individual birds’ core areas and home ranges [39]. We used Shannon’s Diversity Index to quantify landscape heterogeneity, where higher values indicated greater land cover diversity [41]. To compare habitat heterogeneity between the two populations, we employed bootstrap resampling (10,000 iterations) to calculate the difference in mean Shannon’s Diversity Index values between sites and bias-corrected and accelerated (Bca) 95% confidence intervals [37].

Migratory stopover home ranges

We examined factors influencing home range sizes at migratory stopover sites by incorporating both bird-specific characteristics and environmental variables, including bird origin (VT or NY), year, calendar date of arrival at the stopover site, and the stationary duration. We assessed averaged environmental conditions during each stopover using daily temperature, precipitation, and the zonal (u) and meridional (v) wind components extracted from ERA5 data [42], which are provided at a spatial resolution of 0.1° (~11 km). We derived field-specific NDVI values from Sentinel-2 Surface Reflectance imagery (10 m resolution) in Google Earth Engine [43]. For each stopover, we manually delineated the field polygon which bird was in and calculated mean NDVI within that polygon, matching the spatial extent of the stopover habitat. Additionally, we quantified habitat openness as the percentage of open habitat (grassland, row crops or wetland) within 2.5 km of stopover sites using Sentinel-2 land cover data [40]. We selected this spatial scale based on previous research in our study region that demonstrated strong correlations between Bobolink breeding abundance and landscape composition [19], as well as their documented preference for open habitats in our study region during the breeding season [44]. Our model selection process for temporary home range size began with full linear mixed models containing all potential explanatory variables (Table 2) and Bird_ID as a random effect. However, we changed to multiple linear regression without Bird_ID due to model singularity issues from insufficient within-individual replication. Model selection was performed using a backward stepwise approach, sequentially removing the least informative variable until the model with the lowest Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) value was obtained.

We analyzed transmitter performance using the Argos Web Service [45] to assess tag and bird status throughout the tracking period and identify tag failure, potential tag drop or bird mortality. We only used location data where temperature and voltage readings were within valid ranges (temperature maintained diurnal fluctuation without persistent drops below 20 °C, voltage between 3.5 and 4.1 V). This ensures we are not getting unrealistically small home ranges or prolonged stopover resulting from detached tags or bird mortality.

Post-fledging period home ranges

Post-fledging home range size (95% UD; mean ± SD) averaged 8.88 ± 4.00 km2 in VT and 9.14 ± 5.80 km2 in NY, while core areas averaged 1.32 ± 0.61 km2 in VT and 1.42 ± 0.95 km2 in NY (Fig. 3Cand D). Despite these observed differences in mean values and variability between sites, there was no significant difference in either home range (t = 0.11, df = 17, p = 0.912) or core area (t = 0.29, df = 17, p = 0.779) size between VT and NY. However, our paired analysis revealed distinct patterns in home range size changes between nesting and post-fledging periods in the two populations. Among the 19 birds with data in both stages, VT birds significantly reduced their home range sizes from the nesting season (mean: 13.67 km2) to the post-fledging season (mean: 8.88 km2; paired t-test: t = 2.42, df = 12, p = 0.033). In contrast, NY birds exhibited an opposite but non-significant trend, with home range size increasing from 7.52 km2 to 9.14 km2 (t = −0.73, df = 5, p = 0.50). Similarly, core area size significantly decreased in VT birds (2.30 km2 to 1.32 km2; t = 2.31, df = 12, p = 0.039), whereas no change occurred with NY birds (1.09 km2 to 1.42 km2; t = −1.34, df = 5, p = 0.238).

Nocturnal pre-migration movements

Only four birds began their migration directly from their post-breeding grounds. Of the remaining nine, five relocated to a new site before departing for migration, three performed distinct loop movements, returning to their post-breeding grounds before migration, and one bird (2024-A) did both (Fig. 7). These movements were variable in direction and magnitude rather than consistent alignment with the expected fall migratory bearing. Discussion

This study represented the first use of satellite tags to track Bobolinks and one of the first deployments of Sunbird satellite transmitters on small passerines. Our tracking revealed previously undocumented forays during the breeding season, expanding our current knowledge about these hidden avian movements. We documented remarkable behavioral flexibility in Bobolink spatial ecology across breeding and post-breeding migration, reflecting how these long-distance migrants adapt to meet stage-specific ecological demands.

Nesting period home ranges

Previous estimates of Bobolink breeding movements were limited to territory size (1.01± 0.08 ha), determined through observational and re-location data [20]. However, territory size represents a portion of the home range, depicting only an individual’s actively defended space [38]. In contrast, our home range estimates (6–12 km2) exceed historical territory sizes by approximately two to three orders of magnitude, indicating that Bobolinks use substantially more space during the breeding season than previously recognized.

Comparison with other grassland birds further highlights the extensive space use we documented. Available estimates for grassland songbirds primarily come from studies of juveniles or wintering adults: fledgling Dickcissels (Spiza americana) occupied up to 77.0 ± 22 ha and juvenile Eastern Meadowlarks (Sturnella magna) used 109.4 ± 39.2 ha [46], while wintering Baird’s Sparrows (Centronyx bairdii) occupied 0.80 ± 0.11 ha, wintering Grasshopper Sparrows (Ammodramus savannarum) occupied 0.68 ha [47], and wintering Vesper Sparrows (Pooecetes gramineus) used 108.46 ± 36.43 ha [48]. All were tracked with radio-transmitters. Even our core area estimate (representing 50% UD) substantially exceeds the home ranges (95% UD) reported for most of these species. This discrepancy suggests that breeding adult Bobolinks utilize considerably more space than juveniles or wintering individuals of comparable grassland songbirds, potentially reflecting the energetic and territorial demands of reproduction and mate seeking.

Our home range estimates may overestimate absolute area due to inherent error in satellite tracking. For example, some estimated ranges at our VT site extended into Lake Champlain, despite no observations of Bobolinks flying over open water during fieldwork. However, because the same methodology was applied consistently across all individuals, sites, and seasons, any potential biases are expected to be similar across individuals and sties. This consistency allows robust comparisons of relative space use patterns across populations and seasons, even if absolute estimates are inflated.

Our finding that VT males breeding in the larger site maintained larger nesting home ranges than NY males suggests that lower suitable habitat availability and greater fragmentation at the NY site did not necessitate Bobolinks to travel farther to find food. Instead, the fragmented landscape likely acted as a barrier, constraining their movements by increasing predation risk at edge, and resulting in smaller home ranges confined to small fields. If we only look at landscape difference, this is contrary to most studies, because habitat fragmentation usually induces an enlargement in home range in habitat-specialist [21, 49, 50]. But this opposite trend has also been reported [51, 52].

Importantly, landscape configuration alone may not fully explain the observed differences in home range size. Several additional factors could interact with fragmentation to shape space use during the breeding season. These include age and experience [53, 54], as older or more site-faithful males may possess better knowledge of local food and water, reducing the need for extensive movements. Breeding-specific behaviors, such as mate searching, mate guarding, and territorial defense, may further influence movement patterns in ways not directly related to food acquisition [55]. Population density can also play a role, with higher densities could reduce home range through increased territorial interactions [56]. However, this mechanism can’t explain the present results given that the smaller NY site, with smaller home ranges, had lower population density based on our breeding surveys. Here, our analysis of the foray behaviors points to contrasting mate-seeking strategies shaped by landscape configuration.

Our tracking data provided clear evidence that male Bobolinks visited distant grasslands outside their normal home ranges, and males at the isolated small NY site made forays more frequently and traveled farther than males at the larger VT site. Extraterritorial foray is well-documented across animal taxa and can serve multiple functions, including foraging, information gathering, social interaction and reproduction [57–60]. In birds, studies of breeding-season forays for reproduction purposes have reported such behavior in various species, ranging from forest to grassland systems, and involving both sexes. For example, in isolated forest patch, 85% of tracked male Hooded Warblers (Setophaga citrina) traveled up to 2.5 km from their resident forest patches to another [61]. Eastern Kingbird (Tyrannus tyrannus) males traveled up to 15 km in search of extra-pair mating opportunities [62]. Other species show considerable variation in foray distance—Acadian Flycatchers (Empidonax virescens; 50–1500 m) [58], Chestnut-sided Warblers (Setophaga pensylvanica; travelling up to 10 territories distant) [63], and American Redstarts (Setophaga ruticilla; 20–1170 m) [64]—as well as in foray rate [61, 65].

In addition, movement outside established territories has been reported in more studies, many of them have not explicitly defined these behaviors as “forays” or “excursions.” In some cases, reports of territory changes or within-season dispersal—such as in Grasshopper Sparrows [66] and Sedge Wrens (Cistothorus platensis) [67]—likely reflect similar short-term movements that remain undetected or unnamed due to methodological limitations. Our study adds to this body of work by explicitly documenting and quantifying nesting-season forays in a grassland bird species, providing a detailed descriptions of this behavior in the context of a continuously monitored population.

From tracking data, we don’t have direct evidence that suggests the purpose of these forays, but the shared occurrence of extra-pair mating systems across songbird species suggests similar strategies in Bobolinks. An earlier study with our VT population reported 57% of the sampled nests had evidence of extra-pair-paternity and 42% of sampled nestlings were sired by extra-pair males [24]. In addition, they possess a unique reproductive trait that could facilitate extra-pair mating: female Bobolinks produce a distinctive vocalization only during their fertile period [15]. This specialized vocalization allows males to roam widely while listening, enabling them to locate fertile females more efficiently across the landscape, potentially increasing mating success rates. This behavior may explain our observed population-level differences in both foray frequency and distance.

Bobolinks at our larger VT site, where local female density was much higher, exhibited fewer forays. This pattern likely reflects two complementary factors: males may be making shorter movements within their home ranges given the abundance of local mating opportunities, while simultaneously needing to spend more time defending their territories against the higher number of neighboring males seeking extra-pair copulations [23, 68]. Therefore, the larger home ranges of VT males, shaped by the availability of contiguous grasslands, likely encompassed, and thus concealed, most of their forays. In the isolated NY landscape, which supported fewer than 10 breeding pairs, males faced limited local mate availability. Consequently, they could be using a movement strategy that maintained small daily activity areas as restricted by geography but occasionally engage in longer-distance forays beyond their typical nesting home range to access distant breeding populations.

We likely underestimated the frequency of forays due to inherent limitations in satellite tracking. Forays may have gone undetected if they occurred between satellite passes or if they generated only a single high-quality location point, which could not be reliably distinguished from a location outlier. The temporal gaps in our tracking system mean that brief exploratory movements were systematically underrepresented in our dataset. The potential magnitude of this underestimation is highlighted by studies showing that male Hooded Warblers departed from their isolated forest fragments more than once every two hours [61], and male Field Sparrows made an average of 0.28 forays per hour(≈ one foray every 3.6 hours) [65].

Future research across multiple field sites should investigate whether males successfully achieve copulations during these forays using behavioral observation or paternity analysis. Genetic sampling of offspring from neighboring populations would provide crucial evidence for the functional significance of these movements and help test our mate-seeking hypothesis [25, 62, 63]. Such data would also elucidate how landscape configuration influences reproductive behavior and gene flow. From a conservation perspective, maintaining large, connected, and unfragmented habitats appears important for facilitating safe movement and reducing predation and anthropogenic risks associated with traversing grassland edges, urban areas, or other unsuitable habitats, regardless of the underlying motivation for these forays.

Post-fledging period home ranges

Male Bobolinks at the larger VT site reduced their home range sizes from the nesting to post-fledging period, and several mechanisms could explain this pattern. Previous studies showed that bird home ranges are typically small during the breeding season because adults remain near the nest for incubation and chick provisioning [22, 23, 69, 70]. This constraint is stronger for females but also applies to males, who often stay close to defend the nest and their mate [23]. Although the opposite trend exists, it is usually found in species that provide no parental care [21], most notably cuckoos, which must roam widely during the breeding season to locate suitable host nests [71]. Accordingly, one explanation for the reduced post-fledging home range size in VT is that males no longer needed to travel widely in search of extra-pair mating opportunities. This is consistent with our observations of fewer foray events during this period (Fig. 6).

Physiological demands may also alter movement patterns during the post-fledging season. Bobolinks initiate their pre-basic molt on or near their breeding grounds in July–October [30], and molt is energetically costly and can reduce flight performance [72], which may constrain mobility and lead to smaller home ranges. Behavioral shifts such as flock formation could either expand or contract home ranges depending on habitat structure and foraging dynamics [73]. Meanwhile, pre-migratory fattening [29, 74] may increase or decrease space use: birds may forage across a broader area to meet rising energetic demands or may instead remain within a food-rich location, resembling the patterns found where fat reserves influence movement during stopovers [75–77].

At the small site, NY males showed no change in area used between the two breeding stages. The most plausible explanation is that their breeding home ranges were already highly constrained by the limited availability of suitable habitat within a fragmented landscape, leaving little scope for either expansion or further contraction of movements [78, 79]. This isolated 8-hectare field might represent the minimum area required to support their foraging, shelter, and information-gathering needs [19, 44].

Taken together, these behavioral differences—reduced movement in VT and no change in NY—produce convergent patterns of space use during the post-fledging period, underscoring that movement pattern on the breeding ground is shaped by both annual-cycle strategies and site-specific habitat characteristics.

We observed a significant decline in exploratory movements from 18 forays during the nesting season to only five forays during the post-breeding period (Fig. 6). These movements served different purposes than mate-seeking because there were no longer fertile female at this time, but could potentially facilitate exploration of potential future breeding sites and social interactions with neighboring populations to gain information in habitat quality. The practice of investigating alternative breeding locations, known as “prospecting behavior,” has been documented widely across species [80–83]. In Bobolinks, which exhibit high site fidelity [84], awareness of alternative breeding sites in their vicinity could be particularly advantageous. Nocera et al. [85] showed that young Bobolink males used conspecific cues from the previous year to decide where to establish territories in the subsequent year. Therefore, males with failed nests would also benefit from this information, especially under a dynamic relationship between Bobolinks and agricultural practices when fields are mowed or grazed during the breeding season [31].

Nocturnal pre-migratory movements

Study of transitions between stages of the annual cycle, including between breeding and the onset of migration, have been limited by technology to follow the movements of individuals throughout the annual cycle [86]. While physiological preparations during this period—such as increases in flight muscle mass, fat deposition, and gonadal regression [87–89]—are well described, the movement ecology of this phase was only rarely documented [86]. Previous behavioral studies of the pre-migratory phase have primarily focused on migration restlessness (Zugunruhe) in captive migratory songbirds [90]. While this nocturnal pre-migratory activity has been well documented in captivity, how it relates to actual movement patterns in wild populations remains largely unexplored, with documentation limited to juvenile dispersal behavior [86, 91]. A few studies have briefly mentioned or hinted that pre-migratory movements occur in adult songbirds, but only as uncommon events. For example, in Wood Thrushes (Hylocichla mustelina), 5 of 60 tracked individuals exhibited pre-migratory movements immediately prior to fall migration [91], and in American Tree Sparrows (Spizelloides arborea), one radio-tagged individual was detected at nearby stations at night before returning to the tagging site the following day [92]. In contrast, nine of the 13 adult Bobolinks we tracked displayed distinct nocturnal pre-migratory movements, suggesting that such behavior is the norm rather than the exception in this species. To our knowledge, this is the first documentation that pre-migration movement is common in adult songbirds. These findings suggest that pre-migratory behavior may vary among species and populations, offering new insight into the diversity of strategies birds use to prepare for migration.

The occurrence of these movements at night suggests that they likely represent initial migration attempts or practice sessions for nocturnal navigation [91]. As purposed by Züst et al. [86], these nocturnal pre-migratory movements likely served multiple functions, including the development of night flight skills, assessment of weather conditions, refinement of orientation abilities, formation of cognitive maps, and identification of suitable roosting habitat in darkness.

Knowing the age of birds would provide valuable insights into these proposed functions, as experienced birds would presumably not need to practice habitat selection or create mental maps compared to young birds. However, we can’t differentiate second-year bird and after-second-year bird by molt in the summer [30]. Nevertheless, the observation that these experienced adults who had completed at least one annual migration cycle still performed pre-migratory movements suggests that these behaviors were not driven entirely by spatial learning, but rather most likely enable birds to assess whether current physiological condition and meteorological factors are conducive to migration initiation on any given night [86].

Migratory stopover home ranges

Stopover duration and movement patterns are known to depend on both intrinsic and extrinsic factors: fat storage upon arrival [76], age or social status [93], food availability [94], geography [95], and weather [96]. In our sample, we observed substantial variation in stopover movement behavior: some individuals concentrated their activity within a single field, whereas others ranged widely across multiple habitat patches. As a result, temporary home range estimates spanned more than two orders of magnitude (0.88–410.75 km2). Although animals that are dispersing or continually relocating typically do not meet the criteria for defining a home range [36], we included these widely roaming individuals to capture the full range of stopover strategies expressed by Bobolinks.

Our data showed that temporary home-range size was modestly associated with atmospheric conditions and seasonal timing, but overall model fit was limited. Wind components showed the strongest statistical relationships, with larger home ranges observed under stronger eastward and southward winds. These directions broadly correspond to supportive winds during the early portion of fall migration in our study system. However, given the low explanatory power of the model, this association should be interpreted cautiously. Home-range size also declined later in the season and under cooler temperatures, although these effects were weak.

Substantial unexplained variation suggests that individual-level attributes—particularly body condition at arrival, as emphasized in previous studies that showed lean birds are more likely to maintain a territory compared to fat birds at stopovers [76, 77]—may play a more important role in shaping stopover space use than the environmental variables we were able to quantify remotely.

Conservation implication

Although our study focused only on one sex, the multi-period movement patterns we documented in Bobolinks reveal critical conservation considerations for this declining grassland species. The purposes of the breeding season forays we documented—whether related to extra-pair mating, prospecting, or resource acquisition—remain unclear. Nevertheless, the observed capacity for extensive within-season movements suggests that individuals may rely on access to multiple habitat patches within agricultural landscapes. If so, landscape configuration and connectivity could influence how birds respond to spatial and temporal variation in habitat conditions. Such considerations may be particularly relevant in regions where hay harvest timing varies among fields, potentially creating asynchronous breeding conditions. In these contexts, access to multiple patches could provide flexibility in habitat use. However, evaluating the demographic consequences of landscape connectivity will require direct measures of reproductive success and survival.

Our migration analysis identified critical stopover locations along Bobolink’s fall migratory route that warrant targeted conservation attention. All individuals that were tracked through their trans-oceanic migration used Caribbean islands as stopover sites during fall migration, with Cuba being particularly prominent. These stopover locations serve a critical function for energy replenishment, as they are positioned between two major ocean-crossing events: after the non-stop Atlantic Ocean crossing from North America and before the subsequent Caribbean Sea crossing to reach South American wintering grounds. The strategic positioning on the Caribbean islands between these two energetically demanding barriers makes them essential refueling stations for the entire eastern North American Bobolink population during southward migration. Similarly, geolocator studies have revealed that Bobolinks make extended stopovers in northern South America, particularly Venezuela, before continuing to their final wintering destinations [97]. These findings underscore the importance of international conservation efforts that not only protect breeding and wintering habitats but also maintain the integrity of stopover sites throughout the complete migratory corridor. Given that migration mortality can be substantial for long-distance migrants and that habitat loss at even a single critical stopover site can create bottlenecks affecting entire populations [98, 99], conservation strategies must adopt a full annual cycle approach that addresses habitat protection and threat mitigation across the species’ range from North American grasslands through Caribbean islands to South American agricultural landscapes.
