Readiness for Hospital Discharge and 30‑Day Unplanned Readmission in Older Adult Sepsis Survivors from Internal Medicine Wards: A Cross‑Sectional Study
Authors: Shuting Hou, Liqin Wei, Yanting Gong, Qin Tang, Qiuru Huang, Huan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo investigate discharge readiness among older adult sepsis survivors and its influencing factors, and to explore the relationship with 30-day unplanned readmission.MethodsFrom May 2024 to May 2025, a cohort of 330 older adult sepsis survivors was recruited from an infectious disease center in China. Before discharge, participants completed questionnaires on demographic and disease characteristics, the Readiness for Hospital Discharge Scale (RHDS), and the Quality of Discharge Teaching Scale (QDTS). A follow-up survey on the 30-day unplanned readmission was subsequently conducted. The data were analyzed using Spearman correlation and multiple linear regression.ResultsThe mean scores for RHDS and QDTS were 93.54 ± 19.96 and 155.21 ± 24.57. RHDS was primarily influenced by the QDTS, length of hospitalization, medical expenses payment, and general self-efficacy, which together explained 43.0% of the variance. Lower discharge readiness was associated with a higher risk of unplanned readmission within 30 days.ConclusionThe readiness for hospital discharge of older adult sepsis survivors is moderate and influenced by multiple factors, with the quality of discharge teaching being particularly significant. Higher levels of discharge readiness are associated with a lower rate of unplanned readmission.Keywords: sepsis, readiness for hospital discharge, quality of discharge teaching, unplanned readmission

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection.1 Its high occurrence rate, high mortality rate, and high consumption of medical resources have become a major global public health challenge. The incidence of sepsis is rising annually, with a rate in China of approximately 421.9 cases per 100,000 people. Among hospitalized patients, the incidence reaches about 33.6%, a figure substantially higher than those reported in North America and Europe. Patients aged 65 and above are the primary affected demographic, accounting for 57.5% of all cases.2,3 Older patients with sepsis frequently present with multiple chronic comorbidities, including hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease.4 Their condition often progresses rapidly, with a high risk of multi-organ involvement.5 Treatment requires comprehensive measures, including antifungal therapy, organ support, and nutritional support. Some patients also require invasive procedures, including mechanical ventilation and vasoactive drug administration.1 These treatment characteristics result in complex self-management needs for older adult sepsis survivors after discharge, encompassing infection control, complication monitoring, functional rehabilitation, and management of underlying diseases.6,7 The complex self-management required of elderly patients, compounded by their declining physiological functions, renders the post-discharge transition a period of heightened medical risk. Research into the current state of discharge readiness and its determinants is therefore urgently needed.

Although timely treatment can improve the survival rate of sepsis, survivors still face a high risk of reinfection and readmission. For example, the readmission rate within 30 days of discharge can reach 23.6%, with some patients experiencing up to three readmissions.8 Older adult sepsis patients have higher care needs, and their one-year all-cause mortality after discharge is significantly correlated with the level of care required.9 This population often suffers from post-sepsis syndrome, characterized by cognitive impairment, decreased physical function, difficulties with activities of daily living, and reduced quality of life, frequently accompanied by reinfection and rehospitalization.10,11 Readiness for hospital discharge, as a core component of discharge planning, encompasses physiological stability and self-care ability, post-discharge support resources, psychological coping capacity, and relevant knowledge.12 It serves as a key indicator for predicting adverse outcomes after discharge. It can be used to assess the rehabilitation capacity, transition safety, and risks of readmission and mortality among older adult sepsis survivors. Older adult sepsis patients often experience an acute onset and multi-organ involvement. They are prone to residual functional impairments, making it difficult to achieve adequate rehabilitation and self-management learning during hospitalization.13 Under China’s DRG-based healthcare system, some patients are required to be discharged as soon as their condition stabilizes, further compressing the time available for discharge readiness. This results in patients and caregivers failing to systematically master essential skills such as infection monitoring, complication management, and medication administration. Simultaneously, the trauma of the disease and treatment procedures can easily trigger psychological stress, reducing self-efficacy. Coupled with the decline in physiological function due to aging and insufficient social support, these factors collectively exacerbate the risk of inadequate discharge readiness.14,15 Therefore, assessing readiness for hospital discharge is of great significance for ensuring the safety of hospital discharge for older adult sepsis patients, enhancing their self-management capabilities outside the hospital, and reducing the risk of readmission and mortality. However, there is currently no investigation into the discharge readiness of this population.

Given these challenges, this study focuses on older adult sepsis survivors to examine their readiness for hospital discharge and the factors influencing it. The findings are intended to inform the development of targeted interventions and supportive policies. Ultimately, this work seeks to enhance the quality of care and improve post-discharge health outcomes in this patient population.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

This prospective observational study adhered to the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE)” guidelines for reporting observational studies.16 It was conducted from May 2024 to May 2025 at a tertiary hospital in southern China. A convenience sampling method was used to recruit patients aged at least 60 years who were diagnosed with sepsis based on confirmed or suspected infection via auxiliary examinations and a Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score ≥ 2, who were hospitalized for treatment, and who voluntarily participated in the study after providing informed consent. Participants with psychiatric disorders were excluded from the study.

Procedures

This study was approved by the West China Hospital of Sichuan University Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2024 Review (977)) and conformed to the ethical standards for medical research involving human subjects, as laid out in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. All data were anonymized using study numbers to protect participant confidentiality. According to an established methodological report, the required sample size should be 5 to 10 times the number of variables.17 An additional 20% allowance for attrition was included to account for potential follow-up loss. With 14 independent variables included in this study, the final target sample size range was therefore 84–168 participants. We finally recruited 330 participants. Participants could withdraw at any time without affecting their subsequent care. Eligible participants provided written informed consent and independently completed the questionnaire in a quiet room two hours before discharge, with researchers available to provide standardized clarification if needed. This timing was selected to align with the institutional discharge workflow, as discharge education, medication counseling, and final clinical evaluations were typically completed before this stage. Assessing participants shortly before discharge allowed patients to evaluate their discharge readiness after receiving relevant discharge-related information while ensuring feasibility and consistency of data collection prior to leaving the hospital. Researchers checked the questionnaires for completeness and collected them on-site. On the 30th day post-discharge, patients were contacted by telephone to confirm any unplanned readmissions, which were then verified against medical records.

Measurements

A self-designed general demographic and disease-related questionnaire was used, along with the RHDS, QDTS, General Self-Efficacy Scale, and an unplanned readmission follow-up survey.

Demographic and Disease-Related Questionnaire

Demographic data, including gender, age, Body Mass Index(BMI), marital status, educational level, place of residence, and occupation, were collected. Disease-related information, such as length of hospital stay, pathogen type, and comorbidities, was also obtained. All data were gathered using a comprehensive, self-designed questionnaire.

RHDS

The Chinese version of the RHDS employed here was originally developed by Weiss18 and subsequently adapted by Lin19 through localization. This 12-item instrument uses a 0–10 scoring range per item, yielding a maximum total score of 120; higher scores indicate greater patient readiness for hospital discharge. Readiness for discharge is represented by the average score across all items, obtained by dividing the total score by 12. An average score below 7 denotes a low level of readiness, while scores of 7–7.9, 8–8.9, and ≥9 represent moderate, high, and very high levels, respectively. The scale comprises three dimensions: personal status (3 items), adaptability (5 items), and expected support (4 items). Its Cronbach’s alpha coefficient is 0.91.

QDTS

The Chinese version of the QDTS employed here was originally developed by Weiss18 and subsequently adapted by Wang20 through a localization process. This 24-item scale assesses content needed before discharge (6 items), the actual teaching content received (6 items), and teaching skills and effectiveness (12 items). The quality of discharge teaching is determined by the total score of the latter two dimensions, which has a maximum of 180 points; higher scores indicate better teaching quality. An average item score is calculated by dividing the total score by the number of items. Quality levels are defined as follows: low (average score < 7), moderate (7–7.9), high (8–8.9), and very high (≥9). The scale demonstrates high internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.903.

General Self-Efficacy Scale

The general self-efficacy scale employed in this study was originally developed by Schwarzer21 and subsequently adapted for Chinese populations by Wang.22 Its 10 items are each rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with individual item scores from 1 to 4 yielding a total possible score between 10 and 40. Higher total scores indicate greater self-efficacy. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87.

Unplanned Readmission

In this study, unplanned readmission was defined as a hospital admission occurring between 1 and 30 days after discharge for a condition related to the initial sepsis-associated infection, excluding scheduled follow-ups or treatment for unrelated illnesses. On the 30th day post-discharge, the patient’s status was ascertained via a telephone interview. This information was then verified against the hospital’s medical record system or other relevant documentation. All readmission events were subsequently compiled and analyzed.

Data Analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS version 26.0. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent-samples t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were analyzed using the chi-square test. Correlations among the RHDS score, QDTS score, and 30-day unplanned readmission were examined using Pearson correlation analysis. Variables that were statistically significant in the univariate analyses were subsequently entered into a multiple linear regression model to identify factors independently associated with discharge readiness. To further evaluate the relationship between discharge readiness and 30-day unplanned readmission, three complementary analytical approaches were applied. First, a point-biserial correlation analysis was performed to assess the direction and strength of the association between the RHDS total score and readmission status. Second, multivariable binary logistic regression analysis was conducted using 30-day unplanned readmission as the dependent variable and the RHDS total score as the primary independent variable, adjusting for age group, sex, BMI, comorbidity status, living status, and length of hospitalization. Model calibration was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and overall model significance was assessed using the Omnibus likelihood ratio test. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CI were reported for all variables. Third, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the ability of the RHDS total score to discriminate patients with and without 30-day unplanned readmission. The area under the curve and corresponding 95% CI were calculated. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant Characteristics

A total of 330 patients were enrolled before discharge, though eight subsequently declined to participate in the follow-up survey. Among the 330 participants, 55.5% were male, and 44.5% were female, with a mean age of 70.51 ± 8.27 years. The overall population had a relatively low educational level, as 72.1% had attained a primary school education or less. Most survivors were married (88.5%) and not living alone (83.6%), and bacterial pathogens were the predominant cause of infection (86.1%). Assessment of RHDS and QDTS

The RHDS total scores ranged from 38 to 109, with a mean of 93.54 ± 19.96 (Table 2). The mean scores for the personal status, adaptability, and expected support dimensions were 22.72 ± 8.25, 36.10 ± 12.94, and 34.72 ± 6.98, respectively. The average item score was 7.80 ± 1.66. Overall, patients exhibited a relatively moderate level of discharge readiness. For the QDTS, total scores ranged from 47 to 173, with a mean of 155.21 ± 24.57 (Table 3), yielding an average item score of 8.62 ± 1.37. The quality of discharge teaching was positively correlated with discharge readiness (r = 0.521, P < 0.01).

Factors Associated with RHDS

Univariate analysis identified significant associations (P<0.05) between discharge readiness and age, length of hospital stay, occupation, and medical expense payment. These variables, along with the QDTS, were subsequently entered into a multiple linear regression model with discharge readiness as the dependent variable. The final regression model included four significant predictors: QDTS (β = 0.35, P< 0.001), length of hospital stay (β = −0.161, P = 0.014), medical expense payment (β = −0.146, P = 0.026), and general self-efficacy (β = 0.148, P = 0.028), which together explained 43% of the variance (Table 4).

Relationship Between RHDS and Unplanned Readmission

Among the 322 patients included in the study, 79 (24.5%) experienced an unplanned readmission within 30 days after discharge. A statistically significant difference in discharge readiness was observed between patients with and without 30-day unplanned readmission (t = −4.093, P < 0.001). A multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to explore factors associated with 30-day unplanned readmission. After adjustment for clinically relevant covariates, including age, comorbidity burden, and length of hospitalization, RHDS score remained significantly associated with readmission status (B (SE): −0.039 (0.011), P < 0.001) (Table 5). The distribution of RHDS total scores according to 30-day unplanned readmission status is presented in Figure 1. Point-biserial correlation analysis demonstrated a moderate negative correlation between the RHDS total score and 30-day unplanned readmission (rpb = −0.298, P < 0.001). ROC curve analysis showed that the RHDS total score had acceptable discriminative ability for identifying 30-day unplanned readmission, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.705 (95% CI: 0.619–0.791, P < 0.001) (Figure 2). Lower RHDS scores were associated with a higher risk of readmission.

Discussion

This study investigated discharge readiness among older adult sepsis survivors, identified its associated factors, and further examined its relationship with 30-day unplanned readmission. The findings showed that the 30-day unplanned readmission rate in this population was 24.5%. The overall discharge readiness score, assessed using the RHDS, was 7.80 ± 1.66, indicating a moderate level of discharge readiness. These findings suggest that, despite receiving discharge preparation and education, a substantial proportion of older adult sepsis survivors may still face difficulties in achieving a safe transition from hospital to home. Compared with older adults with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) in China, whose reported RHDS score was 6.48 ± 0.19, the patients in this study demonstrated higher overall discharge readiness.23 Older adult sepsis survivors also showed higher scores across all RHDS dimensions, accompanied by greater inter-individual variability. This difference may be related to the distinct clinical characteristics and recovery trajectories of the two conditions. In sepsis, the severity of organ dysfunction, treatment complexity, and pace of recovery can vary substantially among patients, resulting in greater heterogeneity in discharge readiness.1 In contrast, COPD generally follows a more stable and chronic disease course, which may contribute to smaller inter-individual differences in RHDS scores.24 These findings suggest that, although older adult sepsis survivors may demonstrate relatively higher discharge readiness overall, considerable variability remains within this population.

Importantly, discharge readiness was significantly associated with QDTS, length of hospital stay, method of medical expense payment, self-efficacy, and 30-day readmission. Among these, QDTS showed the strongest associations. Several findings of this study are consistent with previous research in other patient populations.25,26 The positive association between RHDS and QDTS highlights the importance of structured and effective discharge education in facilitating the transition from hospital to home. Similarly, self-efficacy has been identified as an important psychological factor influencing discharge preparation, as patients with greater confidence in self-management are more likely to report higher discharge readiness.27 The negative association between length of hospitalization and discharge readiness is consistent with previous findings in older adult populations.28 Prolonged hospitalization is often associated with greater frailty, functional decline, and increased illness severity, all of which may reduce patients’ perceived ability to manage their care after discharge. The association between medical payment method and discharge readiness highlights the important role of financial burden in shaping patients’ preparedness for discharge.29,30 Within the Chinese healthcare system, Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance generally provides higher reimbursement rates and broader healthcare coverage, whereas Urban–Rural Resident Medical Insurance offers comparatively lower reimbursement and more limited benefits. Patients without insurance are required to pay healthcare expenses out of pocket. These differences may affect patients’ access to medications, rehabilitation services, and follow-up care, thereby influencing both their confidence in post-discharge recovery and their psychological readiness to leave the hospital.

Beyond confirming previous evidence, this study provides additional context-specific insights in older adult sepsis survivors, a population characterized by high clinical complexity and vulnerability after hospital discharge. The results showed that lower discharge readiness was significantly associated with an increased risk of 30-day unplanned readmission. Patients who experienced readmission generally reported lower RHDS scores, and this association remained significant after adjustment for demographic and clinical characteristics, suggesting that discharge readiness may be independently associated with post-discharge outcomes. Specifically, each one-point increase in the RHDS score was associated with an approximately 4% reduction in the risk of 30-day unplanned readmission. However, the RHDS alone demonstrated only moderate discriminative ability for identifying readmission risk and therefore should not be considered a standalone predictive tool. This finding likely reflects the multifactorial nature of readmission among sepsis survivors, which may be influenced by persistent physical and cognitive impairment, comorbidity burden, functional decline, and insufficient social or caregiver support after discharge.31 Discharge readiness assessment may therefore be most valuable when integrated into comprehensive clinical and transitional care evaluations, helping clinicians identify patients who may require additional post-discharge support and follow-up.

A notable finding of this study is the discrepancy between relatively high perceived quality of discharge teaching and only moderate discharge readiness. This suggests that satisfaction with discharge education does not necessarily translate into adequate preparedness for post-discharge self-management.32 In older adult sepsis survivors, this discrepancy may reflect limited health literacy, cognitive or functional impairment, and the complexity of post-sepsis recovery. Evidence indicates that patients may report high satisfaction with discharge communication due to supportive and empathic interactions with healthcare providers, while still demonstrating insufficient understanding of key aspects of post-discharge care, including self-management, rehabilitation requirements, follow-up plans, and warning signs of clinical deterioration.33,34 These findings highlight that discharge education should not only focus on information delivery but also on ensuring comprehension and retention. In this regard, the teach-back method represents a practical and effective strategy, allowing healthcare providers to verify patient understanding and reinforce essential knowledge throughout the discharge process.35 Furthermore, the timing and repetition of discharge education are critical. Providing structured and repeated education throughout hospitalization, rather than concentrating all information immediately before discharge, may improve knowledge retention and enhance true readiness for discharge.36,37

Our study shows that the high expected support scores suggest that caregivers are well-positioned to serve as active partners in the discharge education process. Artificial intelligence (AI) may offer additional support for discharge planning by assisting clinicians in developing simplified, individualized, and multimodal educational materials, including pictorial and caregiver-oriented instructions that are more accessible for older adults and individuals with limited health literacy. However, current evidence emphasizes that AI should remain a clinician-supervised supportive tool rather than an autonomous system, due to risks such as inaccurate or hallucinated outputs, privacy concerns, and variability in digital literacy.38 And its integration into nurse-led discharge education must account for practical logistical barriers including staff training, workflow compatibility, and the time required for clinical review of AI-generated content. Accordingly, AI should be integrated into nurse-led discharge education as an adjunct tool to support, rather than replace, clinical judgment.

The findings of this study suggest that improving discharge readiness among older adult sepsis survivors requires a comprehensive and individualized approach. Improving discharge teaching quality may help patients better understand their care needs. Strengthening self-efficacy may also increase their confidence in managing health after discharge. Addressing financial barriers and improving transitional care planning may further enhance preparedness for discharge and reduce adverse outcomes after discharge. Early identification of patients at risk of poor discharge readiness is important. This is especially relevant for patients with prolonged hospitalization or higher financial burden. Timely and targeted interventions may then be provided to these patients. Structured nurse-led discharge programs that continue throughout hospitalization may also be beneficial. These programs should not be limited to the time of discharge. Instead, they should support patients gradually during the hospital stay to promote a safer transition to home care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings and patient populations. Second, indicators of acute illness severity, such as ICU admission, mechanical ventilation, or disease severity scores, were not systematically collected. Because illness severity may influence both patients’ perceived readiness for discharge and subsequent risk of readmission, residual confounding cannot be excluded. In addition, the absence of long-term follow-up precluded further evaluation of the association between discharge readiness and important post-discharge outcomes, such as mortality, quality of home care, and long-term functional recovery. Future multi-center studies with extended follow-up periods and more comprehensive clinical severity assessments are warranted to better clarify the relationship between discharge readiness and long-term prognosis among older adult patients with sepsis, thereby providing stronger evidence to guide individualized nursing interventions across different clinical settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, older adult sepsis survivors demonstrated moderate discharge readiness, which was associated with quality of discharge teaching, self-efficacy, length of hospital stay, medical payment method, and 30-day readmission. Consistent with previous studies, discharge teaching quality and self-efficacy were important factors associated with discharge readiness. Importantly, this study also provides novel context-specific insights into discharge readiness and readmission risk among older adult sepsis survivors, a clinically vulnerable population with complex post-discharge needs. However, given the observational and single-center design, these associations should be interpreted with caution. Strengthening discharge education, addressing psychosocial and financial barriers, and improving transitional care strategies may enhance discharge readiness and potentially improve post-discharge outcomes in this population.
