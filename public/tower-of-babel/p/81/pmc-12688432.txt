Predictors of Sinus Rhythm Maintenance After Catheter Ablation in Atrial Fibrillation Patients With Significant Atrial Functional Mitral Regurgitation
Authors: Mitsuhiko Shoda, Mitsuru Takami, Kimitake Imamura, Ken-ichi Tani, Hidehiro Iwai, Yusuke Nakanishi, Atsushi Murakami, Shogo Yonehara, Hiroyuki Asada, Takahiro Kunigita, Mari Yamamoto, Ryosuke Takahashi, Koji Fukuzawa, Hiromasa Otake
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtrial functional mitral regurgitation (FMR) results from left atrial enlargement and dysfunction, typically observed in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF). Predictors of sinus rhythm maintenance after catheter ablation in atrial FMR patients are not well understood.Methods and ResultsWe retrospectively reviewed 1,410 consecutive patients who underwent initial catheter ablation for AF at Kobe University Hospital between January 2014 and December 2022. Of these patients, 56 (4%; mean [±SD] age 68±8 years; 68% male) had significant (moderate, n=48; severe, n=8) atrial FMR based on pre-ablation transesophageal echocardiography. At follow-up echocardiography, a reduction in the left atrial diameter (LAd) was observed in 30 patients, whereas improvement in mitral regurgitation (MR) was noted in 26. During a mean follow-up period of 835 days, AF recurred in 23 (41%) patients. Kaplan-Meier curves for AF recurrence did not differ based on LAd reductions or MR improvements alone. However, recurrence rates were significantly lower in patients who achieved both LAd reduction and MR improvement than in those who did not achieve both changes (P=0.0259). Multivariate analysis revealed that the combination of LAd reduction and MR improvement was the only significant predictor of a decrease in AF recurrence (hazard ratio 0.275; 95% confidence interval 0.091–0.826; P=0.021).ConclusionsIn AF patients with significant atrial FMR, achieving both LAd reduction and MR improvement after ablation is important to reduce the risk of AF recurrence.

Central Figure

Recurrence of atrial fibrillation (AF) after catheter ablation is influenced by various factors, including the type and duration of AF, left atrial (LA) enlargement, progression of atrial fibrosis, obesity, and lifestyle factors.1–4 However, the association between mitral regurgitation (MR) and AF recurrence has not been fully elucidated. Organic MR (OMR), caused by structural defects in the valve apparatus, has been shown to increase the risk of AF recurrence after catheter ablation due to the persistent LA load caused by the residual MR after ablation.5,6 In contrast, inconsistent results have been reported for the relationship between atrial functional MR (FMR), which is primarily caused by atrial remodeling due to AF without any organic abnormality of the valve, and the risk of AF recurrence. Some studies have reported that atrial FMR is a predictor of recurrence after ablation, whereas others suggest that it is not.7–10 These apparent discrepancies across studies may be related to differences in the FMR grade, the degree of LA reverse remodeling, and changes in MR severity after ablation, all of which may contribute to AF recurrence.

Thus, the aim of the present study was to investigate the predictors of recurrence after AF ablation in patients with moderate or severe atrial FMR.

Methods

Patients

This study retrospectively reviewed 1,410 consecutive patients who underwent initial radiofrequency or cryoballoon ablation of paroxysmal and non-paroxysmal AF at Kobe University Hospital between January 2014 and December 2022. Paroxysmal AF (PAF) was defined as episodes of AF lasting <7 days, followed by spontaneous reversion to sinus rhythm. Those patients who met the criteria for atrial FMR (see below) and were not excluded based on the exclusion criteria were included in the study. The exclusion criteria for this study were previous AF ablation, previous cardiac surgery or congenital heart disease, non-LA enlargement, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <50%, OMR, and no follow-up transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) within 1 year after ablation.

The protocol for this research project was approved by the suitably constituted Ethics Committee of Kobe University Hospital. The study conforms to the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients consented to the use of their anonymized clinical data for research purposes in accordance with an opt-out approach.

Echocardiography

Standard 2-dimensional and Doppler echocardiography with color flow mapping was performed using TTE. The LA diameter (LAd) was measured through the anteroposterior diameter from the parasternal long-axis view, and the LVEF was calculated using Simpson’s method. The degree of MR before AF ablation was assessed using both TTE and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). The degree of MR after AF ablation was assessed using TTE. MR was evaluated as per the recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography. The ratio of the maximum MR color jet area to the LA area (MR/LA ratio) was calculated, and MR was classified as either mild (MR/LA ratio ≥0.1 and <0.2), moderate (MR/LA ratio ≥0.2 and <0.4), or severe (MR/LA ratio ≥0.4).11

In the present study, atrial FMR was defined as a preserved LVEF (defined as LVEF ≥50%) with LA enlargement and no organic abnormalities of the mitral valve leaflets, but improper coaptation due to LA enlargement. By definition, atrial FMR originates primarily from LA enlargement rather than left ventricular (LV) dilatation or systolic dysfunction. In clinical practice, mild LV enlargement or mild LV systolic dysfunction may coexist in patients with atrial FMR due to a volume overload from chronic MR. However, previous studies on atrial FMR have commonly used an LVEF cut-off of 50%;12 therefore, we also excluded patients with LVEF <50% at the time of enrollment. LA enlargement was defined as LAd ≥40 mm or LA volume index >30 mL/m2 (which is the upper 95% limit of the normal range for healthy Japanese individuals).13

For echocardiographic assessments conducted during AF, all measurements were performed using a single beat following serial beats with average R-R intervals. These parameters were carefully analyzed by 2 independent investigators (M.S. and another physician from the research team).

TEE assesses MR severity as accurately as or more reliably than TTE.14 A prior study using both TTE and TEE before AF ablation found that FMR observed on TTE was not linked to recurrence, whereas moderate to severe FMR on TEE was a risk factor for long-term recurrence.15 Accordingly, in this study, MR severity determined by TEE was used for patient enrollment.

Ablation Procedures

Ablation procedures were performed under local anesthesia with mild conscious sedation. Intravenous heparin was administered during the procedure, with the doses adjusted to achieve an activated clotting time of >350 s. Most patients (n=47; 83.9%) underwent circumferential pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) with point-by-point applications performed using an irrigation tip catheter to create contiguous lesions under the guidance of a 3-dimensional mapping system (CARTO System [Biosense Webster, Diamond Bar, CA, USA] or NavX/EnSite System [St. Jude Medical, St Paul, MN, USA]). The remaining patients underwent an individual PVI using a cryoballoon ablation catheter (Arctic Front Advance; Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA). The cryoballoon was inserted into the pulmonary vein ostium over a 10-polar spiral mapping catheter (Achieve; Medtronic), and complete pulmonary vein ostium occlusion was confirmed using contrast medium injection. In each vein, freezing was performed for 160–240 s with a minimum temperature of −60℃ to reduce the risk of pulmonary vein stenosis. Additional procedures (e.g., LA posterior wall isolation, superior vena cava isolation, and non-pulmonary vein foci ablation) were performed at the discretion of the operator. The procedural endpoints were the completeness of continuous circular lesions and the electrical isolation of all pulmonary veins. In all patients in this study, all 4 pulmonary veins were successfully isolated from the LA during the first procedure.

Follow-up

To evaluate AF recurrence after ablation, patients underwent follow-up at outpatient clinics at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after ablation, and every 6–12 months thereafter. In addition, a 24-h Holter electrocardiogram (ECG) was performed within 1 year after the procedure. If patients exhibited symptoms, a new ECG or 24-h Holter ECG was conducted. Recurrence was defined as the detection of AF lasting >30 s, as recorded on an ECG, 24-h Holter monitor, or a portable ECG device, after catheter ablation. There was a blanking period of 3 months, during which transient episodes of AF were considered temporary phenomena. After catheter ablation, antiarrhythmic drugs were prescribed at the operator’s discretion and were generally discontinued within 3–6 months at the operator’s discretion if sinus rhythm was maintained. MR improvement was defined as a reduction in the MR grade, as evaluated by the MR/LA ratio, on TTE during follow-up compared with preprocedural MR grade. A reduction in LAd was defined as a >5% reduction in the LAd after AF ablation.16

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data are presented as the mean±SD if normally distributed and as the median with interquartile range (IQR) if their distribution was skewed. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared between groups using unpaired t tests or the Welch test; skewed variables were compared using Mann-Whitney U tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. The normality of data distribution was tested using. Cox regression analysis was used to identify predictors of AF recurrence. In addition, survival curves were plotted and compared between groups using log-rank tests. P<0.05 (two-tailed) was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Easy R) version 1.61 (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Of the 1,410 patients who underwent AF ablation at Kobe University Hospital, 56 (4%) were diagnosed with significant (moderate or severe) atrial FMR based on pre-ablation TEE and did not meet the exclusion criteria. The characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. The mean age of the cohort was 68±8 years, and 68% were male. Non-PAF was present in 75% of patients. Pre-ablation TTE showed a normal LVEF (60±5%) and no LV enlargement; however, the LAd was enlarged (45±6 mm). The severity of MR on TTE was mild in 50% of patients, moderate in 46% of patients, and severe in 4% of patients. On TEE, the severity of MR was moderate in 86% of patients and severe in 14%. Changes in the TTE Parameters Before and After Ablation

The median follow-up period for the TTE was performed a median of 95 days (IQR 88–138 days) after ablation. There was no significant change in LVEF from before to after ablation (60±5% vs. 61±6%, respectively; P=0.13), whereas there was a significant reduction in LAd (44.9±5.9 vs. 42.5±5.9 mm, respectively; P<0.001). A >5% reduction in LAd was observed in 30 of 56 patients. An improvement in MR was noted in 26 of 56 patients. Twenty of 56 patients exhibited both a reduction (>5%) in LAd and an improvement in MR.

AF Recurrence After Catheter Ablation

The mean follow-up duration was 835.1 days, with a maximum period of 5 years.

During the follow-up period, 23 (41%) of 56 patients experienced AF recurrence. Table 2 presents baseline characteristics and ablation procedures for patients with and without AF recurrence. There were no significant differences between the 2 groups in age, the frequency of non-PAF, the use of antiarrhythmic drugs before the procedure, the addition of antiarrhythmic drugs after the procedure, the use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers/angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor, mineralocorticoid antagonists, or sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, preprocedural LVEF, LAd, or ablation procedures. A reduction in LAd was observed in both the groups from baseline to follow-up: from 45.1±6.6 to 42.7±6.8 mm (P=0.003) in the recurrence group and from 44.9±5.5 to 42.4±5.3 mm (P<0.001) in the non-recurrence group. However, MR improvement was observed only in the non-recurrence group (P<0.001 vs. baseline), and not in the recurrence group (P=0.145 vs. baseline; Figure 1). Predictors of Recurrence After Ablation

All potential confounders that may affect AF recurrence after catheter ablation based on previous studies or expected clinical relevance were included in the Cox regression analysis (Table 3). In the univariate analysis, neither an LAd reduction (≥5%) nor MR improvement alone was associated with a lower recurrence rate after ablation. However, when both an LAd reduction (≥5%) and MR improvement (defined as a structural improvement) were present, a statistically significant association with reduced AF recurrence was revealed. The multivariate analysis also demonstrated that a structural improvement was the only significant negative predictor of AF recurrence after ablation (hazard ratio 0.275; 95% confidence interval 0.091–0.826; P=0.021). Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated that patients who achieved structural improvement had a lower incidence of AF recurrence during the follow-up period (Figure 2). Association Between Structural Improvement and AF Recurrence

Figure 3 illustrates the association between structural improvements, namely LAd reduction (≥5%) and MR improvement, and AF recurrence in this study. At the time of follow-up TTE, AF recurrence was observed in 10 the 56 patients, of whom only 2 (20.0%) had structural improvement. Among the 46 patients who maintained sinus rhythm until the follow-up TTE, 18 (39.1%) had structural improvement (P=0.304). Notably, during follow-up of the 46 patients who maintained sinus rhythm until the follow-up TTE, AF recurred in only 2 (11.1%) of 18 patients with structural improvement, compared with 11 (39.3%) of 28 patients without structural improvement (P=0.049). Predictors of Structural Improvement

Table 4 presents baseline characteristics in patients with and without structural improvement. Although age, pre-ablation LAd, LVEF, B-type natriuretic peptide levels, and the use of medications (e.g., angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers/angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor, mineralocorticoid antagonists, or sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors) did not differ significantly between the 2 groups, patients with structural improvement tended to have a shorter AF history (P=0.059). Discussion

The main finding of this study is that in AF patients with moderate or greater atrial FMR identified via TEE before ablation, achieving structural improvement, consisting of both LAd reduction ≥5% and MR improvement after ablation, and not just one or the other, was associated with a reduced risk of AF recurrence during long-term follow-up.

Etiology and Prevalence of Atrial FMR

MR is traditionally categorized as either OMR or FMR. OMR arises from structural defects in the valve apparatus, often caused by conditions such as a mitral valve prolapse, flail, or endocarditis. In contrast, in FMR, the valve structure remains intact and the regurgitation results from a failure of a coaptation of the mitral valve leaflets. FMR related to AF is believed to arise from LA enlargement and mitral annulus dilation, disruption of the mitral annulus saddle shape, imbalance between the mitral annulus and leaflet areas, and tethering of the atriogenic posterior mitral leaflet leading to regurgitation.17–20 Atrial FMR is common in AF patients, and has been reported in 8.1–29% of patients.21,22 In addition, in AF patients with LVEF >50%, the incidence of at least moderate atrial FMR increases from 3% in those with an AF duration of <1 year to 28% in those with an AF duration of >10 years.21

Atrial FMR and Catheter Ablation for AF

Gertz et al.17 found that atrial FMR improved with sinus rhythm maintenance after catheter ablation, suggesting AF as the primary cause and supporting catheter ablation as a plausible approach. Eliminating AF in FMR may lead to reverse remodeling of the LA, improving MR and potentially reducing recurrence.5 Another study indicated that the risk of AF recurrence is 2.47-fold higher in OMR than in FMR caused by LA/LV dilation.6 Those findings suggest that, unlike in the OMR group (where the underlying cause remained), AF was the primary cause in the FMR group, and treating the arrhythmia improved chamber enlargement, resulting in a lower recurrence rate.

One study indicated that atrial FMR is associated with high recurrence rates and the expansion of low-voltage zones (LVZs).7 However, other studies have not found any such association.8,10 Thus, previous studies on atrial FMR have reported inconsistent results regarding its role as a predictor of AF recurrence after ablation.7–10 The discrepancies in previous studies are thought to arise from differences in the extent of LA remodeling. Therefore, based on these findings, it is suggested that the ability to achieve reverse remodeling by eliminating AF through catheter ablation may influence long-term recurrence rates in patients with atrial FMR.

The present study suggests that in patients with atrial FMR, an LAd reduction alone is insufficient to prevent recurrence, and that a simultaneous improvement in the MR is required. Persistent MR after ablation likely increases AF recurrence by maintaining the burden on the LA, similar to OMR.

In patients with atrial FMR, it has been reported that 64% of patients show an improvement in MR when sinus rhythm is maintained after ablation.23 However, patients with an LVZ exhibit a significantly lesser reduction in LA volume and MR improvement.23 In addition, in AF patients with atrial FMR, the presence of an LVZ in the inferior part of the LA has been associated with a lower likelihood of an improvement in atrial FMR after ablation.24

In the present study, the distribution of LVZs in the LA was not assessed. Although both groups (i.e., those with and without recurrence) showed reductions in LAd, differences in MR improvement were observed. In our cohort of 56 patients, both LAd reduction and MR improvement were achieved in 20 patients, whereas neither was achieved in another 20 patients, of whom 3 had already experienced AF recurrence at the time of follow-up TTE. Six patients exhibited an MR improvement despite not achieving a significant (>5%) reduction in LAd; however, most of these 6 patients (n=4) still exhibited a slight reduction (<5%) in LAd. Conversely, 10 patients had an LAd reduction but did not achieve an improvement in MR. Although a reduction in LAd can provide a broad assessment of the LA reverse remodeling, it remains a limited, 1-dimensional evaluation of LA structure. It is possible that, despite a reduction in LAd, the distribution of the LVZ hindered MR improvement, contributing to AF recurrence. The frequency of an LVZ in the bottom of the LA has been reported to be approximately 14.9%.25 However, as AF persists, LA remodeling progresses, potentially increasing the extent of the LVZ. This highlights the importance of early sinus rhythm restoration. In AF patients with LA enlargement and atrial FMR, early intervention with catheter ablation may improve outcomes following AF ablation.

Association Between Structural Improvement and AF Recurrence

Due to the retrospective and observational nature of this study, it was challenging to determine the direction of causality, specifically whether structural improvement contributed to the prevention of AF recurrence or whether the absence of recurrence allowed structural improvement to occur. Figure 3 illustrates the association between structural improvement and AF recurrence.

To explore whether maintaining sinus rhythm itself contributed to structural improvement, we compared patients who had already experienced AF recurrence before the follow-up TTE (n=10) with those who remained recurrence-free until the follow-up TTE (n=46). Our findings showed a trend for structural improvement to occur more frequently in the group without AF recurrence, but the difference between groups did not reach statistical significance. It has been reported that the maintenance of sinus rhythm can promote reverse remodeling in AF patients.16 The lack of significance in the present study may reflect the small sample size in our subanalysis or it may suggest that, in AF patients with significant atrial FMR, structural improvement is less likely to occur despite the maintenance of sinus rhythm.

To examine whether structural improvement itself reduces AF recurrence, we analyzed the 46 patients who had not yet experienced AF recurrence at the time of follow-up TTE. We excluded patients who had already recurred before the follow-up TTE because including them would compromise the accuracy of assessing this causal relationship. Our findings demonstrated fewer recurrences in the group that achieved structural improvement. Based on this result, we consider that structural improvement may be a useful predictor of freedom from AF recurrence in AF patients with atrial FMR.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, AF recurrence was assessed using intermittent ECG, Holter monitoring, and symptom-based ECGs, which may have missed asymptomatic episodes. Second, the ablation strategy and duration of antiarrhythmic drug use were left to the discretion of individual physicians, resulting in variability that limited the evaluation of their effects on AF recurrence and reverse remodeling. Third, LA size was assessed using LAd, a 1-dimensional measure that does not fully capture structural changes. Parameters such as the LA volume index and LA strain would have provided a more comprehensive assessment, but were not available for all patients due to the retrospective design of the study. Fourth, MR severity was assessed using the MR/LA area ratio, because quantitative parameters like the proximal isovelocity surface area and vena contracta were not consistently available. Fifth, follow-up TTE was performed approximately 3 months after ablation. Although a previous study reported that a reduction in LAd can already be observed at 3 months after ablation in patients with persistent AF,16 a longer follow-up period may be more appropriate for evaluating structural improvement. Sixth, only a small proportion of patients were classified as having severe MR by TTE and TEE (3.6% and 14.3%, respectively), with the majority presenting with moderate MR. Therefore, our findings primarily represented outcomes in patients with moderate atrial FMR. Finally, this was a retrospective single-center study with a small sample size, which may have introduced confounding and limited statistical power.

Conclusions

Among patients with moderate to severe atrial FMR, both a reduction in LAd and an improvement in MR after catheter ablation for AF were crucial for maintaining sinus rhythm, rather than either factor alone. Future prospective studies across multiple centers are warranted.
