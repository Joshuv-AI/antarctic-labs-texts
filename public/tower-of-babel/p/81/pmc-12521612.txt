Platelet‐Macrophage Aggregates in Remodeled Vessels of Patients With Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension
Authors: Christine L Farrell, Mariah Jordan, Janelle N Posey, Kimberly R Jordan, Aneta Gandjeva, Eva S Nozik, Kurt R Stenmark, Rubin M Tuder, Brian B Graham, Cassidy A Delaney
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUsing lung immunohistochemistry and stereology, platelets were found to accumulate and co‐localize with leukocytes, particularly monocytes, within the mural and adventitial space of remodeled vessels of patients with pulmonary arterial hypertension. The presumed signaling between these cell types invites further studies into the role of platelet‐monocyte aggregates in pulmonary hypertension.Keywords: pulmonary hypertension, thromboinflammation, vascular inflammation

Introduction

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is characterized by intimal and medial wall thickening, intraluminal thrombosis, and the disorganized growth of primitive endothelial cells into plexiform lesions [1]. Perivascular inflammation precedes this remodeling in preclinical models, and the degree of inflammatory cell infiltration, often organized as tertiary lymph node follicles, positively correlates with intimal and medial wall thickness [1, 2, 3]. Platelets accumulate in the distal circulation and arterioles of mice and rats with experimental PH, and platelet depletion prevents early hypoxia‐induced perivascular inflammation and PH [4, 5].

Platelets are activated in the systemic circulation of patients with PAH and chronic thromboembolic PH, resulting in an increase in circulating platelet‐leukocyte aggregates, which correlate with a systemic increase in pro‐inflammatory cytokines [6, 7, 8]. Intraluminal thrombosis is commonly observed in patients with end‐stage PAH [1]. Megakaryocytes accumulate in the lungs of patients with decompensated PAH, associated with systemic thrombocytopenia [9, 10]. We recently reported that patients with idiopathic PAH have a significant increase in lung platelets4. However, whether platelets are located within remodeled pulmonary vessels of patients with late‐stage PAH and co‐localize with leukocytes/macrophages has not been addressed.

Platelets drive vascular inflammation directly via their binding and recruitment of leukocytes to sites of endovascular injury and indirectly via the release of pro‐inflammatory mediators from their granules [11]. Platelets may serve as the link between endothelial thrombosis and perivascular inflammation in PAH. We hypothesized that platelets accumulate and co‐localize with leukocytes within the remodeled pulmonary vessels of patients with PAH. To study this, we performed lung staining and stereology for platelets, leukocytes, and macrophages on human lung tissue of patients with severe PAH as compared to unused lung transplant donor controls.

Methods

Deidentified human lung tissue and clinical data were obtained from five patients with PAH and six unused lung donor controls enrolled in the Pulmonary Hypertension Breakthrough Initiative (PHBI) from April 2006 to August 2011. The study was approved by the Colorado Multiple Institution Review Board (COMIRB‐19‐1075). The PHBI implemented a standardized tissue‐processing protocol previously detailed [1]. Collaborating with the Human Immune Monitoring Shared Resource (HIMSR) at the University of Colorado School of Medicine, slides were deparaffinized, heat treated in antigen retrieval buffer, blocked, and stained with DAPI and fluorescent antibodies against human smooth muscle actin (SMA), CD41, CD45, CD68, and CD31 (Supplemental Table 1). To prevent further deposition of fluorescent dyes in subsequent staining steps, the slides were stripped between each stain with heat treatment in antigen retrieval buffer. Images were captured using a 20× objective (0.5‐micron resolution) on an Akoya Phenolmager HT instrument. Images were analyzed with inForm software version 2.5.1 to separate adjacent fluorochromes, subtract autofluorescence, segment tissue and glass regions, segment cellular compartments, and phenotype infiltrating cells according to cell marker expression. Trained phenotyping algorithms developed in inForm for each marker were applied across the entire image set, areas of interest containing pulmonary blood vessels >50 µM in size and surrounding adventitia were manually selected by independent review from two research personnel with discrepancies decided by a third reviewer, and data was compiled and summarized using Phenoptr Reports software. An average of 47+/−20 vessels were analyzed for control slides and 47+/−12 for PAH slides. Area point counting was performed with STEPanizer [12] within selected regions of interest to select blood vessels manually. The fluorescent density (measured in cells/mm2) of each stain was measured within regions of interest and multiplied by the ratio of the vessel area to the area of the region of interest. Areas were considered double positive if fluorochromes were colocalized within the same cell boundary. The data was analyzed with Graphpad PRISM to perform unpaired t‐tests with Welch correction with significance defined as p < 0.05.

Results

The five PAH patients had significant precapillary PH with an average mean pulmonary artery pressure at the time of diagnosis of 66 mmHg and pulmonary vascular resistance of 13 Wood′s units (Table 1). All were on combination therapy with an intravenous prostacyclin and a phosphodiesterase (PDE)−5 inhibitor or endothelin receptor antagonist; two were on triple therapy. All except one patient were thrombocytopenic with an average platelet count of 117 × 106/L. Three patients were on warfarin, and one was on daily aspirin 81 mg. Four of the five patients were female and all were white. Three patients had IPAH, one had congenital heart disease‐associated PAH, and one had methamphetamine associated PAH. The pulmonary blood vessels of PAH patients were notable for increased arterial intimal and medial thickening with numerous plexiform lesions, with controls having morphologically normal pulmonary arteries, as previously described (Figure 1A, Supplemental Figure 1). CD41+, CD45+, and CD68+ cells were approximately 3, 10, and 20 µM in size, respectively.

Platelets were localized within the vessel wall and adventitial space of many of the morphologically abnormal pulmonary arteries in patients with PAH (Figure 1A). Platelet density, defined by CD41+ cells, within the remodeled vessels was found to be significantly increased in PAH patients compared to controls (79.35 vs. 21.85 cells/mm2, p < 0.001) (Figure 1B). These platelets were found to be colocalized with leukocytes (CD41+CD45+ cells; 3.536 vs. 0.71 cells/mm2, p < 0.001) and macrophages (CD41+CD68+ cells; 5.579 vs. 1.559 cells/mm2, p = 0.004) within the remodeled vessels of patients with PAH (Figure 1C,D). CD41 density was evenly distributed across all blood vessel sizes (R 2 = 0.0004, P=ns; data not shown).

Discussion

Platelets and leukocytes, particularly macrophages, are increased in remodeled vessels of patients with PAH and are each implicated in the development of pulmonary vascular remodeling. This study qualitatively and quantitatively shows that platelets in patients with PAH are not confined to intraluminal microthrombi nor platelet aggregates within capillaries. Their increased presence in the mural and adventitial space of these patients' remodeled distal muscular pulmonary arteries suggests that platelets participate in other pathways in PAH in addition to thrombosis and mechanically disrupting blood flow through the pulmonary vessels. As platelets and megakaryocytes are the only known cells to express CD41 outside of the bone marrow and do not express CD68, the enrichment of CD41+CD68+ cells within remodeled PAH vessels demonstrates that platelets are often colocalized and likely bound to macrophages as platelet‐macrophage aggregates. Systemic platelet RNA signatures in patients with precapillary pulmonary hypertension show enrichment of RNAs associated with immune cell regulation [13]. The presumed signaling between these primed platelets and colocalized inflammatory cells invites further studies into the mechanisms between inflammatory cell recruitment and activation in these vessels through the bridge of platelets, such as single‐cell spatial transcriptomic analysis.

This study has several limitations. The sample size is small and heterogenous limiting analysis regarding differences in platelet and macrophage locations when subdivided into sex, age, or PAH etiology. The patients studied had end‐stage PAH with larger caliber vessels analyzed, which precludes conclusions as to whether the interactions between these cell types contribute to the pathogenesis of early pulmonary vascular remodeling or occur in smaller vessels. Finally, all the PAH patients were on intravenous prostacyclin therapy, which is known to suppress platelet counts and function and is a potential confounder to our results. However, these results provide increasing support for the potential role of platelets in mediating thrombosis and inflammation underlying the vascular remodeling in this deadly disease.
