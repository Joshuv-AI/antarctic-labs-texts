Adherence to evidence-based intrapartum care practices and influencing factors among obstetric care providers in Ethiopia: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Gemeda Wakgari Kitil, Abiy Tasew Dubale
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally standardized evidence-based intrapartum care practices are essential for enhancing health outcomes for mothers and newborns. Despite recent advancements, Ethiopia grapples with significant challenges in maternal healthcare, with the implementation of evidence-based intrapartum practices falling below recognized standards. To address this gap, our systematic review and meta-analysis investigate existing literature to unveil the current state of evidence-based intrapartum care practices and identify contributing factors in Ethiopia.MethodsThis study focused on Ethiopian research and adhered to the PRISMA checklist. Thirteen relevant studies were identified through databases such as Google Scholar, Web of Science, PubMed, and Scopus. Using STATA version 14, we systematically extracted data employing a checklist. Heterogeneity was evaluated using I2 tests and the Cochrane Q test statistic. To assess publication bias, a funnel plot, Begg's test, and Egger's weighted regression were employed.ResultsIn this systematic review and meta-analysis, 1770 research articles were identified, with thirteen studies meeting eligibility criteria for analysis. The pooled prevalence of evidence-based intrapartum care practices was 49.50 % (95 % CI: 39.02–59.98). Factors significantly associated with these practices included good knowledge (OR = 2.64, 95 % CI: 2.04–3.41), in-service training related to intrapartum practice (OR = 2.38, 95 % CI: 1.55–3.64), positive attitudes towards intrapartum care practice (OR = 2.68, 95 % CI: 1.78–4.03), a higher number of obstetric care providers per shift (OR = 2.54, 95 % CI: 1.27–5.10), and educational attainment at the level of Master's degree and above (OR = 4.92, 95 % CI: 2.45–9.91).ConclusionThis study reveals that despite recognizing the importance of evidence-based intrapartum practices, only half of the observed obstetric care providers apply them. Factors influencing their application include knowledge, in-service training, attitudes, staffing, and education. Recommendations include continuous knowledge enhancement, reinforced in-service training, and psychological support. Optimizing staffing and creating pathways for advanced degrees are also proposed to improve evidence-based intrapartum care practices.

Introduction

Background

Evidence-based practice (EBP) is a systematic approach that utilizes the latest, valid, and relevant information to enhance health outcomes. It integrates clinical expertise, research evidence, and patient values to create a comprehensive strategy for healthcare decision-making [1]. Intrapartum care encompasses the support and medical attention given during the four phases of labor: the first, second, third, and fourth stages. This care extends typically from the onset of labor through the delivery of the placenta, a period that commonly lasts one to two hours [2]. Evidence-based intrapartum practices (EBPs) were measured through continuous labor support, delayed cord clamping, non-pharmacological pain management, active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL), limiting vaginal examinations, encouraging mobility during labor, oral hydration and nutrition, and immediate postnatal assessment [3]. The utilization of evidence-based care principles is crucial in ensuring optimal care for women during the intrapartum period. Evidence-based care (EBC) plays a fundamental role in maternal and newborn healthcare, serving as an effective method to improve the quality of intrapartum care and mitigate the risks of maternal and neonatal mortality and morbidity [3].

The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes that incorporating evidence-based intrapartum care practices is vital for providing high-quality healthcare and represents a highly effective strategy for improving the standards of midwifery services [4], [5]. While the World Health Organization (WHO) stresses the importance of adopting evidence-based clinical treatments and discarding harmful or ineffective practices, the reality in many developing countries diverges. In these regions, routine care during labor and delivery often involves the use of ineffective or harmful therapies, neglecting beneficial procedures for a significant number of laboring mothers [6], [7]. On the other hand, evidence-based practices are indispensable in therapy as they not only save costs but also enhance patient and family satisfaction, advance professional development, and improve the quality of care [8].

Based on survey findings, a staggering 810 women lose their lives every day due to complications during pregnancy and childbirth. This alarming statistic primarily affects low- and middle-income countries [9]. In addition, there are 2.6 million neonatal deaths and 2.1 million stillbirths globally each year. Nearly half of these tragic events are associated with challenges during childbirth in settings with limited resources. This data underscores the urgent need for targeted interventions and improved maternal and neonatal healthcare in vulnerable regions [10].

Worldwide, compliance with evidence-based intrapartum care practices stood at just 74 % in developing nations. In Ethiopia, a study indicated that the percentage of obstetric care providers implementing evidence-based intrapartum practices ranged from 27.3 % to 83.7 % [11], [12]. Even with the increase in facility-based deliveries, Sub-Saharan Africa continues to face a shortfall in sufficient intrapartum care, and advancements in perinatal health outcomes have been limited [13], [14]. Despite attempts, Ethiopia, like other African countries, does not possess a detailed report outlining the specifics of evidence-based practice. The Federal Ministry of Health in Ethiopia grapples with a shortage of proficient healthcare experts who can contribute to evidence synthesis for policymaking. Additionally, there exists a minimal culture or tradition of having confidence in and applying evidence throughout all levels of the health system [15].

Factors significantly associated with evidence-based intrapartum care practices included good knowledge, in-service training related to intrapartum practice, years of experience, access to computers, a positive attitude towards intrapartum care practice, profession, availability of updated intrapartum care guidelines, the number of obstetric care providers per shift, gender, and educational level [12], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21].

Numerous studies on evidence-based intrapartum care practices in Ethiopia exist, but a comprehensive national estimate and associated factors among obstetrics care providers are lacking. Previous research yields varied, inconclusive, and regionally limited findings. Our systematic review and meta-analysis aim to address this gap by providing a nationally representative overview of evidence-based intrapartum care practices and their correlates in Ethiopia. This study specifically examines prevalence rates, risk factors, and demographic patterns associated with evidence-based intrapartum care practices to shed light on the extent of the problem. The findings contribute valuable insights to the global discourse on intrapartum care practices and have immediate relevance for Ethiopia. The research seeks to inform tailored interventions, educational strategies, and policy recommendations to enhance evidence-based intrapartum care practices at the local level, facilitating improvements in maternal and neonatal health outcomes in the Ethiopian healthcare context.

Methods and materials

Source of information and search strategy

In our efforts to uncover pertinent research, we conducted thorough searches in both the PROSPERO database and the Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effects (DARE), which are accessible at http://www.library.UCSF.edu. Following the guidelines outlined by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) [22], our research endeavors to shed light on factors that are intricately linked to the adoption of evidence-based intrapartum care practices among obstetric care providers (S1 Table). This systematic approach is crucial in gaining a comprehensive understanding of the landscape surrounding intrapartum care, contributing to the advancement of knowledge in this critical domain of healthcare.

We conducted a systematic search across various online databases, Google Scholar, Web of Science, PubMed, and Scopus, spanning from March 10, 2025, to June 10, 2025, to conduct a comprehensive literature review. Our search approach included the integration of keywords, free-text search queries, and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH). Employing Boolean operators, we combined alternative terms, namely: "prevalence," "associated factors," "determinants," "evidence-based intrapartum care practice," "utilization of intrapartum care," "Midwives," and "Obstetrics care providers." The search criteria were articulated as follows: ("prevalence," "associated factors," "determinants," "evidence-based intrapartum care practice," "utilization of intrapartum care," "Midwives," and "Obstetrics care providers") AND ("evidence-based intrapartum care practice" OR "utilization of evidence-based intrapartum care" OR "evidence-based intrapartum care practice obstetrics care providers"). This methodical approach ensured a thorough and organized exploration of the literature during the specified timeframe (S2 Table).

The primary focus of this study was to conduct a comprehensive investigation into evidence-based intrapartum care practices and their associations with healthcare providers in Ethiopia through epidemiological research. The research methodology involved a meticulous screening process, encompassing a thorough review of titles, abstracts, and complete texts of selected original studies.

Eligibility criteria

In this systematic review, our inclusion criteria targeted original research studies specifically addressing evidence-based intrapartum care practices and associated variables within the context of Ethiopian obstetrics care providers. All observational studies, regardless of whether they were published or unpublished, were considered eligible for inclusion. The inclusion criteria did not impose any restrictions based on the publication year, ensuring a comprehensive and up-to-date synthesis of the available literature.

The review encompassed publications up to June 10, 2025, providing a current snapshot of the research landscape. To maintain the precision and relevance of the study, a stringent exclusion criterion was applied: studies that did not disclose information on evidence-based intrapartum care practices and related variables among Ethiopian obstetrics care providers were excluded from the analysis. Additionally, to facilitate a thorough examination of the selected studies, articles lacking abstracts or full texts were not included in the systematic review. This refined and meticulous approach aimed to ensure the quality and relevance of the literature synthesized in the study (S3 Table and S4 Table).

The primary focus of this systematic review was on cross-sectional and analytical cross-sectional research related to evidence-based intrapartum care practices in Ethiopia. We included full-text English papers that were readily available and published in peer-reviewed journals. Conversely, non-cross-sectional research, such as case reports, conference reports, national survey reports, and expert comments, as well as studies conducted in languages other than English, were excluded. Additionally, editorial reports, reviews, letters, or commentary were not considered in this thorough evaluation.

The study's population comprised obstetric care providers, and the contextual scope was limited to Ethiopian studies. The specific condition under examination was evidence-based intrapartum care practices.

Data extraction

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) quality assessment checklist, designed for cross-sectional studies, was employed to evaluate the study's quality [23]. Independently, a team of two skilled reviewers, GWK and ATD, carried out the data extraction process, scrutinizing each article using a predefined checklist in Microsoft Excel to gather pertinent information. Any discrepancies that emerged during this process were collaboratively addressed through rigorous discussions, ensuring the reliability of the study's findings. To streamline the procedure, the initial step involved consolidating search results from multiple databases and using reference management software (EndNote version 20.0) to identify and eliminate duplicate articles. This was followed by a comprehensive review of study titles and abstracts, leading to the exclusion of non-relevant entries. A thorough evaluation of full-text publications was then conducted for the remaining articles.

Data synthesis and analysis

A Microsoft Excel spreadsheet was utilized to extract data for the synthesis and analysis process. Subsequently, STATA version 14 was employed to input the exported data for further examination. Primary studies were thoroughly described and summarized using tables, figures, and forest plots. To provide an overall estimate of evidence-based intrapartum care practice, a random-effects model with a 95 % confidence interval (CI) was employed.

Furthermore, in assessing the degree of association for factors related to obstetric care providers, an odds ratio with a 95 % confidence interval was utilized. The identification of observed heterogeneity among the included studies prompted the utilization of a random-effects model during the meta-analysis. This approach ensures a comprehensive analysis and interpretation of the synthesized data.

To assess the degree of heterogeneity in the reported prevalence across studies, Cochran's Q and I2 statistics were employed. A p-value of less than 0.05 in Cochran's Q statistical heterogeneity test was considered statistically significant. The I2 statistic, ranging from 0 % to 100 %, denotes no (0 %), low (25 %), moderate (50 %), and high (75 %) degrees of heterogeneity, respectively. To investigate the potential existence of publication bias, Egger regression tests and a visual examination of funnel plot asymmetry were utilized.

Outcome measurement

For the first outcome, which focused on the prevalence of evidence-based intrapartum care practices, the data extraction checklist included details such as the authors' names, the year of publication, the study location (region), the study design, the sample size, the response rate, and the number of obstetrics care providers.

Data for the second outcome, which examined factors associated with evidence-based intrapartum care practice, were collected in two-by-two table forms. The log odds ratio (OR) was then computed based on the results from the original articles. To resolve disagreements between the two reviewers, GWK and ATD, additional feedback was provided. This resolution stemmed from a consensus-building discussion. In situations where the main articles lacked sufficient information, corresponding authors of the research articles were contacted by email.

Quality assessment and appraisal

To evaluate study quality, we employed a standardized tool to categorize the potential for bias and understand variations in the included research findings. A quality control check was conducted by two independent reviewers. Methodological issues were appraised using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), designed to gauge the likelihood of bias in observational research. Publications deemed relevant were those with a modified NOS score of seven or higher (S5 Table).

Patient and public involvement

No patients participated in designing the study, selecting participants, conducting data analysis, interpreting findings, or implementing the research during its development. Additionally, patients will not receive information about the study's results, and the general public didn’t actively contribute to its design.

Further refinement involved eliminating an additional 190 articles based on a thorough assessment of their abstracts and titles. Subsequently, we conducted a comprehensive full-text review of 20 articles. Prevalence of evidence-based intrapartum care practice

The overall pooled prevalence of evidence-based intrapartum care practices was 49.50 % (95 % CI: 39.02–59.98). Among the included studies, the Western Oromia study [12] highlighted the highest prevalence at 83.7 % (95 % CI: 62.80–104.60), while the Amhara region Achefer District study [11] recorded the lowest prevalence at 27.3 % (95 % CI: 2.09–52.51). With an I2 of 50.5 % and a p-value of 0.019, the analysis revealed substantial heterogeneity among the included studies. This notable variability indicated the necessity for a subgroup analysis to enhance our comprehension and interpretation of the differences observed among the primary studies (Fig. 2).Fig. 2A forest plot showing Ethiopia's pooled prevalence of evidence-based intrapartum practice.Fig. 2

We employed a visual analysis of a funnel plot along with Egger's regression test to evaluate the presence of publication bias. Factors associated with evidence-based intrapartum care practice

To assess the factors collectively influencing evidence-based intrapartum care practices, we conducted a meta-analysis of thirteen studies. Utilizing the command “metan logor selogor, xlab(0.1, 1, 10) label(namevar = authors) by (factors) random texts(180) eform,” we evaluated the cumulative effects of the odds ratio.

In exploring the determinants of evidence-based intrapartum care practices, several key factors emerged from our analysis. One standout finding is the robust association between good knowledge and evidence-based intrapartum care. Respondents with a solid understanding of intrapartum care demonstrated a threefold increase in the likelihood of practicing evidence-based intrapartum care (OR = 2.64, 95 % CI: 2.04–3.41).

Similarly, the significance of continuous learning is evident in our results, as demonstrated by the positive association between in-service training related to intrapartum practice and evidence-based care. Respondents who underwent in-service training in intrapartum practice were two times more likely to exhibit good evidence-based intrapartum care practices compared to their counterparts (OR = 2.38, 95 % CI: 1.55–3.64).

In addition to knowledge and training, having a positive attitude toward intrapartum care is a key factor influencing practice. Obstetric care providers with a positive attitude are almost three times more likely to implement evidence-based intrapartum care practices, with the odds ratio being (OR = 2.68, 95 % CI: 1.78–4.03).

Furthermore, our analysis emphasizes the influence of staffing levels on evidence-based intrapartum care practices. A higher number of obstetric care providers per shift was associated with a greater likelihood of adherence to recommended guidelines. The likelihood of delivering evidence-based intrapartum care practices is 2.54 times higher when there are five or more care providers per shift compared to situations with fewer than five care providers (OR = 2.54, 95 % CI: 1.27–5.10).

Finally, the study highlighted that educational attainment is a significant predictor of practicing evidence-based intrapartum care. Specifically, individuals with a Master's degree or higher were found to be five times more likely to adhere to evidence-based intrapartum care practices compared to those with a BSc/diploma (OR = 4.92, 95 % CI: 2.45–9.91) (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Forest plot of associated factors of evidence-based intrapartum care practice in Ethiopia.Fig. 7

Discussion

This study conducted a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis to assess the prevalence of evidence-based intrapartum care practices among obstetric care providers in Ethiopia. The aim was to evaluate the updated prevalence of these practices and identify potential contributing factors based on the available data. The pooled prevalence of evidence-based intrapartum care practices in Ethiopia was found to be 49.50 % (95 % CI: 39.02–59.98).

The findings of this study are consistent with those from previous research. For instance, a review of seven studies reported a prevalence of 54.45 %, and a study conducted in Uganda found a similar prevalence of 49.4 % [9]. These results suggest that while evidence-based intrapartum care practices are being implemented in Ethiopia, the prevalence is comparable to other low-resource settings, indicating a need for further improvements in the adoption of these practices.

However, the evidence-based intrapartum care practices reported in this study are lower than those found in similar studies conducted in various locations. For instance, a multicenter study across 14 hospitals in Rwanda and Jamaica reported higher percentages, ranging from 65 % in Rwanda to 81.1 % in Jamaica [29]. Similarly, a multicenter study in tertiary hospitals across the Arab region found rates ranging from 60 % in Egypt to 82 % in Lebanon [30]. The lower rates of evidence-based intrapartum care in this study may be due to differences in healthcare systems, infrastructure, and resource availability. Variations in training, cultural and socioeconomic factors, and the strength of government policies and maternal health programs also play a role in influencing the implementation of evidence-based care.

Contrary to a previous study in Sweden, which reported a prevalence of 22.7 %, our systematic review and meta-analysis found a higher prevalence of evidence-based intrapartum care practices [31]. The variation in findings might be explained by a shift in approach, transitioning from standard obstetrics to interventions focused on technology. Furthermore, the discrepancy could be influenced by the smaller sample size in the prior study and differences in the number of hospitals.

Our analysis revealed several key determinants of evidence-based intrapartum care practices, with one standout finding being the strong association between good knowledge and the likelihood of practicing evidence-based care. Respondents with a solid understanding of intrapartum care were three times more likely to apply evidence-based practices. This finding aligns with a study conducted in Kenya [32], involving obstetric care providers. These studies indicate that obstetric care providers, when equipped with sufficient knowledge of evidence-based practices, are more inclined to apply this knowledge. This underscores the significance of acquiring adequate knowledge for the effective implementation of evidence-based intrapartum practices.

Similarly, the significance of continuous learning is evident in our results, as demonstrated by the positive association between in-service training related to intrapartum practice and evidence-based care. Respondents who underwent in-service training in intrapartum practice were two times more likely to exhibit good evidence-based intrapartum care practices compared to their counterparts. This finding aligns with a study conducted in Kenya, where similar trends were observed [32].

In addition to knowledge and training, a positive attitude toward intrapartum care is a key factor influencing practice. Obstetric care providers with a positive attitude are almost three times more likely to implement evidence-based intrapartum care practices, a result that aligns with research conducted in the Eastern United States of America [33]. One explanation for this discrepancy could be that individuals with a positive outlook are more inclined to apply their knowledge.

Furthermore, our analysis emphasizes the influence of staffing levels on evidence-based intrapartum care practices. A higher number of obstetric care providers per shift was associated with a greater likelihood of adherence to recommended guidelines. The likelihood of delivering evidence-based intrapartum care practices is 2.5 times higher when there are five or more care providers per shift compared to situations with fewer than five care providers. This result is in line with research carried out in both Nigeria [34] and Cameroon [35]. A plausible justification could be that adequate staffing is crucial for promoting patient-centered care, optimizing workload, enhancing care quality, and ensuring patient safety. All of these factors collectively contribute to improved health outcomes for mothers and newborns.

Finally, the study highlighted that educational attainment is a significant predictor of practicing evidence-based intrapartum care. Specifically, individuals with a Master's degree or higher were found to be five times more likely to adhere to evidence-based intrapartum care practices compared to those with a BSc/diploma. This difference may be attributed to the advanced and rigorous education, specialized training, exposure to research methodologies, leadership roles, and commitment to continuous professional development associated with higher academic qualifications. However, this finding contradicts a study conducted in London, which revealed that individuals with a BSc/diploma were more likely to adhere to evidence-based intrapartum care practices than those with a Master's degree or higher [36].

Strengths and limitations of the study

This systematic review and meta-analysis offer several strengths. It involved a comprehensive and systematic search strategy across multiple databases, ensuring the inclusion of relevant studies. The analysis adhered to the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework, allowing for a transparent assessment of the quality of evidence. Additionally, robust statistical techniques, including subgroup and sensitivity analyses, were used to enhance the reliability of the findings and explore variations between studies.

Despite these strengths, there are notable limitations. Significant heterogeneity in the study populations and methodologies across the included studies may affect the generalizability of the results. Some studies were subject to bias, and the lack of standardized definitions for key variables limited the consistency of the findings. Furthermore, the exclusion of non-English studies potentially reduced the comprehensiveness of the review. Lastly, the study's focus on obstetric care providers in Ethiopia limits the transferability of the findings to other regions or healthcare settings.

Overall, while the review provides valuable insights into evidence-based intrapartum care practices in Ethiopia, further research is needed to address these limitations and strengthen the evidence base.

Contribution to the scientific community

This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of evidence-based intrapartum care practices and identifies key determinants that influence their implementation. A major finding of this review is the critical role of healthcare providers' knowledge in the adoption of evidence-based practices. Our analysis underscores that obstetric care providers with a solid understanding of evidence-based guidelines are more likely to apply these practices, highlighting the importance of education and continuous professional development.

Implication

The implications of this review are significant, suggesting that improving knowledge and training can lead to better adherence to evidence-based care, ultimately improving maternal and neonatal outcomes. Moreover, healthcare policies and programs should focus on addressing knowledge gaps and overcoming barriers to the implementation of evidence-based care, contributing to the overall advancement of maternal healthcare globally.

Conclusion

The pooled prevalence of evidence-based intrapartum care practice was found to be 49.5 %. Factors significantly associated with adherence to these practices included good knowledge, receipt of in-service training related to intrapartum practice, positive attitude towards intrapartum care practice, higher number of obstetric care providers per shift, and educational attainment at the level of Master's degree and above.

Recommendation

Based on our findings, we recommend that healthcare systems prioritize the development and implementation of continuous professional development programs for obstetric care providers, focusing on the latest advancements in intrapartum care to ensure practitioners remain updated on evidence-based practices. Additionally, strengthening in-service training initiatives and making them accessible to a wide range of healthcare professionals will help foster a culture of ongoing learning. Interventions should also be introduced to cultivate positive attitudes toward intrapartum care, acknowledging the psychological aspects of care provision to support the adoption of evidence-based practices. Moreover, optimizing staffing levels for obstetric care providers per shift is essential to ensure high-quality care without overburdening staff. Finally, establishing pathways and incentives for healthcare professionals to pursue advanced degrees, particularly at the Master’s level and beyond, should be prioritized, as higher educational attainment is strongly associated with the effective implementation of evidence-based intrapartum care practices.
