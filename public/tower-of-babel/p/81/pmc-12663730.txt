Molecular insights into ligand recognition and receptor activation of GPR15
Authors: Shutian Chen, Xuteng Han, Yuxia Zhang, Limin Ma, Cuiying Yi, Xiaojing Chu, Qiuxiang Tan, Shuo Han, Qiang Zhao, Beili Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn response to the chemokine-like peptide GPR15L, G protein-coupled receptor 15 (GPR15) is crucial for immune cell trafficking and inflammatory diseases. However, understanding of its physiology and pathology is hindered by lack of molecular details of the interaction between GPR15 and the full-length GPR15L. Here, we report the structure of GPR15 bound to the full-length GPR15L and Gi3 protein. Combined with mutagenesis data, this structure reveals key interactions that define ligand recognition and a subpocket that governs GPR15L selectivity and receptor activation. Molecular dynamics simulations suggest that sulfation modifications in the N-terminal region of GPR15 may play a role in stabilizing the binding between GPR15 and the core region of GPR15L. Furthermore, molecular docking of some potential small-molecule antagonists suggests a conserved molecular pattern of these ligands inhibiting receptor activation. These findings provide essential insight into functional modulation of GPR15 and would facilitate development of therapeutic strategies for treatment of inflammation and immune diseases.Subject areas: biochemistry, protein structure aspects, structural biology

Introduction

G protein-coupled receptor 15 (GPR15) plays a central role in immune surveillance by regulating immune cell trafficking, especially the migration of regulatory and effector T cells to mucosal surfaces such as the colon and epithelial tissues including skin.1 As a chemoattractant receptor, GPR15 also serves as a co-receptor for simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV) and human immunodeficiency virus type 1 and type 2 (HIV-1 and HIV-2) to mediate viral entry.2,3,4 Moreover, GPR15 is associated with chronic inflammatory diseases,5,6,7 vascular health,3 cytoprotection,8 angiogenesis,9 and the effects of chronic tobacco smoking.10,11 A recent pan-cancer analysis, which integrated data from multiple cancer databases, revealed a notable upregulation of GPR15 expression across various cancer types,12 suggesting that this receptor serves as a potential target for cancer immunotherapy and therapeutic intervention. The functions of GPR15 are primarily mediated by its endogenous ligand, GPR15L, which selectively binds to GPR15 to promote immune cell migration to inflamed epithelial surfaces,13,14,15 making this receptor-ligand pair a pivotal signaling axis for immune modulation at epithelial barriers.15,16

Recent advances in structural biology provide molecular insights into ligand binding and activation mechanisms of chemokine receptors, such as CCR5 and CXCR4.17,18,19,20 In these receptors, chemokines bind to the receptor transmembrane cores primarily through their N termini, whereas GPR15 activation is driven by the C terminus of GPR15L.13,21 This represents a key divergence in binding modes between GPR15 and canonical chemokine receptors. In contrast, sulfation modifications play a role in both systems: the N-terminal sulfation modifications in several chemokine receptors (e.g., CCR2, CCR5, CXCR4, and CX3CR1) facilitate chemokine recognition,22,23,24,25 and similarly, GPR15L binding is mediated by the sulfotyrosine residues in the N terminus of GPR15.16 These findings broaden the scope of comparative analyses involving GPR15 and classical chemokine receptors. However, the molecular details of how GPR15 recognizes and is activated by the full-length GPR15L remain elusive.

The cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure of GPR15 bound to the C-terminal undecapeptide of GPR15L (C11) was recently determined, which offers insights into the interaction between GPR15 and GPR15L.21 However, the full-length GPR15L exhibits a 40- to 100-fold higher potency than C11, underscoring the critical role of the ligand core region in receptor activation.3,14,26,27 This indicates a gap in our understanding of the molecular mechanism underlying the recognition of the full-length endogenous ligand for GPR15. Thus, we determined the cryo-EM structure of GPR15 in complex with the full-length GPR15L and trimeric Gi3 protein. Combined with mutagenesis, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, and molecular docking data, this structure provides a molecular framework for the GPR15-GPR15L signaling axis, which paves the way for the development of therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating GPR15 in mucosal inflammatory diseases and broader immune modulation.

Results

Overall structure of the GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 complex

To improve the expression of GPR15, its flexible C-terminal region (residues E331-L360) was truncated, which has little effect on the GPR15L-induced Gi activation of the receptor, as evidenced by a comparable agonist potency of GPR15L for the wild-type and truncated GPR15 measured by a bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) assay (Figure S1A; Table S1). The GPR15-Gi3 complex was assembled by co-expressing and co-purifying the receptor with a dominant-negative Gi3 protein in the presence of GPR15L, and further stabilized by incorporating a single-chain variable antibody fragment, scFv16.28 Cryo-EM single-particle analysis was employed to determine the structure of GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 complex with a global nominal resolution at 3.30 Å. The resolution of the receptor transmembrane helical bundle was further improved to 3.05 Å by focus refinement (Figures S1B–S1H and S2; Table S2).

GPR15 shares a canonical overall architecture with the class A G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) with known structures (Figures 1A and 1B). Due to conformational flexibility, the N-terminal regions of the receptor (residues M1-Y31) and ligand (residues K1-P43) were not resolved. Notably, the C-terminal tetradecapeptide of GPR15L (44-EPRLWVVPGALPQV-57) binds to the extracellular cavity within the transmembrane core of GPR15. Structural alignment of GPR15 with the inactive and active structures of the CC chemokine receptor 5 (CCR5) (PDB: 4MBS and 7F1Q), which shares a high sequence similarity with GPR15, reveals that the GPR15L-bound GPR15 adopts a fully active conformation (Figures 1C and 1D). As a hallmark of class A GPCR activation, the outward displacement of helix 6 (∼11 Å, measured at the Cα atom of the residue L6.30 [superscript number represents Ballesteros-Weinstein nomenclature29]), together with the outward movement of helix 5 (∼6 Å, measured at the Cα atom of the residue A5.67), results in the formation of an intracellular cavity necessary for G protein coupling.

Despite overall structural similarity of GPR15 to the chemokine receptors in their previously reported active structures,17,30,31,32,33 the GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 complex exhibits a unique Gi protein binding pose, similar to that observed in the C11-bound GPR15 structure (PDB: 8ZQE) (Figures S3A and S3B). Upon coupling to GPR15, the α5 helix of Gαi shifts toward the third intracellular loop (ICL3) of the receptor, resulting in a 30° tilt compared to its counterparts in the chemokine receptor-Gi complexes (Figure S3B). The distinct α5 helix orientations are associated with different interaction patterns between the receptor and Gi protein. In the GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 complex, the residue D341G.H5.13 in the Gαi3 subunit forms polar interactions with H2255.68 and N2336.27 in the receptor, which are absent in the chemokine receptor-Gi complexes (Figures S3C–S3H). Due to this difference, the α5 helix of Gi3 in the GPR15 complex packs more closely against helix 6, whereas in the chemokine receptor-Gi complexes, the α5 helix primarily interacts with helices 3 and 5 (Figures S3C–S3H). This movement of the α5 helix induces a conformational rearrangement in the cytoplasmic regions of helices 5 and 6 of GPR15, accompanied by an outward displacement of ICL3 (Figure S3I), in which the residue K231ICL3 forms additional polar interactions with the Gi3 residues Q304G.H4.12, E308G.H4.16, and T321G.S6.03 (Figure S3J). Our mutagenesis data show that alanine substitution of K231ICL3 decreased the agonistic activity of GPR15L in triggering the Gi activation of GPR15, supporting the importance of this residue in mediating the GPR15L-induced G protein signaling (Figure S3K; Table S1). The displacement of the α5 helix results in a corresponding shift of the rest of the Gi3 protein, leading to a 30° rotation of the αN helix compared to those in the chemokine receptor-Gi complexes (Figure S3B).

Binding mode of the endogenous ligand GPR15L

The GPR15L-bound GPR15 structure demonstrates an essential role of the C terminus of GPR15L in governing receptor-ligand recognition and agonistic activity of the endogenous ligand. Its C-terminal tetradecapeptide (44-EPRLWVVPGALPQV-57) penetrates deeply into the extracellular ligand binding cavity within the receptor helical bundle and forms extensive interactions with helices 2–7 and the second extracellular loop (ECL2) of GPR15 (Figures 2A–2D).

Consistent with the previously reported GPR15-C11 complex structure, the 11 residues (L47-V57) at the C terminus of GPR15L adopt a hook-like conformation and embed in the ligand binding pocket of the receptor, with the C-terminal end pointing toward a subpocket formed by helices 3–7 (Figures 2F, 2G, and S3M). This conserved binding mode underscores the pivotal role of the C-terminal tail in receptor activation. This ligand binding mode contrasts with that of chemokines, of which the N termini binds to the ligand binding pocket of their receptors (Figure S3N). Consistent with the C11-bound GPR15 structure, the receptor-ligand binding in the GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 complex is largely mediated through polar contacts between the main chains of the residues P55-V57 in GPR15L and the receptor residue K2616.55, as well as hydrophobic interactions formed by the V50-L54 segment of the ligand and the residues W892.60 and R1724.64 in GPR15 (Figures 2B and 2C). These interactions play a pivotal role in ligand recognition and receptor activation, as evidenced by previous data21 and our mutagenesis data, showing that alanine substitutions at these key positions substantially impaired the agonistic activity of GPR15L (Figures 2H–2J; Table S1).

The well-defined densities of the GPR15L C terminus in our cryo-EM map allowed us to identify additional interactions that are absent in the previous GPR15-C11 structure. The carboxyl group at the C-terminal end of the ligand engages in an ionic interaction with the basic residue K1925.35 in helix 5 of the receptor (Figure 2B). The importance of this polar interaction is supported by a substantial decrease of the potency of GPR15L in inducing Gi activation for the mutant K1925.35A (Figure 2J; Table S1). In addition, the residue L54 in GPR15L points toward the bottom of the ligand binding pocket, forming a hydrophobic core with F822.53, I1133.32, M1173.36, W2546.48, F2576.51, and F2927.43 in the receptor, where F822.53 represents an additional interaction unique to the GPR15L-bound structure while the others are conserved relative to the GPR15-C11 complex (Figure 2E). Alanine substitutions at these positions drastically impaired the GPR15L-induced Gi activation, suggesting that these key residues are essential for ligand recognition and/or receptor activation (Figure 2K; Table S1). In addition to the helical bundle, the ECL2 region of GPR15 also plays an essential role in ligand binding. The W48-P51 fragment of GPR15L engages in extensive hydrophobic interactions with the segment Y182-K187 in the receptor ECL2 (Figure 2D). The importance of ECL2 in the GPR15L-induced GPR15 activation was assessed by mutagenesis studies, showing that the mutation A184ECL2W almost abolished the Gi activation (Figure 2H; Table S1).

While the C terminus of GPR15L plays a critical role in inducing receptor activation, the functional significance of the ligand core is also evident, as demonstrated by the fact that the full-length GPR15L exhibits a higher potency than C11 in the G protein activation assay (Figure S3L; Table S1). However, the cryo-EM map lacks densities for the ligand residues prior to E44, implying conformational flexibility in the core region of GPR15. To further investigate the interaction pattern between GPR15 and GPR15L, we performed three independent 500-ns MD simulations of GPR15 bound to the full-length GPR15L (Figure 3A). The missing N-terminal regions of both the receptor and ligand were predicted using AlphaFold3.34 It has been reported that the post-translational sulfation modifications in the N-terminal regions of chemokine receptors are critical to ligand recognition by forming polar interactions with the core regions of their chemokine ligands.30,32,35,36 Similarly, sulfation modifications in GPR15 were also reported to mediate the recognition and binding of GPR15L.12,14,16,36 Of note, GPR15 possesses four tyrosine residues, Y9, Y12, Y13, and Y14, in its N-terminal region, which are potential sites for sulfation. To assess the potential impact of the receptor sulfation on GPR15L recognition, we performed the MD simulations on the GPR15-GPR15L model in the presence or absence of sulfation modifications in GPR15.

Compared to the non-sulfated model, the simulations using the sulfated GPR15 display a larger contact area between the receptor and ligand and a higher number of atomic pairs within 3 Å, suggesting a tighter binding (Figures 3B and S4A). The enhanced binding is attributed to the interactions between the N terminus of GPR15 and GPR15L (Figures 3C and 3D). The sulfated N terminus of the receptor packs over the core region of GPR15L, and may form ionic interactions with several basic residues of the ligand, such as K1, R2, R3, and R12 (Figures 3C and 3D). In contrast, the non-sulfated GPR15 exhibits a more unstructured N terminus with minimal interactions with GPR15L (Figure S4B). These observations suggest that sulfation at the N terminus of GPR15 may enhance the stability of ligand binding by facilitating electrostatic interactions, potentially serving as an anchor to properly position the ligand and enable its coupling to the GPR15L core.

To further explore the ligand recognition pattern, some N-terminal truncations and mutations of GPR15 were tested for their effects on the GPR15L-induced Gi activation in HEK293 cell, with consideration of post-transitional tyrosine sulfation modifications. Removing the residues prior to the first tyrosine (ΔN5 and ΔN8) slightly reduced the maximal level of Gi activation, whereas further truncations that remove the tyrosines, including ΔN10, ΔN11, ΔN12, ΔN13, and ΔN14, led to a substantially decreased level of Gi activation (Figure 3E; Table S1). Furthermore, replacing the four tyrosines with arginines (YR) abolished the GPR15L-induced Gi activation, while their glutamic acid mutations (YE) only displayed a limited effect (Figure 3F; Table S1). These data, together with the MD simulations, imply a possible role of the N-terminal tyrosines and their potential sulfation in GPR15L recognition. However, the specific contribution of each individual tyrosine and the precise sulfation pattern remain to be experimentally determined.

Endogenous ligand selectivity of GPR15

It has been acknowledged that a single chemokine can bind to multiple chemokine receptors and vice versa,37,38,39 highlighting the diverse and promiscuous nature of chemokine-receptor recognition. In contrast, the binding between GPR15 and GPR15L demonstrates remarkable specificity.3,13,36 Although both GPR15L and chemokines function in immune processes, GPR15L does not activate any chemokine receptors, nor does GPR15 respond to any chemokines. However, the structural mechanism underlying this specificity is not yet fully understood.

In the GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 structure, the last four residues (L54-V57) at the C terminus of GPR15L occupy a subpocket formed by helices 3–7, and form extensive polar and hydrophobic interactions with the receptor (Figures 2F and 2G). Compared to the inactive CCR5 structure, the interaction between the GPR15L residue L54 and the bottom region of the ligand binding pocket in GPR15 induces a downward shift of the side chain of the receptor residue F2576.51, which subsequently leads to a rotamer conformational change of W2546.48 that is known as the “toggle switch” of class A GPCRs40 (Figure S5A). This conformational change then triggers a cascade of structural rearrangements, including conformational changes of P5.50, V3.40, and F6.44 in the PVF motif, R3.50 in the DRY motif, and Y7.53 in the NPxxY motif40 (Figures S5B–S5D), and results in an outward movement of the intracellular portion of helix 6 to allow Gi protein coupling. It was reported that removing the C-terminal residue V57 in GPR15L eliminated its agonistic activity.16 This is consistent with the GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 structure, in which this residue forms interactions with helices 5 and 6 of the receptor (Figure 2B). Furthermore, removing this residue potentially disturbs the hydrophobic interaction network formed by the ligand residue L54 and the critical receptor residues such as F2576.51 and W2546.48, and thus, impairs the GPR15L-induced receptor activation. To further validate the critical role of the GPR15L C terminus in receptor activation, we measured the Gi activation of the wild-type GPR15 using the GPR15L variants with its C terminus extended by one or two alanine residues. The results revealed that the extensions reduced the agonistic potency of GPR15L (Figure S4C). This observation is further supported by our molecular docking analysis, showing higher Molecular Mechanics-Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) binding energy scores for the C-terminally extended variants and the GPR15L mutant V57A compared to that of the wild-type GPR15L (Figure S4D). Together, these data suggest that GPR15 activation requires strict sequence and length constraints of the ligand.

Our mutagenesis data support the importance of the interactions within the subpocket that accommodates the C-terminal segment L54-V57 of GPR15L in governing the receptor activation (Figures 2J and 2K; Table S1). Structural alignment of GPR15 and chemokine receptors reveals that the ligand binding mode within this subpocket is likely associated with the conformations of the extracellular regions of helices 5–7 (Figures 4A–4D). The inward positioning of these helices in the chemokine receptors would form a spatial hindrance for the C terminus of GPR15L, and thereby block its binding (Figures 4C and 4D). Moreover, this subpocket in GPR15 requires an extended C terminus of the ligand for effective binding and interaction (Figures 2A, 2F, 2G, and S3N). In contrast, the chemokines typically span only 5–8 residues within the orthosteric ligand binding pocket of their receptors,30,32 suggesting limited potential for them to engage with this subpocket in GPR15 (Figure S3N). To assess this notion, we carried out molecular docking and functional analyses of CCL3 and CCL5. The docking results suggested that neither CCL3 nor CCL5 could form stable interactions with GPR15, with unfavorable binding free energies (Figure S4E). Consistently, BRET-based Gi activation data showed that CCL5 activated CCR5 but failed to activate GPR15 (Figure S4F). Thus, the ligand interaction pattern that is distinct from those of the chemokine receptors likely accounts for the binding specificity between GPR15 and GPR15L. The subpocket at the bottom of the ligand binding site may serve as a promising drug binding site for developing new compounds with improved potency and selectivity.

Recognition of potential antagonists at GPR15

GPR15 has been identified as a key player in the progression of cancers, with its expression patterns and functional roles offering insights into cancer biology.3,36,41 Using a virtual screening approach, eight potential GPR15 antagonists were identified.12 Among them, compound 5 exhibits the lowest binding free energy and emerges as a candidate for GPR15 inhibitor, which provides a potential lead compound for further drug development.

To extend our knowledge of the binding mode of these non-peptide antagonists, the molecular docking of compound 5 was performed. Due to the absence of an inactive GPR15 structure, a modified model was derived from our GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 structure by removing the ligand and G protein, followed by a 500-ns MD simulation to relax and anneal the structure into an inactive-like state. The resulting model was validated by a comparison with the inactive and active CCR5 structures to ensure it adopts an inactive-like conformation, including the inward positioning of the intracellular tip of helix 6 and the upward rotamer conformations of the key residues F2576.51 and W2546.48 (Figure S6).

Using the inactive-like model of GPR15, the docking analyses reveal that compound 5 occupies the orthosteric ligand binding pocket of GPR15, forming extensive interactions with the bottom region of the pocket but displaying limited engagement with the aforementioned subpocket (Figures 4E and 4F). The heterocyclic head group of this potential antagonist is anchored to the receptor extracellular region through polar interactions between its hydroxyamino groups and the residues E185ECL2 and E2857.36 in ECL2 and helix 7 of GPR15, while the two decyl chains make extensive hydrophobic contacts with the surrounding residues in ECL2 and helices 2, 3, 6, and 7 (Figure 4G). Notably, one of the decyl chains inserts into a hydrophobic cavity at the bottom of the binding pocket, packing against F2576.51 and W2546.48 (Figure 4G). These hydrophobic interactions hinder the downward shift of F2576.51, thereby stabilizing W2546.48 in the inactive conformation. Furthermore, we performed a 500-ns MD simulation of the GPR15-compound 5 complex. Binding free energy decomposition suggests that residues R1724.64, K187ECL2, and K2616.55, which potentially form polar interactions with the hydroxyl group of compound 5, serve as major contributors to compound 5 binding (Figures S7H and S7I). In silico alanine scanning further revealed that mutations of these three residues markedly reduced predicted binding affinity (Figure S7J). These computational results provide additional support for the proposed binding mode of compound 5.

We also performed the molecular docking of the other seven potential antagonists, showing that these small-molecule ligands bind to a similar binding site to that of compound 5 (Figure S7). Despite different interaction patterns with the receptor, these compounds adopt a conserved binding mode to F2576.51 and W2546.48 (Figure S7). Similar to compound 5, they constrain the rotamer conformational changes of these two key residues in helix 6 and thus, inhibit receptor activation. Collectively, these findings suggest a common antagonist-binding mode of GPR15, characterized by stabilization of the inactive state through hydrophobic locking of helix 6.

Discussion

Since the initial identification of GPR15L in porcine colonic extracts, this endogenous ligand of GPR15 has been found to be associated with a variety of physiological processes, including lymphocyte homing, antibacterial defense, and tumor growth regulation. However, the molecular basis that underpins its functions remain unclear. In this study, we elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the ligand recognition and receptor activation of GPR15 by employing an integrated approach that combines cryo-EM structural analysis, functional mutagenesis studies, and computational analysis.

This work highlights the pivotal role of the C-terminal region of GPR15L in defining its agonistic activity, with some new interactions that mediate the receptor-ligand binding unveiled. Consistent with the higher agonist potency of the full-length GPR15L compared to the C-terminal C11 peptide, our MD simulations and functional studies suggest that the receptor N terminus—potentially through sulfation modifications—may interact with the core region of GPR15L. While these data align with a model that the N-terminal sulfotyrosines facilitate ligand binding, the precise sulfation landscape and residue-specific contributions remain to be experimentally validated. These findings extend our understanding beyond those from the previously reported GPR15-C11 structure by revealing additional ligand-receptor interactions that are crucial for receptor activation. While GPR15 shares certain features with the classical chemokine receptors, it exhibits unique features in ligand recognition. In both systems, the ligand binds to the receptor in a dual binding-site mode: the ligand core interacts with the receptor extracellular regions, and the terminal segment of the ligand inserts into the receptor transmembrane core to trigger activation. However, unlike chemokines, which rely on their flexible N terminus to occupy the orthosteric pocket, GPR15L engages its receptor through interactions with the sulfotyrosines at the receptor N terminus, while its C terminus extends into the orthosteric pocket of GPR15. This binding mode—N-terminal sulfotyrosine engagement together with C-terminal penetration—defines a distinctive recognition pattern for GPR15L from those of the chemokines.

A critical subpocket in GPR15, which accommodates the C-terminal end of GPR15L, governs the receptor activation. The absence of such spatial arrangements in chemokine receptors excludes their engagement with GPR15L, which may explain the binding specificity of GPR15L at GPR15. Combined with mutagenesis data, the GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 structure suggests that this subpocket serves as a potential binding site for design of selective modulators of GPR15 functions. To further deepen our understanding of the ligand binding modes of GPR15 and provide new clues for future drug development, molecular docking was conducted for several potential GPR15 antagonists and shows that these small-molecule compounds bind to the orthosteric ligand binding pocket of the receptor and stabilize the receptor in the inactive state.

These findings provide essential insights into key factors that define the ligand recognition and receptor activation of GPR15, and offer new opportunities for developing innovative therapeutic strategies targeting GPR15 in diverse disease contexts.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we elucidated the binding mode of the full-length endogenous ligand GPR15L to its receptor GPR15 and uncovered the activation mechanism of this atypical chemoattractant receptor. Through integrated cryo-EM, mutagenesis, and MD simulations, we identified the potential roles of the N-terminal sulfation and a subpocket of GPR15 in ligand recognition and receptor activation. However, the N-terminal and core regions of GPR15L remain unresolved due to their conformational flexibility. Moreover, as the structural studies were performed using the receptor expressed in insect cells, which may lack efficient tyrosine sulfation, the proposed role of N-terminal sulfation—though supported by functional assays in HEK293 cells—remains to be further validated. Although molecular docking and simulations suggest possible interactions between GPR15L residues and the receptor N terminus, these interactions remain hypothetical in the absence of mutagenesis validation. Future structural, biochemical, and cellular studies will be essential to precisely delineate the sulfation landscape of GPR15 and to confirm the contributions of individual residues to ligand binding and receptor activation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines

High Five insect cells (Thermo Fisher) were cultured in SIM HF Expression Medium (Sino Biological, MHF1) at 27°C with shaking at 120 rpm. HEK293 cells were cultured in a humidified 37°C incubator with 5% CO2 using DMEM containing 10% FBS.

Method details

Construct cloning and protein expression

The gene of human GPR15 was cloned into a modified pFastBac1 vector containing a haemagglutinin (HA) signal peptide at the N terminus and a PreScission protease (PPase) site along with a twin-strep tag followed by a FLAG tag at the C terminus. The gene of dominant-negative human Gαi3 (DNGαi3) containing five mutations (S47C, G202T, G203A, E245A, and A326S) was cloned into the pFastBac1 vector. The gene of human Gβ1 with a 6× histidine (His) tag at the N terminus and Gγ2 were cloned into the pFastBac Dual vector (Invitrogen). High-titer recombinant baculovirus were obtained using the bac-to-bac baculovirus expression system. High Five insect cells at a density of 1.5 × 106 cells ml−1 were transfected with viral stocks of the modified GPR15, DNGαi3, and Gβ1γ2 at a MOI (multiplicity of infection) ratio of 1:3:3. The cell pellets were collected by centrifugation after 48 h post transfection and stored at −80°C for further use.

Expression and purification of GPR15L

The gene C10orf99 encoding human GPR15L (residues 25 to 81), with a soluble thioredoxin-His tag (TrxA-His tag) and a TEV protease cleavage site (ENLYFQ/A) fused at the N terminus, was constructed into the pET32a vector (Sangon Biotech). Then the wild type (WT) and variant plasmids were transformed into Escherichia coli strain BL21 (DE3). The cells were cultured in LB medium supplemented with 75 mg mL−1 ampicillin at 37°C until reaching an optical density at 600 nm (OD600 nm) of 0.6, and were then induced with 0.4 mM IPTG at 16°C overnight. The cell pellets were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in a buffer containing 25 mM Tris, pH 8.0, and 150 mM NaCl. After ultrasonication, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation, supplemented with imidazole to a final concentration of 30 mM, and incubated with Ni-NTA Superflow resin at 4°C for 2 h. The resin was washed with buffer A containing 25 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 50 mM imidazole, and then buffer B containing 25 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 140 mM imidazole. The protein was eluted with a buffer containing 25 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 300 mM imidazole. The GPR15L protein was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 75 Increase 10/300 column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated in a buffer containing 25 mM Tris, pH 8.0 and 150 mM NaCl. Peak fractions of protein were collected and concentrated to 4 mg mL−1 with a 10 kDa molecular weight cut-off concentrator (Millipore), fast frozen by liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for further use.

Expression and purification of scFv16

The gene of scFv16 was cloned into the modified pFastBac1 vector with a GP67 secretion signal peptide inserted to the N terminus and a PreScission protease site followed by an 8× His tag attached to the C terminus. The protein was expressed using the High Five insect cells. The culture supernatant was treated with 1 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 1 mM NiCl2, and 5 mM CaCl2 at room temperature for 1 h. Precipitates were then removed by centrifugation at 100,000g for 30 min. The supernatant was incubated with Ni-NTA Superflow resin (Qiagen) in the presence of 5 mM imidazole at 4°C for 2 h. The resin was washed with 20 column volumes of buffer containing 1× PBS and 10 mM imidazole. The protein was then eluted with 10 column volumes of 1× PBS supplemented with 300 mM imidazole and then concentrated along with adding 1× PBS to dilute the imidazole. After treatment with the His-tagged PreScission protease at 4°C overnight, the protein sample was incubated with Ni-NTA Superflow resin at 4°C for 1 h to remove the cleaved C-terminal His tag and PreScission protease. The protein was further purified by size exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 75 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated in 1× PBS, and then the fractions of scFv16 were concentrated to 2–5 mg mL−1 with a 10 kDa molecular weight cut-off concentrator (Millipore) and then stored at −80°C for further use.

Purification of GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 complex

The cells were thawed on ice and disrupted by a homogenizer in lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, EDTA-free complete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche), and 100 μM Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP, Sigma-Aldrich). To assemble the complex in membrane, 50 mU ml−1 apyrase (NEB) was added to the membrane and incubated at 20°C for 1 h. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 14,000g for 30 min, and then lysed in the lysis buffer supplemented with 10 μM GPR15L, TEV protease, and scFv16. The complex was extracted by adding 0.5% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG, Anatrace) and 0.05% (w/v) CHS, and incubated at 4°C for 2 h. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 14,000g for 30 min, and then incubated with Strep-Tactin XT Superflow resin (IBA Lifesciences) overnight at 4°C. The resin was washed with a buffer containing 25 mM Tris, PH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.01% (w/v) LMNG, 0.001% (w/v) CHS, 2 mM MgCl2, and 100 μM TCEP and then the detergent was exchanged in a buffer containing 25 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 100 μM TCEP, 0.25% (w/v) LMNG, 0.025% (w/v) CHS, and 0.083% (w/v) glyco-diosgenin (GDN, Anatrace) at 4°C for 2 h. The receptor was eluted with a buffer containing 150 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 100 μM TCEP, 0.01% (w/v) LMNG, 0.001% (w/v) CHS, 0.003% (w/v) GDN, and 50 mM biotin (Sigma-Aldrich) and then incubated with Ni-NTA Superflow resin at 4°C for 1.5 h. The resin was washed again with a buffer containing 25 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 100 μM TCEP, 0.01% (w/v) LMNG, 0.001% (w/v) CHS, 0.003% (w/v) GDN, and 5 mM imidazole. The complex was eluted with a buffer containing 25 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 100 μM TCEP, 0.01% (w/v) LMNG, 0.001% (w/v) CHS, 0.003% (w/v) GDN, and 300 mM imidazole. The complex was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated in 25 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 100 μM TCEP, 0.002% (w/v) LMNG, 0.0002% (w/v) CHS, and 0.00067% (w/v) GDN. Peak fractions of the complex were collected and then concentrated to 2 mg mL−1 with a 100 kDa molecular weight cut-off concentrator (Millipore) for follow-up cryo-EM studies.

Expression and purification of CCL5

The gene of CCL5 was cloned into the modified pFastBac1 vector with an HA signal peptide inserted to the N terminus and a PreScission protease site followed by a PGS fusion as well as a 10× His tag attached to the C terminus. The protein was expressed using the High Five insect cells. The culture supernatant was incubated with Ni-NTA Superflow resin (Qiagen) in the presence of 5 mM imidazole at 4°C for 2 h. The resin was washed with 30 column volumes of a buffer containing 25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 30 mM imidazole. The protein was then eluted with 10 column volumes of a buffer containing 25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, and 300 mM imidazole and then concentrated along with adding a buffer containing 25 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl to dilute the imidazole. Finally, the protein was concentrated to 3–5 mg mL−1 with a 3 kDa molecular weight cut-off concentrator (Millipore) and then stored at −80°C for further use.

Cryo-EM data collection

The purified GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 complex was applied to glow-discharged holey carbon grids (Nanodim Tech, M01-Au300-R1.2/1.3). The grids were blotted at 4°C, 100% humidity with force of 0 and blot time of 1.0 s, plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using Vitrobot Mark IV (ThermoFisher Scientific), and stored in liquid nitrogen until use. Images were obtained by a Titan Krios G3 electron microscope (FEI) of 300 kV with K3 Summit direct electron detector (Gatan) using pixel size of 1.071 Å. Movie stacks were recorded with defocus ranging from −0.8 to −1.5 μm, exposing to a dose rate of 1.875 electrons/Å2 per frame. Each movie stack contains 40 frames for a total dose of 70 electrons/Å2. SerialEM33 was applied to automated single-particle data acquisition.

Cryo-EM data processing

A total of 9,630 collected movies were subjected to beam-induced motion correction by MotionCor2.34 The contrast transfer function parameters of each micrograph were estimated using CTFFIND4 in CryoSPARC.45 Total of 8,038,291 particles of the GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 complex were picked using template picker by CryoSPARC and then subjected to 2D classification. The best class with 2,738,851 particles was selected for ab initio reconstruction and hetero refinement. The best class with 1,240,093 particles was selected and subjected to non-uniform refinement, resulting in a map with a global resolution of 2.86 Å. Then these particles were exported into RELION44 for 3D focus classification with mask on the receptor. The best class with 286,873 particles was selected, imported into CryoSPARC, and subjected to non-uniform refinement, generating a map at 3.30 Å resolution based on the gold-standard Fourier Shell Correlation (FSC) using the 0.143 criterion. Local refinement on the receptor region was performed, which further improved the map resolution of the receptor to 3.05 Å. The final composite map was yielded in UCSF Chimera50 using ‘vop maximum’ command and used for model building. The local resolution for the map was generated by ResMap.37

Model building

The receptor in the GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 complex was built using the AlphaFold predicted model as a template. Gαi3 and Gβγ were built using the components in the somatostatin-14-SSTR2-Gi3 complex (PDB ID: 7T10) and NmU25-NMUR2-Gi1 complex (PDB: 7XK8), respectively. The antibody scFv16 were built using the component in the US28-CX3CL1-Gi complex (PDB: 7RKM). The models were fitted into corresponding maps using UCSF ChimeraX (https://www.rbvi.ucsf.edu/chimerax).51 Subsequently, the models were merged and rebuilt using COOT, and refined using phenix.real_space_refine in PHENIX.47 The final models were validated by MolProbity.52 The structure figures in this paper were prepared by PyMOL (https://pymol.org/2/).

Bioluminescence resonance energy transfer (BRET) assay

The plasmids of Gαi3, Gβ3, and Gγ9 used for BRET assay were obtained from Addgene (Kit, #1000000163). The wild-type (WT) GPR15 and CCR5 and mutants were constructed into PTT5 vector (Invitrogen) with an HA signal peptide followed by a FLAG tag fused at the N terminus. HEK293F cells were transiently transfected with the receptor, Gαi3, Gβ3, and Gγ9 at an MOI ratio of 4:1:1:1 with total 3,500 ng plasmid DNA using transfection reagent (PEI MAX 2000, Polysciences) and cultivated at 37°C in 5% CO2. Cells were collected after 48 h post transfection. Surface expression of the receptor was measured using Monoclonal ANTI-FLAG M2-FITC antibody (Sigma, F4049; 1:100 diluted by TBS supplemented with 4% BSA) and the fluorescence signal on the cell surface was measured by an FCM (flow cytometry) reader (Millipore). The cells were diluted with 1× HBSS (Hank’s balanced salt solution, Gibco) balanced buffer supplemented with 20 mM HEPES, PH 7.4 and seeded onto 96-well opaque cell culture plates (Beyotime) at a density of 30,000 cells in 60 μL per well. Ten microliters of freshly prepared 50 μM coelenterazine 400a (Nanolight Technologies) were added into each well. After incubation in dark for 5 min, plates were then read in a Synerg H1 microplate reader (BioTek) with excitation at 395 nm and emission at 410 and 515 nm by serially three times. Then, 30 μL of WT and variants of GPR15L were added into the wells at different concentrations ranging from 10−4.5 to 10−11 M. The C11 fragment of GPR15L was synthesized (GL Biochem), and added into the wells at gradient final concentrations from 10−3.5 to 10−10 M. The CCL5 protein was added into the wells at gradient final concentration from 10−5 to 10−12 M. Plates were read immediately again by serially five times. Data were processed and analyzed following the method in the literature53 by using GraphPad Prism 8.2.

MD simulations

All MD simulations were performed using the GROMACS package54 (version 2022.3) with the CHARMM36m force field,55 starting from the GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 cryo-EM structure and the GPR15-compound 5 docking model, the intracellular G protein was removed prior to the simulations. The full-length structures of GPR15 and GPR15L were predicted using AlphaFold3,34 aligned with the cryo-EM structure, and missing atomic coordinates from the AlphaFold predictions were added to the cryo-EM structure.

The GPCR structures were prepared using the ‘Protein Preparation Wizard’ module in Maestro (v12.8.117, Schrödinger),56 where missing side chains and hydrogen atoms were added. Then the simulation systems were prepared in parallel using CHARMM-GUI.57 The transmembrane domains were aligned using PPM2.0,58 and the systems were embedded in a POPC bilayer membrane. The simulation box was then filled with TIP3P water molecules, and 150 mM NaCl was added to neutralize the system.

After the system setup, energy minimization was performed with position restraints (10 kcal/mol/Å2) on the protein backbone. This was followed by a 1-ns heating step, where the temperature was gradually increased from 0 K to 310 K under the NVT ensemble using a Nosé-Hoover thermostat,59 with position restraints of 5 kcal/mol/Å2 on the protein backbone during heating. Subsequently, three stages of 10-ns NPT equilibration were conducted. During the first stage, position restraints of 3 kcal/mol/Å2 were applied to the heavy atoms of the protein. After 10 ns, the restraints were reduced to 1 kcal/mol/Å2 for the second stage of equilibration. In the final stage, all position restraints were removed, allowing the system to equilibrate fully. The pressure was controlled using the Parrinello-Rahman method60 and the simulation system was coupled to a 1-bar pressure bath. Following equilibration, a 500-ns production run was carried out under the NPT ensemble without any position restraints. All simulations were conducted with periodic boundary conditions, and electrostatic interactions were computed using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method. The LINCS algorithm was used to constrain bond lengths involving hydrogen atoms.

To investigate the conformational differences between the active and inactive states of the receptor, a metadynamics simulation was performed. The collective variable was defined by the angle between residues I2.41, S6.47, and I6.34, which reflects the conformational shift of helix 6 distinguishing the active and inactive states.61 To facilitate this transition, the activating ligand (GPR15L) and the G protein were removed from the GPR15L-GPR15-Gi3 protein cryo-EM structure, eliminating activation and enabling the receptor to adopt the inactive conformation.

Metadynamics simulations were performed with GROMACS (version 2022.3) and PLUMED (version 2.8.1), applying enhanced sampling to the defined angle to promote the transition to the inactive state. The parameters included a PACE of 2000, a Gaussian width (sigma) of 0.005, and a Gaussian height (HEIGHT) of 0.05, with 500 ns of simulation to explore conformational states beyond the initial activation conditions and provide insights into the structural dynamics of GPR15 at inactive state.

MD analysis

Molecular Mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area (MM-PBSA) analyses were performed using gmx_MMPBSA62 to evaluate binding free energies and residue-level contributions. All simulation systems were analyzed using an implicit membrane model (dielectric constant = 7.0, thickness mthick = 40.0 Å, external solvent dielectric exdi = 80.0). Residue-level decomposition considered residues within 5 Å of the ligand binding site (print_res = “within 5″). The alanine scanning was performed following the official protocol.

Molecular docking

The inactive-like GPR15 model was used as the molecular docking template, and was processed by using the ‘Protein Preparation Wizard’.56 The ligands were converted from 2D to 3D structures using ‘LigPrep’ (Schrödinger). And molecular docking was carried out using ‘Induced Fit Docking’.

For validating the specific binding and activation mechanism of GPR15L toward the subpocket of GPR15, a series of peptides were docked into the active GPR15 structure using Schrödinger’s Peptide Docking module, which internally calculates Molecular Mechanics-Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) binding energies for each pose.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software, with data presented as mean ± SEM. Comparisons among multiple groups were made using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and differences with p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
