A Versatile Cytochemical Labeling Technique as a Forensic Decision-Support Tool for In Situ Detection of Cell-Derived Markers in Invisible Biological Traces
Authors: Mathilde Bastat, Mathilde Recipon, Séverine Nozownik, Sabrina Kellouche, Vincent Castella, Joséphine Gibert, Franck Carreiras, Rémy Agniel, Olivier Gallet, Sylvain Hubac, Francis Hermitte, Cédric R. Picot, Johanne Leroy-Dudal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Forensic genetic analysis of biological traces collected at a crime scene can aid in the rapid identification of perpetrators. Touch DNA samples consist of invisible biological traces left on an object or person after skin contact. Although these samples represent a significant portion of DNA evidence, they often fail to yield usable DNA profiles due to challenges in detection and collection. A decision-support tool capable of highlighting such traces would optimize sampling strategies and improve forensic outcomes. We previously identified cell-derived markers that persist on shed corneocytes. Here, we propose an in situ cytochemistry-based tool using a dual labeling technique that combines chromogenic and fluorescent visualization to enhance the detection of otherwise invisible traces. Chromogenic labeling of cell-derived mannose residues and keratin 10 allows the visualization of up to 100 cells on surfaces and swabs and enables the acquisition of usable DNA profiles. A combination of FITC-conjugated primary probes and an HRP-conjugated anti-FITC antibody enables versatile visualization, allowing both chromogenic and fluorescent detection of otherwise invisible biological traces and offering adaptability across various substrates. Corneocytes from fingermarks were successfully detected on both light and opaque surfaces (ceramic and metal) using a single cytochemistry process. In addition, the presence of biological material after swabbing a fingermark was highlighted, notably by chromogenic labeling. This approach could improve on-field touch DNA sample detection, proving valuable both before and after sampling. It can highlight areas for collection or prioritize swabs likely to contain biological material, thereby increasing the chances of obtaining usable DNA profiles for criminal investigation.

Introduction

At a crime scene, investigators are faced with various types of traces , that are crucial for forensic investigations. Among them, biological traces that are likely to contain human DNA are particularly relevant. Through forensic genetic analyses, they can be linked to individuals known to the authorities or to other traces using national DNA databases. Today, DNA is widely recognized and used as a crucial investigative tool in most countries around the world. For DNA profiling purposes, biological traces are collected using devices such as swabs or adhesive tapes, from which DNA is subsequently extracted.

Biological traces may be visible (e.g., blood) or invisible. Among the latter, some can be highlighted using specific reagents like BlueStar and Luminol. , However, others, such as touch DNA samples, remain undetectable to the naked eye or with alternative light sources (ALS) and cannot be revealed using existing reagents.

Touch DNA samples refer to DNA transferred to an object or another individual after skin contact with a donor. , In 1997, van Oorschot demonstrated that DNA profiles could be obtained after swabbing a handled object. The ability to collect, amplify, and analyze DNA from invisible biological material deposited on surfaces represents a breakthrough in forensic genetics.

The precise composition of touch DNA samples still remains under debate in the literature. Touch DNA samples may include fragmented or cell-free DNA, epithelial cells carried from sweat or sebum, or cells carried from other biological fluids such as saliva or tears and deposited on the skin. ,, However, since these traces result from physical contact, they are believed to primarily originate from the skin. The skin is a complex organ composed of three main tissues, with the outermost being the epidermis. The latter comprises several strata of keratinocytes, which progressively undergo a process of terminal differentiation. During this process, keratinocytes transform into corneocytes, which are fully differentiated cells that form the skin’s outermost barrier, known as the stratum corneum. Corneocytes are anucleate keratinocytes. They contain structural proteins like keratin and they are shed from the skin’s surface at a rate estimated to be 2–10 × 108 cells per day or approximately 1,000 cells/cm2/hour. , Thus, they can notably be transferred to a surface following skin contact. Although shed cells are primarily anucleate, it has been demonstrated that fragmented DNA can be trapped within corneocyte fragments , or residual keratinocyte elements. , Hence, the concept of touch DNA samples could be extended to include cell-derived fragments.

Therefore, handled objects and surfaces found at crime scenes could serve as promising sources of touch DNA samples even though they are invisible to the naked eye. Crime-scene investigators have to take samples blindly, employing a hypothetico-deductive approach to target areas most likely to harbor invisible biological traces. This results in a large quantity of samples being analyzed, with a reduced proportion yielding usable DNA profiles. For example, in 2024, in France, around 43,000 touch DNA sample analyses were performed, with more than half of them failing to yield usable DNA profiles (internal source, Institut de Recherche Criminelle de la Gendarmerie Nationale, IRCGN). A similar situation is observed at the Unit of Forensic Genetics in Lausanne, Switzerland, where no usable DNA profiles were obtained for 48% of around 13,000 touch DNA samples (these data apply according to the requirements of each country). This could be partly due to their invisible nature, which makes detection and collection challenging. Currently, there are no decision-supporting tools that can assist investigators in detecting and collecting such traces.

Recent studies have explored methods for detecting touch DNA samples. For example, the use of DNA-specific dyes such as Diamond Dye has demonstrated the potential to detect nucleic acid on substrates or swabs after staining. However, the fluorescent signal may remain undetectable if the amount of DNA is insufficient. Since DNA can remain entrapped in shed-cell fragments and corneocytes, another approach relies on targeting not only DNA but also cell-derived markers. This approach first identifies biological fragments that are larger and capable of binding more probes than a DNA molecule, thereby generating a more robust signal to maximize the likelihood of detecting touch DNA. To this end, the sampling targets cell structures and fragmented cellular components, where DNA may be entrapped or associated as cell-free DNA. , Thus, targeting cell-derived fragments that likely contain DNA could increase the number of relevant samples for DNA analysis. In previous work, we detected several cell-derived proteins, including keratin, and carbohydrates such as mannose residues, which persist on shed keratinocytes and corneocytes up to 2 months under uncontrolled climate conditions. Targeting such shed-cell-associated molecules opens up the opportunity to fluorescently visualize biological material on handled objects and to reveal manipulated areas on crime-scene items. Nevertheless, this fluorescent revelation could be limited by the autofluorescence of substrates, such as plastic, or that of most swabs. In this study, we propose to keep the advantages of revealing cell-derived fragments using fluorescence while adapting a cytochemistry protocol to provide additional chromogenic visualization. Thus, we have developed a dual versatile technique for detecting cell markers in invisible biological traces. This approach could serve as a valuable decision-support tool for investigators, enhancing DNA trace detection and verifying the collection of biological material on swabs after sampling.

Materials and Methods

Substrates of Touch DNA Samples Models for In Vitro Experiments

Glass coverslips (12 mm diameter, Epredia) were cleaned with acetone, rinsed with ultrapure water, and then autoclaved. Sterile nylon fibers 4N6FLOQSwabs (Copan Italia S.P.A.) were used directly. Crime scene-like substrates (glass slides, ceramic tile, metal, raw wood) were selected by IRCGN expertise and cleaned with DNA-free water and 70% ethanol. Substrates were used for cell seeding and fingermark deposition. The physicochemical characterization of these substrates has already been established.

Biological Samples

Keratinocyte Cell Line

Human keratinocyte cells (CCD 1106 KERTr, CRL-2309, ATCC, USA), derived from skin tissue, were cultured in keratinocyte-SFM (Serum Free Medium) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher), with supplements recombinant human epidermal growth factor (rEGF, 35 ng/mL) and bovine pituitary extract (BPE, 50 ng/mL). For the experiments, cells were seeded for 4 h to allow cell adherence at the desired density (ranging from 100 to 5,000 cells/cm2) and then dried. Samples were either labeled directly or collected with moistened nylon swabs (45 μL sterile water, Addipak).

Simulated Fingermark Deposition

Fingermarks were deposited on each substrate (see first part of Materials and Methods) under moderate pressure during 10 s. All samples were obtained with the informed consent of the volunteers and the approval of the Ethics Committee of the Pôle Judiciaire de la Gendarmerie Nationale (PJGN) (Recommendation n°19 of 02/11/2022), in accordance with the Helsinki agreements (1975) and the French National Charter for Research Integrity. , In some experiments, deposited fingermarks were collected using one side of a swab (4N6FLOQSwabs, Copan Italia S.P.A) moistened with a drop of sterile water (45 μL Addipak). The entire swab heads were then stained in microtubes at room temperature and left to dry as in their original case.

Cytochemistry Stainings

Samples were permeabilized with PBS-Triton X-100 (0.1%, 4 min, 4 °C). Blocking used PBS-BSA (1% for mannose and 0.5% for keratin 10). The samples were then incubated in a humidity chamber for 30 min with Concanavalin A-FITC at a final concentration of 10 μg/mL to target mannose residues (Con A, FL-1001, Eurobio Scientific), or for 2 h with either nonconjugated antikeratin 10 antibody (K10, diluted 1:100 in PBS-BSA 0.5%; SAB4501656, Sigma-Aldrich) or FITC-conjugated antikeratin 10 antibody (diluted 10 μg/mL in PBS-BSA 0.5%; MBS2057971, MyBiosource). After three successive washes in PBS for keratin 10 detection, the samples were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:300; 12–348, Sigma-Aldrich) or with an FITC-secondary antibody (1:100; F0382, Sigma-Aldrich). For versatile labeling, the samples were then washed with PBS and incubated with an anti-FITC antibody – HRP-conjugated (1:1000; PA1–85440, Invitrogen) for both mannose and keratin 10 detection.

The chromogenic signal was developed with 1× 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole (AEC) solution as the substrate for HRP (NB-23–00140, NeoBiotech) until a red precipitate became visible (approximatively 15–30 min).

For the control conditions, in the case of labeling on a coverslip (“before sampling” conditions), two controls were made: (i) a control with cells but without staining and (ii) a control without cells (only cell culture medium) but with staining. In the case of swab labeling after cell collection (“after sampling” conditions), three kinds of control were done: (i) swabs after cell collection but without staining, (ii) swabs after cell collection with only the rinsing steps of staining procedure, or (iii) swabs collection from a substrate without cells but subjected to staining.

Visualization Method

Chromogenic Visualization

AEC-stained samples were observed with a stereomicroscope (S9D, Leica with Flexacam C3 camera (10450528 0.5×, Leica)) and a digital microscope (AM4113ZT, Dino-Lite Universal), using DinoCapture 2.0 software. Lighting and polarizers were adjusted per substrate. ImageJ software (version 2.9.0) was used for image processing.

Fluorescence Visualization

FITC signals were observed under an upright fluorescence microscope (DM2000Led, Leica) with a 10× objective or a confocal microscope (LSM900, Zeiss). The excitation wavelength for FITC was set at 488 nm, and emission was collected between 500 and 560 nm. Z-Stacks (sections 220 μm/range 0,397 μm) were converted on 2D images with ImageJ/FIJI software.

Genetic Analysis

DNA Extraction, Quantification, Amplification, and Capillary Electrophoresis

DNA extraction was performed using the Crime Prep Adem-kit, Automag Solution (Ademtech), on a KingFisher Duo Prime system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantification was carried out using the Investigator Quantiplex Kit (Qiagen). 2 μL of DNA samples were added to a mix containing QuantiProReaction and primers. Samples were normalized to 0.067 ng/μL.

Amplification used the GlobalFiler IQC PCR Amplification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), covering 21 autosomal STRs, two Y-chromosome markers (DYS391, Y-indel), and a sex-determining marker (Amelogenin), including the 13 core CODIS (COmbined DNA Index System) loci. Samples were amplified for 30 cycles using a Veriti 96-Well Thermal Cycler. Amplification was performed in two independent technical duplicates.

1 μL of PCR products (1 μL) were analyzed by capillary electrophoresis (ABI 3500xxL, Genetic Analyzer), with electrokinetic injection at 1.2 kV for 24 s. Raw data were processed using GeneMapper ID-X version 1.6 software to generate electropherograms (EPGs) representing DNA profiles. (More details are available in the experimental section.)

DNA Profile Analysis

The average fluorescence intensity (measured in relative fluorescence units, RFU) was calculated for each STR marker, and then, the average of replicates was computed. More precisely, the intensity of each STR amplified with the same dye marker (6-FAM, VIC, NED, TAZ, and SID) added during amplification was examined. The EPG was examined by looking at the amplification of quality sensors (IQCS and IQCL) to ensure the correct sample amplification. DNA degradation was assessed by calculating the ratio of human DNA to degraded DNA obtained from qPCR experiments. Based on an internal French Gendarmerie validation process (using a DNA source of known quantity and STR profile, amplifications at 30 cycles were carried out by decreasing the initial DNA quantity until a DNA profile with a true allele, without artifacts, was obtained), the detection threshold (to avoid background noise) was set at 50 RFU. Furthermore, each STR profile was compared to the known reference sample DNA profile (keratinocytes without labeling). In this study, no DNA profiles obtained were sent to the French national database (FNAEG) and only the compatibility with this database was assed. A DNA profile is considered compatible if it is complete (23 STR markers and Amelogenin) or if it is partial, meaning a minimum of 5 STR markers with the two alleles characterized and Amelogenin among the 13 STR markers and Amelogenin from the original 13 CODIS markers. Conversely, a DNA profile is deemed incompatible if it presents fewer than 5 STR markers fully characterized and Amelogenin among the 13 STR markers and Amelogenin from the original 13 CODIS markers. These requirements were in line with the French national database (FNAEG) recommendations and also with the international cooperation accord of Prüm (Decree no. 2008–33 of January 10, 2008).

Statistical Analysis

The different results obtained are presented as the mean ± standard deviation of at least three independent experiments. Data were normally distributed according to the Shapiro–Wilk test (α = 0.01). A statistical analysis was conducted using Excel. Variance equality was verified by performing an F-Test. A T-Test was conducted based on the F-Test. Statistical significances were designated by * indicating a two-tailed p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001, respectively. Data were formatted using the GraphPad Prism 10 software.

Results and Discussion

In Vitro Chromogenic Visualization of Cell-Derived Fragments

This study aims to provide a decision-support tool to improve the detection and subsequent collection of touch DNA samples in crime scenes. This could be valuable both (i) before sampling, by identifying areas likely to contain biological material, and (ii) after sampling, in order to identify swabs likely to contain biological material and prioritize them for genetic analysis. Such an improvement could increase the likelihood of obtaining usable DNA profiles for criminal investigation.

The fluorescent visualization of cell-derived fragments was previously shown to be promising for highlighting invisible biological traces even after two months outdoors, without any subsequent alteration of DNA analyses. However, this visualization requires observations to be conducted in the dark and is limited by some substrates’ autofluorescence, both of which may complicate its use in the field. To keep the advantages of fluorescence labeling while overcoming its potential limitations, we explored here the use of chromogenic labeling as a complementary approach.

We first explored its feasibility on samples that were “rich” in biological material. For “before sampling” conditions, keratinocyte cells were deposited at high densities on glass coverslips (5,000 cells, Figure A,C), while for “after sampling” conditions, keratinocyte cells were deposited on glass coverslips at a very high in vitro density and then collected with swabs (200,000 cells, Figure B,D). In both sets of conditions, cell-derived fragments were labeled by Concanavalin A (Con A) in combination with AEC-HRP revelation.

Mannose residues were labeled and appeared as a reddish precipitate. Observations were performed using a stereomicroscope (S9D, Leica; with Flexacam C3 camera 0.5x, Leica) with a 5.5× lens (high magnification, scale bars 2 mm) or a portable digital microscope (AM4113ZT, Dino-Lite Universal) (200×, high magnification, scale bars 200 μm). The average intensity of the genetic profile for two different cell concentrations (5,000 and 500 keratinocytes cells), either with labeling reagent or without (control), is represented (E). The overall allele intensity and the allele intensity of each STR marker amplified with the same dye marker (6-FAM, VIC, NED, TAZ, SID) added during amplification were examined. The mean intensity for three swabs for each condition was calculated. Data are normally distributed. A t test at 0.01 level was performed, and no significant difference was observed when comparing control vs labeling conditions.

On glass coverslips, no specific staining was observed when no cells were seeded (no cell-control), whatever the device (i.e., stereomicroscope or digital microscope) used for observation (Figure A,C). In the presence of keratinocyte cells, a red/brown staining was detected using both the stereomicroscope and the digital microscope, thus highlighting the remaining and transparent cells. In control conditions (no cell-collected), swabs exhibited a weak background signal, appearing as fiber coloration that varied depending on the observation device (pale yellow under the stereomicroscope, Figure B, and dark yellow with the digital microscope, Figure D). This may be due to the glue used to flock the fibers as a transversal section of the swab reveals a diffuse yellow appearance at the base of the fibers, even without staining (Figure S1). After labeling keratinocyte cells on swabs, reddish spots systematically appeared on the fibers regardless of the observation technique used, thus indicating the presence of trapped cell-derived fragments within the swab and highlighting the efficacy of the sampling.

For field implementation, this detection strategy must be compatible with DNA profiling. The potential impact of the chromogenic labeling on genetic analyses was assessed (Figure E). As compared to the control conditions (no staining), there was no significant difference in the overall fluorescence intensity of the EPG between stained and unstained samples. Each fluorescent dye DNA marker used during DNA amplification was analyzed and no significant differences were observed between stained and control conditions. All STR markers were successfully amplified, and the resulting profiles were of sufficient quality to be considered usable according to the French database requirements. These results remained consistent across a range of cell densities tested from 5,000 down to 500 cells (Figure E). Thus, the chromogenic labeling of cell-derived fragments, which relies on an HRP-mediated reaction with a substrate (AEC) in the presence of hydrogen peroxide, produces a visible color change, enabling the detection of areas that contain cell fragments. Moreover, this method did not interfere with forensic genetic analyses. Chromogenic labeling is commonly used in cell biology, cytohistochemistry, and histopathology, but its forensic application remains rare. Its transposition to crime scenes offers several advantages: it is rapid, cost-effective, and easy to use; it enables visual detection with low magnification and portable devices; and it could even be distinguished with a common and portable digital microscope. Some studies, such as that of Akutsu et al., have demonstrated its utility in detecting certain biological fluids, like urine, for forensic applications. However, to our knowledge, the use of chromogenic labeling to reveal biological material for the purpose of detecting touch DNA samples is underexplored and has never been directly investigated on swabs, prompting us to develop and adapt this approach for field forensic applications.

For the first part of the study, a high number of cells were used to assess the suitability of chromogenic labeling. To maintain consistency with biological trace detection conditions, we then investigated the potential impact of HRP-AEC labeling on DNA profiling using lower cell density.

To evaluate the sensitivity of chromogenic labeling, we decided to target two cell-derived markers: mannose residues (Figure ) and keratinocyte specific protein (Figure ) using Concanavalin A (Con A), a C-type lectin directed against mannose glycosylations or an antibody directed against keratin 10 (K10), respectively. These were applied either before or after sampling, and the DNA profiles were considered. We chose these targets because their stability and persistence over time on cell-derived fragments within fingermarks were previously described. Con A specifically binds to mannose-rich oligosaccharides present on many glycosylated proteins, making it a useful tool for the analysis of glycoconjugates and histochemistry. Indeed, mannose residues are present on the surface of cells, particularly corneocytes from the epidermis. , Adbayen et al. have shown that labeling mannose residues with Con A presents similarities to corneodesmosin labeling, a corneocyte-specific protein involved in cell–cell adhesion.

Moreover, many keratins exist in the epidermis, and the terminal differentiation of basal keratinocytes into corneocytes involves a shift in the level of keratin expression. As basal cells primarily express keratins 5, 14, and 15, and as corneocytes progressively express keratins 1 and 10, we specifically focused on keratin 10.

Regarding “before sampling” conditions (Figure A,B), no specific staining was detected in the absence of cells on the glass slides, despite a weak background signal, which could result from some culture-medium components. As shown in Figure B, whatever the cell density (ranging from 100 to 1,000 cells), the chromogenic labeling of mannose-residue staining allows the visualization of cells on the glass slides as reddish-colored. It could be distinguished with the naked eye and it is also more accurately visible under the stereomicroscope, thus making it possible to identify residual fragments of cells. Rapid and current image processing could be applied to improve contrast and enhance cell detection (postprocessing, Figures and ).

Regarding “after sampling” conditions, the three control swabs (i.e., cells and no staining, cells and only washing steps, or no cells and staining) showed light yellow fibers (Figure C). A wash control was performed to verify whether cells were lost during this step. On swabs, after sampling various cell densities (ranging from 100 to 1,000 cells), chromogenic labeling highlighted the biological material trapped in the swabs as red-stained spots within fibers (Figure D). For a low cell density (100 cells), the red staining of fibers was only visible with a stereomicroscope. Finally, as shown in Figure E, no DNA degradation was observed during the genetic analysis of stained swabs, compared to control swabs (i.e., swabs containing sampled cells but without staining; washed or not), regardless of the sample cell density. This attested to the preservation of genetic material after the whole cytochemistry staining. For all initial cell densities, STR amplification enabled the exploitation of all DNA profiles from both control and labeled swabs in a similar way.

Thus, using Con A lectin as a probe allowed the detection of cells both on a glass slide and after their collection from a swab without affecting subsequent genetic analyses. Thanks to this visualization of mannose residues, we were able to visualize up to 27 cells/cm2 for 100 cells initially seeded. Even as cells were notably lost due to the experimental procedure (for example, drying step or cell adhesion on underlying plastic rather than on coverslip glass slides), it demonstrates the sensitivity of chromogenic labeling in identifying a small number of residual cells, as could happen in the field. By recognizing mannose residues, which are largely found on mammalian cells, this cytochemistry protocol enables an initial screening of surfaces before sampling or of swabs after sampling. A potential drawback, i.e., the nonspecific recognition of mannose residues from various organisms, could be overcome by the subsequent use of an amplification kit (e.g., GlobalFiler IQC PCR Amplification Kit, Thermo Fisher), specifically targeting human STR markers to eliminate any risk of foreign contamination.

As a complementary approach to increase specificity and improve the likelihood of detecting touch DNA samples, we also targeted cell-derived keratin 10 (Figure ). The latter is found in human skin, particularly in the stratum corneum of the epidermis. In “before sampling” conditions, there was no staining in controls without cells (Figure A), while cells (ranging from 100 to 1,000) were distinguished under low magnification thanks to their reddish coloration (Figure B). The stereomicroscope allowed for accurate observation (Figure B).

Regarding “after sampling” conditions (Figure C,D), the fibers of control swabs (after cell sampling and without staining) remained light yellow (Figure S1). As shown in Figure D, after sampling various initial cell densities (ranging from 100 to 1,000), a red staining of cell fragments within the fibers was visible under a stereomicroscope, with low magnification. This observation confirmed the presence of trapped biological material after sampling, even at a very low cell density. Additionally, no significant difference was observed in the quality of the DNA profiles between stained and unstained swabs, indicating that the staining procedure did not degrade the DNA, regardless of cell densities. Full STR marker amplification further supported the suitability of the labeling method for generating usable genetic profiles from all cell densities tested (Figure E).

Thus, residual keratinocyte cell fragments were detected on both glass slides and swabs, respectively, before and after sampling using the K10 probe. The latter did not affect forensic genetic analyses. As touch DNA samples include a mixture of corneocytes, free DNA, and cellular fragments from the keratinization process, the K10 probe could be useful to specifically detect skin-cell-derived fragments within invisible biological traces.

Together, these data validated mannose residues and keratin 10 as complementary targets to detect residual cell-derived fragments in invisible biological traces both before and after sampling as well as their effectiveness in colorimetry without compromising forensic genetic analyses. Indeed, after chromogenic labeling, usable DNA profiles compliant with French DNA database requirements (FNAEG) were obtained.

Combining Fluorescence and Chromogenic Labeling to Provide a Versatile System of Invisible Trace Detection

To take advantage of the chromogenic labeling of cell-derived fragments within touch DNA samples while keeping the benefits of fluorescent detection, we developed a dual labeling system combining fluorescent and chromogenic visualization processes. To this end, mannose residues and keratinocyte specific protein were targeted using this combined revelation method based on the simultaneous use of FITC-conjugated probes and HRP-conjugated anti-FITC antibody (Figures and ). In Figure , using a microscope (DM2000Led, Leica) for both probes (FITC and HRP-AEC), it was possible to detect the corneocytes and cellular fragments after labeling a fingermark with low magnification. More specifically, the same corneocytes (dotted squares on low magnification images) were observed using both the chromogenic (HRP-AEC) and the fluorescence (FITC) visualization, demonstrating that a single staining procedure could lead to the dual labeling of the same corneocyte. To take it further, in Figure , other observation devices better adapted to on-field conditions, such as a stereomicroscope and a digital microscope, were used to observe both chromogenic and fluorescent labeling. This enabled the simultaneous revelation of cell-derived fragments containing mannose residues and keratin by observing both FITC fluorescence and HRP-AEC chromogenic labeling at the same focal plane and magnification.

We decided to use FITC-conjugated probes; although other double approaches combining, for example, nanoparticles with metal-enhanced fluorescence and magnetic properties have been considered, to our knowledge, this combined cytochemistry-based detection procedure is original, easy to perform, and adaptable in the field for decision support. It is also versatile and can be adjusted to various field conditions and substrate properties.

Toward a Decision-Support Tool for In Situ Detection of Touch DNA Samples

To evaluate the relevance of this versatile detection system for the in situ detection of touch DNA samples in a “before sampling” scenario, fingermarks were deposited on substrates commonly found in crime scenes. While no signal was observed on control samples (no fingermark deposition), except a weak pink background, corneocytes and cellular fragments appeared darker after the colorimetric revelation of cell-derived mannose residues on a ceramic tile (Figure A). It made corneocytes and cell fragments visible to the naked eye and with even greater clarity when using the stereomicroscope and digital microscope. However, the revelation method was less informative when fingermarks were deposited on the metal (Figure B), mainly due to the opacity of the metal. A porous substrate, raw wood (Figure C), was also investigated. Despite a background signal, it was possible to reveal the presence of labeled corneocytes or cellular fragments, particularly through observation with the digital microscope. In contrast, fluorescent revelation allowed the visualization of corneocytes and cellular fragments on metal (Figure B, arrowheads for visualization example), whereas the detection was limited and less informative on white ceramic tile or raw wood (Figure A,C, arrowheads for visualization example), mainly due to the autofluorescence background of the substrate. Thus, both types of labeling are suitable for light and opaque substrates or porous substrates. As a result, the versatile support-decision tool we propose could guide sampling toward areas that are more likely to contain biological material and can adapt to the encountered substrate. Indeed, at crime scenes, various substrates can be encountered and Champion et al., showed that some of them are more suitable for fluorescence detection than others (cotton fabric compared to plastic or human tooth, respectively). Thus, a dual labeling system will offer significant benefits in the field. In fact, Chen et al. have demonstrated the benefits of such a dual read-out technique compared to the traditional methods for identifying fingerprints, such as nanopowders or high-resolution fluorescence, which provide the necessary resolution to identify the three levels required for fingerprint identification. In contrast, our tool specifically focuses on detecting biological material to improve the recovery of low DNA quantities from touch DNA samples.

Finally, considering an on-field “after sampling” scenario, fingermarks were deposited on the glass and then swabbed (Figure ). Keratin 10 was then labeled. In control experiments (no fingermark sampling), swab fibers appeared yellow, regardless of the detection system used – digital microscope or stereomicroscope (Figure S1). When a fingermark was collected, the swab fibers darkened, and red elements could be seen, indicating the presence of biological material. Targeting keratin 10 successfully detected fingermarks after swabbing and allowed the distinction between swabs with and without biological material. In contrast to the reduced chromogenic intensity observed on swabs that were sampled from in vitro keratinocyte cell models (Figure ), likely due to the low concentration of keratin 10 at this differentiation stage, an intense labeling was shown on swabs used for fingermark sampling. This demonstrated an abundance of keratin 10 in the corneocytes found in touch DNA samples. On swabs, chromogenic labeling appeared more appropriate due to the background fluorescence of the swabs’ fibers, particularly at their extremities, which could be observed even without fingermark cells sampling (Figure ). After fingermark sampling, in addition to the fibers’ intrinsic fluorescence, intense fluorescent spots appeared (arrowheads for visualization example), likely corresponding to cells trapped within the fiber structure. However, fiber autofluorescence limits accurate detection.

Detection of keratin 10 protein after collection of a fingermark on swabs with chromogenic or fluorescence revelation for use in a decision support tool post-sampling. Fingermarks were deposited and then collected by using a moistened swab. After drying, keratinocyte-specific protein was labeled on the entire swab, appearing either as a reddish precipitate (chromogenic visualization) or as green fluorescence (fluorescent visualization). Observations were performed using a stereomicroscope (S9D, Leica; with Flexacam C3 camera 0.5×, Leica) with a 5.5× lens (high magnification, scale bars 2 mm) or a portable digital microscope (AM4113ZT, Dino-Lite Universal) (200×, high magnification, scale bars 200 μm). Fluorescence observation was performed with a confocal microscope (LSM900, Zeiss) by using the excitation wavelength for FITC at 488 nm, and emission was collected between 500 and 560 nm.

Thus, to successfully reveal cell-derived fragments trapped in swabs after sampling of fingermarks, chromogenic labeling appears to be the more suitable choice. In the field, such a decision-support tool would allow investigators to check whether a swab has effectively collected human biological material, enabling them to prioritize it for DNA analyses. It works with flocked nylon fibers 4N6FLOQSwabs, which demonstrated superior efficiency in DNA extraction and recovery compared to cotton or rayon fiber swabs for touch DNA sample collection. , A previous study highlighted the impact of fiber autofluorescence on distinguishing swabs after sampling. The dual revelation method presented here can be applied to overcome this limitation.

This approach improves efficiency by ensuring that surfaces and swabs without detected biological material are not prioritized for collection and the analytical process. This allows for a focus on more relevant samples, ultimately saving time and money.

Conclusions

This study provides proof of concept for a versatile detection system combining fluorescence and chromogenic labeling, where the choice of visualization method is guided by the substrate’s background, whether light or opaque. This decision-support tool enables the targeting of fragments using both Concanavalin A for broad screening of cells (mostly mammalian cells) and keratin 10 for the specific screening of human-derived biological material. It also enables the identification of areas of interest in small surfaces in “before sampling” processes as well as swabs likely to have collected biological material after sampling multiple areas on a large crime scene. Here, we describe the first step in the proof of concept of a versatile decision-support tool that shows promise for forensic investigations. It could assist investigators in the field by reducing blind sampling and prioritizing areas or swabs likely to contain biological material for further analyses. As a result, it could increase the chances of obtaining usable DNA profiles from touch DNA samples, thereby contributing to the resolution of more criminal cases.
