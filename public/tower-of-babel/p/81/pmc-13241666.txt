Iron overload triggers pathological remodeling of corneal stroma through ferroptosis and senescence-associated secretory phenotype in keratoconus
Authors: Xiaoxue Liu, Shengqian Dou, Chaoqun Wei, Ting Liu, Xiaowen Zhang, Lei Zhang, Longfei Zhao, Wenlong Li, Qingdong Bao, Hua Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Keratoconus (KC) remains a leading cause of blindness among adolescents with no available pharmacotherapies to halt its progression clinically. While stromal thinning and cell loss are established pathological hallmarks, the underlying molecular mechanisms driving disease pathogenesis have been poorly understood. Motivated by the clinical presence of the iron-associated Fleischer's ring, we investigated the role of iron overload in KC pathogenesis. Our subsequent analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from human KC corneas ultimately revealed ferroptosis—an iron-dependent form of cell death—as the predominant mechanism underlying the loss of corneal stromal cells. Next, we confirmed significant iron accumulation and ferroptosis activation in both tissues and primary corneal stromal cells derived from KC patients. Further mechanistic exploration using transcriptomic profiling, pathway analysis and functional experiments revealed that iron overload induces not only ferroptosis but also cellular senescence, accompanied by a pronounced senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). The SASP profile featured elevated collagen degradation, inflammatory activation, and dysregulated fibrosis, these processes directly contributing to stromal thinning and biomechanical weakening in KC. Importantly, these pathological cascades were reversible upon treatment with the iron chelator deferoxamine (DFO). Overall, our study establishes a novel mechanism in which iron overload drives KC progression via ferroptosis and senescence with SASP, and highlights iron chelation therapy as a promising disease-modifying strategy for patients with KC.

Introduction

The cornea is a transparent component of the ocular wall and maintains the normal physiological structure and function of the eye. Keratoconus (KC) is the most common corneal ectatic disorder, characterized by progressive thinning and bulging of the cornea, leading to irregular astigmatism and reduced visual acuity [1]. KC typically presents in adolescence and is often diagnosed in the second or third decade of life. Progression is notably more aggressive in younger individuals, particularly under the age of 18 [2]. If the disease is not slowed, it will advance, leading to the grave outcomes of irreversible blindness and the eventual requirement for corneal transplantation [3,4], making early intervention critical. Epidemiological studies report a prevalence ranging from 1 in 2000 to 1 in 400 individuals [5]. Recent improvements in screening methodologies suggest that KC may be underdiagnosed in its early stages [2]. As a result, it has emerged as a leading cause of vision impairment and permanent vision loss among young adults worldwide. Currently, no pharmacological interventions are available to delay disease progression. In advanced cases, corneal transplantation remains the only therapeutic option, underscoring the need for continued research into effective treatment strategies [6].

KC is recognized as a complex multigenic disorder with multifactorial etiology, where genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, and endocrine dysregulation synergistically drive disease initiation. Due to multifactorial, multigenic, and lack of animal model, its pathophysiology is still an enigma [7,8]. Nevertheless, the most typical pathological feature of KC is keratocytes and extracellular matrix (ECM) loss that causes gradual thinning and steepening of the cornea. The major structural element comprising the corneal stroma is collagen, which accounts for approximately 70% of the corneal total dry weight. Studies have explored that differential expression of inflammatory markers, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), extracellular matrix proteins, and tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs) as central effectors of ectasia [5,9]. However, due to the challenges in obtaining suitable animal models, the intrinsic pathophysiological cascade driving ECM related pathologies remains elusive.

Although keratocytes (also known as corneal stromal cells, CSCs) represent only 3–5% of the stroma, they are specialized neural crest-derived cells responsible for producing and secreting the ECM and MMPs, thereby maintaining the cornea's architecture and transparency [10]. These cells remain quiescent in adults but can become activated and adopt a fibroblastic phenotype in response to corneal infections and injuries. Exposure of the vulnerable CSCs to external insults can trigger subsequent shape changes. Previous in vivo confocal microscopy has shown depleting CSC density from the anterior to posterior region of the corneal stroma in KC corneas compared to normals [11,12]. The death of CSCs leads to thinning of the corneal stroma. A comprehensive and in-depth exploration of the mode of CSC death in KC remains obscure.

It is well established that one of the clinical hallmarks of advanced KC is the Fleischer ring - a partial or complete iron-laden arc formed by hemosiderin deposition at the epithelial-stromal junction, classically attributed to tear film turbulence in areas of apical thinning [13,14]. Despite morphological changes of this ring with disease progression, its specific role in driving KC pathogenesis remains largely elusive. As an essential nutrient, cellular iron participates in multiple biological processes such as oxygen transport, mitochondrial respiration, and DNA synthesis. However, cells with excess iron accumulation sometimes exhibit increased oxidative stress, which damages proteins, lipids, and DNA [15]. Notably, ferroptosis, as an iron-dependent form of cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, has garnered significant research interest in recent years [16]. Our recent work demonstrated oxidative damage exacerbating KC progression [17], while proteomic analyses revealed dysregulation of iron-handling proteins in the KC stroma. In addition, some proteomic analyses also demonstrate iron-handling protein dysregulation in KC stroma [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. Furthermore, a recent study demonstrated altered ferroptosis markers in corneal epithelial cells from keratoconus patients, further implicating ferroptosis in KC pathogenesis [22]. Collectively, the presence of the iron-rich Fleischer ring, aberrant iron metabolism, oxidative damage, and emerging evidence of ferroptosis activation strongly suggest that iron-dependent ferroptosis contributes to the degenerative stromal remodeling in KC. Nevertheless, the specific molecular profiles of ferroptotic corneal stromal cells during KC progression and the translational potential of targeting ferroptosis for KC treatment remain largely uncharacterized, representing a critical research gap.

In this study, we reanalyzed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of human KC and normal corneas and revealed ferroptosis as the predominant cell death mode in CSCs. Integrating clinical observations with mechanistic investigations, we elucidated iron dysregulation in KC CSCs. To investigate how iron accumulation alters the phenotype of corneal stromal cells, we established iron-overloaded cell models combined with RNA sequencing and functional validation. Our results demonstrated that iron overload induces cell cycle arrest and cellular senescence, and triggers the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). SASP is characterized by elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), extensive lysosomal expansion, and abundant secretion of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and matrix-remodeling enzymes [23]. This cascade promotes collagen fiber degradation and aberrant elastic fiber deposition, ultimately leading to corneal biomechanical failure in KC. Critically, iron chelators emerged as novel therapeutics that abrogated KC progression by interrupting the SASP cascade.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomics reveals a prominent ferroptosis signature in KC

Our previous study employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to characterize cellular heterogeneity and molecular alterations in corneal tissues from individuals with keratoconus (KC) and healthy controls (Ctrl). The dataset comprises 39,214 cells obtained from the central corneal tissues (excluding the endothelium) of three KC patients following deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK), along with four control samples from healthy donors. Detailed sample information is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Notably, all three KC patients were at Amsler-Krumeich (AK) stage 4.

To determine which modes of cell death were predominantly enriched in KC, we first scored and compared five major classical cell death pathways—apoptosis, pyroptosis, necrosis, autophagy and ferroptosis—in CSCs using single-cell databases. Among these, the ferroptosis score was significantly higher than that in the others (Fig. 1a). Gene expression profiling revealed the upregulation of ferroptosis driver genes and a concurrent downregulation of anti-ferroptotic genes in KC compared to controls (Fig. 1b). Then, the representative genes for different cell death were selected and their expression levels was analyzed. As shown in Fig. 1c, the expression of ferroptosis-related genes was markedly changed in KC compared to normal controls, and significantly exceeded that of genes associated with other forms of cell death. Moreover, to investigate the mechanisms of cell death in KC from the perspective of metabolic end products, we conducted untargeted metabolomic profiling on corneal samples from both control and KC groups. The results similarly indicated enrichment of metabolic end products related to the ferroptosis pathway in KC samples (Fig. 1d). Significant iron accumulation is observed in CSCs of KC

While it has been conventionally thought that iron accumulation occurs primarily in the epithelial layer of KC corneas (Fleischer ring), whether iron overload exists in the corneal stroma and whether it induces subsequent pathological changes remains unclear. In the present study, we assessed iron accumulation in cornea tissues. Perl's Prussian blue staining was used to recognize ferric iron (Fe3+). As shown in Fig. 2a and b, analysis of iron staining results from three KC samples and three control samples revealed significantly greater iron deposition specifically within the stromal layer of KC corneas compared to controls. We obtained similar observations in the cornea tissues and found a significant increase of iron content in KC corneas by iron assay kit, which detects the overall iron levels in tissues (Fig. 2c). To directly assess intracellular Fe2+ levels in CSCs, we performed primary cells extraction from normal and KC cornea tissues. Compared with the normal human CSCs (HCSC), the CSCs in the KC tissue (KC-HCSC) are larger in size and present a spindle shape (Supplementary Fig. S1). We next assessed Fe2+ deposition in primary corneal stromal cells from both normal and KC samples using the FerroOrange probe. As shown in Fig. 2d, the fluorescence intensity of the FerroOrange probe was significant increased with CSCs of KC compared with normal groups. Cellular iron uptake is regulated by transferrin receptor protein 1 (TFRC) (Fig. 2e). We then explored whether the expression of TFRC was elavated in KC corneas. Hallmarks of ferroptosis are detected in KC CSCs

To directly address the association between ferroptosis and KC, we analyzed the expression of ferroptotic hallmarks in morphologically and histologically defined control and KC human corneas. It is well-established that ferroptosis is characterized by excessive intracellular accumulation of lipid peroxides. To investigate whether lipid peroxidation accumulates in CSCs of KC, we isolated primary CSCs from clinically normal and KC corneas and assessed them using the general ROS indicator, 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFDA). Primary stromal cells from KC corneas showed greater DCFDA signals than those in normal cornea stromal cells (Supplementary Fig. S2). To further decipher ferroptosis, we conducted loading of a fluorescent probe detecting intracellular lipid ROS, Liperfluo, on primary CSCs obtained from KC and normal corneas. Cornea stromal cells from KC showed stronger Liperfluo signals than normal stromal cells (Fig. 3a), revealing higher amounts of lipid peroxidation in KC. Ferroptotic cells have been reported to exhibit characteristic mitochondrial morphology under transmission electron microscopy (TEM). We performed TEM on ultrathin sections obtained from both clinical KC and normal corneas. Within the corneal stroma, cells are distributed between layers of interwoven collagen fibers. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is the essential phospholipid peroxidase responsible for reducing lipid peroxides, thereby protecting cells from ferroptosis. We then measured the levels of GPX4 to evaluate the anti-ferroptotic ability in KC. We found that the expression levels of GPX4 were remarkably decreased in the KC group (Fig. 3c). The antioxidant function of GPX4 depends on glutathione (GSH) as a reductant, with SLC7A11 (solute carrier family 7 member 11) serving as a core component in GSH synthesis. From western blotting, the KC group showed lower levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4 activity compared with that of normal group (Fig. 3d). Next, we examined the expression of canonical ferroptotic markers in the human corneas. Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) is an essential enzyme that synthesizes polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing phospholipids, the primary substrates for lipid peroxidation and subsequent ferroptosis. We found the ACSL4 was substantially increased in KC corneas (Fig. 3e). Ferritin is the main protein that sequesters iron in the cell, and increased expression has been associated with ferroptosis. Ferritin heavy chain, FTH1, was upregulated in KC corneas (Fig. 3f). In summary, our above results demonstrate that the accumulation of ACSL4 and its associated lipid peroxides is elevated in KC, while the intracellular major antioxidant system, the SLC7A11-GPX4 axis, is inactivated. This ultimately contributes to the occurrence of ferroptosis in KC (Fig. 3g).

Iron overload triggers pathological remodeling of the corneal stroma which can be rescued by DFO

Having demonstrated iron deposition and ferroptosis activation in keratoconus stromal cells, we thus sought to determine whether iron accumulation acts as a bystander phenomenon or a driver of KC pathological progression. To address this question, we established a primary corneal stromal cell iron overload model and performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on three experimental groups: control, iron overload, and DFO (an iron chelator) treatment groups. We performed focused pairwise comparisons between the iron overload (Iron) versus negative control group (Ctrl), and between the DFO-treated (DFO) versus iron overload groups. An integrative analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed (|Fold change| > 1, and q-value <0.05). As shown in Supplementary Fig. S3a, hierarchical clustering of RNA-seq data revealed distinct gene expression profiles between the Ctrl and Iron groups, as well as between the DFO-treated and Iron groups. Finally, 1882 DEGs were identified in Iron vs. Ctrl group, including 1418 downregulated and 464 upregulated genes. 3967 DEGs were identified in DFO vs. Iron group, including 1581 downregulated and 2386 upregulated genes (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. S3b). To elucidate the injury mechanisms of iron overload in corneal stromal cells, we focused on the Iron versus Ctrl group comparison. Next, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses identified pathways significantly upregulated or downregulated in iron-overloaded stromal cells versus controls. As shown in Fig. 4c, iron overload treatment upregulated pathways associated with collagen degradation (e.g., extracellular matrix disassembly, collagen catabolic process), inflammation (e.g., cellular response to interleukin-1, response to chemokine), and fibrosis (e.g., transforming growth factor beta binding, fibronectin binding) compared with Ctrl group. These pathways were downregulated in DFO group compared with Iron group (Supplementary Fig. S4). In contrast, downregulated pathways in Iron group were predominantly enriched in developmental and growth-related processes such as cell cycle regulation while these pathways were upregulated in DFO group (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. S5). The PPI analysis also revealed that in the Iron group compared to the control group, the upregulated gene modules were enriched in collagen degradation and inflammation, while the downregulated gene modules were associated with cell growth (Supplementary Fig. S6). In contrast, the DFO group compared to the Iron treatment group showed an opposite trend (Supplementary Fig. S7). Collectively, these results demonstrate that iron overload induces cell cycle arrest in corneal stromal cells while upregulating collagen degradation, inflammation, and fibrosis-related pathways.

Building on GO/KEGG pathway signatures, we then deployed Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) to reconstruct the causal machinery underlying iron-evoked corneal stroma remodeling. IPA results demonstrated that iron overload significantly activated disease-associated pathways, including collagen degradation, inflammatory activation and cellular senescence (Fig. 4e). These pathways were markedly suppressed by DFO treatment (Fig. 4f). Intriguingly, while fibrotic pathway signaling by TGF-β receptor complex was strongly activated, elastic fibre formation also showed significant activation (Fig. 4e). This suggests iron promotes abnormal fibrotic deposition. Conversely, iron overload potently inhibited growth-related pathways and extracellular matrix formation processes. These pathways were robustly reactivated in the DFO intervention group. Meanwhile, we observed significant suppression of both ferroptotic pathways and iron uptake signaling in the DFO-treated group (Fig. 4e and f).

Iron accumulation provokes DNA damage and cell senescence in CSCs of KC

Based on the RNA sequencing data above, iron treatment demonstrated a strong association with cell cycle arrest and cellular senescence. To further validate this finding, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). The results revealed that the top five significantly downregulated pathways in the iron-treated group were all cell cycle-related pathways from the GO database (Fig. 5a), which were upregulated in DFO rescued groups (Fig. 5b). GSEA analysis of the hallmark gene sets revealed that the most significantly upregulated pathways following iron treatment included the p53 pathway, suggesting potential induction of cellular senescence (Fig. 5c). Considering the strong association between iron and cellular senescence in our data, we tested whether cell senescence occurred in KC. As shown in Fig. 5d, a remarkable elevation of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-GAL) signal were detected in KC samples. To gain mechanistic insight into the connection between iron and senescence, we explored the effects of iron on in vitro primary cells. We found iron triggered a remarkable elevation of SA-β-GAL signal, most of which were rescued by DFO (Fig. 5e and f). Consistent with observations in clinical samples, primary corneal stromal cells derived from KC patients exhibited significantly elevated SA-β-Gal staining, further reinforcing the phenomenon of cellular senescence in KC stromal cells. Next, we examined the expression of key cell cycle damage markers, specifically P21 (a downstream effector of the p53 pathway) and the DNA double-strand break marker phosphorylated histone H2AX (γH2AX), across distinct cell populations. As shown in Fig. 5g and h, Western blot analysis revealed elevated expression levels of P21 and γH2AX in iron-treated CSCs. In contrast, DFO-treated cells exhibited reduced expression of both markers. Notably, primary CSCs derived from KC patients displayed expression patterns comparable to those observed in the iron-treated group. Immunofluorescence assays also revealed increased expression of γH2AX in corneal stromal cells within clinical samples of KC (Supplementary Fig. S8). Together, we conclude that iron administration is sufficient to initiate the hallmarks of cell damage, including DNA damage, cell cycle arrest and senescence.

Iron accumulation in senescent CSCs drives the SASP

We subsequently explored how iron accumulation mediates cellular damage and senescence, thereby promoting KC disease development. RNA-seq analysis revealed elevated transcript levels of most senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors in iron-treated cells, which were significantly rescued by DFO treatment (Fig. 6a). Given that ROS is a well-established inducer of SASP, we examined whether iron overload similarly elevates cellular ROS. As demonstrated in Fig. 6b, iron treatment markedly increased intracellular ROS levels. This iron-induced ROS accumulation was significantly attenuated by DFO co-treatment in CSCs. Notably, the level of ROS was also significantly elevated in primary stromal cells isolated from KC corneas compared to normal stromal cells (Fig. 6b). Lysosomal volume quantification revealed characteristic enlargement, a known SASP-associated phenotype. Lysosome-specific fluorescent tracker (LysoTracker) staining demonstrated comparable lysosomal expansion in both iron-overloaded normal stromal cells and untreated primary KC CSCs. These pathological changes were effectively reversed by iron chelation (Fig. 6c), indicating that iron is the key mediator. TEM further revealed this expansion of lysosomal mass containing abundant ferritin-bound iron in both primary KC CSCs and iron-treated normal CSCs (Fig. 6d). Beyond the upregulation of cell cycle inhibitors, elevated ROS, massive expansion of the lysosomal compartment, and hypersecretion of interleukins, cytokines, and chemokines, a key additional phenotype of SASP involves upregulation of ECM-remodeling enzymes. Our transcriptome profiling revealed that the majority of ECM-degrading enzymes (notably Matrix Metalloproteinase, MMPs, cathepsin D/CTSD, and cathepsin K/CTSK) were significantly upregulated following iron treatment, whereas iron chelation downregulated their expression in CSCs (Fig. 6e). Western blot analysis revealed a parallel increase in matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP3) expression in both iron-treated cells and primary KC-CSCs (Fig. 6f and g), which was further corroborated by tissue immunofluorescence staining (Supplementary Fig. S9). Other canonical inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, also exhibited this pattern (Fig. 6f and g). Iron overload activates ferroptosis and abnormal collagen deposition in CSCs of KC

Given the observed iron overload pathology in KC stromal cells, we next tested the hypothesis that excessive iron triggers ferroptosis in this cellular context. We analyzed enrichment of a ferroptosis-related gene signature in transcriptomes from control, iron-overloaded, and DFO-treated groups using our RNA-seq dataset. Most ferroptosis-promoting genes were upregulated in the iron-overloaded group but downregulated in the DFO-treated group, while ferroptosis-suppressing genes exhibited the opposite expression pattern (Supplementary Fig. S10). Similarly, TEM showed ferroptotic morphology (mitochondrial shrinkage and cristae fragmentation) in iron-loaded cells, reversed by DFO treatment (Fig. 7a). Protein expression analysis revealed significant upregulation of both iron storage proteins (FTH1, FTL) and the iron transporter TFRC in iron-overload group, concurrent with marked downregulation of the primary antioxidant enzyme GPX4 (Fig. 7b and c, Supplementary Fig. S11). Iron chelation rescued these phenotypic changes. It is well known that the primary pathological hallmark of KC is corneal thinning. Our RNA-seq and IPA data revealed that beyond elevated MMPs, iron treatment significantly upregulated fibrosis-associated pathways, such as TGF-β binding signaling. Intriguingly, we also observed substantial upregulation of elastic fiber formation pathways, which can be rescued by DFO. Heatmap analysis similarly demonstrated upregulation of elastic fiber formation-related genes in iron-treated cells, which was reversed by DFO treatment (Supplementary Fig. S12). We performed Elastic Van Gieson (EVG) staining, capable of simultaneously staining collagen and elastic fibers, on sections of normal and KC corneas. The results revealed that red-stained collagen fibers were significantly reduced in the stromal region of KC corneas, while purple-black elastic fibers were increased (Fig. 7d and f). Additionally, PAI-1 (SERPINE1), a master regulator of collagen deposition in fibrotic diseases, was significantly upregulated in both the iron overload group and the KC CSC group. FN (Fibronectin) and α-SMA (α-smooth muscle actin), two classic biomarkers of tissue fibrosis, were also found to be significantly upregulated in the same pattern (Fig. 7e and g and Supplementary Fig. S13). In summary, our results collectively demonstrate that iron overload induces atypical ECM remodeling in KC, not only including increased collagen-degrading proteins but also paradoxical accumulation of elastic fibers and aberrant fibrillar deposition.

Discussion

Keratoconus (KC) is a degenerative condition characterized by the loss of corneal cells and extracellular matrix (ECM), leading to progressive corneal thinning, steepening, and visual impairment. Collagen cross-linking with ultraviolet light can enhance ECM strength and slow corneal weakening, though severe cases often require corneal transplantation [10]. Currently, there is no cure for KC. Numerous studies are underway to uncover the molecular alterations underlying the corneal phenotype in KC, with the goals of identifying candidate genes and developing targeted therapeutic strategies. These investigations have revealed several pathological changes and their potential causes in KC [5]. A general consensus in the field posits KC as a multifactorial and multigenic disorder, with the loss of corneal cells and tissue representing a core pathological feature. Recent studies further suggest dysregulation in oxidative stress responses, ECM homeostasis, corneal biomechanics, and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity [17,[24], [25], [26]]. As KC advances, tissue loss continues, resulting in a cornea that is less rigid and less homogeneous compared to that of healthy individuals. Disease progression involves a complex interplay of simultaneous destructive and reparative processes. Emerging evidence indicates that fibrotic markers, such as transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1), contribute to fibrosis in KC [27]. However, the mechanisms driving corneal scar formation remain poorly understood. Moreover, although KC has historically been regarded as a non-inflammatory disease, recent mechanistic studies increasingly implicate inflammatory processes.

The absence of well-established animal models for KC has constrained research largely to individual donor corneal samples. Consequently, most findings to date remain fragmented, revealing isolated pathological phenomena without a unified mechanistic understanding [1]. The dynamic processes driving keratoconus pathogenesis are still difficult to decipher and remain incompletely characterized. Critical questions persist regarding how oxidative stress, MMP dysregulation, inflammation, and fibrosis arise and interrelate. In 2022, our team provided the first single-cell RNA sequencing analysis of keratoconic corneas, delineating the single-cell landscape of KC and establishing the central role of corneal stromal cells in disease pathogenesis. We identified disruptions in biomechanical homeostasis, dysregulated cell–cell communication, and inflammatory activation in KC [28]. Building on this prior single-cell analysis, the present study investigates the notable clinical phenomenon of iron deposition in KC. We explore how iron overload influences corneal stromal cell states and drives the pathological progression toward ECM degradation, inflammation, and fibrosis (Fig. 8). Degenerative diseases arise when excessive cell death occurs [29]. In KC, the phenomenon of cell reduction contrasts with the general observation of active cell proliferation during adolescence, suggesting that cell death exceeds proliferation. Although existing studies have confirmed the presence of apoptosis in the progression of KC [30], it does not play a dominant role. We initially examined several common modes of cell death using single-cell RNA sequencing data from clinical KC samples. Our analysis demonstrated that ferroptosis exhibited significantly higher enrichment scores compared to other forms of cell death. Consistent with this, metabolomic profiling also revealed notable enrichment of the ferroptosis pathway in KC. These findings suggest that ferroptosis may represent the primary mechanism of corneal stromal cell (CSC) death in KC.

Ferroptosis is a novel form of regulated cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, and it has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various degenerative diseases [17]. Different from other types of cell death, ferroptosis possesses distinctive morphological and mechanistical features; for example, unlike executioner protein-centered apoptosis and necroptosis, lipid peroxides accumulation is the cardinal feature of ferroptosis [31]. In our previous investigation into the role of oxidative stress in KC [18], we identified and validated a marked increase in 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) expression—a key lipid peroxidation product and established marker of ferroptosis. In the present study, we further confirmed the presence of lipid peroxidation in primary CSCs derived from KC patients and provided additional evidence for ferroptosis through Liperfluo staining. Transmission electron microscope (TEM) observation shown the mitochondrial damage in KC CSCs. The concomitant downregulation of the key anti-ferroptotic axis (solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11)-glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4)) and upregulation of pro-ferroptotic molecules (e.g., acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4)) provide a molecular foundation for the iron-induced ferroptosis.Consistent with our findings in corneal stromal cells, a recent study by Demirkiran et al. demonstrated that GPX4 levels were decreased, whereas ACSL4 and malondialdehyde levels were increased in corneal epithelial cells from keratoconus patients [22], further supporting that ferroptosis activation is a shared feature across different corneal cell types in KC pathogenesis. While Wu et al. reported associations between ferroptosis-related genes and the corneal epithelium in KC using mRNA microarray data [21], and Gao et al. identified and validated ferroptosis-related genes via bioinformatics approaches [20], Cai et al. further elucidated mitochondrial dysfunction in KC and highlighted ACSL4 as a key biomarker [19]. Taken together, building upon these findings, our study initiated with single-cell data analysis from clinical samples and employed primary corneal stromal cells along with patient-derived tissues to substantiate the occurrence of ferroptosis through multiple experimental modalities.

Iron plays an essential role in human life, iron overload has been reported to induce lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, leading to ferroptosis. Superficial iron deposits observed in the corneal epithelium is one of typical clinical characteristic in KC. Previous studies have suggested that it is caused by iron deposits in the tear fluid accumulating in the unstable areas of the tear membrane [13,14,32,33]. The Fleischer ring undergoes morphological changes as the disease progresses, but whether it promotes the disease development remains unclear. In this study, we observed iron staining in both the epithelium and stroma of the KC. This is the first time that iron deposition in the stroma of KC has been clearly identified. Additionally, using primary corneal stromal cell iron staining and a tissue iron detection kit, we confirmed excessive intracellular iron in the CSCs of KC. TFRC is a vital molecule related to iron uptake. In this study, we found that both ferrous and ferric iron were highly expressed in KC, and the main transporter transferrin receptor (TFRC) also showed an upward trend. Combined with previous literature reporting elevated iron-related proteins in the tear fluid of KC patients, we hypothesize that excessive iron in the tear fluid of KC patients deposits in the corneal epithelium and is transported to the corneal stroma. The excessive iron overload in CSCs leads to further alterations in cell state, resulting in reduced collagen synthesis and increased degradation, thereby causing corneal degeneration. In orthopedic research, iron deposition in chondrocytes has been shown to increase catabolism and decrease anabolism, leading to degenerative changes in bone tissue, which is similar with our hypothesis in this study [34].

We then further investigated how iron overload influences changes in cell state and subsequently contributes to the pathological alterations associated with KC. We identified that the pathways enriched after iron overload were primarily associated with growth arrest, cellular senescence, matrix degradation, fibrosis, and inflammation. Notably, these iron-induced changes in cell state were highly consistent with the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), aligning with recent literature reports [35]. Subsequently, we validated these findings through various biological experiments. We confirmed the presence of cellular senescence in primary stromal cells of KC and clinical corneal tissue sections, observing a significant increase in the expression of senescence-related markers p21 and gamma-H2AX (γH2AX). Lysosomal electron microscopy revealed that iron overload led to enlarged lysosomes in CSCs, along with increased expression of MMP and inflammatory indicators, validating that iron overload induces SASP in these cells. This SASP creates a deleterious microenvironment that promotes the catabolic breakdown of collagenous ECM, directly linking iron accumulation to the destructive remodeling that defines KC. The elevated levels of ECM-degrading enzymes suggest enhanced collagen degradation and corneal thinning, which is consistent with our previous single-cell RNA sequencing research on KC. Although KC was initially considered a non-inflammatory degenerative disorder, growing evidence in recent years indicates that inflammation plays a role in disease progression [24,36,37]. Our study demonstrates that iron overload triggers inflammation and further pathological changes.

Intriguingly, our data reveal that iron-driven pathology in KC is not solely destructive but also involves a maladaptive repair response. Normally quiescent, CSCs maintain the slow turnover of the connective tissue matrix. They can be activated by corneal injury, trauma, or infection, prompting their differentiation into myofibroblasts. Pathway analysis indicated a paradoxical activation of both matrix degradation and pro-fibrotic pathways, including TGF-β signaling and elastic fiber formation. This was confirmed by the increased deposition of abnormal elastic fibers and the upregulation of fibrotic markers —plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), fibronectin (FN), and alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) — in KC corneas. PAI-1, a master regulator of collagen deposition in fibrotic diseases. When the level of PAI-1 increases, the degradation process of fibrin is inhibited, resulting in the accumulation of fibrin. This accumulation not only affects the balance of coagulation but is also crucial in extracellular matrix remodeling and fibrosis [38,39]. Thus, in our study, iron overload orchestrates a dual insult: simultaneous collagen degradation and aberrant fibrotic protein production, culminating in a biomechanically incompetent corneal stroma. Therapeutically, our findings position iron chelation as a promising strategy to intercept this pathogenic cascade. The iron chelator deferoxamine (DFO) effectively reversed all major facets of the disease phenotype in our models—it mitigated intracellular iron levels, suppressed ferroptosis, alleviated DNA damage and senescence, quenched the SASP, and prevented abnormal ECM remodeling. This compelling rescue effect underscores the central role of iron and highlights the potential for repurposing iron chelators to delay or halt KC progression. Ultimately, this study redefines KC pathophysiology through the lens of iron dysregulation, providing a unified mechanism for cellular loss and stromal weakening and opening new avenues for much-needed pharmacological interventions.

Overall, our research, starting from single-cell data, identified ferroptosis as the primary mode of death in corneal stromal cells and confirmed the phenomenon of ferroptosis in KC. We confirmed the occurrence of ferroptosis in keratoconus and linked the clinical observation of iron deposition to altered states of these cells. Specifically, iron overload was found to trigger a series of senescent changes in corneal stromal cells, inducing the SASP. This includes increased expression of matrix-degrading enzymes, inflammation, and the development of fibrosis. These findings indicate that fibrosis in KC is a complex condition-while collagen fibers decrease, elastic fibers increase. These alterations align with the clinical manifestations of corneal thinning and softening. Importantly, our research demonstrates that iron chelators can reverse these cellular changes, suggesting a potential therapeutic strategy for KC.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All human tissues were obtained from the Eye Hospital of Shandong First Medical University (Jinan, Shandong, China). The samples included: (1) refractive lenticules derived from small incision lenticule extraction (SMILE) procedures of non-keratoconus (non-KC) patients; (2) corneal tissues from patients diagnosed with keratoconus; and (3) corneal tissues from healthy donors. All samples were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee (Approval No.: SDSYKYY202101-4; Approval date: January 5, 2021). Healthy corneas obtained from the National Disease Research Interchange (NDRI) met the inclusion criteria: no KC diagnosis, no ocular, corneal, or systemic disease. KC patients were required to have a documented KC diagnosis by a board-certified ophthalmologist and no other ocular disease. Patients with a history of corneal cross-linking (CXL), deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK), or penetrating keratoplasty (PKP) were excluded from this study.

Human samples for single-cell RNA sequencing

The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-Seq) data using 10× Genomics technology were obtained from our previous study(Dou et al., 2022) and sourced from the HRA000728 dataset available in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) for Human (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa-human/browse/HRA000728).

Three central cornea tissues (lacking endothelium) from KC patients after DALK surgeries, and four control samples from healthy donors were involved.

Isolation and culture of human corneal stromal cells

HCSCs and KC-HCSC were respectively derived from SMILE lenticules of non-KC patients and corneal stroma of KC patients obtained from the Eye Hospital of Shandong First Medical University. Following rinsing in sterile phosphate buffered saline (PBS), the lenticules were dissociated in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium/F12 (DMEM/F12) medium (Invitrogen, CA, USA) containing 0.25 mg/mL Liberase TL (5401020001, Roche, Mannheim, Germany) and 0.5 mg/mL DNase I (10104159001, Roche) for 2-3h at 37 °C. Thereafter, the cells were collected by centrifugation. Following centrifugation, the cells were cultured in a medium comprising DMEM/F12, 5 % fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin and incubated at 37 °C, 5% carbon dioxide (CO2).

Perl's Prussian blue staining

To assessed ferric iron (Fe3+) accumulation, paraffin-embedded corneal tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm, and routinely dewaxed and rehydrated. According to the manufacturer's instructions, the sections were sequentially incubated with Perl's staining working solution, followed by incubation solution and enhancer working solution. After washing with distilled water, the sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Observation and image acquisition were performed under an optical microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Image analysis was carried out using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Protein extraction and western blotting analysis

The collected cells and corneal samples were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Absin, Shanghai, China) supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Absin) and phosphatase inhibitors (Absin, Shanghai, China). Cells were treated with ferrous sulfate (FeSO4, F8633, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and iron chelator deferoxamine (DFO, SJ-MX4675, Sparkjade, China) before sample lysis. Total protein was separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membrane was incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an ultra-sensitive chemiluminescent assay kit (Epizyme, Shanghai, China) on a ChemiDoc Touch imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), and quantified with Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Immunofluorescence staining

Corneal samples were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura, Torrance, CA, USA) and sectioned into 6 μm slices. The sections were incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. Secondary antibodies labeled with fluorescent dyes were applied and incubated for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were observed and photographed under a fluorescence confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The primary antibodies used are listed in Table 1.

Quantification of iron in tissue

Quantification of iron in corneal tissues was measured using an iron assay kit (Cat. No.: A039-2-1; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

FerroOrange staining

To directly assess intracellular Fe2+ levels in HCSCs, primary HCSCs extracted from normal and KC patients incubated with 1 μmol/L FerroOrange working solution (F374, DOJINDO, Japan) for 30 min. After washing with PBS, Images were observed and photographed under a fluorescence confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Detection of cellular lipid peroxides

To assessed lipid peroxides level in cellular, primary HCSCs extracted from normal and KC patients were stained by liperfluo kit (L248, DOJINDO, Japan). Briefly, primary HCSCs incubated with 1 μmol/L liperfluo solution at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing with PBS, Images were observed and photographed under a fluorescence confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Flow cytometry analysis

Primary HCSCs isolated from normal patients were randomly divided into three groups: Control (no treatment), Iron (treated with 500 μM Fe(II)sulfate (F8633, Sigma,USA)), and Iron + DFO (treated with 500 μM Fe(II)sulfate + 1 mM deferoxamine (HY–B1625, MedChemexpress, USA)). After 24 h of culture, cells were harvested and subjected to analysis of reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and lysosomal content using flow cytometry. Total ROS was measured using a total ROS detection kit (S0033S, Beyotime, China) and lysosomal mass was assessed using a LysoTracker dye (L12492, Invitrogen, USA), all according to the manufacturer's instructions. Samples were acquired on a Gallios flow-cytometer (Beckman Coulter) and acquired data were analyzed with FlowJo software (Tree Star).

Transmission electron microscopy

Briefly, treated cells were collected into a centrifuge tube and separated at 3000g for 8 min. Then, the cells were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (v/v) in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.2) and osmium tetroxide (1%). The samples were then cut and stained with 2% uranylacetate (UA), dehydrated in acetone solutions at increasing concentrations, embedded in an epoxy resin and processed for TEM. Ultrastructural images were then captured with a transmission electron microscope (H-7700, Hitachi High-Technologies, Tokyo, Japan) using a 100 kV accelerating voltage.

Immunohistochemical staining

Fresh corneal tissues from normal or KC patients were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded by paraffin, then sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm, and routinely dewaxed and rehydrated. After permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, the sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Slides were subsequently incubated with the primary antibodies (the primary antibodies used are listed in Table 1). The slides were washed with phosphate-buffered saline containing Tween-20 (PBST), and a Universal Mouse/Rabbit Enhanced Polymer Two-Step Detection Kit (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) was applied. Then, staining was performed with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine. The samples were dehydrated in graded ethanol. Observation and image acquisition were performed under an optical microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Image analysis was carried out using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

SA-β-GAL staining

For senescence-associated β-galactosidase staining, cells were fixed in an SA-β-GAL fixation solution (C0602-1, Beyotime, China) for 15 min at room temperature and washed thrice with PBS. Subsequently, 1 ml staining solution was added (C0602, Beyotime, China). The incubation took place overnight at 37 °C in a CO2-free incubator. Observation and image acquisition were performed under an optical microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Image analysis was carried out using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Verhoeff's Van Gieson (EVG) staining

To evaluate the content of elastic fibers in the corneal tissue of patients with KC, paraffin-embedded corneal tissues were stained by the EVG staining kit (G1597, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Briefly, corneal tissues sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm, and routinely dewaxed. The sections were sequentially incubated with reagent C, A, and B, rinsed with distilled water, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene twice, and finally mounted with neutral balsam. Observation and image acquisition were performed under an optical microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and plotting were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 software (GraphPad Software, USA). That the metric data for each detection index followed a normal distribution were verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Metric data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was used for overall comparison of each group. Using least significant difference-t tests for multiple comparisons between groups and a two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test for comparisons between two groups with normal distribution, and the two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test for comparisons between two groups with non-normal distribution. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Independent replication experiments have been conducted more than three times.
