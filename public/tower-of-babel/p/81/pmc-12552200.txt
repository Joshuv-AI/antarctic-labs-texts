Adolescents’ adherence to intraoral removable appliances: a scoping review
Authors: Akriti Tiwari, Nathalia Carolina Fernandes Fagundes, Arnaldo Perez-Garcia, Carlos Flores-Mir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescents’ lack of adherence to intra-oral removable appliances (IORAs) can lead to poor treatment outcomes. Available literature on this topic hasn’t been comprehensively summarized. Existing reviews often combine data from various age groups, lack a standardized conceptual definition of adherence to IORAs, and offer limited evaluation of interventions aimed at improving adherence among adolescents. This scoping review aims to map the breadth and depth of the literature on adolescents’ adherence to IORAs and identify research gaps for developing tailored interventions.MethodsIn accordance with Arksey and O’Malley’s framework and PRISMA-ScR guidelines, a comprehensive search strategy was implemented across MEDLINE, CINAHL, Web of Science, and ProQuest Theses and Dissertations. Two researchers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. A pilot-tested form facilitated data extraction. Risk of bias was evaluated using RoB 2, ROBINS-I, and JBI tools. Extracted data then underwent both descriptive and content analysis.ResultsThe 14 included studies focused on adherence to wear-time recommendations (n = 13), with only one examining appointment adherence. Only seven studies (50%) were deemed high-quality. Adherence rates varied across IORAs: vacuum-formed retainers showed wear-time of less than 24 h/day, Hawley retainers averaged 12–15 h/day, Twin Blocks averaged 21 h/day, and Activators averaged 11.5 h/day. Studies pursued various aims: four explored patient perspectives and experiences; one assessed designed interventions; two evaluated treatment adherence and assessed adherence to both recommendations and appointments; and five identified associated factors. Several studies pursued multiple aims. Patient perspectives highlighted challenges like discomfort and social pressures, and enablers such as parental support. Limited intervention studies indicated potential benefits of digital and image-based monitoring. Adherence consistently fell short of recommended daily wear time (DWT), often influenced by motivation, parental involvement, and psychosocial factors.ConclusionAdherence among adolescents to the DWT for intraoral removable appliances is frequently suboptimal, displaying considerable variability depending on appliance type. Both individual and social determinants influence adherence. Robust study designs and a standardized conceptualization of adherence are imperative to generate high-quality, comparable evidence. Evidence-based interventions must be developed and tested to enhance adherence and ultimately optimize treatment outcomes within this patient population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40510-025-00589-9.Keywords: Adolescents, Malocclusion, Intra-oral removable appliances, Adherence, Patients’ experiences, Treatment adherence, Adherence interventions, Adherence-associated factors

Introduction

Malocclusion is a deviation of teeth from the ideal occlusion [1]. It is caused due to an interplay of genetic and environmental factors [2]. It is often first recognized during the mixed dentition stage, typically between the ages of 6 and 12 [3].

It is a highly prevalent condition, with previous studies reporting a range between 39% and 93% [2]. Globally, the prevalence among children and adolescents is estimated to be around 56% [4]. Research suggests that specific malocclusion traits may increase the risk for speech impairments, eating difficulties, and progressive changes in facial structure and the oral cavity [5].

There is a growing need for orthodontic care among adolescents. A recent study evaluating the demand for orthodontic treatment in young adolescents revealed that approximately 55% of adolescents expressed a high demand for orthodontic treatment due to functional, cosmetic, psychological, or social reasons [6]. More than two-thirds (71%) of parents have reported a perceived need for orthodontic services among their adolescent children [7].

Timely intervention with appliances, such as intra-oral removable appliances (IORAs), can prevent malocclusion development and greatly improve occlusion by 95% if worn as recommended [8]. IORAs are commonly used due to their ease of fabrication, adjustment, affordability, removability, and reduced chair-side time [9, 10]. These appliances are preferred due to their removability and comfort [10]. These appliances have become a preferred choice among adolescents due to their excellent aesthetics and convenience [11]. Research has revealed that their prevalence varies geographically; for example, Krey and Hirsch found approximately 16% of 11–14-year-olds in Germany used IORAs [12], while in England, approximately 28% of 12-year-olds and 18% of 15-year-olds were recommended to wear these appliances [13].

Despite the relative effectiveness of IORAs, adherence to these interventions remains an issue of concern. The success of IORAs relies heavily on the patient’s commitment to following treatment recommendations and appointment-keeping. While clinically effective in managing malocclusion, studies show that approximately 50% of adolescents fail to follow wear-time recommendations [14]. Lack of adherence leads to extended treatment duration, reduced efficacy, patient and clinician burnout, and financial burdens on families and healthcare systems [15].

Further research is needed to uncover the complexity of adherence to IORAs among adolescents. Research aimed at understanding adherence to IORAs such as clear aligners, has primarily focused on patient, appliance, and clinician-related factors [16]. Available evidence suggests that factors including patient age, personality traits, perceived aesthetic importance, discomfort, appliance visibility, and effective clinician communication are associated with adherence [17]. Data regarding the role of other factors, such as managing non-cooperation and strategies for enhancing adherence to optimize treatment outcomes remain insufficient or contradictory [16].

The available literature on this topic has yet to be comprehensively summarized. Existing reviews often combine data from various age groups (children, adolescents, and adults), lack a standardized, conceptual definition of adherence to intra-oral removable appliances among adolescents, and offer limited evaluation of interventions aimed at improving adherence among adolescents. While a recent scoping review on patient adherence in orthodontics comprehensively describes the literature across children, adolescents, and adults, and covers both adherence to fixed and removable orthodontic treatment [18], it did not explicitly address adherence to IORAs among adolescents. Therefore, the current scoping review aims to map the breadth and depth of exclusively adolescents’ adherence to IORAs. Scoping reviews are well-suited for providing insights into the volume, nature, and scope of available literature, making them ideal for addressing this gap [19]. Study findings will offer valuable insights into adherence behaviours among adolescents, and guide future research in developing evidence-based strategies to improve adherence. These findings could inform clinical practices and enhance treatment outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This scoping review follows the framework established by Arksey and O’Malley, which comprises five key stages: (1) identifying the research questions, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) study selection, (4) charting the data, and (5) collating, summarizing, and reporting the results [19]. This approach allows us to describe research activity on the topic of interest and identify gaps in the existing literature. Additionally, it followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [20].

Protocol registration

The protocol for this scoping review was registered in the Open Science Framework (DOI 10.17605/OSF.IO/YFM9R) in 2024 by AT [21].

Identifying the research questions

Our scoping review seeks to answer the following questions:

i.What are the key concepts and definitions in the available literature regarding adherence to IORAs among adolescents aged 10 to 19 years?

The search strategy for MEDLINE was developed by two researchers (AT and NF) in collaboration with a librarian (LT) at the University of Alberta. This strategy was subsequently adapted for each database included in the review by AT. Search terms used in each database are presented in Table 1.

Study selection

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were based on the research questions formulated. There were no restrictions applied to the date range, language, research inquiry (quantitative, qualitative, or mixed methods), and study design. Exclusion criteria included studies reporting on fixed appliances among children and adolescents, studies focusing on adherence among adults, and studies where full-text articles were unavailable. The search results were exported to Covidence by researcher (AT). Covidence identified duplicate studies and removed them. Two phases of screening of eligible articles were independently performed by two researchers (AT and NF). AT and NF screened the reference lists of the included studies for potential inclusion. The first phase involved screening titles and abstracts, followed by full-text screening to identify eligible studies. Discrepancies in study selection were resolved through consensus achieved by discussion or, if required, in consultation with two additional researchers (AP and CF). The reasons for the exclusion of full-text articles were documented in Covidence and are shown in Fig. 1.

Charting the data

A standardized data extraction form guided by the review questions was developed and pilot tested with 5 studies by AT and NF. The data extraction encompassed several distinct categories, each with various aspects. Study identification included details such as authors, title, journal, publication year, country, digital object identifier, and geographical area. Similarly, study characteristics comprised aims/objectives, type of research, study design, setting, type of participants, funding source, and quality assessment. Another category, population characteristics, captured sample size and sex. Appliance was a standalone category. The definition of the phenomenon included the concept itself and its definition. Adherence was a category that involved the type of adherence, recommended wear period, follow-up period, and assessment method. Finally, study results encompassed the rate of adherence, associated factors, effectiveness of intervention, perceived challenges, and recommendations for improvement. This form was developed by AT, NF, AP, and CF and shared with the researchers involved. They outlined each data point extracted from the eligible articles. Two trained and calibrated researchers (AT and NF) independently extracted relevant data from the eligible papers using Covidence software. Consensus was achieved through discussion or by consulting researchers (AP and CF). Charting was carried out by the primary author (AT) using Excel 2021. The information that was not available in the included articles was categorized as “not reported (NR).”

Critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence

A critical appraisal and risk of bias assessment were performed on all included studies to assess their reliability and validity. A calibration and training session was conducted with 5 studies between the two researchers (AT and NF) to critically appraise the included articles. For quantitative inquiries, the Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2.0) was employed for Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs), and Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies–of Interventions (ROBINS-I) was used for non-Randomized Controlled Trials (nRCTs). For qualitative inquiries, the JBI Qualitative Research Checklist was utilized, and the JBI-case series for case series was applied to case series studies.

Collating, summarizing, and reporting the results

The charted data were analyzed using descriptive statistics to summarize quantifiable data (AT) [19]. A qualitative content analysis method was used to summarize the results, categorizing data to address the scoping review questions (AT) [22]. Consensus regarding the categories in qualitative content analysis was achieved through discussion or, when necessary, by consulting researchers AP and CF.

Results

Searches across Medline (n = 449), Embase (n = 215), CINAHL (n = 32), Web of Science (n = 18), and ProQuest Theses and Dissertations (n = 4) yielded 718 records. After removal of 189 duplicates, 86 articles were identified as potentially relevant during title and abstract screening. Of these, 13 articles met the inclusion criteria after full-text screening and were included in the review. An additional eligible study was identified by screening the reference lists of retrieved articles, resulting in a final total of 14 articles included in this review. Reasons for exclusion are detailed in Fig. 1.

Studies characteristics

The studies were geographically diverse, with most conducted in Europe (n = 8; 57.1%), followed by Asia (n = 4; 28.5%), and North America (n = 2; 14.2%). Most studies were published between 2011 and 2021, with a distribution of five studies from 2011 to 2015, four from 2016 to 2019, and five from 2021 to the present. Study settings included universities (n = 4, 28.5%), public health system clinics (n = 1, 7.1%), and private clinics (n = 1, 7.1%), though eight studies did not specify a setting. Funding was provided by public sources in four studies, while eight did not report funding, and two reported no funding. Adolescent participants ranged in age from 10 to 19 years. Most studies utilized quantitative inquiry (n = 10; 71.4%), with four (28.5%) employing qualitative inquiries. Reported study designs included randomized controlled trials (n = 6; 42.8%), non-randomized controlled trials (n = 3; 21.4%) including observational [23, 24] and quasi-experimental study designs [25], qualitative description (n = 2; 14.2%), phenomenology (n = 1; 7.14%), and phenomenography (n = 1; 7.14%).

As shown in Fig. 2, four studies (28.5%) focused on vacuum-formed retainers, three (21.4%) on Hawley retainers, two (14.2%) on both types, two (14.2%) on Twin Block appliances, and one each (7.1%) on the Activator, Myobrace/Activator, and Monoblock/Twin Block combinations.

Pie chart representing the distribution of different types of removable appliances in the included studies

Selected studies had different aims. Four studies ([26–29]; 28.5%) explored patient perspectives and experiences, detailing challenges, enablers, and recommendations for improvement; one study ([30]; 7.14%) solely evaluated interventions designed to improve adherence; two studies ([24, 25]; 21.4%) examined treatment adherence, focusing on adherence to treatment recommendations and appointment attendance; and five studies (n = 5; 35.7%) assessed adherence-associated factors, which was assessed in studies with multiple aims [23, 31–34]. Notably, some studies had several aims [23, 31–36].

Indirect and direct methods were employed to assess adherence to wearing the removable appliances. Four studies explored adherence indirectly through interviews, including individual [27, 29, 31] and photo-elicitation interviews [26], and four used questionnaires [28, 33–35]. Three studies assessed adherence directly via microsensors [23, 24, 30]. Some studies applied multiple methods to assess adherence [25, 32, 36]. Study characteristics have been described in Supplementary Table S1.

Definition of adherence to wearing removable appliances among adolescents

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines [37] “adherence” as “the extent to which a person’s behavior—taking medication, following a diet, and/or executing lifestyle changes—corresponds with agreed recommendations from a health care provider.” This patient-centered approach emphasizes active patient involvement in selecting the most suitable treatment option [38].

Within the reviewed literature, a significant conceptual gap emerged: a mere two of the fourteen studies [23, 30] provided an explicit definition for either adherence or compliance. The majority of studies predominantly centered on treatment adherence as defined by daily wear time (DWT). Within this framework, adherence was dichotomized into “full-time compliance” (indicating wear except during eating, brushing, or contact sports) and an unspecified “part-time compliance.” Notably, one study operationally defined adherence as a minimum of eight hours of daily wear [23]. Furthermore, one study incorporated appointment attendance into its consideration of adherence, yet it failed to establish a clear threshold for this aspect [30].

Results of individual studies

The main results of the included studies were organized by type of removable appliance for two reasons. First, adherence is always behavior-specific [39]. Second, this approach more effectively illustrates the research activity related to each removable appliance, which is important for identifying existing knowledge gaps.

Adherence to wearing vacuum-formed retainers

Four studies [26, 28, 33, 34] analyzed adolescent adherence to Vacuum-formed Retainers (VFR), addressing treatment adherence, patient perspectives, and adherence-associated factors. Although the recommended DWT was initially 24 h, it then shifted to nightly wear after 12 months. One study [33] found that 40% of participants wore VFR for less than 18 h/day. Factors like personal and peer influences, and routines were associated with adherence [26]. Overjet, overbite, and irregularity index showed no correlation [28, 34]. Inconsistent findings regarding sex differences in adherence were noted [33, 34]. Patient-reported adherence challenges included pain, forgetfulness, hygiene issues, and financial constraints [26, 28, 34].

Adherence to wearing Hawley’s retainers

Three studies [30, 35, 36] evaluated Hawley’s Retainer (HR) adherence, assessing treatment adherence, the effectiveness of adherence interventions, and patient perspectives. Recommended DWT ranged from 19 to 24 h. If the patient wore the appliance for 19 h/day, it was categorized as full usage, and less than 19 h/day was less than full usage [36]. Image-based interventions also showed positive effects. Specifically, parental exposure to images depicting the consequences of poor adherence resulted in an average of 15.04 h/day of wear. This compared to 13.05 h/day when patients themselves were exposed to images, and 12.37 h/day with no image exposure. Monitoring significantly improved DWT; adolescents who were aware of monitoring wore them for an average of 2.3 h more per day [36]. Specifically, unmonitored adolescents wore HR 10.6–11.1 h/day, while monitored adolescents initially wore them 16.3 h/day, slightly decreasing after day 43 [30]. Clinical appointment adherence was marginally higher in the monitored group [30]. Patients commonly reported challenges such as forgetting to wear retainers, discomfort during insertion and removal, and a perceived lack of importance [35].

Adherence to wearing HR and VFR

Two studies [29, 32] evaluated adherence to wearing HR and VFR with a recommended DWT of 24 h/day. These studies focused on treatment adherence, patient perspectives and experiences, and adherence-associated factors. One study [32] assessed the rate of adherence using microsensor data and self-reported logs. For HR, microsensors recorded a DWT of 15.3 h/day, but self-reports overestimated DWT at 20 h/day. Similarly, with VFR, microsensors indicated 18.3 h/day, while self-reports again showed an overestimation at 20.7 h/day. Perceived challenges included reduced parental support post-treatment, insufficient orthodontist guidance, and age-related influence on parental involvement [29]. Clinical context was associated with adherence. Specifically, patients seen in private orthodontic practices had an increased potential for adherence of 2.16 h/day compared with those in university settings [32].

Adherence to wearing twin block

Studies [24, 31] examined Twin Block (TB) adherence, focusing on treatment adherence, patient perspectives and experiences, and adherence-associated factors. The recommended DWT varied between the two studies. One study recommended 24 h/day for full-time (FT) use and 12 h/day for part-time (PT) use [24], while the other recommended a DWT of 24 h/day [31]. The rate of adherence reported revealed that with FT, the DWT was 20.8 ± 2.7 h/day, while with PT, it was 9.5 h/day [24]. The perceived challenges [31] included wear time overestimation in logs, lack of motivation, negative peer influence, social interactions, self-esteem, and activity interference. Recommendations to improve adherence [31] included clinician-patient communication, individualized wear, appliance modifications, and reminder tools. The factors associated with adherence [24] included an overjet reduction, a decrease in the ANB angle, and an increase in the mandibular base. Additional factors influencing adherence included self-motivation, peer and authority influence, appliance adaptability, perceived progress, quality of life, and recall [31].

Adherence to wearing activator

Only one study [23] assessed adherence to wearing Activator appliances, focusing on treatment adherence and factors associated with adherence. The recommended DWT was 12 h/day. The reported rate of adherence varied between 0.49 and 11.03 h/day. In this study, a reduction in overjet was identified as a factor associated with adherence.

Adherence to wearing Myobrace and activator

Only one study [27] investigated adherence to Myobrace and Activator appliances, focusing on patient perspectives. Adolescents reported feeling discomfort when wearing these appliances. Developing individual strategies, such as remembering to wear the appliance and observing improvements, which helped motivate them to adhere to wearing the appliance. Additionally, meeting with the dentist to receive encouragement, individually adapted information, and feeling disappointed when not being listened to also played a role. The authors recommended the importance of parental and peer support for improved appliance use.

Adherence to wearing monoblock and TB

Also, one study [25] evaluated adherence to wearing Monoblock/Twin Block appliances, focusing on treatment adherence. The recommended DWT was 15 h. The reported rate of adherence was 11.0 ± 4.4 h/day for Monoblock appliances and 10.3 ± 3.5 h/day for Twin Block appliances.

Discussion

This scoping review sought to delineate the scope of existing evidence regarding adolescents’ adherence to intra-oral removable appliances. A key finding was the absence of comprehensive reviews specifically dedicated to adolescent adherence in this context. Most extant reviews on orthodontic adherence incorporate broader age ranges, frequently merging data from both children and adolescents or focusing on adult populations. The results from the current review underscore a critical need for adolescent-specific reviews to inform the development of targeted interventions aimed at enhancing adherence and optimizing treatment outcomes. Compounding this, evidence suggests that younger patients (aged 7–12 years) generally exhibit greater consistency in adhering to intra-oral removable appliance treatment recommendations than adolescents [40], thereby emphasizing the imperative for age-specific research. This observed disparity may originate from increased parental supervision among younger children [41].

The included studies revealed four primary research aims: patient perspectives and experiences, adherence interventions, treatment adherence, and factors associated with adherence. A salient finding was the extensive focus within most studies on elucidating patients’ perspectives and experiences regarding adherence, thereby offering valuable insights into the lived realities of adolescents. Conversely, there was a discernible scarcity of research specifically evaluating interventions designed to enhance adherence to these appliances. This observation corroborates prior studies that have underscored the necessity for further investigation into factors influencing adherence, coupled with the development and evaluation of varied interventions [18]. Compelling evidence indicates that adherence to treatment recommendations frequently wanes during adolescence [42], thus underscoring a critical need for additional research in this domain. Such investigations are imperative to equip clinicians with an evidence base for devising and customizing effective interventions, which will ultimately foster improved adherence rates during this pivotal developmental stage.

A critical appraisal of the included studies revealed moderate quality, which means the current evidence isn’t sufficient or robust enough to confidently back the effectiveness of suggested strategies (like microsensors or self-reported logs) for boosting adherence to intra-oral appliances. We often found inconsistent ways of measuring outcomes, unclear methods for randomization, and a mismatch between the study’s underlying philosophy and its research approach. These weaknesses make the findings less reliable and harder to apply broadly. Therefore, there’s a strong need for more rigorous and well-designed studies to build a stronger foundation of evidence in this field.

A standardized conceptual definition of adherence to intra-oral removable appliances among adolescents is still absent. A recent scoping review, encompassing studies across pediatric, adolescent, and adult populations, sought to define patient adherence to both fixed and removable orthodontic appliances. Mirroring our own findings, this review likewise noted a deficit in conceptual standardization regarding adherence within the field of orthodontics [18]. In the present review, adherence to intra-oral removable appliances was specifically evaluated in adolescents. Among the included studies, adherence was predominantly operationalized as daily appliance wear for a minimum of eight hours [23]. Nevertheless, this reported adherence rate demonstrated variability across studies, and numerous investigations omitted detailed adherence definitions. Without a consistent and widely accepted definition, the synthesis of research findings continues to pose a significant challenge. Consequently, establishing a precise, conceptual definition of adherence to intra-oral removable appliances in adolescents is paramount to enhancing comparability among studies and fostering the development of robust, evidence-based interventions.

This review reveals a consistent trend: adolescents often don’t meet the recommended daily wear time (DWT) for various intra-oral appliances. For instance, many adolescents struggle to wear vacuum-formed retainers (VFRs) for the prescribed 24 h/day; nearly 40% of participants wore theirs for less than 18 h/day [33]. However, some past research suggests VFRs might lead to higher adherence in adolescents due to their discreet appearance and minimal impact on aesthetics [43]. This discrepancy could stem from differences in participant age ranges across studies, with some including both children and adolescents [6, 9, 18, 24], making it difficult to isolate adolescent-specific behaviors.

Similarly, for Twin Block (TB) appliances, the recommended DWT is 24 h/day for full-time wear and 12 h/day for part-time wear. Yet, this review’s findings indicate average DWTs of 20.8 ± 2.7 h/day for full-time wear and 9.5 h/day for part-time wear [24], reflecting a consistent pattern of reduced adherence.

During adolescence, individuals begin managing their treatment more independently, often with less parental involvement and sometimes increased parent-child conflict, which can negatively impact adherence [44]. This also suggests adolescents frequently struggle to follow treatment recommendations due to perceived challenges like peer influence, lack of motivation, discomfort, and forgetfulness [30].

This review also highlighted the introduction of interventions, such as image-based and digital monitoring, aimed at improving adherence. For Hawley retainers (HRs), where the recommended DWT ranges from 19 to 24 h/day [30, 35], image-based interventions showed favorable effects [30, 35]Patients exposed to images illustrating the consequences of poor adherence demonstrated an average DWT of 13.05 h/day, compared to 12.37 h/day for those with no image exposure [35]. These findings align with Wafaei et al.‘s study, which found significant long-term adherence improvement among patients aged 16–35 years after receiving audiovisual instructions for HR compliance [45]. While that sample included adults, the general trend supports the value of visual reinforcement for behavior change across age groups.

Monitoring also significantly improved DWT. Adolescents aware of being monitored wore their appliances an average of 2.3 h more per day than those who were not [36]. This aligns with previous reviews indicating that monitoring and feedback interventions, such as microsensors, consistently enhance adherence [46]. This improvement could be attributed to the Hawthorne effect, where awareness of being observed alters behavior—a phenomenon widely described in both orthodontic and broader health behavior literature [47, 48]. These findings suggest that incorporating behavioral monitoring tools and image-based interventions can significantly support improved adherence among adolescents, especially when interventions are tailored.

This scoping review offers several strengths to the field of orthodontics. First, its specific focus on adolescent adherence to intra-oral removable appliances provides targeted insights for developing tailored interventions. Second, conducting a risk of bias assessment on the included studies enhanced the review’s methodological rigor.

However, it’s important to acknowledge several limitations. First, most included studies were conducted in European countries and predominantly within university-based clinical settings. This concentration may limit the generalizability of findings to other healthcare systems, community settings, and culturally diverse populations. Second, the heavy reliance on quantitative methodologies in most studies offered limited insight into the psychological, emotional, and social dimensions of adolescents’ adherence. The absence of qualitative or mixed-methods research restricts a deeper understanding of the underlying reasons for non-adherence, such as individual motivations, perceptions of treatment, and peer or family influences.

Future studies should aim to establish a standardized, globally accepted definition of adherence to intra-oral removable appliances among adolescents. Given that adherence is influenced by a range of cognitive, emotional, and social factors, there is a clear need for multi-component behavioral interventions that go beyond improving knowledge and skills. These interventions should also address patients’ intentions, environmental barriers, and self-efficacy.

A further notable knowledge gap pertains to Clear Aligner Therapy (CAT). Although CAT is increasingly popular among adolescents, no studies included in this review specifically assessed adherence to these appliances in this age group. Existing evidence from mixed-age populations suggests a consistent discrepancy between self-reported and objectively measured wear times, with patients often overestimating their adherence [18]. For example, one study reported that only 36% of patients met the recommended daily wear time [49]. However, these findings may not be directly applicable to adolescents, who might differ from children and adults in terms of intention, capability, and opportunity to adhere to CAT. Therefore, more research is needed to examine adherence patterns across different removable orthodontic appliances, with a particular focus on CAT in adolescents (aged 10–19 years). Studies conducted across diverse geographic and clinical contexts would improve the generalizability of findings. Addressing these gaps will provide clinicians with evidence-based insights to optimize support and guidance, ultimately improving treatment outcomes for adolescent patients.

Conclusion

This scoping review reveals a limited but crucial evidence base regarding adolescent adherence to intra-oral removable appliances. Most adolescents fail to meet recommended daily wear times, and existing research is often low quality, lacking a standardized definition of adherence. While some studies explore patient perspectives, rigorous evaluations of adherence-enhancing interventions are scarce. Digital monitoring and image-based interventions show initial promise but require further testing in robust studies. Developing multi-component behavioral interventions is also essential for a comprehensive understanding and improvement of adherence.
