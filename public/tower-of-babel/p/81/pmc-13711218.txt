Tricuspid aortic valve morphology predicts favorable midterm outcomes after aortic valve neocuspidization in children
Authors: Guanxi Wang, Sen Zhang, Kai Ma, Kunjing Pang, Benqing Zhang, Keming Yang, Shoujun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the midterm outcomes of the aortic valve neocuspidalization (AVNeo) procedure for aortic valve reconstruction in 83 pediatric patients with complex aortic valve disease and to identify risk factors associated with the outcomes.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on pediatric patients who underwent the AVNeo procedure between January 2017 and June 2025. The composite primary end point included all-cause mortality, reoperation, or moderate-to-severe aortic valve dysfunction (regurgitation or stenosis). Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify risk factors.ResultsAmong the 83 patients (mean age 7.0 ± 3.7 years; 67.5% male) included in the analysis, the predominant preoperative pathology was moderate-to-severe aortic regurgitation (88.0%). Single-leaflet reconstruction (65.1%) using autologous pericardium (94.0%) was the most common surgical approach. Over a mean follow-up of 28.4 ± 23.9 months, 27 patients (32.5%) met the composite end point, including 2 late deaths, 18 cases of moderate-to-severe regurgitation, and 9 cases of moderate-to-severe stenosis; 10 patients underwent reoperation. The probability of freedom from the primary end point was 83.6%, 64.5%, and 54.4% at 1, 3, and 6 years, respectively. Multivariable analysis identified tricuspid aortic valve morphology as an independent protective factor against the primary end point (hazard ratio, 0.408; 95% CI, 0.178-0.935, P = .034), whereas the use of autologous pericardium showed a strong protective trend (hazard ratio, 0.284; 95% CI, 0.078-1.037, P = .057).ConclusionsAVNeo provides midterm outcomes that support its use as an alternative strategy. Tricuspid aortic valve morphology is a strong predictor of favorable outcomes, and the use of autologous pericardium demonstrates significant potential as a protective factor.Key Words: aortic neocuspidization, tricuspid aortic valve, bicuspid aortic valve, autologous pericardium

Tricuspid aortic valve morphology is a strong predictor of favorable outcomes.

Central Message

AVNeo provides midterm outcomes that support its use as an alternative strategy. Tricuspid aortic valve morphology is a strong independent predictor of better outcomes, and autologous pericardium may offer a protective advantage.

Perspective

Although tricuspid morphology and autologous pericardium are associated with more favorable outcomes, the overall midterm results of the AVNeo procedure in this pediatric cohort are tempered by a high rate of valve-related events and a steady decline in event-free survival over time.

The optimal management strategy for aortic valve disease in pediatric patients remains undefined.1 Current approaches primarily include repair and replacement procedures. Although aortic valve repair is often recommended as an initial strategy to stabilize cardiac dimensions and alleviate symptoms, it is not always feasible or successful, ultimately necessitating aortic valve replacement. However, all existing replacement options—including mechanical valves, bioprostheses, and the Ross procedure—are associated with significant limitations in children, such as high rates of valve-related complications and the need for reintervention as the result of somatic growth.2

The Ross procedure is considered the gold standard for aortic valve replacement in pediatric patients. Nevertheless, it inherently places both the aortic and pulmonary valves at risk of long-term degeneration and reoperation. Reported annualized reoperation rates for the aortic valve and right ventricular outflow tract after the Ross procedure are 1.60% and 1.91%, respectively.3 Moreover, the availability of suitable conduits for right ventricular outflow tract reconstruction is limited4 and, combined with the fact that younger age at surgery is a recognized risk factor for reintervention,5 complicates its universal applicability. Consequently, a staged approach—initial aortic valve repair at a younger age to delay the Ross procedure—has been proposed as a strategy to improve long-term outcomes.6,7

Aortic valve neocuspidalization (AVNeo) involves the resection of diseased native leaflets and reconstruction of the valve using autologous pericardium. Although this technique has demonstrated excellent mid- to long-term results in adults,8 evidence in pediatric populations remains limited.9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15

This study focuses on a selected aortic leaflet reconstruction technique, which entails precise excision and replacement of nonrepairable leaflets—whether single, double, or tricuspid. Since May 2017, our center has adopted this approach with the aim of managing complex aortic valve disease in children and potentially delaying the need for a Ross procedure. This study aims to evaluate the midterm outcomes of this technique and to identify risk factors associated with the adverse outcomes in a pediatric cohort.

Methods

Materials

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hospital (approval number: 2026-3040; date: February 3, 2026). Written informed consent was obtained from all legal guardians. Our initial cohort comprised a consecutive series of 90 pediatric patients with complex aortic valve disease who underwent targeted aortic valve leaflet reconstruction between May 2017 and June 2025. Patients who underwent classic aortic valve repair (eg, commissurotomy, leaflet resuspension, fenestration closure) during the same period were not included in this analysis. To avoid confounding the assessment of valve durability, we excluded 7 patients who experienced early adverse events not attributable to neocuspidized valve function, because these events did not reflect structural or functional failure of the pericardial leaflets, resulting in a final cohort of 83 patients for follow-up analysis. Perioperative and follow-up data for the final cohort were systematically collected and analyzed.

End Point

The composite primary end point included all-cause mortality, reoperation, or moderate-to-severe aortic valve dysfunction (regurgitation or stenosis). Echocardiography was used to evaluate the aortic valve function, including aortic valve stenosis (AS) and aortic valve regurgitation (AR) over time. Severity of AS was measured using the maximum velocities and the peak pressure gradient across the valve, and the grades were as follows: mild stenosis, peak velocity <3 m/s and peak gradient <36 mm Hg; moderate stenosis, peak velocity 3 to 4 m/s, and peak gradient 36 to 64 mm Hg; and severe stenosis, peak velocity >4 m/s and peak gradient >64 mm Hg.16 Valve regurgitation was graded as grade 0 (absent), grade 1 (trivial), grade 2 (mild), grade 3 (moderate), and grade 4 (severe) according to the features of the jet flow as measured by pulsed Doppler echocardiography.12,17

Surgical Technique

On the basis of the reported references,10,11,18,19 a targeted aortic leaflet reconstruction was performed, conceptualized as selective leaflet replacement to maximize preservation of native valve tissue. The surgical protocol involved harvesting autologous pericardium (94.0%, n = 78) followed by glutaraldehyde treatment (0.6%, 5-10 minutes); bovine pericardium served as an alternative in reoperation cases with insufficient autologous tissue (6.0%, n = 5). After aortotomy, the intercommissural distances were systematically measured using sizers to determine neocusp dimensions. A defining technical feature included the individualized determination of leaflet replacement number—ranging from single to triple leaflet reconstruction—based on the topography of pathologic leaflets, rather than routine triple-leaflet replacement. In congenitally bicuspid valves with localized pathology (n = 3), anatomic bicuspid configuration was preserved. All neocusps were sutured to the annulus, with commissures elevated slightly above the native position to achieve optimal coaptation height (Figure 1). Finally, satisfactory hemodynamic function was confirmed by intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography before chest closure.

Data Collection and Definition

Patient demographics and clinical data were retrospectively collected from the institutional electronic medical record system. Early mortality was defined as death within 30 days postoperatively, whereas late mortality referred to death beyond 30 days or occurring after discharge in cases in which hospitalization exceeded 30 days. Structural valve degeneration was defined as intrinsic deterioration of the neopericardial leaflet, including calcification, retraction, fibrosis, or restricted mobility, as confirmed by intraoperative inspection or echocardiography. Operative mortality was defined as death within 30 days of surgery or during the same hospital admission. Late mortality was defined as any death occurring after hospital discharge beyond 30 days. In the final analysis cohort of 83 patients, there were no operative deaths; the 2 reported deaths were late mortalities. All patients were systematically followed at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months during the first postoperative year and then annually thereafter. The duration of follow-up was calculated from the date of surgery to the occurrence of the primary end point or the most recent clinical evaluation.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables are described as means with SDs or medians with interquartile ranges, on the basis of their distribution normality assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Univariate Cox regression analysis was first performed, and variables with P < .1 were subsequently included in the multivariate Cox proportional hazards model to identify independent predictors of the outcomes. All analyses were performed using SPSS, version 25.0 (IBM Corp) and GraphPad Prism, version 8.0.

Data-Availability Statement

The data underlying this article will be shared on reasonable request to the corresponding author.

Results

Preoperative Data

On the basis of the demographic and clinical characteristics presented in Table 1, the study cohort consisted of 83 pediatric patients who underwent the AVNeo procedure. The population was predominantly male (67.5%), with a mean age of 7.0 ± 3.7 years and a mean weight of 29.0 ± 20.9 kg. A notable proportion of patients (36.1%) had undergone previous cardiac operations, the most common of which were aortic valve repair (14.5%) and ventricular septal defect repair (13.3%).

The primary etiology for the current operation was congenital valvular deformity (60.2%), followed by surgical injury and infectious endocarditis (each 18.1%). In terms of pathologic manifestations, the vast majority of patients (88.0%) presented with moderate or severe AR, whereas 27.7% had concomitant moderate or severe AS. Preoperatively, 9.6% of the patients were in a state of cardiac insufficiency, and a small subset (1.2%) required invasive mechanical ventilation.

Intraoperative Data

On the basis of the intraoperative and postoperative data summarized in Table 2, the AVNeo procedure was characterized by a number of distinctive technical features. Tricuspid aortic valve anatomy was the most common morphology (61.4%), followed by bicuspid (32.5%). Single-leaflet reconstruction constituted the majority of cases (65.1%), with the right coronary cusp being most frequently replaced (69.9%). Autologous pericardium served as the primary leaflet material (94.0%), and 3-leaflet coaptation was achieved in most patients (96.4%). Three-leaflet coaptation with no more than trace AR was achieved in most patients (96.4%) and 2-leaflet coaptation in 3.6% of the patients, as confirmed by intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography.

The mean cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic crossclamp times were 146.2 ± 53.9 minutes and 94.4 ± 40.0 minutes, respectively. Concomitant procedures were performed in more than one half of the patients (56.6%), with ventricular septal defect repair (16.9%), patent foramen ovale repair (13.3%), and discrete subaortic membrane excision (10.8%) being the most common additional interventions. Postoperative recovery parameters included a mean mechanical ventilation time of 22.2 ± 33.2 hours and an average stay in the intensive care unit of 3.7 ± 3.7 days, reflecting the complexity of the surgical population and the magnitude of the combined procedures.

Follow-Up Data

At hospital discharge, 2 of the 83 patients (2.4%) had moderate AR, and the remaining 81 patients (97.6%) had none or only trivial/mild AR. Over a mean follow-up period of 28.4 ± 23.9 months, the composite end point was met in 27 patients (32.5%), comprising 2 late deaths, 10 reoperations, 18 cases of moderate-to-severe regurgitation, and 9 cases of moderate-to-severe AS. The 2 late mortality cases included 1 death, attributable to a pulmonary hypertensive crisis at a local hospital 8 months postdischarge, and 1 sudden death occurring 33 months after discharge.

Reoperations were stratified according to timing and underlying mechanism. Two early reoperations (at 4 and 7 months postoperatively) were necessitated by recurrent infective endocarditis, which resulted in leaflet perforation and severe AR. The remaining 8 reoperations, which occurred between 6 and 34 months, were attributed to structural valve degeneration characterized by restricted leaflet mobility and resultant heart failure. These cases comprised 3 distinct hemodynamic profiles: 2 with combined AS and AR (at 7 and 10 months), 5 with isolated AR (at 6, 12, 22, 26, and 34 months), and 1 with isolated AS (at 7 months postdischarge).

Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated the probability of freedom from the primary end point was 83.6%, 64.5%, and 54.4% at 1, 3, and 6 years, respectively (Figure 2). To identify predictors of outcome, a Cox regression model was used, incorporating variables selected by univariate screening (P < .10) into multivariable analysis (Table 3). The final model established tricuspid aortic valve morphology as an independent protective factor (hazard ratio [HR], 0.408; 95% CI, 0.178-0.935, P = .034), whereas the use of autologous pericardium exhibited a strong protective trend (HR, 0.284; 95% CI, 0.078-1.037, P = .057). Further stratification within the autologous pericardium cohort confirmed the prognostic significance of valve morphology: patients with tricuspid configuration (n = 47) showed significantly superior outcomes compared with those with nontricuspid morphology (n = 31), with a log-rank of P = .021 (Figure 3). The probability of freedom from the primary end point at 1, 3, and 4 years was 90.4%, 77.4%, and 65.7% in the tricuspid group compared with 78.5%, 49.0%, and 40.8% in the nontricuspid group, respectively.

To assess the robustness of our primary findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis, excluding 5 patients who received bovine pericardium and 5 patients with unicuspid or quadricuspid aortic valve morphology (rare congenital anomalies). After these exclusions, the protective effect of tricuspid aortic valve morphology remained significant (HR, 0.376; 95% CI, 0.141-1.001, P = .013). To address etiologic heterogeneity, we excluded rare anomalies (unicuspid, quadricuspid) and analyzed only the 48 patients with congenital bicuspid (n = 22) or tricuspid (n = 26) aortic valve deformity with single-leaflet reconstruction. In this subgroup, tricuspid morphology showed a protective trend toward better midterm outcomes compared with bicuspid morphology, but the difference was not statistically significant (HR, 0.532; 95% CI, 0.187-1.511, P = .204), likely because of reduced sample size and statistical power.

Discussion

The AVNeo procedure emerged as a reparative alternative to mechanical valve replacement and the Ross procedure in pediatric cardiac surgery. It is initially reserved for complex cases unsuitable for a Ross operation, such as those with severe annular dilation, root pathology, or multiple previous sternotomies. Early series from pioneering institutions report encouraging short-term outcomes, particularly in older children with well-developed annuli.10, 11, 12 However, the procedure presents unique challenges in children younger 6 years of age, in whom risks include coronary ostial obstruction from redundant leaflets, relative valve stenosis attributable to bulky leaflets in small aortic roots, and concerns regarding leaflet material stiffening and retraction over time.15 Notably, comparative analyses reveal no significant difference in repair durability between triple-leaflet and single-leaflet reconstruction techniques.12 In response to these findings and with the objective of preserving native leaflet tissue while maintaining somatic growth potential, our institution developed a strategy of targeted aortic valve leaflet reconstruction, implementing this approach in one of the youngest reported patient cohorts. Building upon this surgical evolution, the present study was designed to evaluate the medium-term outcomes of this refined technique in a younger patient population (mean age 7.0 ± 3.7 years) and to clarify its appropriate position within the sequential management paradigm of pediatric aortic valve disease.

Our study confirmed the technical feasibility of the AVNeo procedure as a complex valve repair. However, it more critically revealed significant concerns regarding its medium-term durability. We observed a progressive decline in event-free survival, with approximately one third of patients reaching the composite end point of valve dysfunction or reintervention during follow-up. This finding is strongly corroborated by recent independent studies. Other studies report midterm reoperation rates of 40% at 3.2 years19 and 29% at 4 years,20 reflecting the procedure's substantial need for reintervention during medium-term follow-up. Collectively, this evidence consistently indicates that, despite technical refinements, the neoaortic valve reconstructed via the AVNeo procedure remains vulnerable to substantial structural deterioration when subjected to the unique demands of a child's evolving hemodynamics and ongoing somatic growth.

A deeper analysis of failure mechanisms highlights 2 pivotal determinants of success: native anatomy and biomaterial selection. First, in accordance with the valve repair experience of Schlein and colleagues,21 our multivariable analysis identified tricuspid aortic valve morphology as an independent protective factor (HR, 0.408; P = .034). This finding has a solid biomechanical basis: the aortic valve neocuspidization technique hinges on creating 3 symmetrical, coapting neoleaflets. A native tricuspid anatomy provides the ideal geometric foundation for this reconstruction, promoting even stress distribution. In contrast, the nontricuspid aortic valves entail significantly greater technical and biomechanical challenges than their application in native tricuspid valves. This heightens complexity originating from 3 principal sources: inherent anatomic asymmetry, the lack of a standardized surgical approach, and concerns regarding long-term durability. First, the procedure is fundamentally compromised by anatomic asymmetry. In contrast to the symmetrical, reproducible framework of a tricuspid valve, nontricuspid anatomies require the surgeon to surgically create a trileaflet architecture from an irregular foundation. This necessitates expert judgment in raphe division, commissural repositioning, and leaflet sizing—processes that are not fully addressed by standardized templates and introduce considerable variability. Second, technical execution is markedly more demanding. The goal of achieving symmetrical coaptation among a manufactured leaflet and 2 often-asymmetrical native leaflets requires exceptional precision. The creation of new commissures and the accurate sizing of the neoleaflets within this aberrant geometry are critical; minor imperfections could readily result in poor coaptation, leading to early valvular regurgitation or stenosis. Finally, the long-term durability of these reconstructions is less predictable. Beyond the inherent longevity of the pericardial material, success is contingent upon the functional stability of the remaining native leaflets, which could be prone to disease progression. Furthermore, the implantation of geometrically symmetrical neoleaflets into an asymmetric root has a risk of creating abnormal stress distributions, potentially accelerating structural deterioration and increasing the likelihood of reintervention. As a result, it suggests that children with tricuspid aortic valves might derive the greatest benefit from the AVNeo procedure.

The choice of leaflet material profoundly impacts the fate of the reconstructed valve. Our data indicate a strong protective trend favoring autologous pericardium (HR, 0.284; P = .057). This aligned powerfully with the findings of Chávez and colleagues,19 who demonstrate significantly superior freedom from reoperation at 5 years with autologous versus bovine pericardium (76% vs 30%, P = .006). The superior biocompatibility, enhanced tissue integration, and greater resistance to calcification and suture-line dehiscence of autologous tissue underlay this advantage. The leaflet fibrosis, calcification, and immobility observed in our late reoperations are hallmarks of the accelerated structural degeneration typical of xenograft materials in children.

Synthesizing our findings with the current body of evidence allows for a more precise definition of the roles of the AVNeo procedure and the Ross surgery. The AVNeo procedure is regarded as a valuable palliative strategy, or “bridge” surgery. Its optimal application is in a carefully selected subset of children, such as those who were older, possess tricuspid valve morphology, and are candidates for autologous pericardium use, particularly when the Ross procedure is contraindicated or needs to be strategically delayed. In these selected patients, the AVNeo technique could effectively “buy time” during critical growth periods, avoiding the need for anticoagulation or dealing with patient-prosthesis mismatch.

Conversely, for the vast majority of children, in whom a long-term, durable solution is the goal, the Ross procedure has remained the unequivocal benchmark. As demonstrated in 2 comparative studies18,20 and large-scale analyses such as the meta-analysis by Notenboom and colleagues,22 the Ross procedure exhibited superior medium- to long-term valve durability (eg, >88% freedom from significant dysfunction at 4 years) and survival. As a living valve substitute with growth potential and near-ideal hemodynamics, it is rightly considered the gold standard for providing a “lifetime solution” in children. Our 6-year probability of freedom from the primary end point (54.3%) reflects the palliative nature of AVNeo, which may serve as a bridge to delay the Ross procedure rather than as a definitive solution. This staged approach should not be viewed as a replacement for Ross but as a strategic delay in complex scenarios. A primary Ross may have a better outcome.

On the basis of the midterm outcomes and evolving institutional experience, the application of the AVNeo procedure at our center has undergone a strategic refinement. Its indications are progressively narrowed to optimize patient selection, with the objective of enhancing the long-term efficacy and clinical utility of this technique. This decision is substantiated by evidence suggesting that procedural success and durability are significantly influenced by specific anatomic considerations, particularly tricuspid valve morphology and the availability of autologous pericardium.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The retrospective, single-center design and moderate cohort size may have limited the generalizability of our findings. The midterm follow-up duration precluded a definitive assessment of long-term durability, particularly in growing pediatric patients. Furthermore, the heterogeneity in baseline valve morphology and leaflet materials introduced potential confounding factors that complicated outcome interpretation. Despite these constraints, this investigation provided valuable insights into the midterm performance of the AVNeo procedure in congenital aortic valve disease and helped identify important considerations for case selection in younger populations. In addition, a significant limitation of this study is that 65.1% of patients underwent single-leaflet neocuspidization, which is distinct from the classic trileaflet leaflet replacement. Therefore, our findings on predictors of outcome are most directly applicable to the setting of partial AVNeo rather than complete trileaflet reconstruction.

Conclusions

In conclusion, AVNeo provides midterm outcomes that support its use as an alternative strategy. Our findings confirm that tricuspid aortic valve morphology strongly predicts favorable outcomes, and autologous pericardium emerges as the superior leaflet material. However, the concerning incidence of midterm valve-related events and declining event-free survival necessitate rigorous patient selection and thorough preoperative counseling, positioning this technique as a strategic choice to delay more complex interventions in a carefully defined subset of children.
