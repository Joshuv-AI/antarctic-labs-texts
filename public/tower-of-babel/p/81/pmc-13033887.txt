Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors are associated with a lower risk of osteoarthritis in patients with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Yu-Hsiu Chen, Virginia Byers Kraus, Yu-Sheng Chang, Tzu-Min Lin, Hsiang-Yen Lee, Ching-Kuei Chang, Tzu-Tung Kuo, Jin-Hua Chen, Chi-Ching Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent form of arthritis. Currently, no disease-modifying OA drugs are available. Increasing interest has focused on repurposing antidiabetic medications to prevent OA. Preclinical studies showed that dipeptidyl-peptidase 4 inhibitors (DPP-4is) may protect against cartilage degeneration via anti-inflammatory and anti-senescent mechanisms.Objectives:To evaluate whether DPP-4i use is associated with a decreased risk of incident OA and joint replacement in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM).Design:A nationwide, population-based, matched new-user, retrospective cohort study.Methods:Using Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (2008–2018), adult patients with newly diagnosed T2DM prescribed with DPP-4is were matched 1:1 to non-users based on age, sex, index year, and Diabetes Complications and Severity Index score. The primary endpoint was incident OA, while the secondary endpoints were total hip replacement (THR) and total knee replacement (TKR) attributed to OA. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate the adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) for comorbidities and concurrent medications.Results:In a sample of 1,282,796 patients with newly diagnosed T2DM, 165,333 pairs were matched for treatment with or without DPP-4is. Among 165,333 matched pairs (mean age: 58.6 years; 56.2% male), DPP-4i use was associated with lower risks of OA (aHR: 0.42; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.41–0.44), THR (aHR: 0.38; 95% CI: 0.30–0.48), and TKR (aHR: 0.42; 95% CI: 0.37–0.46), with benefits increasing with dosage. Moreover, DPP-4i use was associated with a significantly lower cumulative incidence of OA, THR, and TKR.Conclusion:In this first nationwide cohort study, DPP-4is were associated with decreased OA incidence and fewer joint replacement surgeries among patients with T2DM. These findings indicate the joint-protective effects of DPP-4is, highlighting their relevance to drug repurposing and clinical decision-making in patients at high risk of OA.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common form of arthritis worldwide and significantly contributes to disability and healthcare burden, particularly among older adults.1,2 The burden of OA is pronounced in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), which is associated with a higher prevalence of OA and worse functional outcomes. 3 With population aging and the increasing prevalence of obesity, OA in patients with T2DM is becoming a crucial socioeconomic and healthcare challenge. 4 In addition, OA and T2DM are linked by chronic low-grade inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and cellular senescence.5,6 Hyperglycemia and insulin resistance promote pro-inflammatory signaling and oxidative stress, which may accelerate cartilage degeneration and joint damage. 7 Conversely, OA-related pain and physical inactivity worsen glycemic control, reinforcing this bidirectional relationship. 8 These observations underscore the need to understand whether treatments targeting metabolic and inflammatory pathways in T2DM influence OA risk and progression.

Dipeptidyl-peptidase 4 inhibitors (DPP-4is) emerged as a significant class of oral antidiabetic agents since their approval in 2006 for the treatment of T2DM. 9 By inhibiting the enzymatic degradation of incretins, such as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), DPP-4is enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion and improve glycemic regulation. 10 Beyond these metabolic effects, increasing evidence indicates that DPP-4is exert pleiotropic actions relevant for tissue homeostasis and chronic disease pathways. 11 These include modulating immune responses, attenuating pro-inflammatory cytokine activity, and reducing cellular senescence.12,13 Such properties have raised interest in repurposing DPP-4is for conditions beyond diabetes management.

Emerging evidence supports the joint-protective role of DPP-4is. Recent studies have revealed that DPP-4 is upregulated in senescent chondrocytes, and its synovial fluid concentration correlates with radiographic OA progression. 14 Moreover, in vitro and in vivo studies showed that DPP-4is reduce chondrocyte senescence and cartilage degeneration.15–17 These findings provide a strong mechanistic rationale to investigate the association between DPP-4i use and clinically meaningful joint outcomes in human populations. Despite these insights, a critical gap in population-level evidence remains. To date, no large-scale observational study has comprehensively evaluated whether DPP-4i use in patients with T2DM is associated with a decreased risk of incident OA or joint replacement. Addressing this gap is critical given the lack of disease-modifying OA therapies and the widespread use of DPP-4is, highlighting their potential for drug repurposing in OA.

Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) provides an opportunity to examine this question. With nationwide coverage (23 million individuals, 99.6% coverage), comprehensive longitudinal follow-up, and reliable capture of major clinical outcomes, the NHIRD enables the evaluation of real-world treatment effects using a new-user (people who just started treatment), matched cohort design. Therefore, this nationwide population-based study aimed to assess the association between DPP-4i use and the risk of incident OA among patients with newly diagnosed T2DM. Furthermore, whether DPP-4i use was associated with progression to severe OA was examined, as reflected in the need for total hip replacement (THR) or total knee replacement (TKR). This investigation provides real-world, population-level evidence regarding the potential joint-protective role of DPP-4is and their relevance in OA management.

Methods

Data source

This national database study utilized the full NHIRD dataset from 2008 to 2018 to identify patients. 18 Following the implementation of data anonymization protocols to protect patient privacy, relevant information was extracted from the dataset, including patient diagnoses, treatments, and medication utilization. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Medical University, under the reference TMU JIRB-N201908055. Additionally, informed consent was waived in consideration of the study’s nature. The study was conducted according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines 19 ; it adheres to the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration. 20

Study design and participants

This study employed a matched new-user cohort design. Figure 1 illustrates the study design and patient selection process. To enhance diagnostic accuracy, patients newly diagnosed with T2DM between January 1, 2009, and December 31, 2017, were required to have at least three outpatient visits or one hospitalization with a T2DM diagnosis. The index date was the date of the first DPP-4 inhibitor prescription for users and the matched date for non-users. Patients were excluded if they had unknown sex or age; age <45 years; any diagnosis of OA before T2DM; OA diagnosed within 1 year after T2DM diagnosis; follow-up <12 months; interval <12 months between initiation of DPP-4i use and OA occurrence; DPP-4i use before T2DM diagnosis; any cancer; gout or other crystal arthropathies; immune-mediated inflammatory diseases including rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, psoriasis, psoriatic arthropathy, or inflammatory bowel disease; joint surgery other than THR/TKR; or THR/TKR before OA or a history of THR/TKR before T2DM diagnosis. Diagnostic and procedure codes are presented in Table S1. DPP-4i users and non-users were matched 1:1 by age (±1 year), sex, identical index year, and comparable Diabetes Complications and Severity Index (DCSI) scores. The DCSI is a 13-point scale scored from complications identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions (ICD-9 and ICD-10) diagnostic codes and laboratory data, and it predicts both mortality and the risk of hospitalization.21,22 The NHIRD dataset lacked corresponding laboratory data; thus, DCSI scores were calculated solely from ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes.

Outcome

The primary outcome was incident OA, which was identified by at least one diagnostic record with ICD-9-CM code 715 or ICD-10-CM codes M15–M19 in the NHIRD. As OA is clinically diagnosed, ICD diagnostic codes are appropriate for detecting incident OA. The secondary outcomes were THR and TKR, which were defined using procedure codes (ICD-9-CM 81.51 and 81.54; ICD-10-PCS 0SR9, 0SRB, 0SRC, and 0SRD; and NHI reimbursement codes 64162B and 64164B). Because these surgeries require strict administrative review for National Health Insurance reimbursement, the corresponding procedure codes are highly reliable. In prior studies, the NHIRD definitions of OA and joint replacement have shown acceptable validity.23,24 Patients were followed from the index date until the occurrence of outcomes, death, or the end of the study period (December 31, 2018).

DPP-4i exposure

DPP-4is (Table S2) were prescribed for patients with T2DM in the NHIRD, from which information on drug type, dosage, prescription date, and the total number of pills dispensed by the pharmacy were obtained. 25 DPP-4i dosage was defined using the World Health Organization defined daily dose (DDD), representing the average maintenance dose per day for a drug used for its main indication in adults. The cumulative defined daily dose (cDDD) was the sum of dispensed DDDs over the observation period. Because medication adherence or refill gaps cannot be directly measured in claims data, cDDD was utilized as a proxy for sustained exposure. Eligible patients with <28 cDDDs were classified as DPP-4i non-users, while those with ⩾28 cDDDs were classified as DPP-4i users. This threshold corresponded to approximately a month of regular medication use and was used to reduce exposure misclassification from short-term prescriptions. Furthermore, the DPP-4i users were divided into three subgroups based on tertiles of cDDD. The first tertile (low) group included 28–212 cDDDs; the second tertile (mid) group encompassed 213–452 cDDDs; and the third tertile (high) group comprised >453 cDDDs.

Comorbidities and concomitant medications

Potential confounders were determined by associating a given covariate with DPP-4i use based on the literature and identifying the direct or indirect association with concomitant medications and comorbidities, including liver cirrhosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), ischemic heart disease (IHD), hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and dyslipidemia. Comorbidities were identified by at least two diagnoses of a given disease within 180 days before or after the study entry date. Comorbidity codes are presented in Table S1. Pharmacological agents prescribed for OA or T2DM and statins were examined as concurrent medications within the study parameters (Table S2).

Statistical analysis

Additional information pertaining to the patient’s baseline characteristics, including age, sex, coexisting medical conditions, and DPP-4i doses, was collected. Age was categorized in 10-year intervals. The baseline characteristics of DPP-4i users and non-users were compared using the Chi-squared test and t test for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. In addition, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to median values of distributions. To understand the OA risk among DPP-4i users and non-users, the incidence rates (IRs) were calculated using the following equation: incidence = (new cases)/(population × timeframe). Cox regression models were employed to calculate both crude hazard ratio (HR) and adjusted HR (aHR) for relevant factors of OA risk. Baseline characteristics were used for model adjustment. Cumulative IRs of OA and TKR/THR among OA patients were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS for Windows version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and a two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

Figure 1 presents the study flow chart. Moreover, 1,282,796 patients with newly diagnosed T2DM from 2009 to 2017 were identified. After excluding patients based on the previously mentioned criteria, the eligible population was enrolled (n = 479,537) and grouped into DPP-4i users (n = 179,442) and non-users (n = 300,095). After matching the individuals according to the criteria delineated in the methodology, the study sample comprised 165,333 patients in the DPP-4i user and non-user groups. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of DPP-4i users and their matched controls. The mean age of DPP-4i users and non-users was 58.60 ± 9.53 years, with the same age distribution; 56.19% were males. The median follow-up time (interquartile range) for DPP-4i users and non-users was 3.61 (3) and 3.25 (2.77) years, respectively. Compared with non-users, DPP-4i users showed a higher prevalence of cirrhosis, COPD, hypertension, CKD, and dyslipidemia and a lower prevalence of IHD (Table 1). Except for steroids, there were also higher percentages of concomitant medication use in DPP-4i users than in non-users (Table 1).

Association of DPP-4i usage with OA risk in patients with T2DM

Among patients with newly diagnosed T2DM, 5957 and 13,424 events of OA occurred in the DPP-4i user and non-user groups, respectively (Table 2). The IR of OA was significantly lower in the DPP-4i user group (910.47 per 100,000 person-years) than in the DPP-4i non-user group (2241.22 per 100,000 person-years), with a crude HR of 0.41 (95% confidence interval (CI), 0.39–0.42) and an aHR of 0.42 (95% CI, 0.41–0.44) adjusting for age, sex, comorbidities, and medications (Table 2). DPP-4i users demonstrated a lower cumulative incidence of OA (the Kaplan–Meier log-rank test; p < 0.001) than DPP-4i non-users (Figure 2(a)). Moreover, the dose–response relationship was examined by categorizing the cumulative dosage into tertiles. A decrease in the aHR was observed as the cDDDs increased from the first to the third tertile, yielding aHRs of 0.70 (95% CI: 0.67–0.73), 0.51 (95% CI: 0.49–0.54), and 0.18 (95% CI: 0.17–0.20), respectively (Table 2).

Association of DPP-4i usage with THR and TKR risk in patients with T2DM diagnosed with OA

Further investigation assessed whether the use of DPP-4is slows the progression of OA in patients with T2DM by examining their IRs of THR and TKR. During follow-up of patients with T2DM diagnosed with OA, 115 THR events were observed in the DPP-4i user group and 304 THR events in the DPP-4i non-user group (Table 2). The IR of THR was significantly lower among DPP-4i users (17.55 per 100,000 person-years) than among DPP-4i non-users (50.5 per 100,000 person-years), with a crude HR of 0.34 (95% CI: 0.27–0.42) and an aHR of 0.38 (95% CI: 0.30–0.48). Consistent with these findings, DPP-4i users exhibited a lower cumulative incidence of THR than DPP-4i non-users (the Kaplan–Meier log-rank test; p < 0.001; Figure 2(b)). Furthermore, our findings revealed a decreasing trend in the aHRs as cDDDs increased across tertiles: 0.65 (95% CI: 0.48–0.87), 0.49 (95% CI: 0.36–0.66), and 0.10 (95% CI: 0.06–0.17), respectively (Table 2).

During follow-up of patients with T2DM diagnosed with OA, 621 TKR events occurred in the DPP-4i user group and 1321 TKR events in the DPP-4i non-user group (Table 2). The IR of TKR was significantly lower among DPP-4i users (94.75 per 100,000 person-years) than among DPP-4i non-users (219.51 per 100,000 person-years), with a crude HR of 0.42 (95% CI: 0.38–0.46) and an aHR of 0.42 (95% CI: 0.37–0.46). Consistent with these findings, DPP-4i users exhibited a lower cumulative incidence of TKR than non-users (the Kaplan–Meier log-rank test; p < 0.001; Figure 2(c)). Moreover, a dose–response relationship was observed with progressively lower aHR for TKR as cDDDs increased from the first to the third tertile: 0.73 (95% CI: 0.64–0.84), 0.55 (95% CI: 0.44–0.64), and 0.16 (95% CI: 0.13–0.20), respectively (Table 2).

Association of comorbidities and concurrent medications with OA risk in T2DM

In patients with T2DM, the risk of incident OA was influenced by age (patients aged 51–60 years designated as the reference group; Table 3). Those aged <50 years exhibited lower rates of incident OA; no significant increase was noted in incident OA in individuals aged >80 years compared to those aged 51–60 years (Table 3). The incidence of OA was higher in female patients (aHR: 1.62; 95% CI: 1.58–1.67) than in male patients (Table 3). Comorbidities such as COPD (aHR: 1.32; 95% CI: 1.23–1.42), IHD (aHR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.11–1.21), hypertension (aHR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.12–1.20), and dyslipidemia (aHR: 1.15; 95% CI: 1.11–1.19) were also associated with a higher risk of OA (Table 3). However, a lower risk of incident OA was observed in individuals with CKD (aHR: 0.75; 95% CI: 0.68–0.83). Additionally, a decreased risk of incident OA was observed in association with the concurrent use of metformin, meglitinides, thiazolidinediones, insulin, steroids, and GLP-1 receptor agonists (RA; aHR: 0.21; 95% CI: 0.14–0.32; Table 3). However, the use of sulfonylureas and statins was associated with a higher risk of incident OA (Table 3).

Discussion

T2DM and OA are highly prevalent chronic conditions that often coexist, imposing a substantial health and economic burden. Identifying antidiabetic therapies that may mitigate OA risk is crucial for clinical decision-making in internal medicine. In this large, nationwide matched cohort study, DPP-4i use was associated with a significantly lower risk of incident OA and OA progression, as reflected by decreased rates of THR and TKR. These findings indicate a disease-modifying effect of DPP-4is in OA among patients with T2DM and support their consideration in patients at increased risk of OA.

Among the analyzed antidiabetic drugs, DPP-4is and GLP-1 RAs emerged as the two classes with the greatest protective effect against OA. DPP-4i use was associated with a 58% reduction in OA risk (aHR: 0.42), while GLP-1 RAs were linked to an even greater risk reduction of 79% (aHR 0.21). These findings demonstrate that incretin-based therapies may confer joint-protective effects beyond glycemic control. Although DPP-4is and GLP-1 RAs act on the incretin pathway, their mechanisms relevant to OA differ. GLP-1 RAs induce substantial weight loss and improve metabolic profiles, which may reduce mechanical joint loading and systemic inflammation.26,27 Consistent with this hypothesis, the Shanghai Osteoarthritis Cohort reported improved Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index scores, attenuated cartilage damage, and fewer TKR surgeries among GLP-1 RA users with T2DM. 26

In contrast, DPP-4is are largely weight-neutral, indicating that their protective association with OA is mediated by mechanisms other than mechanical load reduction. 27 DPP-4 inhibition may protect joints by modulating inflammation and cellular senescence. DPP-4 expression has been associated with decreased AMPK and SIRT-1 pathway activity, leading to FOXO3A suppression and promoting cellular senescence.13,28 Prior studies demonstrated that DPP-4is, such as vildagliptin and gemigliptin, attenuate TNF-α-induced chondrocyte senescence and matrix degradation.15,16 Moreover, intra-articular injection of DPP-4is eliminated DPP-4-positive senescent chondrocytes and ameliorated OA progression in a destabilization of the medial meniscus mouse model. 17 These findings support a mechanistic framework wherein DPP-4 inhibition may mitigate OA progression through anti-inflammatory and anti-senescent pathways, independent of weight loss.

Although the overall profile of DPP-4is is favorable, one notable adverse event is DPP-4i-associated arthralgia. In 2015, the US Food and Drug Administration issued a safety communication noting cases of severe, sometimes disabling joint pain with DPP-4i use, which typically resolved after drug discontinuation.29,30 This adverse effect reflects acute inflammatory responses mediated by substrates, such as stromal cell-derived factor-1α, rather than structural joint degeneration. 31 These observations underscore the distinction between transient musculoskeletal symptoms and long-term OA risk, which may coexist within the same drug class.

Beyond incretin-based therapies, differences were observed among other antidiabetic drug classes. Metformin, meglitinides, thiazolidinediones, and insulin were associated with decreased OA risk. Metformin activates AMPK signaling and suppresses chondrocyte senescence and inflammation, consistent with prior observational studies reporting its lower incidence of OA compared with sulfonylurea use. 32 Thiazolidinediones, such as PPAR-γ agonists, possess anti-inflammatory and chondroprotective properties that contribute to their observed benefit. 33 In contrast, sulfonylureas and statins were associated with increased OA risk, reflecting weight gain, prolonged hyperinsulinemia, or adverse musculoskeletal effects.34,35 Although SGLT2 inhibitors have been associated with weight loss and decreased cardiovascular risk,36,37 in this study, their impact on OA risk could not be assessed due to limited follow-up after their introduction into the National Health Insurance system.

An association was observed between steroid use and decreased risk of incident OA. This observation should be interpreted cautiously. In this study, steroid exposure was defined using ATC codes that do not allow differentiation of administration routes, including intra-articular versus non-joint-targeted use. The association reflects anti-inflammatory or symptom-modifying effects that delay clinical diagnosis or healthcare utilization for OA, rather than a true disease-modifying effect on joint structure. In addition, residual confounding by indication could not be excluded, as steroids are commonly prescribed for inflammatory or allergic conditions that may influence healthcare-seeking behavior. Future studies with detailed information on administration routes and indications are warranted to clarify the relationship between steroid exposure and OA risk.

In addition to medication-related effects, several comorbid conditions were associated with an increased risk of OA in patients with T2DM, including COPD, IHD, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, consistent with prior studies on the role of systemic inflammation, vascular dysfunction, and metabolic dysregulation in OA pathogenesis.38–41 These findings emphasize the complex relationship between systemic health conditions and the risk of developing OA in T2DM patients. Notably, the inverse association with CKD may reflect differences in prescribing patterns, as patients with CKD often used insulin and less frequently received sulfonylureas.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, to include all patients exposed to DPP-4is, those using combination therapy with SGLT2 inhibitors or metformin were also enrolled. Some of the observed benefits may reflect additive or synergistic effects, although the short duration of SGLT2i use in our cohort likely minimized its influence. Second, as this analysis was based on Taiwan’s NHIRD, variables such as body mass index (BMI), laboratory data, imaging findings, and lifestyle factors were unavailable. Obesity is a major risk factor for OA, and weight loss mitigates its progression; however, unlike GLP-1 RA, DPP-4is is weight-neutral, suggesting that our findings are unlikely to be explained by weight reduction. Nevertheless, the inability to stratify by BMI prevents clear differentiation of weight-related from weight-independent mechanisms. Third, although OA was identified using ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM diagnostic codes (M15–M19), OA subtypes and joint involvement patterns could not be distinguished. The NHIRD lacks radiographic and detailed clinical information required for phenotype classification, and OA is often coded using nonspecific diagnostic codes in administrative claims data. Therefore, analyses of specific OA subtypes, including erosive hand OA, were not feasible. Fourth, while our results were adjusted for multiple comorbidities and medications, residual confounding by indication could not be excluded. Finally, the absence of an active comparator drug class restricts causal inference about relative drug effects. Despite these limitations, this nationwide matched cohort study provides valuable real-world evidence of the potential protective role of DPP-4is against OA in patients with T2DM and provides a rationale for future confirmatory studies and clinical translation.

Conclusion

Our study shows that DPP-4 inhibitors are associated with a significantly lower incidence of OA and reduced need for joint replacement in patients with T2DM. Together with the observed differences across other antidiabetic drug classes, these findings indicate that therapeutic choices in diabetes influence musculoskeletal outcomes. While causality cannot be established in this observational study, the results provide real-world evidence with crucial implications for both diabetes management and the broader burden of OA.
