Using Radial Flow Stream Splitting Columns to Enable High Through‐Put High‐Performance Liquid Chromatography for the Analysis of Low Molecular Weight Polystyrenes Using UV/Vis and Charged Aerosol Detection
Authors: C E Karlsen, J A Cravino, F Li, A Soliven, R A Shalliker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMass‐based detectors, such as, the charged aerosol detector are limited to relatively low flow rates since the mobile phase needs to be removed prior to the detection source. A new column technology, referred to as radial flow stream splitting (RFS) whereby flow can be separated in the radial cross flow directly within the outlet of the chromatography column, enables separations to be undertaken at high flow rates, with high efficiency, but low volume load to the detector. In this study we show the improvement in the separation performance of low molecular weight polystyrenes using columns fitted with RFS end fittings and dual detection with a charged aerosol detector and a UV detector in parallel. There is a reduction in operation pressure by 30% and then a subsequent increase in analysis time by up to three to fourfold, where the detection source is ordinarily flow rate limited. Subsequently, flow rates up to 8 mL/min were tested with some separations being completed within 4 min.Keywords: charged aerosol detector, high‐speed separations, polystyrenes, radial flow stream splitting

Introduction

There is an ever‐increasing need for high‐throughput (HTP) high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) that can decrease analysis time while maintaining the same level of accuracy and precision. What constrains the speed of an analysis by HPLC is maintaining adequate efficiency to meet the separation needs of the samples being tested, the available pressure, that is, system limits for routine operation, optimum flow rate for the detector in use, and the liquid interface between the column and the detector [1, 2, 3]. Typical examples of flow rate limiting detectors are the mass spectrometer (MS), evaporative light scattering detector (ELSD), and the charged aerosol detector (CAD). Each of these detectors is mass based, and they require that all mobile phase be evaporated from the flow stream prior to the sample reaching the detection source [4, 5, 6]. Previous strategies for increasing the analysis speed when the detector was flow rate limited were largely confined to using one of the three techniques: (1) post‐column flow splitters, (2) narrow bore columns, and (3) active flow technology (AFT) columns (although AFT columns were never commercialized) [7, 8, 9]. Each of these three approaches has varying success.

Post‐column flow splitters work by segmenting the flow carrying the elution band toward the chosen detector and the sample is split in the axial direction of the solvent flow [10]. Using narrower bore columns enables the linear velocity of the separation process to be maintained, but with a reduced volumetric flow. A 2.1 mm i.d. narrow bore column transports around one‐fifth the flow of a 4.6 mm i.d. analytical scale column at the same linear velocity. Hence the speed of the assay is maintained, but the amount of solvent entering the detector is reduced, but even so, post‐column flow stream splitting may still be required for optimum detection [10]. Narrow bore columns have found widespread use in the field of HPLC‐with mass spectrometric detection HPLC‐MS [10, 11]. AFT columns enable a parallel segmented flow (PSF) [12, 13] to be undertaken, whereby, the flow is split in the radial cross section, rather than along the axial direction of flow. These columns allow high volumetric flow rates to flow rate limiting detectors in the same manner as for a post‐column axial flow stream split, but with the advantage that the radial flow stream (RFS) split was a more efficient way to split flow than the axial split. Specifically, the difference being, RFS splitting removes the tailing region of a band, whereas the axial flow stream split includes the tailing region of the band. Hence the chromatographic performance achieved on the AFT/PSF columns was higher [12, 13]. Limitations of the AFT column technology are that the column is manufacturer specific, and can only be used in AFT mode, that is, the column can not function as a conventional column and must always be used in parallel flow stream splitting.

Recently, a new column outlet end‐fitting was designed that segments the radial central flow of the mobile phase containing the solute from the peripheral wall region as it exits the column [14]. This design is based on the wide bore column outlet (WBOC) platform whereby, the outlet end of the column has a bore that is approximately the same as that of the column inner diameter. The end of the column is protected by a frit that is housed between the WBCO platform and the column itself. Inserts can be seated into the WBOC platform, some of which are designed to house RFS splitting inserts. The design of this WBOC platform, in concert with the RFS splitting enabled four advantages: (1) High efficiency separations could be achieved because the user can choose to channel either the radial section of the flow through the detection source (eliminating the tailing portion of the band), or alternatively channel the wall region of flow through the detection source (eliminating the central portion of the band); (2) the pressure in RFS splitting columns is reduced as a direct consequence of there being a second exit port [14, 15, 16]; (3) the column with the RFS splitting insert could also be operated as a conventional column simply by blocking the peripheral outlet port or by utilizing a single port insert in the WBOC platform; and (4) the WBOC platform can be retrofitted to almost any currently manufactured column, and thus enable RFS splitting. Operation as a conventional column, or the possibility of a retrofit to any preexisting column was not possible in the design of the AFT/PSF column. This new outlet design that enables RFS splitting has demonstrated in previous studies that it can decrease column pressure (by as much as +30%) [14, 15, 16], increase detector sensitivity (in UV/Vis) by up to one‐third when compared to a conventionally run column, increase column efficiency by up to 37% in particle packed columns and in some monoliths by more than 75% [14], and allow for decreased sample run times as higher flow rates can be used without a comparable loss in separation efficiency.

Although there are numerous advantages with respect to the utilization of RFS columns in separations that incorporate UV/Vis detection, few studies have been conducted using mass‐based detectors. In the present study we are investigating the application of an RFS column with a CAD. The CAD is touted as a “universal detector” but suffers from several limitations: First, the CAD is flow rate limited, with an apparent upper limit of around 2 mL/min, although other studies from our research group have shown that performance deteriorates as the flow rate increases [17], especially beyond 1 mL/min. However, this may be mobile phase dependent. Second, the CAD is reportedly a quasi‐linear detector and the process of linearization of the response is solute dependent, and perhaps even concentration dependent [18]. Hence, quantification using a CAD is complicated.

Nevertheless, our previous work on the analysis of polystyrenes using the CAD indicated that a power function of 2.0 was the most appropriate function for concentration–response linearization when polystyrenes were the sample [19]. This was supported in a separate theoretical study [18]. As continuation of our prior work on polystyrenes, we present a study that utilizes RFS splitting columns that enable higher analysis through‐put, but without placing a flow demand on the CAD since the flow stream can be radially split, thus enabling the CAD to operate at the optimal flow rate most suited to the detector.

Experimental

Chemicals and Reagents

HPLC grade methanol and HPLC grade dichloromethane were obtained from Fisher Chemicals (Loughborough, UK).

Sample Preparation

Polystyrene standard material (Mw 770) was obtained from Sigma–Aldrich (Castle Hill, New South Wales, Australia). A stock polystyrene standard was prepared by weighing 226 mg of polystyrene into an HPLC vial and then dissolved in 1.0 mL of dichloromethane. The polystyrene stock was ultrasonicated to ensure full dissolution. From this stock the polystyrene was diluted 10‐fold in methanol forming a 22 670 ppm (technical) solution of polystyrene.

Equipment

All samples were analyzed using a Thermo Scientific Dionex Ultimate 3000 System consisting of an RS autosampler, RS Pump, RS Diode Array Detector equipped with a semi‐micro flow cell (2.5 µL volume, 7 mm light path, and max pressure of 120 bar) and a Corona Ultra RS Detector (North Ryde, New South Wales, Australia).

The column used for this study was an Avantor ACE Excel C18 column (100 × 4.6 mm, 5 µm dp) obtained from Avantor (Theale, UK). A three‐port RFS splitting end fitting was adapted to this column by Chromaspeed Pty Ltd (Tonsley, Australia).

All assays were undertaken in isocratic mode using 100% methanol mobile phase. When separations were conducted using a conventional column, the CAD and UV detectors were in series, with the UV detector preceding the CAD. When the column was operated in RFS splitting mode the UV detector and the CAD were in parallel, with 50% of the flow sent to both detectors. The CAD was connected to the peripheral port of the RFS insert. When a conventional format column was used the flow rates were either 1 mL/min or 2 mL/min, and when an RFS splitting column was used the flow rates were either 1, 2, 4, or 8 mL/min. The operating conditions are noted in Table 1. The column was operated at ambient temperature (∼21°C).

A needle wash of DCM before and after each injection was set to ensure no carryover of polystyrene from previous injections. The calibration curve for each run was made by varying injection volume. The injection volumes used were 2, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 50 µL. CAD settings were set to a filter of 0, power function of 1, and nebulizer temperature was set to 30°C and 20 Hz data collection rate. The UV was set to 262 nm wavelength and 20 Hz collection rate. Varying flow rates were used for each calibration sequence run, depending on the desired flow to the CAD and the type of column utilized, that is, either a conventional column format or an RFS splitting format.

Data Collection and Analysis

The data was initially processed and exported from the Chromeleon 7 software and then further processed using Microsoft Excel (2024) for data collection and statistical analysis. The graphs were produced in OriginPro 8.5 using Origin labs.

Results and Discussion

In the present work, we perform a variety of tests on oligostyrenes using dual detection—UV and the CAD. The UV detector is a well‐established linear detector, while the CAD is known to have a nonlinear response with respect to concentration. Utilizing the dual‐detection process enables a better understanding of the CAD and this study might guide applications for the analysis of non‐UV responsive polymers. This work follows from our previous study [19] and in this follow‐up we are interested in increasing the sample through‐put by utilizing RFS splitting chromatography columns to overcome the flow rate limitations of the CAD.

The upper flow rate limit of the CAD is around 2 mL/min (since solvent needs to be evaporated prior to solute detection in the CAD). The chromatogram in Figure 1 shows the oligomeric separations of the oligostyrene sample obtained from a conventionally operated column using a flow rate of 2 mL/min with UV detection (Figure 1a—red trace) and a CAD (Figure 1b—black trace). The 10 oligomers were well separated, although from Oligomer 6 onward there is some peak distortion due to the minor separation of the diastereomers. The resolution between the oligomers as detected using the CAD is less than that obtained using the UV detector. This is possibly due to the CAD being in series with, and following, the UV detector, hence additional band broadening has occurred. For comparative purposes, each of the chromatographic responses was normalized to the height of the tallest peak (that is, the detection output was divided by the response of the tallest peak, so that the responses ranged from 0 to 1). The subsequent normalized response from the CAD was then raised to the power of “2”. Subsequently, the CAD chromatographic response was in almost perfect alignment for each oligomer fraction with the UV detection response (alignment with respect to both relative intensity and peak width) (see Figure 2). This is in agreement with our prior work undertaken at the flow rate of 1 mL/min and suggests that the nonlinear response–concentration relationship for these oligomers using the CAD can be linearized using the application of a power function of 2, and that the power function of 2 is valid at flow rates of 1 mL/min (see reference [19]) and 2 mL/min. It is worth noting that the decreased resolution observed in the chromatogram derived from the CAD, as illustrated in Figure 1, has been restored to match almost perfectly the resolution observed using the UV detector. Such a result is due to the application of the power function, which serves to decrease the standard deviation of the peak, hence decreasing the peak width and improving resolution.

In the analysis undertaken here, using the conventional column operating at 2 mL/min, the back pressure was 117 bar, and this low pressure would give opportunity to undertake the analysis at higher flow rates, except, however, the CAD has a flow rate limit of around 2 mL/min. Therefore, to increase sample through‐put using a conventional column, the analyst has two options: (1) use a post‐column flow stream split or (2) use a narrow bore column. Option (2) is unlikely to be suitable on this particular CAD since it is not designed for narrow columns, but that being said, ThermoFisher Scientific do have CADs designed for narrow bore, but one was not available for this work.

Alternatively, the analysis could be undertaken using an RFS splitting column [14, 15, 16]. These columns split the flow in the radial cross section of the column at the internal exit point of the column itself, rather than the post column (external) tubing. Splitting the flow in the radial direction, as opposed to the axial direction (in the post‐column flow stream) offers several advantages: (1) The RFS split provides a higher column efficiency, in part due to the removal of the wall effect and in part due to an improvement in the flow convergence at the column outlet [14, 15, 16] and (2) the RFS split reduces the column back pressure [14–16]. The combination of reduced pressure and higher efficiency means that separations can be undertaken at higher flow rates, without the loss in performance that would be observed using a conventional column.

In a demonstration of the advantages afforded by the RFS splitting column, the segmentation ratio, that is, the amount of solvent exiting the peripheral port relative to the amount of solvent exiting the radial central port was adjusted to 50/50. The UV detector was connected to the radial central port and the CAD was connected to the peripheral port. The flow rate through the column was increased to 4 mL/min, and subsequently, 2 mL/min (50% of the flow) was directed to the CAD (the upper limit of the flow). The chromatogram in Figure 3 (red trace) illustrates the normalized UV response at 4 mL/min, while the chromatogram (black trace) illustrates the normalized CAD response. An important feature of the RFS column is that the multiport outlet fitting allows for a substantial reduction in pressure [14, 15, 16]. In effect, had the conventional column been operated at 4 mL/min the column pressure would be around 232 bar, but in the RFS splitting column the back pressure was 173 bar. Similar to the data obtained on the conventional column using the CAD, some loss of resolution was observed relative to that of the separation obtained with UV detection. When a power function of 2 was applied to the CAD normalized output data, the overlay between the CAD and UV detection outputs were again almost perfectly coincidental (Figure 4), and the decreased resolution observed in the CAD response was recovered to match that of the UV response. The analysis time was decreased to 8 min (half that compared to the conventional column operating at 2 mL/min).

As we have mentioned, an important aspect associated with the operation of the RFS splitting column is that they operate at reduced pressure compared to a conventional column. As noted above, there was an almost 68 bar pressure reduction at the flow rate of 4 mL/min for the column employed in this work. This subsequently allows scope to further increase the flow rate through the column and operate within the limits of the system pressure (limited in this case by the column 408 bar pmax). By adjusting the flow segmentation ratio for a 50/50 split to 75/25 (75 through the central port/25 through the peripheral port) and connecting the CAD to the peripheral port and the UV detector to the radial central port, the flow rate could be increased to 8 mL/min, with just 2 mL/min passing to the CAD. The chromatograms in Figure 5a,b show the oligomeric separations of the polystyrene sample obtained using the column in the RFS mode of operation. Figure 5a is the chromatogram obtained from the UV/Vis detector while Figure 5b is the chromatogram obtained from the CAD. As was the case when the column was operated in conventional mode, the oligomers were baseline resolved with UV detection, but some loss in resolution was observed with charged aerosol detection—likely as a result of the CAD capturing flow from the peripheral port where the column packing heterogeneity results in broader peak profiles. Normalization of the output responses from both the UV and the CAD, then application of a power function of 2 to the CAD response resulted in near perfect alignment of the relative peak heights and peak widths between both detectors, and most of the resolution had been restored in the case of the CAD chromatogram (see Figure 6). With respect to the loss of resolution at the high flow rates it is important to consider that the importance of this loss will depend upon the purpose of the experiment. If for example, the purpose is for quantification then the analyst would need to utilize a slower flow rate to satisfy the necessary resolution criteria. However, if the purpose was for high through‐put screening, then resolution would not be the most important aspect of the separation, rather through‐put would dominate. Hence the higher flow would be preferential as more work could be processed in the shorter time frame. Further, in the example utilized herein, the peak capacity of the separation is saturated, leaving very little room for the displacement of components without overlap. In many instances, as for example, the analysis of less complex samples, the peak capacity may not be saturated and hence there may be more available separation space that would allow for operation at higher flow rates.

An important point to note in the separations shown in Figures 5 and 6 is that the analysis time was decreased fourfold when operation was in RFS mode, consistent with the fourfold increase in flow rate (8 mL/min compared to 2 mL/min). In RFS mode, the analysis time was 3.5 min, and in conventional mode, 15 min.

Irrespective of the mode of operation and the flow rate employed in either mode, the amount of solvent consumed was approximately constant. For example, at 4 mL/min in the RFS mode of operation, the separation was completed in 7.5 min, hence 30 mL of solvent was used. While in the conventional mode of operation at 2 mL/min, the separation was completed in 15 min, which required 30 mL of solvent.

Another important aspect to note is that at 8 mL/min in RFS mode the system back pressure was 302 bar, while in conventional mode of operation at 2 mL/min the pressure was 117 bar. This represents a 2.6 times increase, significantly less than a fourfold increase given that the flow rate was increased by a factor of 4.

Conclusion

The analysis of oligostyrenes was undertaken using reversed‐phase HPLC with UV and CAD detections. The relationship between response and concentration was linear with UV detection, but nonlinear with the CAD. That being said, linearization of the CAD concentration–response relationship was achieved using a power function of 2 that was applied to the output data from the CAD. This was validated in a prior study. Separations were successfully undertaken with a flow rate of 2 mL/min through the CAD (the upper flow rate level of the CAD), but higher flow rates through the column were achievable courtesy of the RFS splitting process. Specifically, up to 8 mL/min through the column was achievable.

Application of separations at higher flow rates, resulted in a reduction in analysis time, and subsequently this reduced the amount of time that the instrument is operational. In part, the period of time an instrument is operational is related to the economics of the analysis and operating costs. Further studies will need to be undertaken to ascertain the balance between power, energy, and cost in order to determine the optimal operational conditions, but it is clear, that RFS splitting columns will provide additional scope to address greening the separations sector.

Perhaps not immediately obvious from the work presented herein, is that operation of the HPLC separation at high flow rates opens scope for rapid screening protocols. Here, resolution might be sacrificed for the purpose of obtaining a first‐level information and then the appropriate samples can be sent for more detailed analysis as required. The separation of the oligostyrenes demonstrates this facet nicely, since the peak capacity of the column is saturated, and the subsequent loss in resolution at higher flow is easily noted. But in many instances the separation space may not be fully saturated, and the loss of resolution might not be important. The purpose of through‐put versus resolution can only be gauged on an individual preferred basis, but the work presented here shows the potential scope. Importantly, for samples that require a detection source whereby the solvent must be removed, the RFS column enables operation in HTP assays where the detection source is not the limiting factor.
