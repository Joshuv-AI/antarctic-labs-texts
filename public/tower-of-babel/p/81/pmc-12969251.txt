Evaluating the Efficacy of Polyphenol‐Enriched Plant Extracts and Pathway Modulators in the Bone Marrow to Suppress Breast Cancer Progression
Authors: Hayarpi Javrushyan, Gayane Petrosyan, Marika Mróz, Barbara Kusznierewicz, Yem Alharithi, Mikayel Ginovyan, Alina Maloyan, Nikolay Avtandilyan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDisseminated tumor cells (DTCs) in the bone marrow can survive in a dormant state and later reawaken to drive metastatic relapse. Hypoxia‐driven angiogenic signaling (HIF‐1α → VEGF), matrix remodeling (MMP‐2), and inflammation (COX‐2, TNF‐α) are central determinants of niche permissiveness for colonization. We investigated whether rational combinations of selected plant extracts with pathway‐targeting small molecules can reprogram the bone marrow microenvironment, suppress pro‐metastatic signaling, and limit tumor progression by promoting dormancy‐like states. Complementary in vitro and in vivo experiments were performed. Human breast cancer cell lines (MDA‐MB‐231, MCF‐7) were exposed to defined plant extracts and to modulators of nitric‐oxide/arginase signaling; endpoints included HIF‐1α, COX‐2, and MMP‐2 expression (Western blot, immunocytochemistry), and migration/invasion (real‐time assays). LC‐HRMS profiled extract composition. In vivo efficacy was tested in a DMBA‐induced rat breast cancer model with systemic administration of extracts alone and in combination with small‐molecule modulators; bone‐marrow biomarkers (VEGF‐α, TNF‐α, IL‐2, MMP‐2, COX‐2) were quantified by ELISA, bone‐marrow cells by histology, and tumor burden and survival were recorded. Plant extract treatments attenuated HIF‐1α, COX‐2, and MMP‐2 in cancer cells and markedly reduced TNF‐α‐driven migration and invasion in vitro. LC‐HRMS identified a reproducible polyphenol‐rich fingerprint across active fractions. In vivo, combined therapies produced consistent decreases in bone‐marrow VEGF‐α, MMP‐2, and COX‐2 and a concomitant increase in IL‐2; these biochemical changes correlated with substantially reduced tumor size, lower incidence of progressive disease, and improved survival compared with controls. Differential responses were observed depending on the small‐molecule companion (e.g., L‐NAME versus arginase inhibition), indicating pathway‐specific modulation of the niche. Histopathology confirmed reduced marrow atypia and diminished tumor infiltration in responding groups. Our integrated dataset supports a model in which coordinated suppression of hypoxia/angiogenesis, matrix remodeling, and inflammatory signaling in the bone‐marrow niche—achieved by combining plant extracts with targeted modulators—constrains metastatic progression, plausibly by enforcing or sustaining dormancy of disseminated tumor cells. These findings identify testable mechanistic nodes (HIF‐1α, VEGF, MMP‐2, COX‐2) for follow‐up causal studies and support further fractionation and translational evaluation of active phytochemicals as adjuncts to niche‐targeted anti‐metastatic strategies.

Introduction

Breast cancer affects 1 in 8 women in their lifetime, making it not just a health crisis but a global challenge that impacts millions of families [1]. According to the latest data, approximately 1 958 310 new cancer cases and 609 820 cancer deaths are projected to occur in the United States alone [2]. Globally, cancer accounted for nearly 10 million deaths in 2020 (WHO). The global cancer burden is expected to rise to 28.4 million cases by 2040, a 47% increase from 2020, with a larger increase in transitioning countries due to demographic changes and increasing risk factors associated with globalization [3]. Breast cancer (BC) is a heterogeneous disease that involves both hereditary and environmental causes. One of the main barriers to achieving an effective treatment is resistance to therapies and the recurrence of the tumor [4, 5, 6]. Various advanced treatments and novel approaches, such as antibody‐drug conjugation systems, nanoparticles, and breast cancer stem cell‐based therapies, have been developed for BC patients [7]. Notwithstanding, BC continues to be the leading cause of death for thousands of young women globally [1]. Furthermore, the properties of the BC cells migrate to other tissues, especially in the bone marrow (BM), and successful treatment needs more studies [8, 9].

BM is a preferred site of metastasis for many solid tumors and is associated with poor prognosis and significant morbidity [10]. Recent studies show that cancer cells colonize specialized niches in the BM to support their long‐term propagation. However, the precise location and mechanisms that mediate niche interactions remain unknown [8]. BM stromal cells have been shown to promote the growth and survival of various tumors, including BC [11]. Furthermore, breast cancer cells start to produce certain proteins that are specific to bones, as well as proteins vital for proper breast development and breastfeeding, enabling them to prosper in bone and trigger bone destruction [12, 13].

Herbal plants have significant, yet underexplored, potential for treating various pathologies, including cancer. Using plant extract compounds to simultaneously target various cellular cancer characteristics, while also regulating the L‐arginine pathway through inhibitors, is an innovative approach and holds great promise in cancer treatment [14]. Our previous data have demonstrated the anticancer potential of Alchemilla smirnovii Juz. (AS), Rumex obtusifolius (RO), and Inula helenium (IH) herbal plant extracts [15, 16]. Specifically, RO and HA extracts in combination with nor‐NOHA and L‐NAME possess promising antioxidant potential, immunostimulatory, anti‐metastatic, anti‐inflammatory, and anti‐angiogenic properties in both in vitro and DMBA‐induced breast cancer in vivo rat model [17, 18, 19].

A noticeable gap in our knowledge exists regarding the scarcity of studies that demonstrate the changes occurring in BM cells following effective treatment and positive outcomes. In this study, we aim to assess whether our combined treatment model impacts BM cells, modulates breast cancer dormancy, and potentially prevents recurrence. To establish a link between BM alterations and the risk of BC relapse, we focused on inflammation and angiogenesis biomarkers, such as VEGF‐α, TNF‐α, COX‐2, and MMP‐2. Hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α (HIF‐1α) orchestrates a broad hypoxic transcriptional program that includes VEGF family members and other mediators of angiogenesis and survival, thus promoting the formation of supportive metastatic niches [20]. Similarly, matrix metalloproteinase‐2 (MMP‐2) and cyclooxygenase‐2 (COX‐2) are central effectors of extracellular matrix remodeling and inflammatory signaling that enable tumor invasion and lymphangiogenesis [21]. Together, these pathways form an integrated hypoxia/inflammation/ECM axis that can determine whether disseminated tumor cells survive, proliferate, or enter prolonged dormancy in secondary sites such as the bone marrow [22].

Materials and Methods

Materials

Chemicals for cell culturing and breast cancer were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (USA) and Abcam. IL‐2 (ab100769), TNF‐α (ab100785), VEGF‐α (ab100786), COX‐2 (ab210574), MMP‐2 (ab213910) rat ELISA kits, and antibodies HIF1a (ab179483), MMP‐2 (ab92536), and COX‐2 (ab179800), Goat anti‐rabbit IgG HandL (Alexa Fluor488, ab150077; Alexa Fluor647, ab150115), anti‐beta actin (ab8227), anti‐ErbB2/HER2 antibody (ab237715) and anti‐rabbit IgG HandL (HRP, ab6721) for WB and ICC/IF were purchased from Abcam. Chlorogenic acid (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), caffeic acid (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), quercetin (Extrasynthese, Genay, France), kaempferol (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), p‐coumaric acid (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), protocatechuic acid (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), (+)‐catechin (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), (−)‐epicatechin (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), ellagic acid (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), ferulic acid (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

Plant Material and Preparation of Crude Extract

The RO, IH, and AS were harvested from the Tavush region of Armenia (1500–1900 m above sea level). Dr. Narine Zakaryan, who identified plant material, carried out the identification of plant material at the YSU Department of Botany and Mycology. Plant materials were deposited at the Herbarium of YSU, where the Voucher specimen serial number was given. The grounded plant materials were extracted by maceration with ethanol (96%) at a 10:1 solvent‐to‐sample ratio (v/w) [23]. The stock solutions of the samples for in vivo assays were prepared by dissolving dried crude extracts in pure DMSO, and they were diluted with saline (the final concentration was 1% v/v) [23]. 500 μL of the extract was dried, and the dry weight was determined by weighing each sample.

LC‐Q‐Orbitrap HRMS Analysis of Plant Extracts

Qualitative analyses were performed on a Dionex UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). For AS and IH, a Kinetex column (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm; Phenomenex) was used, whereas for RO, separations were performed on a Synergi Hydro‐RP A column (150 × 4.5 mm, 4 μm; Phenomenex). The mobile phase consisted of water (A) and acetonitrile (B), each containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid; flow rate 0.8 mL min−1. Injection volumes and gradients were as follows: Alchemilla smirnovii Juz.: 2 μL; 0 min, 10% B; 25 min, 25% B; 27 min, 100% B; 30 min, 100% B; Inula helenium : 6 μL; 0 min, 10% B; 20 min, 50% B; 25 min, 100% B; 30 min, 100% B; Rumex obtusifolius : 1 μL; 0 min, 10% B; 25 min, 40% B; 30 min, 100% B; 35 min, 100% B.

The chromatographic system was coupled to a PDA detector (UV–Vis) and a Q Exactive Focus quadrupole‐Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany) equipped with a HESI‐II source. MS detection was carried out in negative ion mode with the following source settings: sheath/aux/sweep gas 35/15/3 (arb), spray voltage 2.5 kV, S‐lens RF 50, capillary 350°C, heater 300°C. Full‐scan MS: resolution 70 000 (at m/z 200), AGC target 1.0 × 106, maximum injection time auto, scan range m/z 120–1200. Data‐dependent MS2: resolution 17 500, isolation window 3.0 m/z, normalized collision energy (NCE) 30, AGC target 1.0 × 106, maximum injection time auto.

Raw high‐resolution MS data were processed in Compound Discoverer (v. 2.1; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Major metabolites were annotated based on accurate mass and MS/MS fragmentation patterns by matching against a custom in‐house library of phytochemicals compiled from the literature and implemented within the software.

Semi‐Quantitative Analysis of Major Phenolic Compounds by LC‐PDA

Standard solutions were prepared by dissolving 1 mg of each reference compound in 1 mL of 70% (v/v) ethanol. Exception: ellagic acid was first dissolved as a 1 mg mL−1 stock in 0.1 M NaOH. Calibration curves were obtained by LC–PDA analysis of serial dilutions of the standards prepared in 70% ethanol, integrating peak areas at 270 nm (e.g., flavan‐3‐ols, ellagitannins/ellagic acid, flavonols) or 325 nm (hydroxycinnamic acids). Limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were defined as the absolute amounts giving signal‐to‐noise ratios of 3:1 and 10:1, respectively, using peak height. Noise was taken as the baseline fluctuation in blank injections (70% ethanol) measured in a retention‐time window adjacent to each analyte peak. Linear regression (peak area vs. concentration) was used to obtain the calibration equation and coefficient of determination (r2). For analytes with available standards, contents were determined by external calibration with the corresponding authentic standard. For analytes without available standards, contents were semi‐quantified against a structurally related compound from the same class, and are reported as equivalents (e.g., ellagitannins as ellagic acid equivalents, flavan‐3‐ols as (+)‐catechin equivalents, hydroxycinnamates as chlorogenic acid equivalents). The chromatographic column and separation conditions were identical to those used for the LC‐Q‐Orbitrap HRMS method [24, 25].

Post‐Column Derivatization With ABTS

Profiles of polyphenols and antioxidants were obtained using an HPLC–PDA system (Agilent Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA) coupled to a Pinnacle PCX post‐column derivatization instrument (Pickering Laboratories Inc., Mountain View, CA, USA) and a UV–Vis detector (Agilent Technologies). Chromatographic separation conditions were identical to those used for the LC–HRMS analysis. UV chromatograms before derivatization were recorded at 270 nm with the PDA detector. The column effluent was then directed to the post‐column module, where it was mixed with a methanolic ABTS solution (1 mM) delivered at 0.10 mL min−1 and passed through a reaction coil (1.0 mL, 130°C). The resulting ABTS assay profiles were monitored at 734 nm with the UV–Vis detector. The procedure followed by Kusznierewicz et al. with minor modifications [24, 25].

Cell Culture

Human triple‐negative breast cancer MDA‐MB‐231 (cat # HTB‐26) cell culture was obtained from ATCC and maintained in DMEM medium supplemented with L‐glutamine (2 mmol/L), sodium pyruvate (200 mg/L), fetal bovine serum (100 mL/L), and antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/L streptomycin). Cells were grown at 37°C under a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 in a phcbi (Japan) as described before [18]. MCF‐7 human breast adenocarcinoma cells (HTB‐22, obtained from ATCC) were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 10 μg/mL human recombinant insulin, penicillin (100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator (phcbi). For MCF‐7 migration assays, cells in the upper chamber of a CIM‐16 plate were starved in 0.5% FBS, and the lower chamber contained normal growth medium. Primary Renal Mixed Epithelial Cells; Normal, Human (HREC, PCS‐400‐012) were obtained from ATCC and cultured according to the supplier's protocol. Cells were maintained in Renal Epithelial Cell Basal Medium (ATCC PCS‐400‐030) supplemented with the Renal Epithelial Cell Growth Kit (ATCC PCS‐400‐040) with 0.5% FBS to support proliferation. Cultures were kept at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

MTT Cytotoxicity Test

As previously detailed, the inhibitory effects of the Herbal extracts on the proliferation of MDA‐MB‐231, MCF‐7, and HREC cell lines were assessed using the MTT assay [18]. The assay was performed in three experiments, each containing four technical replicates. Cytotoxicity was determined by calculating the percentage of growth inhibition in cells treated with the plant extract compared to control cells treated only with the solvent (resulting in a final concentration of 1% ethanol), which was designated as 100% growth.

Western Blots

MDA‐MB‐231 and MCF‐7 cells (5 × 105 cells per well) were cultured in 6‐well plates and incubated for 24 h. Then, the cell medium (900 μL) was refreshed, and the cells were treated with 100 μL of PBS + 1% ethanol solution or 1% DMSO (control) or test compounds with the following final concentrations in these groups (for MDA‐MB‐231): 1. Control cells (DMSO), 2. Control cells + Herb (IH, AS), 3. Cells + CoCl2 (200 μM), 4. Cells + CoCl₂ + Herb (0.5 mg/mL, IH, AS), and another experimental series included (for MDA‐MB‐231 and MCF‐7): 1. Control cells (DMSO), 2. Cells + 10 ng/mL TNFα, 3. Cells + TNFα + Herbs (IH, AS, RO). After 24 h, the cells were harvested. Cells were suspended in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Abcam). Total protein concentrations in the fraction were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) (Bio‐Rad Laboratories). Total proteins (20 μg) were separated on 4%–20% precast linear gradient gels (Bio‐Rad Laboratories), transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and blocked with 5% (w/v) nonfat milk in TBST for 1 h. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with the primary antibody (HIF1a 1:1000, MMP‐2 1:2500, COX‐2 1:1000, HER2 1:1000, β‐actin 1:2500) diluted in 1% nonfat milk (w/v) in TBST and detected using an appropriate peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibody (anti‐rabbit IgG HandL (HRP) 1:10000). Products were visualized by ECL chemiluminescence (Millipore). Band intensities were measured using the Chemidoc (Bio‐Rad Laboratories). ImageJ software was used to measure the intensities of the protein bands.

Immunocytochemistry (ICC)

MDA‐MB‐231 and MCF‐7 cells were plated in a 96‐well black cell culture microplate and incubated for 24 h. For ICC analysis, the groups were: (1) Control cells (DMSO), (2) Cells + 10 ng/mL TNFα, (3) Cells + TNFα + Herbs (0.5 mg/mL, IH, AS, RO); for MCF‐7, additional groups: (1) Control cells (DMSO), (2) Cells + 200 μM CoCl2, (3) Cells + CoCl2 + Herbs (0.5 mg/mL, IH, AS, RO). TNF‐α and CoCl2 were applied for 4 h, after which the medium was replaced with fresh medium, and the tested plant extracts were added. The cells were then incubated for 20 h, followed by fixation. After the indicated treatment, cells were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized with 1% Triton X‐100, blocked with 1% BSA, and incubated with primary antibody (MMP‐2 1:250, COX‐2 1:250, HER2 1:250) at 4°C overnight. Then, cells were incubated with green fluorescence‐labeled secondary antibody (anti‐rabbit IgG HandL (Alexa Fluor488 and 647) 1:500) and nuclei blue stained with Hoechst (Sigma). Images were visualized on a Cytation C10 confocal microscope using a 20× objective at room temperature and acquired by Gen5 software. Gen5 software was used to analyze average fluorescence intensity.

xCELLigence Migration/Invasion Assay

Cell migration and invasion were assessed using the xCELLigence RTCA DP instrument and CIM‐Plate 16. The lower chamber of each well was filled with 160 μL of medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. The upper chamber was seeded with 5 × 104 MCF‐7 cells in 0.5% FBS medium supplemented with TNF‐α (20 ng/mL) and/or herbal extracts (0.5 mg/mL). Each condition was run in triplicate. Plates were incubated at 37°C, and real‐time cell migration was monitored every 10 min for 24 h using the RTCA software. Impedance values were converted into Cell Index (CI). Migration/invasion curves were generated over 24 h, and endpoint CI values were used for statistical comparison. Data are shown as mean ± SD. One‐way ANOVA followed by multiple comparison tests was applied, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Animals and Experimental Groups

To test the hypothesis that combined chemotherapeutic and plant‐extract interventions modulate bone marrow microenvironmental signals and influence cancer cell dormancy, we designed complementary in vitro and in vivo experiments; the experimental groups and analytical techniques are detailed below. The experimental procedures involving animals followed the guidelines outlined in Directive 2010/63/EU [26]. Ethical approval was obtained from the Armenian National Center of Bioethics. Female albino rats weighing between 120 and 150 g were randomly distributed into eight groups, with each group comprising 8 individuals (except for the DMBA group, which had 10 individuals) as detailed in Table 1.

Sixty‐day‐old female rats underwent a 7‐day acclimatization period followed by subcutaneous injection (into the 2nd set of mammary glands) of 25 mg 7,12‐DMBA dissolved in 1 mL soy oil (Day 0) [27, 28, 29]. After random grouping into eight experimental groups (Day 10), animals received herbal formulations (IH, AS, RO) or reference compounds (nor‐NOHA, L‐NAME, 5‐FU) every fourth day for 8 weeks (Figure 1). Tumor formation was monitored weekly. At Week 16, tumors were measured and surgically removed. Following 16 weeks of DMBA administration, the rats were compassionately euthanized. Bone marrow was collected for histological evaluation and ELISA‐based quantification of cytokines, growth factors, inflammatory, and metastasis‐associated enzymes. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, tumor size, tumor weight, and body‐weight recording were performed throughout the experiment. The laboratory personnel conducted daily health monitoring. The body weights of the animals were consistently monitored throughout the entire experiment. The selection of plant extract concentrations was based on in vitro findings and information available in the literature from similar studies involving other plant extracts [30, 31, 32].

Isolation of the Bone Marrow, Cell Counting, and Fixation

The rats were euthanized by isoflurane using a precision vaporizer with an induction chamber and waste gas scavenger (RWD Life Science). Isoflurane was administered slowly up to 5% until respiratory arrest occurred. Animals were monitored for the cessation of respiration and remained in the euthanasia chamber for an additional 60 s after respiration had ceased [33]. Bone marrow cells were extracted from the femur of the rats as described before [34]. After isolation, the bone marrow cells were resuspended in 25 mL of DMEM and centrifuged for 5 min at 1500 rpm. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was additionally resuspended in 2 mL of DMEM. The cell suspension was afterward lysed in Radio‐Immunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Biovision) and proceeded with the experimental protocol for further ELISA analysis. After resuspending the bone marrow cells in 25 mL of DMEM, 20 μL of cell suspension was added to the slide for cell count analysis.

ELISA For TNF‐α, VEGF‐α, COX‐2, MMP‐2, and IL‐2

Freshly isolated BM cells were suspended in 25 mL of DMEM and centrifuged for 5 min at 1500 rpm. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was resuspended in 2 mL of DMEM. The cell suspension was afterward lysed in lysis buffer (200 μL Bio Vision cell lysis buffer) and proceeded with the experimental protocol for further ELISA analysis. According to the manufacturer's instructions, changes in the levels of TNF‐α, VEGF‐α, MMP‐2, COX‐2, and IL‐2 were measured using ELISA kits. Protein concentration in cell culture medium and lysates was measured with the Bradford method.

Staining of Bone Marrow Cells

For each bone marrow sample, 20 μL of cell suspension was spread on a slide. Specimens were fixed with a 96% ethanol solution for 20 min, then dried and stained with May‐Grünwald‐Giemsa solutions according to Pappenheim's method. According to the staining procedure, the samples are stained with May‐Grünwald solution for 10 min, washed with distilled water, and then stained with Giemsa solution for 10 min. After staining, the samples are washed and dried or rapidly dehydrated and coverslipped to obtain a permanent preparation. For each sample, 1000 cells were counted, distinguishing the main cell populations found in the bone marrow [35].

Sample Size, Attrition, and Terminal Sampling

Animals were initially allocated to eight experimental groups (n = 8 per group) except for the DMBA (carcinogen) group, which was set to n = 10 due to prior laboratory experience and pilot data indicating higher attrition in the DMBA model during the 16‐week carcinogenesis protocol. Because some animals died or met humane‐endpoint criteria before the scheduled terminal sacrifice, the final numbers available for endpoint measurements (body weight, tumor size, tumor weight, histology) differ from initial enrollment; final n values are reported for every figure and in Table S1. The study lasted 112 days, or 16 weeks. Treatment was given from weeks 5 to 13, with the last injection being given on day 91. Animals that felt unwell from that day onwards and subsequently died were included in the statistical analyses. Statistical comparisons were carried out using tests that are robust to modest differences in group size (one‐way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis as appropriate, and post hoc tests), and final n per group is explicitly indicated in each (Data S1).

Statistics

Statistical analyses of ICC in Figures 4B,D and 6D, Western blot results in Figure 5B,D, ELISA results in Figure 8, and rat body weight, tumor size, and tumor weight in Figure 11 were performed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Cell migration data in Figure 7C were analyzed using the Kruskal‐Wallis test with Dunn's multiple comparisons test. Bone marrow cell data in Figure 10 were analyzed using two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (San Diego, CA, USA), and a significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Antioxidant Profiling by HPLC Coupled With Post‐Column Derivatization

To identify the compounds with antioxidant activity within the extracts, an online post‐column derivatization with the ABTS reagent was performed following HPLC separation. Post‐column profiling revealed multiple strong radical‐quenching signals co‐eluting with the compound classes that dominated the quantitative data. The chromatogram recorded after derivatization at 734 nm (Figure 1) showed negative peaks, indicating the presence of redox‐active compounds. The antioxidant profiles were consistent with the phytochemical composition of each plant.

For AS, the most significant antioxidant activity was attributed to its major constituents, the ellagitannins. Specifically, agrimoniin (№14), quercetin‐O‐glucuronide (№15), and ellagic acid (№12) produced the largest negative peaks, confirming their role as primary antioxidants in this extract. In the IH extract, the antioxidant capacity was mainly associated with its abundant hydroxycinnamic acids, with the dicaffeoylquinic acid isomer (№5) being the most potent contributor. The RO extract exhibited a broad antioxidant profile, reflecting its complex mixture of proanthocyanidins. Robust ABTS responses matched oligomeric and especially galloylated procyanidins; both oligomerization and galloylation are known to amplify radical‐scavenging efficacy, and this was reflected by the rich series of dimeric to tetrameric (and galloylated) signals in the chromatographic window. Strong antioxidant activity was observed for flavan‐3‐ol monomers like (+)‐catechin (№6) and epicatechin (№8), as well as for the series of procyanidin oligomers (e.g., №7, 10) and their galloylated forms (e.g., №15, 16, 21), which collectively contribute to the high overall antioxidant capacity of the seed extract. Overall, the ABTS profiles confirm the quantitative patterns: ellagitannins (RO), caffeoylquinic acids (IH), and (galloylated) procyanidins (RO) are the principal contributors to the observed antioxidant capacity in each extract.

Using MDA‐MB‐231, MCF‐7, and HREC models, we first assessed the direct cellular effects of the extracts and chemical modulators on hypoxia and inflammation signaling (HIF‐1α, COX‐2, MMP‐2) and on TNF‐α–driven migration/invasion; the principal cellular observations follow.

No cytotoxic effect was observed at the 4‐h time point for any of the three plant extracts in MDA‐MB‐231 cells. When applied at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, all three plant extracts showed about 7%–10% inhibition (max in AS) at 24–72 h. Taking into account the standard deviations (SDs), it can be concluded that the plant extracts do not exert a clear cytotoxic effect on the cell culture.

Among the tested extracts, RO exhibited the strongest cytotoxic effect, particularly in MCF‐7 cells, where viability decreased markedly at higher concentrations and with prolonged exposure, approaching IC50 levels. Notably, RO at a concentration of 0.5 mg/mL reduced cell viability by approximately 60% at 24 h, exceeding the calculated IC50 value. IH and AS showed moderate inhibitory effects, primarily within the IC10 range.

Notably, the extracts displayed limited toxicity toward normal HREC cells, suggesting a selective action against malignant cells. This selective cytotoxicity highlights RO as a promising candidate for further anticancer studies. However, the other herbal extracts, which do not exhibit cytotoxic activity, contain a rich profile of active phytochemicals identified by HPLC–MS/MS.

Western blotting (WB) and immunocytochemistry (ICC) were employed to quantify changes in HER2, COX‐2, MMP‐2, and HIF‐1α protein expression. These models allowed us to evaluate the potential anti‐inflammatory and anti‐metastatic properties of the herbal extracts.

ICC analysis confirmed the downregulation of COX‐2 and MMP‐2 following treatment with the herbal extracts in MDA‐MB‐231 cells (Figure 4). In the 10 ng/mL TNFα‐induced model of MMP‐2 and COX‐2 activation, fluorescence intensity analysis revealed that AS and RO exerted the strongest inhibitory effects, with AS showing the most pronounced activity (Figure 4B,D). Building on those cellular findings, we evaluated how systemic administration of the same combinations alters bone marrow biomarkers, hematopoietic populations, and tumor burden in the DMBA rat model. We examined the impact of the classical chemotherapeutic drug 5‐FU, its combination with RO herbal extract, and the effects of nor‐NOHA (an arginase inhibitor) and L‐NAME (a NOS inhibitor) combined with other herbal extracts. The results reveal significant alterations in the expression of key signaling pathway molecules and biomarkers, indicating differential therapeutic impacts on BM cells.

To form a preliminary idea about the inflammatory state, we targeted the alterations in the levels of TNF‐α (Figure 8A) and COX‐2 (Figure 8B). Our results mention that COX‐2 is strongly increased in the cancer (DMBA) group (by 95%). We can observe that in all the other groups, COX‐2 decreases after the administered treatment. However, it is worth mentioning that in the DFU group where the treatment was carried out solely with a classical chemotherapeutic agent, the concentration of COX‐2 is decreased, but still, it is higher (by 81%) than in healthy individuals.

Effects of Combined Treatment on VEGF‐α and MMP‐2 Expression

The combined treatment led to a notable decrease in VEGFα (Figure 8C) and MMP‐2 (Figure 8D) levels. The level of MMP‐2 significantly decreased when the rats were treated with a 5‐FU and RO combination (by 70%), compared to when treated only with the classical chemotherapeutic drug (by 37%). The most commonly used chemotherapy compound, 5‐FU, reduces the amount of MMP‐2 in the bone marrow to insignificant levels, leaving the latter with its adverse effect and a high risk of creating a favorable environment for BCC dormancy in the bone marrow. L‐NAME likewise shows a significant reduction in the level of MMP‐2 during combined treatment with AS extract. While AS does not show any significant changes in the concentration of MMP‐2 levels when it is administered separately. IH shows better results when it acts alone, compared with the combined treatment. AS increases MMP‐2 levels exactly like in the DMBA group. The best reduction of MMP‐2 is observed in the rats treated with RO combined with 5‐FU. 5‐FU also decreases the MMP‐2 levels, but data show that together with RO, the reduction is twice as potent. Interestingly, a similar pattern was observed in the VEGFα level alterations (Figure 8C). VEGFα is increased drastically (4×) in the BM of the cancer group (DMBA) compared with the healthy control group. VEGFα is decreased when treatment is carried out with the standard classical chemotherapeutic drug, but it is closer to the DMBA group compared to when it is used with the RO herbal extract, where the data is almost like in the control healthy rats (p ≤ 0.0001). The most dramatic reduction of VEGFα is observed in the DASLN group, where it reaches levels below the control group. Generally, we can see that VEGFα is decreased when the chemical is used in combination with the herbal extract, except for IH. The latter is decreasing the quantity of VEGFα both when combined with a chemical and when acting alone. Though the difference is not significant, we can see that Inula helenium alone decreases VEGFα even more compared to when it is used combined with the chemical, in contrast to other herbals, which alone do not show a significant reduction in VEGFα. This reduction suggests a suppression of neovascularization and extracellular matrix degradation, which are critical for tumor invasion and metastasis.

Alterations in IL‐2 Levels in Bone Marrow

To address the immunomodulatory effect of the treatment, we also observed the post‐treatment changes in the IL‐2 levels in the BM (Figure 8E). Data indicate that the levels of IL‐2 are highly increased in the DAS group, where the BC rats were administered AS herbal extract only. IL‐2 levels are the lowest in the DMBA BC group and are slightly increased in the DIH and DIHNN groups. Notably, we observed an elevation of IL‐2 levels in the group where we administered only IH herbal extract, compared to when IH extract was combined with the arginase inhibitor. We can observe that AS herbal extract administration combined with the NOS inhibitor shows a reduction of IL‐2 levels. In the DFU and DFURO groups, the IL‐2 levels are the same or slightly lower compared with the control healthy rats. This finding underscores the potential of the AS herbal extract treatment to activate immune mechanisms by increasing the IL‐2 levels.

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated that DMBA markedly shortened lifespan (median survival ~60 days), whereas all treatment groups exhibited prolonged survival (Figure 11B). Taken together, the biochemical, histological, and tumor outcome data indicate a reproducible association between suppression of angiogenic/inflammatory mediators in bone marrow and reduced tumor progression—the implications of which are discussed next.

Discussion

In this study, we present in vitro and in vivo evidence that combining selected herbal extracts with conventional chemotherapeutic or enzyme‐modulating agents can attenuate pro‐metastasis, pro‐angiogenic, and pro‐inflammatory signaling in the bone marrow microenvironment and is associated with reduced tumor burden and improved survival in a DMBA‐induced rat model of breast cancer. Specifically, combined treatments produced consistent decreases in VEGF‐α, MMP‐2, COX‐2, and TNF‐α levels in bone marrow together with elevation of IL‐2, and these biochemical changes correlated with reductions in atypical bone marrow cells, tumor size, and mortality.

The tumor microenvironment plays a central role in cancer progression, particularly in processes such as epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and inflammation. COX‐2 and MMP‐2 are two crucial enzymes that contribute to these processes and are regulated by hypoxic conditions through HIF‐1α activation, inflammatory stimuli such as TNF‐α, and cytokine interactions, including IL‐2 [20, 36]. Given their metastatic potential, resistance to therapy, and constitutively active signaling, MDA‐MB‐231 TNBC and MCF‐7 cells serve as a robust model for studying these mechanisms. Although the plant extracts exhibit limited cytotoxicity in the MDA‐MB‐231 and MCF‐7 cell models, this finding aligns with our initial hypothesis that their anticancer potential may not rely on cytotoxicity [37]. Recent studies indicate that compounds with minimal cytotoxicity can still significantly impact key cancer‐associated processes such as inflammation, invasion, and metastasis [38]. Mechanistically, our data point toward suppression of an HIF‐1α → angiogenic (VEGF) / matrix‐remodeling (MMP‐2) axis and of COX‐2–driven inflammatory signaling as plausible mediators of the observed anti‐metastatic phenotype. HIF‐1α is a master regulator of the cellular hypoxic response and controls VEGF expression and other pro‐angiogenic programs that enable tumor cell survival and niche formation [20]. MMP‐2 can directly modulate VEGF signaling via integrin/PI3K–AKT pathways, linking matrix remodeling to angiogenic output; suppression of MMP‐2 therefore has the potential to blunt both invasive capacity and local angiogenic support for disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) [21]. Similarly, COX‐2 activity has been associated with increased VEGF family member expression (including VEGF‐C) and lymphangiogenesis in breast cancer, providing a direct mechanistic route by which anti‐inflammatory interventions may reduce metastatic seeding or expansion. The link between suppression of these pathways and tumor cell dormancy is biologically plausible and supported by current dormancy models. Dormancy reflects an interplay between intrinsic stress signaling and extrinsic niche cues (angiogenic support, ECM composition, immune surveillance); interventions that reduce angiogenic signaling and matrix remodeling can either maintain DTCs in a dormant/quiescent state or prevent successful colonization of a supportive niche [22, 39]. The observed increase in IL‐2 in bone marrow after combined treatment is noteworthy and may indicate reinvigoration of anti‐tumor immune responses within the marrow niche [40]. IL‐2 is central to T cell activation and NK cell function, and marrow stromal cells have been explored as vehicles for IL‐2 delivery in immunotherapeutic contexts [41]. At the same time, inflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α can remodel bone marrow retention niches and negatively affect long‐term plasma cell and immune cell occupancy; therefore, the documented decrease in TNF‐α may further help to restore marrow homeostasis [42]. Our data showing a reduction in monocytes and atypical cell populations after treatment reinforces a narrative of partial marrow normalization. Although all animals in the DMBA control group eventually developed tumors, a subset of rats in the treated groups either developed tumors much later or did not develop detectable tumors within the study duration, indicating partial chemoprevention. Differences between enzyme inhibitors (L‐NAME vs. nor‐NOHA) in combination with extracts suggest that modulation of the NO/arginase axis differentially impacts inflammatory and angiogenic signaling in the BM. For instance, combinations including L‐NAME plus herbal extract exhibited pronounced reductions in TNF‐α, whereas arginase inhibition with nor‐NOHA produced different effects on COX‐2 and VEGF dynamics. Our combined data suggest that rational combinations of selected plant extracts with chemotherapeutic or enzyme‐modulating agents can reshape the bone marrow microenvironment, suppress pro‐angiogenic and pro‐invasive signals (HIF‐1α/VEGF/MMP‐2/COX‐2), elevate IL‐2, and correlate with reduced tumor progression and mortality (Figure 12). These findings suggest a feasible preclinical path for therapies that target the metastatic niche and tumor dormancy, but they also underscore the need for focused mechanistic and translational follow‐up.

Limitations of the Study

First, while correlations between marker suppression and outcome are robust, direct causal links (e.g., that MMP‐2 suppression alone is necessary and sufficient to enforce dormancy) have not been established; targeted perturbation experiments (genetic knockdown/overexpression, neutralizing antibodies, or selective small‐molecule inhibitors) are needed. Second, our study uses a single chemical carcinogenesis model (DMBA) in rats; validation in orthotopic and spontaneous metastasis models, and ideally in multiple species, would increase generalizability. Third, we did not perform single‐cell profiling of bone marrow DTCs to directly demonstrate entry into quiescence or to dissect immune cell subtypes—modern single‐cell or spatial approaches would strengthen mechanistic insight. Finally, the plant extracts are complex mixtures; although LC‐HRMS profiling identified abundant polyphenols and tannins (see Tables 2, 3, 4), isolation of active constituents is required for pharmacological development.
