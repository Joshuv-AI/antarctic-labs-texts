Knowledge graph-augmented large language models for reconstructing life course risk pathways: a gestational diabetes mellitus-to-dementia case study
Authors: Shuang Wang, Yang Zhang, Ying Gao, Xin He, Guanghui Deng, Jian Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop and evaluate a knowledge graph-augmented large language model (LLM) framework that synthesizes epidemiological evidence to infer life-course exposure-outcome pathways, using gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and dementia as a case study.Materials and MethodsWe constructed a causal knowledge graph by extracting empirical epidemiological associations from scientific literature, excluding hypothetical assertions. The graph was integrated with GPT-4 through four graph retrieval-augmented generation (GRAG) strategies to infer bridging variables between early-life exposure (GDM) and later-life outcome (dementia). Semantic triples served as structured inputs to support LLM reasoning. Each GRAG strategy was evaluated by human clinical experts and three LLM-based reviewers (GPT-4o, Llama 3-70B, and Gemini Advanced), assessing scientific reliability, novelty, and clinical relevance.ResultsThe GRAG strategy using a minimal set of abstracts specifically related to GDM–dementia bridging variables performed comparably to the strategy using broader sub-community abstracts, and both significantly outperformed approaches using the full GDM- or dementia-related corpus or baseline GPT-4 without external augmentation. The knowledge graph-augmented LLM identified 108 maternal candidate mediators, including validated risk factors such as chronic kidney disease and physical inactivity. The structured approach improved accuracy and reduced confabulation compared to standard LLM outputs.DiscussionOur findings suggest that augmenting LLMs with epidemiological knowledge graphs enables effective reasoning over fragmented literature and supports the reconstruction of progressive risk pathways. Expert assessments revealed that LLMs may overestimate clinical relevance, highlighting the need for human-AI collaboration in interpretation and application.ConclusionIntegrating semantic epidemiological knowledge with LLMs via GRAG strategies provides a promising framework for life-course epidemiology, enabling early detection of modifiable risk factors and guiding variable selection in cohort study design.Keywords: large language models, graph retrieval augmented generation, literature mining, complex network mining, AI-guided cohort development, intermediate variable mining

Introduction

Understanding the complexity of mechanisms and establishing causality across the life course is crucial to developing preventive strategies for chronic diseases.1 However, this field relies heavily on longitudinal cohort studies, which are costly and time-intensive and further complicated by the pervasive challenge of missing data.2 As biomedical literature contains extensive knowledge linking risk factors to health outcomes, literature-based discovery (LBD) provides new opportunities for detecting risk pathways linking exposure and outcome of interest.3–8 LBD utilizes an A-B-C model (If one article asserts that “A affects B” and another that “B affects C,” then “A affects C” is a natural hypothesis), similar to the chain-of-risk pathway model in life course epidemiology.1 Based on the A-B-C model, Bristol University has developed tools (ie, Text Mining for Mechanism Prioritisation, TeMMPo) to identify potential intermediate mechanisms,6 but they often yield fragmented interphenotypes and require labor-intensive systematic reviews for validation, limiting its scalability and generalizability. Given the massive number of intermediate candidates, the essence of LBD is to rank “A affects C via B” which is novel, plausible, and clinically meaningful. Large language models (LLMs), with excellent capacities for summarizing large-scale texts, can synthesize piecemeal findings and reason the risk chains across life course diseases.9

Due to the potential hallucinations of LLMs,10 Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a cost-effective strategy by retrieving the most relevant, up-to-date, and domain-specific knowledge (eg, epidemiological knowledge) for more accurate responses.11 RAG can be categorized into naïve and advanced approaches. Naïve RAG uses similarity-based snippet retrieval for the LLM context but faces two limitations: (1) the ‘lost in the middle’ problem in unstructured documents,12 and (2) failure to capture global semantic patterns, which impairs complex reasoning.13 Graph-RAG could potentially address this limitation by leveraging fact-based knowledge graphs (KGs) as the graph index to identify the most relevant information. While RAG-enhanced LLMs show proven efficacy in answering clinical questions, such as answering medical licensing examination questions and making diagnostic assessments,14–16 epidemiological questions, for example, exposure-outcome association analyses,17 remain underexplored. We argue that population-level epidemiological associations may represent a more tractable problem space for LLMs than individual-level clinical decision-making, given their inherent strengths in pattern recognition across large and public textual corpora.

Inspired by the pathway model in life course epidemiology1 and by integrating LBD’s A-B-C model with LLMs’ reasoning capacities, we developed a novel automated approach to map risk trajectories from early-life gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) to later-life dementia or other neurodegenerative diseases (abbreviated as dementia in this study) (Figure 1). Our approach processes 35 010 SemMedDB-derived semantic triples (subject-predicate-object) from 28 280 high-argument-strength sentences across 14 733 GDM/dementia studies. Using our fine-tuned Llama 2-7B classifier, we prioritized high-argument-strength content to construct a causal GDM-dementia knowledge graph. Network mining of this graph identified critical bridging variables. We then evaluated three Graph-RAG strategies against a naïve RAG baseline using GPT-4: Global Search, Community Search, and Interface Search. Pathway summaries generated by each method underwent dual evaluation by clinical experts and three LLM reviewers (GPT-4o,18 Llama 3-70B,19 Gemini Adv20).

Methodology

GDM-Dementia knowledge graph construction

To establish a comprehensive understanding of GDM and dementia, we extracted 29 619 GDM-related and 481 762 dementia-related publications from PubMed (as of May 2024). After filtering for high-impact journals (Nature Index list, n = 145),21 31 733 articles were retained. Using Semantic MEDLINE (SemMedDB),22 we constructed a knowledge graph (KG) by extracting subject-predicate-object triples from titles/abstracts, creating a weighted network where nodes represent medical concepts and edges denote number of relationships of the same pair of concepts.

To refine this into a causal knowledge graph for risk pathway analysis, we pruned the network across three dimensions:

Edge types: Retained only ‘functionally_related_to’ and ‘association-related’ categories of relations (eg, CAUSES, INHIBITS, see Supplementary Methods 1).

We used a fine-tuned Llama 2-7B classifier23 (trained on 576 annotated sentences) to exclude hypothetical claims and keep empirically supported triples.

The final causal KG comprised 8488 nodes and 35 010 triples from 14 733 publications. The PRISMA workflow for data collection and preprocessing is shown in Supplementary Figure 1.

GDM-Dementia bridging variables detection and classification

Intuitively, to systematically explore the risk path from GDM to dementia from a global view, rather than considering each node as a unit, we assumed each group of nodes (community) as a unit and hypothesized that there might be several steps (communities) mediating the process from GDM to dementia. Interestingly, Louvain community detection revealed GDM and dementia co-located within a single network module, suggesting they are tightly connected. Thus, we further detected communities within the module and identified boundary nodes between GDM and dementia subcommunities, ranking them by high betweenness centrality and low structural hole constraint. The top 50% (Q50) nodes from each metric were selected, with noise nodes (eg, ‘risk reduction’) manually excluded (see Supplementary Methods 2) and were further categorized into higher-level information groups. To rank the mediating potential of each category, GPT-4 analyzed source abstracts for each bridging category to assess its reliability in linking GDM to dementia. A subsequent human fact-check was conducted by two reviewers to correct for potential hallucinations in LLM-generated summaries (see Supplementary Table 1 for criteria; Supplementary Methods 4-5).

Graph-RAG strategies design and comparison

According to the graph mining trajectories, we designed and implemented four progressive RAG strategies using GPT-4 with two purposes: (1) to identify whether the causal KG mining approach is beneficial in locating the relevant context knowledge to augment the reasoning of GDM-dementia risk pathways; and (2) to explore the minimal sufficient range of contexts in tracing GDM-dementia pathways:

Original abstracts were retrieved for each graph-RAG strategy to provide contextual information (Supplementary Methods 3.1). Briefly, Baseline RAG refers to the original GPT-4 retrieval technique, and Broad Context RAG attaches all abstracts from Nature Index journals as the external knowledge base. Community Graph-RAG uses GPT-4 augmented with all abstracts contained in the GDM-dementia sub-community; for each semantic triple in this community, we included the corresponding original abstracts as context. Interface Graph-RAG focuses on the top 50 bridging variables identified in the sub-community and includes original abstracts linked to these nodes. The filtering rules for semantic triples selection are detailed in Supplementary Methods 3.1. These clarifications make the rationale and implementation of community versus interface Graph-RAG transparent and reproducible.

For each strategy, GPT-4 answered three progressive queries: (1) Summarizing overall pathways from GDM to dementia, (2) Explaining each underlying pathway in detail, and (3) Recommending clinically measurable variables to prevent progressive development from GDM to dementia. The detailed questions are described in the prompt section of Supplementary Methods 4.

Evaluations by clinical experts

Evaluations were conducted across three aspects: scientific reliability, novelty, and clinical relevance. Scientific reliability measures the extent to which a given answer aligns with established domain knowledge. Novelty assesses whether, and to what extent, a response introduces surprising or inspiring content. Clinical relevance evaluates the feasibility of clinical application and the relevance of recommended clinical tests or examinations. Expert scores were analyzed using one-way ANOVA to examine differences among responses from the four RAG versions within each evaluation aspect. To further assess expert scoring robustness, three LLMs (GPT-4o, Llama 3-70B, Gemini Adv) were additionally used as LLM reviewers. The overall clinical evaluation process is described in Supplementary Methods 5, and the expert scoring criteria are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Results

Descriptive analysis of GDM-Dementia KG

Supplementary Figure 1 shows the overall process from building to pruning a causal GDM-Dementia KG using stronger-argument-strength sentences. We fine-tuned Llama 2-7B on a manually annotated set of 576 sentences, yielding an accuracy of 0.84, competitive with state-of-the-art heavyweight LLMs under few-shot settings (GLM-4.5, 0.80, DeepSeek-V3.1-Terminus, 0.88), while being more cost-effective and sustainable (Supplementary Table 3). We then applied our fine-tuned Llama 2-7B to 61 331 source sentences for the argument strength classification task. Among them, 52,347 (85.35%) sentences were classified as stronger arguments, either prior knowledge, new findings, or recommendations on new findings which were included, and 8984 weak argument sentences (ie, research hypothesis, research design statements) were excluded.

A total of 8,488 unique concepts and 35 010 relations were finally included, from 28 280 unique sentences in abstracts of 14 733 primary research studies. Among them, the leading three concept types are Genes & Molecular Sequences (36.97%), Chemicals & Drugs (36.46%), and Disorders (16.32%) and the leading three relations are ASSOCIATED_WITH (21.77%), AFFECTS (19.61%), and CAUSES (16.92%). Supplementary Tables 4 and 5 present the overall distribution of concept types and relation types.

Identification and classification of key bridging variables for GDM-Dementia

Based on the GDM-Dementia KG, we conducted community detection using the Louvain modularity algorithm to detect the network structure. With each pair of concepts as nodes and the count of predicates as their edge weight, the initial graph contains 8488 nodes and 23 214 edges. As a result, 8488 unique nodes were partitioned into 228 communities (Figure 3A). Interestingly, we found that “Gestational Diabetes” and “Dementia” were allocated to the same community, indicating that they are connected tightly.

To further understand the connections between GDM and dementia, we narrowed down the mining zone to the sub-community (924 nodes and 1535 edges) involving both GDM and dementia. We re-analyzed this sub-community and detected 24 communities inside it centered on GDM and dementia (Figure 3B). Interestingly, among all bridging nodes identified by our model (N = 230), including type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, chronic kidney disease, physical inactivity, and comorbidity, were previously validated as mediators between GDM and dementia in an independent UK Biobank study that first reported this association.24 As this external dataset was not included in our KG, it provides strong validation of the pathophysiological relevance and predictive capability of our pathway mining approach. Furthermore, our inferred mediators show remarkable alignment with the 2024 Lancet Commission report on dementia prevention, which highlights 14 modifiable risk factors for clinical guidance.25 Except early-life low educational attainment, all risk factors emphasized by the Commission were also identified among our bridging candidates. This convergence with established clinical frameworks underscores the plausibility and potential significance of the pathways inferred by our model. These consistencies support the hypothesis that the bridging nodes identified may represent meaningful mechanistic pathways linking GDM to dementia. The final filtered core bridging mediator set included 118 variables consisting of the top quantile 50 of all bridging nodes. After GPT-4 automatic categorization and human review, 108 mediator candidates (10 fetal-related factors excluded) were left and categorized into three classes in level 1 and ten sub-classes in level 2 for further class-based pathway prioritization (see Supplementary Table 6 for all 108 variables).

The identified mediating candidates were then classified as ten categories for risk pathway synthesis. For representative variables of each class, by feeding on all relevant abstracts indicating GDM-X and X-Dementia in the KG, GPT-4 was utilized to review and rate the mediating potential of the chosen variable, and further human evaluation on GPT-4 reviews was conducted. The blind-evaluation results of two reviewers revealed that, though factual hallucination and argumentation deficits exist in GPT-4 review results, the overall GPT-4 evaluation quality is fundamentally reasonable, with an average of 6.85 in factuality (consistency of evidence fact and review results) and 6.40 in argumentative logic rationality (Supplementary Table 7). Combining GPT-4 review scores and blind-evaluation scores of those review scores as the adjusted GPT-4 review scores showed that the top three mediating categories are renal factors, cardiovascular factors, and pregnancy complications (Supplementary Figure 2). Out of expectation, the biggest and most well-known mediating category of metabolic and endocrine factors (32 factors supported by 196 abstracts) dropped out of the top three. This might be because, though not exceeding the token limit, with the big volume of texts in place, GPT-4 is inclined to be ‘lazy’ and only read partial abstracts for a generation. Also, when it comes to more common knowledge (ie, the association of obesity and GDM), it tends to mismatch the article index to its already known information instead of the article’s real content, lowering its factuality scores in human evaluation. To further investigate the “lazy response” issue, we re-evaluated the largest category using GPT-4o. It showed significantly improved instruction adherence and response’s systematic coverage, though occasional evidence misinterpretation persisted. However, in general, GPT-4’s outputs are consistent with GPT-4o’s, appearing as the latter’s condensed version, focusing on core tasks, representative variables, and heuristic decision-making via a certain amount of evidence. Supplementary Results 2 detail the explanations.

Due to the above concerns, we further integrated the literature volume of each category and adjusted GPT-4 scores to collectively visualize the mediating potential of each mechanism category (Figure 3C). We found that the metabolic and cardiovascular factors are on the top right with relatively large evidence volumes and high mediating scores. It is worth noting that, though renal factors are supported by merely three abstracts in the filtered KG, the assertive claims of the elevated risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD) after GDM irrespective of the subsequent development of diabetes26 and the independent risk factors of CKD and acute kidney injury (AKI) on the central nervous system27 gave GPT-4 more assurance for a high mediating score on renal factors.

Comparison of four RAG-LLM responses by human experts

Specifically, we designed three questions: (1) Summarizing overall mechanisms of GDM and Dementia, (2) Explaining each underlying mechanism in detail, and (3) Recommending clinically measurable variables for early prevention of long-term dementia in GDM patients (see Appendix A for the detailed questions). For each RAG strategy, we utilized GPT-4 to answer all three questions and have clinical experts (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9) and three LLM reviewers rate each answer from three dimensions, scientific reliability, novelty, and clinical relevance (only for Q3). In general, we found that for each metric and question, review grading scores from all reviewers incrementally increased from the baseline GPT-4 (RAG1) to the RAG with refined bridging variables’ contexts (RAG4) (Figure 4). Supplementary Figure 3 shows the expert grading distribution, and Supplementary Figures 4 and 5 present the grading distributions of other two LLM reviewers. This primarily reveals that, from the naïve RAG baseline to the refined GRAG strategy, gradually optimized RAG strategies are beneficial in improving the quality of GPT-4 answers. This indirectly proved that the reliability of the causal KG and the suitability of the gradual graph mining techniques-based RAG, which helped locate relevant and screen out noisy knowledge to deepen the understanding of the potential risk chain from GDM to dementia.

Further, to determine the statistical differences among each dimension, we conducted a single-factor ANOVA of expert scores of answers across all three dimensions separately (RAG type, metric type, and question type; see Supplementary Methods 5). Statistically, expert scores showed that the mean scores of outputs of RAG4 incorporating contexts of all bridging variables (8.50), and RAG3 including contexts of the GDM-Dementia subcommunity (8.14), are significantly (P <= .001) higher than the means of RAG2 with all unfiltered abstracts (5.86) and the GPT-4 baseline (5.36). However, no significant difference was found between RAG4 and RAG3 or RAG2 and RAG1 in expert scores (R4 = R3>R2 = R1) (Supplementary Table 10). These results were supported by LLM reviewers (Supplementary Tables 11 and 12). It suggests that the modularity-filtering method helped target relevant knowledge and supplement pivotal information to GPT-4 to finally improve the overall quality of the generative answer regardless of question and metric types. Surprisingly, in terms of metric type, we found that in the GPT-4 baseline, the average novelty score (4.33) is significantly (P < .05) lower than the average of scientific reliability (5.83). This suggests that with the baseline GPT-4 RAG, though the scientific accuracy of generation is appropriate, it is hard to capture novel and surprising information. However, other RAG strategy tiers compensate for this limitation, rendering both novel and accurate generative content, with no significant differences found. In addition, no significant differences in scores of different questions are found under each RAG strategy (Supplementary Table 13).

Spearman correlation analysis showed that expert and LLM ratings were highly consistent across most question-metric units (Supplementary Results 3.3, Supplementary Table 14, and Supplementary Figure 6). However, the scoring discrepancy in the metric of clinical relevance where human experts demonstrated significantly stricter criteria (mean score 7.75 ± 0.96 vs LLMs’ 9.83 ± 0.39, P < .05 using T-test, Supplementary Table 15) underscores LLMs’ tendency toward optimistic bias in clinical applicability evaluations. This divergence necessitates hybrid evaluation frameworks combining human expertise with LLM scalability for clinical decision support systems. Appendix B (Supplementary Tables 16 and 17) provides the detailed LLM responses.

Discussion

This study presents a novel approach for reconstructing life-course exposure-outcome pathways in the absence of long-term, high-quality cohort data. We developed and evaluated a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) strategy that enhances LLMs with structured epidemiological knowledge in the form of semantic triples. Specifically, by integrating high-confidence causal associations extracted from the literature, we inferred chains of risk linking GDM to dementia. Our graph-based RAG (GRAG) strategies, particularly those utilizing a minimal yet highly relevant subset of abstracts, performed comparably to broader sub-community-based approaches—and both significantly outperformed strategies based on full GDM- or dementia-related corpora, as well as a baseline GPT-4 model without external curated knowledge augmentation. These findings highlight that context quality—rather than volume—is critical for enhancing causal reasoning in LLMs.

Among the four RAG strategies we tested, those that incorporated community-detected subgraphs from a GDM-dementia semantic knowledge graph demonstrated superior performance in identifying meaningful bridging variables—such as coronary heart disease and chronic kidney disease—that have been independently validated through the UK Biobank.24 This demonstrates the utility of leveraging network topology and community structure to prioritize meaningful links and reduce inference noise.

Compared to other LBD tools, such as TeMMPo and Melodi which mainly identify mediators from the co-occurrence mindset or the flat A-B-C model (high-frequency overlapping B), our method started from the textual network structure, using community detection and topological structure metrics to identify mediators from a global view. Also, our combination of LLMs introduces synthesized insights allowing further summarization and synthesis rather than only identifying those high-frequency overlapping concepts. This in turn makes it possible to synthesize evidence across study designs, rather than restricting synthesis to a single design type (eg, randomized controlled trials), as in TeMMPo’s stage 2 framework.

Importantly, our findings emphasize that LLMs, when guided by structured epidemiological knowledge, outperform native LLMs in both precision and interpretability. This aligns with findings from prior studies where knowledge graph-based RAG has improved LLM performance in diverse clinical tasks, including emergency message triage,28 dietary supplements-related question answering,29 and the generation of meaningful biomedical text rooted in established disease-specific knowledge.30,31 Unlike those efforts, which focus on local or global entity retrieval within knowledge graphs, our method targets the reconstruction of progressive, multi-step epidemiological pathways—providing a complementary perspective better suited for life-course epidemiology.

A notable finding is that, although GPT-4 and GPT-4o were consistent in mediator rankings, their reasoning behaviors differed. GPT-4 occasionally overlooked part of the evidence and condensed multi-step instructions, which is consistent with recent studies showing that earlier LLMs (ie, GPT-3.5-Turbo and GPT-4) may under-use the middle portion of long contexts12 and rely on simplified heuristic strategies when processing extensive instructions.32 Despite broadly comparable text-understanding and reasoning capacities, GPT-4o showed much better instruction adherence, in line with recent findings.33 In complex tasks, stricter adherence to stepwise instructions effectively decomposes the problem, making each subtask easier to solve and reducing the need for shortcut strategies. This suggests that the “lazy reasoning” phenomenon is likely to be mitigated as instruction adherence improves in complex tasks. Newer models, such as GPT-5, which perform more strongly in reasoning, instruction adherence, and long-context handling,34 are therefore expected to further reduce the omission problem in similar tasks.

From a translational standpoint, our method highlights a cost-effective and scalable alternative to traditional cohort studies for generating hypotheses about long-term health outcomes. While LLM-based reviewers tended to assign higher clinical relevance scores than human experts, suggesting a risk of overestimation, this discrepancy also underscores the need for human-AI collaboration frameworks. Such models can serve as quasi-experts that propose—but not finalize—hypotheses, which must ultimately be evaluated by domain experts and validated empirically.

We make four key technical contributions. We constructed a high-confidence causal knowledge graph linking GDM to dementia to filter empirical associations. We designed and compared graph-based RAG strategies, showing that community- and interface-level filtering substantially improved LLM reasoning. We further applied network mining to identify 108 bridging mediators, many validated by external evidence, and introduced a hybrid evaluation combining expert and LLM reviewers. Together, these innovations provide a scalable framework for reconstructing life-course exposure–outcome pathways in epidemiology.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, the analysis was restricted to high-impact journals indexed in the Nature Index, which may exclude relevant findings from broader biomedical literature. Expanding to the full PubMed database could improve coverage and reveal additional mediating pathways. Second, the semantic triples used in the knowledge graph do not capture attributes such as research subject (eg, mother vs offspring in GDM studies), which may introduce ambiguity. Although prompt engineering was employed to emphasize maternal exposure, residual misclassification may persist. Third, accurately distinguishing mediators from confounders remains a challenge. While prompts were designed to guide the LLM in making this distinction, domain complexity and bidirectional associations may limit precision. Fourth, the token limit of GPT-4 (128 K) constrained the RAG strategy involving full-text inclusion, illustrating the importance of intelligent retrieval design over maximal input length. Finally, we did not perform a full manual evaluation of the causal KG against human-annotated gold standards, as this would require extensive annotation across thousands of publications. Instead, we relied on classifier-based filtering and external consistency checks, which provide indirect but supportive evidence of KG quality. In our pipeline, the KG is used mainly to retrieve candidate mediators, whereas the evidential assessment of each mediator is based on its original abstracts and reviewed by both LLMs and human experts. Therefore, imperfections in the KG are more likely to reduce the recall (ie, some mediators may not be retrieved) of potential candidates than to bias the validity of the downstream findings, and the main conclusions remain robust to minor KG noise. Future work should incorporate systematic sampling and manual adjudication to further quantify accuracy and recall at the publication level.

Our framework is designed to be generalizable across disease domains and data sources, not limited to GDM and dementia. The knowledge graph construction and GRAG pipeline do not rely on disease-specific assumptions, making them adaptable to a wide range of exposure-outcome relationships. Furthermore, the model can incorporate non-literature data, such as real-world clinical data from electronic medical records, as well as genotype, multi-omics, and phenotype associations from biomedical databases. Future work will validate and extend this approach across diverse clinical contexts.

In summary, this study demonstrates that combining structured epidemiological knowledge, graph-based filtering, and advanced language models enables the reconstruction of interpretable and clinically relevant pathways between life-course exposures and outcomes. The proposed framework is scalable, data-driven, and generalizable to other exposure-disease pairs, and may inform both cohort study design and hypothesis generation in precision prevention research.
