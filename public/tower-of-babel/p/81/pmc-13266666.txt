Baseline Dementia Prevalence and Subtypes in Older Adults Hospitalized With Acute Coronary Syndrome: A Multicenter Study From a Large Integrated Health Care Delivery System
Authors: Ashok Krishnaswami, Amanda Jain, Howard Dinh, Richard V Ha, Wynnelena C Canio, Michael G Nanna, Alicia Romero, Paola Gilsanz, Neelum T Aggarwal, Michelle M Mielke, Mathew S Maurer, Janine Y Yang, Edward Dong-Ho Shin, Divyesh M Patel, Abdulla A Damluji, Ahmed I Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDementia is increasingly common among older adults (≥ 65 years) and is associated with poor outcomes. However, little is known about its baseline prevalence in patients hospitalized with acute coronary syndrome (ACS), particularly across revascularization strategies.MethodsThe authors conducted a cross-sectional analysis from a current longitudinal cohort study of Kaiser Permanente Northern California members hospitalized with ACS between January 2010 and December 2020. Dementia diagnoses and subtypes were identified using International Classification of Diseases 9/10 codes. Baseline prevalence was compared across revascularization strategies: percutaneous coronary intervention, coronary artery bypass grafting, or no revascularization.ResultsAmong 26,749 patients with ACS, the mean age was 76.7 ± 8.0 years; patients not revascularized were older than those who were (77.7 ± 8.3 vs 74.9 ± 7.0 years; P < .001). Overall, 57.4% were male, with a higher proportion among revascularized patients. The overall baseline prevalence of dementia was 5.9%, substantially lower among revascularized (1.9%) vs nonrevascularized patients (7.9%; P < .001). Dementia prevalence was 2.0% for percutaneous coronary intervention, 0.6% for coronary artery bypass grafting, and 7.9% for no revascularization. Alzheimer’s disease was the most common subtype (85.7%), followed by vascular (5.7%), Lewy body/Parkinson’s (3.7%), frontotemporal (1.0%), and unspecified types (3.9%).ConclusionOne in 17 older adults hospitalized with ACS had a documented diagnosis of dementia at baseline, with substantial variation by revascularization strategy. These findings highlight the baseline burden of dementia in this high-risk population and support the need for future research on the incidence and progression of cognitive impairment after ACS.

Introduction

Dementia, or major neurocognitive disorder, is common in older adults and has important implications in the context of an acute coronary syndrome (ACS), where clinical decisions often involve high-risk interventions and complex care planning. 1,2 Alzheimer’s disease, the leading cause of dementia, defined as a progressive cognitive decline characterized by pathological changes in the brain (deposition of beta-amyloid plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein), accounts for approximately 70% of all cases of dementia. 2,3 Furthermore, the presence of dementia influences both the recognition of cardiovascular symptoms and the clinical trajectory, including decisions around invasive interventions such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). It has also been reported that patients diagnosed with dementia are less likely to undergo these procedures and more likely to experience adverse outcomes. 4–6

Despite the clinical importance of the recognition and management of cognitive impairment in an ACS cohort, limited data exist on the contemporary prevalence of dementia in this population across multiple centers within a unified care model. 2 2,7 Furthermore, the distribution of dementia among those referred for PCI, CABG, or managed medically has not been systematically well characterized. Understanding these patterns could inform clinical decision-making and highlight opportunities to align invasive management strategies with patient-centered goals in older adults with cognitive impairment. 8 In this multicenter study from an integrated health care delivery system, the authors aimed to define the prevalence of all-cause and subtypes of dementia in older adults admitted with ACS stratified by revascularization strategy. Characterizing dementia prevalence among older adults with ACS can help clinicians anticipate challenges related to symptom recognition, informed consent, adherence, and postprocedural recovery. Although this study did not address all downstream clinical decisions, it brought much-needed awareness to the role of cognitive vulnerability in ACS care and underscores the importance of incorporating cognitive function into real-world treatment discussions.

Methods

ETHICS

The study was approved by the Kaiser Permanente Northern California Institutional Review Board with a waiver of written informed consent in accordance with the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act.

Study Population

The authors conducted a longitudinal cohort study and presented the baseline cross-sectional data in patients aged ≥ 65 years who were hospitalized within Kaiser Permanente Northern California between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2020 with a diagnosis of an ACS (N = 26,749). The authors used a previously published method of identifying ACS hospitalizations within Kaiser Permanente Northern California 9 and identified those who underwent coronary revascularization (PCI [N = 8207], CABG [N = 802]) and those who did not undergo coronary revascularization (N = 17,740) (Figure 1).

Cohort diagram of study entry. ACS = acute coronary syndrome; CABG = coronary artery bypass grafting; CAD = coronary artery disease; PCI = percutaneous coronary intervention.

Kaiser Permanente Northern California is a large, integrated health care delivery system serving 4.2 million insured individuals across the northern California bay area and is felt to be representative of the surrounding community. 10 The Northern California health care system includes 21 hospitals and 25 free-standing medical clinics. Among these, 12 hospitals have PCI capabilities, and 3 serve as cardiac surgical centers, operating under a hub-and-spoke model. Standardized interfacility transfer protocols ensure equitable access to surgical centers from nonsurgical spoke sites.

DATA SOURCE

Data were obtained from the comprehensive Kaiser Permanente Northern California electronic health record system (EPIC Systems, Verona, WI), which captures demographics, diagnoses, procedures, laboratory results, and medication records across care settings. The authors identified patients aged ≥ 65 years with a primary hospital discharge diagnosis of ACS using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 codes (prior to 2015) and ICD-10 codes (from 2015 onward), based on the year of admission. 9 Identification of PCI/CABG and procedural details were supplemented using the Kaiser Permanente Northern California CEDARON Cardiac Care database (Davis, CA), and Society of Thoracic Surgeons toolkit, which contains standardized data on cardiac catheterization and coronary revascularization. To ensure procedural accuracy, a subset of patients who received coronary revascularization (n = 100) and those who did not (n = 100) underwent additional validation through direct chart review. A diagnosis of dementia and the types of dementia were obtained from electronic medical records using ICD-9/10 diagnosis codes (Supplementary Table 2). A subset of patients with dementia (n = 100) underwent additional validation by direct chart review to ensure diagnostic accuracy.

DEFINITION OF CORONARY REVASCULARIZATION STATUS

Patients were classified according to receipt of coronary revascularization during the index ACS hospitalization. Coronary revascularization was defined as undergoing either PCI or CABG, without concomitant valve surgery, prior to hospital discharge. Patients were categorized into 3 mutually exclusive groups: PCI, CABG, or no coronary revascularization.

The nonrevascularized group consisted of patients hospitalized with an ACS diagnosis who did not undergo PCI or CABG during the index hospitalization. This group included individuals with low-level troponin elevations or alternative clinical diagnoses (eg, myocarditis, pericarditis, myopericarditis, or type 2 myocardial infarction), as well as patients managed conservatively with medical therapy, noninvasive testing (eg, stress testing), or diagnostic coronary angiography without revascularization based on intraprocedural assessments.

To confirm accurate classification, a random sample of patients who underwent coronary revascularization (n = 100) and those who did not (n = 100) underwent manual chart review, which verified procedural status. An additional focused chart review of patients classified as nonrevascularized (n = 25) further confirmed the absence of PCI or CABG during the index hospitalization.

COVARIATES

The complete details of baseline covariates obtained for the study can be found in the Supplementary Appendix (Supplementary Table 3).

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

The distributions of baseline demographics and clinical comorbidities were summarized overall and by coronary revascularization status; comparisons were done using the student’s t test for continuous variables and χ2 test for categorical variables. The distribution of sex was compared using Fisher’s exact test due to small counts in the “other” category. The prevalence and subtype distribution of baseline dementia were compared between those patients who did and did not undergo coronary revascularization using the χ2 test. Age-stratified analyses and dementia medication use were also assessed. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS, Cary, NC)

Results

OVERVIEW

Figure 1 illustrates the flow of participants included in the current study cohort. A total of 26,749 patients were hospitalized with an ACS during the study duration. The mean age was 76.7 ± 8.0 years, with patients who did not undergo coronary revascularization slightly older (77.7 ± 8.3 vs 74.9 ± 7.0 years, P < .001). Although men made up a greater proportion of the total cohort compared to women (57.4% vs 42.6%), the sex disparity was more pronounced among those who underwent coronary revascularization. Specifically, the proportion of men exceeded women by 29.9% in the group of patients with PCI (64.9% vs 35.0%) and by 51.6% in the group of patients with CABG (75.8% vs 24.2%). By contrast, the group of patients with nonrevascularization had a smaller sex difference of 6.2% (53.1% men vs 46.9% women). Most were self-described as White (63.7%), with an equal proportion of patients identifying as Asian/Pacific Islander (11.5%) or as Hispanic or Latino (11.7%) and a lower proportion identifying as Black (6.7%). The multimorbidity burden was higher in those who did not undergo coronary revascularization compared to those who did undergo coronary revascularization regardless of the index used (Charlson or Elixhauser). Hypertension (86.8%), hyperlipidemia (84.0%), and peripheral vascular disease (64.5%) were the most highly prevalent conditions. Statins were prescribed at baseline for 58.2% of patients, and a high proportion of patients were on other cardiovascular medications (Table 1).

PREVALENCE OF DEMENTIA

Among patients hospitalized with ACS, the overall baseline prevalence of dementia was 5.9% (n = 1572). This was substantially lower in those who underwent coronary revascularization (1.9%, n = 169) compared with those who did not (7.9%, n = 1403; P < .001). Those who underwent PCI had a higher proportion of dementia (n = 164, 2%) compared to those who underwent CABG (n = 5, 0.62%, P < .001), as shown in Figure 2A.

PREVALENCE OF SUBTYPES OF DEMENTIA

Among patients with baseline dementia, Alzheimer’s disease was the most common subtype, accounting for 85.7% (n = 1347) of all cases (Figure 2B). The distribution of dementia type was similar (P = .51) between those who underwent coronary revascularization and the nonrevascularized group, with Alzheimer’s being the most common (87.6%, n = 148% and 85.5%, n = 1199, respectively). This was also true across revascularization strategies (PCI: 87.8%, n = 144; CABG: 80.0%, n = 4).

After Alzheimer’s disease, the next most prevalent subtypes were vascular dementia (5.7%, n = 90), Lewy body or Parkinson’s-related dementia (3.7%, n = 58), and frontotemporal dementia (1.0%, n = 15). Unspecified dementia types comprised 3.9% (n = 62) of the cohort, as shown in Figure 2B.

DEMENTIA MEDICATION USE

In the full cohort, 2.0% of patients (n = 541) were on dementia medications at baseline, with a lower prevalence of dementia medications observed in those who underwent coronary revascularization compared to those who did not (0.7% vs 2.7%).

AGE-STRATIFIED PREVALENCE OF DEMENTIA

Among 1572 patients with baseline dementia hospitalized with ACS, the prevalence of dementia increased with advancing age and was notably higher in those who did not undergo coronary revascularization. Of the 169 patients with dementia who underwent revascularization, the majority were aged 70–89 years, with the highest number in the 80–89 age group (n = 86, 50.9%). Among the 1403 patients with dementia who did not undergo revascularization, 734 (52.3%) were aged 80–89 and 339 (24.2%) were aged ≥ 90. Overall, the baseline prevalence of dementia was higher in the nonrevascularized group (8.9%) compared to the revascularized group (1.9%), with the difference most pronounced in the ≥ 90 years age group. Discussion

By linking cognitive status to treatment decisions, the aim of this study was to better understand the intersection of dementia and ACS management. In this large, real-world cohort of over 26,000 insured older adults hospitalized with ACS in an integrated health care system, the authors found that nearly 6% (approximately 1 in 17) had a documented diagnosis of dementia at baseline. Patients who did not undergo coronary revascularization were older, had a higher burden of comorbidities, and were more likely to have dementia compared to those who underwent revascularization. Notably, the prevalence of dementia was 4-fold higher in the nonrevascularized group (7.9%) than in those who received revascularization (1.9%), with the disparity most pronounced among patients aged ≥ 90 years. Alzheimer’s disease accounted for the vast majority of dementia cases across all subgroups. These findings will inform the authors’ subsequent work examining the incidence of dementia following ACS, with a focus on how revascularization strategies may shape long-term cognitive outcomes.

The true community prevalence of dementia is likely underestimated by this study. In 2022, approximately 4.0% of US adults 65 years and older had a documented diagnosis of dementia, with an increase with age reaching 13.1% among those aged 85 and older. 11 In 2023, an estimated 6.7 million individuals aged ≥ 65 years were living with Alzheimer’s disease, with age-specific distributions of 26.7% for those aged 64–74, 37.9% for ages 75–84, and 35.4% for those aged 85 and older. 3 These reported figures likely underrepresent the actual burden of dementia, given the diagnostic challenges and heterogeneity of presentations. This study added to this evidence by quantifying the documented prevalence of dementia in a large, real-world cohort of insured older adults hospitalized with ACS. This reinforced the need for improved recognition of cognitive impairment in this high-risk population

The clinical and pathological complexity of dementia often contributes to diagnostic uncertainty regarding its presence and subtype, leading to underrecognition of its true prevalence, particularly in high-acuity populations such as those hospitalized with an ACS. Dementia can result from a range of neuropathological processes, including Alzheimer’s disease, cerebrovascular disease, frontotemporal degeneration, hippocampal sclerosis, Lewy body disease, Parkinson’s disease, mixed pathologies, and the recently recognized limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy. Current antemortem diagnosis of dementia and its subtypes relies on a constellation of clinical features supported by laboratory markers (blood and cerebrospinal fluid) and multimodal imaging techniques. These include structural imaging (computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging), functional imaging (fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography [FDG-PET], single photon emission computed tomography [SPECT]), and molecular imaging (amyloid PET, tau PET). These findings likely underestimate the true prevalence of dementia in older adults hospitalized with ACS, reflecting limitations in clinical recognition and documentation. These results support the need for more proactive cognitive screening and diagnostic approaches during acute cardiovascular hospitalizations to better inform patient-centered care.

These findings share some similarities but also notable differences with a recent large observational study examining the association between dementia and in-hospital outcomes. Using the National Inpatient Sample database of just over 1 million patients hospitalized for an acute myocardial infarction, the study findings demonstrated a baseline dementia prevalence of 9.1% with a similar mean age and high multimorbidity burden (70.3% had ≥ 4 Elixhauser Comorbidity Index). The presence of dementia was associated with decreased use of coronary revascularization strategies, decreased prevalence of cardiac arrest, cardiogenic shock, and discharge to home but higher in-hospital mortality, and greater transfer to postacute care facilities. 12 This study had a lower prevalence of dementia where, due to the granularity of Kaiser Permanente Northern California databases, the authors were also able to expound on the different subtypes of dementia and dementia medication use.

Several factors may explain the lower use of coronary revascularization among patients with dementia observed in this study. Clinicians may reasonably weigh higher procedural risk, limited life expectancy, reduced physiologic reserve, and concerns regarding postoperative recovery, particularly in individuals with more advanced cognitive or functional impairment. 12 Challenges related to shared decision-making, including communication barriers and reliance on surrogate decision-makers, as well as patient or caregiver preferences for conservative management, may further influence treatment selection. 8 In addition, variability in institutional practices and the absence of standardized approaches for incorporating cognitive status into revascularization decision-making likely contribute to these observed patterns. Importantly, the present study was not designed to assess the appropriateness of revascularization decisions in patients with dementia, nor could it determine whether observed differences reflect optimal, over-, or underutilization of coronary revascularization in this population.

All dementias are not equal, and heterogeneity in disease severity, progression, and impact on function necessitates a more nuanced approach to care in older adults with an ACS. 13 To better phenotype patients with dementia in this setting, the Functional Assessment Staging Tool (FAST) offers a validated framework for staging cognitive and functional decline. FAST is based on the level of loss in instrumental activities of daily living and activities of daily living. Until validated research in this area is available, the authors believe incorporating FAST (with appropriate consultation with a geriatrician or neurocognitive specialist) into clinical decision-making may help align the intensity of revascularization strategies or medical management with the patient’s stage of dementia, prognosis, and goals of care (ie, higher FAST scales potentially signal futility of certain revascularization strategies). 2 Importantly, for older adults with dementia, preserving functional status and independence remains a central therapeutic goal, underscoring the need for individualized, goal-concordant cardiovascular care.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, dementia diagnoses were based on existing electronic health record documentation, which may have altered the true prevalence, particularly among patients with varying functional levels with major neurocognitive impairment. Also, the purpose of this study was not to address the epidemiology of minor cognitive impairment within the ACS cohort. Patients with severe dementia may not have been hospitalized for ACS. The authors also did not capture variables such as frailty, caregiver support, and goals of care that could have enhanced this study. The authors’ primary purpose was to ascertain all causes of dementia. The authors may not have captured the true incidence of dementia subtypes based on current ascertainment of dementia and its subtypes by ICD codes. Despite these limitations, this study had notable strengths. It drew from a large, diverse, and insured population within an integrated health care system, allowing for comprehensive capture of clinical diagnoses, medication use, and treatment patterns across multiple hospitals.

Conclusions

In this large, real-world cohort of older adults with ACS, nearly 1 in 17 had a documented dementia diagnosis. Although Alzheimer’s disease was the most common subtype, other forms, including vascular dementia, Lewy body/Parkinson’s dementia, frontotemporal dementia, and unspecified types, were also observed. Recognizing baseline cognitive status may improve ACS management and support more informed, patient-centered treatment decisions.
