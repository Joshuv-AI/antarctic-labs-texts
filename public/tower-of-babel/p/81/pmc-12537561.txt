Benzene in cosmetic products: Non cancer and cancer risk assessment
Authors: Abhishek Gautam, Peter J Cressey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCosmetics are widely used products that include luxury beauty items like makeup and perfumes as well as everyday hygiene products like toothpaste, shampoo, deodorant, and soap. Most cosmetics are used by adults, although there are some products, such as sunscreen and shampoo, which are also used by children. Although benzene is not intentionally added in cosmetics, it may potentially be present in trace amounts as a contaminant or leftover solvent in finished goods. IARC has classified benzene carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) based on sufficient evidence that it causes acute myeloid leukaemia in adults. A risk assessment was performed to evaluate non-cancer and cancer human health risks of benzene in leave-on cosmetic products (deodorant and sunscreen) through dermal and inhalation pathways. Quantitative risk assessment estimated potential systemic exposure doses. The non-cancer risk assessment of benzene in cosmetics resulted in a safety margin greater than 100. The probability of lifetime cancer risk resulting from dermal and inhalation exposure to benzene from cosmetics was 2.4 × 10−6 i.e. lower than an excess cancer risk of 10−5 used as a World Health Organization benchmark. Given that it is highly improbable that a consumer would use benzene-contaminated products on a daily basis for the rest of their life, the study's findings indicate that there is no evidence of an elevated risk of non-cancer effects and minimal cancer risks in humans from exposure to benzene in deodorant and sunscreen.Keywords: Benzene, Cosmetics, Deodorants, Lifetime cancer risk, Margin of safety, Risk assessment, Sunscreen

Introduction

Cosmetics range from everyday hygiene products such as soap, shampoo, deodorant, and toothpaste to luxury beauty items, including perfumes and make up. Cosmetic regulations vary from country to country and so do the definitions. A product might be categorised as a cosmetic in one country and as a drug in another country. In the European Union (EU) and New Zealand the definitions of cosmetics are similar: “a cosmetic product means any product or preparation intended to be placed in contact with the various external parts of the human body (epidermis, hair system, nails, lips and external genital organs) or with the teeth and the mucous membranes of the oral cavity with a view exclusively or mainly to cleaning them, perfuming them, changing their appearance and/or correcting body odours and/or protecting them or keeping them in good condition” [23], [8].

Cosmetics are generally categorised as rinse-off or leave-on. Rinse-off products are intended to be removed after application on the skin, the hair or the mucous membranes and include products such as shower gel, hand wash, shampoo, and hair conditioner. Leave-on products are intended to stay in prolonged contact with the skin, the hair or the mucous membranes, such as perfumes, deodorants, lotions, sunscreen, and creams [26], [8].

Benzene is a colorless compound and has a sweet, aromatic, gasoline-like odor. Benzene is found in crude petroleum oils and as a by-product of oil-refining processes. Benzene is also used as an industrial solvent, a chemical intermediate, and is used in the synthesis of numerous chemicals. It is also found in high concentrations in cigarette smoke [3]. Due to the wide applications of benzene, it ranks in the top 20 chemicals produced in the United States (US) by volume [2]. In humans, acute inhalation and oral exposures to high concentrations of benzene have resulted in central nervous system depression and death. The lethal oral dose for humans has been estimated to be 125 mg/kg [3]. Mucous membrane irritation is reported after acute inhalation exposure to benzene vapours in humans at > 60 ppm for up to 3 weeks. Benzene is a moderate eye irritant in animals. Two drops of benzene caused moderate conjunctival irritation and very slight, transient corneal injury in rabbits. Occupational exposures of > 60 ppm for up to 3 weeks are known to cause skin irritation in humans. These effects are due to direct contact of the skin with the vapor, and other dermal effects resulting from direct contact with the skin [2]. Chronic inhalation exposure to benzene primarily causes impairment of the haemopoietic system, with depression of bone marrow production leading to aplastic anaemia being the most common clinical manifestation in humans. In addition to effects on the hematopoietic system, benzene exposures have been implicated in neurological disorders, immune dysfunction and cancer [2], [34].

Benzene is carcinogenic to humans and laboratory animals [18]. Epidemiological and case studies have established that benzene is a human leukaemogen. It causes acute myeloid leukaemia/acute and non-lymphocytic leukaemia. An increased mortality from leukaemia has been demonstrated in workers occupationally exposed to benzene [2]. Several types of tumour, primarily of epithelial origin, have been induced in mice and rats after both oral and inhalation exposure [18], [2]. These include tumours in the zymbal gland, liver, mammary gland and nasal cavity. IARC has classified benzene as “carcinogenic to humans (group 1)” [18]. Benzene is a clastogen and causes both structural and numerical chromosomal aberrations in humans. The same effects have also been observed in animals. Findings of chromosomal aberrations in bone marrow and lymphocytes in animals support the human case reports and epidemiological studies in which chromosomal damage was linked to benzene exposure [18], [2], [34].

Recently benzene was detected in a range of cosmetic products such as sunscreen, deodorants, antiperspirants, shampoos and conditioners ([17], [31]; US [12], [33]). Its presence can be technically unavoidable in the final product and can result at one or more stages of a product’s lifecycle. Benzene may be used as a solvent in the production of raw materials such as carbomer (polyacrylic acid) or butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA, an antioxidant). It is possible that there may be some residual benzene remaining in such products [15]. Organic propellants (such as propane, butane, etc.) are added to aerosol products (deodorants, antiperspirants). These organic propellants may contain residues of benzene (P&G, 2021). Sometimes, polymers used in cosmetic products may be polymerised in benzene which can be another source of contamination [15].

The use of benzene in cosmetic products is regulated due to its carcinogenicity and other adverse health effects. In the EU, benzene is prohibited from use in any cosmetic products. EC [8]. However, benzene can be unintentionally present as a constituent of other substances, or in mixtures, in concentrations equal to, or greater than 0.1 % w/w [7]. In the US, cosmetic products are regulated under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic (FD&C) Act and Fair Packaging and Labeling Act. The food and drug administration (FDA) classifies benzene as a Class 1 solvent “that should not be employed in the manufacture of drug substance. The benzene content of products is restricted to 2 mg/kg, unless otherwise justified. The same levels are applicable to cosmetic products (US [13]). In New Zealand, regulation of cosmetic products is covered by the Cosmetic Products Group Standard 2020 under the Hazardous Substances and New Organisms Act 1996 [23]. Under the group standard, cosmetic products must not contain benzene, other than at a trace level provided that such presence is technically unavoidable in good manufacturing practice [23]. In Australia, the chemicals used in cosmetic products are regulated as industrial chemicals under the Industrial Chemicals Act 2019, which is administered by the Australian Industrial Chemicals Introduction Scheme (AICIS) There is no list of chemicals which are restricted or banned for use in cosmetic products. The bans and restriction on chemicals in cosmetics are regulated by each state and territory authority [1]. For some products like sunscreens that are regulated as listed medicines in Australia, the levels of benzene must be below 2 mg/kg [31]. Many deodorants and sunscreens were found to contain benzene above the limit of 2 mg/kg which may raise health concerns for consumers or public [31], [33].

Safety evaluation of cosmetic products has evolved over the years. Animal testing for cosmetic products and cosmetic ingredients has been banned in many regions such as Australia, EU, New Zealand and the US. New Approach Methods or NAMs (in chemico, in vitro, in silico, read across) are utilized to assess the toxicity of chemical ingredients used in cosmetic products [21]. Certain ingredients and contaminants present in very low concentration in cosmetic products can be assessed by applying a Threshold of Toxicology Concern. In the EU, research projects such as SEURAT-1 and EU-ToxRisk have made strong efforts to establish an animal free safety assessment [4]. The Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety (SCCS) is responsible for assessing the safety and risks associated with non-food consumer products in the EU [27]. The SCCS provides guidance, scientific advice and opinions on the use of certain cosmetics ingredients. In contrast, safety assessment of cosmetic products is not required to place the product in the Australian and New Zealand market.

Due to the detection of benzene in deodorants and sunscreens from well-known companies that are also available in New Zealand raised concerns and prompted this risk assessment. The purpose of this assessment was to evaluate non-cancer and cancer human health risks in adults and children (up to the age of 15 years) from using deodorant and sunscreen products in which benzene contamination has been reported. While it is possible that benzene residues may be present in other types of cosmetic products, the ‘leave on’ nature of deodorants and sunscreens means that these products will represent a worst-case scenario for exposure. The potential systemic exposure doses (SEDs) were estimated for dermal and inhalation pathways and the SEDs were compared relevant toxicological point of departures (PODs) for benzene that is set to protect the general population from appreciable non-cancer and cancer risks.

Materials and methods

Concentration of benzene in deodorants and sunscreens

Recently, there have been product recalls of various well known cosmetic products due to benzene contamination [6], [25], [12]. Valisure (an independent laboratory in the US) analysed 108 unique batches from 30 different brands of antiperspirant and deodorant body spray products for benzene [17], [32], [33]. Significant variability from batch to batch was observed, even within a single brand. Twenty-four body spray products from 8 different brands contained benzene at concentrations in the range 2.24 – 17.7 mg/kg of benzene, 14 body spray products from 8 brands contained detectable benzene between 0.20 – 1.89 mg/kg, and 21 products from 8 brands contained detectable benzene at < 0.1 mg/kg. Benzene was not detected in an additional 49 body sprays from 19 different brands through initial analysis of at least one sample [17], [33].

In Australia, cosmetic products that make therapeutic claims, including sunscreen, are regulated by the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA). The TGA tested 18 aerosol sunscreen products and found three of them to contain levels of benzene above the 2 mg/kg limit. Benzene was detected in nine of eighteen products with concentration in the range of 0.1 – 5.2 mg/kg and was suspected to be a contaminant from raw materials used in product manufacture [31].

The maximum reported concentrations of 18 and 5.2 mg/kg were used in the current risk assessment of benzene in deodorant and sunscreen, respectively [31], [32], [33].

Analytical methods

As stated above, the analyses of antiperspirant and deodorant body spray products was done by Valisure which is an analytical laboratory that is accredited to International Organization for Standardization 17025:2017 standard for general requirements for the competence of testing and calibration laboratories [32]. Therefore, they adhere to the requirements for the competence, impartiality, and consistent operation of laboratories, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of their testing and calibration results to all the necessary quality standards for testing.

In this analytical work, detection of benzene in different spray products (deodorants and antiperspirants) was done using USP < 467 > Residual Solvents Procedure A method that was modified from flame ionization detection to mass spectrometry (MS) [17], [32]. Gas chromatography (Agilent 7890B) equipped with 7697 A headspace autosampler coupled with 5977B MS was utilized for sample analysis, and a DB-Select 624 UI, 60 m × 0.32 mm × 1.8 μm GC column (Agilent Technology, Santa Clara, CA) was used to separate benzene from other compounds.

The method validation of the analytical procedure was performed by evaluating lower limit of detection (LLoD), lower limit of quantification (LLoQ), and correlation coefficient [17], [32]. Linear non-forcing through zero calibration curve was generated from the peak areas of the 11-point calibration standards. Calibration curve was accepted when the coefficient of determination (r2) was equal or greater than 0.995. Lower limit of detection (LLOD) and lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) were determined by spiking known amount of benzene into body spray samples. LLOD was 0.01 μg (equivalent to 0.02 mg/kg in body spray products) and LLOQ was 0.025 μg (equivalent to 0.05 mg/kg in body spray products).

The analyses of sunscreen products was performed by TGA laboratories using a validated headspace gas chromatography tandem mass spectrometry method [31]. No further information on validation parameters was available. However, according to TGA, their testing laboratory targets the most appropriate quality and/or performance aspects relevant to the product being tested [30].

Exposure assessment

The exposure assessment is carried out following the SCCS “notes of guidance for the testing of cosmetic ingredients and their safety” and a guide on inhalation safety assessment for spray products by the European aerosol federation (FEA) [14], [26]. The consumer exposure to benzene from use of deodorant/anti-perspirant and sunscreen is considered incidental. The scenarios considered in the exposure assessment depend on the intended uses of the selected cosmetic products. For deodorants/anti-perspirants, dermal exposure and inhalation exposure during application of aerosol products were considered relevant. Although there is no set age to start the use of deodorants, young children are recommended to use a stick, roll-on, or cream product rather than an aerosol deodorant [20]. Therefore, children (<15 years) were not considered to be users of aerosol deodorant products. For sunscreen, only the dermal route of exposure was considered relevant for both adults and children. While some aerosol sunscreen products exist, they will often be applied outdoors and inhalation exposure to benzene from use of such products was considered to be negligible. Oral exposure to benzene from use of cosmetic products was also considered to be negligible.

Non cancer risk characterisation

A margin of safety (MoS) approach was used to assess risk in relation to non-cancer effects associated with benzene in cosmetic products. For this exercise, MoS is the ratio between a systemic equivalent of a point of departure dose (PoDsys) and an estimate of the SED [26]. For POD doses from oral administration of benzene, it has been assumed that oral absorption is 100 % and PoDsys will be equivalent to the oral external PoD dose [26].

Where PoDsys is a dose descriptor for a point of departure for the systemic exposure to benzene. For MoS risk characterisation, PoD will typically be a Benchmark Dose Lower Confidence Limit (BMDL), no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) or low observed adverse effect level (LOAEL). The benchmark dose (BMD) approach is preferred over NOAEL approach because BMD approach generally makes no particular assumption about the biological basis of observed dose-response relationships other than that of the magnitude of the response ([9]; US [11]). BMDL provides a more robust and informative estimate than NOAEL as it is a statistically derived value from a dose-response curve. For a chemical substance with health thresholds (i.e, not genotoxic and not carcinogenic), a MOS > = 100 is generally considered to be protective [26]. In this assessment, the BMDL10 value of 1.2 mg/kg bw per day (oral) for derived by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) was used [19]. The BMDL10 was derived from a human occupational inhalation study noting decreased lymphocyte count as a critical effect of benzene. As this BMDL10 relates to oral exposure PoDsys will be equal to PoD.

Non-cancer risk

A summary of the non-cancer risk assessment for benzene in leave-on cosmetic products is given in Table 2. Aggregated SED (inhalation and dermal) to benzene from deodorants and sunscreen in adults was 2.3E-05 mg/kg bw per day. Dermal SED for children was 7.78E-05 (P95) and 1.77E-05 (mean) mg/kg bw per day. These SED values were compared against BMDL10 values to provide MOS calculations provided in Table 2. Discussion

The current risk assessment estimation is of non-cancer and cancer risks from dermal and inhalation exposure to deodorants and sunscreen containing benzene

Detection of benzene in cosmetic products has raised concerns regarding the potential health risks. In 2021, several types of cosmetic products (deodorants, antiperspirants, sunscreens) in the US and Australia were tested and found to contain traces of benzene (ConsumerNZ, 2021; [24], [31]; US [12], [33]). The products were voluntarily recalled from the Australian and US market by the respective companies as benzene was found above the restriction limit 2 mg/kg. The current study used the maximum concentrations as a basis for risk assessment [31], [32]. While the use of maximum concentrations for chronic exposure assessment may be viewed as an overly conservative approach, brand loyalty to particular cosmetic products means that consumers are less likely to be drawing products randomly from the available brands and could be habitually using products containing high concentrations of benzene, relative to other products.

For non-cancer risks, all the MOS values were greater than 100 for aggregate exposure to benzene from deodorant and sunscreen (adults) or sunscreen alone (children). Hence, it can be concluded that potential exposure to benzene from using deodorant and sunscreen does not pose a non-cancer human health risk. LCR was estimated as benzene is a human carcinogen [18]. For cancer risk, the maximum LCR calculated was lower than the excess cancer risk of 10−5, used as a benchmark for other contaminants (e.g. in water) [29]. Therefore, the excess cancer risk due to benzene in deodorant and sunscreen is low or negligible. Given the degree of conservatism in the risk assessment, these results should provide some assurance that the levels of benzene detected in cosmetic products should not be a major cause for public health concern.

There are some limitations to this risk assessment. The concentration data used for the risk assessment came from studies that provided limited information on analytical quality control for detection of benzene. In particular, while the studies provided some information on the precision of the method used, no information on the accuracy (spike recovery) was available and further information from studies using well validated analytical methods is desirable. The current study only considered exposure to benzene from use of two classes of cosmetic products. While it is likely that these leave-on products are the major cosmetic contributors to benzene exposure, other cosmetic products may contribute but no information was available to assess this. Other sources of benzene exposure, such as air, water and food were not considered in this risk assessment. The concentration data and exposure factor information used in the risk assessment were drawn from different sources and, as such, the risk assessment will not reflect the particular situation in any single country.

Conclusion

Based on a conservative exposure model developed in this study, the non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risk due to benzene contamination in sunscreen and deodorants has been estimated. The MOS was greater than 100 for children and adults (aggregated risk), which indicates that benzene in deodorants (in adults) and sunscreen (in adults and children) in the concentrations reported is of low concern for public health [26].

LCR was estimated as a lifestage weighted mean for adults (sunscreen and deodorant use) and children (sunscreen use only). The result indicates that lifetime exposure to benzene from sunscreen and deodorant use will equate to an excess cancer risk of < 10−5, or 1 excess cancer in 100,000 individuals, which has been used in New Zealand for standard setting for drinking water standards [29]. Therefore, even if benzene is found in sunscreen and deodorant at the concentration used for the assessment (5.2 and 18 mg/kg in sunscreen and deodorant, respectively), the excess cancer risk due to benzene is low, but its acceptability is a subjective matter.
