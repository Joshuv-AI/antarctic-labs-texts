Can Laws Be Flexible? Rethinking Legislation for Innovation
Authors: Nick K Schneider, Ariel Dora Stern, W Nicholson Price II, Jochen K Lennerz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgile legislation adapts principles from agile software development to lawmaking, emphasizing iteration, multi-stakeholder feedback, and embedded revision. We outline this learning-oriented governance model using three case studies: Germany’s stepwise digital health legislation, the EU AI Act, and U.S. FDA user-fee reauthorization. These examples highlight legislative designs that enable structured generation of real-world data and evidence during implementation, informing regulatory interpretation and iterative refinement in rapidly evolving technological domains.Subject terms: Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Laws are often rightly seen as rigid constructs, deliberately designed to provide stability, predictability, and fairness. At the same time, legal systems are intentionally designed to balance stability with the capacity for evolution. Substantive legal rules routinely change through interpretation, amendment, delegated legislation, and the incorporation of external technical or industry standards. Accordingly the legislative process is intentionally deliberative and involves multiple steps and stakeholders. Yet this rigidity can come at a cost, especially in sectors that demand rapid innovation, such as healthcare and technology, and particularly so when emerging challenges demand timely responses.

Procedural requirements and safeguards are essential to legitimacy, accountability, and the protection of rights. In this context, procedural rigidity does not refer to the existence of due process or safeguards, but to governance structures that limit the systematic incorporation of new evidence or implementation experience into regulatory recalibration, even when procedural integrity is preserved.

Here, we provide an overview of agile legislation as a governance framework that adapts principles from agile software development to lawmaking. We examine how legal systems can be intentionally designed to function as learning institutions, capable of adapting to technological and scientific change without sacrificing democratic legitimacy, legal certainty, or procedural integrity. Accordingly, the focus of this work is not on law as an inherently rigid construct, but on the institutional design of lawmaking and regulatory governance processes, and specifically, how mechanisms for learning, feedback, and revision can be embedded, formalized, and sustained over time.

The regulatory lifecycle

Regulatory governance is not a single legislative act but best understood as a process shaped by statutes, delegated legislation, administrative rulemaking, guidance, and interpretive practices, and realized through rulemaking, implementation, monitoring, enforcement, and revision. Here, distinctions between these instruments are made explicit, as they differ substantially in how adaptation, oversight, and revision occur in practice. The mechanisms discussed in this manuscript thus operate at different points along this regulatory lifecycle, and the capacity for agility varies accordingly across these stages. Many legal systems already incorporate flexibility through mechanisms such as the reference to international, technical, or industry standards, as well as through interpretive discretion. These mechanisms demonstrate that adaptability is not new. However, they often operate implicitly or episodically, rather than as part of a deliberately designed, end-to-end learning framework that links implementation outcomes to systematic revision.

Legislative agility does not arise from statutes alone. In practice, regulatory adaptation emerges from the interaction of multiple legal layers operating across both governance levels and normative domains, including supranational, national, and sub-national law; horizontal and sector-specific legislation; delegated and implementing acts; administrative guidance; technical standards; enforcement practice; and judicial interpretation. Because these instruments differ in procedure, speed of revision, and institutional ownership, they contribute unevenly to learning and adaptability over time. Understanding how these layers interact (and where agility is deliberately designed versus informally accommodated) is essential to understanding the current limitations and the prospective potential of learning-oriented, agile legislative frameworks.

The promise of agility: a blueprint for legislative innovation

The concept of agility, while often associated with software development1,2, has been extensively explored in legal, regulatory, and governance scholarship on adaptive regulation, experimental legislation, and regulatory learning3,4. Agile methodologies emphasize a strategic approach: adapting and re-adapting product development to the actual needs of society through iteration, responsiveness, and mechanisms for collecting continuous feedback5.

These principles stand in stark contrast to traditional legislative cycles that can span years. The traditional legislative process entails an initial needs assessment, evaluation of pre-existing laws, stakeholder consultation, public debate, parliamentary negotiation, as well as regulatory implementation at national, regional, and local levels6.

In agile development, work is broken into smaller components or “sprints,” with each cycle allowing for feedback, adaptation, and course correction. If adapted to lawmaking, an agile legislative model can allow legislators to enact laws iteratively, including experimentation clauses or pilot statutes (with appropriate safeguards), gather real-world outcomes, and then revise accordingly.

Agile legislation is therefore used here to describe an institutionalized approach in which feedback, evidence generation, and revision mechanisms are explicitly embedded within the legislative framework1,7. Accordingly, the term is employed as an organizing concept rather than as a claim of novelty.

Conceptual framework and definitions

To clarify how learning-oriented legislative approaches operate, one must conceptually distinguish three analytical layers that are often conflated in the literature: (a) key principles and boundary conditions, (b) related regulatory concepts, and (c) core design elements (Fig. 1). Key principles and boundary conditions define what agile legislation should aim to achieve and where its application must stop; related regulatory concepts situate it within the broader landscape of governance approaches; and core design elements specify how agile legislative mechanisms are concretely operationalized in practice.

Democratic complementarity

Agile legislative approaches are not intended to displace ordinary legislative procedures. Rather, they complement established lawmaking by making learning, feedback, and revision an explicit and institutionalized part of regulatory governance; embedded within existing democratic institutions rather than replacing them, and moving beyond ad hoc adjustments or ex post responses to failure5. Agile legislation is therefore democratically complementary, not procedurally substitutive, denoting a more deliberate integration of evaluation and recalibration within the life cycle of law.

Selective legal adaptability

The outlined approach differs from conventional legislative practice, not in reliance on participation or deliberation (which are already well-established), but in the selective scope of where adaptability is designed into law. Feedback, evaluation, and revision are formally anticipated and structured at the time of enactment, rather than occurring only through subsequent amendment or crisis-driven reform. In this context, “governance” denotes the broader ecosystem of lawmaking, implementation, oversight, and adaptation; not just the legislative process, but also how systems learn, evolve, and regulate themselves. Accordingly, this framework does not advocate for faster or emergency lawmaking. Instead, it examines how learning-oriented mechanisms (including feedback and revision) can be more deliberately and systematically embedded within ordinary legislative and regulatory processes, while preserving the stability of core normative commitments that are not subject to iterative change.

Domain specificity

Agile legislation is neither universally applicable nor desirable across all areas of law. Its relevance is domain-dependent and most compelling in sectors characterized by rapid technological change, high system complexity, and significant societal stakes1. Healthcare exemplifies such a domain. Here, scientific progress, digital transformation, and evolving clinical practices place continuous pressure on regulatory frameworks to remain both protective and enabling1,3,7. Although learning and inspiration across domains are both possible and valuable, agile legislative mechanisms are not transferable by default5. Where the institutional, normative, or risk conditions that enable agility in one domain are not present in another, adaptive approaches cannot be assumed to function with the same legitimacy or effectiveness5.

Accordingly, this analysis treats healthcare as a primary case for examining agile legislative design. While the underlying principles are not confined to health law, their transfer across domains cannot be assumed: experiences in areas such as financial technology or European data governance (e.g., the European Health Data Space (EHDS)) offer valuable lessons, but must be interpreted in the context of differing institutional, normative, and risk contexts. This underscores a core boundary condition of agile legislation: it is not a universal template, but a targeted governance strategy whose appropriateness depends on sectoral dynamics, institutional capacity, and societal risk tolerance.

The selected key principles collectively clarify that agile legislation does not replace ordinary lawmaking, does not apply across all legal domains, and does not extend to all elements of statutory design (Table 1).

To define agile legislation with analytical rigor, conceptual clarity is needed. The literature uses a range of related terms to describe regulatory adaptation; these concepts are neither synonymous nor interchangeable (Table 2). They reflect distinct governance logics and must therefore be systematically distinguished. Doing so is necessary not only to prevent definitional overlap but also to ensure that the contribution of agile legislation is situated accurately within the broader landscape of regulatory and governance theory. Agile Legislation and Related Concepts

The concepts are listed in the order of their appearance in the text, not in order of their normative or analytical importance.

Experimental regulation refers to time-limited or pilot-based legal instruments designed to test specific policy hypotheses under real-world conditions, generating evidence that can inform subsequent scaling, modification, or termination of regulatory approaches (Table 2). Thus, experimental regulation operationalizes learning within bounded legal spaces and represents a concrete mechanism often associated with agile legislative design but typically applied to test specific policy hypotheses3.

Adaptive regulation describes regulatory systems that evolve over time through interpretation, enforcement practices, and delegated or implementing acts8, allowing legal requirements to be adjusted without reopening primary legislation (Table 2). While not inherently agile, adaptive regulation provides the structural pathways through which feedback and incremental revision can be absorbed into ongoing governance.

Emergency Legislation involves temporary legal measures enacted in response to urgent or exceptional circumstances, often relaxing standard procedural or substantive requirements. The COVID-19 pandemic laid bare the tension between speed and legal safeguards while simultaneously showing that it can be managed9. Indeed, while the pandemic revealed structural weaknesses in legislative systems10, it also demonstrated the potential for extraordinary responsiveness when urgency demanded it11. For example, the FDA’s Emergency Use Authorization (EUA) process during the pandemic allowed rapid deployment of diagnostic tools while gathering real-world data and evidence for post-authorization evaluation12. In other words, rapid change was possible when necessity outweighed procedural rigidity. Unfortunately, momentum is often lost after crises, and the pendulum swings back13. Emergency legislative measures adopted during the COVID-19 pandemic are therefore noted here as contrast cases rather than as examples of agile legislation: while they demonstrate that legal systems can respond under exceptional circumstances, they generally lacked the structured feedback and learning mechanisms characteristic of institutionalized agile legislative design. Further, such responses do not provide a sustainable framework for structured learning, accountability, and predictable revision outside of crisis settings. Recent history calls upon policymakers and scholars to rethink not just how laws are observed and enforced, but how they are crafted in the first place14.

Better regulation framework

Existing alternative approaches to regulatory quality already incorporate structured mechanisms for evidence-based policymaking and accountability. For example, the United Kingdom’s “Better Regulation Framework” (BRF) provides a formal system for governing the regulatory lifecycle, from early options and impact analysis to independent scrutiny of evidence, transparency requirements, and mandatory post-implementation review15. Designed to ensure proportionality and encourage alternatives to regulation, the BRF evaluates implemented rules against real-world impacts, with mechanisms for revision or removal when objectives are not met.

Agile legislation builds on, rather than replaces, such frameworks by embedding learning and revision directly into statutory and governance design ex ante. While better regulation initiatives primarily focus on improving the quality of regulatory decision-making within established legislative processes, agile legislation emphasizes the deliberate design of statutes and governance architectures that anticipate uncertainty, embed learning mechanisms ex ante, and formalize pathways for revision informed by implementation experience; agile legislation extends better regulation principles from regulatory appraisal towards institutionalized legislative adaptability.

Computational law

Computational law involves the formal representation of legal rules in logic or code to enable automation, simulation, and consistent implementation, thereby strengthening the technical foundations of modern regulatory systems. A related concept, Rules as Code (RaC), is best understood as a practical instantiation of computational law: while computational law denotes the broader field concerned with formalizing legal rules for automation and analysis, “Rules as Code” refers to the concrete practice of encoding legislation in machine-readable form to support implementation, testing, and evaluation. This approach has been explored in multiple public-sector contexts, including OECD initiatives and national experimentation programs in Canada and Australia, emphasizing machine-consumable rules, simulation, and iterative learning within rulemaking processes. Specifically, RaC proposes that legal rules and regulations be expressed not only in natural language but also in a machine-consumable form that can be directly executed, tested, and simulated by digital systems16–18. RaC initiatives aim to reduce the gap between legislative intent and implementation by enabling rules to be validated against real or hypothetical scenarios, thereby supporting iterative refinement through a “test, learn, repeat” cycle embedded in the rulemaking process18. RaC operationalizes certain dimensions of agility at the technical and implementation level, particularly by enabling ex ante modeling, consistency of application, and rapid feedback from deployment. However, RaC primarily addresses how rules are implemented and consumed, rather than how and when the underlying legal authority to change those rules is exercised. Agile legislation thus operates at a complementary but distinct level: it focuses on the legal design choices that determine which elements of law may adapt over time, under what conditions, and through which democratically accountable processes16,17. RaC can thus be understood as an enabling infrastructure that serves as a technical and implementation approach for downstream translation of agile legislative frameworks, rather than a substitute for them.

Regulatory sandboxes

Regulatory sandboxes provide controlled environments in which innovators can test new technologies or services under temporary regulatory flexibility, generating evidence for future rulemaking while maintaining supervisory oversight (see also below). Sandboxes are a bounded experimental tool, whereas agile legislation is a system-level design that determines how learning from such tools is institutionally absorbed, evaluated, and translated into durable regulatory change and revision across ordinary lawmaking. Germany has begun to institutionalize this pathway, using statutory pilot programs under SGB V19 and the proposed Reallabore-Gesetz (“Regulatory Sandbox Act”)20 to translate sandbox experimentation into durable regulatory learning. As such, regulatory sandboxes are a means to the end of agile legislation.

Experimentalist governance describes a regulatory model in which high-level goals are set centrally, implementation is decentralized, and performance is reviewed through recursive feedback loops that enable continuous learning and adjustment across institutions. Experimentalist governance is a theoretical governance model, while agile legislation translates similar learning principles into concrete legislative design choices.

Responsive regulation refers to enforcement and oversight strategies that adapt in intensity and form to regulated actors’ behavior and risk profiles, emphasizing escalation and de-escalation rather than uniform application of fixed rules. Responsive regulation focuses on adaptive enforcement, whereas agile legislation focuses on adaptive lawmaking and statutory design.

Post-legislative scrutiny denotes systematic review of enacted laws to assess their real-world effects, implementation challenges, and unintended consequences, informing subsequent amendment or recalibration. Post-legislative scrutiny is typically ex post and evaluative, while agile legislation embeds learning and revision ex ante as part of the legislative architecture.

These related concepts collectively frame agile legislation not as a tool, a technique, or a crisis response, but as a governance design paradigm that integrates experimentation, adaptation, quality control, and enablement within ordinary lawmaking. Building on these conceptual distinctions, the following section specifies the design elements through which agile legislative approaches can be operationalized.

Design elements of an agile legislative model

The design elements are ordered to reflect a top-down governance logic: from immutable normative commitments and institutional authority, through architectural decomposition and bounded mechanisms of change, to learning and feedback generated through implementation.

Normative stability

Importantly, not all elements of the law are suitable for agile or iterative modification8. Core normative commitments, for example, fundamental rights, liability principles, jurisdictional boundaries, and democratic accountability mechanisms, must remain stable and subject to ordinary legislative processes.

Adaptability denotes the capacity of legal systems to accommodate new evidence or technologies without full statutory replacement.

Institutional allocation of adaptive authority

This element concerns how authority to interpret, update, and recalibrate legal requirements is deliberately allocated across institutions8. Agile legislative design aligns adaptive mechanisms with existing governance institutions, oversight structures, and accountability arrangements, thereby preserving democratic legitimacy while clarifying which actors may adjust which elements of the regulatory framework, under what conditions, and through which procedures.

Modularity refers to the intentional decomposition of legal and regulatory frameworks into semi-independent components that can be revised, updated, or sunset without reopening the entire statutory architecture7. This structural separation enables targeted adaptation while maintaining overall legal coherence and maximizing stability.

Technical and operational design

Agile legislative mechanisms are most appropriately applied to those aspects of law that are contingent on evolving scientific knowledge and real-world performance (e.g., technical specifications, implementation pathways, evidentiary thresholds, and operational rules). Making this distinction explicit is essential to ensuring that agility enhances, rather than undermines, democratic legitimacy: core normative commitments remain stable, while adaptive capacity is deliberately concentrated in domains where learning from practice is both necessary and institutionally manageable. For analytical clarity, the technical and operational dimensions of this adaptive space are treated here as distinct, though closely related, design elements. While closely related, they address different aspects of adaptive capacity: technical enablement concerns the infrastructures and tools that make learning and adjustment possible, whereas operational governance concerns the institutional routines and decision pathways through which that learning is translated into regulatory change (Table 3).

Technical enablement refers to the infrastructures and tools that make adaptive governance possible in practice. This includes standardized and machine-readable representations of legal rules, interoperable data and reporting standards, digital systems for monitoring and evaluation, and analytical tools for testing, simulation, and impact assessment. These technical arrangements do not determine regulatory outcomes themselves, but they condition the system’s capacity to generate reliable evidence and to support timely, consistent implementation across institutions.

Operational governance refers to the institutional routines, responsibilities, and decision pathways through which learning is translated into regulatory action. It encompasses review and reauthorization cycles, the use of delegated and implementing acts, coordination among agencies and oversight bodies, and the procedural rules that specify who may adjust which elements of the framework, under what conditions, and through which processes. In this sense, operational governance is the bridge between technical capacity and democratic accountability: it ensures that adaptive mechanisms operate within clearly defined authority structures rather than through informal or discretionary practices.

Bounded adaptability

Rather than attempting to specify all regulatory requirements in advance, an agile legislative model emphasizes the use of bounded and explicit mechanisms, not open-ended flexibility (e.g., including experimentation clauses, review and sunset provisions, reauthorization cycles, and post-implementation evidence requirements) that collectively allow legal frameworks to be adjusted based on real-world performance7,8. Thereby, bounded adaptability is neither ad hoc, unlimited, nor discretionary; it proceeds through predefined legal pathways, applies only to specified regulatory elements, and operates under obligations, timelines, and institutional responsibilities that are determined in advance.

Learning under uncertainty

An agile legislative model treats uncertainty as a design feature; by starting with a general legal framework, clarifying what needs to be ensured, evaluating its impact, and then specifying further rules and guidelines or removing regulatory roadblocks based on empirically observable needs identified through implementation experience21 (e.g., including impacts on patients, healthcare providers, regulators, and other affected stakeholders). The potential benefits of such mechanisms include improved evidence generation, reduced regulatory lag in fast-moving technological domains, and greater transparency about when and how regulatory assumptions are revisited.

Contextual learning (post-implementation feedback)

Contextual learning denotes institutionalized feedback loops (i.e., structured mechanisms) through which real-world outcomes, performance data, and stakeholder impacts observed during implementation are systematically incorporated into subsequent regulatory interpretation, calibration, or revision22.

Examples in practice: early forms of agile legislation

Though still uncommon as an explicit design principle, learning-oriented and iterative elements have begun to surface in legislative practice through mechanisms such as staged implementation, periodic review clauses, delegated updating authority, and structured reauthorization requirements. The following healthcare case studies are illustrative and heuristic in nature. We highlight early signals of agility across different jurisdictions: (1) Germany’s experimentation with iterative digital health legislation, (2) the European Union’s flexible mechanisms embedded in the AI Act, and (3) the United States’ structured reauthorization cycles regarding user fee agreements. Each example illustrates a distinct approach to embedding feedback, adaptability, or periodic review into lawmaking. Together, these cases provide a foundation for a broader call to action and to rethink how legislative design can be applied in healthcare in ways that better align with the pace of innovation.

Case Study 1. Germany’s digital health transformation

In hindsight, Germany’s recent digital health transformation offers a notable case study. Rather than a single sweeping reform, over the past decade (Fig. 2), a clear, strategic approach with broad public and stakeholder consultation led to a series of coordinated legislative acts enabling the stepwise rollout of digital infrastructure, including electronic patient records (ePA), electronic prescribing (ePrescription), and a portfolio of forward-looking health data use laws (Fig. 2). Each act built upon the last, informed by stakeholder feedback, international best-practices and evolving technical capabilities, illustrating how legislation can be structured as an iterative process, incorporating new developments on national and European levels. The German Digitalization Strategy for Health and Care23, first published in 2023 and further developed and adapted to new developments in 202624, sets clear short-, middle-, and long-term measures to guide the legislative process and its implementation, e.g., through regular evaluation and reporting25.

Notably, this commitment to agility is increasingly visible and making inroads at the national level: Germany’s 2025 coalition agreement, which will guide the government’s policymaking priorities until 2029, explicitly promotes iterative development across healthcare, data governance, and public administration (Table 4). Policy mandates such as the 2025 rollout of the electronic patient record (ePA), the push for a culture of data use, and the modular modernization of welfare services all reflect an incentive structure designed to support staged implementation, adaptability, and cross-sectoral learning. Case Study 2. European Union’s Artificial Intelligence Act

At the EU level, the Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act)27 offers a recent example of how formal legislation can embed adaptive design and evolve alongside technological change. It incorporates review cycles and adaptive compliance mechanisms to ensure laws evolve alongside rapid AI advancements (Box 2). While not “agile” in the software development sense, the EU AI Act represents a partial and constrained example of adaptive legislative design; its update mechanisms create the potential for learning and revision, but do not in themselves guarantee agility in practice.

Legal availability is necessary but not sufficient for impact: whether these formal mechanisms translate into meaningful agility in practice depends on how frequently, transparently, and responsively they are used by institutions over time. This adaptive capacity is unevenly distributed across the AI Act’s internal structure. Core obligations and risk classifications are established in EU law through Articles and Recitals, while technical specifications and use-case categorizations are largely concentrated in the Annexes. Crucially, EU Acts are designed from the outset to permit updates through Delegated and Implementing Acts, enabling modifications to technical elements, conformity assessment procedures, and risk categorizations without reopening the full legislative process. These acts are proposed by the European Commission but require scrutiny and feedback from advisory bodies like the AI Board, which includes national representatives, and other specialized expert groups. This architecture reflects a deliberate separation between “relatively stable normative commitments” and “more dynamic technical components,” creating formal pathways for adaptation while preserving legal certainty. It also ensures that the regulatory framework remains adaptable and responsive to fast-paced developments in AI while maintaining a robust, consensus-driven governance structure.

Using these instruments, the European Union’s AI Act incorporates iterative updates to adapt to rapidly evolving technology. Standardization and stakeholder involvement play a central role. Harmonized European standards, guidance documents, and evolving interpretations of the “state of the art” function as soft-law instruments that translate high-level legal requirements into actionable technical expectations. While formally non-binding, these instruments carry significant practical force by shaping conformity assessments, enforcement priorities, and liability exposure. Their iterative development outside the legislative cycle allows regulatory expectations to evolve alongside technological practice, but also shifts substantial normative influence toward standard-setting bodies and enforcement authorities. As such, agility in the AI Act is realized not solely through statutory mechanisms, but through the dynamic interaction between hard law, standards, guidance, and supervisory practice.

In an agile framework for healthcare, legislation could be designed to include provisional approval processes, such as (1) conditional clearance for innovative medical devices pending post-market evaluation (akin to pathways already established for drugs)28; (2) structured feedback loops with stakeholders, like regular consultations with healthcare providers and patients to refine regulatory guidelines; and (3) mechanisms for rapid amendments, such as expedited rule changes in response to emerging evidence on drug efficacy. For instance, after initial assurance, AI diagnostic tools could be deployed under a provisional framework with mandatory post-market evaluations to ensure safety29,30. This would, however, require new regulatory oversight mechanisms balancing faster access to innovation with early regulatory compliance in supervised settings. GenAI tools, undergoing several years of regulatory scrutiny, will already be outdated by the time they are placed on the market—indeed, this may be the case after only a few months. Now it is incumbent upon European policymakers to take advantage of this flexibility; only with legislative innovation will the AI Act and the new simplified rules on medical and in vitro diagnostic devices31 evolve into a portfolio of approaches that encourage and sustain AI-driven innovation in the EU.

Case study 3. The US reauthorization model for regulatory user fees

In the United States, adaptive capacity in regulatory governance often operates through administrative law and agency rulemaking rather than through frequent statutory revision, with formal and informal rulemaking processes under the Administrative Procedure Act providing structured mechanisms for updating regulatory requirements across sectors.

Debates on regulatory flexibility in digital and healthcare governance have largely emerged from administrative law scholarship, emphasizing agency discretion, post-market oversight, and iterative rulemaking rather than statutory redesign29,32. For example, the Prescription Drug User Fee Act (PDUFA) and the Medical Device User Fee Amendments (MDUFA) offer a noteworthy exception33. These laws authorize the FDA to collect user fees from industry and require reauthorization by Congress every 5 years, creating a built-in cycle of stakeholder negotiation, performance review, and policy recalibration (Box 3). While their scope is limited, PDUFA and MDUFA demonstrate how structured reauthorization mechanisms can embed iterative policymaking into otherwise stable federal statutory frameworks governing healthcare product regulation, providing a modest but concrete model for learning-oriented governance within the US system.

These examples and legislative models reflect a shift from static, one-time lawmaking toward modular, adaptive legal architectures. Even emergency legal frameworks, like those enacted during the COVID-19 pandemic, offer lessons in responsive lawmaking, where temporary legal authorizations (e.g., for telemedicine, remote data collection in clinical trials, or diagnostic testing) served as real-world pilots for more permanent statutes.

These cases are not presented as fully realized models or comparative benchmarks, but as diagnostic illustrations of how adaptive design elements can be operationalized within existing legal systems. These cases are used diagnostically to examine how different legal systems operationalize specific design elements (e.g., staged implementation, delegated updating authority, structured review cycles, and reauthorization mechanisms) within otherwise conventional lawmaking processes. In this sense, agility is treated as a relative and context-dependent property, assessed against prevailing legislative norms within each system rather than as an absolute or comparative benchmark.

The chosen examples underscore a more limited but important insight: legislative frameworks can be designed to incorporate structured opportunities for revision informed by evidence and implementation experience, while preserving legal stability, prospective application, and procedural safeguards. Such approaches do not imply constant legal change, retroactive enforcement, or the erosion of due process; rather, they emphasize clearly defined points at which reassessment and adjustment may occur within existing constitutional and administrative constraints. Importantly, the presence of adaptive elements in existing systems does not negate the relevance of more holistic agile approaches; rather, it suggests that such mechanisms are often fragmented, implicit, or inconsistently applied.

Judicial interpretation and liability regimes further shape the adaptive capacity of legislative frameworks. ‘Tort law’ and ‘case law’—with tort law governing civil liability for harm arising from departures from accepted standards of care, and case law comprising published judicial decisions that interpret statutes and regulations in concrete contexts–operate as ex post mechanisms that translate regulatory expectations into enforceable standards of care, influencing how risks are allocated and how compliance is interpreted in practice. Over time, judicial decisions can clarify ambiguities, consolidate emerging norms, and recalibrate incentives for innovation and risk management. These dynamics are particularly salient in healthcare and AI governance, where harm, causality, and responsibility are often contested34. From an agility perspective, courts do not undermine legal stability; rather, they form part of a broader learning system that incrementally refines the practical meaning and consequences of regulatory obligations.

A call for collaborative lawmaking and regulatory governance in real-world contexts

To successfully implement agile legislative models, cross-sector collaboration is essential. While stakeholder input has long been a feature of democratic lawmaking, crafting adaptive laws in complex fields such as healthcare increasingly requires input not only from lawmakers but also from researchers, clinicians, patients, technologists, industry leaders, and ethics bodies. The distinction emphasized here lies not in consultation per se, but in the formal integration of stakeholder input into post-implementation evaluation mechanisms and ongoing strategy development, rather than limiting engagement primarily to the pre-enactment phase.

Democratic control can be preserved through predefined legislative mandates, transparency obligations, public reporting, parliamentary review clauses, and the continued ability of legislatures to amend, revoke, or terminate delegated authorities. Stakeholder input, including dissenting views, patient advocacy perspectives, professional societies, and industry positions, is incorporated through formal consultation processes, calls for evidence, and publicly documented evaluations, collectively ensuring that iterative adjustment remains contestable, visible, and politically accountable.

Regulatory sandboxes as instruments of institutionalized adaptation

While collaborative lawmaking ensures that adaptation remains democratically grounded and politically accountable, it does not by itself specify how evidence about regulatory performance should be generated once laws are implemented. This gap between participatory design and empirical learning is where regulatory sandboxes can play a role.

The term “sandbox” has been used for a wide range of testing and pilot settings, spanning technical development environments, research testbeds, and informal innovation trials. Here, we use a narrower definition by focusing on regulatory sandboxes: legally constituted, time-limited frameworks that permit supervised deviation from standard rules to generate real-world evidence (under defined governance conditions). Within an agile legislative framework, such regulatory sandboxes are not an alternative to lawmaking, nor a stand-alone governance solution, but rather function as bounded, implementation-stage instruments that support institutionalized learning. The value lies in systematically generating empirical learnings and feeding these back into legislatively defined pathways for interpretation, adjustment, or revision.

Importantly, these sandboxes have been conceptualized in the pre-competitive and competitive space35. Initially popularized in financial technology, sandboxes are now featured in the EU AI Act (Article 57), the Proposal for a European Biotech Act (Articles 39 and 40)36, as well as the proposed simplifications to the EU rules on medical devices (new Articles 59b and 59c) and in vitro diagnostic medical devices (new Articles 54b and 54c)31. allowing supervised, real-world experimentation under defined legal conditions (Box 2)37. These legal boundaries remain stable for the duration of the sandbox, while allowing regulators to observe real-world performance and inform future regulatory adjustments. In healthcare, legislative sandboxes can explicitly authorize time-limited, supervised pilot programs for digital health tools, AI diagnostics, health biotechnology, or privacy-preserving data use under predefined legal conditions that go beyond ad hoc research exemptions. While elements of such experimentation are often possible through ethics approvals or derogations, sandboxes formalize scope, oversight, and evaluation criteria at the legislative level.

University clinics and other research-intensive healthcare settings are well-suited to serve as regulated testbeds in which defined cohorts, governance structures, and monitoring obligations allow lawmakers and regulators to systematically observe real-world performance, safety signals, and unintended consequences, and to adjust legal provisions based on empirical evidence rather than ex ante assumptions. The European Testing and Experimentation Facility in Health (TEF-Health) is piloting such an evidence-generating sandbox model by providing shared infrastructure, regulatory support, and access to real-world datasets enabling controlled evaluation of innovative health technologies before broader market authorization38. In this context, regulatory sandboxes are best understood as implementation-stage instruments that enable supervised experimentation and evidence generation within existing legal frameworks. Like agile legislation, regulatory sandboxes are not substitutes for democratic lawmaking but operate under its authority and constraints.

Importantly, not every challenge requires new legislation. Sometimes, the more effective path lies in clarifying or adapting existing regulations within a flexible legal mandate. Legislators already engage in assessing regulatory bottlenecks; the contribution emphasized here is the more systematic use of predefined decision pathways (e.g., targeted guidance, delegated authority, or review triggers) to address such bottlenecks without defaulting to full statutory revision. However, efforts to work within existing regulatory frameworks must also be grounded in statutory reality. The FDA’s Software Precertification (Pre-Cert) Program serves as a cautionary tale: while it was envisioned as a regulatory sandbox to support iterative evaluation of digital health tools, it ultimately ended abruptly because the agency lacked clear statutory authority to implement the program as intended. Notably, this failure does not undermine the sandbox concept itself. It illustrates our central claim: without clear legislative authorization and predefined feedback pathways, sandbox-like initiatives cannot reliably contribute to institutionalized regulatory learning. What is often lacking in practice is not participatory intent, but institutional clarity regarding when existing rules are no longer fit for purpose, how evidence from implementation is evaluated, and which actors are responsible for initiating recalibration.

A paradigm shift for democratic innovation

What happens when laws fail to keep pace with change? In many cases, rigid legislative frameworks, however well-intentioned, create delays, inefficiencies, and unintended barriers to progress. Outdated statutes governing digital health, for instance, can prevent timely access to life-saving tools, impede data-sharing for public health responses, and limit innovation in diagnostics or personalized care. This is not merely a regulatory problem; it is a legislative one. Imagine instead a legal architecture designed to evolve in (near) real time, enabling provisional laws, iterative updates, and embedded mechanisms for revision. Agile legislation offers just that: a shift from static rulemaking toward dynamic, feedback-driven legal systems, and the built-in expectation of revision and improvement over time.

Agile legislation is therefore not a property of individual statutes, but an emergent feature of a multi-layered governance ecosystem spanning legislation, delegated rulemaking, standards, enforcement practice, and judicial review. In this model, legislatures retain exclusive authority over normative objectives and accountability structures, while delegated actors operate within clearly circumscribed mandates subject to political oversight and judicial review.

A common concern is that learning-oriented or iterative lawmaking risks devolving into a technocratic system in which expert-driven processes displace democratic deliberation. This manuscript does not advocate for an autonomous or automatic “technocratic law machine.” Rather, it argues for a recalibration of the division of labor between legislatures and expert institutions, in which democratic bodies set the normative goals, boundaries, and conditions of experimentation, while technical actors generate evidence within those constraints. Agility, in this sense, does not diminish parliamentary authority; it renders the exercise of that authority more informed, transparent, and responsive over time.

Realizing this vision requires more than procedural reform. It demands political will, sustained cross-sector collaboration, and a mindset shift: treating change as an opportunity and a responsibility for lawmakers and regulatory authorities, not a threat. While full legal harmonization across borders may remain elusive, enabling frameworks like the European Health Data Space (EHDS) show that legislative alignment need not be perfect to be powerful. The EHDS provides the scaffolding for a federated, trustworthy, data-driven healthcare ecosystem; one that evolves alongside innovation and empowers responsible access across the EU. The EHDS serves as a pioneering model for the upcoming European Data Union39, demonstrating how secure, interoperable data-sharing frameworks can empower cross-sector collaboration and innovation, while ensuring privacy and ethical standards; its success in health data governance offers valuable insights for the development of other data spaces across Europe (Supplementary Data 2).

Agile legislation is therefore not a call for entirely new forms of lawmaking, but for the consolidation, clarification, and more deliberate integration and use of existing learning-oriented mechanisms within current legal systems. In practical terms, learning-oriented laws are characterized by explicit evaluation criteria, mechanisms for evidence collection during implementation, and predefined processes for revision or guidance updates. While perfection may be unattainable, legal frameworks that incorporate these features can adapt with greater speed, integrity, and institutional accountability.
