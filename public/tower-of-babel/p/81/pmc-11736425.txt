Error mitigation in forensic handwriting examination: the examiner’s perspective
Authors: Nicole Crown, Raymond Marquis, Erich Kupferschmid, Tomasz Dziedzic, Diana Belic, Dorijan Kerzan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLike other pattern recognition disciplines, forensic handwriting examination relies on various human factors. Expert opinions in the field are based on visual analysis and comparison, and the evaluation of findings is generally conducted without reference to tabulated data. This high level of subjectivity may contribute to bias and error in the examination process. In this paper, we draw on our research and practical experience to discuss error mitigation on several levels, addressing both aspects of quality management and the individual responsibility of examiners. Because a good understanding of the concept of error is needed to communicate appropriately about this subject, definitions of error-related concepts are provided. We consider contextual information management essential to reduce the potential for cognitive bias in casework. To ensure completeness of findings and avoid omission errors, the use of checklists during a forensic handwriting examination is encouraged, and an exemplary checklist incorporating all the examination steps is provided. We consider the use of a logical reasoning approach to evaluate findings an important step towards robustness and transparency in the examiner’s report. An independent, blinded peer review of the examination is recommended as a further key step in error mitigation. Regular participation in testing programmes and continuous training and education are essential to maintaining and improving competency at both individual and organizational levels. Finally, developments in the form of tabulated data and the use of algorithms are considered useful ways of increasing objectivity in the field and minimizing human error.

Introduction

The Best Practice Manual for the Forensic Examination of Handwriting [1] recommend the use of a four-step process of analysis, comparison, evaluation, and verification (ACE-V). The initial stage of this process is the analysis of handwriting features observed in both the questioned and known writing samples. This is followed by a comparison of these features to determine if they are similar or different and a subsequent evaluation of the findings. The final stage of the process comprises the verification of the examination conclusions, typically by a second expert [2]. Each of these steps relies on various human factors because expert opinions in the field are mainly based on visual analysis and comparison, and evaluations are generally conducted without reference to tabulated data (such as frequencies of handwriting features). The importance of the human factors involved in this process makes it particularly prone to bias and therefore error. Generally, error is defined as “an act, assertion, or belief that unintentionally deviates from what is correct, right or true; the condition of having incorrect or false knowledge; the act or an instance of deviating from an accepted code of behaviour, or a mistake” [3].

In recent years, the discipline of forensic handwriting examination has been criticized for its lack of supporting empirical studies and validation. Several sources have highlighted a lack of knowledge of errors in forensic handwriting examination and have pointed out the need to document the accuracy of expert opinions [4–6]. These sources emphasize the importance of reporting data on error, even if they do not specify how such data should be collected and represented. Although the concept of error can have different meanings in different contexts (it may denote, for example, error rates, error sources, and risk of error in a case given its specifics), the term generally refers to how often expert opinions do not agree with the ground truth or the likelihood of expressing a misleading opinion in a given case.

To date, several papers have published forensic handwriting examination error rates. In an Australian study, Wilson-Wilde et al. [7] reported error rates in Collaborative Testing Services proficiency tests (PTs) conducted between 2005 and 2017 in different forensic disciplines. They conclude that error exists, even though the tests were not blind (the practitioners knew they were being tested) and the expert opinions were reviewed according to accredited procedures. A recent Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences report [8] summarized the findings of a 5-year study designed to assess the accuracy and reliability of forensic handwriting comparison decisions. Such assessments are important in establishing the scientific validity of the discipline and thus the admissibility of forensic handwriting evidence in court. The researchers report that “the performance of the participants varied wildly in terms of rates of error and correct conclusions” and concluded that “error rate studies cannot be assumed to apply to any particular examiner or any particular sample” [8]. Found and Bird [9] state that “All validation studies to date have shown that examiners [sic] responses on blind trials vary, and can vary widely, particularly in terms of individuals’ correct and inconclusive scores. Therefore, the results from one group of examiners or an individual examiner may not be a good estimate of the potential results of an unrelated group or individual despite these examiners using the same resource materials, being the product of similar training regimes and even using similar methodology. As a human skill this is not entirely unexpected”. The assessment of error is even more complex because error not only is an attribute of expert conclusions but also may occur throughout the examination process. That is, error can be considered an attribute of a discipline, an expert (or group of experts), a laboratory, an instrument, a method, a result, and a conclusion (or of the reporting thereof). Furthermore, error can be an attribute of the materials being examined as their characteristics influence the difficulty of the case and may attract different levels of error. In error causation models, error is assumed to be the result of a chain of events in an organization or laboratory, rather than the isolated action of a particular individual [4]. It is therefore important to establish quality systems on an organizational level to ensure best practices and mitigate error through the development of job guidelines, comprehensive descriptions of methodology, and operational processes. This multilevel approach has been described by using the metaphor of the Swiss Cheese Model1. Although system-level thinking is very important, we believe that individual responsibility should not be neglected.

Considering the high degree of subjectivity in handwriting examination and in line with a multilevel approach to error mitigation, we discuss the essential requirements of quality management at the organizational level (contextual information management, independent peer review, improvement of competency) and provide tools that individual examiners could use (error concept definitions, casework checklist) in daily casework or court hearing preparation. The mitigation tools described here follow the different steps of the examination process. We further address the importance of evaluative reporting, testing procedures as competency benchmarking and potential strategies for increasing the objectivity of forensic handwriting examination in the future.

Error concepts

In court, forensic handwriting examiners (FHEs) may have to answer various questions related to an error, such as: What is the level of certainty of the conclusion (as a percentage)? Is the analysis reliable? Is the method valid? What is the error margin of the evaluation? What is the risk of incorrect results? In addressing such questions, the examiner must refer to many interrelated concepts that are associated with different aspects of error. These concepts are often derived from statistics and may be confusing for examiners who do not have the relevant background knowledge.

Accurate and appropriate communication about error requires the following: clarity (i.e. FHEs must understand the concepts they are talking about), usefulness (recipients of information should be able to understand the way error is addressed), transparency (FHEs should explain what they know and what they do not know), and logic (communication should conform to the European Network of Forensic Science Institutes (ENFSI) guideline on evaluative reporting) [10]. Regarding clarity, it is important that error-related terms are adequately defined and understood. Contextual information management

The examiner relies on various personal factors, such as mood, personal experience, and context [11], which may substantially increase the risk of cognitive bias in decision-making and thus lead to errors. Cognitive bias is driven by “preexisting beliefs, expectations, motives or situational context” [12] and is very difficult to fully control because cognitive processes often occur “without conscious awareness” [11]. Therefore, it is likely that examiners are unaware of what effect contextual information—in the broadest meaning of the term—has on their decision-making and on the analysis itself, which highlights the importance and difficulty of implementing mitigation strategies.

While conducting the examination, the practitioner may be exposed to a wide range of task-relevant or task-irrelevant information. Although there are discussions in the literature about how task-irrelevant information can influence cognitive behaviour and examiners’ decisions [13, 14], it is worth noting that even task-relevant information can influence the cognitive process. Although the question of how task-relevant information can be problematic is beyond the scope of this paper, it is important to identify possible solutions for minimizing the influence of task-irrelevant information2. Gardner et al. [13] defined task-irrelevant information as material that “does not inform the specific proposition in question or informs this question through any means other than an appropriate analysis of the physical evidence in the analyst’s domain of expertise”. It is difficult to explicitly define the main sources of task- or domain-irrelevant information as they can be case specific, but the sources highlighted include the examination context, documentation, and communications with the mandating authority [15].

The influence of task-irrelevant data or context may be mitigated successfully using systems of context data management and triage (of data) and by adhering to certain examination standards. The latter includes the recommendation to examine questioned material before the reference material [16] and the use of line-ups for reference material [15]3. Dror and Kukucka [17] have proposed an improved context management system, called Linear Sequential Unmasking–Expanded, which would enable forensic laboratories to limit the influence of context on forensic science decisions. The system incorporates a scoring option to define biasing power, subjectivity, and relevance of a particular piece of information that may compromise the examiner’s objectivity and impartiality. It is generally agreed that information about the suspect and the victim should be minimized, as well as information about the type of crime committed2. Similarly, statements from the suspect (or the victim and witnesses) are usually unnecessary for forensic analysis. Another aspect of background information that can influence decision-making is exposure to the results of other forensic analyses.

Managing contextual information in forensic practice is extremely important for avoiding bias-driven errors, but it is certainly not sufficient. Cognitive training of forensic practitioners is also needed [18] to ensure that they first understand the risk of contextual information and then learn strategies to handle it. In a completely different environment, the concept of learned ignorance was developed in the 15th century by Nicholas of Cusa [19]. Learned ignorance in a strict sense of the words could be a way to teach experts to avoid unnecessary and potentially biasing information. Similarly, voluntary ignorance can be a tool to reduce contextual bias and gain objectivity (however, this does not mean that the examiner should ignore facts contradicting their beliefs, which would result in a confirmation bias). Forensic decision-making is a very good example of the need to voluntarily ignore context, circumstances, and other peculiarities of a case [20]. Cognitive training for voluntary ignorance or context independence in the decision process of a forensic scientist is thus an important tool for objective reasoning.

Checklists: reducing and avoiding omission errors

Comprehensive training and education programmes are insufficient to ensure that error is avoided. This approach fails to address two of the main factors that reduce human efficiency: memory and concentration [21]. In this respect, the use of checklists has been documented and proven to be efficient to reduce error and improve performance in other disciplines, such as aviation and medicine [22–24]. Therefore, the use of checklists must be encouraged in forensic science as well, not only to overcome memory limitations but also to strengthen compliance with guidelines, limit bias of human judgement, and promote standardization.

Checklists can help FHEs avoid lapses of concentration, help them remember tasks, and facilitate the comprehensive documentation of these tasks, thus reducing omission errors. Although they are typically not exhaustive, checklists may also prompt FHEs to ask relevant questions or execute the correct subtasks. During a case, checklists may help FHEs to follow the different steps of the examination process, but they can also be referred to before accepting a case (while discussing the case with a client), after a case has been reported (to prepare for a hearing), to help identify knowledge gaps and choose appropriate continuing education, to develop arguments, to provide a critique of another examiner’s report or to defend their own report, to prepare for an accreditation exam, or even simply to refresh their memory.

We present a fit-for-purpose checklist for casework in the supplementary material (Supplementary Table S2). The checklist is based on the note-taking form from the Best Practice Manual for Forensic Handwriting Examination [1] and includes information (mainly in the form of questions) that may assist FHEs in appropriately completing each examination task. In the checklist, special emphasis has been given to the peer review process, which is considered particularly important for minimizing error, and which will be addressed more thoroughly in the following sections.

Evaluative reporting

Despite being routinely used in some forensic disciplines, evaluative reporting remains scarcely applied in forensic handwriting examination. This could be due to a variety of reasons such as the policy of the lab, lack of familiarity with probability concepts, and lack of practical step-by-step guidelines on how to implement the approach in casework as well as on how to communicate the results in such a way that they can be clearly understood. However, we consider the evaluative or logical approach to evidence assessment an important tool for a more objective discussion of the examination findings. This approach considers the evaluation of evidence and the expression of expert opinions using a likelihood ratio (LR). This is the ratio of the probabilities of the observed findings in a specific case under at least two competing propositions, within a framework of circumstances. In the case of forensic handwriting examination, the findings are typically the similarities and differences observed between a questioned sample and the reference material. The assessed values are usually of subjective nature but can be supported by data, if available for a particular case scenario. The propositions are derived from the examination request or from the different scenarios presented by the parties, for example, the defence and the prosecution in a criminal case. Evaluative reporting is a valuable tool for increasing adherence to the requirements of balance, logic, robustness, and transparency in forensic reporting [10]. This approach offers several significant benefits for the examination, such as the clarification of the expert’s role in the legal process and the ability to structure the discussion of the findings in a clear and objective manner. Evaluative reporting also provides the possibility of logically combining different types of evidence. Moreover, this approach offers the awareness and the potential to avoid reasoning fallacies.

In the first step of evaluative reporting, competing propositions must be formulated (if not already formulated in the request). These propositions should be mutually exclusive and cover all possibilities as defined in the request. In the second step, the examiner is required to evaluate the plausibility of their observations under the stated propositions. This process helps to structure the expert’s reasoning and avoid arbitrary opinions because probabilities should be informed by justifiable knowledge, whether based on published data or personal experience. This process clarifies the examiner’s reasoning to the judiciary. Any factors that may impact the assessment, such as assumed writing conditions, must be stated and discussed.

Moreover, the logical approach to evidence assessment allows for an update of the examiner’s opinion if new information is disclosed in the case. Such information may refer to purported writing conditions, the health of the comparison writer, or information about the abilities of the suspected forger, among others. This information may influence the probability of the findings under one or both propositions. Because probabilities depend on “what we know, what we are told and what we assume” [25], it is logical that their values can change if the information that is relevant to the evaluation changes.

Although important for transparent and logical reporting, the logical approach to evidence assessment does not replace the need for quantitative data and the implementation of objective models in sufficiently large populations of reference. Without such supporting data, the probabilities of the findings expressed by the examiner remain entirely dependent on human perception, knowledge, and experience and may be prone to personal biases, especially in more challenging or unfamiliar domains of expertise. As stated by Edmond et al. [11], human perception is flawed, and experience does not necessarily translate into expertise and greater accuracy, unless practitioners are provided with continuous training involving domain-specific tasks. Although we do not advocate the use of numerical LRs only, we believe that databases that permit the attribution of numerical LR expressions in forensic handwriting analysis may help to justify the evaluation of the strength of evidence in a case, improve consistency within and between experts, and avoid personal biases.

Independent peer review

According to the ISO 17025:2017 international standards, based on which many forensic handwriting laboratories are accredited, examination results should be reviewed before they are released. The most effective way of meeting this requirement is by conducting a peer review, a process in which experts in a field evaluate their colleagues’ work. As discussed in the Introduction, in pattern recognition-based forensic disciplines such as handwriting examination, the ACE-V framework is widely used, and peer review is considered the verification stage of the process [26]. Because the term “peer review” originated in academia and has been used to describe the process of assessing the quality, originality, and rigour of research papers and grant proposals, some researchers do not support its use in the context of case reporting. Although such researchers prefer the term “case review”, they still consider the verification step an important part of the quality management system of a forensic laboratory [4].

There are several different types of peer review, three of which are relevant for forensic casework: administrative review, technical review, and re-examination. The administrative review mainly focuses on checking whether formal requirements have been met, including adherence to protocols, completeness of documentation, and adequacy of language in the report. It therefore does not require expertise in the discipline, only a good understanding of the laboratory’s processes and quality system. In contrast, a technical review must be conducted by an expert in the field because it confirms that adequate examination steps have been carried out to address the current issue and that the conclusions are well supported by the findings (as documented in, e.g. notes, figures, calculations). Re-examination involves a complete repetition of the three initial examination stages by another examiner, effectively creating an ACE–ACE procedure. The second examiner should therefore have access to all the material, both questioned and known, on which the original examination was conducted and to the same contextual information to be able to reach their own conclusions. At an administrative and technical level of review, the verifier always knows the results obtained by the original examiner. However, in a re-examination, the situation is more complex. Granting the second examiner access to such additional information can undoubtedly make the peer review more time efficient. However, the results obtained by the original examiner provide contextual information that may bias the verifier and lead to confirmation bias, which is the tendency to favour information that confirms and aligns with pre-existing beliefs, views, and expectations, while underestimating information that contradicts them. For FHEs, this may lead to unconsciously overlooking information contradicting the opinion of the first examiner, regardless of whether the verifier strictly adheres to examination protocols. It is important to emphasize that being aware of this risk does not prevent the examiner from being subject to it. Therefore, the easiest and most effective way to prevent confirmation bias is by blinding the verification step with the introduction of a fully independent peer review, in which the verifier is unaware of the initial examiner’s results and refrains from discussing any aspects of the case before the review is complete. However, independent verification does not completely eliminate bias. As has been shown experimentally, forensic scientists’ decisions may also be influenced by the expectation that their work will be verified, as well as by the nature of their role in the process, either as an initial investigator or as a reviewer [11, 26].

An important stage of independent peer review is the discussion of the results obtained by the examiners involved, with the aim of reaching a consensus conclusion. The simplest situation is when the examiners fully agree on which conclusion level to use. However, because there is a considerable margin of subjectivity in the interpretation of forensic handwriting examination results, discrepancies in the strength of the conclusions are not uncommon. In the event of a disparity larger than a predefined threshold, the issue should be resolved according to the protocol of the laboratory’s quality management system. An example of a procedure that can be used in laboratories that use evaluative reporting has been proposed by Montani et al. [27]. According to this approach, opposing experts must start by reaching an agreement on the findings, that is, ensuring that the evaluation of the similarities and differences of the handwriting features are the same. In the next step, the experts must identify the differences in the evidentiary strength they attribute to these findings. This requires tracing the numerators and denominators in the LR calculations they each made to assess the probabilities of the findings under competing hypotheses. If differences in these piecemeal decisions are identified, they are discussed in a scientific way, using relevant literature, conference proceedings, databases, and other sources. The aim of such discussion is to identify areas where flexibility is possible from either or both examiners, which can narrow the discrepancy between their conclusions to below the threshold set. If, despite this discussion, a consensus is not reached that is within the threshold, it is recommended that a third examiner is consulted. This third examiner may support the position of the first examiner or the verifier or may express a third position or advocate for compromise. If this intervention also fails to resolve the disagreement, both conclusions and their respective arguments should be included in the case report [27]. It should be emphasized that a discussion aimed at reaching a common position will be greatly facilitated if the examiners’ detailed case notes are used. For robustness and transparency, it is also essential that the entire review process, including the discussion, is documented.

Testing as a competency benchmark

In real-life casework, the ground truth is unknown to the examiner, so erroneous or misleading opinions are difficult to identify. Ground truth testing is therefore an important part of error reduction and skill improvement. Testing is useful not only to detect erroneous opinions but also to identify error sources. Of the many potential error sources that can affect expert opinions, Beck [28] reported on three sources that seem predominant: failure to properly evaluate differences, failure to detect significant movement characteristics, and the use of self-serving exemplars. Such error sources or subtle personal biases (which are a result of so-called “metacognitive myopia” or failure to identify one’s own limitations) may also be avoided or mitigated if FHEs submit to rigorous and systematic ground truth testing. In the following, we address testing procedures, limitations, and the need for improvement in the discipline.

Proficiency tests and collaborative exercises

Two common types of tests must be distinguished: proficient tests (PTs) and collaborative exercises (CEs). In forensic handwriting examination, both types of tests are available, and laboratories may use them almost interchangeably. However, PTs and CEs do not share the same goal.

According to the guidance on the conduct of PTs and CEs within ENFSI [29], PTs are designed to evaluate participants’ performance against pre-established criteria using interlaboratory comparisons. In contrast, CEs are interlaboratory comparisons designed to address specific issues such as troubleshooting, method validation, or characterization of reference materials. CEs can be used to assist in the monitoring of laboratory performance, although they are not specifically designed for this purpose.

The above definitions do not specify whether the participants are individual examiners or organizations. This may depend on the quality assurance processes in place in particular organizations. However, PTs are generally designed to test the accuracy of a system, rather than the competence of a practitioner or the accuracy of a method [30]. If PTs are used to test whether comprehensive efforts have been made to prevent errors from leaving the laboratory, they should be considered to reflect the performance of the laboratory rather than that of the individual examiners. Competency tests are used to monitor the performance of individual examiners. The use of competency tests, which are neither PTs nor CEs, is part of the training process to ensure that examiners have acquired specialized knowledge and appropriate skills before performing casework [6]. Establishing the competence of examiners is a requirement of accreditation and should be conducted by individual laboratories using specific materials and assessments, which may vary between laboratories.

PTs can be considered to focus mainly on the present time, with the aim of ensuring that the experts have achieved what they claim to have achieved. PTs are delivered by certified providers and are often used by laboratories as part of the accreditation process to demonstrate compliance. CEs can be considered as mainly future-oriented. They help to identify problem areas with the aim of improving skills and extending the boundaries of the area of expertise and can help to generate discussions between experts within a laboratory or between laboratories.

Both types of tests should include realistic samples that reflect the quantity and quality of casework, in order to be considered useful for practicing FHEs. In both types of tests, the feedback provided to participants once the test is completed is very important. However, because the tests have different aims, they differ in the content of their feedback. PT feedback is evaluative, in the sense that it provides information about whether the participant’s answers agree with the predefined answers. In CEs, the expected answers do not necessarily need to be predefined, so the feedback can be considered informative rather than evaluative.

As Koehler [31] points out, the assessment of error rates in forensic handwriting examination is one of the many functions of PTs (other functions include training personnel, promoting baseline competency levels, improving laboratory practices and procedures, and identifying future needs for a laboratory or technique). Error rates can be assessed by calculating the proportion of answers falling outside the range of expected answers, including false-positive, false-negative, and inconclusive rates. Although such measures may indicate the approximate performance of the participants, the findings cannot be directly transferred to casework performance because many of the test conditions are very different to those of casework. For example, examiners may be required to perform many tests for a single PT or CE within a short period of time, to work with digitized instead of original handwriting and signatures, to work on screen without completing the usual documentation, and to make decisions (often forced calls) based on a limited number of known samples. Nevertheless, error rates remain an important source of hypothesis-testing validation for the field.

Finally, positive and negative outcomes from PTs and CEs can be used to construct a personal validation portfolio in the field, as suggested by Kirchübel et al. [32, 33] for forensic voice comparison.

To date, various tests used in forensic handwriting examination have been offered by expert organizations, such as the European Network of Forensic Handwriting Experts (ENFHEX), the German Gesellschaft für Forensische Schriftuntersuchung (GFS, Society for Forensic Handwriting Examination), and the German Bundeskriminalamt (BKA, Federal Criminal Police Office) or by certified test providers such as the Collaborative Testing Services. In the CERTAIN-FORS survey, the majority of the participants reported taking part in at least one test a year (Figure 1).

Yearly participation in testing procedures.

Current tests in the discipline generally cover only some aspects of casework, with single examples (e.g. in terms of variation, contemporaneity, document type, and possible contamination with samples from different authors) tested under the same conditions. Large-scale testing as used during the La Trobe trials4 is seldom undertaken, probably because of logistical difficulties. Such testing procedures can be used to identify problematic and challenging aspects of the discipline. An analysis of the CEs conducted by ENFHEX from 2010 to 2019 showed that tests involving disguised samples generated higher rates of misleading and inconclusive opinions than other types of tests. This observation is consistent with the La Trobe trials [34], which showed that FHEs were proficient in differentiating natural and unnatural writing behaviour but performed worse when differentiating disguised and simulated samples, both of which are associated with unnatural writing behaviours [35, 36].

To address the diversity of the examination claims and to test the examiners’ performance on the range of questions asked in casework, broad testing procedures should be introduced for texts and signatures of different complexities, writing styles, and system differences in the quantity, quality, and variation of the reference material tested under the same conditions; for both original and photocopied documents; for less common areas of the discipline, such as samples written under unusual writing conditions; and for classifying a questioned signature within a given period of time, as well as other conditions5. As recommended by the Expert Working Group for Human Factors in Handwriting Examination [4], such tests can be developed in collaboration between examiners, researchers, and accreditation bodies.

In the evaluation of test results, participants’ responses in the current tests are often collapsed into binary outcomes; they may be classified as correct if they agree with the ground truth or as incorrect if they do not. The strength of the conclusion (which is central in casework because it reflects the measure of uncertainty of the evidence in the expert’s opinion) is usually discarded. Of course, this binary classification of participants’ answers facilitates test preparation because there is no need to define a range of possible responses. However, it also prevents a detailed analysis of the experts’ opinions, which could be useful in calculating meaningful error rates. According to Evett et al. [37], “proficiency testing is far more than the counting of errors” and “forensic interpretation is far richer and more informative than simple yes/no answers”.

Before dissemination, tests are usually sent to pretesters, who evaluate their feasibility and difficulty. However, it remains uncertain whether the opinions of pretesters are reliable and accurate, especially if they are used to determine the range of expected results for PT purposes. The opinions of the pretesters alone may be no more accurate than the opinions of their peers. Additionally, the pretesters’ answers may differ in strength from the opinions of most of the participants, which would lead to high error rates (if error rates were calculated from such test results).

In the CERTAIN-FORS project, PTs of the differentiation of genuine and simulated signatures were developed by a team of FHEs6. The development of ranges of expected or acceptable conclusions was based on team consensus, taking into account the conclusions of the pretesters, together with various other variables such as the ground truth, signature complexity, the extent of variability between known samples, the estimated simulation difficulty, and the comparison findings. Instead of focusing only on the ground truth, emphasis was placed on the quality of the questioned sample and the reference material, as well as on features that affect the strength of the evidence. Although still subjective, this process offers higher transparency and precision in defining expected ranges than procedures based on pretesting only and may contribute to a more robust competency assessment process.

Development of a positive error culture

Errors in annual or infrequent tests may occur for a variety of reasons, such as unfamiliarity with the current task (i.e. insufficient experience with a certain type of signature or handwriting), different than expected assessment of the limitations of the material, different features noted in the analysis, and different evaluation of the similarities and differences noted during the comparison phase. Furthermore, some tests may be conducted without following protocols or without the kind of attention that is required in real cases, owing to workload or time pressures. It can also happen that the case scenarios may be too ambitious and not appropriate for testing. If errors are incorrectly interpreted as a lack of competency and severely punished (which seems to be the case in some laboratories, based on the CERTAIN-FORS survey, Figure 2), participants may become more cautious in their opinions when tested and may tend to provide more inconclusive answers to avoid such consequences. Overcautious answer behaviour, while understandable, may not accurately represent the opinions given in casework and therefore be of limited value for competency assessment [38].

Consequences of incorrect results achieved in testing procedures.

A positive error culture may be a powerful improvement tool on both practitioner and laboratory levels [4]. Error in a testing environment can be interpreted as a learning opportunity, which helps to identify and mitigate possible weaker areas of expertise. In this regard, we fully agree with Evett et al. [37]: “whatever the outcome [of the test], the study must be seen as a learning exercise for all participants: the pool of knowledge has grown” and “the science moves on as a result of proficiency tests”.

Continuous improvement

Finally, the importance of maintaining competency and strengthening the skills of the FHE community through continuous training and collaboration should be emphasized. Examiners should keep up with new publications in the field relating to novel subjects, such as new technological solutions, artificial intelligence, or statistics. Steps towards greater objectivity using data

A common assumption is that objectivity involves freedom from prejudice and bias [39], and an objective view should be based on facts and not influenced by personal beliefs or feelings. Thus, having access to objective, case-specific data sources can improve the information on which personal assessments are based. Improving this basis may also help to assess numerical probabilities to calculate the evidential strength of the findings of an examination, thereby improving the precision of the examiner’s conclusion. Such data may fall into one of the following categories:

Information about the frequency of handwriting characteristics, usually specific within a certain population of reference

Knowledge of the frequency of the observed handwriting characteristics (i.e. the occurrence of a particular letter shape in a defined reference population) can help the examiner assess the probability of the findings under different source propositions because it allows an estimation of the probability of a random match between two writers. It is important to note that many handwriting characteristics are not independent of each other. Therefore, the frequency of the combination of several similar letter shapes, for example, is not the product of the single frequencies of each letter shape; usually, it is substantially larger. Several studies have been conducted on the evaluation of single-letter shapes [40–42] and of full catalogues of allographs [43–45]. Laboratories are encouraged to collect similar data for the writer population(s) that they are required to examine most frequently. Information about foreign handwriting that is less familiar to the examiner can be obtained on a case-by-case basis by referring to collections or databases of handwriting samples. One example of such a collection is the International Handwriting Information System, which is available to ENFHEX members and consists of copybook models and handwriting samples from various countries worldwide.

Use of quantifying models

Examples of quantifying models are the complexity models suggested by Found et al. [46] and Alewijnse et al. [47]. Complexity is one of the parameters intrinsic to the examined signature and influences the probability of the findings when considering that the questioned signature was simulated. The use of models to render complexity assessment more objective may improve the reproducibility and validity of the evaluation of the findings. Additional models for quantifying other parameters, such as variation, may be useful in making the method more objective.

Implementation of automatic handwriting comparison methods

To date, automatic handwriting comparison methods have almost exclusively been used for research purposes or database searches. Implementation of such methods for evaluation in casework is associated with several caveats. Many methods only work with digitally captured handwriting, may require the collection of a large number of known handwriting samples, may require a set of simulated signatures, or may necessitate the use of a corpus of reference handwriting samples from the relevant population.

Comparison algorithms can be used to examine specific handwriting features, such as shape, velocity, and writing pressure, in digitally captured signatures. These features have been studied using dynamic time warping by Linden et al. [48] and Kupferschmid [49] to obtain LRs for questioned signatures.

Other methods, such as those described by Crawford et al. [50], are text independent and can be used with offline text entries (e.g. scans from conventional handwriting). Overviews of comparison methods and recent research can be found, e.g. in Christlein [51] and in Ruiz-Parrado et al. [52]. The main advantage of using automatic handwriting comparison in research is that large numbers of samples can easily be evaluated, producing results (especially estimates of LRs) that can assist examiners in casework examination of analogous writing.

With the emergence of artificial intelligence and machine learning, more practical methods for automatic handwriting comparison may soon be accessible for use in casework examinations. Such methods should not be seen as eventually replacing human examiners but should rather be welcomed as opportunities to provide more support to examiners and ultimately make handwriting comparison more objective and reproducible. The introduction to casework generates new challenges, most importantly the need for the validation of such comparison systems, which usually requires a large and representative validation corpus of handwriting samples of writers from a specific country or region, specific writing conditions, and (depending on the use case) samples of simulation attempts.

Conclusion

Error mitigation should be addressed on several levels that target the examiner, the examination methods and procedures, the examination results, and the organization. Knowledge of error concepts, contextual information management, checklists, evaluative reporting of the findings, independent peer review, testing, and competency improvement all represent protective factors against erroneous conclusions. A single layer of defence, however, is rarely perfect, and, therefore, we refer again to the metaphor of the Swiss Cheese Model. Like a piece of Emmental cheese, each “slice” has holes, representing vulnerabilities in each layer of the system. However, as every successive slice is added to the stack, covering the previous slice, the system becomes stronger as the likelihood that emerging risks will be blocked increases. In this context, all the tools and approaches we discuss here should be implemented in combination to construct a strong defence against errors in casework. However, the discipline of forensic handwriting examination remains based on human perception and prone to bias. To minimize error as much as possible, developments in the form of tabulated data and the use of algorithms should be considered the way forward to achieve better objectivity in the field.
