Indirubin regulates M1/M2 polarization and inhibits ferroptosis in dextran sulfate sodium induced colitis and in cultured THP-1 cells
Authors: Yong-xiang Huang, Jin-ru Zhang, Shi-hua Fu, Zhe Liu, Hui-jun Zhong, Qing-yang Zhong, Yin-di Luo, Ying-jie Hu, Hai-peng Zhu, Bin Wen, Song Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextMacrophages play a critical role in the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis (UC). Indirubin (IDR), a natural ligand of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), has been shown to ameliorate DSS-induced colitis in our previous study (Liu Z et al.).ObjectiveTo investigate whether IDR exerts its protective effects by regulating M1/M2 polarization and inhibiting ferroptosis in macrophages.Materials and MethodsImmunohistochemistry staining targeting CD206 and F4/80 was performed to evaluate the effect of IDR on the polarization of M1/M2 macrophages in colitis. Subsequently, the effects of IDR on the M1- or M2-polarization of THP-1-derived macrophages were investigated. Furthermore, the effects of IDR on ferroptosis in the colon tissue of mice and on RSL3-induced ferroptosis in THP-1-derived macrophages were assessed. The results were verified in mouse peritoneal macrophages.ResultsIn addition to reducing the infiltrated macrophages, IDR treatment preserved CD206+ macrophages in DSS-induced colitis. Using cultured THP-1 cells, we demonstrated that IDR inhibited M1 polarization and prompted M2 polarization. Furthermore, we showed that IDR treatment decreased levels of 4-HNE while increasing GPX4 and NRF2 in DSS-induced colitis and THP-1 cells. IDR treatments also reduced cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and iron content, and mitigated RSL3-induced ferroptosis in THP-1-derived macrophages. Similarly, IDR augmented M2-polarization and alleviated ferroptosis in peritoneal macrophages.Discussion and ConclusionIDR skews the polarization of macrophages from M1 to M2. Furthermore, it inhibits ferroptosis in both mice and THP-1-derived macrophages. These mechanisms may contribute to the therapeutic effects of IDR in the treatment of UC.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a lifelong inflammatory disease affecting the rectum and colon to a variable extent. In 2023, the prevalence of ulcerative colitis was estimated to be 5 million cases around the world, and the incidence is increasing worldwide (Le Berre et al. 2023). The pathogenesis of UC is multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, epithelial barrier defects, dysregulated immune responses and environmental factors (Ungaro et al. 2017). Although huge advances have been made in the arsenal of treatments available to treat UC, for example, Mesalamines, corticosteroids, anti-TNF agents, anti-β7 integrin agent (Vedolizumab), anti-interleukin agents (IL-23 p19, IL-12/IL-23 P40), Janus-kinase inhibitors(tofacitinib), curcumin, probiotics, fecal microbiota transplant, et al. these options are far from perfect. Therefore, prompting the understanding of UC pathogenesis and exploring novel treatment are still very important.

Innate immune system, particularly monocyte-macrophage-mediated, has been implicated in the initiation and progression of UC (Saleh and Trinchieri 2011). During the disease, monocytes are recruited to inflamed tissues and differentiate into macrophages. Traditionally, macrophages can be polarized into either classically activated pro-inflammatory (M1) or activated anti-inflammatory (M2) macrophages, depending on the stimuli they encounter. M1 macrophages are induced by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), such as bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and Th1 cytokines, including interferon gamma (IFN-γ). They produce a wide range of cytokines, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6) and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), which exacerbate inflammation. In contrast, M2 macrophages are induced by Th2 cytokines, such as interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interleukin-13 (IL-13). They have the capacity to express chemokine (C-C motif) ligands 22 (CCL22), arginase-1 (Arg1), chitinase-like 3 (also called Ym1) and IL-10, promoting reparative processes and alleviating inflammation (Mills et al. 2000). In patients with UC, M1 macrophages are significantly enriched, while M2 macrophages are diminished in the inflamed colon (Dharmasiri et al. 2021). Moreover, in vitro-derived alternatively activated macrophages have been shown to reduce colonic inflammation in mice (Hunter et al. 2010). Therefore, restoring the balance between M1 and M2 macrophage polarization represents a promising strategy for the treatment of UC (Hegarty et al. 2023).

Recently, ferroptosis—a unique form of programmed cell death dependent on reactive oxygen species (ROS) and characterized by the accumulation of iron and lipid peroxidation (Dixon et al. 2012), has been critically implicated in the pathogenesis of UC (Xu et al. 2020; Gao et al. 2021); and has been suggested as a therapeutic target for its treatment (Yokote et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023). Although the ferroptosis of intestinal epithelial cells in colitis has been well documented, ferroptosis in macrophages has received less attention. Theoretically, ferroptotic macrophages produce ROS, release proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α), chemokines (e.g., CCL2, CCL7), DAMPs (such as HMGB1) and Fe2+, thereby probably exaggerating the ferroptosis of intestinal epithelial cells. Most importantly, ferroptosis is associated with the balance of M1/M2 polarized macrophages, as M2 macrophages are more susceptible to ferroptosis than M1 macrophages (Ye et al. 2024); inhibiting ferroptosis can rescue M2 macrophages (Feng et al. 2024). Therefore, preventing ferroptosis in macrophages could be a potential strategy for preserving M2 macrophages and alleviating inflammation.

Indirubin (2-(2-oxo-1H-indol-3-ylidene)-1H-indol-3-one; IDR) is an active ingredient derived from Indigofera tinctoria L. It has been clinically approved for the treatment of chronic myeloid leukemia in China since the 1970s. Furthermore, IDR, along with Indigo Naturalis—which contains IDR as its primary active pharmaceutical ingredient—has been shown to ameliorate murine dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis (Gao et al. 2016; Kawai et al. 2017; Gao et al. 2018). IDR acts as a dietary-derived indole-based ligand for the cytosolic aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) (Hubbard et al. 2015). In our previous research, we demonstrated that IDR inhibited inflammation by suppressing the NF-κB signaling pathway, as well as the expression of IL-17A, NLRP3 and IL-1β in DSS-induced colitis in mice (Liu Z et al. 2023). Most notably, we observed that IDR treatment not only reduced the number of infiltrating macrophages in the colons of mice with DSS-induced colitis but also induced a morphological change in these cells from a round (M1-like) shape to an elongated (M2-like) shape. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the regulatory effects of IDR on M1/M2 polarization and the ferroptosis of macrophages.

Materials and methods

Animal experiment, transcriptomic data and ethical statement

This study used paraffin-embedded and frozen tissue samples acquired from our previous experiment as described (Liu Z et al. 2023) and no new animal experiments were conducted. In that experiment, a total of 48 male BALB/c mice at 6–8 weeks old were randomly divided into six groups: normal control (NC), model (DSS-induced colitis) and treatment groups with different doses (10, 20 and 40 mg/kg, respectively) of IDR or sulfasalazine (SASP) as the positive control. According to our original plan, we also treated another 24 mice in three groups with a traditional Chinese medicine (containing IDR as an active ingredient) in high, middle and low doses in that animal experiment; but samples from those three groups were not included in this study for the clarity.

Briefly, the acute colitis was induced in mice with 2% DSS (MP Biomedicals, USA) dissolved in drinking water given ad libitum for 6 consecutive days and replaced with normal drinking water for the next 2 days. IDR with a purity >98% was purchased from Chengdu Herbpurify Co., Ltd. IDR was intragastrically administered at a dose of 20 mg/kg. Mice in the model group received an equal volume of vehicle solution. All treatments were maintained for eight consecutive days from day 1 of DSS treatment. In day 8 after DSS administration, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation after being anesthetized with 1% isoflurane gas at a flow rate of 0.6–1 liters per minute, and colon samples were collected. Aliquots of proximal colon tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. The middle and distal aliquots were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at −80 °C.

The frozen middle colonic samples were subjected to RNA extraction, transcriptomic sequencing and bioinformatics analysis. Those sequences were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive database with accession number PRJNA898498. In this research, the Protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis of the proteins corresponding to the DEGs was performed using the STRING database (Szklarczyk et al. 2023) with the highest confidence (0.9000) (https://www.string-db.org/).

Animals used in the experiment were purchased from and housed in a specific pathogen-free facility at the Animal Experiment Center, Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (with animal certificate number: 44005800014029). These mice were housed under controlled conditions (24 °C–25 °C of temperature, 45%–60% of humidity) with 12-h light/dark cycle. All procedures were performed in accordance with the recommendations of the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council 2011). The protocol of DSS-induced colitis was optimized according to a published protocol (Whittem et al. 2010). All experiments were approved by the University Experimental Ethical Committee (No. 20211125002) on September 8, 2022. As in our preliminary experiment, we observed that female mice exhibited a significant insensitivity to DSS-induced colitis, we revised our animal number per group from 5 male and 5 female mice to 8 male mice as we did not need to consider the gender differences. The less sensitivity of female mice to DSS-induced colitis than males is consistent with others’ report (Chassaing et al. 2014) which is probably due to the protective effects of estrogen.

Histopathological examination and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

The paraffin-embedded colon tissues were cut into 5 μm thick sections. After dewaxing, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. IHC staining was performed to evaluate macrophage infiltration and deposition. Briefly, after incubation with rabbit anti-mouse F4/80 (Proteintech, 14537-1-AP), and CD206 (Proteintech, 18704-1-AP) polyclonal antibodies, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc., China), the slices were visualized with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and hematoxylin counterstaining. Images were scanned with a Digital Pathology Slide Scanner (Konfoong Bioinformation Tech Co., Ltd, China) and snapped with K-viewer software at ×200. The macrophages with F4/80 (macrophage-specific surface marker) or CD206 (M2-macrophage marker) positive staining were counted in 10 random snapshots per animal.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from mouse distal colon tissue or cultured cells using RIPA Lysis and Extraction Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein were separated using 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C, including anti-mouse/human glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4, Proteintech), 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4-HNE, Abcam, USA), ferritin light chain (FTL, Proteintech) and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2/NFE2L2, Proteintech). For THP-1 cells, anti-human p65 (Proteintech) and phospho-p65 (CST), phospho-STAT6 (Abcam, USA) were used. The amount of protein was normalized to that of human/mouse β-actin (Proteintech) or GAPDH (Proteintech). After washing, HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech, both in 1:5000) was incubated at room temperature for 2 h. Proteins of interest were then detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with ChemiDoc MP (Bio-rad, USA) and the images were analyzed using Image-J software.

Cell lines

Human monocytic THP-1 cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (TIB-202, ATCC, Maryland, USA). Cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, USA) supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, USA), 0.05 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Grand Island, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from colon tissue using TRIzol reagent, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA concentration of each sample was determined by UV spectrometry using a NANO 2000 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA was then reverse-transcribed from the RNA in a reaction system containing random primers and M-MLV reverse transcriptase. cDNA samples were subsequently subjected to RT-PCR using SYBR Green Master Mix (TaKaRa Bio, Japan) in the 7500 Real-time System (Applied Biosciences, USA). The RT-PCR conditions included incubation at 95 °C for 5 s, 60 °C for 34 s, 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and 95 °C for 15 s for 40 cycles. The relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2–ΔΔCT threshold method with GAPDH as the reference gene for data normalization. The primer sequences used are listed in Table S1.

Macrophage polarization and indirubin (IDR) treatment

THP-1 monocytes are differentiated into macrophages by 48 h incubation with 50 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, Sigma, P8139). Then, macrophages were polarized into M1 macrophages by incubation with 20 ng/mL of IFN-γ (R&D system, #285-IF) and 100 ng/mL of LPS (Sigma, #8630). Macrophage M2 polarization was obtained by incubation with 20 ng/mL of interleukin 4 (R&D Systems, #204-IL) and 20 ng/mL of interleukin 13 (R&D Systems, #213-ILB) for 48 h.

IDR (Chendu Herbpurify Co., LTD, China) was resolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in 20 mM and diluted with culture medium into working solution (0.1–1 μM). The final concentrations of DMSO were less than 0.1% which showed no significant effect on proinflammatory cytokines production in our previous study.

Analysis surface markers of M1- and M2-polarized macrophages by flow cytometry

CD206 identified M2 population of colonic macrophages in health and inflammatory bowel diseases (Wright et al. 2021). We used CD86 and CD206 as the surface markers of M1- or M2- polarized macrophages, respectively. Briefly, after incubation with IFN-γ/LPS or IL-4/IL-13, M1- or M2- THP-1 derived macrophages were washed with cold PBS and detached with 5 mM EDTA. After washing twice, the collected cells were resuspended in a concentration of 5 × 105 cells/0.1 mL in cold PBS containing 5% human heat inactivated serum and human truStain FcX (BioLegend, #422301). The suspension was then incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Cells were centrifuged 5 min at 200×g and 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended with 50 μL of primary anti-CD86 or anti-CD206 (BD Pharmingen, #555954) antibody in PBS containing 5% serum and incubated 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were also incubated with the control isotype mouse IgG1 κ isotype control (BD Pharmingen, #555749) corresponding to each primary antibody. After incubation and washing with PBS, the pellet was resuspended with 2% paraformaldehyde (in cold PBS) and incubated 20 min at 4 °C. A last centrifugation of 5 min at 200×g and 4 °C was performed before cell resuspension in 0.5 mL of PBS containing 1% serum. Samples were analyzed using CytoFLEX LX flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, USA).

Ferroptosis induction

For ferroptosis induction, THP-1 cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) twice to remove the FBS. The cells were then suspended in RPMI1640 medium at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL and 1 mL of the cell suspension was seeded in each well of a 24-well plate. RSL3 (Aladdin) was added to the wells, and incubated in 5% CO2 at 37 °C. After titration, we chose 10 µM as the working concentration of RSL3 as it induced about 50% to 80% death in THP-1 cells after 24 h, and the contrast between RSL3 and IDR groups was sharp.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

The effect of IDR and ferroptosis inducers on the viability of THP-1 cells was examined using a CCK-8 assay kit (Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan). Cells were digested and seeded in 96-well plates (1000 cells/well) and were incubated with RSL3. After 48 h, 10 µL CCK-8 solution was added to each well and cultured for another 4 h at 37 °C. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was determined using a Varioskan LUX multimode microplate reader (USA), and the viability was calculated using the following equation: viability rate (%) = A (sample)/A (control) × 100.

PI staining

Cells were harvested on successive days and then washed twice with PBS and stained with PI (ebioscience, E-CK-A211) according to the manufacture’s instruction. After adding Precision count beads (BioLegend, 424902) according to the manual, samples were analyzed using CytoFLEX LX flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter).

Iron quantification assay

A Cell Ferrous Iron (Fe2+) Assay Kit (Fluorometric) (HY-K0322, MedChemExpress LLC, China) was applied to quantify iron concentration in THP-1 cells according to the manufacturer’s manual. As mentioned above, THP-1 cells were cultured in 24-well plates and administered with RSL3 and IDR intervention. For each group, 2×105 cells were collected by centrifugation. The cells were washed three times with serum-free medium, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of staining working solution, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the samples were directly measured using the Varioskan LUX microplate reader (Thermofisher, USA) with appropriate filters (Ex/Em = 543/580 nm). The detection was completed within 2 h. Iron concentrations were calculated by normalized to the cell counts determined with CCK-8 assay.

ROS level assay

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation in THP-1 cells was assessed with 2’7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cells were seeded into six-well plates with 1 × 106 cells/well, then were treated with RSL3 for 24 h. DCFH-DA was diluted with serum-free medium to the final concentration 10 μM. The culture medium was removed, and DCFH-DA was added. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. The cells were washed three times with serum-free medium. Add 200 μL of RIPA buffer to each well and incubate on ice for 5 min, then collect cell lysate into 1.5 mL tubes. Centrifuge at 21,130 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Transfer 100 μL of the supernatant to a black 96-well plate and measure the fluorescence intensity using the Varioskan LUX microplate reader (Thermofisher) at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 530 nm.

MDA (malondialdehyde) assay

After 24 h, THP-1 cells treated with IDR and/or ferroptosis inducers were collected. The MDA concentrations were determined with a Malondialdehyde (MDA) Content Assay Kit (Cat.No. BC0025, Solarbio, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration was normalized to protein content determined using a BCA Kit (Bio-rad).

Isolation of mouse peritoneal macrophages

C57BL/6J mice (6–7 weeks of age, half male and half female) were purchased from Animal Experiment Center, GZUCM. The protocol was approved by the University Experimental Ethical Committee (No. 20250704031). Mice were housed in an animal facility under a 12‐h light/dark cycle and were given standard chow and water ad libitum. Primary peritoneal macrophages of mice were isolated following previously published methods (Hunter et al. 2010). Briefly, mice were euthanized, and 5 mL of ice‐cold PBS was injected into the peritoneal cavity. The fluid was then withdrawn, centrifuged (400×g for 5 min at 4 °C), and the cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of RPMI 1640 supplemented with 1% penicillin‐streptomycin and 10% bovine calf serum. The peritoneal exudate cells were cultured on culture plates (2 h at 37 °C), after which non‐adherent cells were removed.

M2 polarization in primary mouse macrophages

Peritoneal macrophages were cultured and polarized with complete RPMI 1640 medium containing 20 ng/mL recombinant mouse IL-4 (Sinobiological Inc., China) for 48 h. IDR in diluted concentrations (0.1–1.0 μM) was added simultaneously. 6-Formylindolo[3,2-b] carbazole (FICZ) was used as a positive control. Subsequently, adherent cells were detached by incubation with 5 mM EDTA for 30 min on ice, followed by vigorous pipetting. This detachment method was used because it is optimized to maintain the M2 macrophage phenotype (Chen et al. 2015).

The detached cells were subjected to intracellular immunofluorescence staining of Arginase-1 (Arg1) followed by flow cytometry analysis. Briefly, after centrifugation, the macrophages were fixed and permeabilized using BD Cytofix/Cytoperm™ Fixation and Permeabilization Solution according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After blocking with 5% BSA (fraction V, fatty acid-free, Beyotime Inc., China), the cells were incubated with rabbit anti-mouse Arg1 (1:400, 16001-1-AP, Proteintech, China) for 30 min at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor® 594 labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:200, ZF0516, ZSGB-Bio Inc., China) for 30 min at 4 °C. After washing, samples were analyzed using CytoFLEX LX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter).

Ferroptosis induction in primary macrophages

The adherent primary macrophages were treated with 10 μM RSL3 for 24 h. IDR in diluted concentrations (0.1–1.0 μM) was added simultaneously, while 1 μM Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, Aladdin Inc., China) was used as a positive control. Cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay.

Ferrous iron content was also determined using the Cell Ferrous Iron (Fe2+) Fluorometric Assay Kit (MCE, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The stained cells was observed under a microscope (Leica DMi8) with filters of Ex/Em = 543/580 nm. The fluorescence intensity of the images was analyzed using ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

The results are presented as mean ± SD values. Multiple groups comparisons were assessed through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the least significant difference or Kruskal-Wallis multi-comparisons test using IBM SPSS 20.0. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

IDR preserved M2-like macrophages in colitis

We previously showed that IDR alleviated DSS-induced colitis in mice (Liu Z et al. 2023). Using H&E staining, we observed that mice in the DSS group exhibited severe epithelial injury, marked destruction of crypts, and extensive areas of inflammatory cell infiltration in their colonic tissue compared to those in the NC group. In contrast, middle-dose IDR treatment (20 mg/kg) significantly mitigated these pathological changes and was superior to the low-dose (10 mg/kg) and high-dose (40 mg/kg) IDR treatment groups (Liu Z et al. 2023). The body weight, Disease Activity Index (DAI) score, and colon length of NC, DSS, and IDR (20 mg/kg) groups are shown in Figure 1. These results further confirmed the successful establishment of the model and the protective effects of IDR.

In this study, we found that the infiltration of macrophages (F4/80 positive) in the colonic submucosa was significantly increased in the DSS group, which was partially ameliorated in the IDR group (Figure 1a-1d). Interestingly, we noted that the morphology of macrophage-like cells in the colonic submucosa of IDR-treated mice was distinctly different from that of the mice in the DSS group. Macrophages infiltrating the submucosa appeared predominantly round (M1-like) in the DSS group, whereas they exhibited an elongated (M2-like) shape in the IDR group. Therefore, we deduced that IDR treatment likely skewed M1-polarized macrophages toward an M2-polarized phenotype. Furthermore, we confirmed that IDR treatment increased the abundance of CD206+ M2 macrophages compared to DSS group, which were primarily enriched in the colonic submucosa (Figure 1e–1h).

IDR inhibits M1 polarization

We aimed to confirm whether IDR skews M1/M2 polarization toward M2 macrophages in vitro. First, we validated our experimental system for M1/M2 polarization using THP-1 monocytes. M1-polarized macrophages exhibited a round shape, while M2-polarized macrophages appeared elongated (Figure 2a). Flow-cytometry analysis revealed that M1-polarized macrophages expressed higher levels of CD86 compared to M0 cells, whereas M2-polarized macrophages showed elevated levels of CD206, a specific marker for M2 macrophages (Figure S2B and S2C). Additionally, the expression of M1 markers, such as Il6, Il12, and Tnfa, was upregulated in M1-polarized macrophages, while the expression of M2 markers, including Il10, Arg1, and Ccl22, was upregulated in M2-polarized macrophages (Figure S2D).

Then, we initially investigated the impact of IDR on M1 polarization. As the dissociation constant (KD) of IDR binding to the human AhR-ARNT complex was 294.9 nM; and the EC50 values of IDR activating AhR were 104.2 and 418.3 nM in Hep3B and HaCaT cells (Diao et al. 2025), we selected 0.1–1.0 μM as the concentration range of IDR in our study. As shown in Figure 2a, IDR treatment significantly reduced the proportion of CD86-positive cells. Furthermore, the mRNA expression levels of M1 markers (Il6, Il12, and Tnfa) were notably decreased following IDR treatment (Figure 2b). These results indicate that IDR treatment inhibits the expression levels of M1 markers.

As numerous studies have demonstrated that the NF-κB signaling pathway plays a critical role in M1 polarization (Liu et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2017; Liu et al. 2020). In our previous research, we also showed that IDR downregulated several NF-κB target genes with the transcriptomic analysis (Liu Z et al. 2023). Therefore, we hypothesized that IDR suppresses M1 polarization by regulating the NF-κB signaling pathway. Through Western blot analysis, we demonstrated that IDR inhibited the phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 in M1 macrophages (Figure 2c). We concluded that IDR inhibits M1-like polarization, at least partially, through the suppression of the NF-κB pathway, although the potential contribution of other signaling pathways cannot be excluded.

IDR augmented M2 polarization

Then, we evaluated the effects of IDR treatments on M2 polarization in vitro. As shown in Figure 3a, IDR treatments (in 0.1 and 1.0 μM) notably increased the immunofluorescence intensity of CD206 staining on M2 macrophages. FICZ, a classic AhR ligand, also augmented CD206 immunofluorescence intensity similarly (Fig. S3). These results were further supported by the increased proportion of CD206-positive cells in the IDR treatment groups (0.11, 0.33, and 1.0 μM) compared to the M2 group, as determined by flow-cytometry analysis (Figure 3b and 3c). Additionally, IDR treatments significantly upregulated the gene expression of Arg1, Il10, and Il22 mRNA (Figure 3d).

As STAT6 is the key transcription factor responsible for M2 polarization, we evaluated the effect of IDR treatments on STAT6 phosphorylation. We observed a significant increase in phosphorylated STAT6 (p-STAT6) at 2 h and 4 h in the IDR treatment groups, but not at 8 h (Figure 3e and 3f).

IDR induced transcriptomic alterations related to oxygen transport and lipid metabolism

We subsequently reanalyzed the transcriptomic data to uncover additional details. As predicted by the protein-protein interactions (PPI) network analysis, we observed that numerous hubs were clustered, which were associated with oxygen binding (members of the hemoglobin family), host defense (members of the serpin family), and lipid transport and metabolism (such as apolipoproteins, Lep, and SCD1, and others). The representative components of the PPI network were shown in Figure 4a and 4b. Utilizing Gene Ontology (GO) analysis modules, we further predicted the enriched pathways. As shown in Figure 4c and 4d, in both comparisons between ‘DSS vs. NC’ and ‘IDR vs. DSS’, the pathways were enriched in oxygen transport (including ‘Oxygen binding’, ‘Oxygen carrier activity’, ‘Hemoglobin binding’, ‘Haptoglobin binding’, ‘Haptoglobin-hemoglobin complex’, and ‘hydrogen peroxide metabolic process’ et al. were highlighted in red color in Figure 4c and 4d, and lipid transport and metabolism (highlighted in brown color). Therefore, we deduced that IDR may inhibit lipid peroxidation.

IDR ameliorated ferroptosis in DSS-induced colitis

As mentioned above, DSS and IDR caused profound alterations in genes involving in oxygen transport and lipid metabolism, we sought to determine whether IDR treatment mitigated ferroptosis in DSS-induced colitis. We first adopt a gene profile of 479 ferroptosis-associated genes which had been associated with UC (retrieved from the FerrDb) (Zhou et al. 2023; Zhu et al. 2024). Among them, we identified 11 DEGs in the comparison between the DSS group and the NC group with a threshold of p < 0.05 and change folds <0.5 or >2 (log2FoldChange >1) from our transcriptomic data (Table S2). Among them, eight genes were significantly upregulated (NOS2, IDO1, CDKN2A, LCN2, IL1B, CHAC1, FANCD2, MS4A15) while three genes were significantly downregulated (NR1D1, ACSL3, ETV4). Further, in the comparison between IDR and DSS groups, three genes of them (CDKN2A, IL1B, CHAC1) were identified with the same criteria, and another gene. All the three genes were significantly downregulated in IDR group. In consistency with this result, we observed similar alterations in the colonic mRNA levels of Chac1 and Cdkn2a with real-time PCR (Figure S4A and S4B). CDKN2A was believed to be a ferroptosis suppressor which has been associated with immune infiltration (Dong et al. 2023). Glutathione specific gamma-glutamylcyclotransferase 1 (Chac1) plays a pivotal role in oxidative-stress-induced cell ferroptosis via the regulation of glutathione depletion (Liu Y et al. 2023). Therefore, these results implied that IDR could be a ferroptosis inhibitor in DSS-induced colitis.

Subsequently, we tried to confirm that IDR treatment inhibited ferroptosis in mice with DSS-induced colitis. As shown in Figure 4e and 4f, IDR treatments (40, 20 and 10 mg/kg) increased the protein abundance of GPX4 and NRF2 and decreased the abundance of 4-HNE. These results imply that IDR mitigated ferroptosis in DSS-induced colitis.

IDR inhibited ferroptosis in macrophages

Hemoglobin, heme, and hemin produced by aged red blood cells (RBC) can trigger ferroptosis in macrophages (Imoto et al. 2018). In UC, ferroptosis of macrophages may be exacerbated by erythrophagocytosis (Youssef et al. 2018). In this research, we observed the macrophage engulfing RBC with HE staining (Figure S5A–S5D). Using IHC stating targeting hemoglobin α (HBA), we showed the deposition of hemoglobin into the lamina propria of mice in the DSS group, and cells with strong HBA staining appeared to be macrophages (Figure S5E–S5G). This result suggests the presence of ferroptosis inducer to macrophages in DSS-induced colitis.

Subsequently, we investigated whether IDR inhibits ferroptosis in monocyte-macrophages in vitro. We first determined the optimal range of IDR concentration for ferroptosis protection. As shown in Figure S6, 1.0 μM IDR showed the best protection against RSL3-induced THP-1 cell death. Interestingly, higher doses of IDR could not exert superior protection to 1.0 μM IDR. The protective effect of IDR was similar to 1.0 μM Fer-1 as the positive control.

We then assessed the inhibitory effect of IDR on RSL3-induced ferroptosis in THP-1-derived M0, M1, and M2 macrophages, as well as in THP-1 monocytes. As shown in Figure 5a–5d, 10 μM RSL3 treatment resulted in an average cell death rate of 53.0%, 49.6%%, 64.4%, and 40.1% of in M0, M1, M2, and monocytes, respectively. The highest death rate was observed in M2 macrophages, which is consistent with previous reports (Piattini et al. 2021). High-dose IDR (1.0 μM) significantly inhibited RSL3-induced cell death in THP-1 monocytes and all subsets of THP-1-derived macrophages.

As the CCK-8 method could not exclude the impact of IDR on cell proliferation, we utilize flow-cytometry analysis with propidium iodide (PI) staining, accompanied by Precision Count Beads to determine cell death rates and loss of cell counts. As shown in Figure 5e, RSL3 induced a significant increase in the proportion of PI-positive cells, along with a notable decrease in cell counts. In the IDR treatment groups, the proportion of PI-positive cells decreased (significantly in the 1.0 μM group); furthermore, in both the 1.0 μM and 0.33 μM IDR treatment groups, the loss of cell counts was significantly inhibited, as illustrated in Figure 5f and 5g, which present the percentage of PI-positive cells and cell counts, respectively.

Moreover, we observed a significant increase in the fluorescence intensity of DCFH-DA staining, which implies an elevation in cellular ROS levels following RSL3 incubation. This increase was significantly inhibited by both 1 μM and 0.33 μM IDR treatments (both p < 0.05) (Figure 5h). Similarly, the MDA level was significantly elevated in the RSL3 group and decreased in both IDR treatment groups (Figure 5i). Moreover, iron content was significantly increased in RSL3-treated THP-1 cells and this increase was inhibited by IDR treatment (Figure 5j).

We further analyzed the expression of a ferroptosis-associated gene profile, which including Gpx4, Acsl4, Nrf2, and Slc7a11. The transcription levels of Gpx4, Nrf2, and Slc7a11 were significantly downregulated in the RSL3 treatment group, while Acsl4 was significantly upregulated. In the IDR treatment groups, the transcription levels of Gpx4, Nrf2, and Slc7a11 were significantly upregulated in both doses of IDR treatment groups, whereas the transcription of Acsl4 was significantly downregulated (Figure 5k).

Then, we assessed the abundance of 4-HNE, GPX4, NRF2, and FTL. As shown in Figure 5l and 5m, RSL3 increased the abundance of 4-HNE, suppressed GPX4, NRF2, and FTL. In contrast, IDR treatments decreased 4-HNE and enhanced the levels of GPX4, NRF2, and FTL.

IDR prompted M2-polarization and inhibited ferroptosis in primary macrophages

In order to avoid being misled by results obtained from THP-1 cell line, we further validated the major findings using mouse peritoneal macrophages. First, we examined the effect of IDR on IL-4-induced M2 polarization in peritoneal macrophages. As shown in Figure 6a, the proportion of M2-polarizated cells, determined by surface Arg-1 staining, was significantly upregulated in the presence of IDR (Figure 6b). This effect is likely due to activation of the AhR pathway, as FICZ treatment produced similar effects on M2 polarization.

Furthermore, we evaluated the protective efficacy of IDR against RSL3-induced cell death. Using the CCK-8 assay, we showed that both IDR and Fer-1 significantly reduced RSL3-induced cell death (Figure 6c). Employing a probe specific for cellular ferrous iron, we observed that RSL3 caused a substantial accumulation of cellular ferrous iron, which was significantly diminished by treatment with IDR and Fer-1 (Figure 6d and 6e). Therefore, we conclude that IDR protects primary macrophages from RSL3-induced ferroptosis.

Discussion

AhR was originally identified as a receptor mediating the toxic effects of environmental pollutants. For many years, AhR activation was actively avoided during drug development. However, AhR was later identified as an important regulator of the immune response and emerged as a potential therapeutic target in inflammatory diseases, cancer, and infections. For example, Tapinarof, the first AhR-activating drug, was approved for the treatment of psoriasis in 2022. Consequently, AhR agonists have attracted significant attention in recent years for their potential in drug development. Nevertheless, their therapeutic mechanisms remain unclear, which hampers progress in this field.

IDR is a potent AhR ligand that exhibits anti-inflammatory and immune-modulatory properties in various human diseases, including UC. Manipulating the recruitment, activation, polarization, and cell death of macrophages represents an attractive strategy for the treatment of UC. In this study, we showed that IDR can inhibit M1 polarization and prompt M2 polarization. The suppression of the NF-κB pathway in M1 polarization and the activation of the STAT6 pathway in M2 polarization, respectively, further supported the regulatory effect of IDR on M1/M2 polarization. The inhibition of the NF-κB pathway through AhR activation has been well documented (Baglole et al. 2008; Vázquez-Gómez et al. 2022; Liu Z et al. 2023). Similarly, IDR inhibits NF-κB activation in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Sethi et al. 2006). However, further research is needed to unravel the underlying molecular mechanisms by which IDR activates the STAT6 signaling pathway and prompts M2 polarization.

In this study, we also showed that IDR provides protection against RSL3-induced ferroptosis in both THP-1 cells, THP-1derived M0, M1, and M2 macrophages, as well as in mouse peritoneal macrophages. Since ferroptotic macrophages produce ROS, release proinflammatory cytokines and iron, we believe that the protective effects of IDR on macrophage ferroptosis could be beneficial in alleviating the colon inflammation in UC.

However, it is noteworthy that the biological activity of IDR does not strictly correlate with its dose or concentration in vivo or in vitro. First, we observed that a higher dosage (40 mg/kg) of IDR s did not produce greater improvement compared to the 20 mg/kg group in DSS-induced colitis. Similarly, increasing IDR concentration to 10 μM or higher did not enhance its protective effects against RSL3-induced ferroptosis or promote M2 polarization compared to 1 μM IDR. Although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, it appears that IDR at relatively lower concentrations primarily exhibits immunomodulatory effects, whereas at higher concentrations, it predominantly exerts toxic effects, such as exacerbating inflammation and ferroptosis. Therefore, our results suggest that the dosage of AhR agonists in clinical use should be carefully considered. Additionally, as numerous studies have shown that AhR activation induces oxidative stress (Alsharif et al. 1994; Dalton et al. 2002), it is reasonable to infer that targeting oxidative stress mediated by AhR, such as the AhR-Cyp1A1 axis, could potentially improve the clinical efficacy of IDR (Coelho et al. 2022). This could be a potential strategy to improve the clinical application of this kind of drugs.

We also observed that IDR functions as an inhibitor of cyclin-dependent kinases (Hoessel et al. 1999) and GSK-3. The EC50 in activating AhR of IDR and the IC50 targeting CDK1/cyclin B and GSK-3 were 0.059 μM, 10.000 μM, and 1.000 μM, respectively, in hepatoma cell line (Knockaert et al. 2004). Therefore, we deduced that the bioactivity of IDR at concentrations below 1 μM is primarily mediated by AhR pathway. Moreover, no studies have reported that inhibition of CDK1/cyclin B and GSK-3 contributes to the toxicity of IDR at high concentrations.

This research also presents two primary limitations that warrant further investigation in future studies. First, we did not fully elucidate the mechanisms by which IDR promotes M2 polarization and inhibits ferroptosis. Additionally, our understanding of the compound’s toxicity mechanisms remains insufficient. We plan to conduct more comprehensive investigations in subsequent studies.

In addition to regulating macrophage polarization and ferroptosis, we believe that other mechanisms, such as maintaining gut barrier function and improving gut dysbiosis, may also contribute to the protective effects of IDR on DSS-induced colitis. AhR controls the survival and function of gut-resident innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) and maintains the gut barrier function (Qiu et al. 2012). Activation of AhR by dioxin directly alters gut microbiota (Sun et al. 2019). The roles of these mechanisms in the therapeutic efficacy of IDR for DSS-induced colitis warrant further investigation and validation.

Overall, our research suggests that regulating macrophage polarization and inhibiting ferroptosis through AhR may contribute to the therapeutic mechanisms of IDR. Our results will support the development of this drug for treating various inflammatory diseases, including UC. Further studies are needed to fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms that mediate the immunomodulatory effects and potential toxicity of IDR.
