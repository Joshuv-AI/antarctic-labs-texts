Implementing Clinical Practice Guidelines for Oral Care for (Frail) Older Patients: Facilitators, Barriers, and Recommendations
Authors: Johanna Margaretha Kroese, Jan Joseph Mathieu Bruers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe Dutch organisation for the development of clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) in oral care known as the Kennisinstituut Mondzorg (KIMO) developed three CPGs to support professionals in oral care for (frail) older patients: (1) treatment of root caries, (2) xerostomia and hyposalivation related to medication and polypharmacy, and (3) oral care for homebound frail older adults. However, actual CPG use in practice is not guaranteed. This study aims to assess adherence to these CPGs among Dutch dentists, identify opportunities and barriers for implementation, and formulate recommendations for an effective implementation strategy.MethodsA modified two‐round Delphi study investigated current practices and opinions of dentists; 260 dentists were invited to participate. For round one, an online survey was developed based on the KIMO CPG recommendations. Round two, informed by round one results, explored perceived facilitators, barriers, suggestions for adapting less‐followed recommendations, and preferences regarding CPG information and accessibility.ResultsIn round one 41 dentists responded and in round two 44 responded. While the value of CPGs was acknowledged, adherence to several recommendations was limited due to practical barriers, such as time, equipment, training, and reimbursement. Further, communication with other healthcare providers was infrequent. Dentists preferred e‐mail notifications and on‐demand webinars. Digital availability via a website or app was favoured, though half still appreciate paper versions.ConclusionPractical barriers hinder the use of CPG recommendations in oral care for (frail) older patients. Involving clinicians in guideline development is essential to improve feasibility and implementation.Keywords: frail older people, guidelines, implementation, oral care

Introduction

The global population is experiencing an increase in the number of older adults. In the Netherlands, an expected 25% of the population will be 65 years or older by 2040 [1]. Nowadays, people retain their natural teeth to an older age [2], while physical impairment and/or cognitive decline can complicate oral hygiene. Further, hyposalivation can occur with chronic diseases, as a side effect of specific medications, or due to polypharmacy in general [3]. Thus, increasing age comes with an increase in oral health problems, such as periodontitis and caries [4], and consequently a higher need for oral care [5]. Global evidence indicates that older adults in less favourable socioeconomic contexts experience lower oral health‐related quality of life, underscoring the broader social determinants of oral care needs [6]. Nevertheless, the frequency of oral care utilisation decreases with age [7]. Barriers for regular and adequate oral care exist for both (a) the older patient, such as chronic diseases and impaired mobility [5], and (b) the dentist, such as insufficient knowledge, the complexity of the required care, and inadequate reimbursement [8].

To support oral health care professionals in the oral care for (frail) older patients, three clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) were developed by the Dutch organisation for the development of CPGs in oral care (Kennisinstituut Mondzorg; KIMO), on topics relevant to the treatment of this specific patient group: [1] the treatment of root caries (2019), which provides recommendations of diagnosis, prevention, and minimally invasive treatment of root caries; [2] xerostomia and hyposalivation related to medication and polypharmacy (2021), which addresses assessment and management of dry mouth symptoms often linked to multiple medications; and [3] oral care for homebound frail older adults (2021), which offers guidance on maintaining oral health and delivering care in non‐clinical settings.

Regardless of their importance, the actual usage of CPGs is not guaranteed. Barriers may include a lack of knowledge of the CPG, the inaccessibility of the CPG, environmental context or unavailability of adequate resources [9, 10], and broad acceptance among the intended users is essential for successful implementation. By identifying the barriers and facilitators to implementing the KIMO CPGs, strategies can be developed to achieve broad usage and eventually improve oral care for the (frail) older patients. To develop a strategy that aligns with the current situation, it is important to first understand the extent to which dentists already follow the guideline recommendations.

Accordingly, the aim of this exploratory research was to first determine whether dentists already adhere to the KIMO CPG recommendations in usual care, regardless of their familiarity with the CPGs. Subsequently, this study aimed to gain insight into possible opportunities and barriers for successful implementation, and to compose recommendations for an effective implementation strategy.

Material and Methods

Study Design

A modified two‐round Delphi method was used to survey and categorise common practices and opinions of dentists. In the first round, participants reported their usual care practices related to the KIMO CPG recommendations, with the primary outcome being the dentists' adherence expressed as the proportion of respondents reporting specific practices. The second round allowed respondents to reflect on the relevant topics identified in round one and provided additional insight into possible facilitators and barriers for implementation of the KIMO CPGs. However, unlike the classic Delphi method, the second round within this study did not necessarily aim at reaching consensus.

Participants and Recruitment

Dentists were recruited in two groups: 129 dentists with an affinity for gerodontology (Gero group) listed on the publicly available website of the Dutch Association for Gerodontology, and 121 general dental practitioners (GDP group) aged 67 or below, as dentists above this age typically no longer practice due to retirement, with a known residential and/or work address in the Netherlands, randomly selected from the Royal Dutch Dental Association database. Due to a low response in the GDP group, an additional 10 general dental practitioners were approached through convenience sampling, using the professional network of one of the authors (J.M.K.).

Dentists in both groups received an invitation through e‐mail with a weblink to participate, sent by an external and independent research agency (KBA, Nijmegen, the Netherlands). In the following weeks, up to three reminders were sent by e‐mail. Besides, the Dutch Association for Gerodontology encouraged its members to participate through their newsletter.

For round two, all 260 dentists were approached again by e‐mail, regardless of their response to round one. Along with the invitation, a summary of the results from round one was sent.

Measurement Instrument

For both rounds, a digital questionnaire was developed by the research team based on the recommendations of the three KIMO CPGs and refined through expert review to ensure relevance and clarity. Within the research team, J.M.K. is a geriatric dentist with experience in treating frail older patients, and J.J.M.B. is an expert in the field of methodology and guideline implementation at the Royal Dutch Dental Association.

The round‐one questionnaire included general questions about the work situation to characterise the respondent population. In addition, the CPG recommendations were transformed into statements describing treatment options for specific clinical situations, formulated to encompass both patient‐reported symptoms and dentist‐observed signs, as guideline adherence is recommended in either context. Participants indicated whether they never, sometimes, often, or always chose these options. While the guidelines on xerostomia and root caries provide specific recommendations for assessment and management of these conditions, the guideline on oral care for homebound frail Elders differs in scope, as it focuses on organising and delivering oral care in a non‐clinical setting. It addresses aspects such as indication, safety, and infection prevention, but does not include recommendations regarding specific equipment or treatment procedures. The questions for this third guideline were therefore adopted to its broader focus, addressing aspects highlighted in the guideline as well as potential barriers or facilitators identified in previous research.

Because round one aimed at identifying whether current usual care complies with the KIMO CPG recommendations regardless of familiarity, the guidelines were not mentioned in round one, and accordingly no questions on self‐reported knowledge or confidence were included to avoid biasing responses. When the answer to a statement was not in accordance with the CPG recommendation, follow‐up questions were asked to inquire about the reasons.

The summary of round one accompanying the invitation for round two included a short introduction on the KIMO CPGs. The questionnaire for round two was developed based on the results of round one. The CPG recommendations less complied with in usual care were selected, and follow‐up questions were formulated to inquire about possible facilitators, barriers, and suggestions for adjustments of, or alternatives to, the current recommendations. The questionnaire concluded with general questions on the visibility and accessibility of the KIMO CPGs.

Data Analysis

All data from the questionnaires were collected by the independent research agency, supplemented with additional demographic information from the database of the Royal Dutch Dental Association, and compiled in a pseudonymized dataset, in which no individual dentists are identifiable. The pseudonymized dataset was made available to the research team.

The data were analysed using IBM SPSS 27.0. Descriptive statistics were used to describe the groups of participants. To compare the Gero group and GDP group, an independent sample t‐test was used for continuous variables, and chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test was used for categorical variables.

Xerostomia and Hyposalivation Related to Medication and Polypharmacy

Table 2 shows the responses in round one to the statement questions based on the CPG concerning xerostomia and hyposalivation related to medication and polypharmacy. Most dentists considered medication as a possible cause for subjective complaints or objective signs of a dry mouth and advised additional fluoride measures. However, only a quarter of the dentists stated to always or often consult the prescriber of the medication or the pharmacist. Reasons for not consulting these professionals were uncertainty about which healthcare provider to approach (n = 11), a lack of time (n = 10), the believe that it is not appropriate to interfere with medication as a dentist (n = 8), a lack of medical knowledge (n = 8), and the lack of a billing code (n = 3). Unfeasibility to change anything due to the number of medications (n = 1) or need for specific medications (n = 2) and difficulties reaching other professionals by phone or e‐mail (n = 1) were also mentioned. Further exploration in round two and identification of possible facilitators indicated that 34 dentists (81.0%) did see reasons to consult the prescriber of the mediation or the pharmacist (Table A4). Including the possibility to adjust the medication (76.2%), to increase awareness of the interaction between oral and general health (66.7%), and to promote structured interprofessional collaboration (50.0%). Although the majority seemed to recognise value in consulting other professionals, round one showed that it is not common practice to do so. However, round two showed that 27 of the 42 dentists (64.3%) did sometimes advise patients to contact their doctor or pharmacist themselves.

Treatment of Root Caries

Table 4 shows the responses in round one to the statement questions based on the CPG concerning the treatment of root caries. The majority of dentists applied a minimal invasive treatment policy for root caries and increased the frequency of regular dental check‐ups for patients with an increased risk of root caries. Two thirds of dentists also increased the frequency of radiological examination in patients with a history of root caries. When asked why the frequency of radiological examination was increased only sometimes or never, reasons included a lack of patient cooperation/too stressful for the patient (n = 5), because no x‐ray machine is available on location (n = 5), it does not improve the quality of care (n = 3), no additional value of more frequent radiological examination is seen (n = 3), a shorter interval between radiological examinations is already used as standard care (n = 3), and the extra exposure to radiation does not outweigh the benefit (n = 1). Due to the relatively good adherence to this CPG overall, it was not included in the second round of the study.

Oral Care for Homebound Frail Older Adults

Of the 41 participants in round one, 33 (80.5%) provided oral care to a (frail) older patient at home at least once. Another two (4.9%) dentists indicated that not they themselves, but someone else from their dental clinic performed at least one home visit. The other 6 dentists indicated that no home visit was performed because it was never requested by a patient (n = 6). In addition, it was reported that no equipment is available for a home visit (n = 3), that there is a lack of personnel (n = 1), and that reimbursement is insufficient (n = 1).

When asked which aspects were considered during the most recent home visit, the majority of the 35 dentists surveyed (85.7%) indicated that proper equipment was available for the intended visit. In most cases, an oral care plan (57.1%), current list of medications (60.0%), and an informal caregiver or legal representative (68.6%) were present during the home visit. In half of the cases, attention was paid to infection prevention (54.3%). However, the availability of contact details of the general practitioner and/or pharmacist was less frequent (42.9%).

More generally, several barriers were identified for dental treatment at home (Table A5). Most dentist agreed that they are unable to perform all necessary treatments at home (82.9%), and often no mobile unit is available (58.5%). The majority stated that it is not possible to work ergonomically (87.8%) and that current reimbursement does not cover the costs of a home visit (78.0%).

Facilitators were also identified; the majority of dentists acknowledged the value of a home visit for the continuity of oral care (70.7%) and agreed that it is gratifying to help a home bound older patient (75.6%). Furthermore, most dentists did not feel uncomfortable visiting someone at home (70.7%) or asking their assistant to come along (70.7%), and invasion of privacy was not considered an issue (70.7%). Most dentists disagreed when asked whether they felt incompetent in treating homebound frail older patient (75.6%) or interacting with frail older patients with cognitive decline (82.9%).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the extent to which dentists adhere to the KIMO CPG recommendations in usual care, regardless of their familiarity with the CPGs, and to explore perceived barriers and facilitators to implementation. A key observation was that implementation of several guideline recommendations remains limited, even among the respondents who were predominantly dentists in the Gero group and thus presumably interested in geriatric oral care.

In particular, adherence to the recommendations of the CPG on xerostomia and hyposalivation related to medication and polypharmacy does not seem self‐evident. Measurement of the saliva secretion rate for supplementary diagnostics was rarely implemented. Although the majority of dentists did see clinical value in this measurement, the research also yielded many barriers. Including a lack of time and knowledge, and a lack of proper materials and the costs of the measurement. Some dentists reported not performing the salivary flow test because they perceived it as unvaluable, which may indicate limited awareness of its diagnostic relevance. This finding suggests that the guideline could provide stronger justification for the test's role in clinical decision‐making.

Interestingly, many dentists were open to referring a patient to a dental hygienist trained in performing the measurement, something to further explore and consider when revisiting the CPG. Dentists also considered a short questionnaire or a score based on a short clinical examination to be acceptable alternatives to the measurement proposed in the CPG. However, it is important to recognise that simplified alternatives do not fully capture all diagnostic or evaluative purposes intended by the comprehensive method recommended in the CPG. When revisiting the CPG, it could be explored whether a stepwise approach is useful, with a questionnaire or clinical score as an initial screening tool, followed by salivary flow measurement when indicated. Such flexibility might improve feasibility and adherence without compromising diagnostic quality.

Further, the recommendation to consult the prescriber of the medication or pharmacist was often not adhered to. Although the majority of dentists did see reasons to consult the prescriber or pharmacist, important barriers included uncertainty about who to approach, a lack of time and medical knowledge, and the belief that it is not appropriate to interfere with medication as a dentist. Dentists did advise patients to contact their doctor or pharmacist themselves. However, this approach does not support the reasons for consultation outlined in the CPG. It may be particularly ineffective in (frail) older patients with possible cognitive or functional impairments, for whom self‐initiated actions may be challenging [11].

The relatively good adherence to the CPG recommendations concerning the treatment of root caries is an encouraging finding, and may be attributed to the fact that these interventions are more easily integrated into routine practice. The main challenge in adhering to this CPG seemed to be increasing the frequency of radiological examinations, mainly caused by a lack of patient cooperation or unavailability of an x‐ray machine; circumstances that align with the study population of geriatric‐focused dentists who often work in nursing homes.

The majority of responding dentists provided oral care to a (frail) older patient at home at least once. This is likely due to their background and not representative of the general Dutch dentist, as previous literature suggests such services are rare. A study showed that 42% of Dutch general dentists are not willing to visit homebound frail older people for a periodic oral examination [12], and a more recent study on dental practice websites indicated that a home visit for oral care is still far from common in the Netherlands [13]. However, the current study does provide valuable insights into both the challenges and the intrinsic rewards associated with a home visit. Attention to infection prevention and availability of contact details of the GP and/or pharmacist seemed topics for improvement. Important barriers for a home visit included that it is not possible to perform all necessary treatments at home, related to the barrier that often no mobile unit is available. Further, it is not possible to work ergonomically and current reimbursement does not cover the costs for a home visit, the latter being in line with an earlier study [12]. Adult oral healthcare, including home visits, is not covered by basic health insurance in the Netherlands. Although a supplementary billing code with a higher fee for home visits was introduced after our data collection period, these visits generally involve higher logistical costs, and it remains unclear whether the new code will sufficiently cover these expenses or increase dentists' willingness to provide home care. Further, we did not collect data on the types of treatments performed during home visits, which limits the interpretation of barriers in relation to treatment complexity. Future research should explore this aspect to better understand how treatment type influences feasibility and adherence to clinical guidelines.

Altogether, a recurring theme across all CPG domains was the prevalence of practical barriers, including a lack of time, equipment, training, and financial reimbursement. This not only hinders implementation, but may also contribute to a sense of frustration among clinicians who recognise the value of certain recommendations but feel unable to act on them. Clinical usability and support by end users is essential for successful implementation [10]. This highlights the importance of aligning guideline content with actual clinical practice.

Another recurring issue concerns the limited interprofessional collaboration, both in the management of dry mouth and during home visits. Direct communication between dentists and other healthcare providers appears to be infrequent. This may reflect systemic fragmentation within the healthcare system, as well as a tendency among some dental professionals to shift responsibility onto patients. It is a topic already recognised in the literature, and strategies for improvement may include interprofessional education [14], as well as integrated care models or collaborative networks in daily practice [15, 16].

The facilitators, barriers, suggestions and alternatives identified in this study can inform the development of an effective implementation strategy for the current CPGs. When revising or developing new CPGs, it is crucial to consider not only scientific evidence and clinical rationale, but also organisational factors that may affect implementation [17]. While practical barriers do not necessarily warrant changes to the recommendations themselves, they do justify the inclusion of contextual remarks. These can help identify and guide efforts towards conditions under which adherence becomes more feasible. Involving practicing clinicians in the consultation phase of CPG development, and specifically asking for feedback on practical and clinical feasibility, is therefore essential.

In addition to involving clinicians in the development of CPGs, the importance of their engagement in developing effective guideline implementation tools, such as training materials, is also recognised [18]. As for the dissemination of the CPGs, the dentists in this research exhibited diverse preferences. For the notification on (the content of) new CPGs, digital ways seemed sufficient. Considering all findings, with notifications through e‐mail combined with on‐demand webinars, almost all participating dentists would be served. Live sessions on the content of CPGs would also be appreciated, but are more expensive and less efficient than webinars. Digital availability was mainly appreciated on a website or in an app. Notably, tools like a dedicated AI operated chatbot and social media were less popular. Availability of the CPGs on paper was still appreciated by half of the dentists, indicating that with digital notification only a certain group of dentists may not be reached. Considering the mean age of the responding dentists, future research could explore generational differences, as younger dentists may be more receptive to innovative digital tools [19].

Important limitations of this study include the relatively low response rate and the overrepresentation of dentists with a special interest in geriatric care, which limits the generalizability of the findings. However, it also strengthens the argument that implementation of CPGs concerning the care for older patients is challenging. If motivated and experienced practitioners already struggle with guideline adherence, barriers faced by general practitioners may be even more pronounced.

Further, the age profile of the respondents may also have implications for guideline implementation. Research shows that young professionals are more inclined to use guidelines than older, more experienced colleagues [10]. Future research should explore the perspectives of younger dentists, who may have different attitudes towards technology [19] and interprofessional collaboration [20, 21], and may already be more familiar with the CPGs, as these are increasingly integrated in dental curricula. Understanding generational differences could support the development of more effective and future‐proof implementation strategies.

Conclusion

Practical barriers hinder the use of CPG recommendations in oral healthcare for (frail) older patients. Involving clinicians in guideline development is essential to improve feasibility and implementation. Future research should also explore generational differences among dentists to inform sustainable implementation strategies.
