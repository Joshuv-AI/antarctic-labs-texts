Trust in Cancer Information Sources and Awareness of Human Papillomavirus as a Cause of Cervical Cancer Among US Adults
Authors: Olajumoke Ope Oladoyin, Paula Cuccaro, Lara S. Savas, Robert Yu, Joel Fokom Domgue, Sheryl McCurdy, Sanjay Shete
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionIs there an association between trust in cancer information sources and awareness of human papillomavirus (HPV) as a cause of cervical cancer?FindingsIn this cross-sectional study of 3914 US adults, compared with having lot of trust, having some and little or no trust in government health agencies, and having some trust in physicians for cancer information were associated with a lower odds of awareness that HPV causes cervical cancer. In contrast, having little or no trust in religious organizations was associated with higher odds of awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer.MeaningThese findings suggest that strengthening trust in government health agencies and physicians and organizing innovative public health promotion campaigns, community outreaches, and engagement initiatives could improve awareness about the role of HPV in cervical cancer and may contribute to eliminating this disease in the US.

Introduction

Approximately 90% of cervical cancers are attributed to the human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, the most common sexually transmitted infection in the US.1 Persistent infection with high-risk HPV is the primary cause of cervical cancer, with HPV types 16 and 18 being the most virulent.2,3,4 The HPV vaccine is safe and effective for preventing cervical cancer, but vaccine uptake remains suboptimal in the US,5,6,7,8 with a HPV vaccination completion rate of 61.4% in 20238 that is below the Healthy People 2030 target of 80.0%.7,9 The introduction of the HPV vaccine and several vaccination-promoting interventions initially increased public awareness of HPV; however, awareness of HPV decreased from 70.1% in 2019 to 68.3% in 2022, and awareness of HPV as the primary cause of cervical cancer decreased by 5.5% from 2017 to 2022.10 Studies have shown that awareness of the HPV virus11,12 influenced HPV vaccine uptake.12,13,14,15,16 The decrease in HPV awareness as a cause of cervical cancer may adversely affect HPV vaccine knowledge and uptake, which suggests the need for increased public health education about HPV.17

The political polarization of health information and the inconsistent messaging from health agencies during the COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated individual behavioral changes in response to public health initiatives.18,19 The reduced trust in government health agencies for cancer information20 and the influence of trust in health care professionals on vaccination outcomes21,22 highlighted the critical role of trust in these information sources. In 2023, it was observed that public trust in the US government decreased to 16.0%, the lowest since 2011.23 Trust is an important factor influencing the patient–health care professional relationship24,25; in 2021, trust in physicians decreased by 12.0%,26 and trust in physicians and clinics decreased from 72.0% in 2020 to 40.0% in 2024,22 suggesting that trust in clinicians is decreasing and is subject to doubt by clients and patients.

Faith-based organizations may influence vaccine uptake.27,28,29,30 A study showed that individuals who belong to organized religious bodies have lower awareness of HPV infection, the HPV vaccine, and HPV vaccine uptake.28 Despite the numerous advantages of faith-based organizations in public health initiatives, discussions surrounding HPV vaccination remain taboo in this milieu due to its perceived link with sexual activity and expectations of adolescent sexual abstinence.27,31 Religion and state religiosity are meso-level factors that have been found to influence HPV vaccine uptake.28,32

Social media play a powerful role in shaping public opinion, with 72.0% of US adults using it.33 In the field of public health, social media are valuable platforms to enhance public awareness, educate the public about HPV, and promote vaccination.34,35 However, the platforms are also rife with misinformation,36,37 with misleading themes on adverse reactions, conspiracy theories, and mistrust of authorities.37 Incomplete, negative, and inaccurate information can spread quickly, undermining public trust in government health agencies and physicians.

Although trust has been demonstrated to be important in previous studies,24,25,28,38 and in evaluating awareness of HPV and the HPV vaccine,39 to our knowledge, the association between trust in information sources for cancer and awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer has not previously been investigated. Understanding this association could lead to targeted interventions for improving HPV vaccination and cervical cancer awareness. Thus, it is necessary to evaluate how trust in these sources influences individuals’ awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer.

Methods

The data used in this cross-sectional study were obtained from the Health Information National Trends Survey (HINTS) collected from March 7 to November 8, 2022, on cancer-related information among noninstitutionalized US adults aged 18 years or older.40 HINTS is a periodic cross-sectional survey administered by the National Cancer Institute (NCI) since 2003.40 A total of 29 600 addresses were included in the 2022 HINTS, with a response rate of 28.1%.40 The HINTS data are publicly available without identifiers (ie, the data were deidentified).40,41 Thus, our study was deemed exempt by the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center institutional review board. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for reporting cross-sectional data.

Outcome Measure

The primary outcome was the awareness of HPV as the cause of cervical cancer. It was measured as a response to the question, “Do you think HPV can cause cervical cancer?” (“yes,” “no,” “not sure”).41 This outcome measure was assessed only among individuals who responded that they were aware of HPV with a yes to the question, “Have you ever heard of HPV? HPV stands for human papillomavirus.” The outcome variable was analyzed as a binary variable. In the binary variable, the “no” and “not sure” responses were combined into a single category.

Predictor Variables

The primary variables were trust-related measures, including (1) trust in government health agencies about cancer information, (2) trust in a physician for cancer information, and (3) trust in religious organizations about cancer information. Respondents were asked, “How much do you trust information about cancer from each of the following: (1) a doctor, (2) government health agencies, (3) religious organizations and leaders?” The response options were “not at all,” “a little,” “some,” and “a lot.” For this analysis, “not at all” and “a little” were combined into a single category, whereas “some” and “a lot” were kept separate with “a lot” as a reference category.

Covariates

We included participants self-reported sociodemographic variables, such as age (18-29 years, 30-45 years, 46-65 years, and >65 years), sex (male and female), educational level (up to high school, after high school or some college, and college or postgraduate), annual income level (<$35 000, $35 000 to <$50 000, $50 000 to <$75 000, and ≥$75 000), race and ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic Asian, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, and non-Hispanic other, which includes non-Hispanic American Indian or Alaska Native; Guamanian or Chamorro; or Native Hawaiian, Samoan, or Other Pacific Islander; and non-Hispanic multiple races), and occupational status (employed or other occupation, homemaker only, multiple status selected, retired or disabled, student only, and unemployed). Race and ethnicity were included in the study as a covariate because they have been shown to influence trust in physicians,42,43 government agencies,20 and religious organizations,44 as well as HPV awareness.5,45

The other covariates included were personal history of cancer, assessed with the question “Have you ever been diagnosed as having cancer”? (yes or no), and social media use to make decisions about health (we combined the questions: “How much of the health information that you see on social media do you think is false or misleading?” [recoded it into use and never used] and “I use information from social media to make decisions about my health” [recoded into agree and disagree]). Based on these 2 questions, we then categorized individuals into 3 groups: those who do not use social media, those who use social media but do not use the information from social media to make decisions about health, and those who use social media and use the information from social media to make decisions about health.

Statistical Analysis

A sample weight was applied in the analysis to account for the sampling method and nonresponse. Descriptive statistics were used to estimate the percentage of participants who were aware that HPV causes cervical cancer and to present the distribution and weighted percentages for the study population by trust in physicians, trust in government health agencies, and trust in religious organizations for cancer information and by age, sex, race and ethnicity, occupational status, personal history of cancer, and social media use to make health decisions. A survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression model was used to examine the association of trust in physicians, trust in government health agencies, trust in religious organizations with awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer, adjusting for age, sex, race and ethnicity, occupational status, personal history of cancer, and social media use to share personal health information. The adjusted odds ratio (aOR) and 95% CI were reported, and the analyses were conducted at a 2-sided significance level of .05 with SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).46 The missing data for the outcome variable were excluded from the analysis. However, for covariates with missing data, we created a “missing” category to ensure that these cases were included in the logistic regression analysis. The data were analyzed from July 23, 2025 to April 9, 2026.

Results

In this study, 5957 participants were asked about their awareness of HPV, of whom 3942 (67.7%) responded that they had heard of HPV. Of these 3942 participants, 3914 responded to the question “Do you think HPV can cause cervical cancer?” and were included in the study. The study demographics were as follows: 625 (15.0%) were Hispanic, 558 (10.0%) were non-Hispanic Black, and 2290 (65.2%) were non-Hispanic White; 2557 (55.8%) were female and 1316 (44.2) were male; 2086 (58.9%) were employed; and 1453 (36.5%) were between the ages of 46 and 65 years. Furthermore, 2910 (75.1%) had a lot of trust in physicians, 808 (20.7%) had some trust in physicians, 1173 (30.3%) had a lot of trust in government health agencies, 1696 (43.4%) had some trust in government health agencies, 157 (3.5%) had a lot of trust in religious organizations, 798 (20.1%) had some trust in religious organizations for cancer information, and 507 (13.1%) used information from social media to make decisions about their health.

Awareness of HPV as a Cause of Cervical Cancer

Table 1 highlights the awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer in the US adult population. Overall, 2468 (61.4%) respondents were aware that HPV causes cervical cancer. Awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer was higher among individuals who had a lot of trust in government health agencies (870 [70.2%]; 95% CI, 65.3%-75.2%) and among those who had a lot of trust in physicians for cancer information (1922 [64.8%]; 95% CI, 61.6%-67.9%). Conversely, awareness was higher among individuals who had little or no trust in religious organizations for information about cancer (1882 [63.8%]; 95% CI, 60.7%-66.9%). HPV awareness was also higher among individuals who were between the ages of 30 and 45 years (728 [70.7%]; 95% CI, 66.1%-75.2%), non-Hispanic White (1550 [65.4%]; 95% CI, 61.6%-69.2%), female (1682 [65.7%]; 95% CI, 62.5%-68.8%), homemakers (97 [69.0%]; 95% CI, 58.7%-79.4%), had a college or postgraduate education (1527 [74.3%]; 95% CI, 70.7%-77.8%), earned $75 000 or more (1204 [68.1%]; 95% CI, 64.2%-72.0%), had a personal cancer history (355 [66.8%]; 95% CI, 60.4%-72.8%), and did not use social media to make decisions about their health (1816 [63.4%]; 95% CI, 60.1%-66.7%).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined the association of trust in government health agencies, physicians, and religious organizations as sources for cancer information with awareness that HPV causes cervical cancer in the US adult population. We found that individuals who did not have a lot of trust in government health agencies and physicians were less likely to be aware of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer. In contrast, individuals who had little or no trust in religious organizations were more likely to be aware of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer.

These findings highlight the importance of trust in shaping HPV-related awareness and its implications for HPV vaccine uptake. Knowledge of HPV’s role in causing cancer is a key determinant of vaccination,47 and individuals who trust health care professionals have higher odds of vaccine uptake.38 The results from those studies suggest that trust facilitates progression from awareness to vaccine acceptance. Consistent with prior research, trust in physicians increases exposure to accurate health information and increases the likelihood of acting on vaccine recommendations, thereby increasing HPV vaccination uptake.38,43 Some populations may be at greater risk for vaccine refusal because of a lack of knowledge,48 and sources of information, such as reliance on nonmedical authority structures, may limit access to or acceptance of HPV-related information, particularly in contexts where sexual health topics are sensitive.49,50 These findings highlight the need for health promotion strategies that both strengthen trust in health care systems and engage community and religious leaders to improve HPV awareness. Integrating trusted community networks into health communication efforts may be critical to increasing vaccine uptake and reducing the burden of HPV-related cancers.

Our study found that approximately 62.0% of the US population who had heard of HPV were aware that HPV causes cervical cancer. In past years, awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer among US adults has decreased consistently, particularly from 2019 to 2022.10 The prevalence of the awareness of the causal link between HPV and cervical cancer in our study is consistent with findings in previous studies conducted among US adults10 and college students.51

Our study found that awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer was lower among individuals who did not have a lot of trust in government health agencies for cancer information, and combined with the decreasing trust in government health agencies observed in past years, this is a matter of concern. National and local messaging strategies must prioritize rebuilding trust through transparent, consistent, and culturally relevant health information. Improving public trust in government health agencies requires a multilayered approach centered on clear, timely, and synchronized communication across all levels of government, as inconsistent messaging can create confusion and undermine the public’s trust.52,53

To increase trust in health information, government agencies should communicate science-based guidance that acknowledges uncertainty and avoids politicization.54 Government agencies must also understand and respond to public expectations by identifying what people want from their institutions and tailoring communication to reflect the values and needs of different demographic groups. As it is important to acknowledge that demographic differences exist regarding levels of trust,55 communication strategies should be tailored to the population. Importantly, trust building must be ongoing and not just reactive; low public trust should prompt health agencies to reassess not only what they communicate but also how and through whom they communicate it.54 Efforts should focus on better communicating health information and engaging the public. This can be done through establishing a network of community health workers to counter misinformation about health agencies. An example in which such trust-building efforts have been successful was during the COVID-19 pandemic56 and the measles outbreak in Chicago in 2024.57,58 Cities successfully used community health worker networks, which serve as intermediaries between health agencies and the public to deliver timely, community-specific health messages, increasing vaccine uptake and public cooperation.56,57,58 These networks show that trust building is most effective when national communication efforts are paired with grassroots partnerships that reflect the values, languages, and concerns of the communities they serve.

Trust in health care professionals has been known to improve and encourage open patient–health care professional communication.24 Our study found an association between trust in physicians and awareness of HPV infection as a cause of cervical cancer. A survey of parents of children aged 10 to 12 years in Washington, DC, found that parents who trust their adolescents’ health care professional have approximately 2-fold higher odds of accepting the HPV vaccine for their adolescents.21 Another study based on a representative sample of US adults found that trust in physicians and clinics was associated with higher rates of accepting preventive services.22 With the well-documented influence of health care professionals’ recommendations on HPV vaccine uptake,38,59,60 health care professionals can tailor communication to patients’ needs while emphasizing HPV infection as cancer causing and the HPV vaccine’s role in cancer prevention.

While trust in government institutions and physicians remains limited among some population groups, this gap highlights important challenges in health communication. Notably, even among individuals who reported trust in government health agencies and physicians, 30.0% to 35.0% were unaware that HPV causes cervical cancer. This underscores the need for alternative, evidence-based approaches to disseminating health information that is more accessible and acceptable to diverse populations. Evidence suggests that community engagement is an effective strategy for improving health outcomes. A meta-analysis on vaccination promotion found that higher levels of community engagement were associated with increased vaccination uptake,61 while another study reported similar positive associations between community-based interventions and immunization rates.62 Their findings highlight the value of participatory, locally grounded approaches for enhancing awareness and behavior change. Public awareness campaigns also play a critical role in informing the general population. Studies showed that multichannel campaigns delivered through digital media, television, radio, and print can significantly improve health knowledge and behaviors. Greater exposure to campaign messaging has been associated with increased vaccination uptake and higher confidence in vaccine safety.63 Furthermore, community health outreach and the use of community health ambassadors have proved to be effective and trusted models for disseminating health messaging and building confidence in public health information.64

Religious organizations often engage with HPV-related science through moral and theological frameworks, in which religious beliefs about sexuality and morality shape how individuals interpret HPV risk and vaccination.65 Religious teachings on abstinence and sexual conduct shape perceptions of HPV risk, HPV vaccine knowledge, and vaccination, influencing vaccine attitudes and acceptance.50,66,67 Furthermore, religious affiliations or denominations and attendance are associated with HPV vaccine acceptance.50,68 The higher awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer among individuals who had little or no trust in religious organizations, combined with the lower awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer among individuals who have trust in religious organizations, may be due to religious organizations’ focus on abstinence among adolescents, which could limit discussions surrounding HPV infection and vaccine uptake.27,31,69 In studies among college women, religious belief was negatively associated with HPV vaccine attitude29 and HPV vaccine uptake, and this association was mediated by sexual activity, overlooking the importance of the HPV vaccine in cancer prevention.27

There is limited and mixed evidence on the relationship between religiosity and HPV awareness. One study reported that religious practice and the promotion of abstinence before marriage were associated with higher HPV knowledge,66 and another reported that high religious commitment was not associated with knowledge of HPV’s link to cervical cancer.69 Our findings are consistent with another study examining the association of religion with HPV vaccination awareness among women of an organized religious body in Utah.28 Also, consistent with the influence of religious beliefs, in a study in Florida, individuals identifying as evangelical Christians have lower HPV knowledge.70 A lack of HPV awareness may reflect a conscious decision to avoid seeking information, the belief that the HPV vaccine is a cause of promiscuity, or seeing information surrounding HPV as irrelevant.69 In 2022, 54.0% of US adults reported that religiosity is an important part of their daily lives.71 Thus, regardless of whether individuals consciously recognize religion as guiding their daily decisions, participation in organized religious practices may shape their lives in ways that influence HPV vaccine–related decisions and outcomes. Given the importance of religion to individuals who belong to religious organizations, organized religious bodies can play a critical role in promoting HPV awareness and HPV vaccination.31 Faith-based health programs can produce positive effects,72 and because religious leaders are trusted messengers holding significant morals within their communities,73 they can help provide tailored information that aligns with their community values.27,69 To that end, targeted interventions aimed at educating religious leaders on the causal link between HPV and cervical cancer are needed.

Other factors that could contribute to both mistrust and limited HPV knowledge include political ideology,74 exposure to online misinformation, and broader informational environments in which influential actors may discourage or undermine trust in scientific evidence and public health institutions.37,75,76 Targeted social media interventions can help address mistrust and poor HPV knowledge by correcting misinformation. For example, the “HPV Vaccination NOW” campaign demonstrated that delivering clear, credible, and audience-tailored online content can improve HPV knowledge and attitudes, thereby fostering greater trust in public health messaging and vaccine uptake.77

We found that men were less likely to be aware that HPV causes cervical cancer. This finding emphasizes the need for health promotion efforts adapted to men. As primary decision-makers in many households,78 men’s awareness of HPV as a cause of cancer is crucial for increasing awareness and knowledge about HPV-related cancers. This finding is consistent with other studies that found that being a man was significantly associated with lower awareness of the causal link between HPV and cervical cancer.51,79

Awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer was found to vary across racial and ethnic groups. We found that compared with non-Hispanic White individuals, non-Hispanic Asian individuals had lower odds of awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer. This finding is consistent with previous reports that concluded that Asian individuals had lower knowledge of HPV and the HPV vaccine80 and lower odds of knowledge of the causal link between HPV and cervical cancer.45 This highlights the importance of considering cultural norms when designing and implementing culturally tailored health education and promotion programs.

Individuals with a high school and post–high school education or some college education had lower awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer compared with those with a college or postgraduate degree. This is similar to previous studies that indicated that, over 10 years, awareness of HPV and the HPV vaccine is lower among individuals with a high school and post–high school or some college education than among those with a college or postgraduate degree.5 Awareness of HPV, HPV vaccine, and associated cancer increases with a higher level of education.45,80,81 Reducing lower HPV awareness gaps among the those with a high school and post–high school or some college education requires approaches that address overlapping socioeconomic, cultural, and educational barriers through more tailored and accessible strategies.5 While culturally adapted, low-literacy communication tools have proved effective, they have also significantly improved awareness by presenting information in engaging, relatable formats tailored to community characteristics and comprehension levels.82,83

Individuals with a personal history of cancer were more likely to be aware of the causal link between HPV and cervical cancer. This finding is consistent with another study among female teachers that found that individuals with a history of cervical cancer were aware of the causal link between HPV and cervical cancer.84 This higher awareness could result from the information that they have been exposed to after their cancer diagnosis.

Insights from this study suggest that increasing trust in government health agencies and physicians will provide the opportunity to raise HPV awareness in the population, leading to informed decisions on HPV vaccine uptake. Furthermore, approaches used by faith-based organizations could focus on the alignment with cancer prevention,73 appealing to their community’s health, family well-being, and stewardship values.

Limitations

One limitation of this study is that its cross-sectional nature prevents the establishment of causal relationships between trust in information sources for cancer and the awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer. Additionally, the survey response rate may introduce sample bias and affect the generalizability of our findings. However, sample weighting was applied to address this limitation, although this adjustment may not fully mitigate the potential biases introduced by nonresponse.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study of US adults reported for the first time, to our knowledge, the association of trust in government health agencies, religious organizations, and physicians with the awareness of HPV as a cause of cervical cancer. Given the critical role of HPV and HPV vaccination awareness in eliminating cervical cancer in the US, it is crucial to increase trust in government health agencies and physicians. Collaborative efforts with community leaders, faith organization leaders, health care professionals, and media outlets can further amplify the reach and effectiveness of health messages, ensuring the public views these agencies as credible and trustworthy.
