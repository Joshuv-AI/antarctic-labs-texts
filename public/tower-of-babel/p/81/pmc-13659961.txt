Acute effects of elevation training mask on anaerobic performance and post-exercise heart rate and blood lactate responses in young males: a randomized crossover study
Authors: Burcu Özdemir, Ümid Karlı, Selcen Korkmaz Eryılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Elevation Training Mask (ETM) has gained popularity as a wearable training device that increases respiratory workload by raising inspiratory and expiratory resistance during exercise. However, evidence regarding the acute effects of ETM use on anaerobic performance and post-exercise physiological responses is limited. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the acute effects of ETM use on anaerobic performance and post-exercise heart rate and blood lactate responses in young male athletes.MethodsFifteen trained young male athletes (age = 20.40 ± 1.68 years) participated in a randomized, counterbalanced, crossover design involving three experimental conditions: no-mask, ETM set at the manufacturer-defined 9,000 ft resistance setting, and ETM set at the manufacturer-defined 12,000 ft resistance setting. Peak power, average power, repeated-sprint power, fatigue index, heart rate (HR), rating of perceived exertion level (RPE), and blood lactate levels were evaluated.ResultsIn the 9,000 ft ETM and 12,000 ft ETM trials, peak power, average power, and repeated-sprint power values were found to be statistically significantly lower compared to the no-mask condition (p < 0.05). Furthermore, RPE values were statistically significantly higher, and blood lactate levels were statistically significantly lower in both ETM conditions (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThese findings demonstrated that the use of ETM during high-intensity exercise in young male athletes may result in lower peak, mean, and repeated-sprint power, higher rating of perceived exertion, and lower blood lactate levels. Therefore, the potential effects of ETM use on performance in sports activities where anaerobic performance is important should be considered, and the purpose of use should be carefully evaluated in line with performance requirements.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01892-6.Keywords: Anaerobic performance, Blood lactate, Elevation training mask, High-intensity exercise, Rating of perceived exertion, Respiratory resistance, Trained athletes, Wearable technology, Young male athletes

Introduction

The existing literature on sports performance emphasizes that it is influenced by many factors, including training load, recovery processes, and environmental conditions during performance [1, 2]. Among these factors, environmental conditions such as temperature, humidity, wind, and seasonal variables have been shown to influence training stimuli by affecting physiological responses to exercise and performance adaptations; this is also supported by findings from long-term follow-up studies [3]. Therefore, athletes and coaches use various training methods and ergogenic support strategies to optimize athletic performance [2, 4]. Among these approaches, altitude and hypoxic training methods are widely used due to their potential to stimulate physiological adaptations related to low oxygen partial pressure [5–7]. It is known that hypoxic stress at altitudes of approximately 2000 m and above can affect respiratory, cardiovascular, and metabolic responses, with these effects becoming more pronounced at altitudes above 3000 m [8]. Therefore, altitude training is frequently preferred to improve athletic performance by controlling the effects of these physiological stimuli and supporting the adaptation process [9–11]. However, the logistical and economic challenges of accessing actual altitude conditions have encouraged the development of alternative training methods and wearable technologies [6, 7, 12, 13]. Thus, the use of wearable technologies in sports science and rehabilitation has increased significantly. These systems are widely used to manage training load, performance evaluation, and recovery processes by enabling real-time monitoring of physiological (heart rate, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, etc.) and biomechanical (power output, movement speed, acceleration, etc.) data [14–16].

Among these technologies, the Elevation Training Mask (ETM) is widely used by athletes, with the claim that it simulates different altitudes through an adjustable valve system that increases inspiratory and expiratory airflow resistance [4, 6, 7, 12, 17, 18]. The widespread use of ETM by a broad range of users, from recreational athletes to elite sports enthusiasts, has increased scientific interest in the device’s effects on performance. However, current evidence suggests that ETM does not create hypobaric hypoxia as seen at actual altitude, but rather increases respiratory workload by creating mechanical respiratory resistance. Therefore, it is suggested that the effects of ETM should be evaluated from the perspective of respiratory mechanical stress rather than hypoxic stimulation [4, 6, 7, 12, 17–23]. In the literature, the chronic [4, 13, 17, 24] and acute [6, 18, 21, 25–28] effects of ETM have been examined in various studies. Studies evaluating acute effects have mostly focused on anaerobic performance and some physiological variables [20, 27, 28]. Jung et al. [21] reported that oxygen saturation was lower with ETM use, while systolic blood pressure, perceived exertion, and recovery heart rate were higher than in the no-mask condition.

To our knowledge, only one study has simultaneously examined the acute effects of ETM on both exercise performance and post-exercise physiological responses [21]. Although several previous studies have investigated the acute effects of ETM use, evidence on its effects during repeated high-intensity anaerobic exercise remains limited. Previous studies have primarily focused on performance outcomes; however, assessing exercise performance alongside post-exercise physiological responses may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the acute responses to ETM use. Comparing different ETM resistance settings and monitoring post-exercise heart rate and blood lactate level responses at multiple recovery time points may further expand the current body of knowledge in this area [6, 12, 21, 25, 26, 29]. Considering that age- and sex-related physiological differences may affect exercise performance and responses to exercise [30, 31], a homogeneous group of young male athletes was chosen for this study. Accordingly, this study aimed to evaluate the acute effects of ETM use, as a wearable respiratory resistance device, on anaerobic performance, exercise heart rate, rating of perceived exertion, and post-exercise heart rate and blood lactate level responses in young males.

Methods

Subjects

Fifteen trained young male athletes voluntarily participated in this study. The sample size was determined a priori using G*Power 3.1.9.7 software [32]. Since one of the main physiological outcomes of the study was post-exercise blood lactate level, the effect size was calculated based on post-exercise blood lactate data reported by Jagim et al. [26], who examined the acute effects of using an elevation training mask during high-intensity exercise. Based on the reported mean and standard deviation values for the ETM and no-mask conditions, Cohen’s d was calculated as 1.01 and converted to Cohen’s f (f = 0.50). A repeated-measures ANOVA (within-subjects) F-test was selected with an alpha level of 0.05, statistical power of 0.90, three measurements, and an assumed correlation of 0.50 among repeated measures. The analysis indicated a minimum required sample size of 11 participants. To account for potential participant attrition, 16 participants were initially recruited; however, because one participant did not complete the protocol, analyses were conducted on fifteen participants.

The inclusion criteria of this study are as follows: (1) not having any health problems, (2) being active in running-based sports branches and training regularly, and (3) between 6 and 8 h per week.

The exclusion criteria of this study are as follows: (1) presence of any musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, or metabolic disorder, (2) current injury or illness that could affect performance, (3) use of any medication or ergogenic aid affecting physiological responses, and (4) irregular training status or failure to meet the weekly training requirement.

Prior to participation, all participants were informed about the purpose and procedures of the study and provided voluntary written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the B.A.I.B.U. Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 2021/04). Study design

In this study, a randomized crossover experimental design comprising two stages and four laboratory visits (B.A.I.B.U.; Exercise and Physiology Laboratory) was used. In the first stage (Visit 1), participants’ height, body weight, and body fat percentage were measured as baseline characteristics. In the second stage (Visits 2–4), participants completed the RAST protocol [33] under three experimental conditions in a randomized, counter-balanced order [34]: no-mask, ETM set at 9,000 ft, and ETM set at 12,000 ft. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three testing sequences, with five participants per sequence. Prior to the experimental trials, all participants completed at least two familiarization sessions with the RAST protocol under ETM conditions and with no mask to minimize potential learning effects. In addition, a minimum 48-hour interval was provided between testing sessions, consistent with previous studies reporting adequate recovery following repeated-sprint and high-intensity exercise protocols [35, 36].

Participants were instructed to maintain their usual dietary habits throughout the study period; avoid caffeine, alcohol, and strenuous physical activity for at least 24 h before each test session; maintain their normal sleep patterns before testing; and attend each session under similar daily conditions. Verbal confirmation of compliance was obtained before each trial. RAST test trials were conducted in the afternoon (16:30–19:00) to control for the effects of potential circadian rhythms. It has been reported that conducting all assessments within the same time frame helps to minimize time-related variability between test sessions and reduce potential temporal confounding factors [37]. The gym’s physical environment was consistent, with an altitude of 888 m, a temperature of 22–24 °C, a humidity of 75–79%, and a wooden parquet floor. The ETM used in the present study was the Elevation Training Mask 2.0 (Training Mask, Inc., Cadillac, MI, USA). The 9,000 ft and 12,000 ft settings used in the present study correspond to the manufacturer’s adjustable resistance levels. These settings were used to impose different levels of airflow resistance on the device. The dead-space volume of the elevation training mask was not directly quantified in the present study, and its potential influence on ventilatory dynamics and carbon dioxide rebreathing was not specifically assessed. Participants used the same ETM model and size-adjusted configuration across all testing sessions to ensure consistency of exposure. In each experimental trial, power (W), heart rate (bpm), blood lactate level (mmol·L⁻¹), and rating of perceived exertion (RPE) were assessed. Following a standardized warm-up, participants completed the RAST protocol under their assigned conditions. The timing of the measurements and the details of the experimental procedure are presented in Fig. 1.

Test procedure stages

Measurements

Anthropometric measurements (height, body weight, and body fat percentage)

Participants’ height and body weight were measured using a Seca 700 measuring device (Medical Scales and Measuring Systems, Hamburg, Germany), with an accuracy of ± 0.1 cm for height and ± 0.1 kg for weight. Participants’ body composition was measured using a bioelectrical impedance analyzer (Tanita BC-418 Segmental Body Composition Analyzer, Tanita Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

RAST (running-based anaerobic sprint test)

The RAST test is based on the assessment of six repeated-sprint performances, each separated by 10-second rest intervals, performed over a distance of 35 m [33]. Before the test, participants performed a standardized warm-up protocol (5 min of slow-paced running, 10 knee bends, and 10 × 10-meter ascents). A rest period of 3–5 min was provided to relieve fatigue resulting from warming up. Depending on the test condition, ETM was worn at this stage, and the RAST test was initiated. In the RAST test, running times were recorded in seconds using photocells at the start and finish lines of the 35-meter course. The participant to be measured waited at the starting point of the test area (just behind the photocell). When the participants felt ready, they completed 35 m sprint runs at maximum performance, and the running times were recorded. Based on calculations using these test measurement results, the following values were obtained: repeated-sprint power (W), peak power (W), average power (W), and fatigue index (W/sn). Additionally, total work output (J), representing the cumulative external mechanical work performed during the six RAST sprints, was calculated and included as an additional performance variable.

Heart rate

Heart rate was continuously recorded throughout each test session using a Polar RS400 heart rate monitor. Resting heart rate was determined as the lowest value measured during a 5-minute period of supine rest prior to the exercise protocol. Heart rate was recorded immediately after each 35-meter sprint during the RAST protocol and at the 1st, 5th, 10th, 15th, and 20th minutes post-exercise. Participants remained seated throughout the post-exercise measurement period.

Blood lactate level

Resting blood lactate levels were measured at rest before the exercise protocol and at the 1st, 5th, 10th, 15th, and 20th minutes after completion of the RAST test. Capillary blood samples (0.2 µL) were collected from the fingertip and analyzed using a Lactate Scout 4 analyzer (SensLab GmbH, Leipzig, Germany). Participants remained seated throughout the post-exercise measurement period. In addition to absolute blood lactate levels, the peak post-exercise blood lactate level, normalized to total work output (mmol·kJ⁻¹), was calculated.

Rating of perceived exertion

The rating of perceived exertion was measured immediately after the RAST test by the participant’s subjective, verbal expression of the fatigue felt during the exercise on a scale of nothing (6), very very little (7–8), very little (9–10), easy (11–12), a little difficult (13–14), difficult (15–16), very difficult (17–18), very very difficult (19), exhausted (20) [38].

To assess overall perceived effort for each condition, RPE was recorded only once, immediately after completion of the entire RAST protocol. Separate measurements were not taken after each sprint to avoid disrupting the standard 10-second recovery interval.

Statistical analysis

The arithmetic means and standard deviations of all the data presented in the study were calculated. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality, and Levene’s test was used to assess homogeneity of variances. One-way repeated-measures analysis of variance was used to compare peak power, average power, total work output, fatigue index, RPE, and normalized peak blood lactate values across trials (no-mask, 9,000 ft ETM, and 12,000 ft ETM). A Bonferroni correction was used for post hoc analysis. A repeated-measures two-way analysis of variance was employed to analyze repeated-sprint power and HR values (3 trials × 6 trials) and blood lactate and HR values after the RAST test (3 trials × 5 trials). Sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test. When the assumption of sphericity was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser-corrected results together with the corresponding epsilon estimates were reported. Detailed sphericity statistics are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons were used for post hoc analyses. Mean differences and 95% confidence intervals were reported where relevant. The partial eta-squared (η²) effect size measure was used to interpret the analysis results. The eta-squared values expressed as effect size are classified as a small effect when η² ≥ 0.01, a medium effect when η² ≥ 0.06, and a large effect when η² ≥ 0.14 [39]. To examine whether the randomized crossover sequence influenced the primary study outcomes, additional mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVAs were performed. Peak power was selected as the primary performance outcome, whereas peak post-exercise blood lactate level was selected as the primary physiological outcome. The testing order was included as a between-subjects factor to evaluate potential sequence effects and trial × order interactions (Supplementary Table S2). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). This study was designed and reported in accordance with the CONSORT reporting guidelines for randomized crossover trials recommended by the EQUATOR Network.

Results

Power values

A significant decrease was observed in peak and average power values in the ETM trials compared to the no-mask condition. Peak power was significantly lower in both the 9,000 ft ETM (p = 0.028) and 12,000 ft ETM (p = 0.001) conditions, while average power was significantly lower in both ETM conditions (both p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between the 9,000 ft ETM and 12,000 ft ETM conditions for either peak power (p = 1.000) or average power (p = 0.295). Similarly, total work output was lower in both ETM conditions than in the no-mask condition (both p < 0.001), with no significant difference between the two ETM conditions (p = 0.263). Fatigue index did not differ significantly among trials (p = 0.358) (Table 2; Fig. 2). Pairwise comparisons, mean differences, and 95% confidence intervals are presented in Table 3.

Additional analyses indicated that the randomized testing sequence did not significantly influence peak power responses across experimental conditions (order effect: p = 0.761; trial × order interaction: p = 0.842) (Supplementary Table S2).

Rating of perceived exertion

A significant main effect of the trial was observed for the rating of perceived exertion (Table 2). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons indicated significantly higher RPE values in the 9,000 ft ETM (p = 0.025) and 12,000 ft ETM (p = 0.011) conditions compared with the no-mask condition. However, no statistically significant difference was observed between the two ETM conditions (p = 0.721) (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Blood lactate level

Post-exercise blood lactate levels showed significant main effects of trial and time, as well as a significant trial × time interaction (all p < 0.001) (Table 4). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons revealed that overall blood lactate levels were significantly higher in the no-mask condition than in the 9,000 ft ETM (p < 0.001) and 12,000 ft ETM (p = 0.005) conditions, whereas no significant difference was observed between the two ETM conditions (p = 0.623) (Table 5).

Further analyses showed that during post-exercise, blood lactate levels were significantly higher in the no-mask condition than in the ETM conditions at specific time points (Fig. 4). Regardless of condition, post-exercise blood lactate levels peaked at the 5th minute and then gradually decreased throughout recovery. However, no significant differences were observed in the time-dependent changes in blood lactate levels between conditions following pairwise comparisons. Peak blood lactate levels were also re-evaluated after normalization to total work output. This analysis showed that a significant difference remained only between the no-mask and 9,000 ft ETM conditions (p = 0.002), whereas no significant differences were observed between the no-mask and 12,000 ft ETM conditions (p = 0.133) or between the two ETM conditions (p = 0.647) (Tables 2 and 3).

Additional analyses showed that the randomized crossover sequence did not significantly affect peak post-exercise blood lactate levels (order effect: p = 0.630; trial × order interaction: p = 0.370) (Supplementary Table S2).

Heart rate

During repeated sprints, heart rate showed a significant main effect of time (p < 0.001); however, neither the main effect of trial (p = 0.273) nor the trial × time interaction (p = 0.460) reached statistical significance (Table 4). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons revealed no significant differences between the no-mask and 9,000 ft ETM (p = 0.484), the no-mask and 12,000 ft ETM (p = 1.000), or the two ETM conditions (p = 0.503) (Table 5). Heart rate gradually increased throughout successive sprint repetitions, independent of ETM use (Fig. 3).

Similarly, post-exercise heart rate showed a significant main effect of time (p < 0.001); whereas neither the main effect of trial (p = 0.996) nor the trial × time interaction (p = 0.221) was statistically significant (Table 4). Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons revealed no significant differences between any trial conditions (all p ≥ 0.484) (Table 5). Heart rate gradually decreased during the post-exercise recovery period regardless of ETM use (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The main findings of this study demonstrate that the use of ETM during anaerobic exercise in trained young male athletes may decrease peak, mean, and repeated-sprint power, increase the rating of perceived exertion, and lower post-exercise blood lactate levels. In contrast, no significant difference was found in heart rate responses between conditions during exercise or post-exercise during recovery. Taken together, these findings suggest that the respiratory workload induced by ETM may negatively impact high-intensity anaerobic exercise performance in the current sample under the conditions applied in this study, affecting perceptual responses but not heart rate responses to the same extent.

Power values

Peak power, average power, and repeated-sprint power values measured in the RAST test decreased in the 9,000 ft ETM and 12,000 ft ETM trials compared to the no-mask condition. These findings are consistent with previous studies that reported similar performance decreases associated with the use of ETM [6, 18, 19, 21, 25–27, 40]. No significant difference was observed between the two ETM conditions. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as the relatively small sample size may have limited the detection of small differences between the conditions.

In the literature, it has been reported that ETM use can be accompanied by changes in respiratory condition during exercise [17, 19], and that these changes can affect performance outputs and various physiological responses during high-intensity exercise [6, 19, 41–43]. Accordingly, the lower power outputs observed under ETM conditions in the present study may be related to these different responses that occur during exercise depending on device use. Similarly, the significantly lower total work output (J) observed under ETM conditions indicates that the performance decline affects not only instantaneous power output but also the total work output performed during exercise. Consistent with these findings, previous studies have also reported a decrease in the total work output that can be sustained during exercise following ETM use [18, 19, 25].

Furthermore, previous studies have suggested that ETM-related changes during exercise may influence exercise performance and physiological responses through mechanisms such as increased respiratory load, altered ventilatory responses, CO₂ rebreathing, and different perceptual responses [42, 44–51]. However, because no ventilatory parameters or other physiological measurements (oxygen saturation, respiratory muscle activity, respiratory pressures, or gas exchange, e.g.) were performed in the present study to directly evaluate these mechanisms, the underlying reasons for the performance declines observed under ETM conditions cannot be definitively explained, and these mechanisms should only be considered as possible explanations [42, 44–51].

Devereux et al. [24], in their study examining the chronic effects of high-intensity interval training using ETM (2000 m) on anaerobic power performance, did not report any additional advantage in power values compared to the no-mask condition. In contrast, in the present study, a decrease in both peak power and average power values was observed during acute use of ETM. These differences suggest that the effects of ETM use on exercise performance may vary with duration of use. Similarly, recently published studies have shown that the effects of ETM use on physiological and performance responses to exercise are inconsistent. Fernández-Lázaro et al. [22] reported some respiratory adaptations associated with long-term ETM use, whereas Jerves-Donoso et al. [23] found that ETM use did not provide a significant advantage in performance or hematological biomarkers. When these findings are considered together, it is thought that responses associated with ETM use may differ depending on the exercise model, the duration of application, and the outcome variables evaluated.

When the participant’s fatigue index values were analyzed, the use of ETM did not result in a difference between the trials. The fatigue index is a value that reflects the ability to maintain performance and the rate of power decline during repeated sprints. In the present study, although ETM use reduced peak and average power values, it did not affect the fatigue index. This finding indicates that ETM reduced absolute power output but did not alter the relative power loss between sprints. Therefore, the similarity of the rate of performance loss across conditions may explain the lack of a significant difference in the fatigue index. Veiga et al. [27] concluded that ETM (12,000 ft) did not alter fatigue index values. To our knowledge, no other study in the literature has examined the acute effects of ETM on the fatigue index during anaerobic exercise.

Rating of perceived exertion

When participants’ RPE scores were analyzed, both ETM conditions resulted in significantly higher scores compared to the no-mask condition. Interestingly, although power outputs were lower under ETM conditions, RPE values were higher. This suggests that participants perceived the exercise as more difficult despite producing less mechanical work, indicating that ETM may reduce exercise tolerance.

This finding is consistent with previous studies reporting increased rating of perceived exertion levels after ETM use [12, 18, 21, 27, 40, 52]. In addition, previous studies have suggested that changes in respiratory conditions may alter breathing perception during exercise and, consequently, influence responses to rating of perceived exertion [42, 45, 46, 53]. Furthermore, the literature reports that using face masks during exercise may affect individuals’ perceived responses to exercise [26, 54]. Accordingly, the higher RPE values observed under ETM conditions in the present study, when considered alongside previous findings, suggest that ETM may have different effects on perceived effort responses [17, 55, 56]. Previous studies have also shown that ETM use is associated with increased dyspnea and perceived exertion during acute exercise [12, 17, 19, 40]. In the current study, higher RPE values were observed in both ETM conditions compared with the no-mask condition, suggesting that ETM use may increase perceived exertion during exercise.

However, Maspero and Smith [57] reported that ETM (12,000 ft) conditions did not affect RPE values in their study examining the acute effects of ETM on RPE during exercise. While Maspero and Smith [57] used a fixed walking-and-running protocol with healthy subjects, the present study involved trained athletes performing maximal-effort sprints in the RAST test. These differences in fitness status, exercise protocol, and intensity may partly explain the similar rating of perceived exertion levels in the ETM and no-mask conditions.

Blood lactate level

ETM caused significant differences in blood lactate levels across trials and over time. The lower blood lactate levels observed at 9,000 ft and 12,000 ft ETM conditions should be considered alongside the performance decline during these trials. Since participants produced lower total work output under ETM conditions, the overall metabolic demand of exercise may also have been reduced. Given that lactate production is closely related to exercise intensity, lower lactate levels may reflect a lower anaerobic energy requirement due to reduced external work output [58–61]. This finding, when considered alongside similar lactate level responses reported in previous ETM studies, suggests that the differences observed in the current study may be related to conditions that arise during ETM use [26, 62, 63]. It should also be considered that changes in respiratory conditions related to ETM use may have influenced exercise tolerance during high-intensity exercise, thereby contributing to the differences observed in metabolic responses.

Therefore, to assess whether the observed differences could be explained solely by decreased performance outputs, additional analyses were conducted by normalizing peak blood lactate levels to total work output. Normalized analyses showed that some differences between conditions persisted, suggesting that the observed responses in lactate levels could not be fully explained by the decrease in total work output alone. However, because more comprehensive metabolic analyses based on workload were not performed in this study, the underlying mechanisms of the observed responses in lactate levels cannot be directly determined. Furthermore, blood lactate levels reflect not only lactate production but also the balance between formation and clearance processes [63]. Therefore, the lower blood lactate levels observed under ETM conditions in the present study may not be fully explained by decreased metabolic contribution or lower external work output alone, and changes in the balance between lactate formation and removal could also theoretically contribute to these responses. However, because no measurements were made in the present study that could directly evaluate lactate clearance mechanisms, this interpretation remains speculative.

In all three time-dependent trials, the peak blood lactate level was reached during post-exercise at the 5th minute. In previous studies, peak blood lactate levels were reported between the 3rd and 8th minutes, supporting the consistency of the present study’s results [33, 64–71]. Blood lactate levels decreased after the 5th minute in all trials. In a study examining the acute effects of ETM (9,000 ft) on resistance exercise and sprint performance, post-exercise blood lactate levels were lower under ETM conditions [26]. Similarly, other studies [6, 25, 29] have reported lower blood lactate levels associated with ETM application.

However, some studies show results that contrast with our findings. Maspero & Smith [57] studied the effects of ETM (12,000 ft) and Barbieri et al. [72] ETM (15,000 ft) on blood lactate levels and found no difference. These differences may be due to exercise protocols and participant characteristics. Maspero & Smith [57], using a constant walking/running protocol with healthy individuals, and Barbieri et al. [72], employing a cycling-to-exhaustion protocol with recreationally active males and females, found no significant changes in blood lactate levels following ETM use. These discrepancies may be attributed to differences in exercise protocols (e.g., submaximal vs. maximal effort), participant characteristics (e.g., fitness level, training frequency), movement modalities (e.g., sprinting vs. cycling), ETM resistance levels, and potential hormonal influences, particularly in female participants.

Heart rate

During repeated-sprint performance on the RAST test, HR increased over time across all conditions, regardless of ETM use. Heart rate increases in parallel with exercise intensity as a physiological response to the organism’s elevated metabolic demands. During high-intensity exercise, this increase is a normal physiological response that meets the increased oxygen and energy requirements of working muscles, facilitates the removal of metabolic by-products, and increases cardiac output [73]. In line with this information, many studies in the literature also report that HR, independent of ETM, is similar across conditions, as observed in the present study [18, 20, 21, 27].

During the post-exercise period, a time-dependent decrease in HR was observed across trials, regardless of the ETM, reflecting reduced metabolic demands. The organism tries to reestablish homeostasis in the post-exercise recovery period. Therefore, a time-dependent decrease in HR values after exercise is a usual mechanism for maintaining homeostasis [73]. These findings suggest that, under the conditions of the present study, no detectable differences between conditions were observed in post-exercise cardiovascular recovery responses.

However, in contrast to the current study, Jung et al. [21] reported that ETM (1,829 m) use significantly increased HR values in the post-exercise period and could delay cardiac autonomic recovery. The differences between the current study and the aforementioned study may stem from variations in exercise protocols, participant profiles, and ETM settings. Unlike our study, which used the RAST test requiring maximum effort, the aforementioned study utilized a 10-minute submaximal test protocol on a bicycle ergometer with maximum oxygen uptake (VO2max) (50% and 70%). These differences in test protocols, variations in participant age and physical activity levels, and the differing ETM settings across studies may explain the observed differences in heart rate responses. Furthermore, the inclusion of female participants may have contributed to this difference by influencing their hormonal profiles and physiological responses to exercise.

Limitations of the study

The study has several limitations. First, although supported by prior power analysis, the relatively small sample size (n = 15) may limit the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, only trained young male participants were included in the study, and the results cannot be directly generalized to female athletes or other populations. The dead space volume of ETM was not directly measured. Therefore, some of the observed physiological responses may have been influenced by hypercapnic factors, such as increased inspiratory and expiratory resistance, altered respiratory mechanics, increased dead-space volume, and possible carbon dioxide rebreathing associated with ETM use [43]. Given that ETM does not induce true hypobaric or normobaric hypoxia and primarily provides mechanical respiratory resistance, and because variables such as oxygen saturation, ventilation parameters, and respiratory muscle function were not directly assessed during ETM use, the underlying physiological mechanisms for the observed decrease in performance could not be directly determined within the scope of this study. Furthermore, the absence of a placebo (sham mask) condition limits the ability to determine whether the observed effects are attributable solely to ETM’s physiological effects or to expectations, discomfort, or psychological factors related to device use. Another limitation is that the study uses an experimental design that evaluates only acute effects. Therefore, the current findings cannot be directly generalized to potential long-term training adaptations related to ETM use. Finally, recovery was assessed only through heart rate and blood lactate levels. Thus, the current findings reflect only certain physiological components of the recovery process, not all aspects. Future studies using placebo-controlled designs, ensuring equipment standardization, directly monitoring oxygenation and respiratory parameters, evaluating respiratory muscle function, and including neuromuscular and performance-based recovery indicators will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the acute effects of ETM use.

Conclusions

Our findings showed that, in the current sample, the use of 9,000 ft and 12,000 ft ETM during the RAST protocol resulted in lower power outputs and higher rating of perceived exertion compared to the no-mask condition. These findings suggest that the mechanical respiratory resistance generated by ETM may negatively impact acute repeated-sprint performance under the conditions examined in this study. Furthermore, post-exercise blood lactate levels were lower under ETM conditions. Given the reduction in power output, this finding may be related to lower external work output and, consequently, reduced metabolic demand under the ETM conditions. In conclusion, the use of ETM may lower anaerobic performance indicators and increase the rating of perceived exertion under the conditions of this study. Therefore, the potential effects of ETM use on high-intensity, repeated-sprint performance should be considered and evaluated in accordance with its intended use.

Practical application

From a practical application, coaches and athletes should consider that acute ETM use during high-intensity exercise may increase perceived effort while reducing anaerobic power output and total work output. Therefore, ETM use should be carefully evaluated during training sessions in which maintaining maximal anaerobic performance or repeated-sprint capacity is a priority.
