Costs of Care for Neonates with Hypoxic-Ischemic Encephalopathy Treated with Therapeutic Hypothermia and Validation of the Canadian Neonatal Network Costing Algorithm

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTherapeutic hypothermia (TH) is the standard treatment for neonates with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE). Validated cost estimates are required to better evaluate the cost-effectiveness of additional interventions during TH. The goal of this study is to identify clinical factors associated with costs of care and validate the Canadian Neonatal Network (CNN) costing algorithm for neonates with HIE receiving TH.Study designSingle-center retrospective cohort study among neonates with HIE treated with TH in a tertiary neonatal intensive care unit from 2016 to 2018. Actual costs per patient were obtained from the hospital cost accounting system, Coût par Parcours de Soinset de Services, and linked to patient data. Estimated costs per patient were calculated using the CNN case-costing algorithm. Neonates were grouped into cost tertiles to identify characteristics of high resource users. Comparisons of actual costs and estimated costs were performed across 8 cost domains.ResultsAmong 98 neonates treated with TH, 77 (79%) had mild-moderate HIE and 21 (21%) had severe HIE on admission. Factors associated with higher costs were severity of HIE and other markers of disease severity (seizures, mechanical ventilation, length of stay, and presence of brain injury on magnetic resonance imaging). Total median cost per neonate was $24,692 [IQR: $17,466; $39,234], which highly correlated with the CNN algorithm (median: $28 558 [IQR: $23 644; $40 704]) (R = 0.93, P < .01). The mean difference in total costs between estimates was $5339 (95% CI: $2697, $7981). There was a moderate-to-strong correlation between actual and estimated costs in 5/8 cost domains (R range: 0.68-0.98).ConclusionsSeverity of HIE and other markers of disease severity were associated with higher hospital costs. The CNN costing algorithm cost estimates for neonates with HIE treated with TH highly correlate with actual costs but overestimates the costs by approximately 15%.

Neonates with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) are at high risk of mortality and long-term neurodevelopmental impairments.1,2 Therapeutic hypothermia (TH) has improved outcomes of eligible neonates significantly and has become a standard of care in high-income countries.3,4 The number of neonates with HIE and are treated with TH is increasing each year in Canada.5 Although these neonates account for only 5% of admissions in Level 3 neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), they often present with associated multiorgan failure requiring intensive treatments from dedicated and trained teams with specialized equipment.6,7 The median cost estimates for care during NICU admission for a neonate with HIE treated with TH have ranged from $25 000 to $60 000 depending on the country and unit, but no studies have included Canada to date.8, 9, 10 In preterm infants, factors associated with higher costs include clinical status on admission (measured via acuity and risk score), interventions (such as use of vasopressors, mechanical ventilation), and length of stay, yet less is known about neonates receiving TH.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17

Accurate and accessible estimates of inpatient costs can facilitate the evaluation of economic impacts of new interventions. Challenges in estimating hospital costs include variations in methodology (eg, variations in input costs), differences in how hospitals categorize costs and limited access of such data by researchers and clinicians.18 These variations in costing methodologies can hinder comparisons between hospitals and reduce generalizability for population-based estimates. Recently, the Canadian Neonatal Network (CNN) developed a costing algorithm to estimate inpatient costs for preterm neonates admitted to Canadian NICUs based on resource use data items in the CNN database.19,20 The current study first aimed to characterize clinical factors associated with higher NICU costs of care for neonates with HIE treated with TH, and second, to validate the CNN costing algorithm for neonates with HIE treated with TH using hospital costing data.

Methods

Study Design and Eligibility Criteria

This retrospective cohort study included neonates admitted for HIE and treated with TH in a 52-bed tertiary NICU between April 1, 2016, and December 31, 2018. The Montreal Children's Hospital admits an average of 800 neonates per year (inborn or outborn), has all pediatric and surgical consultants available on site, and is comparable with other large Canadian NICUs in staffing and structure of care. Diagnosis of HIE was based on the presence of HIE with documented evidence of fetal distress (ie, cord prolapse, uterine rupture, reduced fetal movements, abruption) and neonatal distress (≥1 of the following: 10-minute Apgar of ≤5, mechanical ventilation or resuscitation for ≥10 minutes, cord or postnatal pH of ≤7.00 within 60 minutes of birth or base excess of ≤−16 mmol/L within 60 minutes of birth). The severity of HIE was classified as mild, moderate, or severe using the modified Sarnat score.21 The greatest severity of HIE before initiation of TH was recorded. We defined markers of disease severity as dysfunctions as the following: hypotension, cardiac dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, renal failure, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, and hepatic dysfunction. We excluded neonates born at <36 weeks gestational age or with a birthweight of <1800 g, and with major congenital anomalies. The study used a convenience sample size based on data availability. This project was approved by the McGill University Health Centre Research Ethics Board.

Actual Costs and CNN Costing Algorithm

Costs in this study reflected the perspective of the public healthcare system and included only direct in-patient medical costs. Societal costs, such as lost productivity for parents and out-of-pocket expenses for parents, were not considered. Actual costs per patient were retrieved from the local cost accounting system: Coût par Parcours de Soins et de Services (CPSS). The CPSS is a harmonized accounting software used in the Province of Quebec that combines clinical information on procedures and interventions (pharmacy, radiology, and laboratory), human resources and financial data during every patient's hospital stay.22 Each costing item follows standard provincial reporting and is grouped into 8 cost domains (Supplementary Table 1, online; available at www.jpeds.com).23 Physician billing costs were based on the Régie de l’Assurance Maladie du Québec billing handbook, and vary according to weekday/weekend visit.24

The CNN costing algorithm was developed to match unit costs with resource use in the CNN database and generate patient-specific estimates. A detailed description of the costing algorithm has been published previously.19 In summary, any consumed resource that was collected in the database was attributed a cost (based on 2017 Ontario reference costs), and was then assigned to one of the 8 cost domains defined in Supplementary Table 1, online; available at www.jpeds.com.

Both the actual (CPSS) and the estimated (CNN algorithm) costs were linked for each neonate. Eight cost domains were comparable between the 2 costing systems: nursing, physician (not directly included in CPSS, but was calculated using physician billing), respiratory, transfusion, procedures, imaging, medications, and indirect (Supplementary Table 1, online; available at www.jpeds.com). CPSS non-nursing costs were pooled with indirect costs to cover all variables defined by the CNN algorithm. Procedure costs included surgical interventions and related billing items offered during a hospital stay. Laboratory test costs were not directly estimated in the CNN algorithm (included within indirect rather than as a separate cost domain), but were included in the CPSS (as a separate cost domain and represented <1% of hospital costs). Therefore, these costs were used to calculate the total costs for each system, but were not included in our cost domain analyses. Because the CNN costs were derived from the Mount Sinai Hospital in Ontario, costs from the CNN algorithm were adjusted to CPSS using the Costs of a Standard Hospital Stay (Canadian Institute for Health Information) indices (McGill University Health Center/Mount Sinai Hospital) (Supplementary Table 2, online; available at www.jpeds.com).25,26 All costs were then adjusted to reflect the 2023 market prices estimated using consumer price indices from Statistics Canada and were reported in Canadian dollars.22,27

Data Collection and Clinical Variable Definitions

Neonatal characteristics and outcome data were obtained from the local CNN database and chart review. Information on the NICU stay of each neonate was entered electronically by trained abstractors into a data entry program with built-in error checking according to a standard protocol, which has previously shown high reliability and internal consistency.28,29 Neonatal characteristics included: mode of delivery, outborn status, gestational age, birthweight, sex, 5-minute Apgar score, 10-minute Apgar score, HIE severity, Score for Neonatal Acute Physiology with Perinatal Extension-II (SNAP-II), multiorgan failure (seizure, hypotension, cardiac dysfunction, persistent pulmonary hypertension, renal failure, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, and hepatic dysfunction), and interventions during hospitalizations (mechanical ventilation, surfactant and total days on mechanical ventilation).30 The severity of the brain injury on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was scored using the classification described by Barkovich et al: a score of 0 for no brain injury, a score of 1 for basal ganglia injury pattern, a score of 2 for watershed injury pattern, and a score of 3-4 for severe extensive brain injury, with 4 corresponding to near-total injury pattern.31

Statistical Analysis

To describe the characteristics of higher cost admissions, neonates were grouped into tertiles of total actual costs. Due to sample size considerations, a supplemental comparison of total and daily costs across HIE severity pooled mild and moderate neonates into one category (with <3% of mild neonates receiving TH). Descriptive statistics were used to compare characteristics, interventions and outcomes. Unadjusted comparisons were made using the Pearson χ2 test for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables. Relative proportion of cost domains (mean costs of each domain divided by total costs) were compared across the tertiles. Scatter plots and Pearson correlation coefficients (R) were used to measure the correlation between actual and CNN estimates for both total and specific domain costs. Mean differences with corresponding 95% CIs were used to estimate variations between actual and CNN total and specific domain costs. A 2-sided P value of <.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

There were 193 neonates admitted with HIE during the study period and 95 were excluded (14 were <36 weeks gestational age or weighed <1800 g and 81 did not receive TH) (Supplementary Figure 1, online; available at www.jpeds.com). Among the 81 neonates who did not receive TH, 88% (71) had mild HIE and 10 (12%) had moderate or severe HIE but were admitted at >6 hours after birth. The final study sample included 98 neonates, of whom 2 had mild HIE, 75 had moderate HIE, and 21 had severe HIE.

Overall, the median total cost was $24 692 (IQR, $17 466-$39 234). A comparison of neonatal characteristics across cost tertiles is presented in Table I. The median cost in tertile 1 (lowest cost) was $16 421 (IQR, $13 858-$17 290), in tertile 2 was $24 692 (IQR, $22 232-$27 163), and in tertile 3 (highest cost) was $44 424 (IQR, $39 384-$61 453). One-third of neonates in tertile 3 had severe HIE, compared with 12% in tertile 2 and 15% in tertile 1. Overall, high-cost neonates more frequently had a SNAP-II score of >20, experienced seizures during hospitalization, presented with multiorgan failure (hypotension and/or pulmonary hypertension), and required mechanical ventilation. High-cost neonates had longer median length of NICU stay (17 days vs 10-11 days) and higher rates (48% vs 9%-25%) and more severe types of brain injuries on MRI when compared with mild- or moderate-cost neonates. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study among all neonates with HIE treated with TH in a single Level 3 NICU in Quebec, we found that severity of illness, multiorgan failure (hypotension, cardiac dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, and thrombocytopenia), and interventions (surfactant use and mechanical ventilation) correlated with higher resource use and costs during hospitalization. Using patient-level costing data (obtained from hospital accounting software), we evaluated the CNN costing algorithm among neonates with HIE treated with TH. Although the CNN algorithm correlated well with total costs, we identified 3 specific cost domains that had low correlation (imaging, medications, and procedures).

Our cost estimates are lower than previous reports from the United States. A prospective large study including 19 NICUs across the United States estimated that the NICU stay cost for a neonate with HIE treated with TH and survived to discharge was $58 552 (IQR, $32 476-$130 203) for survivors and $29 760 (IQR, $16 897-$61 399) for nonsurvivors in USD.11 This difference is comparable with other North American analyses of in-hospital costs and have been attributed to lower physician salaries and lower administrative expenditures.32,33 In contrast, our results are closer to estimates from the United Kingdom that reported a mean cost of $39 100 (Canadian dollars) per neonate receiving TH.10 Overall, such variations in costs between countries highlight the importance of validating cost estimates within each country to account for variations in price of labor, price of goods (equipment and pharmaceuticals), salaries, and administrative costs.

We identified that disease severity on admission reflected by higher acuity score (SNAP-II), seizures on admission, and severe encephalopathy correlate with higher NICU costs. In addition, complications and NICU interventions that correlate with total costs include multiorgan failure, duration of mechanical ventilation, length of stay, and brain injury. These findings were supported by the increasing proportion of respiratory, imaging, and medication cost domains among high-cost neonates, which show that increased costs among these neonates are not simply due to an increased length of stay, but rather to an increased number of interventions that are required for their care.

Although the CNN costing algorithm has been validated previously for inpatient costs of preterm neonates using overall estimates for gestational age and/or case-mix groups in Ontario and Alberta, this study was the first to use linked patient-level costing and to validate the algorithm among infants receiving TH based on cost tertiles.14,15,20,34,35 We demonstrated the strong prediction power of the CNN algorithm in estimating total costs for neonates with HIE and different severity and/or outcomes. Although the algorithm shows a moderate to strong correlation for 5 of the 8 cost domains, there remained a $5339 mean difference in total costs. This 15% difference in total cost estimates is mainly attributable to the CNN algorithm overestimating indirect and respiratory costs, which may attributable to variations in human resources and logistical differences in practices and procedures between institutions (because the initial cost estimates for the CNN algorithm are based on a single site costing data). There was also a low correlation in cost estimates across 3 domains (imaging, medication, and procedures) between the actual costs and CNN costs. These discrepancies may be due to differences in accounting and cost-attribution methodologies (eg, some pharmacy costs may be included in indirect costs or technician time for medical imaging). However, the absolute costs differences owing to these costs center were relatively low in relation to total costs of hospitalisation because these combined cost domains account for approximatively 10% of total costs. Although showing good correlation in costs, the respiratory domain demonstrated a consistent overestimation in estimates relative to actual costs. Such differences can be studied and improved through the implementation of correction factors via future iterations of the algorithm.

In summary, our estimates highlight the promising potential of the CNN algorithm in predicting total and specific estimates for neonates with HIE treated with TH, and whose margin of error can be improved with continued optimization and validation.

Implications

This Canadian study is the first to estimate the costs of hospitalization of neonates with HIE and identifies factors that are associated with higher resource use among these neonates. Our findings add a deeper scope to previous studies, which have estimated the hospitalization cost of neonates with HIE in other countries such as the US and UK. In the context of increased collaborations for national and international trials, benchmarking and quality improvement initiatives for neonates with HIE treated with TH, having a validated costing algorithm will help evaluate the economic impacts of such additional interventions.36

Strengths and Limitations

As a single-site study, costs are influenced by local practices, differing definitions of cost items in both algorithms, and access to hospital financial data. There are differences in both algorithms in methods to account for certain costs. For example, the CNN algorithm does not account for repeated imagery (ie, MRI) and some medications, which limits the overlap between data sources. Nevertheless, we alleviated such differences by dividing the cost of hospitalization into individual cost domains and validating each separately. We also used tertiles of cost to maximize insights obtained from total costs and to identify variance and dominance of specific cost domains among neonates with HIE treated with TH. We leveraged the use of a validated clinical database (the CNN database) to provide a detailed portrayal of interventions and outcomes for each neonate. Most important, we used single-patient costs and outcomes for our analysis, rather than grouping admissions by gestational age or reason for admission to validate the CNN algorithm and provide singular estimates for each neonate's total and domain costs. Because our estimates are focused on costs of initial hospitalization, they do not account for global costs of care (ie, loss of productivity of parents, costs of follow-up, or longitudinal educational resources) and the long-term economic impacts. Nevertheless, continuous validation of the CNN algorithm, in combination with the CNN follow-up database, can help to generate broader estimates for these neonates in the future.

Conclusions

Severity of HIE and the presence of organ dysfunctions are associated with higher inpatient costs among neonates with HIE treated with TH and admitted to a single Level 3 NICU in Quebec. The CNN costing algorithm predicts with strong correlation hospital stay costs for these neonates in our center, albeit with a consistent overestimation of approximately 15%. Results of this study can help the assessment of the cost-effectiveness of additional interventions during NICU stay.
