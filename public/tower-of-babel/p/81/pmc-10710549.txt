Redefining primary hyperaldosteronism as “The Syndrome of Inappropriate Aldosterone Secretion (SIALDS)”: A common but unrecognized cause of hypertension
Authors: Rajat Lamba
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe current screening and diagnostic recommendations for detecting Primary Hyperaldosteronism (PHA) focus on diagnosing the more severe and overt instances of renin‐independent aldosterone production. However, milder forms of autonomous aldosterone secretion have been demonstrated to exist below the diagnostic thresholds of current PHA guidelines, and associate with clinically relevant cardiovascular risk. PHAencompasses a spectrum of renin independent aldosterone production, progressing from a subclinical state in normotensives to a full‐blown clinical syndrome representing the resistant hypertension population. The authors propose the Syndrome of Inappropriately Elevated Aldosterone Secretion (SIALDS) concept as a potential new paradigm for understanding and diagnosing PHA and expanded diagnostic approach to improve early detection even in well‐controlled hypertension. The authors also delve into the impact of treatments, including mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists and emerging aldosterone synthase inhibitors. Furthermore, The authors outline future research directions, proposing clinical trials to investigate the long‐term identification and treatment outcomes of SIALDS.Keywords: hypertension, potassium/hypertension, primary hyperaldosteronism, secondary hypertension

INTRODUCTION

Recent research has illuminated the substantial role of renin‐independent aldosterone production in hypertension, challenging its traditional perception as a rare cause. A comprehensive understanding of this condition's true prevalence, diagnostic complexities, and optimal treatment approaches remain areas of significant uncertainty. This review aims to consolidate current knowledge on the spectrum of autonomous aldosterone secretion, delineate its under‐recognized contribution to hypertension, and propose a new conceptual framework while addressing diagnostic gaps and implications for future research.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF ALDOSTERONE

The renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system (RAAS) plays a pivotal role in regulating blood pressure and volume status. Under normal physiological conditions, a drop in renal perfusion activates the RAAS, which has a multitude of effects, primarily leading to an increase in blood pressure (BP), including secretion of the mineralocorticoid steroid hormone aldosterone from the outer adrenal cortex. 1 Aldosterone binds to the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) located within the distal convoluted tubule, which stimulates gene expression and surface placement of epithelial sodium channels (ENAC) on the tubular surface and sodium‐potassium ATPase on the basolateral surface. This results in the reabsorption of sodium chloride into the bloodstream, restoring volume status. A net negative charge is created within the tubular lumen that drives K+ and H+ ions into the lumen, leading to hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis (Figure 1). 1

In the pathological state of autonomous aldosterone production, aldosterone secretion occurs independently of renin. This leads to excessive sodium and water retention, volume overload, and feedback suppression of renin secretion (Figure 2) but it does not result in edema due to the phenomenon of “aldosterone escape.” 2

DIAGNOSIS OF PRIMARY HYPERALDOSTERONISM

Screening and confirmatory tests

The primary screening test for primary hyperaldosteronism (PHA) is serum aldosterone/renin ratio (ARR), typically considered positive if ARR > 30 ng/dL with suppressed renin (<1.0 ng/mL/h) and aldosterone >15 ng/dL. 3 However, ARR sensitivity varies widely (10%–100%), reducing its reliability. 4 Since aldosterone levels fluctuate throughout the day, a spot serum aldosterone level may not rule out PHA. 5 Recent studies suggest that a suppressed renin level may be a more sensitive indicator of PHA than ARR or random serum aldosterone levels alone. 6

Confirmatory testing usually follows a positive screening, but it can be deferred if patients meet all three criteria: (1) spontaneous hypokalemia, (2) random aldosterone level >20 ng/dL, and (3) suppressed renin levels. 3 The most common confirmatory test is the oral salt suppression test, involving a high‐salt diet (>200 mmol/day) for 4 days to maximally suppress aldosterone. On day 4, a 24‐h urine sample is collected, with PHA defined by urine aldosterone level >12 μg/24 h. 3

The intravenous salt suppression test involves infusing 2 L of normal saline over 4 h and measuring plasma aldosterone concentration; a value above 10 ng/dL confirms PHA. 3

High salt intake can worsen hypertension and hypokalemia in PHA, requiring careful monitoring of potassium levels and blood pressure during testing. 3 The primary regulators of aldosterone secretion include angiotensin II, serum potassium and ACTH. Since hypokalemia can independently inhibit aldosterone production, normalizing serum potassium before diagnostic testing is advised. 1 , 3 For optimal sensitivity, collect mid‐morning samples from patients who have been out of bed for at least 2 h and seated for 5−15 min due to the influence of ACTH. 3

Should medications be discontinued before testing for PHA?

Several medications, for example, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, beta‐blockers, Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists (MRAs), for example, spironolactone and eplerenone, and epithelial sodium channel [ENAC] blockers, for example, amiloride and triamterene, can influence renin and aldosterone levels. Traditionally, it has been recommended to replace these medications with alternatives, such as alpha‐blockers, and allow a washout period of 4−6 weeks prior to testing. However, stopping or switching medications may not be necessary in most cases with the only strict exception being MRAs. 2 Even with continued use of MRAs, a suppressed renin level may indicate the presence of PHA. 2

While the guidelines recommend a similar approach to ENAC inhibitors and MRAs, some experts suggest that ENAC inhibitors do not significantly impact diagnostic testing, unless the patient is receiving high doses. 2 , 3

An underlying PHA is strongly suggested when renin levels are suppressed despite ACE inhibitor use, like a failed captopril challenge test. 2

Why is screening for PHA essential?

Screening for PHA is crucial, as the benefit of treatment extends beyond merely controlling BP. Aldosterone driven MR activation can cause inflammation and fibrosis of the heart, kidney, and blood vessels, contributing to unfavorable long‐term outcomes. 7 A 2017 systematic review and meta‐analysis demonstrated that, compared to essential hypertension, PHA patients had a significantly increased risk of stroke (odds ratio [OR] 2·58, 95% CI 1·93–3·45), coronary artery disease (1·77, 1·10–2·83), atrial fibrillation (3·52, 2·06–5·99), heart failure (2·05, 1·11–3·78), and left ventricular hypertrophy (2·29, 1·65–3·17). 8

Early screening for diagnosis enables timely intervention, potentially enhancing cardiovascular and renal outcomes.

Who should be screened for PHA?

Contrary to the classical syndrome first described by Jerome Conn in 1955, the absence of hypokalemia or metabolic alkalosis does not exclude PHA. 2 Furthermore, resistant hypertension, or even hypertension itself, is not a prerequisite for PHA.

PREVALENCE OF PHA

Over the last two decades, prevalence estimates of PHA have increased substantially, challenging the historical perception that renin independent aldosterone production was rare and primarily associated with secondary hypertension. The reasons behind this increased prevalence remain uncertain, with factors such as excess visceral obesity, changes in population demographics, and more frequent testing all potentially playing a role.

PHA prevalence varies in studies due to population differences and PHA definitions. Some studies focus on resistant hypertension, while others include mild hypertensive and normotensive individuals. A 2016 meta‐analysis including 39 studies and 42,510 patients, found prevalence rates ranging from 3.2%−12.7% in primary care and 1%‐nearly 30% in referral centers. 11 This variability results from inconsistent, unvalidated cutoff values in screening and confirmatory tests, leading to significant heterogeneity and no precise prevalence estimate.

STUDIES SUPPORTING THE SPECTRUM OF AUTONOMOUS ALDOSTERONE SECRETION

Since the first description of PHA by Jerome Conn in 1954, several studies have provided evidence for the existence of renin independent aldosterone secretion along a continuum, spanning from biochemically overt PHA in normotensive individuals to those with resistant hypertension.

Monticone and colleagues screened 1672 patients at an Italian hypertension center, finding PHA prevalence rose from 3.9% in stage 1 to 11.8% in stage 3 hypertension. 12 Rossi and colleagues examined 1180 individuals, finding PHA prevalence rose from 7.2% in normotensives to 19.5% in grade 3 hypertension. 13 Mossi and colleagues observed a similar spectrum of PHA prevalence, with 2% in stage 1, 8% in stage 2, and 13% in stage 3 hypertension among 609 patients with essential hypertension. 14

A case control study by Markou and colleagues and a cross‐sectional analysis of the Hypertensive Pathotype cohort by Baudrand and colleagues found biochemically overt PHA on a continuum in normotensive populations. 15 , 16

Longitudinal cohort studies of normotensive individuals showed a higher risk of developing hypertension in future in those with higher aldosterone and suppressed renin levels, even with levels below the current clinical thresholds for PHA diagnosis. Vasan and colleagues in 2004 measured baseline aldosterone levels in a cohort of 1688 non‐ hypertensive participants in Framingham offspring study and followed them over a period of 4 years. Increase in aldosterone levels, within the physiological range, were associated with higher risk of development of hypertension. 17 A follow up these participants in 2007, revealed consistent findings. 18 Another notable cohort study by Brown and colleagues in 2017, of 850 untreated normotensive participants in MESA (Multi‐Ethnic study of Atherosclerosis), also showed that suppressed renin and higher aldosterone concentrations in the context of renin suppression was associated with higher risk of hypertension. 19

A cross sectional study by Hundemer and colleagues enrolled 663 normotensive and mildly hypertensive patients who were confirmed to not have PHA using current guideline criteria and were not on antihypertensive medications. Stimulated plasma renin activity (PRA) was measured after sodium restriction, categorizing participants into PRA tertiles: lowest PRA tertile denoting “Inappropriate/Excess MR Activity,” middle PRA tertile indicating “Intermediate MR Activity,” and highest PRA tertile representing “Physiologic MR Activity.” In those without clinical PHA, the inability to stimulate renin was associated with heightened autonomous aldosterone secretion and higher blood pressures, indicating a wide spectrum of unrecognized MR activation. 20

A recent multicenter cross‐sectional study in United States by Brown and colleagues, evaluated a cohort of nearly 1000 patients with normotension, stage 1 hypertension, stage 2 hypertension, and resistant hypertension. Irrespective of their baseline renin or aldosterone levels, all participants underwent an oral salt suppression test. PHA was confirmed biochemically when urine aldosterone levels were ≥12 mcg/24 h. The study found biochemically confirmed PHA across all groups, varying from around 11% in normotensive individuals to 22% among resistant hypertensives. 21

Interestingly, over half of the low renin‐resistant hypertension group met the criteria for confirmed PHA. Adjusting the 24‐h aldosterone cutoff to ≥10 mcg/24 h revealed even higher prevalence rates across all groups. These included 20% in normotensives, 27% in stage 1 hypertensives, 40% in stage 2 hypertensives, and an astounding 60% among resistant hypertensives. The study noted the presence of renin‐independent aldosterone production in every BP category, where higher aldosterone levels (even those below traditional cutoffs) corresponded with higher BP and lower serum potassium levels. 21

However, renin was measured in either a supine or standing position, which may introduce bias in interpreting the results.

Further support for this proposition comes from the Pathway 2 study on resistant hypertension, where suppressed renin and higher aldosterone was noted in spironolactone responders, suggesting potential undiagnosed PHA. 22

This perspective could reshape our view of the 1970s concept of “renin profiling” by Laragh and Sealey, who associated low renin hypertension with volume overload and advised diuretics in such cases. A practice now mostly considered outdated, may have overlooked patients with aldosterone excess who exhibited volume overload and suppressed renin. 23

TREATMENT OF PHA

PHA has traditionally been attributed to either an overactive Aldosterone producing adenoma (APA) or Primary Adrenal Hyperplasia (PAH). However, recent advances reveal that many cases of high aldosterone production originate from isolated clusters of autonomous aldosterone‐producing cells, which are not discernible on imaging. 24

After diagnosis of PHA is confirmed, the 2016 Endocrine society guidelines recommend obtaining adrenal CT to distinguish APA and PAH from Idiopathic Hyperaldosteronism (IHA) (Figure 3). If surgery is desired, Adrenal Vein Sampling (AVS) is indicated to distinguish unilateral from bilateral disease and determine candidacy for unilateral adrenalectomy. Unilateral adrenalectomy in APA or PAH leads to resolution of hypokalemia and improvement in hypertension in all, and cure of hypertension in 30%−60% cases. 3

IHA should be managed medically with MRAs, such as spironolactone or eplerenone. 2

Key biomarkers for effective medical therapy in PHA include minimal antihypertensive agents for blood pressure control, potassium normalization without supplements, and increased renin levels. 24

A unilateral adrenalectomy is rarely needed in bilateral adrenal hyperplasia for persistently elevated BP and suppressed renin despite maximally tolerated MR antagonist therapy. 24

One challenge with spironolactone is its non‐selectivity causing menstrual irregularities and gynecomastia. ENAC inhibitors present as a favorable alternative due to less significant adverse effects. In a sub study of PATHWAY 2, Amiloride was at least as effective as spironolactone in controlling blood pressure in the resistant hypertension population. 25

In the Pathway 3 trial, amiloride‐HCTZ combination proved better at controlling blood pressure and preventing glucose intolerance than monotherapy. 26

In PHA, ENAC inhibitors can effectively control blood pressure as well as hypokalemia and may even protect vascular health. 27 Uncertainty still exists about aldosterone's independent impact beyond ENaC activation and whether prolonged ENaC inhibitor use in PHA offers similar cardiovascular benefits as MRAs.

A new class of drugs, aldosterone synthase inhibitors, is being explored, with a recent phase 2 trial of Baxdrostat showing promising BP reduction. However, its effects in patients with lower glomerular filtration rates and potential for hyperkalemia remain to be evaluated in future trials. 28

FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS: A PROPOSED APPROACH

According to the 2016 Endocrine Society guidelines, urine aldosterone levels of less than 10 μg/24 h after an oral salt suppression test make PHA improbable. A urine aldosterone level of >12 μg/24 h at the Mayo Clinic and >14 μg/24 h at the Cleveland Clinic renders PHA highly likely. 3 In a state of volume expansion with salt suppression, renin should reach its maximum level of suppression, leading to minimal aldosterone production. Based on recent evidence supporting the spectrum of autonomous aldosterone secretion, John Funder suggested that even aldosterone levels between 6 and 12 μg/24 h may indicate possible primary hyperaldosteronism (PHA). 6 We propose to redefine PHA as the Syndrome of Inappropriately Elevated Aldosterone Secretion (SIALDS), which identifies lower aldosterone levels as pathological in a salt‐loaded, volume‐expanded state. SIALDS is present on a spectrum, and can be classified as mild, moderate, or severe but with subjective thresholds defining each category (Figures 3 and 4). SIALDS supplements and does not replace the diagnosis of PHA. This dynamic condition may progress if untreated, with higher aldosterone levels associated with increased cardiovascular risks, emphasizing the need for early intervention. Even mild SIALDS may warrant aldosterone blockers, pending validation through clinical trials for long‐term outcomes and potential risks.

We recommend assessing spot renin/aldosterone levels in all hypertensive patients (BP > 130/80 mmHg). If renin is suppressed, a 24‐h urine sample for aldosterone should be collected after salt suppression. 6 While suppressed renin is a sensitive marker of potentially inappropriate aldosterone elevation, it may not always indicate PHA, as renin levels can be influenced by factors like age, sex, and renal function. 3

RALES 29 and EPHESUS 30 trials firmly established spironolactone and eplerenone as crucial components in reducing heart failure mortality. The FIDELIO‐DKD trial marked a significant breakthrough, showing better renal outcomes in diabetic kidney disease patients when adding the novel non‐steroidal MRA, finerenone, to maximum tolerated ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers. 31 Steroidal MRAs effectively lower blood pressure. However, randomized controlled trials investigating their long‐term benefits or harms, when used as antihypertensive agents are still lacking. 32 , 33 Ongoing trials in China and the University of Oxford aim to address this knowledge gap. 34 , 35

Future trials should investigate the long‐term benefits of MRAs and aldosterone synthase inhibitors, particularly in low‐renin hypertension.

CONCLUSIONS

The traditional view of PHA as a rare cause of hypertension is being reconsidered, given emerging evidence of its wider prevalence. Current PHA screening guidelines target severe cases, but milder autonomous aldosterone secretion poses cardiovascular risks below diagnostic thresholds. We propose renaming PHA as SIALDS to encompass the entire range of autonomous aldosterone production, from subclinical normotensive stages to full‐blown clinical syndromes, facilitating early diagnosis and improving cardiac and renal outcomes.
