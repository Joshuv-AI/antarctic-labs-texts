Machine Learning Algorithm to Predict Atrial Fibrillation Using Serial 12‐Lead ECGs Based on Left Atrial Remodeling
Authors: Ji‐Hoon Choi, Sung‐Hee Song, Hongryul Kim, Jongwoo Kim, Heesun Park, JaeHu Jeon, JoongSik Hong, Hye Bin Gwag, Sung Ho Lee, Jaichan Lee, Soo Jin Cho, Seung‐Jung Park, Young Keun On, Ju Youn Kim, Kyoung‐Min Park
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe hypothesized that analysis of serial ECGs could predict new‐onset atrial fibrillation (AF) more accurately than analysis of a single ECG by detecting the subtle cardiac remodeling that occurs immediately before AF occurrence. Our aim in this study was to compare the performance of 2 types of machine learning (ML) algorithms.Methods and ResultsStandard 12‐lead ECGs of patients selected by cardiologists between January 2010 and May 2021 were used for ML model development. Two ML models (single ECG and serial ECG) were developed using a light gradient boosting machine‐learning algorithm. Model performance was evaluated based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and F1 score. We trained the ML models on 415 964 ECGs from 176 090 patients. When testing the 2 ML models using external validation data sets, the performance of the serial‐ML model was significantly better than that of the single‐ML model for predicting new‐onset AF (single‐ versus serial‐ML model: sensitivity 0.744 versus 0.810; specificity 0.742 versus 0.822; accuracy 0.743 versus 0.816; F1 score 0.743 versus 0.815; area under the receiver operating characteristic curve 0.812 versus 0.880; P<0.001). The Shapley Additive Explanations analysis ranked P‐wave duration and amplitude among the top 10 ECG parameters.ConclusionsAn ML model based on serial ECGs from an individual had greater ability to predict new‐onset AF than the ML model based on a single ECG. P‐wave morphologies were associated with future AF prediction.Keywords: artificial intelligence, atrial fibrillation, ECG, machine learning, prediction, remodelingSubject Categories: Arrhythmias, Atrial Fibrillation, Electrocardiology (ECG), Digital Health

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent arrhythmia, and the number of patients with this condition is increasing rapidly, as are related health care costs. 1 , 2 , 3 Many patients do not experience symptoms in the early stages of AF and are unaware of it until the development of major complications such as stroke or heart failure. 4 , 5 Thus, a significant number of AF cases are not diagnosed, resulting in an increase in the high risk of ischemic stroke in such patients. 6 , 7 The importance of early AF diagnosis was emphasized in the EAST‐AFNET 4 (Early Treatment of Atrial Fibrillation for Stroke Prevention) trial, which showed that early rhythm control of AF resulted in a better stroke prognosis, regardless of symptoms. 8 Wearable ECG monitoring devices are widely used in clinical practice to detect early AF. 9 However, patient selection, duration of AF, and frequency of AF vary widely among studies. 10

Recently, artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms have been used to screen, diagnose, and classify diseases. 11 Attia et al demonstrated that patients with AF could be identified through a deep learning (DL) algorithm based on a normal sinus rhythm (NSR) ECG. 12 Raghunath et al reported a DL algorithm trained on a 12‐lead ECG to predict new‐onset AF within 1 year with high accuracy. 13 A common feature in previously published studies is prediction of AF using DL convolutional neural networks (CNNs) based on a single NSR ECG. The agnostic approach of CNNs can provide high‐quality results, but the underlying mechanisms are not understood by most users. 11 , 14 In contrast, explainable machine learning (ML) algorithms are expected to provide reliable, interpretable information in clinical medicine, where high‐stakes decision‐making is required.

Left atrial (LA) remodeling is the pathophysiologic basis of AF initiation and progression. Electrophysiologic and structural remodeling of the left atrium promotes the development of AF. 15 Thus, we hypothesized that analyzing the difference between an individual's serial NSR ECGs (paired ECGs performed within a certain period) could predict new‐onset AF more accurately than analysis of a single NSR ECG by detecting the subtle cardiac remodeling that occurs immediately before AF occurrence. Our aim in this study was to compare the performance of 2 types of ML algorithms (single‐ and serial‐ECG models) in predicting new‐onset AF. In addition, ECG features used for training ML algorithms were investigated to advance explainable AI.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

ECG Data Sources

All standard 12‐lead ECGs obtained from patients at Samsung Medical Center who were >18 years of age between January 2010 and May 2021 were identified. All ECGs were conducted using a Philips ECG instrument (PageWriter TC70, TC50, TC30, and Trim III) at a 500 Hz sampling rate with 5 μV resolution, and raw data were stored in extensible markup language format. An ECG database was constructed using all collected ECGs labeled with trained physician and cardiologist readings. The Samsung Medical Center Institutional Review Board approved the research protocol and issued a waiver for informed consent following our institutional ethics policy. ECG data from Samsung Changwon Hospital and Samsung Kangbuk Hospital were added to develop the AI models. ECG data were divided into training, validation, and test sets at an 8:1:1 ratio without overlap. External validation of the developed AI models was performed using a Philips ECG machine at Samsung Medical Center between June 2021 and December 2023 and using a General Electric ECG instrument at Wonju Severance Hospital.

Study Population and Validation of AF

Medical record and diagnostic codes were reviewed for all cases. Definite AF was designated only for those patients with a documented AF ECG (12‐lead ECG or Holter monitoring) and AF diagnosis confirmed by medical records or diagnostic codes. Patients with only atrial flutter ECG and no AF diagnosis were categorized as not having AF. All patients included in this study were classified into the AF or NSR group according to the criteria for definite AF. The index date for AF was defined as that of initial AF diagnosis (the oldest date among any AF ECG and medical records). The last NSR ECG was defined as the most recently performed NSR ECG in the NSR group. In the AF group, the NSR ECG performed within 2 years of the index date for AF was collected, and in the NSR group, the NSR ECG performed within 2 years of the last NSR ECG was collected for ML model development. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients diagnosed with AF in their medical records or a diagnostic code of AF before the index AF ECG, (2) patients without an NSR ECG before the index AF ECG, (3) patients with only 1 NSR ECG, (4) patients with a medical record or diagnostic code for AF but no AF ECG, (5) insufficient medical records to evaluate a patient's medical status, and (6) abnormal ECGs (all ECGs except those labeled as NSR or within normal range). The ECGs of all patients who were not excluded were included in this study.

Analysis was conducted using 2 models, single‐ and serial‐ECG ML models, both of which were designed to predict new‐onset AF. We defined the blanking period as the minimum period during which the ML model could detect subtle changes in the NSR ECG reflecting LA remodeling before index AF ECG.

For the single‐ECG model, all NSR ECGs within 2 years from the last NSR ECG in the NSR group and all NSR ECGs within 2 years from the index date for AF in the AF group were selected (Figure 1A). For the serial‐ECG model, paired NSR ECGs were required as input. NSR ECGs within and outside the blanking period were paired and trained using all possible combination methods (Figure 1B). For example, if there were 2 NSR ECGs within the blanking period and 2 ECGs outside the blanking period, 4 ECG pair combinations are possible (2 ECGs within the blanking period×2 ECGs before the blanking period). In the serial‐ECG ML model, various intervals between 2 NSR ECGs (eg, 1 month–2 years, 2 months–2 years, 3 months–2 years) were evaluated to determine the optimal interval between 2 paired NSR ECGs for predicting AF (Figure 1B).

ML Algorithm Development

The start of ECGs was set to the onset of the P wave, the first wave of the beat. Then, ECGs were band‐pass filtered (0.5–45 Hz, an infinite impulse response filter) to remove unexpected artifacts, such as baseline wandering 16 and power line interference. 17 , 18 , 19 Then, all components of P‐QRS‐T waves, including peaks, intervals, and segments, were detected and located using Neurokit2, an open‐source Python package for neurophysiological signal processing. 20 Based on the extracted P‐QRS‐T components, descriptive statistics were calculated and used as input features for our models. 21 In particular, the mean, minimum, maximum, and standard deviation of the peaks, intervals, segments, and durations were used. To incorporate the shape of the P wave into our model, we extracted the P wave from the P‐off to the P‐on point and computed the skewness and kurtosis of the P wave. This allowed us to quantify the skew and peak intensity of the P wave, respectively. Furthermore, changes between 2 successive peaks, intervals, and durations were calculated and used as input features to capture the variability of waves in the ECG. In addition to P‐QRS‐T components, correlation statistics (ie, the average of the beat‐wise Pearson correlation coefficients between a template beat and other beats) and f‐wave indices were added to include AF‐related information. 22 Descriptive statistics of heart rate variability were added to incorporate additional ECG information. 23 All ECG features were extracted from all 12 leads. Last, age and sex were added to account for individual characteristics. All ECG features used in developing the single‐ and serial‐ECG models are presented in Tables S1 and S2.

For both the single‐ and serial‐ECG models, a light gradient boosting machine algorithm, which is an ML algorithm based on a gradient boosting decision tree, was used. 24 To select the best hyperparameters for the light gradient boosting machine models, a Bayesian optimization method, which is more efficient and generalizable than other optimization methods, was used. 25 We used the Youden J statistic for comparison with DL models and for external validation. The models' thresholds were adjusted using the Youden J statistic to classify the outcomes, ensuring an optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity. For other experiments, a threshold of 0.5 was used.

Developing DL Models for Comparison

We compared the performance of the single‐ECG models and existing DL models. DenseNet‐121 and Transformer models were developed using the same ECG data used to develop single‐ECG models. Detailed information on the development of DL models for comparison is provided in Data S1.

Explainable AI

The Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) technique was developed to enhance the interpretability of model predictions. 26 This technique is primarily used to transform black‐box models into interpretable ones. SHAP helps understand and interpret predictions by explaining the contribution of each feature to the prediction. Through this functionality, SHAP increases the understanding of the inner workings of the model. SHAP is considered more reliable and interpretable than traditional feature importance in tree models due to the consistency of SHAP feature measurements. 27 This consistency allows a clearer understanding of how each feature influences the model predictions.

We used the Tree‐SHAP method to interpret the light gradient boosting machine models for both the single‐ECG and serial‐ECG models. This method offers the advantage of faster computation than traditional SHAP methods, enabling quicker and more accurate evaluation of feature importance.

Outcomes

The primary outcome in the present study was prediction of new‐onset AF using standard 12‐lead NSR ECGs using 2 ML models (single‐ECG model versus serial‐ECG model). We also determined the optimal blanking period and interval between 2 NSR ECGs in the serial‐ECG model. ML model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and F1 score. The secondary outcome was identification of ECG features that were significant predictors of AF.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in R statistical software (version 4.2.1) and Python (version 3.8). Continuous variables are reported as mean values±SDs, whereas categorical variables are reported as numbers of patients (percentages). To compare the means of 2 variables, we used the Student t test for continuous variables and Pearson χ2 test for categorical variables. Receiver operating characteristic analysis and Kaplan‐Meier survival analysis were used to evaluate the performance of the developed ML models. Statistical significance was defined as a 2‐tailed P value <0.05.

RESULTS

Study Population

A total of 2 162 637 ECGs were identified from 894 356 adult patients, and 1 707 800 ECGs from 710 071 patients were excluded based on the study criteria (Figure 2). We finally used 415 964 ECGs from 176 090 patients to develop single and serial models. The mean age of patients in the NSR group was 56.5±13.3 years, whereas in the AF group it was 65.8±13.0 years (Table 1). Patient age was significantly higher in the AF group compared with the NSR group in both AI models (P<0.001). The proportion of men was significantly higher in the AF group compared with the NSR group (NSR group versus AF group: 47.2% versus 54.8%; P<0.001). The average number of ECGs per patient was 2.3±1.8 in the NSR group (P<0.001) and 3.6±4.2 in the AF group (P<0.001). SHAP for Explainable AI

In both single‐ and serial‐ECG models, age and sex were more important than any individual ECG parameter in predicting the outcomes (Figures 6 and 7). The smoothing trend for AF status versus age for both sexes is depicted in Figure 7. In the single‐ECG model, elements related to P‐wave morphology (P‐wave duration and amplitude) ranked among the top 10. In the serial‐ECG model, P‐wave duration was among the top 5 ECG parameters.

The serial‐ECG model calculates the difference between 2 ECGs, and the key predictive factors may change according to the time interval. This understanding is crucial for our research. To comprehend the characteristics of these predictive factors over different time intervals, we divided the data into short‐term, midterm, and long‐term intervals and trained separate models for each. We then analyzed these models using SHAP, a method that has proven to be effective in our study. The principle of the serial‐ECG model was to analyze the changes according to the time difference between 2 ECGs, rather than simply analyzing the average. To perform a more precise analysis, we segmented the data according to the time interval. Specifically, we divided the time intervals into short term (1–4 months), midterm (4–8 months), and long term (8–14 months) and created SHAP plots for each interval (Figure S2). This approach visualizes the important features of ECG changes across different time intervals, enhancing the interpretability of the model. Finally, multivariable logistic models were created using the top 15 features identified by SHAP. An additional 5 features were gradually included, and the best results were obtained when 20 features were included (Table S4).

DISCUSSION

In the present study, an ML model using serial ECGs was able to predict new‐onset AF with higher accuracy than an ML model using a single ECG. In the serial‐ECG model, model performance tended to decrease as the blanking period became longer. In the serial‐ECG model, performance was lowest when the distance between 2 ECGs was <1 month. Finally, P‐wave morphologies (duration and amplitude) were associated with future AF prediction.

Attia et al first reported that a DL CNN algorithm using an NSR ECG could identify patients with paroxysmal AF. 12 When using all ECGs of a window of interest (31 days before the first documented AF ECG), algorithm performance improved slightly compared with that of the algorithm based on a single ECG; area under the curve increased from 0.87 to 0.90, accuracy from 79.4% to 83.3%, and F1 score from 39.2% to 45.4%. A subsequent study by Raghunath et al showed that a deep‐learning CNN algorithm based on a single ECG could predict new‐onset AF within 1 year with good accuracy (area under the curve 0.85). 13 Furthermore, Raghunath et al used that AI algorithm to identify patients with AF‐related stroke before the event.

Recent studies have shown that cardiac remodeling is a key process in developing and maintaining AF. 15 , 28 A recent study by Van Den Berg et al demonstrated that extracellular matrix remodeling of the atrium long precedes incident AF. 29 Noirclerc et al suggested that left ventricular mass and LA enlargement can appear early in clinical AF. 30 In addition, Attia et al indirectly demonstrated that a CNN model could detect subtle ECG changes caused by subclinical cardiac remodeling, which is difficult to identify in humans. 12 They used ECGs 31 days before the first documented AF ECG, assuming that LA remodeling would be most reflected in the ECG immediately before the occurrence of AF. We also assumed that the closer to the time of AF occurrence, the more LA remodeling would be reflected. This study showed that model performance gradually decreased as the blanking period became longer, supporting the hypothesis that subtle ECG changes resulting from cardiac remodeling become more pronounced over time, resulting in a more accurate AI prediction of AF when using serial ECGs. The performance of our serial‐ECG model far exceeded that of our single‐ECG model and has sufficient high sensitivity, specificity, and predictive performance for clinical application.

The strength of our model is that it is explainable and outperforms existing DL models. Our single‐ECG ML model showed similar performance to other DL models, whereas our serial‐ECG ML model outperformed DL models. In our serial‐ECG model, input data were the differences in a patient's P‐QRS‐T statistics among ECGs over time. However, for DL models, raw data were used as input, and serial implementation of DL was not possible.

Recent advances in pathophysiology have revealed that a disease spectrum called atrial myopathy, which is represented by changes in atrial structure and function, precedes AF. 31 In other words, electrical and structural remodeling occurs in the atrium due to various causes, resulting in an abnormal atrial substrate. P‐wave morphology and left atrium enlargement have been suggested as biomarkers of atrial cardiomyopathy. 31 In this study, we showed that subtle ECG changes, suggestive of atrial myopathy, precede the onset of AF. Among various ECG features used for ML, the change in P‐wave morphologies (duration and parameter) were associated with new‐onset AF. Gorenek et al found that immediate recurrence of AF in patients was indicated by a lower P‐wave amplitude. 32 Park et al reported that P‐wave amplitudes <0.1 mV in lead I predicted clinical recurrence after high‐frequency catheter ablation in patients with paroxysmal AF. 33 These findings suggest that a low P‐wave amplitude is associated with AF, backing our finding. The reliability of AI models is of critical concern in clinical practice, because most clinicians are reluctant to rely solely on AI for medical decisions. Explainable AI can use understandable ECG changes to predict new‐onset AF based on its pathophysiology.

The present study has several limitations. Although the results of the study were clinically significant, the number of patients in the external validation group was small. An additional limitation is that, due to the nature of paroxysmal AF, undetected subclinical AF likely occurs before diagnosis using a 12‐lead ECG. Furthermore, although only patients with at least 2 NSR ECGs were included, and an extensive medical record review was performed to minimize this bias, the possibility of undetected AF in the NSR group cannot be excluded. In addition, the timing of AF diagnosis was unclear. However, this is expected in an AF prediction study using AI, because there is no way to know the exact timing of the first AF. Because we used only an NSR ECG in the study to develop the ML model, there may be limitations in applying it to various clinical scenarios. NSR ECGs from the index AF ECG were not evenly distributed over time, which may have impacted our findings. Prospective studies are needed to address these issues. Last, in DL models, hyperparameter tuning was not sufficiently optimized compared with the light gradient boosting machine model.

CONCLUSIONS

We demonstrated that an ML model based on serial ECGs can predict new‐onset AF more accurately than an ML model based on a single ECG. In the serial‐ECG model, model performance tended to decrease as the blanking period became longer. The DL models outperformed our single‐ECG ML model, but our proposed serial‐ECG ML model outperformed all existing AI models. P‐wave morphologies (duration and amplitude) were associated with future AF prediction. Further clinical investigation is necessary to confirm the performance of this serial‐ECG model.
