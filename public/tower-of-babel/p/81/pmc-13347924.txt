Colchicine enhances macrophage cholesterol efflux by targeting DTL-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of ABCA1
Authors: Lingfei Tong, Wenming Wu, Yan Wang, Lihua Duan, Hui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to elucidate the potential pharmacological mechanisms by which colchicine confers clinical benefits in patients with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, focusing on its effects on cholesterol efflux.MethodsThis study was conducted in multiple cellular models. First, cell viability was assessed and the optimal dosage of colchicine was screened via the Cell Counting Kit-8 assay. Subsequently, Oil Red O staining, commercial kit detection and Boron dipyrromethene (BODIPY) -cholesterol labelling were employed to analyse intracellular lipid accumulation, foam cell formation and cholesterol efflux rate in macrophages, respectively. Furthermore, molecular docking and transcriptome sequencing were performed to explore the potential therapeutic targets and underlying mechanisms of colchicine, followed by a series of cellular experiments (quantitative reverse transcription-PCR, ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) degradation analysis, plasmid construction and transfection, co-immunoprecipitation, immunoblotting) for comprehensive validation of the identified potential mechanisms.ResultsIn the present study, we demonstrate that colchicine markedly promotes cholesterol efflux, a key process that reverses macrophage foam cell formation and consequently contributes to its cardioprotective effects. We identified modulation of the ubiquitination of ABCA1 as the pivotal mechanism by which colchicine enhances cholesterol efflux. Our findings reveal for the first time that denticleless E3 ubiquitin protein ligase homologue (DTL) functions as a crucial E3 ligase in this regulatory pathway. Mechanistically, colchicine suppresses DTL expression and disrupts its interaction with ABCA1, leading to increased ABCA1 protein stability, enhanced ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux and a subsequent reduction in foam cell formation.ConclusionColchicine shows great promise for drug repurposing as a novel therapeutic agent. Its strong capacity to reverse foam cell formation highlights its potential to extend clinical use beyond gout management to cardiovascular secondary prevention.Keywords: Cardiovascular Diseases, Lipids, Atherosclerosis

Introduction

Colchicine, a classic anti-inflammatory agent, has been used for centuries in the treatment and prevention of gout.1 Recently, the concept that ‘coronary artery disease is the ‘gout’ of the arteries’ has gained increasing attention, leading to extensive investigations into the potential cardiovascular benefits of colchicine.2 Epidemiological and clinical studies have shown that colchicine reduces the recurrence risk of acute and recurrent pericarditis by approximately 50%.3 Moreover, colchicine has been reported to lower the risks of cardiovascular mortality, myocardial infarction, ischaemic stroke and revascularisation in patients with acute coronary syndromes or chronic coronary syndromes (CCS), while also enhancing plaque stability in those with acute coronary syndromes.4,6 In June 2023, colchicine (0.5 mg/day) was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for use in individuals with multiple cardiovascular risk factors or those diagnosed with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD).7 This approval was primarily supported by the Colchicine Cardiovascular Outcomes Trial (COLCOT) and Low‑Dose Colchicine 2 (LoDoCo-2) clinical trials, which demonstrated that patients with chronic coronary heart disease and those with recent myocardial infarction experienced a reduction in cardiovascular event risk of approximately 33.3% and 23%, respectively, following colchicine therapy.5 6 Collectively, colchicine represents the first specialised anti-inflammatory agent proven to effectively reduce cardiovascular risk, thereby expanding the therapeutic options available for clinical decision-making.8

Despite its inclusion in the 2024 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines for the management of CCS as an adjunctive therapy (class IIa, level A) for patients with atherosclerotic coronary artery disease-related CCS,9 research elucidating how colchicine reduces cardiovascular risk remains limited, and its underlying mechanisms continue to be debated.10 Since inflammation is involved in all stages of ASCVD—from initiation and progression to prognosis—colchicine has been postulated to reduce ASCVD risk by inhibiting NOD-like receptor family, pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activity through multiple anti-inflammatory pathways.11 12 However, previous studies have shown that NLRP3 expression in the heart is relatively low, suggesting that colchicine may have limited efficacy in ASCVD, particularly in acute myocardial infarction.13 Moreover, some researchers have proposed that colchicine may not effectively inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation at all.14 Evidently, current research on the cardiovascular benefits of colchicine remains complex and inconclusive, and the precise mechanisms underlying its protective effects in ASCVD require further elucidation.

Reportedly, macrophage-derived cholesterol efflux—a key initial step in reverse cholesterol transport (RCT)—is recognised as the primary physiological process for eliminating excess cellular cholesterol in humans, and increasing attention has been devoted to the regulatory mechanisms underlying this process in recent years.15 The RCT process consists of three main stages16: (1) cholesterol efflux, in which ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) transfers intracellular free cholesterol (FC) to lipid-poor apolipoprotein A-I (apoA-I) to form nascent high-density lipoprotein (HDL), while ATP-binding cassette transporter G1 (ABCG1) facilitates direct cholesterol transfer to mature HDL; (2) cholesterol esterification, during which lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase esterifies FC into cholesterol esters (CE) to form mature HDL; and (3) hepatic metabolism, where mature HDL delivers CE to the liver via scavenger receptor class B type I or transfers CE to very-low-density lipoprotein or low-density lipoprotein (LDL) through cholesteryl ester transfer protein, followed by hepatic uptake via LDL receptors and eventual biliary excretion. Among these stages, cholesterol efflux from macrophages constitutes the initial and rate-limiting step of RCT,17 primarily mediated by ABCA1 and ABCG1. Upregulation of these transporters promotes HDL formation and enhances RCT efficiency.18 Epidemiological studies have demonstrated a strong inverse association between plasma HDL levels and cardiovascular risk, with each 1% increase in plasma HDL concentration corresponding to an approximately 3% reduction in cardiovascular events.19 20 Therefore, enhancing cholesterol efflux from macrophages is expected to strengthen RCT function, thereby contributing to the prevention and treatment of ASCVD.

This study aims to elucidate the potential pharmacological mechanisms by which colchicine confers clinical benefits in patients with ASCVD, focusing on its effects on cholesterol efflux. Through a series of experimental investigations, we demonstrate that colchicine attenuates denticleless E3 ubiquitin protein ligase homologue (DTL)-mediated, ubiquitin-dependent degradation of ABCA1 by suppressing DTL expression and disrupting its interaction with substrate proteins. This mechanism enhances ABCA1 protein stability, promotes ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux in macrophages and ultimately reverses foam cell formation. These findings provide a mechanistic basis that may inform future updates to clinical guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of cardiovascular disease.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

Colchicine was obtained from Selleck Chemicals (Shanghai, China). Cell culture reagents, including Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), RPMI-1640 medium, dimethyl sulfoxide, 100× penicillin–streptomycin solution, 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) and bovine serum albumin (BSA), were purchased from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology (Beijing, China). Trypsin (0.25%) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from Corning Incorporated (Shanghai, China). TRIzol reagent was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The inhibitors cycloheximide (CHX), dorsomorphin and MG132 were obtained from Selleck Chemicals.

Antibodies used in the current study were as follows: anti-ABCA1 (ab307534, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-AMPK (D63G4, CST, Danvers, Massachusetts, USA), anti-p-AMPK (D4D6D, CST), anti-Ubiquitin (E4I2J, CST), anti-GAPDH (D16H11, CST), anti-ABCG1 (BA3853-2, BOSTER), anti-liver X receptor alpha (LXRα) (BM5134, BOSTER) and anti-DTL (BM5502, BOSTER); Tag antibodies including anti-Flag (AE005, ABclonal, China), anti-HA (AE008, ABclonal) and anti-MYC (AE070, ABclonal); horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (A0216 and A0208, Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

Cell culture

Human acute monocytic leukaemia cell line (THP-1), RAW264.7 and J774A.1 cells were provided by the BeNa Culture Collection (Beijing, China), while HepG2 and 293T cells were purchased from EallBio Life Sciences (Beijing, China). RAW264.7, J774A.1 and HepG2 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 U/mL); 293T cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS; THP-1 cells were grown in suspension in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Differentiation of THP-1 monocytes into macrophages was induced by treatment with 100 ng/mL PMA for 24 hours. All cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. Cells used for subsequent experiments were between passages 5 and 10 and reached approximately 80%–90% confluence (three to six replicate wells set for each experimental group).

Cell viability

The RAW264.7, THP-1 and J774A.1 cells were uniformly seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1×104 cells per well. Once cells reached 80%–90% confluence, they were treated with different concentrations of colchicine (0, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100 and 200 µM) for 24 hours to explore the optimal treatment concentration. Subsequently, cell viability was evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 μL of serum-free medium containing 10% CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37°C for 30 min. Absorbance was then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated using the following formula:

Cell viability (%)=(optical density (OD)sample−ODblank)/(ODcontrol−ODblank)×100%.

Oil Red O staining

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells/mL. Cells were pretreated with colchicine (10 ng/mL) or PBS for 24 hours, followed by stimulation with oxidised LDL (ox-LDL, 25 µg/mL) for another 24 hours. Oil Red O (ORO) staining was performed using a commercial kit (BL1541, Biosharp) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, cells were fixed with ORO fixative for 20 min after being rinsed twice with PBS, then ORO staining solution was added for incubation of 15 min. Subsequently, the remaining staining solution was removed with 60% isopropanol, and Mayer’s haematoxylin staining solution was added for incubation of 2 min after rinsing with PBS. Finally, lipid accumulation was visualised under a light microscope, and the degree of lipid deposition was expressed as the ratio of ORO-positive cells to the total number of cells.

Quantitative analysis of intracellular cholesterol

RAW264.7 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells/mL. After experimental stimulation, cells were gently rinsed with PBS and then 200 μL of BeyoLysis Buffer A for Metabolic Assay was added to each well. The cells were thoroughly lysed by ultrasonic disruption at 4°C, followed by centrifugation at 12 000× g at 4°C for 5 min. The supernatant was collected for subsequent detection. Protein concentrations of lysates were detected using Pierce BCA assay kit (23227, Thermo Fisher). For total cholesterol (TC), FC and cholesteryl ester (CE) measurement, the Amplex Red cholesterol and cholesteryl ester assay kit (S0211S, Beyotime) was used in current experiments. All procedures were performed in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions and normalised to total protein concentrations.

Cholesterol efflux evaluation

THP-1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1×10⁵ cells per well and differentiated into macrophages by treatment with 100 ng/mL PMA for 24 hours. Cells were then labelled with 5 μM BODIPY-cholesterol (HY-125746, MedChemExpress) for 2 hours. After that, macrophages were rinsed with serum-free DMEM and underwent 24 hours incubation with 10 μg/mL apoA-I (P7280, Beyotime) as a cholesterol receptor in serum-free DMEM, along with colchicine (5 and 10 ng/mL) or PBS co-stimulation. Subsequently, the supernatant was collected to determine the fluorescence intensity (Fsup) (Ex/Em: 505/515 nm), while the remaining cells were lysed by Radio-immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer on ice for 10 min. Lysates were collected to measure the fluorescence intensity (Flys). Cholesterol efflux rate was calculated as follows:

Cholesterol efflux rate (%)=Fsup/(Fsup+Flys).

Protein extraction

Total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (R0010, Sorlarbio) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (P1045, Beyotime). Following lysis on ice, the samples were centrifuged at 12 000× g at 4°C for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected as the total protein sample. Membrane protein extraction was performed using a membrane and cytosol protein extraction kit (P0033, Beyotime), with detailed procedures referring to our previous work.17 All extracted protein samples were normalised for concentration using a BCA protein assay kit (23227, ThermoFisher Scientific) and then stored at −80°C until detection.

Molecular docking

The ABCA1 protein structure (Protein Data Bank (PDB) ID: 5XJY) was retrieved from PDB and preprocessed using AutoDockTools. Crystallographic waters and heteroatoms were removed, hydrogen atoms added and Gasteiger charges assigned. Energy minimisation was conducted using the Chemistry at HARvard Macromolecular Mechanics (CHARMM) force field to optimise the structure. The three-dimensional structure of colchicine (compound ID: 6167) was obtained from the PubChem database. The molecular docking between colchicine and ABCA1, as well as the subsequent result analysis, was conducted on the Easy2MD platform (https://easy2md.com/).

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA of THP-1 cells was extracted using TRIzol reagent after the cells were treated with colchicine. Transcriptome sequencing, including library construction, sequencing, data quality control and reference genome alignment, was entrusted to Beijing Jinghe Biotechnology for completion. Differential expression, quantitative and enrichment analyses were subsequently performed on the raw sequencing data to elucidate the potential regulatory mechanisms of colchicine on ABCA1 expression.

Quantitative RT-PCR analysis

Total RNA of J774A.1 and THP-1 cells was extracted using TRIzol reagent after the cells were treated with colchicine. The detailed procedures of RT-PCR refer to our previous work.21 Primers used in current experiments (LXRα, ABCA1, Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH)) were designed by PrimerBank and checked by the oligonucleotide property calculator as follows:

All the primers were synthesised by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The transcript abundance was normalised to the GAPDH messenger RNA (mRNA) level.

ABCA1 degradation analysis

To further investigate whether colchicine affects the stability of ABCA1, the present experiment conducted an analysis of the degradation rate of ABCA1. Specifically, THP-1 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells/mL. After the cells reached 70%–80% confluence, they were induced with 100 ng/mL PMA for 24 hours to differentiate into THP-1 macrophages. Subsequently, the cells were pretreated with colchicine (10 ng/mL) or 1% BSA for 24 hours. Finally, after rinsing the cells with 1× PBS, the cells were incubated in 1% BSA-RPMI 1640 medium containing 20 μg/mL CHX for different time periods (0, 15, 30, 60, 120 and 240 min). Following cell collection, the expression levels of ABCA1 at different time points were detected using western blotting.

Plasmid construction and transfection

Based on transcriptomic data analysis, three overexpression plasmids, including pCMV-3Flag-ABCA1 (NM_005502.3), pCMV-Myc-DTL (NM_016448.4) and pcDNA3.1(+)-HA-Ubiquitin, were constructed in current experiments to investigate the regulatory mechanism of colchicine on the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway (UPP) of ABCA1. Above plasmids were synthesised by Beijing RuiBiotech.

293T cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5×105 cells/mL. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent and Opti-MEM medium when the cells reached 70%–90% confluence. Briefly, the transfection reagent was prepared by mixing 3.5 μL Lipofectamine 3000 with 125 μL Opti-MEM medium. Subsequently, 2.5 μg plasmid was mixed with 125 μL Opti-MEM medium, and P3000 reagent (2 μL/μg DNA) was added to obtain the final plasmid mixture. Then, the above-prepared transfection reagent and plasmid mixture were mixed and incubated at room temperature for 10–15 min to form liposome complexes. Finally, 250 μL liposome complex was added dropwise to each well. Transfection efficiency was detected by western blotting at 24, 36 and 48 hours after cell transfection, respectively.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Dynabeads protein-A immunoprecipitation kit (10006D, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for the current co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiment, with procedures performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, 50 μL Dynabeads were resuspended and subjected to magnetic separation to remove the supernatant. Subsequently, 200 μL Ab binding and washing buffer containing Flag antibody was added, and magnetic separation was performed again to remove the supernatant after incubation at room temperature for 10 min. Thereafter, cell lysates containing 500 μg of total protein were added, followed by rotation incubation at room temperature for 30 min to form the Dynabeads–Ab–Ag complex. The complex was then washed three times with 200 μL washing buffer. Finally, 20 µL elution buffer and 10 µL of premixed NuPAGE LDS sample Buffer (NP0007, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and NuPAGE sample reducing agent (NP0004, Thermo Fisher Scientific) were added. After heating at 70°C for 10 min, magnetic separation was conducted, and the supernatant samples were retained for subsequent detection.

Immunoblotting

The isolated proteins were resolved using 8% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis to assess protein expression levels. After that, proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes, which were blocked with 5% non-fat milk or 3% BSA for 1 hour at room temperature. Following this, the entire membrane was washed with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) to remove residual milk. Next, membrane sections containing target protein bands with significant molecular weight differences were excised and incubated overnight at 4℃ with their respective primary antibodies: ABCA1 (254 kDa, 1:1000 dilution), ABCG1 (75 kDa, 1:1000 dilution), LXRα (51 kDa, 1:1000 dilution), DTL (95 kDa, 1:1000 dilution), Flag (1:10 000 dilution), Hemagglutinin (HA) (1:5000 dilution), Myc (1:10 000 dilution) and GAPDH (37 kDa, 1:2000 dilution). After that, all bands were incubated with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (A0208 and A0216, Beyotime) and visualised using Enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (PE0011, Solarbio). Lastly, the multiple small membrane sections excised from the same original membrane were visualised individually, and the grey values of the protein bands were quantified. These data were then used to perform statistical analysis on the relative protein expression levels, with normalisation against GAPDH.

Statistical analysis

All the aforementioned experiments were independently replicated three times to ensure reproducibility. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism V.9.0.0, with results presented as mean±SD or SEM. Differences between two groups were assessed using Student’s t-test, while comparisons involving three or more groups were analysed by one-way analysis of variance. Dunnett’s multiple comparison test was applied to normally distributed data, whereas non-parametric tests were used for non-normally distributed data. Statistical significance was defined as a p value <0.05.

Results

Colchicine hinders macrophage conversion to foam cells by promoting cholesterol efflux

The CCK-8 was used to detect cell viability after intervention with different concentrations (1, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100 and 200 ng/mL) of colchicine, so as to determine the safe dose of colchicine in following experiments. As shown in figure 1A–C, significant cytotoxicity was observed in multiple macrophage cells (RAW264.7, THP-1, J774A.1) when the experimental concentration of colchicine exceeded 10 ng/mL. Therefore, the dosage of colchicine was set to 10 ng/mL in subsequent experiments.

Given that macrophages typically exhibit increased cell volume when excessive uptake of ox-LDL leads to lipid accumulation, we performed morphological analysis of RAW264.7 cells prior to ORO staining. The results revealed that ox-LDL treatment increased cell volume compared with the control group, while colchicine treatment reduced cell volume compared with the ox-LDL group (25 µg/mL) (figure 1D). Besides, ORO staining results showed that intracellular neutral lipid droplets (such as triglycerides and CE) accumulated markedly after ox-LDL treatment, indirectly reflecting the state of intracellular cholesterol overload. However, colchicine intervention significantly reduced intracellular lipid droplet accumulation compared with the ox-LDL group (25 µg/mL) (figure 1E,F). Moreover, since the ratio of intracellular CE to TC can directly reflect the degree of foam formation, the present study used a CE detection kit to measure the contents of intracellular FC, CE and TC. The results showed that colchicine dramatically decreased the CE/TC value compared with the ox-LDL group, indicating that colchicine can reverse the foam cell formation process of macrophages (figure 1G,H). In addition, the current findings also found that therapeutic intervention with colchicine had a stronger effect on reversing macrophage foam cell formation than preventive intervention, suggesting that colchicine may reverse macrophage foam cell formation mainly by acting on the cholesterol efflux rather than the cholesterol uptake period (figure 1I).

Colchicine promotes the ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux in macrophage

To further confirm the promotion of colchicine on cholesterol efflux in macrophages, we used BODIPY-cholesterol to incubate macrophages to evaluate the cholesterol efflux rate. Intracellular BODIPY-cholesterol levels were observed using fluorescence microscopy, and the contents of BODIPY-cholesterol in both intracellular and extracellular compartments were quantitatively analysed using a fluorescence microplate reader to calculate the cholesterol efflux rate. As displayed in figure 2, colchicine significantly reduced cholesterol content in THP-1 macrophages and J774A.1 macrophages (figure 2A). Additionally, colchicine (10 ng/mL) also notably enhanced the cholesterol efflux process in macrophages (J774A.1 and THP-1 macrophages) (figure 2B,C). Interestingly, this promotion was limited by the inhibition of AMPK Thr172 phosphorylation (figure 2D). The above results confirm that colchicine indeed promotes cholesterol efflux in macrophages, but the underlying pathways involved remain to be further explored.

Reportedly, macrophage cholesterol efflux is mainly mediated by ABCA1 and ABCG1; therefore, we next focused on investigating these two pathways. As shown in figure 3, colchicine sharply increased the expression level of ABCA1 but had no obvious effect on the expression level of ABCG1 (figure 3A–C). It is thus inferred that the promotion of colchicine on macrophage cholesterol efflux is mediated by ABCA1 rather than ABCG1. Indeed, further studies revealed that colchicine dramatically upregulated the expression of functional ABCA1 in various macrophages (THP-1, J774A.1 and RAW264.7 cells), which again confirms the notion that colchicine can accelerate the ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux (figure 3D–K). Notably, molecular docking simulation analysis showed that the binding constant (Kd) for the direct interaction between colchicine and ABCA1 was 5.2 μM, which is much higher than the concentration of colchicine used in the current experiment (10 ng/mL) (figure 3L,M). This indicates that there is no effective direct binding between colchicine and ABCA1 under the current experimental conditions, and there may exist other key intermediate regulatory molecules.

Colchicine promotes ABCA1 expression not through the LXRα-targeted transcriptional pathway

We next conducted studies on the transcriptional regulation of ABCA1 by combining RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) mining and experimental validation, aiming to identify the potential mechanism by which colchicine regulates ABCA1 expression. RNA-Seq analysis found that there were 11 454 co-expressed genes between the colchicine group and the normal group, among which 237 genes had significantly changed expression levels when macrophages were treated with colchicine, with 163 genes downregulated and 74 genes upregulated (figure 4A,B). However, analysis on differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed that the LXRα, a key transcription factor regulating the transcriptional expression of ABCA1, had no alteration in the expression level after colchicine treatment (figure 4C). Experimental results also showed that the protein expression level of LXRα remained unchanged in THP-1 and J774A.1 macrophages, and there was no significant difference in the mRNA level of ABCA1 either (figure 4D–I). These findings collectively indicate that colchicine regulates ABCA1 expression not through the LXRα-targeted transcriptional pathway, and the potential mechanism involved remains to be further explored. In addition, we applied Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis to analyse the biological processes, molecular functions and cellular components related to colchicine. The results showed that besides participating in its classic immune system processes, colchicine was also involved in cellular response to lipid (figure 4J–L). Meanwhile, the Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis further revealed that colchicine was involved in the cellular cholesterol metabolism pathway (figure 4M). These findings collectively support the view that colchicine can participate in lipid metabolism and reverse the process of macrophage foam formation.

Colchicine delays the degradation of ABCA1 through the ubiquitination pathway

Reportedly, in addition to the transcriptional regulatory pathway, post-translational modification (such as phosphorylation, ubiquitination, acetylation, etc) is also an important pathway involved in the regulation of protein expression.22 Therefore, the protein synthesis inhibitor CHX was used to monitor the degradation process of ABCA1 at different time points so as to evaluate whether colchicine enhances ABCA1 expression by improving its stability. As shown in figure 5A–D, in both J774A.1 and THP-1 macrophages, the relative content of ABCA1 gradually decreased over time after CHX treatment. However, after preventive intervention with colchicine for 24 hours, the decrease in the relative content of ABCA1 over time was smaller, showing a significant difference from the CHX group (figure 5C,D). This indicates that colchicine can indeed enhance the protein stability of ABCA1 by blocking its degradation process. Subsequently, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 was employed to determine the specific pathway through which colchicine delays ABCA1 degradation. Present data showed that MG132 significantly inhibited CHX-induced ABCA1 degradation in both J774A.1 and THP-1 macrophages (figure 5E–G), indicating that colchicine most likely enhances ABCA1 expression by inhibiting the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation pathway of ABCA1. This inference was further confirmed by Co-IP experiments, which showed that colchicine can significantly reduce the ubiquitination level of ABCA1, thereby enhancing its own expression (figure 5H–J).

Colchicine blocks the ABCA1 ubiquitination modification process centred on DTL

To deeply reveal the potential mechanism by which colchicine blocks the ubiquitination of ABCA1 (as confirmed in figure 5), the transcriptomic data were carefully mined again. Surprisingly, after analysing all the 237 DEGs, we identified an important target protein—DTL, which has ubiquitin-protein transferase activity and is involved in the protein ubiquitination process. The expression level of this gene decreased significantly after colchicine treatment (figure 6A, marked with a red box). This targeted screening, based on the confirmed ubiquitin-dependent degradation of ABCA1 (figure 5), strongly suggests that colchicine may exert its effect of promoting ABCA1 expression by targeting DTL-mediated ubiquitination modification of ABCA1.

To verify whether ABCA1 is a substrate protein of DTL, the current study constructed corresponding overexpression plasmids (pCMV-3Flag-ABCA1, pCMV-Myc-DTL) to perform co-transfection analysis as well as Co-IP experiments to determine whether ABCA1 is a substrate protein of DTL. As shown in figure 6B,C, compared with the control group (only transfected with pCMV-3Flag-ABCA1), co-transfection of pCMV-3Flag-ABCA1 and pCMV-Myc-DTL sharply reduced the expression level of ABCA1, while this reduction was considerably reversed after colchicine treatment. Although there was still a gap compared with the control group, it confirmed the view that ABCA1 was the substrate protein of DTL.

Based on the above findings, we next examined the endogenous expression correlation between DTL and ABCA1 to validate this regulatory relationship in physiological contexts. As presented in figure 6D,E, colchicine treatment led to a significant decrease in DTL protein expression, accompanied by a concurrent increase in the ABCA1 protein level, exhibiting a distinct negative correlation between DTL and ABCA1. This result aligns with the expected regulatory pattern of an E3 ligase and its substrate, further reinforcing that colchicine stabilises ABCA1 by downregulating DTL.

To our knowledge, the function of E3 ubiquitin ligase in degrading substrate proteins is related not only to its quantity but also to the strength of its interaction with substrate proteins.23 Our data showed that colchicine can not only reduce the expression level of DTL in the lysates (by 20.2%) but also further decrease the expression level of DTL in the IP samples (by 32.3%) (figure 6F,G). This finding indicates that colchicine can collectively hinder the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of ABCA1 by reducing both the absolute content of DTL and the interaction strength between DTL and ABCA1. This dual inhibitory effect directly explains how colchicine impairs DTL-mediated ABCA1 degradation, which is consistent with the reduced ABCA1 ubiquitination observed in figure 5.

Collectively, the data from figure 6, integrated with the key findings in figure 5, establish a coherent regulatory pathway underlying colchicine-mediated ABCA1 stabilisation. Colchicine downregulates DTL expression and inhibits the interaction between DTL and ABCA1—two critical steps for E3 ligase-mediated substrate ubiquitination. This mechanism directly contributes to the reduced ABCA1 ubiquitination (figure 5) and subsequent blockade of proteasomal degradation, ultimately leading to the stabilisation and upregulation of ABCA1 protein levels. These results collectively confirm that DTL is the key E3 ubiquitin ligase mediating the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation of ABCA1, and colchicine exerts its regulatory effect by targeting DTL.

Discussion

To date, CVD remains the leading cause of death and disability worldwide.24 Among various risk factors, dyslipidaemia, particularly hypercholesterolaemia, plays a central role in the initiation and progression of CVD.25 Hence, maintaining cholesterol homeostasis to control lipid levels represents critical strategies to mitigate cardiovascular risk.26 In this study, we observed that colchicine, beyond its classical anti-inflammatory properties, markedly reverses ox-LDL-induced foam cell formation in macrophages. This effect is primarily mediated through enhanced cholesterol efflux, suggesting that the cardioprotective effects of colchicine are not solely attributable to its anti-inflammatory action. Rather, acceleration of cholesterol efflux and inhibition of foam cell formation may represent an additional mechanism through which colchicine confers atheroprotection in patients with ASCVD.

Macrophage cholesterol efflux involves multiple pathways, such as ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter-mediated pathways and aqueous diffusion pathways, among which ABC transporter-mediated routes play a predominant role.27 Thus, we next combined molecular biology approaches with transcriptomic profiling to elucidate the above mechanism. Our data demonstrated that colchicine upregulates ABCA1 expression in various macrophage models, implicating the ABCA1-dependent pathway as a critical mediator of its cholesterol efflux-promoting effect. Interestingly, molecular docking revealed no direct interaction between colchicine and ABCA1, implying the existence of key intermediary molecules responsible for this regulation.

Transcriptomic analysis further confirmed colchicine’s involvement in cholesterol metabolic pathways, as supported by GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. However, neither DEGs analysis nor experimental validation revealed alterations in LXRα, a major upstream transcriptional regulator of ABCA1. These results suggest that colchicine does not affect ABCA1 transcription but rather regulates its post-translational fate. Consistent with this notion, protein half-life experiments showed that colchicine prolongs ABCA1 stability and delays its degradation. Further analysis revealed that colchicine reduces ABCA1 ubiquitination and inhibits its degradation via the UPP. Following this line of investigation, we screened the gene descriptions of all 237 DEGs and found that only DTL and ubiquitin-specific protease 18 (USP18) were associated with ubiquitination. Among them, USP18 is a special member of the USP family as it only deconjugates the ubiquitin-like protein interferon-stimulated gene 15 from target proteins but is not active towards ubiquitin. Accordingly, we focused our subsequent research on DTL—a previously unreported E3 ubiquitin ligase that may mediate the ubiquitination and degradation of the ABCA1 protein. Functional verification confirmed that colchicine stabilises ABCA1 by attenuating DTL-mediated ubiquitination, revealing a previously unrecognised post-translational mechanism through which colchicine enhances cholesterol efflux.

Importantly, the identification of DTL as a novel E3 ubiquitin ligase involved in ABCA1 regulation represents a major mechanistic advancement in understanding how protein ubiquitination influences macrophage cholesterol metabolism. Previous studies have largely focused on canonical E3 ligases such as Neural precursor cell-expressed developmentally down regulated gene 4 (NEDD4-1), HECT, UBA and WWE domain containing 1 (HUWE1) and HECT domain containing E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 1 (HECTD1) in ABCA1 regulation28 29; the present findings expand this paradigm by introducing DTL as a new player in cholesterol efflux control. This discovery not only uncovers a new molecular target for modulating ABCA1 stability but also provides a mechanistic bridge linking colchicine therapy to lipid metabolic regulation in the context of atherosclerosis.

Further experiments revealed that colchicine not only suppresses DTL expression but also disrupts its interaction with ABCA1, jointly inhibiting DTL-mediated ubiquitin-dependent ABCA1 degradation. Another notable finding is that inhibition of AMPK Thr172 phosphorylation attenuated colchicine-induced ABCA1 upregulation and cholesterol efflux, suggesting that AMPK activation may also participate in this regulatory cascade. Whether colchicine functions as an allosteric modulator of AMPK and how the AMPK-DTL-ABCA1 axis contributes to macrophage lipid homeostasis warrant further exploration.

In summary, our study identifies DTL as a key E3 ubiquitin ligase in the post-translational regulation of ABCA1 and establishes a novel DTL-dependent mechanism underlying the cardioprotective actions of colchicine. By downregulating DTL and disrupting its interaction with ABCA1, colchicine delays ABCA1 ubiquitin-dependent degradation, enhances its stability, promotes cholesterol efflux and ultimately reverses foam cell formation (figure 7). These findings not only deepen our mechanistic understanding of colchicine’s cardiovascular benefits but also highlight DTL as a promising therapeutic target for ASCVD.

Limitations

Given that colchicine has been well-established as an effective agent for reducing CVD risk in the general population and has been incorporated into relevant clinical guidelines, this study did not perform comprehensive in vivo experiments to reevaluate its cardiovascular protective effects. Instead, we focused on elucidating its underlying pharmacological mechanisms, with the aim of providing a theoretical foundation for its broader clinical application. Our future studies will further verify the accuracy of the proposed mechanisms through animal experiments. Specifically, we will focus on delineating the regulatory axis between AMPK and ABCA1, which may further clarify how colchicine orchestrates the crosstalk between metabolic and inflammatory pathways. A deeper understanding of these mechanisms will provide a stronger scientific foundation for updating ASCVD clinical management guidelines and optimising the rational therapeutic application of colchicine.

Conclusion

In summary, this study elucidates a novel pharmacological mechanism underlying the cardiovascular protective effects of colchicine in patients with ASCVD. Beyond its well-established anti-inflammatory properties, colchicine was found to effectively reverse ox-LDL-induced macrophage foam cell formation, primarily by enhancing cholesterol efflux rather than acting solely through anti-inflammatory pathways. Mechanistically, we excluded both direct and transcriptional regulation of ABCA1 by colchicine and instead identified a post-translational regulatory mechanism centred on DTL. Specifically, colchicine significantly downregulates DTL expression and weakens its interaction with ABCA1, thereby synergistically inhibiting DTL-mediated ubiquitin-dependent degradation of ABCA1. This process stabilises ABCA1 protein levels, promotes ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux and ultimately contributes to the reversal of foam cell formation. These findings reveal a previously unrecognised DTL-dependent mechanism through which colchicine exerts atheroprotective effects, highlighting DTL as a novel E3 ligase involved in cholesterol homeostasis. Importantly, this work expands the pharmacological profile of colchicine from a classical anti-inflammatory drug to a multifunctional agent that modulates both inflammation and lipid metabolism in ASCVD.
