Thyroid storm triggered by immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy
Authors: Junya Hagiwara, Miku Sekine, Eiji Sakawaki, Sonoko Sakawaki, Yoshihiro Takeyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWhile immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionised cancer treatment, they can cause immune-related adverse events, including thyroid dysfunction and rarely, thyroid storm. Here, we present a rare case of thyroid storm occurring during ICI therapy, in which clinical and immunological findings strongly suggested Graves’ disease. A man in his 80s with advanced lung cancer was hospitalised for multiple rib fractures. During admission, he developed fever, delirium, atrial fibrillation and marked thyrotoxicosis, leading to a diagnosis of thyroid storm. Given his prior treatment with nivolumab and ipilimumab, destructive thyroiditis was initially suspected, as this is the most common mechanism of ICI-associated thyrotoxicosis. However, marked thyroid-stimulating antibody positivity was more suggestive of Graves’ disease than destructive thyroiditis. This case highlights that ICI therapy-associated thyroid storm can arise not only from destructive thyroiditis but also from Graves’ disease. Therefore, careful clinical evaluation is essential to determine the underlying pathophysiology and guide optimal management.Keywords: Thyrotoxicosis, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors, Endocrine system, Adult intensive care

Background

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionised cancer therapy by enhancing the host immune response against malignant cells, leading to significant improvements in overall survival across various malignancies.1 2 However, by unleashing the immune system, ICIs can also disrupt immune tolerance and trigger immune-related adverse events (irAEs), which often mimic autoimmune disorders.3 Thyroid dysfunction is a frequent endocrine irAE, occurring in up to 5%–20% of patients receiving ICIs.4 5 Most cases are mild and present as destructive thyroiditis or transient thyrotoxicosis followed by hypothyroidism.6 7 Conversely, thyroid storm, a life-threatening exacerbation of thyrotoxicosis, is extremely rare in the context of ICI therapy.8,14 The underlying mechanism is typically destructive thyroiditis; however, cases with Graves’ disease are exceedingly uncommon. Given its rarity and potential for rapid deterioration, early recognition and appropriate management of thyroid storm related to ICI therapy are essential.

In the present report, we describe a rare case of thyroid storm occurring during ICI therapy in a patient hospitalised for traumatic injuries, in which clinical and immunological findings strongly suggested Graves’ disease rather than the more common destructive thyroiditis. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges and therapeutic considerations in differentiating destructive thyroiditis from Graves’ disease in patients with ICI-induced thyrotoxicosis and underscores the importance of careful pathophysiological assessment to guide optimal management.

Case presentation

A man in his 80s with stage-IVA squamous cell carcinoma of the lung, rheumatoid arthritis, diabetes mellitus and hypertension presented with left chest pain after falling and striking his chest against a stone wall. He had been receiving combination ICI therapy with nivolumab and ipilimumab for lung cancer, with the most recent dose administered 25 days before admission. On arrival, the patient was alert with a temperature of 36.0°C, blood pressure of 152/69 mm Hg, heart rate of 87 beats/min, respiratory rate of 18 breaths/min and oxygen saturation of 100% on room air. Physical examination revealed bruising and tenderness over the left chest wall. Laboratory tests revealed no remarkable abnormalities. Chest CT demonstrated fractures of the left 5th to 11th ribs. Accordingly, the patient was diagnosed with multiple left-rib fractures and admitted for pain control owing to limited mobility. From the time of admission, the patient received intravenous fentanyl, oral loxoprofen sodium and a combination tablet of tramadol hydrochloride and acetaminophen; however, pain control remained inadequate. On hospital day 5, the patient developed delirium and tachycardic atrial fibrillation, followed by the onset of fever on day 7.

Investigations

On hospital day 7, inflammatory markers became elevated, and CT revealed infiltrates suggestive of pneumonia. Subsequent laboratory tests indicated a thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) level of <0.005 mIU/L (reference range: 0.50–5.00 mIU/L), a free thyroxine (FT4) level ≥100.0 pmol/L (reference range: 11.6–21.9 pmol/L) and a free triiodothyronine (FT3) level of 22.7 pmol/L (reference range: 3.53–6.14 pmol/L); these results suggested the presence of thyroid storm. Thyroid ultrasonography on day 9 demonstrated no significant enlargement or increased blood flow in the thyroid gland; on day 11, laboratory testing revealed antithyroglobulin antibody levels of 1590 kIU/L (reference<19.3 kIU/L), antithyroid peroxidase antibody levels of 36.8 kIU/L (reference<3.3 kIU/L), thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor antibody (TRAb) levels<1.0 IU/L (reference<1.0 IU/L) and thyroid-stimulating antibody (TSAb) levels of 640% (reference<110%).

Differential diagnosis

Fever, delirium and atrial fibrillation were observed, and thyroid function tests demonstrated suppressed TSH and elevated FT3 and FT4 levels, raising clinical suspicion for thyroid storm. The Burch–Wartofsky Point Scale (BWPS) is a clinical scoring system that is widely used to assess the likelihood of thyroid storm, with a score ≥45 strongly suggestive of the diagnosis.15 The BWPS score was 75 in the present case. Differential diagnoses for underlying causes of severe thyrotoxicosis included Graves’ disease, destructive thyroiditis (including ICI-induced thyroiditis), medication-induced thyrotoxicosis, toxic adenoma and toxic multinodular goitre. Ultrasonographic findings revealed neither thyroid adenoma nor multinodular goitre. Moreover, the patient had no history of exposure to amiodarone, iodine-containing agents, salicylates, mefenamic acid or other medications known to affect thyroid hormone levels, making medication-induced thyrotoxicosis unlikely. Given the recent administration of nivolumab and ipilimumab, ICI-induced destructive thyroiditis was initially considered. However, the marked TSAb positivity strongly supported antibody-mediated stimulation of the TSH receptor, making destructive thyroiditis unlikely. Collectively, these findings were most suggestive of Graves’ disease associated with ICI therapy.

Outcome and follow-up

On day 5, continuous intravenous landiolol, a short-acting β1-selective blocker, was initiated for rate control. On day 7, treatment with hydrocortisone (300 mg/day), potassium iodide (200 mg/day) and thiamazole (60 mg/day) was initiated, after which the symptoms of thyrotoxicosis improved rapidly. As ICI-induced destructive thyroiditis was initially considered, thiamazole and potassium iodide were discontinued on day 11 (figure 1). However, the patient again developed fever, delirium and tachycardic atrial fibrillation. When both medications were resumed, symptoms promptly improved, supporting the diagnosis of Graves’ disease associated with ICI therapy. On day 15, the general condition of the patient stabilised, and he was transferred to the hospital, where he was receiving treatment for lung cancer (figure 2). The patient died of progressive lung cancer approximately 2 months later.

Discussion

We present a rare case of thyroid storm associated with ICI therapy, in which clinical and immunological findings strongly suggested a diagnosis of Graves’ disease rather than the more commonly encountered destructive thyroiditis. To the best of our knowledge, reports of thyroid storm occurring amid ICI therapy and associated with Graves’ disease are extremely limited.

ICIs have recently become widely used and demonstrated unprecedented therapeutic efficacy in various cancers.1 2 However, immune-related adverse events (irAEs) are increasingly reported with the use of ICIs. These reactions occur owing to excessive immune responses against normal organs and can involve multiple organ systems.3 Thyroid dysfunction is one of the most common endocrine irAEs, with significantly higher incidence in combination therapy than in monotherapy.7 Thyroid dysfunction due to irAEs most often occurs within 2–6 weeks after initiation of ICI therapy.16 Among patients who develop thyroid irAEs, 22% present with hypothyroidism as the initial manifestation, whereas the remaining 78% initially present with thyrotoxicosis, approximately 80% of these patients subsequently progress to hypothyroidism.4 The underlying pathophysiology is considered to involve immune-mediated acute inflammation followed by destruction of the thyroid gland, resulting in thyrotoxicosis followed by hypothyroidism or isolated hypothyroidism.17 However, persistent hyperthyroidism resembling Graves’ disease has also been reported in rare cases, although its underlying mechanism remains unclear.18 While most cases are asymptomatic, approximately 1.6% require hospitalisation, and cases progressing to thyroid storm are extremely rare. In the present study, a database search in PubMed using the search terms (‘immune checkpoint inhibitor’ OR ‘immune-related adverse event’ OR ‘immune-related adverse events’ OR nivolumab OR ipilimumab OR pembrolizumab) AND (‘thyroid storm’ OR ‘thyrotoxic crisis’) revealed seven previously reported cases of ICI-induced thyroid storm (table 1).8,14 Generally, antithyroid drugs are ineffective for thyroid storm caused by destructive thyroiditis and should not be used for its management.19 20

We initially considered ICI therapy-associated destructive thyroiditis owing to the absence of thyroid tenderness or enlargement, lack of increased blood flow on ultrasonography and a history of ICI treatment. However, TRAb/TSAb positivity strongly suggested a diagnosis of Graves’ disease and is atypical for destructive thyroiditis. Although antithyroid drugs were discontinued based on the presumed diagnosis of destructive thyroiditis, the rapid recurrence of thyrotoxicosis and prompt clinical improvement after therapy resumption further supported the diagnosis of Graves’ disease.

One important limitation of this case report is the lack of serial thyroid hormone measurements surrounding the discontinuation and reinitiation of antithyroid therapy. As a result, dynamic biochemical confirmation of recurrent thyrotoxicosis was not available and we cannot be certain that his clinical improvement, apparent decompensation and subsequent improvement in the 8 day window were necessarily related to changes in his degree of thyrotoxicosis. However, the diagnosis of Graves’ disease in the context of ICI therapy has been established in prior reports based on the combined presence of thyrotoxicosis and TRAb/TSI positivity, given the high specificity of these antibodies for autoimmune stimulatory hyperthyroidism.21 22

Two potential mechanisms may explain the development of thyrotoxicosis due to Graves’ disease in the present case. First, pre-existing TSAb/TRAb may have been exacerbated by ICI therapy. A previous study reported that some individuals who are asymptomatically positive for TSAb/TRAb may develop thyrotoxicosis triggered by ICI treatment.22 23 Second, ICIs may induce de novo production of thyroid autoantibodies; patients who initially tested negative for autoantibodies have subsequently become TRAb-positive and developed thyrotoxicosis following ICI therapy.22 24 Furthermore, we believe that the progression of this case to a life-threatening thyroid storm was likely multifactorial. Specifically, combination ICI therapy, the presence of thyroid autoantibodies and severe stress due to trauma may have synergistically contributed to the abrupt elevation of thyroid hormone levels, ultimately leading to the development of thyroid storm.

In conclusion, we report a rare case of thyroid storm occurring during ICI therapy, in which clinical and immunological findings strongly suggested a diagnosis of Graves’ disease. With the increasing use of ICIs across various malignancies, recognition of atypical thyroid irAEs has become increasingly important. This case reveals that thyroid irAEs manifest not only as destructive thyroiditis but also as a rare Graves’ disease capable of progressing to thyroid storm. Although destructive thyroiditis is the typical mechanism of ICI-induced thyrotoxicosis, this case demonstrated marked TSAb positivity and the rapid recurrence of thyroid storm immediately after withdrawal of antithyroid therapy, followed by prompt clinical improvement when these agents were resumed. These findings emphasise the need for clinicians to consider Graves’ disease even when imaging and laboratory results initially appear consistent with destructive thyroiditis, particularly in the presence of TRAb/TSAb positivity. Careful assessment of the underlying pathophysiology is essential, as premature discontinuation of antithyroid medications may lead to rapid deterioration.
