Effects of albumin-platelet-rich fibrin as an adjunct to non-surgical periodontal therapy in patients with periodontitis: a randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Erkan Özcan, Berrak Ulu Emir, Emre Avcı, Savaş Özarslantürk, Şeyma Çardakcı Bahar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) is a biological material with high regenerative potential, its rapid resorption limits its clinical effectiveness. Albumin-platelet-rich fibrin (Alb-PRF), developed in recent years, exhibits prolonged resorption time. The aim of this study was to evaluate the clinical, radiographic, and biochemical effects of Alb-PRF application into periodontal pockets following non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT) using a randomized split-mouth design.MethodsThis study included 23 patients diagnosed with Stage III Grade B periodontitis. Following completion of NSPT, Alb-PRF was applied into periodontal pockets at the test sites. Gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) samples were collected on days 7, 14, and 21. Clinical periodontal parameters were recorded at baseline, 3 and 6 months, and radiographs were obtained at baseline and at 6 months. Levels of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), platelet-derived growth factor-BB (PDGF-BB), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and basic fibroblast growth factor (b-FGF) in GCF were analyzed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Primary outcome measures included changes in probing depth (PD), clinical attachment loss (CAL), and GCF growth factor levels, while secondary outcome measures included changes in plaque index, gingival index (GI), bleeding on probing, gingival recession, and radiographic parameters.ResultsGI, PD, and CAL were significantly lower at test sites at 3 months (p < 0.01). However, these differences were not maintained at the 6-month follow-up. Radiographic defect depth and bone loss percentage were significantly lower at test sites at 6 months (p < 0.01). Transforming growth factor-β values were significantly higher at test sites at all subsequent time points (p < 0.01, p < 0.01, and p < 0.05, respectively).ConclusionsAlb-PRF may represent a beneficial adjunctive approach for early periodontal healing following NSPT; however, further studies are needed to clarify the sustainability of these effects over time.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT07363135, registered on 15 January 2026 (retrospectively registered).

Background

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by the destruction of the periodontal ligament and alveolar bone surrounding the teeth and may lead to tooth mobility and eventual tooth loss [1]. Non-surgical periodontal therapy (NSPT) constitutes the most fundamental step in the treatment of periodontitis [2]. However, to enhance the effectiveness of NSPT, various adjunctive approaches, including local antimicrobial agents, photodynamic therapy, and low-level laser therapy, have been applied to periodontal pockets [3, 4].

Platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) is an autologous biological material derived from blood and contains high concentrations of cells and growth factors. Growth factors such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), basic fibroblast growth factor (b-FGF), and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) enhance cellular proliferation, differentiation, and migration, thereby supporting wound healing [5]. PDGF-BB plays a key role in the activation of neutrophils, macrophages, and fibroblasts and promotes increased protein synthesis [6]. VEGF is primarily involved in angiogenesis, while FGF contributes to both vascularization and fibroblast activation [7, 8]. In addition, TGF-β exhibits both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory properties, regulating the inflammatory response and playing a critical role in tissue healing and regenerative processes [9].

In recent years, intra-pocket application of PRF has been shown to exert beneficial effects on probing depth (PD), clinical attachment loss (CAL) and growth factor levels in gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) [10–12]. Despite these favorable outcomes, the clinical effectiveness of PRF is limited by its rapid resorption, with a decline in the release of growth factors occurring approximately 10–15 days after application [13]. To overcome this limitation, a novel formulation known as albumin-platelet-rich fibrin (Alb-PRF) has been developed. Heating followed by cooling of the platelet-poor plasma (PPP) layer induces albumin gelation, thereby extending the resorption time from approximately 2 weeks to up to 3–4 months [14, 15]. This modification reduces the rapid degradation of PRF and enhances its scaffold function at the application site [16].

In this modified protocol, the upper PPP layer is heated at 75 °C for 10 min and subsequently cooled to room temperature before being mixed with PRF. During this process, proteins such as albumin within the PPP layer undergo heat-induced denaturation, resulting in the formation of an albumin gel. This albumin gel is then combined with PRF to obtain Alb-PRF [17, 18]. Albumin-enriched biomaterials provide a more stable fibrin network, support cell proliferation, and exhibit improved mechanical properties and sustained growth factor release compared with conventional PRF [14, 19]. Owing to its excellent biocompatibility, reduced inflammatory response, and prolonged presence at the application site, Alb-PRF has been suggested to enhance tissue healing and regenerative outcomes [16, 18].

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the intra-pocket application of Alb-PRF using a non-surgical approach. Based on this gap in literature, the primary research question of the present study was whether the prolonged resorption time of Alb-PRF would result in more favorable outcomes in periodontal parameters, such as probing depth PD and CAL. The secondary research question was whether the extended resorption time of Alb-PRF would contribute to radiographic bone level and lead to higher concentrations of growth factors in GCF compared with the control sites.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to evaluate the adjunctive effects of intra-pocket Alb-PRF application following NSPT on GCF growth factor levels, clinical periodontal parameters, and radiographic outcomes at predefined intervals.

Methods

Study design and patient recruitment

The study was designed as a randomized controlled split-mouth clinical trial and received ethical approval from the Health Sciences University Gulhane Training and Research Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee (2023/248). This clinical trial was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration (as revised in 2013) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07363135) and adhered to the CONSORT guidelines [20]. This study included 26 patients who applied to the Department of Periodontology, Gulhane Faculty of Dentistry, University of Health Sciences, Ankara, Turkey, between November 2024 and October 2025. Patient recruitment continued until the required sample size was reached. All participants were informed about the study and signed an informed consent form. CONSORT flowchart of the study

Patients with systemic conditions known to influence periodontal status, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, autoimmune diseases, smoking, pregnancy or lactation, recent use of antibiotics or anti-inflammatory medications, or a history of radiotherapy or chemotherapy, were not included in the study [21]. Eligible patients were diagnosed with generalized Stage III Grade B periodontitis according to the 2017 World Workshop on the Classification of Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases [22]. Grade B periodontitis was established using indirect evidence of disease progression based on the radiographic bone loss-to-age ratio at the worst affected tooth. In addition, smoking and uncontrolled diabetes mellitus were among the exclusion criteria; thus, none of the included patients met the criteria for Grade C periodontitis [23].

Eligible patients presented with vertical intrabony defects in the molar and/or premolar regions (excluding third molars) in two different quadrants, with probing depths ranging from 5 to 9 mm. Patients with acute infections requiring antibiotic therapy, tooth mobility in the teeth selected for sampling, or furcation involvement were excluded from the study. A detailed medical and dental history was obtained from all participants, and demographic data, including age and sex, were recorded. Potential adverse events, such as pain or swelling, were systematically assessed at each follow-up visit.

Clinical and radiographic evaluation

In all enrolled patients, plaque index (PI), gingival index (GI), and bleeding on probing (BOP), PD, CAL and gingival recession (GR) were recorded at six points (mesiobuccal, buccal, distobuccal, mesiopalatal/mesiolingual, palatal/lingual, and distopalatal/distolingual) for each tooth. All clinical measurements were performed by a single experienced blinded investigator (B.U.E.) using a manual periodontal probe (Williams, Hu-Friedy, Chicago, IL) and repeated at 3 and 6 months. Intra-examiner calibration was performed in five patients who were not included in the study by repeating PD and CAL measurements at 48-hour intervals. Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.82 for PD and 0.85 for CAL measurements. Periapical radiographs were obtained at baseline and at 6 months from the teeth exhibiting the greatest periodontal destruction in both the test and control sites (molar and/or premolar teeth with vertical intrabony defects from which GCF samples were collected), using the long cone paralleling technique. Radiographic measurements were performed by a single experienced blinded investigator (S.Ö.). Intra-examiner calibration was performed by repeating the assessment of defect depth, defect angle, and bone loss percentage in five radiographs at a 2-week interval. Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.86, 0.83, and 0.84, respectively.

Randomization

Following clinical and radiographic assessment, two quadrants containing the deepest periodontal pockets in posterior teeth (molar and/or premolar teeth) were selected for each participant. In the split-mouth design, one quadrant was randomly allocated to the test site, while the other quadrant was assigned to the control site. If several sites had comparable characteristics, selection was based on accessibility and the ability to achieve adequate isolation and material application. The split-mouth design was preferred to minimize the influence of individual factors that could affect healing, such as genetic background and nutritional status. The random allocation sequence was generated by the investigator (E.Ö.) using a coin toss for each participant to determine which site would serve as the test and which as the control. Participant enrolment and assignment to the test and control sites were performed by the same investigator, who was not involved in clinical, biochemical or radiographic outcome assessment. The primary outcome measures were changes in PD and CAL, along with changes in growth factor levels in GCF. Secondary outcome measures included changes in PI, GI, BOP, GR, and radiographic parameters of vertical periodontal bone defects.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the intervention, the investigator performing the periodontal therapy and Alb-PRF application could not be blinded. However, all clinical measurements were performed by a single experienced blinded investigator (B.U.E.), who was unaware of the allocation of the test and control sites. Radiographic measurements were performed by a single experienced blinded investigator (S.Ö.). In addition, the investigators performing biochemical and radiographic analyses were blinded to group allocation. To minimize participant awareness of site allocation, the control sites were irrigated with sterile saline and compressed with sterile gauze for 1 min, similar to the procedure performed at the test sites.

GCF sampling

For biochemical analysis, GCF samples were collected from the deepest periodontal pockets at the test and control sites. To ensure standardization, GCF samples were obtained exclusively from posterior teeth and from the mesial and/or distal aspects of the selected teeth. Moreover, samples were consistently collected from the same aspects. To account for circadian variations, samples were collected in the morning, at least one hour after the participants had eaten or brushed their teeth. The sampling area was isolated using cotton rolls, and supragingival plaque was removed with cotton pellet. Following air-drying of the sampling area, a paper strip (PerioPaper; Oraflow Inc., Smithtown, New York, USA) was carefully inserted into the periodontal pocket until minimal resistance was encountered and maintained in position for 30 s without applying pressure. Strips that were contaminated with saliva or showed visible blood staining were excluded. The collected strips were subsequently placed in a calibrated electronic measuring device (Periotron 8010; Oraflow Inc., Amityville, New York, USA) to calculate the volume of GCF absorbed by each strip for the determination of biomarker concentrations. Immediately after measurement, the strips were transferred into sterile 1.5-mL Eppendorf tubes. To avoid cross-contamination, the device electrodes were wiped with dry gauze after each measurement. Eppendorf tubes with strips are stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Following treatment, GCF sampling was repeated from the same sites at days 7, 14, and 21.

Periodontal therapy

Following the collection of GCF samples, Step 1 NSPT was performed, including full-mouth supragingival scaling using an ultrasonic device (Woodpecker, UDS-J, Guangxi, China) and oral hygiene instructions [24]. Patients were recalled after one week. At the second visit, Step 2 NSPT was performed in a single visit, including full-mouth subgingival instrumentation using both manual instruments (Gracey curettes, Hu-Friedy, LLC, Chicago, IL) and ultrasonic devices (Woodpecker, UDS-J, Guangxi, China) [24]. This was followed by irrigation of the periodontal pockets with 5 mL of isotonic saline (0.9% NaCl) using blunt-tipped syringes to remove any remaining debris.

Application of Alb-PRF

Approximately 20 mL of venous blood was collected from the antecubital vein into two plastic tubes without anticoagulant and centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 8 min (maximum RCF ≈ 700 g) using a fixed-angle centrifuge (Nuve NF 200, Ankara, Turkey). From the upper PPP layer, 2 mL was collected using a sterile 5 mL syringe and incubated at 75 °C for 10 min in a heating device (TS-100, Biosan, Latvia), followed by cooling to room temperature (Fig. 2a and c). The resulting albumin gel was mixed with injectable platelet-rich fibrin (i-PRF) obtained from the middle layer of the tube using a three-way stopcock system (with PRF connected to one port and the albumin gel to the other), thereby preparing Alb-PRF (Fig. 2d and e) [16]. The final volume of Alb-PRF prepared for each application was approximately 2.5 mL. The prepared Alb-PRF was applied into the deep periodontal pockets of posterior teeth (molar/premolar regions) at the test sites, ensuring that the material did not overflow beyond the pocket margins (Fig. 2f). Following application, gentle digital compression with sterile gauze was applied to the treated sites for 1 min to avoid displacement of Alb-PRF. In the control sites, deep periodontal pockets were irrigated with sterile saline and compressed with sterile gauze for 1 min to blind the patient to the test and control sites. All periodontal pockets, including those other than the selected test and control sites, received the same Step 1 and Step 2 NSPT. After the procedure, patients were instructed to avoid eating or drinking for 1 h and to refrain from interdental cleaning for 24 h. No antibiotics or antimicrobial mouth rinses were prescribed, and no adjunctive therapy other than NSPT was performed at the control sites.

Biochemical and radiographic analysis

GCF samples were analyzed at the Department of Biochemistry, Gulhane Faculty of Pharmacy, University of Health Sciences. On the day of analysis, 200 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution was added to each tube to facilitate the dissolution of the sample. The tubes were vortexed for 30 s to ensure homogenization of the contents. Subsequently, the tubes were left at room temperature for 5 min, followed by another 30-second vortex. This process was repeated 3 times. After the procedure, the tubes were placed in a refrigerator at + 4 °C for 30 min to further enhance dissolution. Following the cooling step, the samples were vortexed again for 30 s. Finally, to remove any particulates, the samples were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min. The resulting supernatant was carefully collected and prepared for analysis. The levels of b-FGF, PDGF-BB, VEGF and TGF-β1 in the obtained samples were determined using Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (BT LAB, Shanghai, China). For b-FGF, the intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) was < 8%, inter-assay CV was < 10%, and analytical sensitivity was 2.52 pg/ml. For PDGF-BB, the intra-assay CV was < 8%, inter-assay CV was < 10%, and the analytical sensitivity was 1.15 ng/L. For VEGF, the intra-assay CV was < 8%, inter-assay CV was < 10%, and analytical sensitivity was 10.42 ng/L. For TGF-β1, the intra-assay CV was < 8%, inter-assay CV was < 8%, and analytical sensitivity was 5.11 ng/L.

Radiographic analysis was conducted at Department of Dentomaxillofacial Radiology, Gulhane Faculty of Dentistry, University of Health Sciences and included the assessment of defect depth (mm), defect angle (°), and bone loss percentage relative to root length (%). Defect depth was defined as the vertical distance from the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) to the base of the periodontal bone defect measured on radiographs. The defect angle was defined as the angle between a line drawn parallel to the long axis of the tooth and a line connecting the CEJ to the base of the periodontal bone defect; the angle formed between these two lines was recorded as the defect angle. Root length was calculated as the distance from the CEJ to the point where a line drawn parallel to the long axis of the tooth intersected a horizontal line passing through the root apex. Bone loss percentage was calculated by dividing the defect depth by the root length and expressing the result as a percentage.

Sample size calculation

An a priori power analysis was performed using (G*Power 3.1, Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany) [25]. The sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome variable of changes in PD between the evaluation times, assuming a standardized effect size of dz = 0.8 based on a previous similar study evaluating adjunctive platelet concentrate therapies in periodontal defects, a desired power (1 − β) of 0.95, and a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05 [10]. Based on these assumptions, a minimum sample size of 23 patients was required. To compensate for a possible dropout rate of approximately 10% during follow-up, 26 patients were initially included in the study. Due to a higher-than-anticipated dropout rate, the clinical and radiographic analyses were completed with 21 patients. Therefore, a post hoc power analysis was performed based on the final sample size (n = 21) and the same effect size (dz = 0.8) and significance level (α = 0.05). The analysis indicated that the statistical power of the study remained high (1 − β = 0.93).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using statistical software package (SPSS v.21.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, which confirmed normal distribution. Comparisons between test and control sites at different time points were performed using the paired t-test. Changes within each group over time were analyzed using one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA), and post hoc comparisons were performed with Bonferroni correction. Delta (Δ) values were calculated as the difference between the 6-month value and the baseline value (Δ = 6 months−baseline).

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships between parameters. For all statistical analyses, the patient was considered the unit of analysis. Local analyses were performed using patient-level mean values derived from measurements of a single test or control tooth per patient. In all analyses, a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 26 patients were enrolled, 3 patients withdrew for various reasons; therefore, periodontal treatment and Alb-PRF application were performed in 23 patients (13 males and 10 females). 2 patients failed to attend the 3-and 6-month follow-up visits; consequently, biochemical analyses were completed in 23 patients, whereas periodontal clinical and radiographic analyses were completed in 21 patients. The mean age was 45.26 ± 9.29 years (range: 31–64 years). No adverse events related to the interventions were reported during the study period.

Accordingly, full-mouth and local PI, GI, BOP, PD, and CAL values were significantly reduced at both 3 and 6 months compared with baseline (p < 0.01), whereas no statistically significant differences were detected between the 3- and 6-month mean periodontal parameters.

At 3 months, the test site demonstrated significantly lower values of GI, PD, and CAL compared with the control site (p < 0.05). Because baseline PD values differed between the sites and baseline CAL values showed near-significant differences, delta (Δ) changes from baseline to 6 months were additionally calculated. The mean ΔPD from baseline to 6 months was significantly greater at the test site than at the control site (2.071 ± 0.796 vs. 1.534 ± 0.901; Δp = 0.041). Although the mean ΔCAL was greater at the test site than at the control site (1.750 ± 1.274 vs. 1.593 ± 0.995), this difference did not reach statistical significance.

Baseline and 6-month defect depth, defect angle, and bone loss percentage values are presented in Table 2. At baseline, no statistically significant differences were observed between the sites in defect depth, defect angle, or bone loss percentage. At 6 months, defect depth and bone loss percentage were significantly lower at the test sites than at the control site (p < 0.05), whereas defect angle did not differ significantly. Within-group analyses further showed that both sites exhibited significant reductions in defect depth and bone loss percentage at 6 months compared with baseline (p < 0.01), while no significant changes were observed in defect angle.

Discussion

In the management of periodontitis, elimination of etiological factors represents a fundamental step. However, the persistence of residual periodontal pockets may provide a favorable niche for microorganisms, thereby increasing the risk of re-infection and disease progression [26]. In recent years, the use of biological agents adjunct to NSPT has been proposed to enhance clinical outcomes. Within this context, PRF products have attracted increasing interest in clinical practice [27, 28]. However, one of the major limitations of PRF is its rapid resorption, which may restrict the duration of its biological effects [13]. In recent years, Alb-PRF, has been proposed to exhibit a prolonged resorption time and a more favorable release profile for certain growth factors [29]. Based on this rationale, the present study aimed to evaluate the outcomes of Alb-PRF application into periodontal pockets following NSPT.

In the present study, all periodontal clinical parameters except GR improved at 3 months compared with baseline, and these improvements were maintained at 6 months. No significant differences were observed between test and control sites in GR, possibly because both treatments were non-surgical. When local parameters were compared, GI, PD, and CAL were significantly lower at the test sites at 3 months. Although differences in PD and CAL were not statistically significant at 6 months, a trend toward greater PD reduction at the test sites persisted. However, the absence of significant differences between the groups at 6 months may indicate that the main effect of Alb-PRF is related to enhanced early healing rather than long-term clinical superiority. In our previous study, intra-pocket application of PRF following NSPT resulted in favorable effects on clinical parameters, particularly PD and CAL [10]. Similarly, Parwani et al. reported that PRF application into periodontal pockets following NSPT led to lower PD values and greater clinical attachment gain [28]. Cin et al. also demonstrated that application of i-PRF adjunct to NSPT had positive effects on clinical periodontal parameters [30]. These findings suggest that the clinical improvements observed at the test sites may support the potential use of Alb-PRF as an intra-pocket adjunctive therapy; however, these findings should be interpreted with caution given the lack of sustained differences between the groups at 6 months.

In the present study, radiographic evaluation at 6 months was selected to identify potential surgical needs after NSPT. Nibali et al. reported substantial radiographic bone gain after one year follow-up in intra-bony defects treated with minimally invasive non-surgical periodontal therapy [31]. Consistent with these findings, statistically significant reductions in defect depth and bone loss percentage were observed at both sites at 6 months compared with baseline, with greater improvements noted at the test sites. These findings suggest that adjunctive intra-pocket application of Alb-PRF following NSPT may be associated with greater radiographic improvement compared with conventional treatment alone, even within a relatively short follow-up period of 6 months. However, radiographic findings should be interpreted as early bone fill rather than definitive periodontal regeneration.

PRF may support early soft tissue healing and modulation of inflammation through the growth factors it contains [32]. In the present study, the greater clinical attachment gain observed at the test site at 3 months is consistent with the biological effects of Alb-PRF, while the lack of a significant difference at 6 months may reflect continued healing in the control site. In contrast, the more pronounced radiographic reduction in bone loss percentage at the test site suggests a potential contribution of Alb-PRF to hard tissue healing through enhanced bone matrix stabilization and sustained growth factor release. Although no statistically significant difference in CAL was observed at 6 months, consistently lower mean values in the test site indicate that these effects may become more evident with larger sample sizes.

Previous studies have emphasized that the biological activity of PRF and its derivatives depends not only on the concentration of released growth factors but also on the total amount released and the sustainability of their release over time [32]. This consideration is particularly relevant for modified PRF products such as Alb-PRF, in which heat treatment may alter fibrin stability and release kinetics, making the combined evaluation of total amount and concentration values more appropriate [33].

VEGF plays a critical role in regulating early tissue responses by promoting local vasodilation and increased microvascular permeability, while also stimulating endothelial cell proliferation and migration [34]. Increased VEGF expression in periodontal tissues accelerates tissue regeneration and contributes to bone remodeling [7]. Accordingly, the elevated VEGF concentrations observed at days 14 and 21 in the test site may be associated with more favorable early clinical outcomes in PD and CAL at 3 months.

The higher baseline total amounts of PDGF-BB and b-FGF observed at the test sites warrant consideration. Although the sites were randomly allocated, the test sites showed a tendency toward deeper periodontal pockets at baseline, consistent with previous reports showing elevated PDGF-BB and b-FGF levels in deeper pockets [35]. The absence of baseline differences in concentrations suggests that these differences were not related to increased biological production [36]. Therefore, post-treatment biomarker findings should be interpreted with caution, as baseline differences in pocket depth and GCF volume may have influenced total amount measurements.

The earlier increase in PDGF-BB and b-FGF at the test site may indicate earlier healing activation associated with Alb-PRF [37]. The increased concentrations of VEGF, PDGF-BB, and TGF-β at day 14 in the test site, together with persistently elevated VEGF and TGF-β levels at day 21, may indicate prolonged growth factor expression associated with Alb-PRF. As TGF-β plays an important role in cellular regulation, matrix formation, and bone remodeling in periodontal tissues, its sustained elevation may be associated with the observed clinical and radiographic findings [38].

While growth factor levels following PRF application are typically reported to decline or remain unchanged beyond 14 days, the sustained elevation observed at day 21 suggests a more stable release profile. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as some between-group differences were observed only in total amounts and not in concentrations. Baseline differences in pocket depth and GCF volume may also have influenced total amount measurements. This interpretation is partly supported by in vitro findings showing greater structural stability and higher growth factor expression for Alb-PRF compared with leukocyte platelet-rich fibrin (L-PRF) [10, 29, 35].

The observed positive correlations between BOP and both VEGF and b-FGF suggest that these growth factors may reflect the initial inflammatory status of periodontal pockets. VEGF is associated with increased vascular permeability in inflamed tissues, while b-FGF is involved in early fibroblast activation and tissue remodeling [5, 39]. The positive correlations among the growth factors may indicate that growth factors are not independent mediators, but rather participate in interconnected biological processes, as they can modulate each other’s expression [5].

The present study has some limitations. The exact retention time of Alb-PRF could not be precisely determined. In addition, no customized stents were used for the standardization of clinical and radiographic measurements in the present study. Despite the use of the long cone paralleling technique, the measurement of sub-millimeter bone changes may have been affected by projection variability and limited reproducibility. In addition, the present study did not include an i-PRF-only group, since the study was designed to compare Alb-PRF adjunctive therapy with NSPT alone. Future studies including an additional i-PRF-only group may help to further clarify the specific contribution of Alb-PRF. Furthermore, the follow-up period was limited to 6 months, which may not be sufficient to fully evaluate long-term periodontal healing and radiographic bone changes.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of the study, adjunctive application of Alb-PRF was associated with favorable findings, including reductions in PD, lower defect depth and bone loss percentage, and increased TGF-β levels during early healing. However, given the limited sample size and methodological constraints, these results should be interpreted with caution. Further randomized controlled trials with larger study populations and longer follow-up periods are required to better define the clinical relevance of Alb-PRF and to determine whether the observed early healing effects are sustained over time.
