Comparison of effectiveness and safety of different haemostatic materials in patients undergoing transradial coronary intervention and diagnosis: a protocol for systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Shuo Liang, Hongwei Sun, Ruopei Liu, Xinxin Xie, Yuying Zhao, Yanmei Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionEffective compression haemostasis is essential after transradial coronary procedures. However, evidence directly comparing different haemostatic materials remains limited. This study aims to compare and rank the efficacy and safety of different haemostatic materials used as adjuncts to compression after transradial coronary angiography and/or percutaneous coronary intervention, and to explore whether compression modality modifies their relative effects.Methods and analysisWe will systematically search nine databases (Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Chinese Biomedical Literature Database, Wanfang Data, VIP Database and Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature) for randomized controlled trials on post-transradial access (TRA) compression haemostasis with different haemostatic materials, from database inception to December 2025. Introduction

Currently, the preferred approach for coronary angiography (CAG) and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is transradial access (TRA).1 TRA has advantages over other traditional methods, including reduced postoperative bed rest time, increased patient comfort and fewer puncture site problems. It is a core technique for the diagnosis and treatment of coronary heart disease due to its steadily increasing global application rate.2,4 Effective compression haemostasis following puncture procedures is a key component of clinical nursing care, which has a direct impact on patient satisfaction, the rate of recovery and the efficient use of healthcare resources.5 6 Inappropriate selection of haemostatic materials or compression methods may lead to adverse complications such as bleeding, haematoma formation, pain and radial artery occlusion. Furthermore, issues like prolonged haemostasis time, excessive material costs or operational inconvenience can put more strain on clinical managers and patients.7 8 Therefore, identifying a haemostatic strategy that is effective, practical and clinically applicable remains an important issue in transradial coronary procedures.

A range of haemostatic materials is currently used in clinical practice to improve haemostatic outcomes. Materials such as kaolin, chitosan and potassium ferrate have gradually gained acceptance in clinical practice due to their properties of promoting blood coagulation, adsorbing exudate and reducing adverse tissue reactions.9,11 According to a meta-analysis, the adjunctive use of haemostatic materials offers clear advantages in reducing the incidence of 24-hour radial artery occlusion and shortening haemostasis time.12 However, different haemostatic materials may vary in their effectiveness and safety profiles, and evidence to support comparisons across individual materials remains limited. As a result, healthcare professionals still lack sufficient evidence to guide the selection of the most appropriate haemostatic material for different clinical situations.

As a crucial component of haemostasis protocols, pressure application at puncture sites is primarily categorised into two main types: manual compression and mechanical compression.13 Research findings suggest that manual compression is widely acknowledged for its immediate efficacy and practicality, as operators can directly assess the puncture site without relying on specialised equipment.14 Mechanical compression devices (such as pneumatic tourniquets and spiral tourniquets) effectively eliminate pressure fluctuations induced by operator fatigue or inexperience, via standardised pressure settings and stable regulation. They exhibit notable advantages in alleviating compression-related pain and reducing postoperative vascular occlusion rates, thereby improving safety outcomes.15 Although the effectiveness of both compression methods is widely recognised, current research has not clearly identified the optimal operational approach.16 A meta-analysis indicates that both manual and mechanical compression are effective for achieving haemostasis, although mechanical compression requires a relatively longer time to attain a haemostatic effect.13 In addition, it remains unclear whether compression modality influences the relative effects of different haemostatic materials.

The present study will conduct a systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials evaluating different haemostatic materials used as adjuncts to compression after TRA-CAG and/or PCI. The study aims to compare and rank the efficacy and safety of different haemostatic materials and to explore whether compression modality may modify their relative effects. The findings are expected to provide evidence-based support for the selection of postoperative haemostatic strategies in patients undergoing transradial coronary procedures.

Methods

This systematic review and network meta-analysis has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420251250499. The protocol of this review adheres to the guidelines specified in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Protocols.17 The findings of this study will be reported in compliance with the guidelines of the PRISMA extension for Network Meta-Analyses.18 Any modifications to the protocol will be updated simultaneously on PROSPERO.

Study selection

To reduce the possibility of bias, two researchers (SL and HS) with training in evidence-based medicine will independently retrieve and select the studies. Before implementation, the search strategy will be peer-reviewed by competent, experienced medical or healthcare librarians based on the Cochrane Handbook guidelines. EndNote X9 software will be used to import and screen the articles that were initially retrieved. Duplicate research will be eliminated first. All abstracts and titles will then be examined for first selection. Finally, the chosen articles’ complete texts will be read.

To decide whether to include the study, researchers will confer with a third researcher (YMG) if they cannot agree during the screening phase. To guarantee traceability and transparency in the study selection process, we will systematically record the justification for each exclusion from the final analysis after full-text review and display this in the PRISMA flow diagram (figure 1).19

Missing data

In instances of missing data across the included studies, we will initiate email communication with the authors of the respective articles. If no reply is obtained or the missing data are irrecoverable, such data will be excluded from the present analysis, and a detailed account of this handling approach will be provided in the discussion section.

Statistical analysis

A network meta-analysis will be conducted within a frequentist framework using Stata V.16.0. The primary analysis will be performed using a random-effects model. For continuous outcomes, mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs will be used when outcomes are measured using the same scale or instrument, version, scoring method and compatible outcome definition across studies. Standardised MDs with 95% CIs will be used when the same underlying construct is measured using different scales, instruments, versions or scoring methods. Where necessary, outcome units and scoring direction will be standardised before analysis. For TAH, data will be converted to a common unit where possible. Studies with materially different definitions of TAH that cannot be reasonably harmonised will not be pooled together and will instead be analysed separately or summarised narratively. For 24-RAO, only data judged to be sufficiently comparable in outcome definition, assessment method and timing will be synthesised quantitatively; where substantial differences cannot be reasonably harmonised, the results will be analysed separately or summarised narratively.

Intervention nodes will be defined primarily by the principal active haemostatic material rather than by commercial product name alone. Different commercial products with the same principal active haemostatic material, similar intended use and a comparable mode of application will be grouped within the same node; materials with different principal active haemostatic materials or substantially different formulations will be analysed as separate nodes. Interventions that are clinically non-comparable or supported by insufficient data will be summarised narratively.

A network plot will be constructed for each outcome to display the geometry of the treatment network. Relative treatment effects will be presented in league tables, and the ranking probabilities of interventions will be estimated using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve. Pairwise meta-analysis will also be conducted, where appropriate, for direct comparisons with sufficient data. Global inconsistency in the network will be assessed using the design-by-treatment interaction model when the network for a given outcome is connected and includes both direct and indirect evidence, where feasible. Local inconsistency will be examined using the node-splitting method only for comparisons informed by both direct and indirect evidence. Loop-specific inconsistency will also be assessed where closed loops are available. Comparison-adjusted funnel plots will be used to explore possible small-study effects or publication bias only when at least 10 studies are available for a given outcome. Studies that meet the eligibility criteria but cannot be included in the quantitative synthesis because of insufficient or unusable data will be summarised narratively. Furthermore, compression modality (manual vs mechanical) will be examined as a prespecified effect modifier through subgroup analysis, where data permit.

Risk of bias assessment

Two researchers (SL and HS) will independently assess the risk of bias in the included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias V.2 tool. The following domains will be evaluated: bias arising from the randomisation process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome and bias in selection of the reported result.22 The overall risk-of-bias judgments will be categorised as low risk, some concerns or high risk. In the event of disagreement during the evaluation process, a third researcher (YMG) shall provide a professional opinion.

Assessment of evidence quality

The confidence in the results of the network meta-analysis will be assessed using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis framework. The certainty of evidence will be evaluated across six domains: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity and incoherence. The overall confidence for each comparison and outcome will be categorised as high, moderate, low or very low.23 24

Heterogeneity assessment

Statistical heterogeneity will be assessed within the random-effects framework of both the pairwise meta-analyses and the network meta-analysis. For direct comparisons including at least two studies, heterogeneity will also be described using the I2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test.

Assumption of transitivity

The assumption of transitivity will be assessed by examining whether the included studies are sufficiently similar in terms of important clinical and methodological characteristics that may modify treatment effects.18 Prespecified potential effect modifiers will include compression modality (manual vs mechanical), background compression protocol characteristics (including compression device type, compression duration and use of patent haemostasis), procedure type, heparin dosage, sheath size and outcome assessment methods. The distribution of these variables across treatment comparisons and intervention nodes will be summarised in a study characteristics table and examined qualitatively before quantitative synthesis. If important imbalance or lack of clinical comparability is identified, indirect comparisons will be interpreted with caution; where data permit, subgroup and sensitivity analysis will be conducted to explore its influence. Studies or interventions judged to be clinically non-comparable will not be combined in the network meta-analysis and will instead be summarised narratively.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis will be conducted, where data permit, to explore potential sources of heterogeneity and possible violations of transitivity. Prespecified subgroup analysis will be performed according to compression modality (manual vs mechanical) to examine whether the background compression strategy modifies the comparative effects of haemostatic materials. If sufficient data are available, additional subgroup analysis will be undertaken according to study-level characteristics that may modify treatment effects, including heparin dosage, sheath size, compression duration, patent haemostasis strategy, compression device type, procedure type and outcome assessment methods.

Sensitivity analysis

To determine whether there is a single study that excessively influences the analysis results, sensitivity analysis will be performed for each included study individually. Meta-analysis will be conducted using both fixed-effects and random-effects models for estimation, thereby verifying the stability and reliability of the pooled results.

Discussion

Coronary atherosclerotic heart disease has a high incidence and mortality rate, and innovation and optimisation in cardiovascular interventional diagnostic and treatment technologies are still being driven by this.25 The radial artery approach has been widely adopted, and this has profoundly transformed the clinical diagnosis and treatment landscape of cardiovascular diseases. As a crucial link between surgery and recovery, postoperative haemostasis management therefore faces heightened demands. Patients’ immediate safety, long-term quality of life, later hospitalisation expenses and the effectiveness of healthcare resource allocation are all strongly correlated with haemostatic efficacy. Because of this, improving haemostatic techniques is a crucial development for raising the general standard of TRA diagnosis and care.6 26 This study will use network meta-analysis to compare and rank different haemostatic materials used as adjuncts to compression after transradial coronary procedures, and to explore whether compression modality may modify their relative effects, thereby providing evidence to inform postoperative haemostatic strategy selection.

Due to their various coagulation activation mechanisms and biocompatibility, haemostatic materials such as chitosan, kaolin and potassium ferrate exhibit clear advantages in shortening haemostasis time and minimising risks associated with radial artery use. Selectively porous clay minerals that can activate coagulation factor XII make up the kaolin haemostatic agent. According to studies, it activates the intrinsic coagulation pathway to induce rapid haemostasis within 15 min, with a 0% rate of radial artery occlusion within 24 hours.27 Chitosan materials, derived from chitin, are biodegradable complex carbohydrates that rely on stable pressure to maintain platelet adhesion and aggregation effects. Within 10 min, they successfully established haemostasis without raising any safety concerns.28 However, the potassium ferrate haemostatic patch uses a hydrophilic polymer as its base. Through the dual effects of physical dehydration and chemical aggregation, it neither relies on endogenous pathways nor requires long-term pressure maintenance. Instead, it achieves rapid haemostasis through local sealing material and component accumulation, with a significantly lower rate of haemostatic failure and complications than traditional compression methods.11 Different haemostatic materials may differ in their mechanisms of action, onset of haemostatic effect, biocompatibility and cost, which may lead to variation in clinical efficacy and safety. Evidence directly comparing these materials remains limited, making it difficult for clinicians to select the most appropriate adjunctive haemostatic strategy.

Although a consensus has been reached that ‘120 min of compression duration represents the balance point between haemostatic efficacy and safety’, both manual and mechanical compression remain established haemostatic methods.5 However, there have been no discernible differences in clinical efficacy between the two methods, and both have serious drawbacks because of their reliance on experience. Due to variations in operator expertise and levels of weariness, manual compression pressure may fluctuate, which could raise the risk of bleeding and haematoma formation. Mechanical compression has the drawbacks of longer haemostasis times and high dependence on equipment. Furthermore, even if auxiliary technologies like ‘patent haemostasis’ have somewhat enhanced haemostatic efficacy and user experience, efficiency, safety and comfort still need to be balanced.29 Therefore, subgroup analyses in this study will primarily examine whether the comparative effects of different haemostatic materials differ between manual and mechanical compression settings. In addition, several study-level characteristics, including heparin dosage, sheath size, compression duration, patent haemostasis strategy, compression device type, procedure type and outcome assessment methods, may modify treatment effects, contribute to heterogeneity and affect the comparability of findings. Accordingly, where sufficient data are available, additional subgroup analyses will be undertaken to explore whether the relative effects of haemostatic materials vary according to these prespecified study-level characteristics.

To provide a more comprehensive evaluation of postoperative haemostatic strategies, this study will assess not only key efficacy outcomes, including time to achieve haemostasis and 24-hour radial artery occlusion, but also patient-centred outcomes such as pain and comfort. But certain limitations remain. First, only aggregate study-level data will be available, which may limit the ability to fully explore potential effect modifiers at the patient level. Second, variations across studies in compression modality, background compression protocol characteristics (including compression device type, compression duration and use of patent haemostasis), heparin dosage and sheath size may introduce clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Third, outcome definitions and assessment methods, particularly for radial artery occlusion, pain intensity and patient comfort, may differ across studies and affect the comparability of findings. Fourth, some haemostatic materials may be represented by a limited number of trials, which could result in a sparse treatment network and reduce the precision and certainty of the estimates. Finally, only studies published in English and Chinese will be included, which may introduce language bias.
