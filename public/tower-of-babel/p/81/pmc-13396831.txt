Development and Validation of a Haptic Virtual Reality Simulator for Tooth Extraction and Inferior Alveolar Nerve Block
Authors: YingQi Ren, YiHan Xu, ZiYa Yuan, ManYu Xiong, ZiLi Zou, Reinhard Chun Wang Chau, YanLing Dong, Xian Li, Lin Zeng, Bi Zhang, YuJia Wu, Liang Pang, Sheng Yang, Ping Ji, MengWei Pang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and aimsExodontia and local anaesthesia are critical competencies in dental education, yet existing simulators lack integration. This study aimed to develop and validate a Tooth Extraction and Local Anaesthesia simulator (TELA-sim), one of the first haptic virtual reality platforms to immerse learners in the complete clinical workflow for tooth extraction (TE) and inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB).MethodsDental students (undergraduates without clinical experience) and residents (postgraduate trainees with clinical experience) (N = 155) were grouped as novices (n = 72) or intermediates (n = 83) based on clinical experience for face and construct validity assessment. A separate skill-transfer cohort compared fourth-year undergraduates who received TELA-sim training (n = 79) with a historical control group that received only traditional training (n = 77). Performance was evaluated via simulator metrics, questionnaires, and practical skills examination (PSE) scores on phantom heads.ResultsThe simulator showed strong face validity (median Likert scores > 3.0; no significant group differences). Construct validity was demonstrated by significant intergroup differences, with novices unexpectedly outperforming intermediates on overall protocol adherence (TE: median 91.50 vs 85.00, P < .001, δ = 0.39; IANB: 71.00 vs 62.00, P = .004, δ = 0.27). The TELA-sim group achieved markedly higher PSE scores 6 months later (median 84.00 vs 75.00, P < .001, δ = 0.76).ConclusionsThis study successfully developed the TELA-sim and established its face and construct validity. The TELA-sim led to superior skill retention and transfer to clinical practice compared with traditional training alone. However, longer‑term retention beyond 6 months requires further investigation, while not all students responded favourably to the simulator during face validity assessment.Clinical relevanceThe TELA-sim may help preclinical students prevent errors through repeated practice of standardised protocols in a risk-free environment. Its alignment with licensing requirements suggests potential for objective skills assessment. For practising clinicians, the simulator could offer skill refinement and retraining opportunities to reinforce safe protocols and support continuous professional development, though further study is needed.Key words: Haptic feedback, Virtual reality, Simulation training, Tooth extraction, Inferior alveolar nerve block, Workflow integration

Introduction

Tooth extraction (TE) and inferior alveolar nerve block (IANB) remain cornerstone competencies in undergraduate dental education. Preclinical training has traditionally relied on phantom heads, extracted teeth, and instructor-led demonstrations, which help students develop basic psychomotor skills.1, 2, 3 However, traditional instruction is constrained by a limited supply of suitable standardised patients, faculty issues, increasing student numbers, and a lack of opportunities for repetitive deliberate practice, leaving many new graduates feeling least prepared and least confident in these procedures. Therefore, existing preclinical training for tooth extraction and local anaesthesia is insufficient, and new complementary teaching methods are needed.

In recent years, virtual reality and haptic feedback simulators have been increasingly adopted across dental disciplines, for example in scaling and root planing training and through artificial intelligence driven feedback systems.4,5 Compared with traditional methods, haptic simulators offer advantages such as unlimited repeatable practice without consuming materials, objective automated feedback, standardised training conditions, and avoidance of ethical and biosafety concerns.6, 7, 8 Nevertheless, most existing haptic feedback platforms focus on isolated technical acts such as needle insertion or forceps application, largely neglecting the complete clinical workflow.9, 10, 11, 12, 13 This gap limits their utility in cultivating the integrated clinical judgement and protocol adherence necessary for safe practice. Therefore, a research gap exists regarding a haptic feedback system that integrates a complete standardised clinical sequence for tooth extraction and inferior alveolar nerve block. To fill this gap, the Tooth Extraction and Local Anaesthesia simulator (TELA-sim) – a haptic feedback system incorporating the full clinical sequence – was developed.

Following the multidimensional validation framework proposed by Graafland et al,8 we hypothesised that this approach would (1) demonstrate face validity (the simulator can present a level of realism and task difficulty acceptable) and construct validity (it can discriminate between learners of different experience levels) and (2) improve performance in a physical model.

To test these hypotheses, this study had the following objectives: (1) to develop and validate the TELA-sim, a haptic virtual reality platform that immerses learners in the complete clinical workflow for TE and IANB; (2) to evaluate its face and construct validity; and (3) to assess its transfer effect on physical model performance. The primary objective, consistent with the abstract, was the development and validation of the TELA-sim.

Materials and methods

The study adhered to the strengthening of the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology guidelines for cross-sectional studies.14

This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational studies.14 This encompassed both the cross-sectional validity assessment of the TELA-sim and the nonrandomised historical cohort comparison of simulator-based training versus traditional training. In addition, relevant items from the International Network for Simulation-based Paediatric Innovation, Research, and Education (INSPIRE) reporting guidelines – which provide extensions to the STROBE statement for healthcare simulation research – were followed.15

Development of TELA-sim

System overview

The TELA-sim was designed to address the critical gap in existing TE and IANB trainers by replicating the complete clinical workflow. The TELA-sim consisted of a single software kernel that drives two mutually-exclusive instrument modules (IANB and TE) running on a common visuo-haptic platform (Figure 1A). A desktop PC (Intel i7-12700KF, GTX 1060 Super) rendered graphics at ≥ 60 Hz and haptics at 1 kHz. Three core components were integrated into the haptic feedback algorithm: The haptic rendering module calculated feedback forces and differentiated between the user’s physical tool and a constrained graphical proxy to manage virtual tissue penetration; a virtual sleeve model was incorporated to restrict tool motion to simulate procedural constraints; finally, a refined mass-spring model with dynamic force adjustment realistically simulated soft tissue deformation during injection.16 Visual feedback was delivered via a stereoscopic monitor and a modern mixed-reality head-mounted display (3664 × 1920, 90 Hz, Pico Neo 3, Chuangjian Weilai Technology Co., Ltd., Qingdao, Shandong, China). There were two custom hand-pieces: a syringe body with a sliding plunger for the IANB task (Figure 1B) and a forceps handle for the TE task (Figure 1C). The quick-lock adapter preserved the device kinematics and allowed changeover in <1 minute, ensuring that hardware factors did not confound the construct validity of the study.

Software architecture

Both the IANB and TE modules ran on a standard task thread organised into five sequential stages (Figure 2): (1) history taking, (2) preoperative preparation, (3) core procedure execution, (4) postoperative management, and (5) performance assessment.17,18

Stage 1: History taking

A voice-user interface transcribed the student’s questions in real time and fed the text to a BiLSTM-CRF entity extractor coupled with a domain-specific BERT intent classifier (Figure 2A). The dialogue manager then returned context-aware answers that emphasised factors relevant to subsequent surgical planning.18

Stage 2: Preoperative preparation

Within Unity 2021.3.16f1 (Unity Technologies, San Francisco, CA, USA), spatialised audio and 3D visuals were synchronised in real time to support instrument selection, choice of anaesthetic (Figure 2B), chair-light adjustment (Figure 2C), target-tooth confirmation, and surgical-field disinfection.

Stage 3: Core procedure execution

Task-specific, self-designed 7-DoF control loops delivered high-fidelity visuo-haptic feedback for both TE and IANB.

For exodontia (Figure 2D), the 7-DoF tooth-extraction algorithm allocated an additional degree of freedom to realistic buccolingual movement of the forceps. The algorithm first ensured that the root did not breach the cortical bone, and then a multiphase controller orchestrated static movements, dynamic movements with energy accumulation, axial traction, and final release. Throughout the sequence, the simulator recorded grasp accuracy, movement kinematics, traction vectors, and completion time while progressively reducing periodontal ligament stiffness to emulate increasing tooth mobility.16

For anaesthesia (Figure 2E), the extra degree of freedom assigned to syringe-plunger travel enabled the 7-DoF block anaesthesia algorithm to constrain the needle to anatomically valid paths and, once the mucosa was pierced, to lock all motion except axial insertion. This mechanism reproduced calibrated force peaks, the puncture caused by soft tissue constraints, and the classic loss-of-resistance cue while continuously logging needle trajectory, depth, and peak insertion force.

All haptic feedback was rendered at 4 kHz on a single-hand device (Unidental 4.0, 0-20 N; Unidraw, Beijing, China) driven by the exact configuration-based 7-DoF solver, thereby extending conventional 6-DoF systems with the two task-specific degrees of freedom essential for high-fidelity dental training.

Stage 4: Postoperative management

The same Unity-based interactive scene guided haemostasis, wound dressing, and delivery of postoperative instructions, enhanced by synchronised auditory and haptic cues.

Stage 5: Performance assessment

To ensure a comprehensive assessment of competency, the scoring system integrated criteria from the Chinese National Dental Practical Examination and the Chongqing Medical University Objective Structured Clinical Examination, which emphasised assessing standardised procedural competency, unlike the U.S. focus on evaluating independent clinical efficacy (Supplementary Tables 1-2).17,19, 20, 21 Each action was evaluated against a protocol whose content validity was rigorously established through expert consensus (S-CVI/Ave = 0.790 for TE, 0.775 for IANB).22 The consensus process ensured that even items prompting initial discussion were retained to fully capture the procedural standard, thereby enhancing the instrument’s validity for identifying deviations. The system provided real-time feedback and domain scores for both formative and summative assessments (Figure 2F).

Validity evaluation

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using α = .05, a power of .80, a medium effect size of Cohen d = 0.50, and two independent groups.23, 24, 25 This resulted in a total sample of 128 participants. Accounting for a 10% attrition rate common in clinical studies, we targeted 142 participants to ensure adequate statistical power.26 Sample size calculations were performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7).

Participants

The study was conducted between 2023 and 2024. Participants were recruited from undergraduate and postgraduate dental students and residency trainees at the College of Stomatology using purposive sampling.

To mitigate potential selection bias, recruitment announcements were systematically distributed across internal communication platforms, widely used by students and trainees at the college. Each participant received an allowance of approximately US $14 to cover transportation and meal costs.

A total of 198 participants were initially recruited for this study. They were categorised into two groups based on their level of clinical exposure: (a) novice group, comprising fourth-year undergraduates who had completed at least half (≥50%) of their theoretical courses in oral and maxillofacial surgery but had minimal clinical practice; (b) intermediate group, consisting of graduates with at least two (≥2) years of clinical experience.27 42 participants with less than 2 years of experience were excluded because they did not meet the predefined criteria for either group. One participant, who had 4 missing primary outcome data points, was excluded for failing to meet the predefined data integrity criteria (ie, having 2 or more missing data points). The final analysis included 155 participants, with 72 assigned to the novice group and 83 to the intermediate group. The participant flow is detailed in Figure 3.

Intervention design

The study employed two representative clinical tasks to assess performance: the IANB and TE of the mandibular first molar (FDI notation 36). The IANB, a widely used mandibular anaesthetic technique, had been associated with high failure rates among students and interns, underscoring the need for enhanced training.28 In the case of extraction of tooth 36, strict adherence to a standardised protocol was mandatory to ensure contraindications were properly evaluated and the correct tooth was selected, as 36 is a key masticatory tooth and the most frequent site of caries-related tooth loss.29,30 As both procedures were commonly encountered in clinical practice and included in professional licensing examinations,17 their combined use offered a robust framework for evaluating multifaceted competencies in cognitive reasoning and procedural execution.

The study was divided into 2 phases: the practice phase and the test phase. In the practice phase, the study’s instructor demonstrated the use of the simulator to the participants. Each participant was given 15 minutes for each module to familiarise themselves with the equipment. This 15-minute exposure was intended solely to familiarise participants with the equipment and allow them to demonstrate their pre-existing skills during the formal assessment. During the practice phase, the instructor provided guidance and assistance in the use of TELA-sim when the participant asked for help. In the test phase, each participant accomplished a TE task and an IANB task. After the tasks, participants completed a self-designed questionnaire using an internet platform (wenjuanxing, similar to platforms such as MTurk; Changsha, China).31

Face validity

Face validity was assessed based on the framework by Graafland et al8 with the following specific criteria: 1) A median score greater than 3.0 on a 5-point Likert scale was considered indicative of sufficient validity, a threshold adopted from Alkadri et al32 2) A comparative analysis was performed to assess whether novices and intermediates shared a consensus on the simulator.33 Sufficient validity was defined as the absence of statistically significant differences in results between the groups.8,32,33

Two questionnaires were developed for participants following completion of the simulator experiment. Questionnaire 1 collected demographic characteristics, including age, gender, dominant hand, academic grade, and years of clinical practice. We adopted a gender-based approach in this study and collected data through questionnaires. Questionnaire 2, ‘TELA-sim evaluation questionnaire’, was used to evaluate face validity. It encompassed two dimensions: Usefulness (6-item) evaluated the modules’ ability to support user training objectives, adapted from McAlpin (2022);34 and Realism (3-item, self-designed) focused on haptic fidelity since tissue-instrument interaction authenticity serves as the primary determinant of perceived realism and skill transfer potential.35 The complete questionnaire comprised 18 items (9 per module) and is presented in Supplementary Table 3. Items were rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). Prior to the formal experiment, a pilot test was conducted with 43 participants to assess the reliability of the TELA-sim evaluation questionnaires. The results demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for both modules exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.7 (Shrestha, 2021).36

Construct validity

Construct validity was assessed using a priori metrics established independently for the TE and IANB tasks. During a simulation procedure, the TELA-sim recorded a series of data on participants’ performance throughout the process. The primary metrics included: (1) Time-to-completion for each task simulation (seconds, s), (2) Total performance scores of each task simulation (100-point scale), (3) Detailed scores of seven parts (history taking, tool selection, adjust chair position, gingival separation/local disinfection, affected tooth extraction/aspiration of anaesthetic drugs, socket treatment/anaesthetic injection, postoperative instructions/questions). Sufficient validity was assumed to be achieved if there were significant differences in intergroup comparisons of intermediates and novices in these variables.8

Statistical analysis

For datasets with <15% missing values (non-normally distributed), we performed median-based univariate imputation for one case with a single missing value.37 For missing data exceeding 15%, Little’s Missing Completely at Random test was performed including all available covariates.38, 39, 40 Multiple imputation was then conducted using the predictive mean matching method, generating three imputed datasets.41 All the above-mentioned covariates were included as predictors in the imputation model.

The data were summarised using descriptive statistics, including means, SD, percentages, medians, and IQR. Effect sizes were quantified with Cliff’s δ, a nonparametric measure that indicates the magnitude and direction of differences between groups. Normality was evaluated with Shapiro-Wilk tests. The data distribution was skewed, supporting the use of nonparametric statistical analyses. The Mann-Whitney U test was applied for group comparisons (two-tailed α = .05). Specifically, P-values from the analyses of step scores were subjected to False Discovery Rate correction for multiple testing.42,43 Effect sizes were quantified with Cliff delta δ.44 Interpretation followed established benchmarks: negligible (|δ|<0.15), small (0.15≤|δ|<0.33), medium (0.33≤|δ|<0.47), and large (|δ|≥0.47).

The reliability and construct validity of the self-designed questionnaire were assessed using Cronbach’s α (≥0.70 is regarded as satisfactory) for internal consistency and exploratory factor analysis (EFA) for construct validity.45 EFA prerequisites included Bartlett’s test of sphericity (P < .05) and Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO≥0.80). Extraction used principal component analysis with varimax rotation; items were retained if communality ≥ 0.40 and factor loading ≥ 0.50.36 A 1% significance level (P < .01) was adopted to reduce the risk of Type I error, given the relatively small sample size.33

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics). Data visualisation was conducted using Tableau 2019.1 (Tableau Software, LLC, Salesforce) and GraphPad 9.5.0 (Dotmatics).

Skill transfer assessment

Participants and recruitment

Undergraduate dental students were recruited and divided into two groups: an intervention group and a historical control group (prior-year retrospective cohort).46

The inclusion criteria were applied consistently across both cohorts at the time of the study: participants were fourth-year undergraduate stomatology students who had completed the theoretical coursework in oral and maxillofacial surgery but had not yet entered clinical internships, ensuring a homogeneous skill baseline.

The intervention group (TELA-sim group) was prospectively recruited in March 2024. 79 eligible participants were enrolled. Data from the historical control group were collected in 2021. A total of 77 individuals from this cohort were included as the control group.

Interventions design

The TELA-sim group completed traditional training and supplemental simulator training, including at least a 60-minute TE and IANB practice session (30 minutes each module). The control group received only traditional training (same curriculum and total of 8.5 hours as the traditional component received by the TELA-sim group). Six months after the intervention, a practical skills examination (PSE) was administered to evaluate participants’ clinical competence in TE and IANB using phantom heads.

Performance was independently assessed by the calibrated faculty using a structured rubric aligned with the Clinical Skill test of the National Dental Licensing Examination and objective structured clinical examination standards.17,19,20

PSE scores (100-point scale) were collected as the primary outcomes, along with demographic variables (age, gender), to evaluate intergroup comparability.

Results

Validity evaluation

Demographic characteristics

The 155 included participants fully completed the TE and IANB module tasks and the questionnaires (response rate 155/155, 100%). However, questionnaire data from 30 participants were lost due to a system malfunction on the electronic data collection platform (response rate: 125/155, 80.6%). Little’s Missing Completely at Random test including all available covariates (age, clinical year, TE scores, TE times, IANB scores, IANB times, TE usefulness, TE realism, IANB usefulness, and IANB realism) was significant (χ² = 50.647, df = 6, P < .001), indicating that the data were not missing completely at random. All 30 missing questionnaires were from the novice group. Compared with novices with complete data, those with missing data showed significant differences in IANB total scores (P = .007) and clinical year (P < .001), whereas no significant differences were observed for the other covariates (age, gender, TE scores, TE times, and IANB times). Because the missingness was associated with these observed covariates (group, IANB scores, and clinical year), the data were considered missing at random conditional on them. Therefore, multiple imputation was performed using the predictive mean matching method with three imputed datasets, including all the above-mentioned covariates as predictors. Missing data did not materially affect the conclusions; detailed imputation results are presented in Supplementary Table 4.

The mean age of the novice group was 21.39 (SD 0.742) years, and that of the intermediate group was 25.22 (SD 1.389) years. Furthermore, the mean clinical experience was 0.02 (SD 0.036) years in the novice group and 2.49 (SD 0.592) years in the intermediate group (Table 1).

Face validity

TELA-sim evaluation questionnaires for the TE and IANB modules both demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α values of 0.890 and 0.888, respectively.45 The KMO measures were 0.838 for the TE module and 0.851 for the IANB module (both P < .001). EFA revealed a two-factor structure for each questionnaire, consistent with the theoretical design, thereby supporting the construct validity of the questionnaires (Supplementary Tables 5-6). For the face validity, the questionnaire responses were presented in Supplementary Table 7 and Supplementary Figures 1-2.

Both novice and intermediate groups rated the ASVR-sim highly across all assessed factors, with median scores ranging from 3.00 to 3.92. A Mann-Whitney U test showed no statistically significant differences in realism and usefulness scores between the 2 groups across both modules (P > .01 for all comparisons). The highest-rated aspect in both groups was IANB usefulness. TE’s usefulness was also rated highly. In contrast, IANB realism received slightly lower ratings compared to other factors (Table 2).

In the TE task, intermediate participants demonstrated significantly superior performance compared to novices in the affected tooth extraction step (Supplementary Table 8, Figure 4C-D). Intermediates also outperformed novices in the socket treatment step. Conversely, intermediates scored significantly lower than novices in history taking, adjusting chair position, and postoperative instructions steps of the TE task. In the IANB task, novice participants achieved significantly higher scores than intermediates across some procedural steps: history taking, local disinfection, and aspiration of anaesthetic drugs. However, for the outcome-based step (anaesthetic injection) scores, there was no significant difference between novices and intermediates.

Skill transfer assessment

A total of 156 participants were recruited in this study. The response rate for each group was 100%: all participants fully completed the test. 26 out of 77 participants (33.8%) were male in the control group, while 43 out of 79 participants (54.4%) were male in the TELA-sim group (P = .009). The mean age of the control group was 22.30 (SD 0.689) years, and that of the TELA-sim group was 22.35 (SD 0.680) years (P = .543).

The PSE results showed a skewed distribution. The scores of the TELA-sim group were statistically significantly higher than those of the Control Group (median 84.00, IQR 79.00-90.00 vs median 75.00, IQR 68.00-79.00; z = 8.245, P < .001, 95% CI: 8.00-13.00; δ = 0.76) (Table 4, Figure 5).

Discussion

This study reports the initial development and validation of an immersive haptic simulator (TELA-sim) that integrates two independent modules for TE and IANB. The device covers haptic simulation of core procedures while encompassing the full clinical workflow, including preoperative risk assessment, preparation, and postoperative instructions. Evidence of skill retention at 6 months is also provided.

Haptic VR simulators are increasingly prevalent in dental education and have been applied across various domains.34,47 However, to the authors’ knowledge, existing haptic simulators for TE and local anaesthesia have been limited to the simulation of isolated core procedures, such as the process of tooth removal or needle insertion, without integrating the complete clinical workflow.6,9,12,13,28 Furthermore, the algorithms underpinning these simulators remain relatively basic, resulting in suboptimal haptic fidelity. The TELA-sim addresses these gaps by embedding the discrete acts of extraction and anaesthesia within a comprehensive clinical workflow, while also incorporating a new, more refined haptic feedback algorithm (7-degree-of-freedom).16

The principal findings of this study are as follows. The TELA-sim demonstrated good face and construct validity and significantly discriminated between participants with differing levels of clinical experience. More importantly, the TELA-sim group that received supplementary simulator training in addition to the conventional curriculum outperformed the control group that received only traditional training in the practical skills examination conducted 6 months later. These findings indicate that the TELA-sim can serve as an effective supplement to conventional training. As reported in the literature, haptic simulators offer advantages such as unlimited repeatable practice without consuming extracted teeth or artificial materials, objective feedback, and standardised conditions (Bandiaky et al,47). Nevertheless, conventional phantom-head training may provide higher clinical realism (eg, tactile feedback from natural tooth structure and soft tissue simulation). Thus, the two approaches work well together, with the TELA-sim complementing conventional training.47

Analysis of the original data showed that both groups rated the TELA-sim positively (median = 3.00), with no significant differences at α = 0.01, indicating considerable face validity. Sensitivity analyses across three imputed datasets (12 comparisons) yielded results that were consistently in agreement with the original data. The consistency between original and imputed results suggests that missing data (19.4%, all from novices) had a limited impact on the conclusions. Nonetheless, the low-to-moderate missing rate warrants caution, and future studies should use redundant data collection to prevent data loss (Supplementary Table 4).

The novice group expressed a high level of satisfaction with the usefulness of the TE module (median 3.75, IQR 3.29-4.00). They reported particularly high satisfaction with the statement that it provided a more intuitive and comprehensive understanding of the tooth extraction process (from preoperative preparation to completion) compared to traditional learning methods (4.00, 3.75-4.00). They also demonstrated extremely high satisfaction with their overall experience of using the TE module (4.00, 4.00-4.25). Furthermore, the novice group held positive attitudes towards the realism of the TE module (3.50, 3.00-4.00), reporting satisfactory haptic feedback for both hard and soft tissues and for the process of forceps placement. Evaluations from the intermediate group for the TE module were more conservative than those of the novice group (usefulness 3.50, 3.00-4.00; realism 3.33, 2.67-4.00), although this difference was not statistically significant. For the IANB module, participants in both groups expressed positive attitudes towards its usefulness (novice group 3.92, 3.17-4.00; intermediate group 3.67, 3.00-4.00). They agreed that the simulator could aid in understanding the IANB procedure, and improved their precision in needle placement and confidence in preoperative preparation, as well as their understanding of needle insertion and injection procedures. The novice group reported satisfaction with the realism of the IANB haptic realism (3.50, 3.00-4.00). The positive user perceptions observed in this study align with previous systematic review findings, which reported that haptic technologies were well perceived by participants and facilitated their understanding of performed tasks (Bandiaky et al,47). These results collectively indicate that the simulator possesses sound face validity.

However, a subset of learners rated the simulator neutrally or negatively. This may be attributed to individual differences in confidence, cognitive load, prior experience with virtual reality, simulator sickness or perceived realism.48,49 To address this, future versions could introduce adaptive difficulty levels, personalised real-time feedback, and a pretraining orientation, thereby better supporting learners who did not benefit from the current intervention.

The significant differences in simulator scores and completion times between the two groups reflect the simulator’s capacity to discriminate between participants with varying levels of experience, thereby demonstrating good construct validity. Significant differences were observed between the novice and intermediate groups in total scores for both the TE (P < .001) and IANB (P = .004) modules. A significant difference was also found between the two groups in the time spent on the IANB task (P < .001). This difference, together with the wide IQRs in Figure 4, reflects heterogeneity in baseline skills and task complexity, underscoring the need for standardised simulation training. Future studies may explore behavioural strategies (eg, time-use patterns), but this was not the main research question of the present study.

To investigate the simulator’s potential for translating into tangible clinical skills, a nonrandomised controlled cohort study was conducted. It was found that the TELA-sim group, which received additional simulator training, demonstrated superior performance in a phantom assessment after 6 months compared to the control group, which received only traditional instruction (median 84.00 vs median 75.00; z = 8.245, P < .001, 95% CI: 8.00-13.00; δ = 0.76). This core finding provides preliminary evidence that immersive simulation integrated into the clinical workflow may promote medium-term retention of procedural skills (up to 6 months). Such a platform may serve as a standardised platform for developing core competencies in basic oral surgery. In this study, participants were likely aware that the TELA-sim was a newly developed device, which may have introduced a Hawthorne effect. However, the 6-month follow-up period and the use of automated, objective metrics minimise its potential impact on the main conclusions.50

However, contrary to the hypothesis, the intermediate group scored lower than the novice group (85.00 vs 91.50). This counterintuitive pattern likely reflects ‘experiential drift’, where intermediate practitioners may adopt efficiency-driven shortcuts that diverge from formal, safety-critical protocols. This phenomenon may warrant further investigation in future research.51,52 Additionally, the checklist-based VR scoring system may favour strict rule-following (more common among novices) over the strategic, efficiency-oriented adaptations that intermediates develop in real practice.53, 54, 55 This is reinforced by the observation that intermediates excelled in technical execution but scored lower on preparatory and communicative steps, despite completing IANB faster.56 Such a pattern suggests a potential limitation of protocol-adherent rubrics in capturing advanced clinical judgment. This phenomenon warrants further investigation in future research.

In this study, participants were likely aware that the TELA sim was a newly developed device, which may have introduced a Hawthorne effect. However, the potential impact of this bias is limited for several reasons. First, during the validity assessment, participants were blinded to group comparisons, reducing performance bias. Second, the skill transfer assessment was conducted 6 months after training, by which any short-term novelty effect would likely have faded. Third, all simulator-based performance metrics were recorded automatically without subjective rater interpretation. Therefore, we believe the Hawthorne effect does not undermine our main conclusions.50

The significance of this work extends beyond the specific procedures simulated, aligning with broader trends in dental education and healthcare. For preclinical dental students lacking clinical experience, developing an understanding of the complete, standardised procedural workflow is as critical as mastering key technical skills.11 The ability to adhere to a standardised procedural workflow holds significant implications for surgical success rates and complication management.19,21 However, this critical competency has been largely overlooked by existing haptic simulators. The TELA-sim addresses this gap by incorporating key steps often omitted in other devices. For instance, it includes a dedicated disinfection step, thereby emphasizing the importance of aseptic technique and reinforcing principles of infection control.20 Furthermore, the simulator mandates the critical step of tooth identification, a process essential for preventing wrong-site surgery and safeguarding patient functional health.57 The simulator’s emphasis on adherence to clinical workflows directly aligns with the requirements of the Chinese National Dental Practical Examination, suggesting its potential application in Objective Structured Clinical Examinations.17,58 Its use could facilitate more objective and granular skills assessment, reduce the human resource costs associated with examiner proctoring, and mitigate the variability in scoring preferences among different examiners.59,60 Looking ahead, such technology holds promise for assisting higher education institutions and even licensing bodies in achieving more standardised and reliable competency recognition.59

Therefore, the findings of this study support the use of TELA-sim in low-stakes contexts, such as routine skills practice, formative evaluation, and self-directed learning, where its objective metrics can provide meaningful feedback without the pressure of pass/fail decisions. For external examinations (eg, national licensing exams, standardised residency training assessments, preclinical skills competitions, and postgraduate entrance skill tests), TELA-sim can be embedded by aligning scoring rubrics, standardising case scenarios, and establishing normative pass/fail thresholds. However, before the simulator can be adopted for high-stakes purposes (eg, determining eligibility for clinical practice or certifying procedural competence), further validation is necessary. This would include establishing normative pass/fail thresholds, demonstrating predictive validity against real-world performance, and ensuring equivalence across different examination formats.

From a patient safety perspective, the implications are profound. By allowing learners to repeatedly practice and internalize safe, standardised protocols in a risk-free environment, the simulator serves as a tool for error prevention before clinical entry.47,57,61 The objective, fine-grained data captured by the simulator could eventually support personalised feedback and the creation of standardised performance benchmarks for certification.62 Moreover, the skill transfer demonstrated in this study hints at the potential for extending the simulator’s technology and workflow-integrated design to other oral surgical procedures requiring similar haptic and procedural fidelity, including impacted tooth extraction and implant placement, though further investigation is needed to confirm this generalizability.47 Finally, the observation of ‘experiential drift’ in this study hints at a subtle phenomenon where learners may deviate from standardised protocols without direct supervision.51,52 This finding opens a new perspective on the potential utility of such simulators not only for undergraduate education but also for postgraduate training and skills recalibration, offering a tool for continuous professional development and the maintenance of clinical standards throughout a practitioner’s career.

While this study provides preliminary support for the face and construct validity of the TELA-sim, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the novice subgroup may not fully represent the broader student population, as their ratings of realism and acceptability could be influenced by an initial ‘novelty effect’. Second, the current scoring rubric prioritises protocol adherence over the strategic adaptations often seen in experienced clinicians, such as prophylactic anaesthetic supplementation, which may lead to an underestimation of intermediate proficiency. Future assessments would benefit from hybrid rubrics that balance procedural fidelity with adaptive efficiency. Third, the relatively lower haptic realism ratings for IANB soft-tissue simulation from intermediate participants suggest a need for further refinement to meet expert expectations. Furthermore, the use of a historical control group introduces potential temporal bias, including subtle curriculum shifts over time, meaning that prospective randomised controlled trials are required to strengthen causal claims. While the 6‑month follow‑up provides initial evidence of medium‑term skill retention, longer‑term retention (eg, beyond 6 months) requires further investigation; future studies with extended follow‑up periods (eg, 12 or 18 months) are planned as the simulator continues to evolve.63 Finally, as a single-institution study, these findings may have limited generalisability, warranting future multicentre validation.

Future research should refine the scoring algorithms to incorporate clinical decision-making, motion economy, and outcome quality, ensuring a more balanced assessment across all experience levels.64 The integration of generative AI to create adaptive, patient-specific scenarios could further enhance realism and personalised learning.5,65 Additionally, longitudinal studies are essential to map learning curves beyond the current cross-sectional design, while deeper investigation into the underlying cognitive and psychomotor mechanisms will help elucidate the skill transfer process. Multicentre trials with larger, more diverse cohorts are necessary to confirm the broader applicability of the platform.66

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study successfully developed and validated the TELA-sim, a haptic virtual reality simulator that integrates the complete clinical workflow for TE and IANB, partially addressing the lack of integration in existing simulators. The findings suggest that workflow‑integrated simulation supports skill acquisition and retention up to 6 months, with evidence of transfer to clinical practice. However, longer‑term retention beyond 6 months requires further investigation, and although face validity was generally supported, not all students responded favourably to the simulator.
