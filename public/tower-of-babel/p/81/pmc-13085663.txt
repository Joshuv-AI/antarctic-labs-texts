Maternal and neonatal outcomes and histological chorioamnionitis in preterm premature rupture of membranes complicated by group B streptococcus colonization: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Yun Chai, Wei Zheng, Yun Wang, Hongmin An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHistological chorioamnionitis (HCA) is frequently identified in placental examinations following preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM). Colonization of the genital tract by Group B Streptococcus (GBS) represents a major risk factor for serious neonatal infections. This study evaluated the influence of GBS on both maternal and neonatal outcomes in pregnancies complicated by PPROM before 34 weeks’ gestation managed expectantly.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of clinical records from pregnant women experiencing spontaneous PPROM between 23 + 0 and 33 + 6 weeks’ gestation, who underwent vaginal microbiota screening at Hangzhou Maternal and Child Health Hospital between January 2019 and December 2023. Data on sociodemographic characteristics, vaginal microbiota, pregnancy outcomes, and neonatal Apgar scores were collected for patients with PPROM.ResultsAmong 308 patients meeting the inclusion criteria, 24 (7.79%) tested positive for GBS. The GBS-positive group demonstrated significantly higher rates of HCA, neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), and neonatal intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) compared to the GBS-negative group (p < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups regarding clinical chorioamnionitis, puerperal infection, postpartum hemorrhage, fetal distress, Apgar scores, neonatal sepsis, or necrotizing enterocolitis.ConclusionIn patients with PPROM before 34 weeks’ gestation, colonization with GBS may be associated with poorer neonatal outcomes and an increased incidence of maternal HCA.Keywords: Group B Streptococcus, Histological chorioamnionitis, Preterm premature rupture of membranes, Neonatal outcomes

Introduction

Preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), defined as membrane rupture prior to the onset of labor in pregnancies of less than 37 weeks’ gestation, is a significant obstetric complication [1]. It is implicated in approximately 30% of preterm deliveries and is associated with substantial maternal and neonatal morbidity [2, 3]. The contribution of PPROM to overall preterm birth rates shows population-based variation, reported at around 10% in some national datasets. Clinical chorioamnionitis is a well-established correlate of PPROM, especially at earlier gestational ages [4].

Group B Streptococcus (GBS), or Streptococcus agalactiae, stands as a leading global cause of infectious morbidity and mortality in newborns [5]. While often asymptomatically colonizing the human gastrointestinal and lower genital tracts, GBS exhibits high pathogenicity in susceptible hosts. Maternal colonization prevalence exhibits considerable geographical disparity, with a global estimate of approximately 18% across 85 countries, ranging from 11% to 35% regionally [6]. GBS colonization can precipitate conditions ranging from asymptomatic bacteriuria and urinary tract infections to severe peripartum infections like chorioamnionitis, endometritis, and sepsis [7]. Without intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis, about 50% of infants born to GBS-colonized mothers become colonized, with vertical transmission during labor or post-membrane rupture leading to invasive disease (e.g., pneumonia, sepsis, meningitis) in roughly 1% of neonates [8, 9].

Histological chorioamnionitis (HCA) denotes inflammation of the chorionic plate, fetal membranes, umbilical cord, and chorionic vessels, and is strongly associated with preterm birth. It is observed in 50–60% of PPROM cases and correlates inversely with gestational age and birth weight [10–13]. Prior studies report GBS detection in approximately 9% of vaginal secretions and 1% of amniotic fluid samples from PPROM patients [14]. This retrospective study aims to analyze pregnancies delivered before 34 weeks with available GBS screening data. Its primary objectives are to assess the effect of genital tract GBS colonization on HCA incidence and neonatal complications in PPROM, and to examine the impact of HCA itself on maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study included all singleton pregnancies admitted for expectant management of PPROM at Hangzhou Women’s Hospital from January 2019 to December 2023. The study protocol received approval from the hospital’s Medical Ethics Committee (Approval No. 2025A148). Diagnosis of PPROM involved a sterile speculum examination confirming fluid pooling in the vaginal vault, with supportive positive fern and/or nitrazine tests. Inclusion criteria were: (1) PPROM occurring before 34 weeks’ gestation; (2) singleton pregnancy; (3) complete vaginal flora test (VFT) results during pregnancy; and (4) availability of comprehensive medical records detailing pregnancy outcomes. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) multiple gestations; (2) conditions predisposing to adverse outcomes (e.g., uterine malformations, abnormal fetal lie, cervical insufficiency); and (3) lost to follow-up. The final cohort consisted of 308 women. Data on maternal baseline characteristics, intrapartum course, and obstetric/neonatal outcomes were extracted from electronic health records.

Vaginal secretion analysis

Trained obstetricians collected vaginal fluid specimens using sterile swabs from the lower third of the vagina. Specimens were processed in a standard microbiology laboratory. Four swabs were routinely used for testing Mycoplasma, Chlamydia, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and for routine bacterial culture, respectively. Sampling occurred upon hospital admission for patients presenting with PPROM.

Assessment of pregnancy outcomes

Gestational age at delivery was calculated from the first day of the last menstrual period. PPROM diagnosis was confirmed clinically as described. Neonatal Apgar scores (assessing respiration, heart rate, reflex irritability, muscle tone, and color at 1 and 5 min) and birth weight were recorded. Endometritis was diagnosed based on maternal temperature > 100.4 °F (38.0 °C) or leukocytosis, combined with uterine tenderness or foul-smelling lochia in the absence of an alternative infection source. HCA diagnosis was based on placental pathology reports, following the diagnostic criteria outlined by the Amsterdam Placenta Workshop Group consensus [15].

Definition of neonatal outcomes

Intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) is a critical condition primarily observed in preterm neonates, posing significant risks of mortality and long-term complications. This condition occurs due to the immaturity of the central nervous system and vascular structures, which makes the fragile vessels in the germinal matrix prone to rupture. The hemorrhage often extends into the lateral ventricles, resulting in IVH [16, 17].

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) is a well-documented and severe gastrointestinal disease that poses a significant threat to the lives of newborns. It is particularly prevalent among preterm infants, with an incidence rate of approximately 10% in those weighing less than 1500 g at birth [18, 19]. NEC is characterized by intestinal inflammation and necrosis, predominantly affecting the terminal ileum and proximal colon. To date, the Bell staging system remains the most commonly used clinical definition for NEC. It is specifically designed for evaluating the severity of NEC [20]. Its clear descriptive nature has made it a widely accepted tool in the medical community, which classified NEC into three stages according to this system, namely stage I (suspected NEC), stage II (confirmed NEC), and stage III (advanced NEC) [20].

Neonatal sepsis was diagnosed by the clinical findings and the presence of a positive blood culture, in combination with clinical and laboratory parameters [21]. The diagnosis of neonatal sepsis in this study was based on the clinical diagnostic criteria from the study “Diagnosis and treatment of neonatal sepsis” [22–24]. Confirming the diagnosis requires clinical manifestations and a positive culture of blood or cerebrospinal fluid (or other sterile cavity fluid) [25].

Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) is a severe and potentially life-threatening form of lung injury recently defined by the International Neonatal RDS Consensus. It is marked by extensive lung inflammation and damage to the alveolar epithelium and vascular endothelium [26]. Characteristics of ARDS include an acute trigger with rapid clinical onset, diffuse bilateral lung opacities, the absence of cardiogenic lung edema, and oxygenation deficits used to stratify disease severity [27].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS software (Version 20.0, IBM). Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and clinical variables. Comparisons between groups (GBS-positive vs. GBS-negative; HCA vs. non-HCA) employed Student’s t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 defined statistical significance.

Results

Clinical characteristics of participants

The analysis included 308 patients with PPROM. Among them, 24 (7.79%) were positive for GBS colonization, while 284 (92.21%) were negative. Comparative analysis of 12 maternal parameters (e.g., age, BMI, pre-delivery WBC) is shown in Tables 1 and 2. Two parameters differed significantly: the latency period (from membrane rupture to delivery) was shorter in the GBS-positive group (p = 0.016), and the prevalence of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) was higher in the GBS-positive group (p < 0.0001). The remaining ten parameters, including maternal age and maternal BMI, showed no significant intergroup differences.

Obstetric and neonatal outcomes

Comparative outcomes for fourteen parameters are presented in Table 3. Three outcomes were significantly worse in the GBS-positive group: HCA rate (58.33% vs. 32.04%, p = 0.009), neonatal RDS incidence (p = 0.02), and neonatal IVH rate (p = 0.02). The rate of clinical chorioamnionitis was 33.33% in GBS-positive vs. 17.96% in GBS-negative women, though this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.07). Rates of puerperal infection, postpartum hemorrhage, assisted vaginal delivery, and primary cesarean section were similar between groups. Incidences of fetal distress, low 1-minute Apgar scores (< 7), NICU admission, neonatal sepsis, and necrotizing enterocolitis did not differ significantly.

Discussion

GBS remains a predominant pathogen in neonatal sepsis [28, 29]. Vertical transmission predominantly occurs via ascending infection from maternal genital or gastrointestinal tract colonization after membrane rupture or labor onset [30]. A shorter membrane rupture-to-delivery interval elevates the risk for neonatal GBS sepsis [29]. GBS-related sepsis contributes to increased maternal and neonatal mortality, particularly in resource-limited settings [7, 31, 32]. Evidence suggests GBS may induce cytokine release and inflammatory mediator production, potentially contributing to membrane weakening and rupture [33, 34], underscoring a plausible biological link between GBS and PPROM [35]. Thus, GBS screening in PPROM patients is clinically warranted. Reported GBS colonization rates in PPROM vary regionally from 4.2% to 25% [1, 31, 32, 35, 36]. Our finding of a 7.8% positivity rate aligns with this range.

While our study found no significant difference in gestational age at delivery between GBS groups, the shorter latency period in GBS-positive women is consistent with studies linking GBS colonization to preterm birth [37, 38]. The strong association between GBS colonization and GDM in our cohort is supported by a meta-analysis indicating a 16% increased risk of GBS colonization in women with GDM [39], suggesting a potential bidirectional relationship or shared risk factors.

The literature presents varying findings on GBS and pregnancy outcomes. Some studies associate GBS with intrauterine infection, fetal distress, postpartum hemorrhage, PROM, and postpartum infection [40], preterm birth, and low birth weight [41], or with PROM and HCA [42]. Conversely, others report no significant association with adverse perinatal outcomes [43]. Our study adds nuance: while clinical chorioamnionitis rates were not significantly different, HCA was markedly more frequent in GBS-positive women. In a retrospective study of mid-pregnancy spontaneous abortions, microorganisms were detected in 85 cases via culture, with GBS being the most significant pathogen. Among culture-positive cases, 81% were diagnosed with histological chorioamnionitis, suggesting a strong association between GBS infection and HCA [44]. Our findings are consistent with their results. This highlights the importance of placental histology, beyond clinical signs alone, in diagnosing related inflammation. The lack of difference in other maternal outcomes (puerperal infection, hemorrhage, delivery mode) suggests GBS’s primary maternal impact in PPROM may be localized to the fetoplacental unit (HCA).

Regarding neonatal outcomes, while some studies link GBS to neonatal pneumonia, sepsis [40], or pathological jaundice and low birth weight [42], we found no significant differences in fetal distress, low Apgar scores, sepsis, or necrotizing enterocolitis. However, the increased rates of RDS and IVH in neonates from GBS-positive mothers are notable. The mechanism may involve aspiration/inhalation of infected vaginal secretions, leading to early-onset respiratory compromise [45]. Our findings align with this pathophysiological model. Recent experimental evidence from a murine model indicates that gestational diabetes can exacerbate fetal GBS infection and impair neonatal outcomes via immune and microbiota disruption [46], offering a potential explanation for the interplay observed in our clinical data. The study indicates that magnesium sulfate can induce measurable changes in maternal inflammatory profiles [47]. The 2019 SOGC guidelines recommend magnesium sulfate for women at risk of preterm birth (≤ 33 + 6 weeks gestation) [48]. All subjects included in this study received magnesium sulfate therapy.

This single-center analysis confirms that women with genital GBS colonization have a significantly increased incidence of HCA in PPROM. Reported HCA rates vary from 33% to 71% [49]; our observed rate of 34.1% falls within this spectrum. Consistent with known pathophysiology, HCA was associated with earlier delivery and lower birth weight.

The results presented in Table 4 indicated that among women who delivered preterm before 34 weeks of gestation, although the incidence of puerperal infection was numerically higher in the histological chorioamnionitis (HCA) group, the difference was not statistically significant (P > 0.05). This finding suggests that in the context of very preterm birth, HCA identified by placental pathology is not a decisive factor for increased maternal infectious morbidity postpartum, and this phenomenon is closely linked to the pathophysiological characteristics of preterm labor. Firstly, from the perspective of histology and infectious process, HCA in this study was mostly associated with subclinical intrauterine infection. Preterm birth before 34 weeks is often regarded as a “defensive expulsion response” of the host to infection. This mechanism of preterm labor initiation may limit the continuous progression of the infection, resulting in a relatively low pathogen load at the time of delivery, which is insufficient to cause extensive and severe invasive damage to the maternal reproductive tract. Secondly, the definition of HCA in this study primarily reflects the maternal inflammatory response and does not mandate the inclusion of fetal inflammatory response syndrome (FIRS). Furthermore, the specific clinical interventions for preterm birth also explain this negative result. For preterm labor before 34 weeks, clinicians usually adopt a more aggressive strategy for termination of pregnancy. Preterm labor itself is often characterized by a shorter duration, which reduces the time window for ascending intrapartum infection. Meanwhile, broad-spectrum antibiotics are routinely administered prophylactically before and after delivery for preterm patients with fever or suspected infection. This standardized anti-infective management has largely offset the potential impact of HCA on postpartum infections. Therefore, in this specific population of preterm births < 34 weeks, HCA diagnosed by placental pathology is more indicative of the etiology of preterm labor rather than an independent risk factor for predicting puerperal infection.

Key strengths of this study include its focus on vaginal microbiota changes in PPROM—a less commonly studied area domestically—and the use of a uniform diagnostic and management protocol within a single center, including routine placental pathology, ensuring cohort consistency. Limitations are inherent to its retrospective design, which precluded assessment of vaginal microbiota dynamics over time. The sample size of only 24 cases in the GBS-positive group limited the statistical power of the study for low-incidence neonatal outcomes such as intraventricular hemorrhage. The findings require validation in larger, multicenter studies. Given that relevant clinical variables such as gestational diabetes mellitus and latency period differed between groups, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Furthermore, multivariate analysis was precluded by sample size limitations. These factors constrain the study’s generalizability, underscoring the need for larger, multicenter prospective studies.

Conclusion

This retrospective cohort study indicates that in pregnancies with PPROM before 34 weeks, maternal genital tract GBS colonization may be associated with adverse neonatal outcomes (specifically increased RDS and IVH) and a higher incidence of histological chorioamnionitis, without significantly affecting other measured maternal outcomes.
