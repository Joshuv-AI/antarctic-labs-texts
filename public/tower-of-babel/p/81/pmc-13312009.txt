Pre-war induced theta oscillations moderate adolescents’ psychological responses to war exposure
Authors: Nir Orpaz, Gil-Shner Livne, Rany Abend, Daniel S. Pine, Tomer Shechner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAdolescents exposed to war face heightened risk for psychological difficulties, yet outcomes vary widely, underscoring the need to identify neurocognitive mechanisms of resilience and vulnerability. Error monitoring, a core facet of cognitive control, may play a critical role in shaping responses to real-world stressors. This longitudinal study examined whether two error-related neural markers: error-related negativity (ERN) and mediofrontal induced theta oscillations, moderate the impact of war exposure on adolescents’ psychological adjustment.MethodFifty adolescents (ages 12–17) completed a laboratory flanker task with electroencephalography (EEG) recordings prior to the outbreak of war. One year later, during an escalation of armed conflict, 30 participants completed follow-up assessments of anxiety, perceived stress, and avoidant coping.ResultsModeration analyses revealed that induced theta, but not ERN, moderated the associations between objective exposure to war (number of missile alert sirens) and psychological outcomes. Specifically, higher levels of induced theta amplified the link between war exposure and both perceived stress and avoidant coping. No overall increases in mean anxiety levels were observed, highlighting the heterogeneity of adolescents’ responses to conflict. By contrast, ERN, although established as a developmental biomarker of anxiety risk, did not predict stress-related outcomes under prolonged war conditions.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that mediofrontal induced theta oscillations, reflecting dynamic and context-sensitive cognitive control processes, may serve as a neurobiological marker of vulnerability to maladaptive coping in real-world stress contexts. This study advances understanding of adolescent neurocognitive responses to threat and underscores the value of age-sensitive markers for identifying individuals at risk.

Clinical significance statement

These findings suggest that pre-war theta oscillations may serve as a neurobiological marker of vulnerability, predicting which adolescents are at increased risk for heightened stress and avoidant coping during active military conflict. Accordingly, clinicians could use age-sensitive neurocognitive screening to identify high-risk youth who may appear resilient at the group level but possess underlying vulnerabilities. Early identification may, in turn, enable the implementation of targeted preventive interventions before maladaptive coping strategies become entrenched.

Introduction

Individuals living in areas affected by war or terrorism are at heightened risk for a range of psychopathologies (Bayer & Shtudiner, 2023; Levi-Belz et al., 2024; MacDermid Wadsworth, 2010; Mahat-Shamir et al., 2018). Adolescents may be particularly vulnerable to the mental health consequences of war and terrorism, partly due to the ongoing maturation of stress-sensitive brain systems such as the cortico-limbic circuitry (Dimitry, 2012; Eiland & Romeo, 2013). Specifically, while the amygdala, which plays a key role in emotional and threat reactivity, matures relatively early, regions of the prefrontal cortex, involved in cognitive control and emotion regulation exhibits a more protracted developmental trajectory (Gogtay et al., 2004). This developmental mismatch can lead to heightened threat sensitivity and less efficient regulation of fear responses, especially in contexts of adversity (Casey, 2015; Gee et al., 2013; Ruge et al., 2024; Tottenham & Galván, 2016).

In conflict zones, repeated exposure to direct attacks and potential-attack situations create a persistent state of threat, often triggering heightened anxiety and physiological arousal. These have been linked to elevated PTSD symptoms (Braun‐Lewensohn et al., 2011) and increased psychological distress across multiple domains (Henrich & Shahar, 2013). Yet, despite these risks (Kletter & Carrion, 2018; MacDermid Wadsworth, 2010; Slone & Shechner, 2009), outcomes among war-exposed youth vary substantially, from severe and persistent symptoms to minimal long-term impact (Kilmer, 2014; Laufer & Solomon, 2006). Meta-analytic and longitudinal studies indicate that while many initially experience distress, only a subset develop persistent disorders such as PTSD and depression (Attanayake et al., 2009; Neria et al., 2010). This variability likely reflects individual differences in the maturation of the neural circuits supporting fear expression and regulation (Casey, 2015; Gee et al., 2013; Pattwell et al., 2012; Tottenham & Galván, 2016). Elucidating these neurocognitive mechanisms is therefore essential for understanding resilience and for identifying adolescents at heightened risk who may benefit from early, targeted interventions.

One candidate mechanism may be cognitive control, a set of abilities that enable individuals to monitor performance and adapt to changing circumstances (Cohen, 2014). As a multifaceted construct, cognitive control encompasses several components, including error monitoring, the ability to detect when performance deviates from goals and to adjust behavior accordingly (Taylor et al., 2007; Ullsperger et al., 2014). This regulatory process closely mirrors the demands of coping with sustained stress, which similarly requires recognizing discrepancies between current and desired states and flexibly adjusting behaviors. Consequently, individual differences in error monitoring may translate into differences in adaptive coping under threat. Error signals can also represent potential threat cues, triggering defensive and evaluative responses that recruit mediofrontal control systems (Cavanagh & Shackman, 2015; Weinberg et al., 2016). Neural reactivity to such signals may capture individual differences in threat sensitivity and the capacity to regulate affective responses to maintain goal-directed behavior under stress. For instance, heightened neural reactivity to errors has been linked to anxiety and reduced coping flexibility (Moser et al., 2013). Error monitoring is indexed electrophysiologically by error-related negativity (ERN), a mediofrontal event-related potential, and by increases in 4–7 Hz theta oscillations reflecting control-related activity (Cavanagh & Frank, 2014; Cohen, 2014). Both ERN and theta oscillations are reliably elicited using the flanker task across development (Meyer, 2022; Meyer et al., 2012).

Extensive research links ERN magnitude to the later emergence of psychopathology in adolescents. A more negative ERN, reflecting a stronger neural response to errors and heightened error monitoring, has been consistently associated with greater risk for internalizing disorders, particularly anxiety (Meyer et al., 2018, 2021), even in nonclinical youth (Meyer, 2017; Olvet & Hajcak, 2008). ERN matures from childhood through adolescence (Buzzell, Richards et al., 2017; Meyer, 2017; Tamnes et al., 2013) and is linked to temperament-based risk factors for anxiety such as behavioral inhibition (Buzzell, Troller-Renfree et al., 2017). Importantly, ERN is also associated with real-world stress responses; for example, following Hurricane Sandy, children with a more negative ERN pre disaster, especially those high in behavioral inhibition, were more likely to develop internalizing symptoms (Meyer et al., 2017). Collectively, these findings highlight ERN’s potential to identify adolescents at risk for maladaptive responses to future stressors (Meyer, 2017).

Like the ERN, error-related theta oscillations reflect mediofrontal mechanisms of conflict monitoring and adaptive control, yet their links to psychopathology is more limited. Some work shows promise. Increased theta activity has been associated with higher anxiety in adults with generalized anxiety disorder (Cavanagh et al., 2017) and youth with obsessive-compulsive disorder (Suzuki et al., 2023). However, our recent developmental findings suggest age-dependent effects: in children exposed to COVID-19 lockdown stress, higher induced theta predicted lower anxiety and worry (Shner‐Livne et al., 2023). This variability aligns with broader evidence that neural biomarkers of emotion regulation may show developmental shifts (Beauchaine, 2015; Grillon et al., 2019; Gunnar & Quevedo, 2007; Meyer, 2017), highlighting the need to clarify how theta-related neural mechanisms contribute to emotional vulnerability across development.

This longitudinal study examined whether two neural indices of error monitoring, ERN and mediofrontal induced error-related theta oscillations, prospectively moderate the associations between exposure to war and subsequent perceived stress, anxiety symptoms, and avoidant coping in adolescents. Importantly, the baseline phase of this study (T0) was originally designed to cross-sectionally examine neural mechanisms among adolescents across varying levels of anxiety symptomatology during a relatively stable, non-crisis period. However, given the unexpected outbreak of the Israel-Hamas war in October 2023, following neural data collection we shifted our focus to a longitudinal design to prospectively examine whether these pre-existing neurocognitive markers predict responses to a real-world war stressor. Although ERN is a well-established biomarker of vulnerability for internalizing psychopathology, its ecological validity in predicting real-world responses is less clear. Although induced error-related theta is increasingly recognized as integral to cognitive control (Shner‐Livne et al., 2023; Tan et al., 2024), it has rarely been studied for its predictive value, particularly among youth. This study therefore sought to evaluate the predictive utility of both neuro markers in the setting of sustained, real-life threat.

We hypothesized that anxiety symptoms would increase following the onset of war and that a greater increase in anxiety, perceived stress and avoidant coping would be associated with higher levels of exposure to war-related adversity. Given our adolescent sample, our second hypothesis predicted that a stronger (i.e. more negative) ERN and higher induced error-related theta activity, measured prior to the onset of the armed conflict, would each moderate the association between objective exposure to war and subsequent outcomes, including perceived stress, anxiety symptoms, and avoidant coping. This approach builds on prior work linking enhanced error monitoring to internalizing symptoms in adults and adolescents.

Method

Participants

Fifty-nine adolescents aged 12 to 17 years (M = 14.65 years, SD = 1.43; 44.1% females) participated in the first laboratory visit (T0), as part of a larger study examining neural mechanisms of anxiety. Of them, three participants (M = 14.15 years, SD = 1.77; 1 female) did not complete the laboratory visit and six others (M = 15.23 years, SD = 1.35; 3 female) met one or more exclusion criteria during the clinical assessment and were not included in the final sample. The final sample in T0 included 50 participants (M = 14.61 years, SD = 1.43; 44% females). Further exclusions were made based on poor behavioral performance on the Flanker task (n = 1), EEG artifact rejection (n = 3), EEG data reliability (ERN: n = 1, induced theta: n = 3), and one statistical outlier (n = 1) in which error-related theta power exceeded 3.3 standard deviations from the sample mean. Details regarding artifact rejection and measure-specific valid trials are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Recruitment was conducted through social media and by word to mouth. Exclusion criteria included current use of psychotropic medications, history of head injuries, history of a current or a previous mental disorder (psychosis, bipolar, autism, major depression, or eating disorders), or an IQ score below 70 as measured by two subtests (vocabulary and matrix reasoning) of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for children (WISC-4). See supplemental materials for demographics and clinical characteristics of the sample in T0 (see supplemental materials Tables S1 and S2).

Approximately 5 months later (M = 4.73 months, SD = 2.53), and about 2 weeks after the onset of the Israel-Hamas war (October 7th, 2023), participants were invited to complete a brief online follow-up assessing their exposure to war and terrorism. Of the initial baseline sample, 42 participants (M = 15.02 years, SD = 1.41; 40.5% females) agreed to participate. The purpose of this initial follow-up was to document participants’ immediate exposure to the war and related traumatic events following the October 7th terror attack. This assessment served as the first step in a longitudinal effort aimed at understanding how initial exposure to these highly distressing circumstances might predict the development of psychological symptoms over time. About a year later (October 2024), following a severe escalation of the military conflict along Israel’s northern border, directly impacting the Haifa area via massive missile barrages, participants were invited again to complete a more comprehensive follow-up online assessment (T1).

Of the initial baseline sample, 30 (M = 16.23 years, SD = 1.24; 53.3% females) completed the follow-up assessments. From this cohort, exclusions were applied for poor behavioral performance in the Flanker task (n = 1) and EEG artifact rejection (n = 2). In addition, one participant was excluded from the ERN analyses due to insufficient reliability (n = 1). For the induced theta analyses, four participants were excluded in total: three due to insufficient reliability (n = 3) and one a statistical outlier, with error-related theta power exceeding 3.3 standard deviations from the sample mean (n = 1). A detailed overview of all participant exclusions and the specific reasons for data rejection across both timepoints is presented in Table S7 in the Supplementary Materials. Collecting data within this short window was critical to capture adolescents’ acute responses to war exposure. This timing was essential for assessing the immediate effects of war-related stress before potential adaptation or habituation processes occurred. No significant differences in baseline anxiety symptoms or EEG patterns were found between participants who completed T1 and those who did not (all ps > 0.05; see supplemental materials Tables S3, S4 and S5). Legal guardians provided consent and adolescents gave assent prior to participation. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time and received a modest gift card. The study was approved by the university’s Institutional Review Board (352/23). The longitudinal follow-up was initiated rapidly in response to the unexpected outbreak of the Israel–Hamas war and the violent escalation along Israel’s northern border, requiring immediate data collection and was therefore not pre-registered.

Measures

As the current study focuses on clinical aspects, only clinical measures are reported below. Comprehensive details regarding the flanker task, EEG recording and analysis, as well as the internal consistency of EEG indices, are provided in the supplemental materials.

Baseline visit (T0)

Screen for Child Anxiety Related Emotional Disorders (SCARED). The SCARED questionnaire is a commonly used self-report measure assessing anxiety symptoms in youth (Birmaher et al., 1997). It consists of 41 items assessing anxiety symptoms, each rated on a 3-point Likert scale (0–2), with total anxiety scores ranging 0–82. Internal consistency in our study wasα = 0.929.

Follow-up assessment (T1)

Objective exposure to war. Exposure to war was assessed via the number of air-raid sirens alerting of incoming missile barrages experienced in the specific areas in which participants resided, since the onset of the war (October 7, 2023) until the follow-up assessment (T1). Sirens are sounded with relatively high geographical precision based on missile trajectories and therefore provide an accurate indication of imminent missile hits. These data were obtained for each individual using the official, publicly-available "National Emergency Portal" (https://www.oref.org.il/eng), based on their place of residence over the past year.

Subjective exposure to war. This scale included four statements in accordance with the A2 criteria for PTSD in the DSM-IV (e.g., “I felt that my life was in danger”) based of previous research (Lavi et al., 2013). The statements were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“not at all”) to 5 (“a lot”). Total score (sum of all ratings) ranges from 4 to 20 (see Supplemental Materials for the full list of items). Internal consistency was α = 0.812 at the exposure measurement (two weeks after October 7th) and α = 0.897 at T1. For the final analyses, a composite score was computed by averaging the total scores from both time points (two weeks after October 7th and at the T1 follow-up) to reflect participants' ongoing subjective experience during the military conflict.

Perceived Stress Scale (PSS). This 10-item questionnaire (Cohen et al., 1983) evaluates the degree to which individuals perceive their lives as stressful, with specific adjustments to reference the October 7th terror attack and the ensuing war. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“Never”) to 4 (“Very often”), with the total score ranges from 0 to 40. Internal consistency in our study was α = 0.875.

Brief - Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced Inventory (Brief – COPE). The Brief COPE (Carver, 1997) is a 28-item measure assessing coping strategies, adjusted to the October 7th events and subsequent war. Items are rated on a 4-point scale (1 = “I haven’t been doing this at all” to 4 = “I’ve been doing this a lot”) and grouped into three subscales: Problem-Focused, Emotion-Focused, and Avoidant Coping (Buchanan, 2021; Dias et al., 2012). Subscale scores represent the mean of corresponding items (range: 1–4). Internal consistency was α = 0.788 for Problem-Focused Coping, α = 0.692 for Emotion-Focused Coping, and α = 0.722 for Avoidant Coping. We focused on avoidant coping, given its robust and well-documented association with psychopathology, particularly anxiety and maladaptive stress responses in youth (Compas et al., 2017; Myruski et al., 2024). While emotion-focused coping is also a broadly relevant dimension, it encompasses a heterogeneous mix of both adaptive (e.g., seeking emotional support) and potentially maladaptive (e.g., venting) strategies. Thus, to maintain a precise theoretical focus, we exclusively analyzed avoidant coping, as avoidance operates as a core pathogenic mechanism that directly contributes to the development and maintenance of anxiety and trauma-related symptoms following acute stress (Ehlers & Clark, 2000; Pittig et al., 2018). Similar to T0, self-reported anxiety was measured using the SCARED. Internal consistency was α = 0.961.

Data analysis

For analysis of the Flanker task behavioral data (T0) see supplementary materials. Of note, the statistical analyses reported below rely on residualized neural indices (ERNresid and induced error-related theta power (dB)resid); for brevity, these are referred to as ERN and induced theta throughout the text. Detailed pre-processing and computation procedures are provided in the supplementary materials.

To examine our first hypothesis regarding differences in anxiety symptoms before and during the war, we conducted RM-ANCOVA on SCARED scores while controlling for the time passed between T0 and T1. We used Pearson correlations to examine the association between anxiety change and level of exposure to war-related adversity.

To test our second hypothesis, we conducted three moderation analyses to examine the link between objective exposure to war and perceived stress, anxiety symptoms, and avoidant coping at T1 for each error-related neural index (ERN and Theta) measured at T0. We used SPSS Hayes macro PROCESS (Preacher & Hayes, 2004), model number 1, which provides an estimate of the interaction effect, an estimated standard error, and 95% confidence intervals for the population value of the interaction effect.

In all the analyses we controlled for the time passed between T0 and T1, and participants’ T0 SCARED score to control for initial anxiety levels. We also controlled for participants’ subjective exposure to war, computed as the average of the two subjective exposure assessments (two weeks after October 7th and at the T1 follow-up); without this adjustment, the effects of actual threat intensity could be confounded with participants’ subjective perceptions and appraisals of the war.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27), with a significance threshold set at an alpha = 0.05 (two-sided). We corrected for multiple comparisons within each set of theoretically distinct models. Specifically, error-related induced theta and ERN were treated as separate families of hypotheses, each tested across three dependent variables (stress, anxiety, and avoidant coping). Within each family, we applied the Holm–Bonferroni correction (Holm, 1979) to adjust p-values for the three analyses.

Avoidant coping: A significant interaction between error-related induced theta power (t0) and war exposure was observed, F(1, 16) = 9.70, p = .007, Holm-adjusted p = .021, ∆R² = 0.280, Cohen's f² = 0.61. Post-hoc analyses revealed that more exposure to war was significantly associated with greater avoidant coping strategies in participants with higher levels of error-related induced theta, B = 0.03, t = 2.82, p = .012. Discussion

This longitudinal study examined whether two neural indices of error monitoring, mediofrontal induced error-related theta oscillations and ERN, moderated the impact of war exposure on adolescents’ psychological adjustment. Two main findings emerged. First, group mean anxiety did not increase following exposure to war, and objective exposure to threat during war was unrelated to perceived stress, anxiety, or avoidant coping. Second, induced theta, but not ERN moderated the associations between exposure to direct threat during war, and both perceived stress and avoidant coping during the war. Namely, only among adolescents with greater threat responsivity reflected in induced theta, exposure to war predicted threat-related responses and behaviors. Collectively, these findings highlight the specific utility of induced theta as a neurobiological marker of vulnerability to maladaptive coping in real-world stress contexts.

Although prior research has documented substantial psychological consequences of war exposure (Attanayake et al., 2009; Charlson et al., 2019), we did not observe such an effect in our sample. This contrasts with recent findings in adults (Levi-Belz et al., 2024), and a larger adolescent sample (Haham et al., 2026) during the same conflict, which reported high levels of psychopathological symptoms. This discrepancy may be explained by the concept of multifinality (Cicchetti & Rogosch, 1996), which posits that the psychological impact of war exposure in developing populations varies widely according to individual differences in pre-existing vulnerabilities and evolving developmental processes. Such between-subject variability, also evident in our study, may obscure group-level changes in mean scores, particularly in a relatively small sample where individual differences can more easily overshadow average effects compared to larger cohorts (Button et al., 2013). These findings emphasize the importance of examining individual differences and identifying markers of risk and resilience that influence psychological outcomes (Cohen et al., 2024; Kaim et al., 2024).

The moderating role of induced theta activity in the link between exposure to war and psychological outcomes indicates that theta may capture individual differences in vulnerability to maladaptive coping under real-world stress more effectively than the ERN, consistent with evidence that theta oscillations reflect dynamic, context-sensitive cognitive control processes (Shner‐Livne et al., 2023). This pattern aligns with prior work showing that higher error-related theta is associated with increased stress and anxiety in older adolescents and adults (Cavanagh et al., 2017; Suzuki et al., 2023), despite differences in analytic approaches to quantifying theta activity. However, it contrasts with our earlier findings in younger children, where higher induced theta predicted lower anxiety and worry during the first COVID-19 lockdown (Shner‐Livne et al., 2023). This developmental discrepancy suggests that the functional significance of induced error-related theta in relation to anxiety, stress, and avoidance shifts with age, consistent with evidence for protracted maturation of error monitoring and cognitive control systems across childhood and adolescence (Bethlehem et al., 2022; Buzzell, Richards et al., 2017; Tamnes et al., 2013; Tan et al., 2024). In earlier developmental stages, when typical induced theta levels are relatively low, higher theta may still reflect adaptive or normative engagement of cognitive control. In later stages, when typical levels increase, elevated induced theta may exceed the normative range, potentially reflecting overactivation of control mechanisms. Together, these findings point to a vulnerability that emerges from the interaction between neurobiological sensitivity and environmental context. Adolescents with elevated induced theta may exhibit heightened threat responsivity that, under conditions of severe or prolonged stress, exacerbate stress reactivity and avoidance. Thus, induced theta may represent a context-dependent risk marker, indexing sensitivity to environmental threat rather than a fixed vulnerability marker.

The null result regarding ERN should be interpreted in light of several methodological and contextual considerations. First, the relatively small sample size may have reduced statistical power, making it difficult to detect effects of the size typically observed in ERN studies. Second, our participants represented a heterogeneous group with varying subclinical and clinical characteristics. Such variability can add noise to the signal, obscuring subtle associations between ERN and psychological outcomes. Third, much of the prior research linking ERN to later psychopathology has examined either laboratory-based stressors or discrete real-world events, such as natural disasters, where the onset and duration are relatively well-defined. In contrast, the present study assessed coping in the context of an ongoing and multifaceted conflict involving repeated siren alerts and sustained uncertainty. It is possible that ERN, while sensitive to vulnerability in certain acute stress contexts, may be less suited to predicting responses to prolonged, fluctuating threat. Finally, the developmental period studied here, mid-adolescence, may also influence the relationship between ERN and stress-related outcomes. ERN undergoes maturation during adolescence, and the timing and trajectory of this development could modulate its predictive utility. Together, these factors may help explain why ERN, despite being a robust marker of risk in other contexts, did not emerge as a significant predictor in this particular setting.

The study has several important limitations. First, given the rapid onset of war exposure and time-sensitive data collection, the follow-up sample was relatively small, hence our findings should be considered preliminary and require replication. Adolescents most severely impacted by the war in cases of displacement or direct trauma, may have been less likely to participate, and thus are underrepresented in the present sample. The mechanisms identified here may nevertheless help inform support for a broad range of youth affected by conflict. Second, anxiety was assessed using the SCARED questionnaire, which measures trait anxiety and may lack the sensitivity to detect subtle, state-like fluctuations in symptoms over time. This may explain why we did not observe an increase in anxiety following the war or moderation effects of war exposure on the association between induced error-related theta activity and anxiety. Employing a more sensitive measure could have revealed patterns more consistent with our findings for perceived stress and avoidant coping. Third, our measure of objective war exposure was the number of sirens that occurred in each participant’s disclosed area of residence. While sirens reflect potential missile hits with relatively high geographical accuracy, we could not verify participant physical location during every siren event. Nevertheless, it was the most reliable and objective indicator available for assessing direct exposure to a war related threat. Fourth, perceived stress and coping strategies were assessed retrospectively using the PSS and Brief-COPE; retroactive reporting may be subject to recall bias and inaccuracies in reporting (Robinson & Clore, 2002; Shiffman et al., 2008). Future studies would therefore benefit from real-time assessment methods, such as ecological momentary assessment (EMA).

In conclusion, our findings highlight mediofrontal-induced error-related theta activity as a potential neurobiological marker for identifying adolescents at heightened risk of long-term psychopathology after war exposure, a critical insight given the diverse ways war exposure affects youth. This work advances understanding of the neurodevelopmental trajectory of cognitive control and error monitoring, and underscores the need for age-sensitive approaches to assessment and intervention. While ERN is a well-established marker of psychopathology, our results suggest that induced theta warrants greater attention, particularly for predicting outcomes in real-world stress contexts. Continued research across developmental stages and populations may yield critical insights into mechanisms of vulnerability and resilience.
