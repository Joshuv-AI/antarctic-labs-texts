The influenza B virus candidate vaccine expressing H3 hemagglutinin developed in suspension MDCK cells confers protection against lethal H3N2 avian influenza in BALB/c mice
Authors: Yushu Wu, Weiyang Sun, Yeting Xia, Yue Feng, Menglin Zhao, Tiecheng Wang, Xianzhu Xia, Fang Yan, Yuwei Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Optimized a range of culture conditions for a chimeric recombinant influenza virus rA/B-H3 in MDCK suspension cells.•The optimized vaccine demonstrated complete resistance to the lethal attack of wild-type H3N2 influenza virus.•Demonstrated the reliability of the MDCK suspension cell platform for the production of the chimeric H3N2 candidate vaccine.Keywords: MDCK suspension cell, Chimeric vaccine, Process optimization, BALB/c mice, Immunization

Introduction

Influenza viruses, belonging to the Orthomyxoviridae family, are extremely contagious RNA viruses that causes seasonal epidemics and have the potential to cause a pandemic (Janke, 2014; Uyeki et al., 2022). Globally, influenza viruses seriously impacts public health due to amino acid mutations (antigenic drift) and reassortment events (antigenic shift), especially in the viral surface proteins hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (Biswas et al., 2020). Vaccination is the best option for the prevention and control of influenza (Wu and Wilson, 2020). The antigenic drift during egg-induced vaccine production promoted immune escape of influenza viruses from vaccine-induced immunity and required regular vaccine updates (Ankomah et al., 2022; Gou et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2018; Kostolansky et al., 2020; Kostolansky et al., 2020). There are two types of influenza vaccines in the clinic: inactivated influenza vaccines and live-attenuated influenza vaccines. Vaccination with the live-attenuated influenza vaccine have triggered cellular, mucosal, and humoral immune responses, resulting in a long-term immune response and strong antigenic cross-protection (Jang et al., 2018; Holzer et al., 2019; Rodriguez et al., 2019; Subbarao, 2021). Compared to inactivated influenza vaccines, live-attenuated influenza vaccines have shown reduced virulence and high production efficiency and are more effective and affordable (Uyeki et al., 2022). To avoid the recombination issue between attenuated live vaccine strains and wild-type strains, we selected influenza B virus (IBV) as the vaccine vector. Although IBV belongs to the same genus, Influenza virus, within the family Orthomyxoviridae as influenza A virus (IAV), the probability of recombination between IBV and IAV observed in nature is extremely low. Compared to IAV, IBV has a smaller epidemic scale and lower mutation frequency, making it an excellent vaccine vector for reducing the risk of recombination in live attenuated influenza vaccines (LAIV).

Mammalian cell culture is routinely used in the biopharmaceutical industry to produce vaccines. Madin–Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells (Ganguly et al., 2020), human embryonic retinal cells (PER.C6) (Berdichevsky et al., 2008; Bockstal et al., 2018), monkey kidney cells (Vero) (Genzel et al., 2010; Song et al., 2013), and human embryonic kidney cells (HEK293) (Zamudio et al., 2021) are widely used to produce influenza vaccines. MDCK cells have shown excellent productivity and applicability as the main cell line used for producing influenza vaccines (Bissinger et al., 2019). Considering the relative complexity of culturing adherent MDCK cells, MDCK suspension cells, which can produce high virus yields in a short time, have been developed using a serum-free culture medium. As the most commonly used vessels for whole-suspension cultures of MDCK suspensions, bioreactors serve as the best containers for the production of influenza vaccines. During the production of influenza vaccines in MDCK cell cultures, using a high-volume agitated suspension culture reactor not only expands the yield of an influenza vaccine and improves the quality of an influenza vaccine but also adjusts the culture parameters according to the state of MDCK suspension, realizing the production of vaccines with low cost, high yield, high nutrient utilization, and low metabolic products (Biener et al., 2023; Jang et al., 2023; Tapia et al., 2016). For example, Novartis developed an MDCK suspension culture cell platform to produce a commercial influenza vaccine called Optaflu/Flucelvax using MDCK 33,016-PF suspension cells (Jang et al., 2023). SK Chemicals used MDCK-Sky3851 cells to produce SKYCellflu trivalent and tetravalent subunit vaccines, which were licensed for marketing in South Korea (Rockman et al., 2020). In 2012, Optaflu/Flucelvax, a trivalent subunit influenza vaccine produced in the MDCK 33,016 cell line using serum-free whole suspension, was approved by the FDA (Doroshenko and Halperin, 2009). The tetravalent influenza virus lysate vaccine from Shanghai Bioproducts Research Institute of China Biologicals was the first influenza vaccine based on serum-free whole suspension cell culture technology (MDCK cells) in China and obtained the Notification of Approval for Clinical Trial of Drugs (https://www.cde.org.cn/). Therefore, the preparation of influenza vaccines using MDCK suspension cells has great research value and application prospects.

In this study, a bioreactor and MDCK suspension cells were used as a platform for influenza vaccine production. Continuous passaging for the adaptive growth of chimeric influenza virus rA/B-H3 was performed in this system. During the process, the optimized culture conditions and process parameters for the high production of an influenza vaccine were identified. Further in vivo experiments have demonstrated that the vaccine produced by MDCK suspended cells has immune protective effect on BALB/c mice, and resist lethal attacks from viruses of the same subtype.

Materials and methods

Cell lines, viruses and mice

MDCK suspension cells (abbreviated as MDCKs) cultured in Celkey CD MDCK 244 medium (cat. no. 22,039–244.1, JS Biosciences Co., Ltd., Gansu, China) supplemented with 0.5 % penicillin–streptomycin in a humidified shaking atmosphere containing 5 % CO2 at 37 °C. The candidate vaccine was prepared as described previously (Hai et al., 2011). A reassortant influenza virus rA/B-H3 was rescued with the backbone from B/Yamagata/16/1988 chimerized with the ectodomain of A/Cambodia/e0826360/2020 (H3N2) hemagglutinin (HA) protein by using reverse genetics, and truncating the NS1 gene of influenza B virus, the recombinant influenza virus rA/B-H3 was successfully constructed. The virus was passaged on MDCK suspension cells (abbreviated as MDCKs) cultured in Celkey CD MDCK 199 medium (cat. no. 22,039–199.1, JS Biosciences Co., Ltd., Gansu, China) supplemented with 0.5 % penicillin–streptomycin (30–002-CI, CORNING, USA) using shake flasks or bioreactors in a humidified shaking atmosphere containing 5 % CO2 at 33 °C. The mouse-adapted strains A/baikal teal/Shanghai/SH-89/2013 (H3N2-S89) originated from the Institute of Changchun Veterinary Research (Yu et al., 2017). Specific pathogen-free (SPF) 6–8-week-old female BALB/c mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All virus infection experiments were performed in a biosafety level 2 laboratory (BSL-2).

Determination of growth conditions for rA/B-H3 in MDCK cells

The cells grow to the target cell density, rA/B-H3 was inoculated into an MDCKs cell culture diluted with CD MDCK 199 and cultured under different conditions to determine the time of harvest (TOH), the cell concentration of infection (CCI), the proportion of inoculation (POI) (Viral fluid:cell culture,V:C), and the L-1-tosylamide-2-phenylethyl chloromethyl ketone (TPCK) addition. The specific method is as follows, The MDCKs cell density was adjusted to 3.0 × 10^6 cells/mL, 4.0 × 10^6 cells/mL, and 5.0 × 10^6 cells/mL. After inoculating the viral vaccine solution, supernatants were collected every 12 h to measure hemagglutination titers. A specific amount of TPCK trypsin was added to determine the CCI. When diluting MDCKs cells with viral maintenance medium to achieve the determined CCI, the viral vaccine solution was inoculated at V:C ratios of 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, and 0.0001. A specific amount of TPCK trypsin was added, and supernatants were collected every 12 h to measure hemagglutination titers to determine the POI. When diluting MDCKs cells with viral maintenance medium to the determined CCI, rA/B-H3 was inoculated into the MDCKs culture at the determined POI. TPCK trypsin concentrations of 2 μg/mL, 3 μg/mL, and 5 μg/mL were added, and supernatants were collected every 12 h after cultivation initiation to measure hemagglutination titers to determine TPCK addition. Based on these experimental results, the TOH was simultaneously determined. All conditions were independently repeated in three separate experiments.

Identification of genetic stability and determination of titer after successive culturing

To evaluate the genetic stability of the recombinant viruses, viruses were passaged in MDCK cells 15 times, and supernatants were collected for RNA sequencing after each passage. Specifically, the virus stock saved by adherent MDCK cells was inoculated into MDCKs cells under optimized culture conditions. The viral supernatant harvested at 72 h post-infection (hpi) was used to infect new MDCKs cells under optimized conditions each time. The virus was cultured continuously for 15 generations according to this method and the HA titer of the harvested virus was monitored. The viral RNA was isolated using a Qiagen Viral RNA Extraction Kit (QIAGEN, Germany), and cDNA was obtained by reverse transcription. The cDNA was amplified with primers (Table S1) designed using Premier 5 software, and the PCR products were sequenced through full-length sequencing. The viral titers in supernatants were determined using specific pathogen-free embryonated chicken eggs and MDCK cells, and they were calculated using the Reed–Muench method.

Vaccination and virus challenge studies

The mouse study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Changchun Veterinary Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural. SciencesBALB/c mice (females, 6–8-week-old) were housed in microisolator units, and allowed free access to food and water (Fig. 4A). Mice in the vaccine groups were intranasally inoculated with rA/B-H3-P0 or rA/B-H3-P15 (50 % median tissue culture infectious dose [TCID50] was 105.5/50 μL) in weeks 0 and 3, respectively. Phosphate buffered saline(PBS)-infected animals served as controls. Blood samples were collected from mice cheeks using a needle at 20 and 41 d after vaccination. Serum samples were extracted by centrifuging at 4000 rpm for 10 min and stored at −20 °C. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid was collected from mice that had been euthanized by introduction and removal of 400 μL PBS into the lungs through intratracheal intubation at 41 d after vaccination. Then, 21 d after booster immunization, the mice were challenged with H3N2-S89, and their survival state and body weight were monitored for 14 d Mice with >25 % weight loss were humanely euthanized. At day 5 post-challenge, five mice were randomly selected from each group and euthanized. The nasal turbinates, tracheae, and lungs of mice were dissected for detecting virus titer. The left caudal lung lobes were preserved in 10 % phosphate-buffered formalin for histological analysis.

Hemagglutinin inhibition assay

Sera of immunized mice or unvaccinated control mice were treated with receptor destroying enzyme (cat. no. 340,122, Denka Seiken, Japan) overnight at 37 °C and heat-inactivated at 56 °C for 30 min. Serial 2-fold dilutions of mouse sera were incubated with the indicated viruses (4 HAU [hemagglutination unit] /well) for 30 min. The mixture was incubated with 1 % red blood cells from guinea pigs for 45 min at room temperature (RT), and hemagglutinin inhibition (HI) titers were recorded as the highest serum dilution at which no agglutination was observed.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay

Indirect enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed to determine the levels of antigen-specific IgG, IgG1, and IgG2a in the serum and IgA in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. The wells of an ELISA plate were coated with 100 μL of HA target protein solution (A/Cambodia/e0826360/2020) at 2 μg/mL overnight at 4 °C. The next morning, the plate was blocked with 1 % bovine serum albumin diluted in Phosphate buffered saline with tween 20 (PBST) at 37 °C for 2 h and incubated with serial 2-fold dilutions of sera at 37 °C for 90 min. The secondary antibodies were diluted in 1 % bovine serum albumin buffer (1:10,000) and 100 μL was added per well. The following secondary antibodies were used: goat anti-mouse IgG-Horseradish prroxidase (HRP) (cat. no. ab150113, Abcam), goat anti-mouse IgG1-HRP (cat. no. ab133045, Abcam), goat anti-mouse IgG2a-HRP (cat. no. ab97235, Abcam), and goat anti-mouse IgA-HRP (cat. no. 1030–05, SouthernBiotech). The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After each incubation, the cells were washed with PBS three times. Next, 100 μL of TMB color developing agent was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min in the dark. Then the TMB stop solution (50 μL/well) was added to each well, and optical density was read at 450 nm within 30 min.

Enzyme-linked immunospot (ELISPOT) assay

Single-cell suspensions of mice spleens were prepared using a 70-μm cell strainer aseptically and diluted to 2.5 × 10^6 cells/mL. After overnight stimulation, IFN-γ or IL-4 secreting T cells were analyzed using a mouse Enzyme-linked immunospot kit (Mabtech, Nacka Strand, Sweden), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The wells were incubated with RPMI 1640 medium at 37 °C for 35 min. Thereafter, 200 μL cell suspension was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C in 5 % CO2 for 36 h. A/Cambodia/e0826360/2020 HA protein was added as a specific antigen. Primary antibodies (1 µg/mL) and secondary antibodies (1:1000) were incubated for 2 and 1 h at 37 °C and 5 % CO2, respectively. Then BCIP-NBT substrate solution (cat. no. B1911, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to develop color. Color development was terminated when distinct spots emerged. The resultant spot forming cells were counted with an IRIS 2 reader (Mabtech, Sweden).

Lumenix assay

For the Lumenix assay, 2 mL of isolated mouse splenocyte suspension (5 × 10^6 cells) was added to a 6-well plate. A/Cambodia/e0826360/2020 HA was used as a stimulant at a final concentration of 10 μg/mL; nothing was added to the wells of the negative control group. The splenocytes were cultured at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 for 72 h. Then, the culture supernatant in the 6-well plates was collected for the mouse splenocyte Luminex assay using ProcartaPlex Mouse Th1/Th2 Cytokine Panel, 11plex (cat. no. EPX110–20,820-901, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

The 50% median tissue culture infective dose

MDCK cells seeded in 96-well plates were used to detect TCID50. The viral fluids from cell supernatants or tissue lysates were serially diluted 10-fold from 10−1 to 10−8 in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum, 1 % penicillin–streptomycin) and inoculated with cells at 37 °C. After 48 h, cell cytopathic changes were observed under a microscope, and positive wells (with > 50 % cytopathic effects) were recorded. Viral titer was calculated using the Reed–Muench method, and the result was expressed as log10 the 50 % embryo infective dose (TCID50).

The 50% embryo infective dose

The tracheae, nasal turbinates, and lungs from mice were dissected and placed in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium with 10 % penicillin–streptomycin. The tissues were grounded, and 10 % tissue homogenate was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. Ten-fold serial dilutions of tissue homogenates were made, and 100 μL of each dilution was inoculated in embryonated 8-D-old specific-pathogen-free chicken eggs. The virus was propagated for 72 h, harvested from the allantoic fluid, and titrated, as described. The 50 % embryo infective dose (EID50) was calculated using the Reed–Muench method.

Statistical analysis

Data are representative of results from three independent experiments. Standard deviation bars indicate variations across the experiments. GraphPad Prism 9.0 software was used for statistical analysis of data. Comparisons between two groups were performed using a T-test. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Results

The culture conditions of the recombinant vaccine strain rA/B-H3 were determined

The production conditions for the rA/B-H3 viral vaccine were established through cultivation in MDCKs cells (Additional file 1: Table S1). According to the cell culture manual, the optimal culture conditions for MDCKs cells were prioritized (supplementary materials). When the live cell density of MDCKs exceeds 9.0 × 10^6 cells/mL and the cell survival rate is above 92 %, the cells can be passaged. Specifically, when the passage density reaches 1.5 × 10^6 cells/mL, the cell cultures meet the requirements for passage after 48–72 h.

MDCKs cells were grown to 9.0 × 10^6 cells/mL and diluted with viral maintenance fluid to different densities. Specifically, at any time after infection, the HA titers of rA/B-H3 measured at CCI of 4.0 × 10^6 cells/mL were consistently higher than those in the other two groups, peaking at 6log2 (HAU/50 μL) at 72 hpi (Fig. 1A). The HA titer measured at 24–48 hpi when CCI was 5.0 × 10^6 cells/mL was always lower than that of the other two groups, but HA titer of the group was higher than that of CCI 3.0 × 10^6 cells/mL group after 48 hpi. We measured four conditions for POI (V:C), replication of the rA/B-H3 at POI = 0.0001 was always unsatisfactory (Fig. 1B). Compared with POI = 0.1 and POI = 0.01 groups, the rA/B-H3 inoculated at POI = 0.001 showed higher HA titer at 24 hpi and reached peak at 72 hpi (6log2 HAU/50 μL). Based on HA titration results, when TPCK trypsin was added at 2 μg/mL during infection, HA titers began to rise continuously from 24 hpi onward, peaked at 72 hpi, and remained elevated through 96 hpi (Fig. 1C). Consistent with previous studies, adding TPCK trypsin during viral infection significantly enhances viral-hospital cell binding, thereby accelerating the infection process.

Next, rA/B-H3 was further cultured in a 5 L stirred-tank bioreactor under (33 ± 0.1) °C conditions. The DO parameters were determined by inputting the pH values measured from the culture medium. As shown in Fig. 1D, viral HA titer consistently remained higher in reactors operating at 50 % DO than those running at 30 % or 70 % DO. The peak HA titer reached 8log2 (HAU/50 μL) at 72 hpi, with the lowest HA titer at 2log2 (HAU/50 μL) when DO was set to 70 % (Fig. 1D). Under identical other conditions, 50 % DO was selected as the baseline for subsequent reactor cultivation. Optimal parameters were identified by adjusting the pH value within a 0.1 range around 7.2 (Fig. 1E). Notably, maintaining pH at 7.2 ± 0.1 ensured sustained high viral HA titer. During the 48–96 hpi period, viral HA titer remained higher when pH was set at 7.0 ± 0.1 compared to groups with pH values of 6.8 and 7.4.

When MDCKs cells reached a certain density, fresh viral maintenance medium was supplemented to maintain the optimized viral culture conditions. The nutrient content in the medium was monitored every 24 h (Fig. 1F). Results showed that the initial glucose concentration was 16.67 mmol/L and gradually decreased during viral infection, dropping to 0 mmol/LmM by 72 hpi Lactate levels initially declined between 0–24 h post-infection, increased from 24–48 hpi, then began depleting after 48 hpi, reaching 1.335 mmol/L at 120 hpi. This decrease was likely due to viral inoculation occurring on the second day of the cell cycle, when lactate production continued, causing a minor spike in early infection. Glutamate levels progressively decreased from 1.06 mmol/L to 0.04 mmol/LmM post-infection. Notably, the metabolic pathways underwent significant changes post-infection, as evidenced by the altered rates of glucose, lactate, and glutamate fluctuations in the medium compared to pre-infection conditions (Additional file 1: Fig S1, Fig. 1D). These findings suggest that real-time regulation of medium components through flow processing technology could enhance viral replication efficiency in future viral cultures.

Continuous subculture of the recombinant vaccine strain rA/B-H3 in MDCK cells

To investigate the genetic stability of the viral vaccine strain, researchers conducted serial subcultures of rA/B-H3 virus in MDCKs cells while continuously measuring viral HA titers (Fig. 2A). The HA titer of rA/B-H3 produced by MDCKs cells showed a stable trend, but a brief increase in HA titer was observed at the 9th generation, reaching 8log2 (HAU/50 μL), and then HA titer remained at about 7log2 (HAU/50 μL). By monitoring the EID50 and TCID50 values of P0, P5, P10, and P15-generation seed viruses during subculture (Fig. 2B), we found that the virus failed to grow in chicken embryos. This aligns with previous findings: when rescuing the viral vaccine strain, the viral transfection solution could not replicate in SPF chicken embryos. When POI = 0.001 (i.e., 30 μL viral solution per 30 mL MDCKs cells), the TCID50 titer gradually increased to 107.50/mL levels. To detect genetic mutations in rA/B-H3, researchers performed PCR sequencing on eight fragments from P0 and P15-generation viruses. Results showed no differences in PCR product band sizes for polymerase basic protein 2 (PB2), polymerase basic protein 1 (PB1), acidic polymerase protein (PA), hemagglutinin (HA), neuraminidase (NA), nucleoprotein (NP), matrix protein (M), and non-structural protein (NS) (Fig. 2C). Notably, sequencing revealed an adaptive amino acid mutation at position 181 of the HA fragment in rA/B-H3, where Asn was replaced by Lys, while no amino acid changes were observed in the other seven gene fragments.

Strong humoral immune response was induced in BALB/c mice with rA/B-H3 vaccine strain

To determine if the immunogenicity of rA/B-H3 was affected by serial passage in MDCKs, we evaluated the immunoreactions of BALB/c mice vaccinated with rA/B-H3-P0 and rA/B-H3-P15. First, we detected humoral immune responses in BALB/c mice. The HI test results showed that both rA/B-H3-P0 and rA/B-H3-P15 could generate high-titer HI antibodies in serum. In the immunized group of rA/B-H3-P15, the HI antibody titers reached 1:2560 and 1:5120 after two immunizations (Fig. 3A). However, the HI titers against the challenge strain H3N2-S89 were not detected(data not shown). Similarly, both rA/B-H3-P0 and rA/B-H3-P15 produced high-titer MN antibodies in serum, with rA/B-H3-P15 consistently showing higher MN antibody levels than rA/B-H3-P0, reaching 1:5120 and 1:20,480 after two immunizations (Fig. 3B). Following booster immunization, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BAL) secretions from mice vaccinated with rA/B-H3-P0 and rA/B-H3-P15 achieved IgA levels of 1:1000, significantly higher than the control group (Fig. 3F). However, rA/B-H3-P0 induces higher levels of IgA antibodies. Notably, Both rA/B-H3-P0 and rA/B-H3-P15 vaccines induced higher concentrations of IgG (Fig. 3C), IgG2a (Fig. 3D), and IgG1 (Fig. 3E) antibodies after booster immunization. The former produced antibody levels of 1:25,600,1:25,600, and 1:51,200 respectively, while the latter generated 1:64,000,1:32,000, and 1:32,000 levels. Notably, the antibody levels induced by rA/B-H3-P15 were significantly lower than those from the rA/B-H3-P0 candidate vaccine. Overall, the rA/B-H3 vaccine administration effectively stimulated a robust humoral immune response in mice.

Strong cellular immune response was induced in BALB/c mice with rA/B-H3 vaccine strain

The spleen of mice was extracted and ground to separate individual spleen cells, rA/B-H3-P0 immunized mice had significantly higher levels of secretory IL-4 (Fig. 4A) and IFN-γ (Fig. 4B) detected in the splenocytes than mock mice. And rA/B-H3-P0 immunized mice were essentially comparable to the IFN-γ levels secreted in splenic cells of rA/B-H3-P15 immunized mice. Next, we detected a wide range of Th1 cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-2, as well as Th2 cytokines, such as IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13 (Fig. 4C—4H). These cytokines were significantly induced by rA/B-H3-P0. However, some differences in the induction of cytokine secretion were observed with rA/B-H3-P15. This may be due to the production of defective interfering particles (DI) during continuous subculture of cells in suspension. During co-infection, DI RNA interfered with normal genome replication, resulting in relatively low cytokine secretion induced by rA/B-H3-P15, but still higher than the control group.

The rA/B-H3 candidate vaccine strain protected BALB/c mice from the lethal challenge of H3N2-S89

To assess the protective efficacy of rA/B-H3-P0 and rA/B-H3-P15, the H3N2-S89 lethal infection model in mice was used. In the mock group, the body weight of all of the mice decreased by > 25 % until 7 d post-infection. However, in the rA/B-H3-P0 and rA/B-H3-P15 immunized groups, the body weight of mice decreased to 93 % and 89 % at 5 d post-infection, respectively, and then gradually recovered from 6 d post-infection (Fig. 5B), providing a 100 % survival rate (Fig. 5C). Then, we tested the viral titers in respiratory tract organs, including the nasal turbinate, trachea, and lung (Fig. 5D). The results showed high replication of the H3N2-S89 virus in the nasal turbinates, tracheae, and lungs of BALB/c mice in the mock group, which was remarkably inhibited by vaccination with both rA/B-H3-P0 and rA/B-H3-P15. Vaccination with rA/B-H3-P0 and rA/B-H3-P15 significantly mitigated the severe lung pathology mediated by H3N2-S89 (Fig. 5E). Hematoxylin and eosin staining showed granulocyte infiltration in the alveolar wall (black arrow), occasional necrosis of epithelial cells in the alveolar wall (purple arrows), several necrotic bronchiolar epithelial cells (blue arrowheads), and loss of local mucosal structure in the lung tissues of mice in the mock group. By contrast, the lungs of mice immunized with rA/B-H3-P15 and rA/B-H3-P0 occasionally showed intra-alveolar macrophages (orange arrowheads) and lymphocyte infiltration (red arrowheads) in the fine bronchioles. Collectively, these results demonstrated that vaccinating with rA/B-H3-P0 and rA/B-H3-P15 not only effectively decreased viral load in mice tissues but also significantly attenuated the pathological damage caused by the lethal infection of H3N2-S89.

Discussion

Mutations in influenza viruses (antigenic drift and antigenic transformation) often trigger global influenza pandemics (Ankomah et al., 2022; Kostolansky et al., 2020), and the influenza vaccination protection rate is only 40 %–60 %, thus reducing vaccine efficacy (Gou et al., 2020). As a cell line suitable for culturing influenza virus, MDCK suspension cells are widely used for producing industrial influenza vaccines. Because the growth state of MDCK suspension cells and the culture conditions during virus infection can impact virus replication, we optimized the growth conditions of rA/B-H3 during shake flask culture. The findings of the study suggest that when the POI is too high, the cells may undergo apoptosis prematurely, which is unfavorable for virus production. However, when the POI is too low, the cells remain in the proliferation mode at the early stage of virus pick-up, which affects viral replication. Virus production can be increased by manipulating the addition of TPCK trypsin, because MDCK cells do not contain trypsin, whereas HA on the influenza virus envelope requires trypsin to hydrolyze the disulfide bond between HA1 and HA2 when recognizing and binding to the salivary acid receptor on the surface of the host cell. Therefore, the production of influenza viruses using a serum-free suspension cell culture platform requires the addition of TPCK trypsin to activate influenza virus HA (Youil et al., 2004).

Because the bioreactors can be adjusted in real time for growth parameters, we optimized our analysis of viral growth in 5-L bioreactors. The process parameters DO and pH were optimized, and 50 % DO and pH 7.2 ± 0.05 were identified as the process parameters for following high-volume production of high-titer vaccines. rA/B-H3 reached peak HA potency of up to 7log2 (HAU/50 μL), and the TCID50 titer increased after 15 passaging in MDCK suspension cells. Unlike vaccines produced from chicken embryos, the rA/B-H3 strain used in this study was reverse-engineered from MDCK adherent cells. When the viral vaccine concentrate rA/B-H3-P0 was directly inoculated into MDCK cells, high HA titers were immediately detectable. The viral titer peaked during continuous passage through the 15th phase. Notably, a decrease in viral titer was observed during this process. To investigate the mechanism behind this change in recombinant influenza viruses, we performed Sanger sequencing on PB2, PB1, PA, HA, NA, NP, M, and NS gene fragments from rA/B-H3 strains at P0, P5, P10, and P15 stages. Sequencing results revealed a single amino acid mutation in the HA fragment starting from rA/B-H3-P5, indicating that chimeric influenza viruses undergo adaptive site mutations during adaptation to MDCK suspension cell growth. Therefore, it is speculated that the amino acid substitution N181K (AAC-AAA) at the beginning of HA protein of the 5th virus vaccine strain led to a downward trend in the titer of the virus vaccine strain. In the continuous culture from the 5th to the 15th, the virus vaccine strain with a single amino acid mutation gradually adapted to MDCK suspension cells and showed high viral titer by the 15th, further scientific verification is needed.

To evaluate whether the mutations occurring during passaging affect viral immunogenicity, this study further examined the efficacy of the vaccine candidate strains before and after passaging.The rA/B-H3-P0 and rA/B-H3-P15 groups produced comparable levels of hemagglutination-inhibiting antibodies after initial immunization, and the mouse organs could produce some secretory immunoglobulin A after booster immunization. Analysis of experimental immunological data indicated that no significant changes in immune responses were observed between the two groups. Interestingly, the rA/B-H3-P15-immunized group did not induce as high a level of antibodies as the rA/B-H3-P0-immunized group. This discrepancy may be attributed to the site-adapted mutation of the HA fragment of rA/B-H3 produced during transmission, leading to a change in antigenic conformation, which affected mouse serum antibodies and A/Cambodia/e0826360/2020 HA binding. Another explanation is that defective interfering particles are produced during large-scale adaptive production of viral vaccine strains, which have been shown to exist in many viral cell systems and interfere with the replication of intact viruses. Therefore, this study has limitations. In future research, we will investigate the impact of mutations on antigen conformation and antigen-antibody binding to further evaluate whether this amino acid mutation alters vaccine antigenicity.

Evaluation of attack protection after the infection of mice with homosubtypic influenza virus H3N2-S89 showed that immunization with rA/B-H3-P0 and rA/B-H3-P15 protected mice against lethal attack by homosubtypic influenza virus. IFN-γ and IL-4 were detected in the splenocytes of all of the immunized mice, and the splenic lymphocytes of the mice secreted some cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-13, IL-5, IL-4, and IL-2. It is worth noting that the level of cytokines and mucosal immunity induced by rA/B-H3-P0 were significantly higher than that of rA/B-H3-P15, indicating that rA/B-H3-P15 was more likely to exert immune function through humoral immunity, the mutation did not affect the final antiviral effect. Using the same methodology, our research team constructed a cIBV by chimerizing the HA extracellular domain of B/Yamagata/16/1988 influenza virus with B/Washington/02/2019. After 15 passages in MDCKs cells, no amino acid mutations occurred in the cIBV genome. This finding indirectly demonstrates the safety of influenza virus vaccines produced through MDCKs cell culture.

In this study, the process parameters of influenza vaccine production by MDCK suspension cells were optimized to provide reference data for the large-scale production of influenza vaccine, to evaluate the immunogenicity and attack protection properties of the vaccine strains as well as to validate the controllability and reliability of the rA/B-H3 vaccine candidate strain production by using the MDCK suspension cell platform. These findings will provide a new option for the large-scale production of influenza vaccines.
