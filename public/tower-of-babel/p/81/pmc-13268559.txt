Poor carbohydrate quality is associated with impaired lung function: A population-based cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Kaida Hou, Qing Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diet quality, particularly carbohydrate composition, may influence lung function; however, population-based evidence remains limited. This study aimed to examine the association between carbohydrate quality, assessed by the carbohydrate-to-fiber (CH:Fiber) ratio, and pulmonary function in U.S. adults. In fully adjusted models, each unit increase in CH:Fiber ratio was associated with lower forced vital capacity (FVC) (β = −0.03 L, 95% CI: −0.04 to − 0.02, P <.001) and FEV1 (β = −0.02 L, 95% CI: −0.03 to − 0.01, P <.001). Associations with peak expiratory flow and FEF25% to 75% were weaker but directionally consistent. Stronger inverse associations were observed among older adults, current smokers, and individuals with chronic bronchitis. Poor carbohydrate quality, indicated by a higher CH:Fiber ratio, is independently associated with lower lung function in U.S. adults. Diets emphasizing fiber-rich carbohydrate sources may support respiratory health. Future prospective studies and dietary intervention trials are needed to clarify causal relationships and elucidate the biological mechanisms linking carbohydrate quality to pulmonary function.We analyzed data from 11,414 adults aged ≥ 18 years from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey 2007 to 2012 with valid dietary and spirometry data. Dietary intake was assessed using 24-hour recalls, and CH:Fiber ratio was calculated as total carbohydrate (g/day) divided by total dietary fiber (g/day). Lung function outcomes included FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC ratio, peak expiratory flow, and FEF25% to 75%. Multivariable linear regression models were applied, with subgroup and interaction analyses performed.

1. Introduction

Impaired lung function, which may manifest as obstructive, restrictive, or mixed in nature, is a major public health concern and a key predictor of respiratory morbidity, cardiovascular disease, and all-cause mortality. Chronic respiratory conditions, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma, contribute substantially to global disease burden and healthcare costs.[1] Although smoking and environmental exposures are well-established determinants of lung health, growing attention has been directed toward modifiable lifestyle factors, particularly diet, as potential contributors to respiratory function across the lifespan.[2] Understanding how dietary patterns influence lung function may offer new opportunities for prevention and risk reduction at the population level.

Accumulating evidence suggests that diet also influences respiratory health. Notably, high dietary fiber intake has been linked to better lung function and lower COPD prevalence.[3] These results imply that fiber-rich foods (e.g. fruits, vegetables, whole grains) may protect lung function.[4] Conversely, diets high in refined carbohydrates and sugars are thought to promote inflammation and disease.[5] For example, foods with high inflammatory index scores have been associated with an increased risk of early COPD.[6] Similarly, poor overall diet quality (e.g. low Alternative Healthy Eating Index) has been linked to poorer spirometry measures.[7] Recent large-scale population data further underscore the global relevance of poor-quality carbohydrate consumption. For example, findings from the Indian Council of Medical Research-INDIAB study (2025) reported that a substantial proportion of daily caloric intake in Indian adults is derived from refined and low-fiber carbohydrate sources, which were associated with adverse metabolic risk profiles.[8] These findings highlight the widespread nature of suboptimal carbohydrate quality and support the need to examine its potential impact beyond metabolic health, including respiratory outcomes.

Despite this growing body of evidence, most prior studies have focused on individual nutrients (e.g., fiber alone) or overall diet quality indices, without explicitly addressing carbohydrate quality.[9] Total carbohydrate intake encompasses a wide range of foods with markedly different physiological effects, from fiber-rich whole grains and legumes to refined grains and sugars.[10] Recently, the carbohydrate-to-fiber (CH:Fiber) ratio has been proposed as a simple and meaningful indicator of carbohydrate quality, with higher values reflecting diets dominated by refined, low-fiber carbohydrates.[11] This ratio has been linked to cardiometabolic outcomes.[12,13] The CH:Fiber ratio provides a simple proxy for carbohydrate quality by capturing the balance between refined carbohydrate intake and fiber consumption. Lower ratios generally reflect diets richer in whole grains, legumes, fruits, and vegetables, whereas higher ratios indicate diets dominated by refined grains and low-fiber foods.[14] Prior nutritional epidemiology studies have linked higher CH:Fiber ratios to adverse cardiometabolic outcomes,[15] metabolic syndrome,[16] and mortality risk.[17] Despite its growing application in cardiometabolic research, its relationship with lung function has not been well characterized.

Using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2007 to 2012, this study aimed to investigate the association between dietary carbohydrate quality, assessed by the CH:Fiber ratio, and multiple measures of lung function among U.S. adults. We further explored whether these associations varied across key population subgroups. The novelty of this study lies in its focus on carbohydrate quality rather than quantity, its use of a large, nationally representative dataset, and its comprehensive evaluation of lung function outcomes. Our findings provide new evidence that poor carbohydrate quality is independently associated with impaired lung function, underscoring the potential importance of dietary composition in respiratory health.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and population

We used data from the NHANES 2007 to 2012, a continuous, cross-sectional, nationally representative survey of the civilian, noninstitutionalized U.S. population conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).[18] Participants aged ≥18 years who completed both dietary assessment and valid spirometry testing were eligible. We excluded participants with missing data on key exposures, outcomes, or covariates. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria and sampling-weight adjustments, the analytic sample comprised 11,414 adults (Fig. 1).

Flow chart of the study population.

NHANES protocols were approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent). This secondary analysis of de-identified public data was considered exempt from additional institutional review.

2.2. Dietary assessment and carbohydrate-to-fiber ratio

Dietary intake was assessed using NHANES 24-hour dietary recall interviews. For participants with multiple recalls, we used the mean intake across available recalls to better represent habitual intake. Total daily carbohydrate (grams/day) and total dietary fiber (grams/day) were derived from the NHANES dietary files. Carbohydrate quality was operationalized as the carbohydrate-to-fiber ratio (CH:fiber), calculated for each participant as total carbohydrate (g/day) divided by total fiber (g/day). Higher values indicate proportionally greater carbohydrate intake relative to fiber and therefore lower carbohydrate quality. Because no universally accepted cut-points exist, “higher” values reflect relative comparisons within the study population, with upper quartiles representing diets more heavily dominated by refined, low-fiber carbohydrate sources.[11] The CH:Fiber ratio was modeled both as a continuous variable and as quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on the study sample distribution.

2.3. Lung function assessment

Spirometric measurements were obtained in NHANES following standardized protocols conducted by trained technicians. The primary spirometric outcomes extracted for analysis were forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1, L), forced vital capacity (FVC, L), FEV1/FVC ratio (%), peak expiratory flow (PEF, L/s), and forced expiratory flow between 25% and 75% of FVC (FEF25%–75%, L/s). Raw volumes and flows were recorded in liters and liters per second respectively. The FEV1/FVC ratio was calculated as (FEV1/ FVC) × 100. We included only tests that met NHANES quality control criteria. Participants were excluded from spirometry if they had medical or safety contraindications to forced expiratory maneuvers, such as recent cardiovascular events, major surgery, pregnancy, or active respiratory infection. Participants were also excluded if they were unable to perform spirometry adequately or if their tests failed to meet American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) acceptability and repeatability criteria, including suboptimal effort, insufficient acceptable maneuvers, or invalid spirometric values. Continuous spirometry measures were used as outcome variables in regression models.

2.4. Covariates

Potential confounders were selected a priori based on prior literature and directed acyclic graph considerations. Demographic variables included age (years), sex (male/female), and race/ethnicity (Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Other). Socioeconomic status was represented by poverty-income ratio (PIR). PIR was categorized as <1.3 (low income), 1.3 to 3.5 (middle income), and >3.5 (high income), consistent with U.S. federal assistance eligibility thresholds and prior NHANES analyses. Smoking status was classified as current smoker (self-reported current smoking or serum cotinine ≥10 ng/mL) and nonsmoker (never smoker and cotinine <10 ng/mL). Combining self-reported smoking status with serum cotinine levels improves classification accuracy by capturing both reported and unreported tobacco exposure and reduces potential misclassification bias. Alcohol consumption was dichotomized as yes or no based on self-reported alcohol intake during the past year. Physical activity level was categorized as low, moderate, or high based on self-reported NHANES physical activity questionnaires and calculated metabolic equivalent (MET) minutes per week. Participants were classified as having low physical activity if they accumulated <600 MET-min/week, moderate physical activity if they accumulated 600–2999 MET-min/week, and high physical activity if they accumulated ≥3000 MET-min/week, consistent with established NHANES and World Health Organization physical activity classification guidelines. Clinical covariates included body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, asthma, chronic bronchitis, coronary heart disease, congestive heart failure, stroke, cancer (self-reported physician diagnosis or medication use where applicable), and fasting laboratory measures (HDL, LDL, triglycerides, total cholesterol).

2.5. Statistical analysis

All analyses accounted for the complex, multistage probability sampling design of NHANES by incorporating sample weights, strata, and primary sampling units per NHANES analytic guidance for combining survey cycles (2007–2012). Combined 6-year sample weights were applied to produce nationally representative estimates. Descriptive statistics for participant characteristics across CH:Fiber quartiles were computed using weighted means (±SD) for continuous variables and weighted proportions for categorical variables; between-group differences were evaluated with survey-weighted linear regression or Rao-Scott chi-square tests as appropriate. Associations between CH:Fiber (continuous and quartiles) and lung function measures were examined using survey-weighted multivariable linear regression. We fitted 3 hierarchical models: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, and race/ethnicity), and Model 3 (fully adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, PIR, smoking status, alcohol intake, physical activity, BMI, HDL, LDL, triglycerides, total cholesterol, serum cotinine, and clinical comorbidities listed above). Tests for linear trend across quartiles were performed by modeling quartile ordinal rank as a continuous variable. Effect estimates are presented as β coefficients with 95% confidence intervals. Two-sided P <.05 was considered statistically significant.

We conducted stratified analyses to evaluate potential effect modification by age group (<40, 40–59, ≥60 years), sex, race/ethnicity, BMI category (<25, 25–29.9, ≥30 kg/m2), PIR, smoking status, alcohol intake, physical activity level, hypertension, diabetes, asthma, and chronic bronchitis. Statistical interaction was assessed by adding multiplicative interaction terms between CH:Fiber (continuous) and the stratifying variable to the fully adjusted model; P for interaction <0.10 was considered suggestive of heterogeneity. Participants with missing covariate data were excluded from multivariable models. Where appropriate, multiple imputation was performed as a robustness check. Analyses were performed using R (version 4.x) with the survey package.

3. Results

3.1. Participant characteristics

A total of 11,414 adults from NHANES 2007 to 2012 were included in the analysis. The mean age of participants was 46.5 ± 16.1 years, and 50.2% were female. Participant characteristics across quartiles of the carbohydrate-to-fiber (CH:Fiber) ratio are shown in Table 1. Individuals in the highest quartile (Q4) of CH:Fiber ratio were older, more likely to be female, had higher BMI and waist circumference, and exhibited higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, asthma, and chronic bronchitis compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q1) (P-values ranging from .009 to <.001). Significant differences were also observed across quartiles for sex, race/ethnicity, PIR, and alcohol consumption (P ≤.032). Higher CH:Fiber ratios corresponded with lower average lung function measures, as indicated by progressively lower FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC across quartiles.

3.3. Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses of CH:Fiber ratio and FVC (Table 3) showed generally consistent inverse associations across demographic, lifestyle, and clinical subgroups. Stronger associations were seen in participants aged ≥60 years, current smokers, and those with chronic bronchitis, while weaker associations were observed in younger adults, nonsmokers, and participants without chronic conditions. Significant interactions were found for sex, physical activity, smoking status, PIR, hypertension, diabetes, and chronic bronchitis, indicating potential variation in the association across these subgroups.

4. Discussion

In this large, nationally representative sample, higher dietary carbohydrate-to-fiber ratio was consistently associated with worse pulmonary function. After adjusting for demographics, socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors and health conditions, individuals in the highest CH:Fiber quartile had significantly lower FVC and FEV1 compared to those in the lowest quartile. Each unit increase in CH:Fiber ratio was associated with incremental declines in FVC and FEV1 in the fully adjusted models. A similar inverse trend was seen for PEF and mid-expiratory flow (FEF(25–75)), though these were attenuated after full adjustment. Importantly, the association held even after controlling for smoking, BMI, diabetes, and other comorbidities, suggesting an independent effect of carbohydrate quality on lung function. Stratified analyses indicated that the deleterious effect of a high CH:Fiber ratio was more pronounced in older adults, men, and current/former smokers, although interaction tests did not reach significance.

Our results are consistent with a growing body of literature indicating that carbohydrate composition affects respiratory health. Multiple cross-sectional and cohort studies have highlighted protective role of dietary fiber on the lungs. For instance, Hanson et al (NHANES) concluded that fiber intake was positively associated with lung function.[19] Similarly, Jin et al reported that higher fiber intake significantly decreased COPD prevalence and correlated with better FEV1 levels in NHANES adults.[20] In prospective cohorts, high-fiber consumers had substantially lower COPD incidence. The Nurses’ Health Study and Health Professionals Follow-up Study found about 33% lower COPD risk in the top fiber quintile,[21] and a Swedish cohort reported approximately 38% lower risk with high-fiber intake.[22] These epidemiologic findings align with our observation that a diet rich in fiber (low CH:Fiber) is associated with better lung function, whereas diets dominated by refined carbohydrates confer harm. Conversely, diets high in simple sugars and refined grains appear detrimental to respiratory outcomes. A recent meta-analysis by Bao et al showed that free-sugar intake raised asthma risk while fiber and whole-grain intake lowered it,[23] emphasizing that high-quality carbohydrates (low in added sugar, high in fiber) are important for lung health. In another NHANES analysis, Xu et al found that total carbohydrate and fiber intakes were positively associated with FVC and FEV1, but higher 2-hour post-load glucose (indicative of poor glycemic control) predicted lower lung function.[9] This suggests that adequate carbohydrate intake can be neutral or beneficial if paired with good glucose metabolism and ample fiber, whereas dysregulated carbohydrate metabolism is harmful.

Several biological pathways may explain how poor carbohydrate quality impairs lung function. High glycemic diets (e.g. refined starches, sugars) induce systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, which can damage lung tissue.[24] Excess fructose intake raises uric acid and advanced glycation end-products (AGEs). These trigger inflammatory cascades (via RAGE receptors), dysregulate the renin–angiotensin system, activate aldose reductase, and stimulate the mTORC1 pathway in pulmonary cells.[25] The net effect is enhanced oxidative injury and chronic inflammation in the airways.[26] Animal and cellular models show that fructose-mediated AGEs and uric acid promote endothelial dysfunction and airway constriction, consistent with lung damage.[25] Similarly, hyperglycemia and insulin resistance can lead to chronic low-grade inflammation and impaired microvascular blood flow in the lungs, contributing to declines in FEV1/FVC.[27]

In contrast, dietary fiber exerts anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects. Fiber is fermented by gut microbiota into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (e.g. acetate, propionate, and butyrate) which enter the circulation and influence distant organs, including the lungs. Emerging evidence highlights a “gut–lung axis” whereby high-fiber diets can reshape pulmonary immune function. For example, Trompette et al showed that mice on a high-fiber diet had altered gut microbiota and elevated SCFAs, which attenuated allergic airway inflammation.[28] In our stratified analysis, the stronger association of CH:Fiber ratio with lung function among older, male, and current smokers may reflect higher baseline inflammation in these groups, which fiber may help mitigate. An additional consideration is the potential for reverse causality. Individuals with established chronic respiratory conditions, such as chronic bronchitis, may modify their dietary behaviors due to disease status, symptoms, or medical advice. For example, reduced appetite, altered food preferences, or increased health awareness could influence carbohydrate and fiber intake. Therefore, the observed stronger associations in individuals with chronic bronchitis should be interpreted cautiously, as dietary patterns may partly reflect disease-related changes rather than causal effects. Indeed, Jin et al found that part of the fiber–COPD association was mediated by white blood cell count,[20] indicating that immune cell-related inflammation links diet to lung disease. Taken together, diets high in refined carbohydrates likely aggravate pulmonary oxidative stress and inflammation, whereas fiber-rich diets may protect the lungs via anti-inflammatory metabolites (SCFAs) and improved glucose regulation.

This study has several strengths. NHANES provides a large, nationally representative sample of US adults with standardized spirometry and detailed dietary data, enhancing the external validity of our findings. We adjusted for a wide range of potential confounders (age, sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, smoking, socioeconomic status, comorbidities, etc), reducing bias. By focusing on the carbohydrate-to-fiber ratio, we addressed carbohydrate quality in a comprehensive way that goes beyond single nutrients. Our results are consistent across multiple lung function measures (FVC, FEV1) and persist under rigorous adjustment, lending robustness. However, important limitations exist. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences. Dietary intake was assessed using 24-hour dietary recalls, which may not fully capture habitual intake due to day-to-day variation. Although the mean of available recalls was used when multiple recalls were available, within-person variability and measurement error may still have resulted in exposure misclassification. Such misclassification is likely non-differential and may have attenuated observed associations. Information on passive smoke exposure and occupational exposure to dust, fumes, or airborne pollutants was not consistently available and could not be fully accounted for. Residual confounding from environmental or occupational respiratory exposures may therefore remain. The CH:Fiber ratio is a crude metric of quality; therefore, we did not directly measure glycemic index/load or types of carbohydrates (e.g. whole vs refined grains). Notably, subjects in the highest CH:Fiber quartile were younger but had lower income and higher BMI, suggesting they may have other unhealthy behaviors or limited food choices. While we controlled for many variables, unmeasured factors (e.g. physical environment, unrecorded dietary habits) could confound the associations. Finally, although NHANES is multiethnic, our findings may not fully generalize to populations with very different diets or genetic backgrounds (e.g. children, non-US groups).

5. Conclusion

Our analysis indicates that poor carbohydrate quality is associated with reduced lung function in US adults. This relationship was independent of other risk factors and showed a dose-response pattern. These findings suggest that dietary patterns emphasizing fiber-rich, low-GI carbohydrate sources (e.g. whole grains, vegetables, legumes) may support better respiratory health, whereas diets high in refined grains and sugars could contribute to pulmonary impairment. Future prospective studies and interventional trials are warranted to determine whether improving carbohydrate quality can help preserve lung function and reduce respiratory disease risk in the population.
