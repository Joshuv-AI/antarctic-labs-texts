Deep learning–empowered triboelectric acoustic textile for voice perception and intuitive generative AI-voice access on clothing
Authors: Beibei Shao, Tai-Chen Wu, Zhi-Xian Yan, Tien-Yu Ko, Wei-Chen Peng, Dun-Jie Jhan, Yu-Hsiang Chang, Jiun-Wei Fong, Ming-Han Lu, Wei-Chun Yang, Jiann-Yeu Chen, Ming-Yen Lu, Baoquan Sun, Heng-Jui Liu, Ruiyuan Liu, Ying-Chih Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Integrating generative artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots with acoustic perception textiles allows everyday clothing to retrieve information, seek advice, and perform tasks through voice interactions. Here, we present a deep learning (DL)–empowered triboelectric AI acoustic textile (A-Textile) leveraging electrostatic charges on clothing for imperceptible, active voice perception and AI access. The multilayered A-Textile features a composite coating of three-dimensional SnS2 nanoflowers (NFs) embedded in silicone rubber to enhance charge capture and transfer, along with a SnS2 NFs–decorated graphite-like carbonized textile for charge accumulation and preservation. This design maximizes the charge density on the textile, achieving a 21-volt output, 1.2 volts per pascal sensitivity, 1-hertz resolution, and a wide sound response frequency range of 80 to 900 hertz. Using a well-trained DL model, the A-Textile precisely classifies and visualizes voice commands for internet-of-things control and cloud information access. Furthermore, we demonstrate its integration with ChatGPT, providing an intuitive interface for engaging with generative AI services to perform sophisticated tasks.

INTRODUCTION

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) models have recently demonstrated remarkable achievements, unlocking problem-solving capabilities across various fields (1). The emergence of generative AI chatbots, such as ChatGPT, allows easy access to powerful AI services through simple queries. Although voice interactions with AI systems offer intuitive engagement with this technology, conventional acoustic devices are often rigid and bulky, hindering their integration into daily use (2–5). In contrast, textiles offer various desirable advantages, including softness, intimacy, and comfort (6–10). Seamlessly integrating acoustic perception into textiles with generative AI chatbots could therefore empower daily clothing to recognize and process voice commands, allowing users to access AI services more intuitively during their everyday routines.

In the past few decades, piezoelectric technology has been developed for application in fabric acoustic sensing, demonstrating a self-powering capability, fast response, and flexibility (11–13). In this context, a cylindrical crystalline P(VDF-TrFE) [poly(vinylidene fluoride-trifluoroethylene) copolymer] piezoelectric fiber was reported for acoustic sensing (11). However, this fiber responds only to ultrasonic waves within the kilohertz-to-megahertz range and requires immersion in an impedance-matched water tank for operation. An improved P(VDF-TrFE)/barium titanate (BaTiO3) composite piezoelectric fiber was developed to detect the human voice in an air environment, while the voice-induced nanoscale vibrations resulted in extremely low output signals (microvolts to millivolts), a limited sensitivity (<20 mV Pa−1), discrete response frequencies, and critically substrate-dependent output (12). These limitations could lead to inaccuracies in data acquisition and feature extraction, posing challenges for advancing reliable machine learning models essential for advanced voice-interactive systems. In particular, the development of sensitive acoustic fabrics is severely hindered by the inherent properties of the fabrics (11, 12), including hierarchical structures and numerous interfaces formed by their fiber and yarn components. These intrinsic factors scatter and dissipate phonons, thereby dampening sound.

Triboelectrification is a natural phenomenon in which electrostatic charges are generated on the surface of a material through friction (14). Using triboelectric techniques that leverage triboelectrification and electrostatic induction for acoustic perception (i.e., triboacoustic devices) could address the limitations associated with piezoelectric acoustic devices. Triboacoustic devices have revealed advantages, including higher outputs, superior sensitivities, and a continuous range of resonant frequencies (15–28). However, the development of triboacoustic devices faces two primary challenges. First, they are typically operated on the basis of physical contact and separation of two dielectric films, the performance of which depends on the contact-and-separation distance (14–16, 20, 24, 29). To optimize the output, rigid and bulky external resonant cavities are often required to amplify sound pressure and enhance the spatial vibrations of the active layers (15, 20–22). However, these additions complicate the architecture of devices and enlarge their sizes, rendering them incompatible with wearables. In the absence of these additional cavities, the devices tend to suffer from poor stabilities, low outputs (typically ranging from nano- to submicroampere current or 0.1 to single-digit voltages), low sensitivities, and narrow resonance frequencies that fail to fully cover human speech frequencies (tables S1 and S2) (16–19, 23–26). These crucial issues arise from the tiny spatial vibrations [nanometer amplitude displacements (12)] generated by sound, in addition to the minimal contact-and-separation distance between the active films, thereby leading to a reduced output (15, 20–22, 24, 29). In recent years, notable progress has been made in addressing the limitations of conventional triboacoustic sensors. Park et al. (30) realized broadband acoustic detection using hierarchical composite architectures, while Kang et al. (31) designed a bioinspired tunable sensor system achieving high sensitivity through microstructural optimization. Despite these advances, most existing devices are still based on film structures or localized arrays, and the development of wearable acoustic sensing systems remains a major challenge.

Fabric electrostatic charges are prevalent in daily life. Given their unique attributes, fabrics offer ideal platforms for creating imperceptible, wearable, and self-powered triboacoustic devices for voice-AI interactions, ensuring usability and flexibility across diverse environments. However, a fully textile-based triboacoustic device that combines high performance, wearability, and generative AI technology into clothing has yet to be described.

Here, we develop a triboelectric acoustic-perceiving textile (termed A-Textile) that leverages natural electrostatic charges for active and imperceptible voice sensing through clothing. By implementing a deep learning (DL) model, the DL-empowered A-Textile achieves high recognition accuracy across a variety of spoken phrases, enabling intuitive clothing-integrated voice-AI applications. As illustrated in Fig. 1A, the intelligent voice-perceptive clothing facilitates voice-assisted control of internet-of-things (IoT) devices, access to cloud-based information, and interaction with generative AI chatbots for advanced applications. The A-Textile features a rough-surfaced SnS2 nanoflowers (NFs)–doped silicone rubber (SR) coating (SnS2 NFs-SR) as an electrostatic charge-capturing and transport layer, which is placed on a SnS2 NFs–decorated graphite-like carbonized textile (SnS2 NFs-GT) as an electrostatic charge reservoir. This multilayer architecture maximizes charge collection and retention within the textile, leading to enhanced signal output in response to subtle sound-induced vibrations. It thus achieves an output voltage of up to 21 V, a superior sensitivity as high as 1.2 V Pa−1, a resolution of 1 Hz, a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 59.3 dB, and a wide frequency response range of 80 to 900 Hz. The textile also exhibits wearability, stability, humidity resistance, bendability, and washability, facilitating integration into garments for voice perception (Fig. 1, B to D). Moreover, the excellent voice acquisition capabilities of the A-Textile ensure high-quality input for two-dimensional (2D) convolutional neural network (CNN) DL models for accurate speech recognition (Fig. 1, E and F). This allows the wireless control of smart appliances and effortless access to smartphone applications such as Google Maps. Last, the connection with ChatGPT is performed to support interactive dialog, information-sharing, and task assistance. This work develops voice-AI clothing by combining advanced materials, device design, and DL to transform everyday garments into intuitive voice-AI interfaces.

RESULTS

Design and preparation of the A-Textile

Figure 1G shows an exploded view of the multilayered A-Textile (3.3 cm by 3.3π cm) consisting of seven different functional layers, namely a tribopositive nylon fabric laminated with a conducting silver (Ag)-fiber textile (fig. S1) that contains several holes for air circulation and acoustic response enhancement, a gap-created spacer layer (60 μm thick), a tribonegative SnS2 NFs (fig. S2)-SR composite coating layer (fig. S3), a SnS2 NFs-GT layer (figs. S4 to S6), an isolating SR layer, and a bottom Ag-fiber textile electrode layer.

A long-standing challenge in acoustic sensing technologies is the reliable detection of voice-induced mechanical vibrations on textiles. To address this, we developed the A-Textile, an acoustic-sensing textile that harnesses naturally generated electrostatic charges on clothing for active acoustic perception, as illustrated in Fig. 1H. It captures natural speech by detecting electrostatic field variations induced by acoustic waves. Initially, the nylon fabric and SnS2 NFs-SR composite undergo triboelectric charging upon contact caused by body motion. Opposite charges appear on the two surfaces owing to their disparate triboelectric affinities, and these charges create an interlayer electrostatic field that induces carriers in the electrodes. Vibrations induced by acoustic waves cause fluctuations in the electrostatic field between the two tribolayers, thereby generating an electron flow across the electrodes. Compared with previously reported triboacoustic sensors (15–28), the A-Textile uniquely enables direct acoustic sensing on fabric by leveraging enhanced electrostatic charge density to support a noncontact vibration response. This allows carrier flow without physical contact between the tribolayers, eliminating the need for resonant cavities (32–35). This is a demonstration of imperceptible textile-based voice sensing using natural electrostatic charges, marking a key step toward practical clothing-integrated acoustic interfaces.

Moreover, the fabric characteristics and configuration of the A-Textile provide various advantages, such as flexibility, wearability, bendability, washability, and lightweight nature (7.59 g) (fig. S7). Consequently, these properties facilitate the integration of A-Textile into everyday garments, allowing unrestricted movement and seamless intuitive applications.

Acoustic sensing performance of the A-Textile with varied compositions

The design concept for the bottom part of the A-Textile is illustrated in Fig. 2A, wherein the rough-surfaced SnS2 NFs-SR with a high electron affinity is used to attract substantial negative electrostatic charges to its surface. The SnS2 NFs with large specific surface areas serve as electrostatic charge collectors and transport media, facilitating the rapid transport of electrostatic charges to the SnS2 NFs-GT (32–35). Simultaneously, the SnS2 NFs-GT functions as a charge reservoir, leveraging its large surface area and trapping states to accumulate electrostatic charges while minimizing charge dissipation on the textile (32). This synergistic approach, which combines multiple charge modulation techniques, maximizes the electrostatic charge density on the textile, enhancing the sensing performance of the A-Textile. These breakthroughs in triboelectric materials and device design enable a highly efficient, self-powered acoustic sensing system, advancing the performance and application boundaries of triboacoustic technology.

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images presented in Fig. 2B show that the SnS2 NFs (2-μm diameter) have a large surface area, a highly controllable structural symmetry, and a well-preserved flower-like structure. The x-ray diffraction (XRD) profiles confirmed the crystallinity and phase purity of the synthesized powder, with diffraction peaks matching the database results for the Berndite-2T layered structure of SnS2 (JCPDS card no. 23-0677) (fig. S8A) (36, 37). Furthermore, the high-resolution TEM image in Fig. 2C provides detailed structural information, demonstrating that each SnS2 nanosheet in the flower-like structure exhibits the characteristic layered structure of 2D transition-metal dichalcogenides. The magnified image from the red rectangular region shows well-resolved lattice spacings of 0.19 and 0.28 nm, which can be indexed to the ( 12¯0 ) and ( 101¯ ) planes of Berndite-2T SnS2, respectively, consistent with the XRD results (36, 37). The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern, shown as an inset in the upper-left corner of Fig. 2C, displays a periodic diffraction spot pattern characteristic of the SnS2 nanosheet, confirming the single-crystalline nature of the individual nanosheet (36, 37). High-resolution x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) performed for the Sn and S regions (Fig. 2D and fig. S8B) confirmed the composition and valence states of the SnS2 NFs, with no substantial surface contamination. Specifically, the peaks observed at 487.1 and 495.5 eV reflect the Sn4+ 3d5/2 and Sn4+ 3d3/2 valences, while those at 161.8 and 163.1 eV correspond to S2− 2p3/2 and S2− 2p1/2, respectively (38, 39). These characterization results confirm the high quality of the SnS2 sample used in this study.

The GT component of this system was obtained by carbonization of a plain-weave cotton textile, wherein twisted warp and weft yarns composed of substantial quantities of cotton fibers were used to enhance the degree of electrostatic charge accumulation. As shown in Fig. 2E, the plain-weave structure of the GT was well retained after carbonization. The corresponding elemental mappings of C, Sn, and S indicate that the SnS2 NFs were well decorated on the GT (Fig. 2F). In addition, Raman spectroscopy depicts a G band (crystalline sp2 carbon) at 1581 cm−1 and a D band (defects or heteroatom doping) at 1306 cm−1; the additional presence of a broad and weak 2D band at ~2770 cm−1 indicates the presence of defects or heteroatom doping in the GT (Fig. 2G) (40, 41). These findings, combined with the TEM image, SAED pattern (fig. S9), and XPS analyses (fig. S10), confirm the formation of hexagonal graphite in the GT (40, 41). An energy band diagram illustrating the working process of the bottom triboelectric layer is presented in Fig. 2H (32–34, 42, 43).

As outlined in fig. S11, the acoustic sensing characteristics of different A-Textile configurations were evaluated using a linear standard-swept audio signal with frequencies ranging from 1 to 1000 Hz (120 dB). The data presented in Fig. 2I show that the device composed of the SnS2 NFs-SR composite along with the SnS2 NFs-GT layer generated a maximum open-circuit voltage (Voc) of 21 V, which is four orders of magnitude higher than that of a previously reported piezoacoustic fabric (0.00015 to 0.008 V) (12). The high electrical signal imparts the A-Textile with a high sensitivity to subtle sounds and excellent recognition accuracy in noisy environments, confirming its suitability for DL training and enabling reliable feature extraction from voice signals. The corresponding frequency characteristics obtained via fast Fourier transform (FFT) are presented in Fig. 2J and fig. S12. The observed wide and continuous frequency response range of 80 to 900 Hz demonstrates the robustness of the A-Textile for continuous voice perception. The introduction of SnS2 NFs and GT boosts output while maintaining resonant frequency stability (fig. S13), demonstrating improved sound perception without compromising the frequency response.

To further investigate the optimal A-Textile configuration, the surface potentials of devices with different configurations were investigated (Fig. 2K). It was found that the incorporation of the SnS2 NFs-SR layer with the SnS2 NFs-GT produces the highest negative surface potential (−2.08 kV) (Fig. 2K), as well as the most extended surface potential duration (Fig. 2L). The surface potential was measured using an electrostatic voltmeter (fig. S14). The notable surface potential was attributed to the efficient charge capture and transmission of the SnS2 NFs, whereas the prolonged charge storage time arose from the SnS2 NFs-GT layer. According to triboelectrification theory, the surface potential directly reflects the surface charge density, which governs electrostatic induction and determines device output performance (44, 45). Here, the elevated surface potential indicates enhanced charge retention, which reinforces electrostatic induction and therefore amplifies the acoustic output voltage. The surface potential increased upon increasing the SnS2 NF content from 0 to 0.5 wt %. However, a subsequent decrease was observed at an NF content of 0.7 wt % (Fig. 2M). This result indicates that an appropriate concentration of SnS2 NFs can form numerous microcapacitors that effectively maintain the electrostatic charges, whereas higher concentrations create conducting percolation paths that lead to the loss of electrostatic charges (32–34, 42). It was also found that a SnS2 NF content of 0.5 wt % led to the longest charge retention of 52,400 s (Fig. 2N). Correspondingly, with an increase in the SnS2 NF content from 0 to 0.5 wt %, Voc exhibited a pronounced increase (21 V) with respect to pure SR (12 V). However, an excessively high NF content (>0.5 wt %) led to a reduction in Voc (Fig. 2O). Notably, the resonant frequency exhibited stable characteristics independent of the SnS2 NF content (Fig. 2P and fig. S15).

Acoustic sensing properties

To optimize the acoustic sensing performance of the A-Textile, a range of structural parameters was comprehensively studied, including the spacer thickness, hole diameter, and hole area. The output Voc and resonant frequencies as functions of the spacer thickness are shown in Fig. 3A and fig. S16 (A and B). It can be seen that the Voc increases from 13 to 20 V upon increasing the spacer thickness from 30 to 60 μm and progressively decreases at a thickness of 150 μm (fig. S16A). This was attributed to the insufficient potential difference generated by the smaller spacer distance between the two tribolayers, whereas a larger spacer distance attenuated the electrostatic induction effect. A slight shift in the resonant frequencies was observed with an increasing spacer thickness, although all frequencies remained within the range of 80 to 900 Hz (fig. S16B).

The effects of the hole diameters and areas on the output Voc and resonant frequency were also evaluated, as presented in Fig. 3 (B and C) and figs. S17 and S18. The experimental results indicated that Voc is strongly dependent on both properties, with a maximum Voc being obtained for a hole diameter of 1.25 mm (fig. S17A) and a hole area of 13% (fig. S18A). In addition, it was found that the resonant frequency varied only slightly with the hole diameter and area (figs. S17B and S18B). These results can be explained by the fact that the electrical output primarily results from membrane vibrations. A moderate hole diameter and area lead to a larger amplitude of vibrations, which in turn produces a peak in Voc. These findings offer fundamental guidance for attaining a high-sensitivity A-Textile through structural optimization while preserving a relatively constant resonant frequency and a wide response frequency range. Figure 3D shows the stable electrical output of the A-Textile over a total of 7000 operational cycles at 218 Hz and 120 dB.

In addition, Fig. 3E plots the output amplitudes of the A-Textile (60-μm spacer thickness, 1.25-mm hole diameter, and 13% hole area) under a constant resonance frequency of 218 Hz but with different sound pressure levels (SPLs) between 70 and 120 dB. As indicated, Voc exhibits exponential growth (from 0.066 to 20 V) with an increase in the SPL level (fig. S19) because the increase in sound pressure causes a larger vibration of the A-Textile (5). The device exhibits an SPL-dependent response with a high resolution and excellent SNR at low SPLs (SNR = 24.4 dB at 70-dB SPL; SNR = 35.8 dB at 80-dB SPL), ensuring reliable voice detection under realistic conditions (fig. S20). Notably, at an SPL of 80 dB, the sensitivity reached 1.2 V Pa−1, outperforming many other reports (table S1). For instance, a triboelectric auditory sensor for robotics/hearing aids displayed a sensitivity of 513.9 mV Pa−1 (17), while a piezoacoustic fabric exhibited a sensitivity of 19.6 mV Pa−1 (12). The superior sensitivity observed for the current system was therefore attributed to the maximized electrostatic charge quantity in the textile.

Subsequently, the performance of the A-Textile was evaluated at different frequencies (0 to 1000 Hz, 120 dB), demonstrating resonance characteristics with Voc values of up to 21 V (Fig. 3F). The A-Textile achieves efficient acoustic sensing over the range of 80 to 900 Hz, exhibiting strong and stable electrical outputs across this window. The FFT analysis of the generated electrical signals reveals a continuous broadband response within this range, effectively covering the diverse spectrum of human phonetic frequencies. The frequencies were then amplified within a narrower bandgap (174 to 235 Hz), specifically targeting the most pertinent frequencies in daily human conversations. Competing piezoacoustic fabrics suffer from discrete and confined frequency responses (12, 13, 46). The presented A-Textile exhibits a broader and more consistent response, allowing proficient detection of voice sources across wider populations.

To assess the frequency resolution, three sound waves with similar frequencies (i.e., 217, 218, and 219 Hz) were applied to the A-Textile. As shown in Fig. 3G and fig. S21, three peaks were unambiguously discriminated at 217, 218, and 219 Hz in the FFT (Fig. 3G, inset), revealing an exceptional frequency resolution of 1 Hz for sound perception. This high resolution enables the capture of finer sound details, improves the recognition accuracy, and reduces distortion. Furthermore, the developed A-Textile was able to distinguish between three sound waves with larger frequency intervals of 100, 200, and 300 Hz (fig. S22). The frequency response characteristics of the A-Textile remained consistent when detecting 70- to 120-dB sweep signals in 5-dB steps (Fig. 3H). A prominent response was even maintained at a smaller SPL of 70 dB, rendering this device particularly suitable for detecting low-intensity speech acoustics.

As a smart acoustic garment, washability is an important consideration, as is the case for normal clothing. Thus, to evaluate the effects of washing on the A-Textile, the frequency and SPL (70 to 120 dB and 218 Hz, respectively) response characteristics were examined over a total of nine washing cycles, confirming the high dependability and mechanical compliance of this device (Fig. 3, I and J, and figs. S23 to S25). The data presented in fig. S26 further confirm the excellent tolerance of the A-Textile over a 9-day storage period. It was also found that the A-Textile operated reliably with a high Voc of 15.0 V even at a relative humidity of 90% (fig. S27), thereby ensuring continued functionality in the case where spitting may occur while talking. These results not only support the notion that electrostatic charges can penetrate the SnS2 NFs-SR but also suggest that the configuration of the SnS2 NFs-SR composite, combined with the SnS2 NFs-GT, preserves the electrostatic charges, preventing their degradation by water molecules (47). To assess mechanical durability, the A-Textile was subjected to bending tests under curvatures ranging from 10 to 50 m−1. After 1000 bending cycles, the output voltage remained nearly unchanged, demonstrating excellent mechanical robustness and long-term reliability for wearable applications (fig. S28).

DL-empowered A-Textile for sophisticated applications

The high sensitivity, wideband response, and high resolution demonstrated by the A-Textile indicate its potential for enabling advanced user-centric AI voice applications. Through integration with a 2D CNN DL model, the A-Textile achieved the precise recognition, prediction, and visualization of various phrases and commands across daily scenarios. It was therefore considered desirable to investigate the possibility of using voice commands to control IoT appliances in a smart home (representing a voice assistant), to access smartphone navigation applications (representing voice-activated computing and cloud information access), and to interact with ChatGPT (representing access to generative AI chatbots).

Use of voice to remotely control IoT appliances

The DL model is highly feasible for complex data analytics because of its comprehensive feature extraction, which is desirable for fine-grained analysis and precise identification (9, 10, 48). In addition, CNNs have been previously tailored to process data across multiple arrays (49). In this context, a 2D CNN dominated by image discrimination constitutes a facile method for recognizing speech time-series signals captured by acoustic sensors.

A potential application scenario based on the use of the A-Textile for voice control in smart home appliances is illustrated schematically in Fig. 4A. In addition, Fig. 4B shows 10 distinct electric signals corresponding to 10 different voice commands for IoT control in a smart home. These commands include the following: “Turn on/off the air conditioner,” “Turn on/off the TV,” “Turn on/off the lamp,” “Robot vacuum, go home,” “Robot vacuum, clean up the bedroom,” and “Turn on/off the audio.” Each signal exhibits a distinct waveform, providing valuable data for the DL training datasets. The optimized 2D CNN structural parameters are shown in fig. S29, and the detailed confusion probabilities for the 10 commands are shown in the confusion matrix (Fig. 4C). The total recognition accuracy of the established model was determined to be 93.5% (fig. S30).

Following data visualization, the feature clustering results for the 10 commands in the input layer were achieved using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) (Fig. 4, D and E), revealing inferior feature clustering with overlapping categories before passing through the 2D CNN (Fig. 4D). After feature extraction and classification, the visualization results indicated improved classification performance, showing well-defined boundaries and minimal overlap among the 10 categories (Fig. 4E). This demonstrates the effectiveness of the developed 2D CNN model for speech recognition in smart home applications.

The established algorithm model was also connected to a wireless network to achieve the practical voice control of appliances. As demonstrated in Fig. 4F and movies S1 to S3, the user can wirelessly control various electric appliances (e.g., the TV, lamp, and stereo) using simple instructions, such as “Turn on the TV,” “Turn on the lamp,” or “Turn on the audio,” which were spoken into the DL-empowered A-Textile incorporated into worn clothing.

Voice-enabled cloud information retrieval

Subsequently, the capability of the DL-empowered A-Textile to control an intelligent cell phone was evaluated to represent a computing system with access to cloud information. In this demonstration, the user accessed a smartphone navigation application by speaking into the DL-empowered A-Textile, as illustrated in Fig. 4G. This hands-free, on-garment voice access system for smartphones and cloud information improves accessibility, rendering it particularly valuable in specialized work contexts and for populations such as firefighters, paramedics, and individuals with visual or physical impairments.

Figure 4H shows the electrical signals generated when 10 different geographic names and commands were spoken into the A-Textile, i.e., “National Palace Museum,” “HSR Taichung Station,” “Home,” “Taroko National Park,” “Taipei Confucius Temple,” “Taipei 101,” “Taichung National Opera House,” “National Chung Hsing University (NCHU),” “Share position to Daniel,” and “Navigation.” The optimized 2D CNN structural parameters for the signals are provided in fig. S31. As presented in Fig. 4I, the confusion matrix validated the overall accuracy of the developed algorithm, reaching a value of 97.5% after 15 epochs (fig. S32). Furthermore, the t-SNE feature clustering results demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed model in feature segmentation and speech identification for voice navigation (Fig. 4, J and K).

The voice identification results were subsequently projected onto human-machine interfaces and translated into commands and texts, enabling remote access to smartphone navigation programs (e.g., Google Maps) and cloud information retrieval. As demonstrated in Fig. 4 (L and M), along with movies S4 to S6, voice commands can be used to ask the Google Maps application to initiate navigation to the desired location, such as the “National Palace Museum,” the “HSR Taichung Station,” and the “Taichung National Opera House.”

Leveraging voice interaction for generative AI chatbots

To maximize clothing-based intuitive voice-AI utilities, the DL-empowered A-Textile was used to engage in in-depth interactions with a generative AI chatbot (i.e., ChatGPT) to achieve real-time knowledge acquisition and personalized responses (Fig. 5A). This application goes beyond simple information retrieval, enabling multiturn interactions and complex tasks, such as problem analysis, advice provision, and intelligent task execution through natural voice interactions. This demonstration highlights the system’s potential to enable sophisticated voice-based applications, advancing wearable platforms from passive sensing to more intelligent functionality.

As demonstrated in Fig. 5G and movie S7, the user spoke the inquiry, “What is ChatGPT?” into the A-Textile, which then transferred the request to ChatGPT for a suggestion. In Fig. 5 (H and I), in addition to movies S8 and S9, the ability of the A-Textile to handle information-query commands and transfer the questions to ChatGPT is demonstrated. Example commands included “What is NCHU?” and “Give me a good cocktail recipe.” This system was designed to collect, analyze, categorize, and integrate vast amounts of information, leading to accurate predictions and personalized recommendations. Ultimately, this opens up opportunities for textile-based voice AI assistance, offering individuals smarter and more tailored AI experiences.

To validate the model’s robustness and generalizability, we evaluated its performance across different users and noise environments. As shown in figs. S35 and S36, the established 2D CNN model achieves an average accuracy of 93.8% across users despite variations in vocal characteristics, demonstrating generalizability for user-independent phrase recognition. Moreover, as shown in fig. S37, the model maintains stable performance under varying background noise levels (65 to 75 dB), confirming its robustness against acoustic interference and suitability for real-world wearable applications.

In addition, the A-Textile achieved reliable differentiation between acoustically similar phrases (e.g., “Big cat” versus “Big hat,” “Good boy” versus “Good toy,” “Light on” versus “Light off,” and “Open the door” versus “Open the drawer”), yielding accuracies of 95.0, 90.0, 95.0, and 92.5%. respectively (fig. S38). These results underscore the high resolution and excellent signal recognition capabilities of the A-Textile, reinforcing its applicability. This work represents an initial step toward voice sensing and voice-AI integration on clothing; however, it currently lacks an integrated system for signal processing and autonomous decision-making. Future efforts will focus on the development of all modules on textiles, including signal transmission components, signal conditioning circuits, and computing systems.

DISCUSSION

Leveraging natural electrostatic charges, a high-performance, wearable, bendable, humidity-resistant, and washable DL-empowered triboacoustic sensing textile is presented, which seamlessly incorporates self-powered, imperceptible voice perception and voice AI access into clothing. This approach is based on the integration of a rough-surfaced charge capture and transport SnS2 NFs-SR composite coating with a SnS2 NFs-GT charge reservoir, which maximizes the electrostatic charge density and retention time for noncontact acoustic vibration perception. The resulting acoustic textile yields a high electric output of 21 V, a sensitivity of 1.2 V Pa−1, a resolution of 1 Hz, an SNR of 59.3 dB, and a broadband frequency response of 80 to 900 Hz. It also exhibits consistent output over 7000 cycles of repeated use and 9 days of storage, retains 15.0 V under a 90% relative humidity, and maintains stable performance after 1000 bending cycles, demonstrating superior environmental robustness and mechanical durability. With the assistance of a DL algorithm, an intuitive AI-voice interactive system is constructed that achieves recognition accuracies of 93.5 to 97.5% for diverse daily activities, allowing for wireless voice-controlled teleoperation of IoT devices and smartphone access applications. The textile-based voice AI interaction system is also demonstrated to enable intuitive, intelligent interactions with generative AI for sophisticated tasks when combined with an AI chatbot. It is believed that the integration of imperceptible voice AI systems into clothing will provide easy access to AI while providing previously unexplored application prospects in various areas such as health care, fitness monitoring, and personalized assistance. By merging advanced materials science, device engineering, and DL, this work lays the foundation for the next generation of intelligent, wearable voice-AI systems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Synthesis of the SnS2 NFs

SnS2 NFs were synthesized using a previously reported hydrothermal method (50). Tin chloride (SnCl4, 1.77 g) and thiourea (CH4N2S, 1.15 g) were dissolved in 76 ml of absolute ethanol under a nitrogen atmosphere and magnetically stirred until a clear solution formed. The solution was sealed in a high-pressure hydrothermal autoclave and heated at 200°C for 9 hours. After cooling to room temperature, the product was collected by centrifugation, washed repeatedly with water and ethanol, and dried at 60°C to yield SnS2 NF powder.

Preparation of the GT

Under a nitrogen atmosphere, the cotton fabric was heated to 1000°C at a rate of 3°C/min and subjected to carbonization for 200 min to generate the desired GT.

Fabrication of the A-Textile

The preparation of the A-Textile consisted of two components: the upper and lower parts. For the upper component, a commercially available conductive textile and a nylon fabric were bonded using 3M double-sided adhesive tape, and composite samples with different hole numbers and area ratios were created by laser engraving. A nylon fabric spacer with an outer diameter of 66 mm and an inner diameter of 40 mm was then laser cut and bonded to the porous composite using 3M double-sided adhesive tape. To fabricate the lower part, P600 sandpaper was secured inside a mold, and SR (Ecoflex 0030, Smooth-On Inc., PA) doped with SnS2 NFs at varying weight ratios was poured and evenly spread. The mold was then cured in an oven at 80°C for 1 hour. Next, the carbonized plain cotton textile doped with SnS2 NFs was placed in the mold, encapsulated with pure SR, and cured again under the same conditions. Last, the cured component was coated with an SR layer, covered with the conductive Ag-fiber textile, and dried at 80°C for 1 hour before being removed and demolded. The upper and bottom components were then sewn together and attached to the sleeve of a shirt to create the A-Textile.

Characterization

SEM (ZEISS ULTRA PLUS), energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy (OXFORD X-Max 50 mm2), XRD (Rigaku Ultima IV), Raman scattering spectroscopy (UniDRON), and field-emission TEM (Tecnai G2) were performed to explore the morphologies, valence states, crystal structures, components, and crystallinities of the as-synthesized GT, SnS2 NFs, and SnS2 NFs-SR composites. The electrical outputs of the acoustic sensors were measured using an ENKOR E50 speaker with an adjustable frequency. Voc, Qtr, and charging curves were measured using a Keithley 6517A electrometer/high-resistance meter. Isc was measured using a Stanford low-noise current amplifier (model SR570). A National Instruments BNC-2120 device was used as a junction box for the physical connection of the external signal to be tested, and a National Instruments PCI-6259 multifunction device was used for data collection. A Trek Model 542A electrostatic voltmeter was used to measure the static voltage data for the determination of the maximum surface potential and potential degradation over time. LabVIEW was used as the software platform for data acquisition, analysis, prediction, and feedback commands.

Calculation of the sound sensitivity

The sound sensitivity (S) was calculated using the following equations (19): SPL = 20lg(SP/SPref) and S = V/SP = V/[SPref × 10(SPL/20)], where SP represents the acoustic pressure, SPref is the standard reference acoustic pressure (2 × 10−5 Pa), and V is the output voltage.

Data collection and dataset configuration

A signal acquisition module acquired the triboelectric signals generated by different phrases/commands in a Keithley 6517A electrometer/high-resistance meter. For voice control of the IoT, 100 samples were collected for each phrase/command, with 80 samples (80%) being used for training and 20 samples (20%) for testing. Thus, a total of 1000 samples were used for the 10 phrases/commands examined in the study. It was found that the 2D CNN model with 8 by 8 kernels, 32 filters, and 3 convolutional layers exhibited optimal accuracy (fig. S29). Speech features were then sampled using maximum pooling layers. For the voice searching of cloud information, each data class included 100 training samples, of which 80 were used for training and 20 for testing, along with 100 training epochs. Again, a total of 1000 samples was used for the evaluation of the 10 geographic names/commands. The 2D CNN model with 8 by 8 kernels, 32 filters, and 3 convolutional layers gave the highest accuracy (fig. S31). For interaction with the generative AI chatbot, each data category consisted of 100 training samples (80% for training and 20% for testing) over 100 training epochs. A total of 1000 samples was used to incorporate the 10 life-themed questions. Once again, the 2D CNN model with 8 by 8 kernels, 32 filters, and 3 convolutional layers gave the highest accuracy (fig. S33).

Use of voice to remotely control IoT appliances

Data preprocessing was performed on the audio signals generated by the phrases/commands, and each category of data was input into a CNN for training to obtain a highly accurate model. Last, the model was connected to a cloud link network to realize a smart home that switched the electronic devices on and off wirelessly.

Use of voice to remotely control IoT appliances

Triboelectric acoustic signals were generated by speaking into the A-Textile, preprocessing the electric signals with noise reduction and filtering, and inputting the processed signals into the optimized CNN for training to obtain an effective model. Using the wireless Wi-Fi connection between a computer and a smartphone, the result of the real-time prediction of the voice generated after the speech was displayed in the LabVIEW interface and was converted into a trigger command to simultaneously control the smartphone navigation application.

Use of voice to interact with a generative AI chatbot

Following the generation of the induced electric signals using the A-Textile and subsequent data collection and processing into a DL database, this database was imported into a CNN to obtain an effective 2D CNN model with an excellent decision-making capability. Real-time prediction results were displayed through the LabVIEW interface and were simultaneously converted into trigger commands to enable conversations with ChatGPT.
