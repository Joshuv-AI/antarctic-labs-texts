A model system test of trophic transmission of RNA interference effects using the milkweed bug Oncopeltus fasciatus (Hemiptera)
Authors: Ebony Michelle Argaez, James B. Digel, Katie Reding, Muhammad Salim Hakeemi, Leslie Pick, Kelly A. Hamby
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Designing species-specific dsRNAs makes gene knockdown by RNA interference (RNAi) ideal for pest control. Here, we establish the milkweed bug Oncopeltus fasciatus as a lab model to assess RNAi impacts. Oncopeltus are harmless, easy to rear and highly susceptible to RNAi. Knockdown of developmental regulatory genes by parental RNAi–dsRNA administered to adult females with effects observed in their offspring–is technically straightforward and even partial knockdown results in scorable, gene-specific morphological defects. Defects were detectable at low dsRNA doses and duration of effects increased with dose, exhibiting improved sensitivity over measurements of mortality. While delivery by injection is most effective, we show that Oncopeltus are susceptible to RNAi via ingestion. We tested potential passage of RNAi effects from prey to predator, exploiting Oncopeltus egg cannibalism, injecting females with dsRNA and feeding their offspring to different females never exposed to exogenous dsRNA. The offspring of this second group of females displayed gene-specific defects in similar ranges to females directly fed dsRNA. Thus, RNAi effects can be passed generationally and trophically. Although effects on non-target organisms through the food chain are possible, the subtle responses in this scenario suggest they are unlikely to be problematic for agricultural RNAi insecticides.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-54197-6.

Introduction

To protect global food security and maintain productivity in the face of growing demand, crop losses caused by pests must be reduced, which requires use of effective pesticides1–3. However, many pesticides pose risks to human health and the environment, warranting reduced-risk alternatives. The discovery of RNA interference (RNAi4–12) raised the possibility of producing pesticides with fewer non-target effects. This approach has been particularly exciting for the prospect of developing dsRNAs that are species-specific pesticides and pose lower risk to non-target organisms than traditional pesticides (for examples and review, see13–25.

The goal of RNAi-based insecticides is to reduce the levels of specific RNAs for essential genes in target pest species, thus causing lethality and/or inhibiting fertility. dsRNAs can be designed to match sequences that are present in the pest species, while avoiding sequences exactly matching genes in non-target species. The information needed for RNAi design is genome or transcriptome sequences of the target and potential non-target species, along with information about expression and function of genes to be targeted. Several screens have been carried out in both pest and model insect species to identify ideal target genes as candidates for use in agriculture (for examples in different systems, see26–36.

Researchers have used this approach to design and test dsRNA-based insecticides that target agricultural pests. In an agricultural setting, dsRNA-based insecticides that can be delivered by feeding, as has been documented for the model system C. elegans37–39, would be most useful. Accordingly, a study demonstrating that western corn rootworm and other Coleoptera are susceptible to RNAi via feeding raised the possibility that insect pests could be controlled by ingestion of dsRNAs targeting genes required for viability40. Specifically, this study showed that adding dsRNAs targeting essential genes such as those encoding vacuolar ATPase (V-ATPase) subunits to the diet, or feeding transgenic corn expressing this dsRNA, impacted growth and caused lethality. To date, delivery of dsRNA by feeding has been shown to be effective for gene knockdown in a number of arthropod pests, such as ticks, termites, kissing bug, fruit fly, and Colorado potato beetle41–44. However, other species are less sensitive or even resistant to ingested dsRNA due to factors such as the degradation of dsRNA by gut nucleases or inefficient cellular uptake. The successful use of RNAi in Hemiptera has been documented for a large number of species, including agricultural pests, especially in recent years45–51. As for other insect orders, effectiveness varies between species and in some cases, degradation of dsRNA limits the ability to effectively deliver dsRNA via feeding (reviewed in Refs.52–54). To improve RNAi-based insect pest management strategies, chitosan-based nanoparticles have been developed to improve the stability and delivery of dsRNA (e.g.55). Feeding bacteria modified to express dsRNA has also been shown to induce a systemic response in the beet armyworm56 and the kissing bug Rhodnius prolixus57, an hemipteran vector of Chagas disease. Ingesting dsRNA-expressing yeast resulted in reduced fitness of spotted wing Drosophila58. These novel methods for dsRNA delivery would allow for effective RNAi via spraying, soaking, or other methods that obviate the need to generate dsRNA-expressing transgenic crops.

The first dsRNA-based insecticide approved by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) for field use in 2017 was the transgenic corn SmartStax Pro® which was developed to control western corn rootworm (Diabrotica virgifera virgifera) populations by expressing dsRNAs that target the Snf7 gene from this species (Dv-Snf7) (USEPA Registration Number: 68467-7). Recently, several countries including the U.S., Japan, Brazil, and Canada have approved the use of SmartStax Pro®. In addition, in 2023, the EPA approved the first sprayable dsRNA insecticide, Ledprona, targeting proteasome subunit beta 5 (PSMB5) in the Colorado potato beetle59. Approval and registration of pesticide products requires non-target testing, and RNAi-based products are examined using a risk assessment framework established for other plant-incorporated protectants or sprayable formulations40,60. For example, lady beetle species were found to vary in their sensitivity to dsRNA and detectably respond to dsRNA targeted to the pest D. virgifera virgifera60,61. While some omnivorous predators will feed on pollen and other plant material, the route of exposure for many predators will be direct consumption of prey that has fed upon topically applied or plant-expressed dsRNA. Aphid herbivores were shown to uptake plant expressed gfp-dsRNA and transmit the dsRNA to lady beetle predators, although this experimental design did not assess phenotypic effects of dsRNA(s) targeting a gene in the genome of the prey or predator62. Thus, whether or not dsRNA can be trophically transferred at sufficient dose and quality to impact non-target predators remains an open question.

Here, we established a model to test for trophic effects of RNAi in an insect that is highly susceptible to RNAi effects, the large milkweed bug Oncopeltus fasciatus. Oncopeltus are not themselves pests but are an ideal lab model to assess RNAi mechanisms63–67. We made use of two developmental regulatory genes whose knockdown by RNAi was previously shown to cause specific and easily identifiable defects in embryos and nymphs: Of-even-skipped (eve) and Of-Sex combs reduced (Scr)63–68. The genes were chosen because they are shared by likely all insect species, impact two different timepoints in embryonic development, and knockdown generates different and highly specific phenotypic changes that can be easily scored without extensive training required. We used parental RNAi (pRNAi), whereby dsRNA targeting an embryonically expressed gene is injected into females and knockdown occurs in embryos laid by these females. Tests of RNAi often rely on genes that cause death of the organism to which dsRNA is applied, as this is often the main purpose of pest management. To determine if this type of assay is as sensitive as scoring subtle developmental defects, we also tested Of-V-ATPase subunit A (Vha68-1) at doses of dsRNA similar to those used for Of-eve and Of-Scr. Because knockdown results in mortality69–71, this gene is commonly used for RNAi-based pest management experiments15,40. Oncopeltus respond to very low levels of dsRNA administered by injection. We also found that Oncopeltus are susceptible to RNAi by feeding, although the knockdown effect is much lower following ingestion than injection. Finally, we investigated the potential for trophic exposure to dsRNAs by feeding adult females eggs laid by different adult females that had been injected with dsRNA. As the “predator” in this experiment is the same species as the target, this represents a maximal-risk scenario in which the exogenous dsRNA is an exact match to an mRNA in the non-targeted organism. We found a low level of transmission of RNAi effects via this mode, documenting for the first time to our knowledge that RNAi effects can be transmitted through the food chain. However, our studies also showed that this transmission requires very high levels of dsRNA and, based on RNAi mechanisms, will likely only occur if there is a good match between the dsRNA and a related target gene in the predator and prey. Thus, while still a possibility that should be considered for field studies, we conclude that the risk of RNAi trophic transmission in real-world scenarios remains very low.

Results

RNAi knockdown of developmental regulatory genes Of-eve and Of-Scr causes a range of specific morphological defects

To establish a system that would allow us to accurately assess RNAi parameters, we made use of two genes whose knockdown by pRNAi was previously shown to cause specific and easily identifiable defects in embryos and nymphs: Of-even-skipped (eve) and Of-Sex combs reduced (Scr)63–68. Adult females were injected with varying amounts of Of-eve-dsRNA or Of-Scr-dsRNA side-by-side with negative control gfp-dsRNA (Fig. 1a) and defects in embryos were scored. For Of-eve-dsRNA, unhatched embryos were scored as either: (1) no observable defect (Fig. 1aii); (2) moderate defects with a range of segment deletions or fusions in the thoracic and/or abdominal segments (Fig. 1bi-iv), corresponding to Class II and III categories of Liu and Kaufman67; or (3) severe defects with embryos lacking almost the entire body, referred to here as “head-only” embryos (Fig. 1bv-vi), corresponding to Class I defects observed in Liu and Kaufman67.

For Of-Scr-dsRNA, hatched embryos were scored and classified as follows: (1) no observed defects (Fig. 1aiii–iv); (2) moderate transformation of the mouthparts, characterized by a rounder or a small split at the tip of the proboscis (Fig. 1ci–cii); or (3) severe transformation of the mouthparts, characterized by expansion of the proboscis into bulbous structures of various sizes split in the center, previously characterized as a transformation toward leg or antennal identity64,68 (Fig. 1ciii–vi). A small number of eggs were not possible to score for Of-eve- or Of-Scr-related defects because they were cannibalized (Fig. 1di), undeveloped (likely unfertilized) (Fig. 1dii), or failed to show any developmental progression (Fig. 1diii).

The developmental genes Of-eve and Of-Scr are sensitive indicators of RNAi susceptibility

To assess the sensitivity of Oncopeltus to dsRNA, increasing amounts of Of-eve or Of-Scr dsRNA were injected into females (Fig. 2). Injecting even small amounts of Of-eve dsRNA into females was sufficient to cause defects in some offspring (Fig. 2a). Injection of increasing amounts of Of-eve dsRNA into females led to an increasing number of offspring showing defects; on average 13.00 ± 8.52% (mean ± standard error) of embryos exhibited moderate or severe defects after injection of 0.1 ng of eve dsRNA, and 10.69 ± 8.14% after injection with 1 ng. At higher doses, defects increased with 75.29 ± 20.68% of embryos displaying moderate or severe defects at 10 ng, and 68.04 ± 31.96% displaying defects at 40 ng. All embryos exhibited defects at 100 ng, 400 ng, and 1000 ng. The dip in percentage of affected embryos after injection of 1 ng of dsRNA reflects variation in the assay system and is not statistically significant. Importantly, even at these low doses of eve dsRNA, gene-specific defects were observed. At doses closer to those used in typical developmental genetic RNAi experiments, most embryos displayed developmental defects, with saturation at 100 ng injected. No eve-like defects were observed for any amount of ds-gfp injected.

Injecting small amounts of Of-Scr dsRNA into females was also sufficient to cause gene-specific defects in offspring (Fig. 2b). The lowest dose at which defects were observed was 1 ng of Of-Scr dsRNA, with one embryo displaying a moderate Scr-specific defect (0.78 ± 0.78%). Average defects increased to 11.66 ± 7.08% at 10 ng and 91.58 ± 5.99% at 40 ng. Note that as the dose increased, moderate defects decreased with an accompanying increase in severe defects (Fig. 2b). Injection of 100 ng, 400 ng, or 1000 ng Of-Scr dsRNA resulted in nearly all of embryos showing Scr-specific defects (99.24 ± 0.76, 100.00 ± 0.00%, and 100.00 ± 0.00%, respectively). No Scr-like defects were observed for any amount of gfp dsRNA injected.

These experiments show that Oncopeltus are sensitive to low doses of injected dsRNA and that the genes Of-eve and Of-Scr are useful for assessing parameters for RNAi in this species. For both genes, response to dsRNA was stronger at higher doses. Saturation was achieved with 100 ng with almost all embryos showing defects. This amount of dsRNA is lower than the amounts frequently used for assessment of gene function in developmental genetic studies.

Duration of dsRNA response is dose-dependent

In a separate experiment to test duration of response, we found that both the presence and duration of defects were dose-dependent. At 1 ng, embryos displaying eve-specific defects were first observed on day 6 with an average of 12.15 ± 3.51% (Fig. 2c). This decreased to 1.87 ± 1.37% embryos with defects by day 10, with no embryos with defects observed before day 6 or after day 10. At 10 ng, embryos with defects remained high (> 90%) from days 4 to 8 and decreased to 15.41 ± 6.22% by day 10, with no defects observed before day 4 or after day 14 (Fig. 2c). At 50 ng, defects were first observed on day 4 and all embryos showed defects, with 99.71 ± 0.29% average defects on day 6 (Fig. 2c). There was a gradual decrease to 50.88 ± 27.59% by day 16, when the experiment was terminated. In sum, defects lasted for 4 days at 1 ng, 10 days at 10 ng, and persisted until the end of the experiment (> 12 days) at 50 ng. Defects were observed sooner and persisted longer for higher doses. Thus, in comparisons of effectiveness of different dsRNAs and anticipated duration of response, protocols must take into account the dose delivered and the timepoints at which effects are analyzed.

Death due to knockdown of V-ATPase is less sensitive to dsRNA than developmental genes

Adults injected with Of-vha68-1 dsRNA experienced 100% mortality on day 31 at 40 ng, day 21 at 100 ng, and day 19 at 400 ng and 1000 ng (Fig. 3a–g). For this experiment, 10 replicate females were injected per dose of Of-Vha68-1 dsRNA (10 females for each amount) and 10 replicate females were also injected with gfp dsRNA as a negative control. At day 16, approximately halfway through the assay period, survival of control adults injected with gfp dsRNA was 80% at 0.1 ng, 90% at 1 ng, 60% at 10 ng, 90% at 40 ng, 70% at 100 ng and 400 ng, and 50% at 1000 ng, while survival of adults injected with Of-Vha68-1 dsRNA was 50% at 0.1 ng, 80% at 1 ng, 70% at 10 ng, 50% at 40 ng, 20% at 100 ng, 40% at 400 ng and 20% at 1000 ng (Fig. 3a–g). Survival of control adults injected with gfp dsRNA over the assay period of 35 days ranged between 10% and 60% across doses of dsRNA, with decreased survival at higher doses (Fig. 3a–g). Thus, overall, greater mortality was seen for Of-Vha68-1 dsRNA than for the gfp dsRNA control, but even at high doses of dsRNA, 100% mortality was not observed for Of-Vha68-1 dsRNA until ~ 20 days after injection, when control animals were also dying. The long delay before observing a meaningful phenotype, non-specificity, and weak effect makes this a poor assay for assessing susceptibility to dsRNA, at least for this gene in this species.

However, we observed that females injected with Of-vha68 dsRNA rapidly ceased egg laying, as reported in the corn planthopper72. Fecundity was measured alongside mortality, shown as average number of eggs laid per female per day (Fig. 3h). Those injected with Of-Vha68-1 dsRNA laid an average of ~ 0–4 eggs per female in the 35 day monitoring period, over the range of doses of dsRNA injected. In contrast, those injected with gfp dsRNA laid ~ 14–17 eggs per female (Fig. 3h). In addition, the impact on egg production was rapid, with females injected with Of-vha68 dsRNA ceasing to lay any eggs 1–2 days after egg laying began.

To determine why eggs were not laid after Of-Vha68-1 dsRNA, ovaries were dissected. In animals injected with gfp dsRNA, wild type-like ovarioles that are yellow in color and contain oval-shaped eggs were observed (Fig. 3i). In contrast, oogenesis was blocked in animals injected with Of-Vha68-1 dsRNA: ovarioles were not pigmented, appeared translucent, and no developed eggs were observed (Fig. 3j) This sublethal sterility effect was observed within days of injection, much sooner and more robust than effects on mortality.

Oncopeltus are susceptible to dsRNA via feeding

To test whether Oncopeltus are susceptible to dsRNA via feeding, we fed similar amounts of Of-eve dsRNA to adult females as were used in the injection experiments and scored defects in their offspring. We observed eve-specific defects in offspring of females fed Of-eve dsRNA (Fig. 4a, right panel) but not in control females fed gfp dsRNA (Fig. 4a, left panel). Both moderate (pink bars) and severe (green bars) defects were observed after feeding of 10 ng or more Of-eve dsRNA, with more than 10% of offspring (130/1059 offspring for 400 ng dsRNA and 114/1047 for 1000 ng dsRNA) showing defects after feeding of 400 or 1000 ng of dsRNA. When similar amounts of dsRNA were injected, 100% of offspring showed defects, showing that injection is much more efficient as a delivery method. These experiments demonstrate that Oncopeltus are susceptible to RNAi via feeding and that these effects can be passed from mothers to offspring, the first demonstration of this for Oncopeltus to our knowledge.

Trophic exposure results in low level transmission of RNAi effects

Given that Oncopeltus are susceptible to dsRNA via ingestion, we next asked whether RNAi effects can be passed from one insect to another via trophic exposure. We made use of the fact that Oncopeltus cannibalize eggs if sufficient food is not present73. Figure 4bi outlines our experimental design: Adult females (P1) were injected with Of-eve dsRNA at levels that produce severe eve-specific defects in 100% of offspring; others were injected with the same amount of control gfp dsRNA. Eggs (G1) were collected from these females and fed to other females (P2) who had never been exposed to exogenous dsRNA. Offspring of P2 females were scored for eve-specific defects. Surprisingly, in a preliminary experiment, effects of eve dsRNA were observed in these G2 eggs laid by P2 females. To rule out the possibility of bias in this experiment, a blinded experiment was carried out, following the experimental design in Reding et al.74. One researcher performed the P1 injections and the other researcher with no knowledge of the treatment - “the analyzer” - scored the defects. In both the preliminary experiment and the blinded experiment, the G1 eggs all displayed severe, head-only defects as expected, but G2 eggs included both severe and moderate defects, strongly suggesting they were not G1 contaminants. Next, this experiment was repeated a third time by one of the same researchers and a different second researcher; results of this experiment are reported here (Fig. 4b,c). In this third experiment, to further rule out the possibility of contamination of the G2 population with G1 eggs, G1 eggs were marked with green dye (Fig. 4Bii), counted before and after feeding to P2 females, and glued to a piece of cardboard to ensure they could not be transported to cottons from which G2 embryos were collected. To prevent contamination of P2 cages with P1 females, these cages were housed in different incubators in different rooms. As shown in Fig. 4biii, females actively fed on the eggs provided. The G1 eggs that were fed to P2 females displayed 100% total defects, with > 99% of these being severe, head-only defects, as expected (Fig. 4ci). G2 eggs laid by P2 females also displayed eve-specific defects: in total, 8.03% (n = 51) of the eggs scored showed eve-specific defects, with 6.61% (n = 42) showing moderate defects and 1.42% (n = 9) showing severe defects (Fig. 4cii). These defects can only be attributed to trophic exposure. For all three experiments, no eve-specific defect was ever observed in G1 or G2 eggs resulting from P1 females injected with gfp dsRNA (Fig. 4ci,ii). In sum, these experiments demonstrate that effects of dsRNA can be transmitted to untreated insects by feeding on other insects displaying RNAi-induced defects. The rate of transmission was in the range of 10% in this within-species experimental design, which represents a worst-case scenario in that the dsRNA injected into P1 females exactly matches an mRNA expressed in the G2 embryos.

What explains this trophic transmission?

As direct deposition of dsRNA into embryos has been documented for C. elegans75 and the beetle Tribolium76, we attempted to estimate how much dsRNA could have been present in G1 eggs and how much would have been ingested by P2 females. To do this, we first determined the total numbers of eggs laid by Oncopeltus females over their lifetime in our laboratory conditions (Fig. 5). 36 single crosses were set up using 0–1 day post-molt adult females and males, with sunflower seeds, water, and cotton for egg laying. Male and female viability and number of eggs were scored every 24 h. Under these conditions, females lived an average of 67.14 ± 3.51 (SE) days (Fig. 5a). They began laying eggs 7–12 days after molting to adulthood (Fig. 5b) and continued to lay eggs for their entire lifespans, though egg laying decreased with age (Fig. 5c). On average, clutch size was 18.55 ± 0.23 (SE) embryos (Fig. 5d) and females laid an average of 913.53 ± 59.07 (SE) over their lifetimes (Fig. 5e).

Based on this, a rough estimate of the amount of dsRNA fed to P2 females via G1 eggs was calculated as follows: Each P1 female was injected with 5 µg dsRNA. If dsRNA is all transported into eggs at an equal rate over time, and each female lays ~ 900 eggs over her lifetime, each G1 egg would contain ~ 6 ng dsRNA (5 µg divided among 900 eggs). G1 eggs collected from these females were placed in a cage with 5 P2 females and 5 males. These 10 P2 adults in the cage ate 411 eggs from the Of-eve dsRNA group. Assuming males and females ate a similar number of eggs, each female ate ~ 40 eggs. Each P2 female eating 40 eggs would give her a total of ~ 240 ng dsRNA. This may be an underestimate if more dsRNA is transported into eggs during the first days of egg production, which were the eggs used for this experiment. However, this potential underestimation is balanced by processes that likely decrease the amount of dsRNA in eggs such as degradation due to factors affecting the stability of dsRNA caused by the environment, such as temperature, and after delivery to the Oncopeltus adult including passing through the digestive system. Nevertheless, the amount of dsRNA likely transmitted via the G1 eggs is within a range for which we saw effects of directly feeding dsRNA to adults (Fig. 4a), suggesting that the amount of dsRNA directly transmitted from adult to egg would be sufficient to cause the phenotypic effects seen without amplification of dsRNA or changes in chromatin necessarily playing a role.

Discussion

Susceptibility to RNAi using two well-characterized developmental regulatory genes, eve and Scr, was assessed in an Oncopeltus model using parental RNAi. The parental RNAi approach avoids analyzing animals that may be physically compromised by the injection procedure itself and also allows for scoring a large number of embryos. These features make it useful for lab studies aimed at determining parameters impacting RNAi effectiveness. Knockdown of these developmental genes resulted in specific morphological defects, facilitating our documentation of subtle and long-lasting effects due to RNAi (Fig. 2). When comparing RNAi effectiveness of developmental genes to V-ATPase, we found that responses to knockdown of developmental genes eve and Scr are evident using lower doses of dsRNA and impacts are faster than death due to V-ATPase knockdown (Fig. 3). Further, the sublethal effect of V-ATPase RNAi on oogenesis occurred more rapidly than death and the block to oogenesis can be readily quantified to test RNAi effectiveness. A number of studies in different insects, including hemipterans, have been carried out to identify genes well-suited for pest control via RNAi33,34,36,47,50(reviewed in23,52–54). Some examples are genes involved in ecdysone and juvenile hormone synthesis or signaling, that encode cytochrome P450s, and others that affect development and molting48,49,51. Other genes affect fecundity by impacting ovarian or embryonic development including the genes used in our study.

To our knowledge, this is the first report of Oncopeltus’ susceptibility to ingestion of dsRNA, with effects passed from mothers to offspring. Based on this, we asked whether dsRNA-induced effects could be transmitted via trophic exposure. We took a within-species, worst-case scenario approach to assess this in which the “predator” genome exactly matches the dsRNA administered to the “prey” because they are the same species. This represents a worst-case scenario with respect to risk assessment since in most cases, the real-life predator will not have a genome sequence exactly matching that used to target the pest insect (prey). We carried out this lab-based risk assessment assay by feeding Of-eve pRNAi-treated embryos to females never exposed to dsRNA. We then scored eggs laid by these P2 females and found ~ 10% displayed Of-eve-specific defects. These results suggest that pRNAi effects can be explained by direct transmission of dsRNA rather than by signal amplification. However, they do raise the possibility that effects of RNAi could be transmitted from prey to predatory insects in a field setting.

Despite this potential, our results suggest that trophic effects on non-target insects would be unlikely in the field: first, the amounts of dsRNA we delivered were extremely high (well over saturation) and via injection, achieving a level of dsRNA in the ‘prey’ that is unlikely to be achieved through dsRNA-based pest management. Second, this experiment was carried out within a single species (Oncopeltus eating Oncopeltus); thus, the dsRNA sequences transmitted trophically exactly matched a gene expressed in the ‘predator.’ Third, we used developmental genes that appear to be more sensitive to RNAi effects than the type of genes typically being tested for agricultural use and, even using a highly sensitive gene, saw less than 10% trophic transmission. We also note that if a pest species exhibits cannibalistic behavior and consumes their own eggs or offspring containing residual/transmitted dsRNA, this will provide an additional layer of control. This reinforces the effectiveness of RNAi insecticides through direct exposure and also through secondary (trophic) transmission. Thus, although trophic effects of dsRNA may be important to monitor in the future, we posit that the ability to generate species-specific dsRNA sequences still leaves the RNAi approach to pest management more specific and with fewer risks of non-target effects than is the case for conventional chemical pesticides.

Methods

Gene isolation

RNA was extracted from Oncopeltus fasciatus embryos 48–72 h after egg laying (AEL) and reverse transcription was performed to prepare cDNA, using M-MuLV RT (NEB) and RNase Inhibitor (NEB). To isolate even-skipped (eve) and Sex combs reduced (Scr), gene specific primers were used with Phusion or Q5 high-fidelity DNA polymerase (NEB). To isolate V-ATPase subunit A (Vha68-1), gene specific primers and 5′ and 3′ RACE were performed using the cDNA amplification kits for 5′ and 3′ RACE (Takara Bio). Gene products were purified and inserted into pGEM-T Easy Vector (Promega) by TA cloning. Products were verified by Sanger sequencing; schematics of gene structures are in Fig. S1. Primer sequences are listed in Table S4.

dsRNA synthesis

Primers with T7 promoter sequence at the 5′ ends were used to PCR amplify dsRNA templates for Of-Vha68-1, Of-eve and Of-Scr (Table S5). The PCR products were used as templates for dsRNA synthesis using the MEGAscript T7 Transcription kit (Ambion) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After ethanol precipitation, dsRNA was dissolved in injection buffer (5mM KCl, 0.1 mM phosphate buffer, pH 6.8). For feeding experiments, the dsRNA was dissolved in water. 633 bp-long gfp dsRNA was used as a negative control in all experiments. dsRNA was stored at − 20 °C. A 1:100 dilution of the dsRNA was used to run on a gel to check the quality after synthesis. This dilution was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer on the RNA setting at 260 nm; the average of 3 measurements was used to determine the concentration.

dsRNA injections

dsRNA was diluted to desired amount in injection buffer (5 mM KCl, 0.1 mM phosphate buffer, pH 6.8) with McCormick green food coloring (1:40 or 1:50 ratio) such that the desired dose could be seen and delivered in a 3 µl injection. Needles were prepared from borosilicate capillary tubes (1.0 mm OD, 0.75 mm ID, World Precision Instruments catalog # TW100-4) pulled by a program with heat at 535, pull at 90, velocity at 100, and time of 160 on the P-97 Flaming/Brown™ Micropipette Puller. The pulled needles were backloaded with the dsRNA solution using a pipette fitted with Eppendorf Microloader Tips. The needles were inserted in a microelectrode holder attached via tubing to a 60 ml syringe.

To collect females for injection, cages were synchronized from 0 to 24 egg collections. We monitored cages as soon as the last instar nymphs began to eclose to adulthood. They were collected for injection at approximately 10 days after eclosion, in keeping with the finding that egg laying begins 7 to 12 days after eclosion (Fig. 5b) and our observation that ovaries mature at that time (data not shown). They were anesthetized with CO2 and injected under a dissection microscope. They were injected horizontally using the pulled glass capillary tubes between the 4th and 5th sternites, using the pressure exerted by the syringe. Successfully injected females were placed into a small cage, containing water, sunflower seeds, paper shelter and cotton used for egg collection. One day after injection males of similar age were added to each cage at a 1:1 female to male ratio.

Assays for Of-eve, Of-Scr and Of-Vha68-1 RNAi dose and duration effects

For Fig. 2, adult females were injected with 0.1 ng, 10 ng, 40 ng, 100 ng, 400 ng, or 1000 ng dsRNA. Successfully injected females were placed individually into a vial containing water, sunflower seeds, and cotton used for egg collection. One day after injection, males were added to each female’s cage. Eggs were collected from three replicate females every 24 h for days 2–6 AEL. For each replicate female, we calculated the percentage of embryos displaying phenotypes and report means and standard errors in Fig. 2. Non-specific defects (Fig. 1d) were observed in very few embryos (0.83–3% of embryos from the 40–1000 ng injections), which precluded scoring of gene-specific defects. The number of embryos scored for each experiment is reported in Tables S1–S3. To determine whether the amount of dsRNA affects duration of dsRNA-induced defects, we injected Of-eve dsRNA at a range of doses (1 ng, 10 ng and 50 ng) and scored defects daily for 16 days after injection across 3 replicates, 5 females per replicate, pooling data every two days.

For Fig. 3, we injected 10 replicate females per dose with Of-Vha68-1 dsRNA or gfp dsRNA as a negative control, at a range of doses (0.1 ng, 1 ng, 10 ng, 40 ng, 100 ng, 400 ng, 1000 ng). Individual females were housed with single males in a vial with sunflower seeds, water and cotton for egg laying.

For Fig. 4a, adult females were starved of water and food overnight, and then fed a droplet of dsRNA dissolved in water and watched individually for 10 min to ensure consumption of the droplet. If adults did not drink within the allotted time, a new female was used. For ds-gfp, each dose was fed to five females. For ds-eve, each dose was fed to fifteen females; these were housed in three separate cages (5 females/cage). Eggs were collected for 7 days after ingestion of dsRNA.

Embryo collection and analysis

0–24-h embryos were collected, counted, and then placed in a 5 cm petri dish. Embryos were kept at 25 °C and 50% humidity, 16:8 h light: dark cycle. Six days AEL, a cotton ball soaked with water and food were added to the petri dish to reduce cannibalism, which occurred in all experiments. Eight days AEL the dish was placed in the − 20 °C for at least 5 min to halt hatchlings from moving. Hatchlings and embryos were transferred to a 1.5 ml tube and 300–400 µl of Pampel’s solution (Bioquip, catalog #1184 C) was added. Pampel’s solution was removed after 3 days and PBST was added for scoring, analysis and storage.

Photographs of embryos and hatchlings were taken in a glass well plate using a Zeiss Discovery V12 stereo microscope with color image capture using an Axiocam 506 color capture device or Olympus SZX16 microscope. For photography, hatchlings and embryos were immersed in PBST. A pair of tweezers was used to remove the chorion when needed. Some wells in the plate were partially filled with resin, making a flat, less slippery surface for securing the embryos for photographing.

Ovary dissections

To dissect Oncopeltus ovaries, females were placed in the freezer at − 20° C for ~ 5 min. Once asleep, a blade was used to remove legs and head. On a dissecting dish on ice, the abdomen was pinned down at the outer four corners. Small dissecting scissors were then used to make an incision in the abdomen, the exoskeleton pinned with needles, and ovaries were removed with a fine forceps by cutting below the oviduct and the tracheal tubes. Ovaries were placed in a well in PBST for image capture using Olympus SZX16 microscope.

Trophic exposure

The protocol for this experiment is shown schematically in Fig. 4bi. gfp and Of-eve dsRNA were each diluted in injection buffer (5 mM KCl, 0.1 mM phosphate buffer, pH 6.8) with McCormick green food coloring (1:50 ratio) such that 5000 ng of dsRNA could be delivered in a 3 µl injection. To get enough eggs for feeding, a total of 15 female adults per treatment (P1) were each injected with 5 ug Of-eve-dsRNA or gfp-dsRNA. Eggs were collected every 24 h for 12 consecutive days. Eggs collected on the first day were discarded since RNAi effects are often not observed on day 1 after injection. Embryos and injected adults were kept at 25 °C and 50% humidity, 16:8 h light: dark cycle. 25% of the eggs collected were aged to confirm eve RNAi defects. These were analyzed under a dissection scope 5 days AEL. The majority of the eggs collected (G1 eggs) were counted, colored with McCormick green food coloring, and then glued to a piece of cardboard egg carton using gluten based non-toxic wallpaper paste (Fig. 4bii) for feeding to P2 females. Before adding them to the cage, the cardboard was tapped to remove any loosely attached eggs. The G1 eggs on the cardboard were counted to keep track of the number of eggs fed to P2 females. We verified that P2 females ate these eggs (Fig. 4biii).

The same day P1 adults were injected, P2 females were separated from the colony to be fed. They were placed in a cage, made from a pipette box, with sunflower seeds, water, and cotton for egg-laying. 5 P2 females, along with 5 males, were placed in a cage per treatment: 5 females for the ds-gfp control eggs and 5 females for the Of-eve-dsRNA eggs. No food was provided for one day, to encourage them to eat eggs. An excess number of eggs was provided to each P2 cage for two days, switched to sunflower seeds for two days, and this was repeated over a period of twelve days. Offspring from P2 females (G2 eggs) were collected every 24 h, starting 48 h after the first exposure to G1 eggs; any eggs laid before this were discarded. G2 eggs were counted under a microscope, and checked for food coloring stains. Once egg collection was performed, dyed eggs on cardboard were removed, and counted to keep track of how many eggs were eaten. Cages were cleaned and any loose eggs were removed and discarded. P1 and P2 females and G1 and G2 offspring were kept in different incubators in different rooms under the same conditions to avoid contamination.
