The Effect of Use of Tricyclic Antidepressants or Serotonin‐Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitors Versus Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors on the Risk of Urinary Tract Infection Among People With Multiple Sclerosis: A Nested Case–Control Study
Authors: Melissa W Y Leung, Ewoudt M W van de Garde, Bernard M J Uitdehaag, Patrick C Souverein, Olaf H Klungel, Marloes T Bazelier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo study the effect of tricyclic antidepressant (TCA)/selective serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (SNRI) versus selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI) on the risk of urinary tract infection (UTI) among people with multiple sclerosis (MS).MethodsA case–control study was conducted using data from the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink Aurum, nested within a cohort of people with MS. Each person with a UTI was matched to ≤ 4 controls on sex, region, age (±2 years), and time since MS diagnosis (±20%). Conditional logistic regression analyses were conducted to calculate adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to compare TCA/SNRI to SSRI use and each antidepressant group to no antidepressant use. ORs were adjusted for smoking status, BMI, comorbidities, and recent drug prescriptions.ResultsTwo thousand six hundred and sixty‐four cases were matched to 3722 controls. TCA/SNRI versus SSRI use did not increase the risk of UTI (conditional aOR 1.21 [95% CI: 0.84–1.75]). TCA/SNRI use versus no antidepressant use did show an increased risk (conditional aOR [95% CI: 1.43 [1.21–1.69]), but SSRI versus no use did not (conditional aOR 1.15 [0.96–1.37]).ConclusionsThis study showed no increased risk of UTI for TCA/SNRI versus SSRI use among people with MS. However, effect estimates were imprecise due to small sample sizes.Keywords: antidepressants, clinical practice research datalink, multiple sclerosis, nested case–control study, urinary tract infection

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic demyelinating disease in which the prevalence of bladder dysfunction among people newly diagnosed with MS is up to 15% [1] and the lifetime prevalence of lower urinary tract symptoms is 80%–90% [2]. People with MS and lower urinary tract dysfunction commonly experience urinary tract infection (UTI) [3]. Moreover, MS may cause neurogenic bladder, a risk factor of UTI [4]. The burden of UTI on people with MS is reflected in the associated mortality and hospitalization rates [5, 6, 7] as well as in primary care: a previous study reported over twice as many UTI‐related physician visits among people with MS as among the general population in Canada [8], and we found that UTIs pose a particular challenge in the outpatient infection management among people with MS in the Netherlands [9]. In line with this, we observed in a recent study using primary care data from the United Kingdom (UK) Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum and GOLD an increased rate of UTI among people with MS compared to people with RA and the general population [10].

People with MS may also experience neuropathic pain and depression. The global prevalence of chronic neuropathic pain among people with MS is 50%–86% [11] and the global prevalence of depression among people with MS is approximately 23.7% [12]. Pharmacological treatment of neuropathic pain and depression in MS includes antidepressants, mainly tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), the serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) venlafaxine and duloxetine, and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) [11, 13].

There may be a link between the choice of an antidepressant and the risk of developing UTIs for people with MS. It is known that TCAs and SNRIs can cause urinary retention: TCAs because of their anticholinergic effects [14] and SNRIs because they increase the levels of serotonin and norepinephrine in the spinal cord [15]. Urinary retention reduces the washout of bacteria and is thus a risk factor of UTI [4]. Further, a recent systematic review with meta‐analysis reported an odds ratio (OR) [95% confidence interval (CI)] of 3.01 [1.37–6.64] of voiding disorder on TCA versus SSRI, and 8.87 [2.27–34.64] on SNRI versus SSRI [15].

Although it is known that the use of TCA/SNRI versus SSRI increases the risk of urinary retention, it is unknown whether it also increases the risk of UTI. This knowledge could be relevant for people with MS who are particularly at risk of UTI and in whom initiation with antidepressant treatment is considered. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate whether the use of TCA/SNRI versus SSRI has an effect on the risk of UTI among people with MS.

Methods

Data Source

The data source for this study was the United Kingdom Clinical Practice Research Datalink Aurum, which contains electronic healthcare records from routine clinical care from general practitioners in the UK. These include diagnoses and prescriptions from primary care and diagnoses from secondary care of over 16 million people, covering approximately 24% of the English population [16].

Study Population

A cohort of people with MS was identified by the first‐ever diagnosis of MS within the observation period: 1 January 2000 until 30 April 2024. The start of the observation was the date of the first‐ever record of MS diagnosis (MS date). The end of the observation period was the death date, date of deregistration from the practice, the end of data availability, or the end of the observation period (whichever occurred first). People with MS were eligible for inclusion in the cohort if they were at least 18 years old on the MS date, had 1 year of medical history prior to the MS date, no history of malignancy, catheter use, or urolithiasis prior to the MS date (measured ever before), and at least 12 UTI‐free months prior to the MS date.

Cases and controls were sampled from the cohort of people with MS. Cases were people who experienced a UTI, identified by the first occurrence of either (1) an antibiotic [17] prescription that was preceded within 7 days by a UTI diagnosis, symptom or laboratory test (see Supporting Information file 1 for code lists), or (2) a nitrofurantoin prescription, which is only indicated for UTI [18]. In both scenarios, the index date was thus defined as the date of the antibiotic prescription. Each case was matched to up to four controls through risk‐set sampling on the matching criteria determined on index date: age (±2 years), MS duration (i.e., time since MS date, ±20%), sex, and region.

Exposure

Antidepressant exposure, restricted to TCA/SNRI and SSRI, was ascertained by prescription records. People taking antidepressants other than TCAs (ATC code starting with N06AA), SSRIs (ATC code starting with N06AB), and SNRIs (ATC codes N06AX16 and N06AX21) were excluded from the “no exposure” group (all other ATC codes starting with N06A). Exposure windows of TCA/SNRI and SSRI were constructed by defining the exposure start date as the prescription date and the exposure end date as the start date plus duration as recorded by the GP, with records of unlikely duration of < 7 or > 112 days set to 28 days. Overlaps in exposure between prescriptions of the same drug were added at the end of the last prescription; a switch between different drugs of the same class was considered an immediate switch, i.e., with no adding of overlap. The maximum “permissible gap” considered exposed time between two windows was 30 days [19]. The exposure status on the index date was “current” if the index date was within the antidepressant exposure window and “no” otherwise. The antidepressant exposure categories were: TCA/SNRI, SSRI, and no antidepressant exposure. People with simultaneous exposure to TCA/SNRI and SSRI on the index date were excluded.

Covariates

Smoking, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, recent use of antibiotics, and other medication classes were selected as covariates. Smoking status and BMI are considered possible risk factors for UTI [20]. Comorbidities, a recent antibiotic prescription as a proxy for a recent infection, and several recent other drug prescriptions were chosen because they were identified as risk factors of infection among people with MS in our previous study using CPRD data [10].

Smoking status (current/past or never/unknown) was determined up to 1 year prior to the index date, BMI up to 2 years prior, and comorbidity (yes/no) ever before. Comorbidities included the following conditions: diabetes mellitus, chronic lung disease, inflammatory bowel disease, rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, kidney disease, and heart disease. Prescriptions (yes/no) for antibiotics and symptomatic treatment (i.e., treatments for migraine, sexual dysfunction, dystonia, neuropathic pain, tremor, epilepsy, enuresis and muscle stiffness and spasms) were assessed up to 12 months prior to the index date. In the baseline table, prescriptions of drugs with an immunomodulatory effect and corticosteroids were assessed up to 90 days prior to the index date.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive Statistics

Descriptive statistics were calculated and presented separately for cases and controls: median and interquartile range (IQR) of age, MS duration, and BMI; and percentages of people with index date in 6‐year calendar periods, female sex, each category of smoking status, each comorbidity, and each recent drug prescription.

TCA/SNRI Versus SSRI Exposure and UTI

We conducted conditional logistic regression analyses to calculate the OR of UTI with a 95% CI per contrast of antidepressant exposure. In the primary analysis, the risk of UTI while on TCA/SNRI was compared to the risk of UTI while on SSRI. In the secondary analysis, the risk of UTI while on TCA/SNRI and the risk of UTI while on SSRI were compared to the risk of UTI while on no antidepressant. For both analyses, ORs were adjusted for smoking status, BMI, comorbidity (yes/no), recent antibiotic prescription, and recent symptomatic drug prescription.

Post Hoc Analyses

In the main analysis, many cases and controls were dropped because they did not form a pair, i.e., either the case or the control was not exposed to an antidepressant. Therefore we conducted a post hoc analysis in which all cases and controls were maintained, using an unconditional logistic regression model. The matching variables were included in these analyses as covariates to calculate ORs adjusted for sex, region, age (continuous), and MS duration (continuous); in addition to smoking status, BMI, comorbidity, recent antibiotic prescription, and recent symptomatic drug prescription.

Sensitivity Analyses

Two sensitivity analyses were conducted. In the first one, the definition of “current exposure” was identical to the main analysis, while “no exposure” was categorized as “never exposure” (no prescriptions recorded), “recent exposure” (0–90 days after the last day of the intended prescription duration) and “past exposure” (> 90 days after the last day of the finished prescription). A second sensitivity analysis investigated a possible time lag between antidepressant initiation and its effect on UTI risk, and a continued effect following antidepressant cessation: “current exposure” was defined as exposure between day 14 after initiation and 30 days after the last prescription day. All other time was defined as “no exposure.”

Results

Study Population

Of the 24 765 of people with a first record of MS during the study period, 11 348 (45.8%) met the cohort eligibility criteria. Four thousand four hundred and thirty‐five people (39.1% of the cohort) had a first UTI during follow‐up, of whom 2664 (23.5% of the cohort) could be matched as cases to 3722 controls. Sixty‐three cases and 85 controls had exposure to both TCA/SNRI and SSRI on the index date and were excluded. Thirty‐eight cases and 41 controls had exposure to another antidepressant on the index date and were excluded. Two thousand five hundred and seven cases remained in the analysis set, matched to 3434 controls (Figure 1). 1851 (73.8%) cases were matched to 1 control, 449 (17.9%) to 2 controls, 143 (5.7%) to 3 controls, and 64 (2.6%) to 4 controls. 1890 (75.4%) cases and 2378 (69.2%) controls were women. Post Hoc Analyses

The risk of UTI while on TCA/SNRI use versus the risk while on SSRI was investigated further in post hoc unconditional logistic regression analyses, which included all antidepressant‐exposed cases and controls from the secondary analysis and included the initial matching variables, i.e., sex, region, age, and MS duration as additional covariates. The unconditional analysis showed similar results as presented in Table 2, only with more precision: aOR 1.19 [0.97–1.46] (Table S1).

The effect of each antidepressant group versus no antidepressant on the risk of UTI was also similar in the unconditional analysis to the conditional analysis (Table S2).

Sensitivity Analyses

The sensitivity analysis with alternative exposure categorization yielded results similar to the main analysis (Tables S3–S5 in the Supporting Information results).

Discussion

In our study, we found that the use of TCA/SNRI versus SSRI did not increase the risk of UTI in people with MS. The risk was higher with the use of TCA/SNRI versus no antidepressant, compared to SSRI versus no antidepressant, but the limitations of using an unexposed comparator group should be taken into account there.

An increased risk of UTI while on TCA/SNRI versus SSRI was expected based on existing literature. A recent meta‐analysis reported an increased risk of voiding disorder, a risk factor for UTI, for both TCA and SNRI compared to SSRI, and both for TCA and for SNRI versus placebo [15]. It could be that TCA/SNRI versus SSRI does increase the risk of UTI among people with MS, but that the sample size in this study was too small to detect a difference. We did not observe an increased risk of UTI while on SSRI compared to no antidepressant, while an observational cohort study using data from the Dutch PHARMO Database Network reported a relative risk of 1.61 [95% CI: 1.41–1.82] for urinary incontinence due to SSRI use [21]. Although urinary incontinence is a risk factor of UTI, it could be that the increased risk of urinary incontinence due to SSRI use does not directly translate into an increased risk of UTI, or not as strongly as urinary retention. The antidepressant user prevalence across cases and controls was 25.6%, similar to the 26.8% during the year up to the first recorded MS diagnosis in another study of people with MS using CPRD data (2001–2016) [22].

Further research is needed to study the effect of antidepressant use on the risk of UTI among people with MS. If it becomes clear that among people with MS the use of TCA/SNRI compared to SSRI increases the risk of UTI, choosing an SSRI may be preferred in people with MS who are particularly at risk of UTI and in whom initiation of antidepressant treatment is considered. This may help limit the burden of UTI itself as well as the risk of infection‐triggered MS relapse or pseudo‐relapse [23]. Further study of the risk of UTI while on antidepressant may also provide insight into the mechanisms that result in the increased risk of UTI among people with MS. Such insights may help clarify whether the effects of antidepressants on the risk of UTI are homogeneous in the population with MS, or whether TCA/SNRI use could affect the risk of UTI among some people more than others because of the course of the underlying disease, presence of other UTI risk factors, or other personal characteristics.

A strength of this study was that we used not only UTI diagnosis records confirmed by antibiotic prescription records to ascertain cases of UTI [18], but also nitrofurantoin prescription records independent of UTI diagnosis records. The definition of UTI in studies using routine electronic health records is usually based on a combination of diagnostic codes with antibiotic prescription or urinalysis, but none has been formally validated. The reliance on UTI diagnostic codes may lead to misclassification of people with antibiotic prescriptions for UTI but without a diagnosis of UTI. Including nitrofurantoin prescription to the case definition of UTI thus lowered the likelihood both of misclassification of cases as controls and of selection bias: antibiotic prescriptions, as any prescriptions, are recorded reliably in CPRD regardless of antidepressant exposure status.

A major limitation of the study was the relatively small number of antidepressant users and therefore effect estimates and confidence intervals were imprecise. To mitigate this, we conducted post hoc unconditional analyses in which we included all antidepressant‐exposed cases and controls. As the matching between cases and controls was broken in the unconditional analyses, the matching variables were added as potential confounder variables, in addition to the other covariates. More research remains needed to provide conclusive evidence. Secondly, confounding bias due to unmeasured variables could have affected the results, as little individual‐level data is available in the CPRD on immunomodulatory drug prescription, disease‐modifying treatment use and (MS‐related) disability. The preclusion of a measure of MS disease severity (direct or indirect) raises concern for residual confounding, possibly by indication, as it could be speculated that more advanced MS could be associated with higher prevalence of both UTI and indication for TCA/SNRI or SSRI. Especially in the analyses that used an unexposed comparator group, this type of residual confounding is an important limitation. The higher UTI risks that were found for TCA/SNRI use versus no antidepressant use should therefore be interpreted with caution. Also in the active comparative design, treatment choices may have been made differently for patients with more severe MS and/or depression. We did adjust for possible confounding by adjusting for a recent symptomatic drug prescription as a proxy variable for level of disability. Age, sex, and MS duration were also accounted for as possible confounding variables: as matching variables and as potential confounding variables in the unmatched analysis.

Among people with MS, both the antidepressant use and the burden of UTI are high. This study showed no increased risk of UTI for TCA/SNRI versus SSRI use among people with MS, but the sample size was limited in this active comparator design. Further research is needed for conclusive evidence of the effect of different antidepressants on the risk of UTI among people with MS.

Plain Language Summary

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a disease in which one's own immune system disturbs message transmission in the brain and spinal cord. This can cause pain and depression. People with MS who experience pain, depression, or both may be prescribed antidepressant medications by their doctor. These antidepressants can be tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), serotonin‐norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), or selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). TCAs and SNRIs, because of how they work, can cause incomplete bladder emptying and might therefore increase the risk of urinary tract infection (UTI). It is already known that people with MS experience a high burden of UTI. We used general practitioners' data from the United Kingdom to investigate whether people with MS who use a TCA or SNRI are at a higher risk of experiencing UTI than people with MS who use an SSRI. We could not confirm a possible effect of TCA or SNRI use versus SSRI use on the risk of UTI among people with MS, but this may be due to limited sample size. This is important for those people with MS for whom UTI is a particular concern and who consider starting antidepressant therapy.
