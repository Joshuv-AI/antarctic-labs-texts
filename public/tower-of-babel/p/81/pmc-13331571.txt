Interplay of Probe Stability and Oscillation During Very‐High‐Power Short‐Duration Ablation in Pulmonary Vein Isolation for Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Ernesto Cristiano, Eduardo Celentano, Barbara Ignatiuk, Martina Renda, Elena Bia, Raffaele Rainone, Jacopo Viganò, Natasja M. S. De Groot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundVery‐high‐power‐short‐duration (vHPSD) ablation is increasingly adopted for pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) in atrial fibrillation (AF). While inadequate catheter–tissue contact is known to impair lesion formation under fixed 90 W/4‐s setting, independent role of probe stability versus rapid oscillatory motion has not been quantitatively defined.ObjectiveTo evaluate the independent and interactive effects of contact force (CF), positional stability (PS/PSV), and oscillation (OS/OSV) on lesion biophysics during vHPSD ablation in vivo.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 80 consecutive AF patients undergoing PVI with a QDOT‐MICRO catheter in vHPSD‐mode (90 W/4‐s, temperature‐controlled). For each application (5198), CARTO3 trace data provided impedance, temperature, tip‐to‐tag distance, and CF (62.5Hz‐sample). Stability parameters were defined as mean and variability of tip‐to‐tag distance (PS, PSV); oscillation parameters as mean and variability of tip velocity (OS, OSV). Primary endpoints were impedance drop (ID) and maximum temperature (Tmax). Mixed‐effects regression models adjusted for baseline impedance, temperature, and CF; sensitivity analyses tested operator effect, and regional segments.ResultsMinimum CF (p < 0.001) and oscillation parameters (OS p < 0.001, OSV p = 0.004) were the strongest predictors of ID and Tmax, whereas stability parameters had attenuated effects after adjustment. Interactions showed that increasing PSV and OSV reduced ID and Tmax, especially under higher CF. Regional analysis confirmed modestly lower signals in posterior/inferior segments, but oscillation remained the main determinant.ConclusionOscillation, more than positional stability, is the primary motion determinant of lesion efficacy in vHPSD. Minimizing oscillation rather than increasing CF is key to optimizing energy delivery. Prospective studies should assess whether reducing oscillation improves long‐term outcomes.

Introduction

Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) using radiofrequency (RF) is the gold standard treatment for symptomatic paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF) [1, 2]. The application of standardized techniques using open‐irrigated catheters and contact force (CF) sensors for reducing procedure time and fluoroscopy exposure has gained attention. These methods can improve temperature control at the catheter tip‐tissue interface, thereby reducing the risks of thrombosis, char formation, and atrial wall perforation [3].

In this sense, the introduction of very‐high‐power short‐duration (vHPSD, defined as 90 W for 4 s in temperature‐controlled mode) technologies has reduced RF application time to as low as 4 s [4, 5]. In addition, owing to the multi‐thermocouple system situated on the surface of the probe tip (i.e., temperature‐control system), the QDOT MICRO Catheter (Biosense Webster, Irvine, CA, USA), which is a novel open‐irrigated CF sensor catheter, automatically modulates power if the maximum temperature cutoff (60°C) is reached [6]. Indeed, the modulation of the power and flow around the target temperature is important for not only decreasing the risk of adverse events, like pericardial effusion or popping phenomena, but also improving lesion formation in the myocardium [7, 8, 9]. As a result, a more uniform lesion formation can be achieved by controlling and standardizing the ablation procedure. Similarly, monitoring the conductivity changes caused by resistive heating is a well‐established method for assessing tissue response during lesion formation [7, 10]. This variation can predict the long‐term efficacy of PVI in various clinical scenarios [11, 12]. This phenomenon is referred to as impedance drop (ID). Therefore, Tmax and ID are the two main biophysical indicators of lesion formation during RF application and energy titration.

The expertise of an operator in probe manipulation remains essential for regulating two critical factors: the CF between the endocardium and the catheter tip and the stability of the probe during RF application.

In relation to this, the minimum CF has been considered a pivotal factor in obtaining an adequate ID [13], resulting in stable and more extensive myocardial lesions [14, 15]. Notably, probe stability is the most essential element influencing lesion homogeneity and first‐pass isolation rate according to various studies [16, 17, 18] and the European Heart Rhythm Association/Heart Rhythm Society AF ablation consensus [2]. However, current research has not comprehensively explored the importance of maintaining a stable catheter tip position during vHPSD mode ablation, even though it had promising outcomes [14, 19].

While it is intuitive that suboptimal coupling impairs energy transfer during 90 W/4 s vHPSD, prior work has not quantified tip micro‐motion speed independently from positional stability nor linked it—in adjusted models—to ID/Tmax. We therefore introduce oscillation speed (OS) and its variation (OSV) (see Methods for more details) as complementary motion domains and test their association with biophysical markers in vivo.

Consequently, this retrospective observational study aimed to investigate the following: The influence of the probe tip position and its variation/oscillation during vHPSD mode ablation on predicting the biophysical characteristics of the resulting endocardial lesion.The interaction between these parameters and the applied CF.

Methods

This retrospective, single‐center observational analysis evaluated consecutive patients with symptomatic paroxysmal and persistent AF treated at Humanitas Gavazzeni, Bergamo, Italy, using the QDOT MICRO (Biosense Webster, Inc., Irvine, CA, the USA) irrigated catheter between December 2022 and May 2024. The study protocol was approved by the local institutional review board and performed in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. In addition, the study was conducted in compliance with good clinical practice.

Ethical approval was obtained from the IRCCS Humanitas Clinical Institute Rozzano Ethics Committee (protocol no. 32/23 GAV). All patients provided written informed consent.

The trial design and statistical analysis of this study followed the STROBE statement for observational research [20].

Study Population

The study population comprised adults (aged ≥ 18 years) who were diagnosed with symptomatic AF according to the European Society of Cardiology guidelines [21] and had an electrocardiographically documented episode within 6 months before enrollment. All participants in the study did not respond to or could not tolerate at least one antiarrhythmic medication classified as Class I or III. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients who had a previously undergone surgical ablation for AF; (2) those who had undergone coronary artery bypass grafting, carotid stenting, or endarterectomy within the last 6 months; (3) those who had left atrial thrombus on preprocedural imaging; (4) those who had a left ventricular ejection fraction <40%; (5) those who had a left atrial diameter ≥50 mm; (6) those who had a thromboembolic event within the past 12 months; and (7) those who had an uncontrolled New York Heart Association functional Class III or IV heart failure.

Ablation Procedure

All procedures were conducted under general anesthesia, following an anesthesiologic protocol for induction that included a combination of midazolam for anxiolysis, along with fentanyl, propofol, rocuronium, lidocaine, ketamine, and magnesium sulfate. During mechanical ventilation, high‐frequency low tidal volume (HFLTV) ventilation was adopted to improve catheter stability [22]. For transseptal puncture, continuous transesophageal echocardiography was conducted according to the current procedural guidelines [23] to assist puncture and reduce the risk of complications and catheter entrapment [24].

All procedures were conducted while patients were in spontaneous sinus rhythm: those who presented with AF underwent electrical cardioversion prior to the initiation of the procedure.

Electroanatomical mapping was performed using the CARTO 3V7 system with a PENTARAY high‐density catheter. The ablation strategy required point‐by‐point, wide‐area, circumferential PVI (Figure S1). The QDM (D–F curves) probe was operated in the QMODE+ mode for all applications, utilizing the nGEN RF Generator. vHPSD ablation was conducted using settings similar to those of the QDOT‐FAST protocol [5]. The target temperature for temperature‐controlled ablation was set at 55°C with a cutoff of 60°C based on the hottest surface thermocouple. For the anterior wall of the left atrium, the inter‐TAG distance (calculated from the center of the adjacent ablation tags) was ≤4 mm. Otherwise, it was ≤6 mm. In the QMODE+ mode, power and irrigation were adjusted based on the target temperature. At 2 s before RF delivery, the irrigation flow rate was increased from 2 to 8 mL/min to cool the tissue, and 90 W of RF energy was applied for 4 s. If the target temperature (55°C) was reached during ablation, the flow rate was adjusted to maintain the temperature below the target. Conversely, if the temperature continued to rise despite irrigation, the power was reduced to maintain the target temperature. After energy delivery, the flow rate remained at 8 mL/min for an additional 4 s before reverting to 2 mL/min.

After ablation, all linear lesions underwent confirmation of bidirectional conduction block by mapping and pacing maneuvers [2]. All procedures were performed by three experienced operators (>300 AF ablations each). Operator‐specific variability was tested in a mixed‐effects model with operator as a random effect (see Statistical methods).

Data Collection

Clinical data were collected during outpatient visits and hospitalization. Ablation data were obtained from the CARTO 3 V7 system, which recorded trace‐level data such as impedance, temperature, tip and tag position, and CF at a very high sampling rate (every 0.016 s, storing 250 data points per energy application). RF applications were excluded from analysis in case of non‐protocol deliveries, that is, vHPSD applications that did not complete 4 s (intentional interruption/repositioning) or showed clear technical artefacts (sudden loss of position/temperature signal, irrigation/power alarms) or loss of contact for more than 20% of application. Starting impedance and temperature were the respective values at the start of RF. Instead, Tmax was the highest temperature recorded during RF. ID was calculated as the difference between the starting and lowest impedance values. The mean CF was calculated as the integral of the CF divided by the application time. In addition, the position‐derived data were position stability (PS), defined as the mean Euclidean distance tip‐to‐tag calculated as the integral of the tip‐to‐tag distance divided by the application time; position stability variation (PSV), defined as the standard deviation of the tip‐to‐tag distance; oscillation speed (OS), defined as the mean absolute velocity of the catheter tip, calculated as first derivative of position in time; and oscillation speed variation (OSV), defined as the standard deviation of the tip velocity, all calculated during the ablation application. PS and PSV represent the stability parameters, OS and OSV represent the oscillation parameters.

To sum up, position‐derived metrics quantified two orthogonal motion domains. Stability was the time‐averaged spatial deviation from the VisiTag reference: PS (mm), the mean tip‐to‐tag distance over the application, and PSV (mm), its standard deviation. Oscillation was the instantaneous speed of the tip irrespective of where it sat on average: OS (mm/s), the mean absolute tip velocity, and OSV (mm/s), its standard deviation. All metrics were computed at 62.5 Hz (Δt = 0.016 s) over the RF epoch (see Figure 1).

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was calculated based on previously published data [7, 13, 14]. Considering energy applications at approximately 60 RF per patient and 10% expected data loss, the analysis required approximately 4500 RF applications in 75 patients. This sample size could provide 95% power and a two‐sided alpha error of 0.05 to detect a 10% decrease in ID and temperature rise, based on the distance between the probe and tissue and probe stability. Calculations were performed using simulations of the linear effect models implemented in R 4.4.1.

Statistical Methods

Descriptive data were reported as the proportions or means ± standard deviation for continuous variables with normal distribution, and as the medians and interquartile ranges for non‐parametric variables, as appropriate. The normality of the data was evaluated using indirect methods, including kurtosis and symmetry indices, as well as the Shapiro–Wilk test or the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, as applicable based on the degree of freedom. Between‐group differences were assessed using the Student's t‐test for variables with normal distribution and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non‐parametric variables, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson's χ 2 test, Fisher's exact test, or continuity correction, as appropriate. To address missing data, the imputation process utilized the chained random forest technique. To assess how the single position and CF variables affected the ID and Tmax, the mixed‐effects linear regression model was utilized. These models, adjusted for baseline impedance and temperature values, were used to calculate the beta coefficient (β) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The models incorporated fixed effects, including baseline impedance, starting temperature and CF, position stability of the tip and OS data, while treating patients as random effects. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to explain the variance data attributed to the differences among patients. Values of 0.10, 0.10–0.30, 0.30–0.50, and >0.50 were considered negligible, mild, moderate, and high, respectively.

Primary outcomes were ID and Tmax; key predictors were PS/PSV and OS/OSV computed at 62.5 Hz over each 90 W/4 s application. Mixed‐effects models adjusted for baseline impedance and temperature; a MANCOVA evaluated joint effects on ID and Tmax and interaction terms with minimum CF. All clinical and procedural variables that had demonstrated a p value <0.10 in the univariate analysis were included in the model. In the multivariate analysis, variables that exhibited significant multicollinearity, determined by a variance inflation factor >10, were discarded.

To evaluate the secondary outcomes, interaction models between the CF, stability, and oscillation variables were obtained and graphically represented for better clarity.

Finally, a variance‐based sensitivity analysis using the Sobol method was conducted, and to account for potential clustering by operator, we added operator as a random intercept to the mixed‐effects models. As an additional sensitivity analysis, we evaluated PVI segment (anterior, superior, posterior, inferior on each PV site) as a potential confounder of the biophysical endpoints and as an effect modifier of the studied PS, PSV, OS, OSV. We refit linear mixed‐effects models with a random intercept for patient, adjusting for baseline temperature (Tmax models) or baseline impedance (ID models) and minimum CF; anterior served as the reference segment. Statistical significance of fixed effects and interaction terms were assessed with two‐sided Wald tests. Influential outlier observations were screened using hat leverage (hII ) and Cook's distance (DI ) computed from linear models for ID and Tmax. A priori, observations with hII > 2p/n or DI > 4/n (where p is the number of parameters and n the number of applications) were excluded.

Sensitivity analyses retaining all observations were also performed.

All analyses were performed using the RStudio statistical software (version 4.4.1, R Development Core Team; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). A p value of 0.05 indicated statistically significant differences.

Biophysical Parameters, Tip Stability, and Oscillation Parameters

Data from 5198 RF applications were collected and analyzed. Position, impedance, and temperature were recorded at 0.016 s intervals, yielding 1,214,791 data points for each biophysical parameter.

The initial mean impedance and temperature readings were 113 ± 15 Ω and 35°C ± 1°C, respectively. Results showed that the average ID was 11 ± 4 Ω and a Tmax was 50°C ± 4°C. The PS was 2.4 ± 4.6 mm, with a variation (PSV) of 6 ± 3 mm. The average OS was calculated at 1.3 ± 2.9 mm/s, while OSV was 8.5 ± 14 mm/s. The CF values ranged from 4 ± 3 to 17.3 ± 10.4 g, with an average of 9.5 ± 4.6 g.

Interaction Effects

The effects of the interplay between the minimum applied CF and the stability and oscillation parameters on temperature elevation and impedance reduction were investigated.

For Tmax, the stability parameter only PSV, but not PS, showed interaction with the minimum CF (p < 0.001 and 0.253, respectively; Figure 2A,B). Additionally, both oscillation variables (OS and OSV) exhibited significant interactions with CF (p < 0.001 for both; Figure 2C,D).

Based on these results, in cases where statistical significance was observed, increasing instability and oscillation of the probe tip resulted in decreased Tmax and ID. This effect became more evident as the minimum applied CF increased.

Sensitivity analysis confirmed that the results were independent from the operator (all p > 0.05), and outlier analysis confirmed that our results are not driven by those points.

Regional Analysis Along the PVI Lines

In the sensitivity analysis for regional differences, posterior and inferior segments showed modestly lower biophysical signals versus anterior (reference): Tmax −0.40°C (95% CI −0.60 to −0.20; p < 0.001) and −0.33°C (−0.55 to −0.11; p = 0.003), respectively; ID −0.34 Ω (−0.56 to −0.12; p = 0.002) and −0.30 Ω (−0.52 to −0.08; p = 0.010) (Table S2). Superior did not differ significantly from anterior. There were no differences by PV laterality (global interaction p > 0.05). Site‐by‐metric terms indicated limited effect modification that did not alter the main inferences: the association of oscillation (OS/OSV) with biophysics was more pronounced in superior/inferior segments for Tmax, and OS was slightly reduced for ID in non‐anterior segments (Table S3A,B).

Discussion

Stability and Oscillation

This study is the first to show in vivo direct association between probe position, new oscillation measurements during vHPSD ablation, as well as ID and Tmax, which are the two key biophysical markers of myocardial lesion development and adds quantitative evidence that OS, distinct from positional stability, is a significant motion determinant of ID/Tmax in vHPSD. Previous studies on catheter stability only considered the average distance from the tag or maximum displacement as stability metrics [12, 14]. In addition, Aizer et al. recommended the use of the standard deviation of CF as an indirect measure of oscillation [16]. However, these earlier investigations did not link these parameters to the biophysical indicators of lesion formation.

This study introduces OS and OSV as novel in vivo validated metrics, which capture rapid micro‐movements not reflected by conventional stability measures (Figure 1).

Second, based on the multivariate analysis (Table 4), probe stability had a minimal impact on ID and Tmax. Only substantial probe movements (>9 mm) can lead to inadequate temperature increases caused by the efficient temperature‐control system [5, 9]. Conversely, rapid oscillations have a greater influence on temperature rise (Figure 2). A comparable pattern is observed for ID (Figure 3). The temperature‐controlled system likely modulates power automatically if the maximum temperature threshold is reached within certain limits. However, excessive probe movements result in energy and heat dispersion that cannot be compensated for by the automatic power delivery system.

Third, our research further examined the effects of probe tip stability and oscillation, thereby distinguishing these two concepts and introducing two oscillation parameters: OS and its standard deviation (OSV) as accurate factors.

The abovementioned findings contribute to our understanding of vHPSD ablation biophysics. A probe tip with a minimal average distance from the target tissue and rapid oscillations around the equilibrium point may become ineffective, thereby confirming the differences between stability and oscillation concepts.

Stability, Oscillation, and Minimum Contact Force Interaction

Our findings further substantiated the importance of minimum CF as the most influential factor in impedance reduction and effective lesion creation, which is in accordance with previous clinical research [13, 14]. Notably, this study provided initial insights into how minimum CF interacts with various aspects of probe stability and oscillation to determine ID and Tmax during vHPSD ablation procedures. Interestingly, in cases of low probe stability, increasing the CF not only fails to enhance ID but actually diminishes it (Figure 3). Tmax had a comparable negative effect (Figure 2). The parameters that present the major effect for both ID and Tmax were OSV and PSV, one from stability and one from oscillation parameters, confirming the role of both in the lesion formation.

These observations indicate that applying excessive CF to the probe not only lacks benefit but also possibly hinders lesion formation, thereby emphasizing the need for a delicate, refined approach [13]. The intricate interplay between probe stability, oscillation, and CF underscores the importance of meticulous operator techniques and vigilant monitoring of these biophysical parameters during ablation to ensure optimal energy delivery and effective lesion formation. Maximizing CF is ineffective when the probe is unstable; reducing oscillation (OS/OSV) yields larger gains in ID/Tmax than increasing CF in those settings. Rather, a more comprehensive grasp of the complex biophysical factors at play is essential.

Regional Differences in vHPSD

Consistent with prior observations of regional heterogeneity along the PVI line, we found modestly lower Tmax and ID at posterior/inferior segments versus anterior/superior (≈ 0.3°C and 0.3 Ω on average), with no clinically meaningful effect. This pattern aligns with clinical data showing lower impedance responses at posterior/inferior [25], and likely reflects thinner posterior tissue [26]. Importantly, oscillation (OS/OSV) remained the principal motion correlate across regions; site primarily modulated the magnitude, not the direction, of the associations.

Practical and Future Implications

Rather than asserting a qualitative relationship that high‐frequency vibration impairs lesions—a known concept under fixed 4‐s vHPSD—our data provide in vivo, quantitative evidence that OS is the principal motion correlate of ID/Tmax, independent of and stronger than positional stability metrics in multivariable models. Practically, when OS/OSV are elevated, increasing CF does not rescue ID/Tmax. These metrics therefore offer actionable intra‐procedural guidance. One approach that can accomplish this involves the use of a steerable sheath, which enables better adaptation to the atrial wall, thereby enhancing stability without significantly increasing CF and lowering AF recurrence rates [27, 28]. This type of sheath allows operators to navigate the complex atrial anatomy more effectively, reduce sudden movements, and potentially improve lesion formation and procedural efficacy.

In addition, general anesthesia may be a potential strategy that can reduce patient movement and enhance probe stability during ablation. Data in literature on this topic remains controversial. However, some studies have shown that mild conscious sedation for vHPSD is feasible and has promising outcomes at 1‐year follow‐up [29]. A recent Danish nationwide cohort study has revealed that general anesthesia can be an independent protective factor of AF recurrence, decreasing the risk by up to approximately 25% [30]. In particular, the use of high‐frequency ventilation and rapid pacing enhances catheter stability and long‐term outcomes [16, 18]. This effect may be partly attributed to a regular respiratory pattern, decreased beating excursion, and reduced patient movement under general anesthesia, which could improve probe stability and maintain oscillations below an acceptable threshold. This may lead to a more effective lesion formation and procedural success. In the authors’ case series, all patients received general anesthesia. This study did not primarily focus on clinical outcomes. However, the findings emphasize the significance of high probe stability and minimal oscillations, similar to those observed during mechanical ventilation. Nevertheless, a prospective randomized study should be performed to identify the superiority of general anesthesia in vHPSD ablation, with a specific assessment of probe stability and oscillation parameters to elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying any between‐group differences.

One more solution could be switching to standard‐power/AI or repeating vHPSD at the same site.

As suggested by Falasconi et al. in the randomized QDOT‐by‐LAWT trial [26], a hybrid, anatomy‐guided strategy that alternates vHPSD (90 W/4 s) with standard‐power AI‐targeted applications in thicker segments achieved non‐inferior 12‐month arrhythmia‐free survival compared with the CLOSE protocol, while markedly reducing procedure and RF times and without safety trade‐offs. These data support an intra‐procedural switch when local conditions predict suboptimal vHPSD energy transfer—for example, when high OS/OSV persist despite adequate contact—rather than indiscriminately repeating vHPSD at the same site.

The findings can also provide crucial insights that can be utilized to enhance vHPSD technology. These results suggest the following potential avenues for advancement: Stability and oscillation monitoring system: The correlations between catheter stability and oscillation, as well as lesion formation, indicate the feasibility of developing a real‐time monitoring mechanism. This system could offer immediate feedback to clinicians during vHPSD procedures, thereby potentially improving ablation accuracy and efficacy.vHPSD real‐time scoring mechanism: Inspired by the Ablation Index used in standard power ablation [31, 32], a comparable scoring mechanism for vHPSD could be established. This system would likely integrate the key parameters identified in this research, which include catheter stability, oscillation metrics, and CF, to deliver a comprehensive, real‐time evaluation of the quality of lesion formation.

Although a new algorithm shows variations in stability and tag annotation [19], it does not offer direct proof of enhanced clinical outcomes or the durability of lesions. The creation of new, comprehensive, and robust tools should be explored further. Additionally, validation studies and clinical trials are necessary to evaluate their reliability, effectiveness, and impact on medical practice.

Strengths and Limitations

Although this study used an innovative approach and comprehensively collected data, it still had several limitations. In particular, it was retrospective nature and had a single‐center design, which can potentially limit its widespread applicability. Our findings pertain to the 90 W/4 s temperature‐controlled implementation evaluated here and should not be extrapolated to other power‐time settings without dedicated study. Notably, 25% to 40% of the variance is attributed to individual differences (Table S1), thereby indicating that anatomical features such as atrial dimensions, venous configuration, atrial fat content, catheter‐tissue orientation and scarring can significantly affect the observed impedance reduction and temperature peaks. Moreover, this study was based on generator ID and surface thermocouples Tmax, two validated surrogate indicators of myocardial injury, without directly assessing lesion characteristics (like local ID, intramyocardial temperature, scar extension): definitive validation would require histological or outcome‐based studies.

Finally, the nGEN RF Generator only provides generator impedance rather than local impedance, which is the most predictive parameter for lesion formation. Nevertheless, it remains the most effective surrogate for local impedance and lesion formation itself [33].

With consideration of these limitations, definitive conclusions about the long‐term clinical efficacy cannot be made. Despite these drawbacks, the current study also had several strengths. For example, it is an original study, and extensive measurements were performed. Finally, the parameters that were analyzed were relatively independent from lifestyle and risk factors. All these factors can enhance the broad applicability of its findings.

Conclusion

The current study performed the first comprehensive analysis of how probe‐tissue interface stability and oscillation affect biophysical outcomes during AF ablation using the vHPSD energy delivery system.

The key findings of this study are as follows: Probe stability, oscillation, and minimum CF are the primary determinants of tissue lesions during ablation.The introduction of a novel parameter referred to as “oscillation speed” provides a distinct perspective on stability, with significant biophysical implications. Notably, this newly defined oscillation parameter is a reliable predictor of increased ID, appropriate Tmax, and, consequently, lesion formation.The temperature‐control system of QDOT MICRO has certain limitations in terms of self‐regulation. A probe tip speed >20 mm/s results in a decreased Tmax and ID, even with a high CF, thereby compromising the ablation outcome.The interplay between CF and stability parameters is essential in determining the ID and Tmax achieved during ablation. If the probe is unstable, increasing CF diminishes the efficacy of the lesion, rather than enhancing it.
