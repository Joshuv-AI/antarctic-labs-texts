Analysis of potential biomarkers for diabetic kidney disease and non-diabetic kidney disease based on urinary metabolomics analysis
Authors: Qiuyue He, Binbin Ye, Huanqin Li, Yuqing Pan, Yan Du, Kai Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a leading cause of chronic renal failure. However, not all patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) develop DKD. Some are diagnosed with non-diabetic renal disease (NDRD) or a combination of DKD and NDRD. Considering NDRD patients generally have a better prognosis than those with DKD, it is necessary to find biomarkers to distinguish them. In this study, we used urinary metabolomics to identify biomarkers of DKD and NDRD.MethodsUrine samples were collected from patients diagnosed with DKD and NDRD. Differential metabolites between the two groups were screened by untargeted metabolomics, followed by Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, Pearson correlation analysis, and correlation with renal function to identify potential differential diagnostic markers. These markers were subsequently validated by targeted metabolomics.ResultsA total of 18 differential metabolites were identified by untargeted metabolomics. L-Tyrosine was significantly down-regulated in the DKD group. Moreover, our targeted metabolomics results showed a statistically significant difference in L-Tyrosine between the two groups (p < 0.05) and a trend consistent with the untargeted metabolomics results.ConclusionL-Tyrosine showed consistent results in both untargeted and targeted metabolomics studies and was significantly down-regulated in the urine of the DKD group compared to the NDRD group. This suggests that decreased urinary L-Tyrosine could serve as a promising diagnostic biomarker for DKD, aiding in its differentiation from NDRD.Keywords: DKD, NDRD, Untargeted metabolomics, Targeted metabolomics, L-Tyrosine

Introduction

The latest data released by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) shows a global diabetes prevalence of 10.5% in 2021, and the IDF predicts that by 2045, approximately 784 million adults will have diabetes [1]. T2DM can accrue multiple systems and lead to serious complications such as coronary artery disease, peripheral neuropathy, and DKD [2]. DKD is the most common chronic complication of diabetes mellitus (DM) and the leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD). It is estimated that 20% to 40% of DM patients will develop DKD during the course of their disease [3]. Currently, there is a lack of specific treatment for DKD, and the main emphasis is on early and intensive control of blood glucose, blood pressure, and blood lipids through the use of angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, sodium-glucosecotransporter-2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, to slow down the progression of the disease [4]. In contrast, NDRD encompasses various pathological types, including membranous nephropathy, IgA nephropathy, mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis, minimal change disease, and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis [5]. Compared to DKD, some NDRD nephropathies are remissible or curable and have relatively better renal outcomes.

Numerous studies have shown that proteinuria and renal function status of patients with NDRD or DKD combined with NDRD significantly improved after application of glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, and medications to control blood pressure and lipids [6–8]. In addition, the incidence of cardiovascular events and the need for renal replacement therapy are significantly higher in DKD than in NDRD [9]. The treatment and prognosis of DKD and NDRD differ greatly, so it is important for the differential diagnosis of the two. Although renal biopsy is the gold standard for diagnosing DKD and NDRD, it is invasive and can lead to some postoperative complications [10], such as pain, hematoma and hematuria, which in severe cases may require blood transfusion and surgical intervention [11]. Currently, the non-invasive diagnostic method of DKD is mainly based on clinical diagnosis, which is comprehensively determined by combining the history, proteinuria, glomerular filtration rate(GFR), Diabetic retinopathy (DR) and other clinical manifestations. However, this method is deficient in sensitivity and specificity [12]. Therefore, it is important to find new methods of differential diagnosis.

Metabolomics is an emerging technique that enables the study of the metabolites and downstream products of biochemical events in an organism, revealing complex metabolic networks and providing insights into physiological or pathophysiological processes. Metabolomics is a promising tool not only for diagnosis, but also for the identification of pre-disease states, which will allow a rapid and personalized clinical work [13]. Metabolomics is mainly categorized into targeted and untargeted metabolomics. Untargeted metabolomics is an unbiased, comprehensive and systematic study of metabolites in organisms, providing complete metabolite information but with limited linear range and poor reproducibility. Targeted metabolomics, on the other hand, focuses on specific metabolites for quantitative analysis based on untargeted metabolomics results, offering a more intuitive representation of metabolic changes under disease conditions [14].

In this study, we conducted a comparative analysis of urine samples from patients with DKD and NDRD using untargeted metabolomics. Through the identification of statistically significant differential metabolites and subsequent pathway analysis, we aimed to discover potential biomarkers for distinguishing between these two conditions. The identified biomarkers were further validated through targeted metabolomics, providing new insights for the differential diagnosis of DKD and NDRD.

Materials and methods

Subjects of study

Patients hospitalized in the Department of Nephrology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University from October 1, 2022, to November 30, 2023, who were clinically diagnosed with DKD or diagnosed with NDRD by renal biopsy, were selected as study subjects.

Inclusion criteria

The diagnostic criteria for DKD were based on the Chinese expert consensus on the diagnosis and treatment of DKD [15]. In summary, the diagnosis of DKD is primarily established on the presence of typical manifestations, characterized by albuminuria or reduced eGFR accompanied by diabetic retinopathy. Conversely, the emergence of atypical features, such as an active urinary sediment, rapidly declining renal function, or the absence of retinopathy, warrants a renal biopsy to differentiate DKD from NDRD or a combination of them. NDRD pathohistological staging was based on the WHO 1995 revised criteria [16].

Exclusion criteria

(1) unclear medical history; (2) infection, fever, surgical stress within 24 h; (3) acute cardiovascular disease; (4) malignant tumors; (5) ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar coma within 3 months; (6) other renal diseases, such as kidney stones, urinary tract infection or polycystic kidney disease. (7) age < 18 years or > 75 years.

The study was developed and conducted in compliance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and this study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University. All have obtained informed consent from the participants.

Clinical data of study subjects

The following clinical and epidemiological data were collected: ethnicity, age, height, weight, smoking history, alcohol consumption, presence of retinopathy, duration of diabetes mellitus, and history of hypertension. Laboratory indicators included hemoglobin (Hb), white blood cell count (WBC), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (Scr), uric acid (UA), glucose (Glu), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), urine total protein (UTP), microalbumin (mALB), urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR), and N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminidase (NAG).

Sample collection and processing

Patients were instructed to collect at least 2 mL clean mid-stream urine, which was then centrifuged at 2000–3000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was pipetted into a 1.5 mL EP tube and stored at -80℃ for further analysis.

Untargeted metabolomics

The samples (100µL) were resuspended with prechilled 80% methanol, vortexed, and incubated on ice for 5 min. After centrifugation at 15,000 g, 4℃ for 20 min, the supernatant was diluted to a final concentration of 53% methanol and analyzed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) [17–18].

Targeted metabolomics

Samples were diluted with water, then 50 µL of them were homogenized with 200 µL of acetonitrile/methanol (1:1) containing internal standards. After that, they were placed on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. Finally, the supernatant was analyzed by LC-MS/MS.

LC-MS/MS analysis

Untargeted metabolomics

An ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) was employed for the analysis. Chromatographic separation was acquired using a Hypersil Gold column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) under a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. The mobile phase system consisted of eluent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and eluent B (methanol) was used for positive ion mode analysis, while for negative ion mode, eluent A was replaced with 5 mM ammonium acetate (pH 9.0). A 17-minute linear gradient elution procedure is as follows: 2% B, 1.5 min; 2–85% B, 3 min; 85–100% B, 10 min;100-2% B, 10 min; 2% B, 12 min. Mass spectrometric detection was performed using a QExactive™ HF-X mass spectrometer operating in both positive and negative ionization modes. The instrument parameters were optimized as follows: electrospray ionization (ESI) voltage at 3.5 kV, capillary temperature maintained at 320 ℃, sheath gas flow rate at 35 psi, auxiliary gas flow rate at 10 L/min, S-lens RF level at 60, and auxiliary gas heater temperature set to 350 ℃.

Targeted metabolomics

A UHPLC-MS/MS system (ExionLCTM AD UHPLC-QTRAP® 6500+, AB SCIEX, Boston, MA, USA) was used to quantitate amino acids in Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) maintained at 50 ℃. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in 5mM ammonium acetate (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B), was delivered at a flow rate of 0.30 mL/min. The solvent gradient was set as follows: initial 80% B, 0.5 min; 80 − 70% B, 2 min; 70 − 45% B, 4 min; 45–80% B, 6.01 min; 80% B, 9 min. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ionization mode with multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). Parameters were as follows: ionspray voltage, 5500 V; curtain gas, 35 psi; ion source temp, 550 ℃; ion source gas of 1 and 2, 50 and 60 psi.

Data processing and metabolites identification for untargeted metabolomics

The raw data files generated by UHPLC-MS/MS were processed using the Compound Discoverer 3.1 (CD3.1, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for peak alignment, peak picking and metabolite quantitation. The key processing parameters were set as follows: retention time tolerance, 0.2 min; mass tolerance, 5ppm; signal intensity tolerance, 30%; signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio threshold, 3 and minimum intensity. Subsequently, peak intensities were normalized to the total spectral intensity. The normalized data were used to predict the molecular formula based on additive ions, molecular ion peaks and fragment ions. These predicted formulas were then matched against the mzCloud(https://www.mzcloud.org/), mzVault and MassList databases for accurate metabolite identification and relative quantitation. Statistical analyses were performed using R(version 3.4.3), Python(version 2.7.6) and CentOS(release 6.6). For non-normally distributed data, standardization was performed using the formula: Standardized value = Sample raw quantitation value / (Sum of sample metabolite quantitation value / Sum of Quality Control (QC) sample metabolite quantitation value) to obtain relative peak areas. Compounds with a coefficient of variation (CV) of relative peak areas greater than 30% in QC samples were excluded. Finally, the identified metabolites and their relative quantitation results were obtained.

Metabolomics data analysis for untargeted metabolomics

The identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html), HMDB database (https://hmdb.ca/metabolites) and LIPIDMAPS database (http://www.lipidmaps.org/). Multivariate statistical analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), were performed using the metaX software [19], a flexible and comprehensive tool for metabolomics data processing. Univariate analysis (Student’s t-test) was performed to assess statistical significance, with metabolites having a variable importance in projection (VIP) score >1, a P-value < 0.05, and a fold change (FC) ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 considered differentially expressed. Volcano plots were generated using the ggplot2 package in R to visualize interesting metabolites based on log2 (Fold Change) and -log10 (P-value). The functions of these metabolites and their associated metabolic pathways were studied using the KEGG database. Enrichment analysis of differential metabolites was performed based on the following criteria: a metabolic pathway was considered enriched if the ratio x/n >y/N, and statistically significant enrichment was defined as a pathway with a P-value < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 27.0). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed continuous variables, differences between groups were evaluated using independent two-sample t-tests, and data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For non-normally distributed variables, the Mann-Whitney U test was used. Continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range) and continuous non-normally distributed data were summarized as median (5th-95th percentile). Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test and were reported as frequencies (%). P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Total sample principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) has the advantages of eliminating the scale differences among variables and reducing the information redundancy caused by inter-variable correlations, it has been widely used in many fields. In this study, PCA was used to assess the overall distribution trend between the two sample groups (DKD and NDRD) and the results were shown in Fig. 1. In both the positive and negative ionization modes, the DKD and NDRD groups showed an overall trend of separation, but not complete separation. Compared with the DKD group, the NDRD group was more dispersed, mainly due to the fact that DKD is a single disease type, while NDRD contains multiple pathologic types.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of the DKD and NDRD urine samples. A represents the total PCA score plots under positive ionization modes. B represents the total PCA score plots under negative ionization modes

Partial least squares discrimination analysis

PLS-DA is a supervised discriminant analysis statistical method that employs Partial Least Squares regression to model the relationship between metabolite expression and sample class to achieve prediction of sample class [20]. In this study, 7-fold cross-validation was used to obtain the model evaluation parameters (R2, Q2), and the results of R2 and Q2 were close to 1, indicating that the model was more reliable. In addition, in order to determine whether the model is overfitting or not, we carried out sequential validation of the model (Fig. 2 A, B) by randomly disrupting the grouping markers of each sample before modeling and predicting, and each modeling corresponds to a set of R2 and Q2 values, and according to the 200 times of disrupting and modeling after the values of Q2 and R2 can be obtained from their regression lines [21]. The results show that R2 is greater than Q2 and the intercept of the Q2 regression line with the Y-axis is less than 0, indicating that the model is not overfitted. The PLS-DA results showed significant separation between the DKD and NDRD groups in both positive and negative ionization modes, suggesting a significant difference between the DKD and NDRD groups. Differential metabolite analysis

Volcano plots were used to visualize differential metabolites based on fold change (FC) and statistical significance (-log10 P-value) (Fig. 3A, B). In the positive ion mode, 105 metabolites were differentially expressed (76 up-regulated, 29 down-regulated in DKD vs. NDRD). In the negative ion mode, 107 metabolites were differential (18 up-regulated, 89 down-regulated in DKD vs. NDRD). To identify metabolites with high diagnostic potential, ROC analysis was applied to select those with an area under the curve (AUC) ≥ 0.7. 50 metabolites with potential diagnostic value were selected for further consideration. Subsequently, Pearson correlation analysis was carried out with commonly used renal function indexes (BUN, SCr, UA, UCr, NAG, mAlb, UACR), and exogenous metabolites were excluded. Combining these results together, 18 metabolites with potential diagnostic value were shortlisted in Table 2. These include (±)-Abscisic acid, Acetylcysteine, cis-Aconitic acid, Homocysteic acid, Malonic acid, L-Tyrosine, Guanosine, Pseudouridine, L-Anserine, Xanthohumol, L-Aspartic acid, 7-Ketocholesterol, 1-Methylnicotinamide, N-Phenylacetylglutamine, D-(-)-Glutamine, L-Pyroglutamic acid, 5-Hydroxyindole, and Pyrogallol. The correlation between 18 metabolites and renal function indicators was shown in Table 3.

KEGG enrichment pathway analysis

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the differential metabolites revealed several significantly perturbed metabolic pathways (Fig. 4). The most relevant pathways included pyrimidine metabolism (including Pseudouridine, Orotic Acid, Malonic acid, 2’-Deoxyuridine), beta-Alanine metabolism (including L-Aspartic acid, Malonic acid), Aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (including L-Aspartic acid, L-Tyrosine), Cholesterol metabolism (including Glycocholic acid, Glycochenodeoxycholic acid), and Phenylalanine metabolism (including 2-Hydroxyphenylacetic acid; 4-Hydroxyphenylacetic acid). This analysis underscores the significant dysregulation of amino acid and nucleotide metabolism in DKD compared to NDRD.

Targeted metabolomics validation

Verification cohort characteristics using targeted metabolomics

Next, we sought to validate 23 amino acids identified from our untargeted metabolomics analysis. Using the same inclusion and exclusion criteria, we collected urine samples from an independent cohort comprising 20 DKD patients and 20 NDRD patients. The clinical characteristics of this validation cohort were presented in Table 4. The two groups were well-matched in terms of gender, age, and BMI. Significant differences were observed in key parameters consistent with the disease profiles, including TP, Alb, BUN, Scr, Glu, TC, HDL-C, and LDL-C (all p < 0.05), similar to the discovery cohort.

Targeted quantification of 23 amino acids was performed. Results revealed that 13 amino acids exhibited statistically significant differences between the DKD and NDRD groups (Table 5). This consistent downregulation highlights L-Tyrosine as a robust differential marker.

Discussion

DKD, as a major complication of DM, poses a huge burden to the society [22]. However, the clinical differentiation between DKD and NDRD remains challenging due to overlapping manifestations such as proteinuria and renal insufficiency. The current gold standard for differential diagnosis between the two is renal puncture biopsy, but it is invasive and can further exacerbate kidney damage, especially in patients with DKD. This has led to the fact that renal biopsy is often chosen with caution in the clinical diagnosis of renal disease in patients with comorbid DM. Given these limitations, the development of noninvasive diagnostic methods is of paramount importance. Leveraging metabolomics technology, this study aims to identify differential metabolites between DKD and NDRD, offering novel insights for their differential diagnosis.

In this study, a total of 212 differential metabolites were obtained by untargeted metabolomics, including 105 in the positive ion mode and 107 in the negative ion mode, which were categorized mainly as lipids, amino acids, and organic acids. KEGG analysis revealed pyrimidine metabolism, beta-Alanine metabolism, Aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, Cholesterol metabolism, and Phenylalanine metabolism as the most relevant metabolic pathways, indicating that amino acid metabolism was significantly different between the two groups. It has been reported that abnormal amino acid metabolism was closely related to the development of DKD, which was mainly involved in pathological processes such as oxidative stress, inflammation, and immune response, and also closely related to autophagy and mitochondrial dysfunction [23]. Therefore, we chose targeted metabolomics for further quantitative analysis of amino acids.

23 amino acids were analyzed by targeted metabolomics. The results showed that a total of 13 amino acids were statistically different, of which L-Tyrosine showed consistent results with untargeted metabolomics data, demonstrating significantly down-regulated urinary levels in the DKD group compared to the NDRD group. Functionally, L-Tyrosine participates in aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis and serves as a precursor for catecholamines (norepinephrine, epinephrine, and dopamine), which modulates insulin resistance and secretion [24–25]. Some studies have shown that low levels of L-Tyrosine can increase the risk of DR in T2DM patients, and renal insufficiency further increases the risk of DR [26–27]. Other studies have also pointed out that urinary tyrosine level is reduced in patients with DKD [28–29]. DR and DKD are both microvascular complications of DM and have a strong correlation, so it is hypothesized that the observed decrease in urinary L-Tyrosine excretion reflects a systemic metabolic alteration contributing to DKD pathogenesis. Further mechanistic studies are needed to identify this hypothesis.

In conclusion, L-Tyrosine demonstrated consistent performance in both untargeted and targeted metabolomics analyses. Its levels were significantly down-regulated in the urine of the DKD group compared to the NDRD group, suggesting strong differential diagnostic potential. These findings indicate that L-Tyrosine could serve as a promising biomarker for distinguishing between DKD and NDRD. Future studies with larger, prospective cohorts are warranted to confirm the clinical utility of L-Tyrosine and to explore its role in the pathophysiology of DKD.
