What affects the performance of hospital financing interventions in low and lower-middle income countries from the program design and implementation perspective? A scoping review
Authors: Wu Zeng, Mara Boiangiu, Natalie Trachsel, Eva Jarawan, Vincent Turbat, Bruno Meessen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHospitals, as an important component of the health system, consume a substantial amount of health resources and are instrumental in improving population health. While many health financing interventions have been implemented at hospitals, evidence exploring common factors facilitating their implementation in low and lower-middle income countries (LLMICs) remains limited. We conducted a scoping review of existing hospital financing interventions in LLMICs. A combination of search strategies and key informant consultations were used to search for relevant literature. A total of 35 articles spanning six categories of hospital financing interventions were included in the review. The review centered on design and implementation factors associated with hospital financing interventions. Factors affecting a hospital financing intervention’s results were numerous and context specific. From the design and implementation perspective, five interconnected factors—governance and accountability, participatory process, proper intervention design, adequate resources and capacity, and monitoring and evaluation—underline the most influential factors across the six categories of hospital financing interventions. Understanding the connections among these factors and making efforts to align them with the country’s context make for a more promising intervention. The evidence on specifics across different types of hospital financing implementations remains limited, requiring more implementation studies guided by comprehensive theoretical frameworks to generate more concrete evidence.Keywords: hospital, health financing, implementation

Introduction

While many countries are committed to moving toward Universal Health Coverage (UHC), providing affordable and good quality care while protecting the population from financial risks, progress toward UHC in most low and lower-middle income countries (LLMICs) has been slow. There are numerous reasons why a health financing intervention (e.g. revision of the provider payment mechanism, removal of user fee) may fail to deliver its promises in these settings. They can relate to inappropriate design, weak implementation, insufficient funding, unexpected changes in the context, unfavorable interaction with other interventions, reaction by some independent parties (e.g. private competitors), weakness at the level of the service delivery or of another health system building block (e.g. drug stock-outs), etc. (Atim et al. 2021). Understanding these design and implementation factors would facilitate the implementation of the interventions.

While health financing discussions in LLMICs have predominantly focused on primary care services (Chabrol et al. 2018, Domapielle et al. 2022), a strategic focus on hospital financing is also critical. First, hospitals are a crucial component of the health system and command a significant portion of domestic health resources, through the health budget, health insurance, and out-of-pocket payments (Zeng et al. 2017b). Second, given that primary care and hospital care often compete for limited health resources, reforming hospital financing to improve the efficiency of the hospital sector could free up resources for strengthening primary care. Third, systemic weaknesses in hospitals (e.g. shortage of funding and misalignment of incentives and health objectives) can exacerbate socioeconomic inequalities in access to medical care and increase vulnerability to catastrophic household costs. Addressing these hospital-specific challenges is essential for advancing the UHC agenda (Sambo and Kirigia 2014).

The existing literature on hospital financing interventions has focused more on high or upper-middle income countries, i.e. settings that are more welcoming for accommodating technically complex health system reforms, for various reasons, including resources and capacity. Propositions for better-resourced settings [e.g. Disease Related Group (DRG) payment system] and the implementation lessons emerging from these contexts require cautions when applied to LLMICs where challenges are often more foundational (e.g. drug stock-out, partial execution of budget). Hospital financing solutions in LLMICs may have to be customized in their concept and design features to consider the countries’ constraints.

However, there may also be an issue with the knowledge generated by the studies documenting hospital financing interventions in LLMICs. Most focus on the effectiveness of the interventions (i.e. whether the interventions work or not), with inconclusive conclusions for systematic reviews (de Vries et al. 2019, Xu et al. 2019, Ghazaryan et al. 2021). This raises a question: instead of focusing on effectiveness, for LLMICs, should we not also focus on gathering evidence on conditions for successful implementation, that is to identify design and implementation features and contextual factors that enhance the chances of success of the intervention? From the conclusion that “more research is needed,” we could reach more concrete findings with a greater value for hospital financing practice. Previous studies examining implementation factors typically focused on a specific financing mechanism (e.g. community-based health insurance or pay for performance) and often do not specifically target hospitals (Adebayo et al. 2015, Fadlallah et al. 2018, Diaconu et al. 2021, Singh et al. 2021a, Diaconu et al. 2022). While a wide range of hospital financing interventions have been implemented in LLMICs, the information remains limited, from the implementation perspective, on what key common factors hinder or facilitate the successful implementation of these interventions. To address this gap, we conducted a literature review of hospital financing interventions in LLMICs to synthesize and identify key “design and implementation factors” to guide their future implementation efforts.

Materials and methods

This review focused on “design and implementation feature” of hospital financing interventions in LLMICs. Using the World Health Organization (WHO)’s framework for health financing and UHC (Kutzin 2013), we defined hospital financing interventions as health financing interventions whose primary goal is to alter institutional arrangements shaping how financing for hospitals is functionally organized. Hospital financing interventions are part of health financing interventions, and they include not only changes initiated by hospitals but also national or sub-national reforms targeting hospitals or covering hospital services. Thus, the hospital financing interventions in this study included those aiming to change the entitlement of beneficiaries, or those focusing on institutional arrangements setting how purchasing agencies or individuals or health authorities pay/reward hospitals for the services they deliver (e.g. provider payment reforms), or any combination of the above interventions. We also included benefit design of hospital services and hospital autonomy over resource use in the review, as these two interventions are closely related to health financing and resource use (Doshmangir et al. 2016, WHO 2022). Furthermore, revisions to the health benefits package often constitute a key part of financing reforms at the national or sub-national level. We covered not only full-fledged hospital financing interventions but also pilots implemented in a limited number of hospitals. Additionally, during the literature search, we did not distinguish the type of hospitals (e.g. district hospital or tertiary hospital) included in the review but acknowledged that most hospitals were likely to be public or non-government organization hospitals, with a limited number of private-for-profit or tertiary university hospitals.

Literature search strategy

To ensure the efficient identification of relevant articles for the literature review, we started with known hospital financing interventions in LLMICs. For example, under the interventions related to beneficiary entitlement, we included the free C-section policy in Benin and the health equity fund (HEF) policy in Cambodia. Under the interventions related to payment arrangements, we brought, for instance, reforms revising per-diem rates in Kenya, as well as results-based financing (RBF) schemes (in this study, RBF refers to all programs that tie the hospital payment to achieving predetermined indicators, including performance-based financing and performance-based contracting programs, and so on). Health insurance schemes covering hospital services are regarded as a comprehensive hospital financing intervention that could combine benefit entitlement and payment changes. We did not put too much stress on the distinction of specific hospital financing categories (e.g. beneficiary entitlement, provider payment reforms, contract arrangement, etc.), aware that many interventions have the overlapping traits (a HEF, for instance, targets the poorest, but compensates the hospital for the revenue loss with a case-based payment).

With this preliminary understanding, we reached out to potential key informants in LLMICs to inquire about hospital financing interventions that have been or are currently being carried out to obtain associated implementation evaluation documents. Once country-specific hospital financing interventions were identified, we conducted a search using the specific program/scheme names and the country name where the programs were implemented on databases to retrieve relevant documents on the implementation of interventions and/or their impact. The literature of interest was more focused on the description of the design and implementation of the interventions and factors that made the intervention work or not work. Thus, both interventional studies and implementation studies were included in the review.

Additionally, we performed a literature search using a specific search strategy (see Supplementary Appendix S1, for detailed search algorithm), initially in February 2023, in four major electronic bibliographic databases: PubMed, EconLit, Academic Search Premier, and Web of Science. For all the data searches, we limited the literature to publications after the year 2000 to ensure the information to be collected is relatively new and has the potential to be included for drawing lessons.

Inclusion criteria

All the search records were uploaded in Endnote X20 and independently screened by two reviewers. After eliminating duplicates, the remaining records were first reviewed through titles and abstracts to assess their relevance: (i) studies that cover the hospital sector, either targeting hospitals or covering hospital services; (ii) interventions concerned with hospital revenue generation or payment interventions or enhancing the implementation of payment changes; (iii) studies conducted in LLMICs; and (iv) studies that focus on the design and implementation evaluation of the interventions. Articles that meet or possibly meet the inclusion criteria were included in the full-text review. While financing interventions like RBF and health insurance can apply to all facility types, this review only included studies that specifically discussed their implementation in hospital settings.

Data collection and extraction

In this review, we drew on the theories of policy design and implementation to identify potential factors associated with the policy outcomes (Brinkerhoff and Crosby 2002, Hardee et al. 2004). Regarding the policy design, features of interest included the type, size, and intensity of the interventions; services that are affected by the interventions; target populations for the interventions; and so on. Features of interest related to the policy implementation included organizational structure, stakeholder engagement and advocacy, resources available for implementation, and supervision and monitoring support. These factors were identified as essential elements of design and implementation and were therefore specifically collected and analyzed in this study.

For each identified category of hospital financing interventions, we reviewed the identified articles and extracted information on the design and implementation, including key actors of hospital financing interventions and how the actors interact with each other to achieve intended health objectives.

An Excel template was developed to extract data from the identified full-text articles. The collected information included (i) characteristics of each study (e.g. publication year, name of the country where the study was conducted, and type of hospitals); (ii) components of hospital financing interventions and potential design and implementation factors mentioned above. Please see Supplementary Appendix S2 for detailed definitions of the data collection components. Please note that although we collected the information on several components, we reported only design and implementation factors. Data extraction was carried out by two researchers, with researchers reviewing and cross-checking each other’s data.

Data synthesis

We first described the characteristics of the identified articles, including the geographic and time distribution of the studies, and data sources. The number of studies on each type of intervention was provided. We then described the key implementation characteristics collected by type of intervention. After the intervention-specific description, we summarized key implementation features across all types of interventions to capture common themes that affected intervention implementation. The analysis was conducted using Excel. This scoping review was not registered.

After reviewing the collected articles and consistent with the defined health financing interventions previously, the following categories of interventions were included in the review: (i) “beneficiary entitlement interventions”, including user-fee exemptions and HEFs (six articles); (ii) “contracting changes,” including external/internal/mixed contracting models, and accreditation-based reimbursement (six articles); (iii) “public-private partnership” (two articles), which constitutes co-operative ventures between public and private sector to meet defined public needs through the appropriate allocation of resources, risks, and rewards (Hrab 2004). It often involves pooling resources from the public and private sectors jointly; (iv) “provider payment changes” (nine articles); (v) “hospital autonomy” (five articles), which grants autonomy to hospitals to use and reallocate resources; and (vi) “health insurance schemes” (seven articles). We acknowledge the partly arbitrary nature of these categories of interventions, given the overlap among them. We chose to distinguish them according to the names used in the articles by policymakers and researchers. We made a distinction by sorting articles that specifically name “contracting” or “accreditation” into “contracting changes” and sorting articles more specific to different “types” of provider payment arrangements (DRGs, RBF, etc.) into the category of “provider payment changes.” Supplementary Appendix S3 provides a brief definition of each category of intervention.

Findings drawn from the identified interventions were organized by intervention category, with insights into interventions’ design and implementation features that affected the performance of the interventions.

Beneficiary entitlement reform: user fee exemptions and health equity funds for hospital services

This category includes six articles that examine experiences in Kenya, Senegal, Benin, Uganda, and Cambodia. Five articles are related to user fee exemptions for hospital services, and one article addresses Cambodia’s HEF at hospitals during its early formulation and implementation. For details, please see Supplementary Table S1.

Design features

One of the key features is that both use exemptions and HEF targeted specific groups of vulnerable populations. In Kenya, Senegal, Benin, and Uganda, e.g. mothers unable to pay for safe, in-facility deliveries, would often face a choice between receiving the necessary care—sometimes at the risk of being detained in the hospital for not paying—or not receiving care at all (Witter et al. 2010, Dossou et al. 2018, Tama et al. 2018, Clarke et al. 2020, Dossou et al. 2020). In such circumstances, user fee exemption schemes were generally materialized as payment exemptions for mothers who delivered in public hospitals, to incentivize safer, professionally attended deliveries while preventing potentially impoverishing health payments. The respective Cambodian and Ugandan schemes sought to install similar safeguards for the extremely poor and “indigent” population groups (Kivumbi and Kintu 2002, Noirhomme et al. 2007).

The second feature was the use of the government resources to subsidize the programs. As the intervention aimed to shift the financial burden from beneficiaries to others, subsidies were needed and came from different sources. For user fee exemption schemes for hospital services in Kenya and Senegal, costs for delivery and/or maternal care were diverted from being charged to mothers to being reimbursed by a government-funded budget (Witter et al. 2010, Tama et al. 2018). Both countries demonstrated role separation to a certain degree.

However, the interventions across countries differed by the generosity of service under the exemption or subsidy. User fee exemption schemes, analyzed in Uganda and Benin, relieved populations from all, or a portion of, health costs incurred within public hospitals where user fees acted as barriers to service accessibility and utilization (Kivumbi and Kintu 2002, Dossou et al. 2018, Dossou et al. 2020). In Ugandan, the intervention offered three levels of financial safety nets—full payment coverage with user fee exemptions, partial coverage with waivers, and paid treatment to be repaid by the patient with credit—depending on patients’ respective abilities to pay (Kivumbi and Kintu 2002). The management of the scheme was left to hospitals with health workers evaluating each case for exemption qualification. In Benin, user fee exemption was limited to in-hospital C-section delivery, and the management was implemented by an autonomous, national agency with a purchaser-provider separation (Dossou et al. 2018, 2020).

HEFs overlapped with user-fee exemption schemes regarding the scheme’s basic function, as the HEFs offered user-fee exemptions for targeted populations at hospitals. In Cambodia, this came in the form of provider payments from an independently managed, specially designed fund (Noirhomme et al. 2007). Initially, several HEFs existed across the country with varied benefit packages and organizational models. Despite these variations, each took a holistic approach to beneficiary entitlement, being implemented alongside other health financing strategies and seeking to cover nonhealth financial obstacles to service utilization (including transport to hospitals, etc.). In the initial stage of HEFs, external bodies [often nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) or, in one district, Buddhist pagodas] funded and operated the schemes. Viewing HEFs’ success, the government has become the major source of funding for the HEF, and HEF now has been a pivotal element of Cambodia’s national health policy framework (Annear et al. 2019).

Implementation features

Country- and scheme-specific findings offer lessons learned regarding the root causes of interventions’ performance. Performance may be linked to implementation challenges and the schemes’ inherent abilities (or lack of) to overcome them. The articles link intervention performance to three main elements: “stakeholder involvement,” “incentivization alignment,” and “demand generation.”

Several reasons were responsibility for inadequate stakeholder involvement in the policy design and implementation. In Kenya, the user fee exemption policy was launched as a political response to external pressures (from donors, the general population, or a combination of actors), which led to a sudden policy shift without key stakeholders’ agreement before passing the policy and with limited stakeholder participation in the policy cycle. In Benin and Uganda, the limited stakeholder engagement was primarily due to the failure to address cultural obstacles that disinterested key stakeholders. For example, in Benin, the central government’s push for abolishing user fees at hospitals created some mistrust and tension during the scheme’s implementation period due to prior culture of supporting user fees under Bamako Initiative (Dossou et al. 2018, 2020). Similar clashes were seen in Uganda, where the Ministry of Health (MoH) and many hospitals preferred the previous cost-sharing system— despite its instability as a policy that was instituted and revoked several times, and despite its reportedly negative impacts on healthcare utilization by indigent populations—over user fee exemptions pushed by the MoH (Kivumbi and Kintu 2002). Providers were thus less likely to adhere to the user fee exemption policy. The non-adherence was further exacerbated by the absence of consistent, formalized policy guidelines, and delayed reimbursements to hospitals (Kivumbi and Kintu 2002, Tama et al. 2018).

The HEFs in Cambodia highlighted the importance of provider incentives and demand-generation efforts in providing financial protection to the target population. It is worth noting that without proper “provider” incentivization in the form of guaranteed reimbursement payments to address “supply”-side concerns, these singular interventions could only offer limited benefits. Like Benin, Cambodia exhibited a purchaser-provider role separation for its HEF schemes (Annear et al. 2019). Whereas in Benin, health workers held the job of evaluating cases for exemption qualification (because of its medical nature), Cambodian providers neither held roles in purchasing nor in beneficiary identification. It was community organizations that were involved in beneficiary criteria development through a series of methods, empowering beneficiaries to claim their benefits and supplementing demand (Jacobs et al. 2018, Annear et al. 2019). At the same time, donor or external stakeholders, and, more recently, government, responsible for implementation provided reliable reimbursement funding, thus incentivizing providers to adhere to policy goals,

Contracting changes: hospital contracting models and accreditation-based contracting

This category includes six articles that examine interventions in Malawi, Cambodia, Lebanon, and Rwanda. Five articles relate to external, internal, or mixed contracting models; and one article discusses an experience with accreditation-based payments (Supplementary Table S2).

Design features

Contracting changes in this category included expanding contracts with hospitals and instituting contracts based on hospital accreditation. Three major models were observed when contracting hospitals: namely, external, internal, and mixed contracting. External hospital contracting is an agreement in which an NGO, or private entity, operating a hospital is asked to provide health services to the target population. In Malawi, the MoH externally contracted facilities owned by the Christian Health Association of Malawi (CHAM) to deliver free care included in the Essential Health Package through Service Level Agreements (SLAs) (Chirwa et al. 2013). Similarly, at the start of contracting reforms in Cambodia, a few NGOs were contracted to manage health service delivery in pilot operational districts (Soeters and Griffiths 2003).

By contrast, the internal contract model seeks to improve the management of health services and strengthen service delivery within the public system. In Cambodia, internal contracting of health services converted district health units to special operating agencies, or semi-autonomous institutions, that were contracted to deliver services (Vong et al. 2018).

In Lebanon, the Ministry of Public Health (MoPH) initiated a mixed-model contracting system with hospitals (private and public) to expand coverage of services. The contract included an assessment of the hospital case-mix index, patient satisfaction, and selected policy indicators. The mixed-model system followed the adoption of hospital accreditation, trying to address the concern of hospital accreditation that was used as the sole determinant of hospitals’ reimbursement rates (Khalife et al. 2017).

While accreditation-based contracting, based on national standards, is undertaken to improve quality of care, it is often linked to financial incentives for providers. Adherence to said standards, or lack thereof, influences provider reimbursement rates, as per the purchaser-provider contract. Hospital accreditation can complement other interventions to bolster stakeholder participation. In Rwanda, for example, tertiary hospitals were subject to the MoH’s three-tier accreditation scheme which was initiated alongside pre-existing RBF initiatives (Binagwaho et al. 2020). In this program, an established Accreditation Steering Committee was tasked to monitor the intervention’s performance according to standards across five focus areas, which not only included the clinical quality of care and competence of the workforce but also the leadership process and accountability and safe environment for staff and patients (Binagwaho et al. 2020).

Implementation features

A series of factors—including “institutional arrangements,” “stakeholder involvement and incentivization,” “monitoring and accountability measures,” and “resource availability and usage”—underlined the performance of the studied contracting change-based interventions.

For contracting, the “institutional arrangements” between contractor and provider, active involvement of all stakeholders from the conception of the initiative, and participatory governance were crucial. In Lebanon’s mixed model contracting intervention, the close partnership between the contracted hospitals and the MoPH was key to supporting the new hospital contracting system (Khalife et al. 2017). Furthermore, identifying dedicated quality improvement officers at each hospital, as well as providing flexibility to hospital leadership to orchestrate their operational policies and procedures, was key to improving engagement in the program (Khalife et al. 2017). On the contrary, a lack of consistent and active involvement was detrimental to contracting reform. This is evidenced in Malawi’s external contracting reform, where miscommunication and unreliability between the District Health Office, government, and CHAM impeded the successful contracting of services (Chirwa et al. 2013).

“Communication and aligning incentives” amongst “stakeholders,” especially those involved in hospital leadership, were essential to a successful contracting change. Rwanda’s accreditation-based intervention encountered implementation issues when attempting to convince key stakeholders—namely, hospital staff and leadership—of the need for accreditation in their hospitals (Binagwaho et al. 2020). If the contract was clear concerning ownership and decision-making, and the incentives were appropriate, health workers were more likely to adhere to work regulations and abide by the contracting reform. To achieve these objectives, Rwanda’s accreditation scheme, built on the RBF program, developed a multi-level incentivization process. At the same time, it granted autonomy to hospitals to enact their preferred strategies and procedures to meet accreditation standards, which simultaneously allowed hospitals to tailor their practices to their functional contexts and incentivized adherence to the broader contracting policy.

“Monitoring and enforcement” measures were crucial to promote accountability within contract functioning and various measures were observed for contract changes across countries. In Lebanon’s mixed-model contracting system, the MoPH created three committees to oversee the contract (Khalife et al. 2017). In Cambodia, monitoring groups were established in all stages at different levels of the contracting reforms, which, coupled with incentive payments, improved the effectiveness of internal contracting (Vong et al. 2018). Monitoring procedures were also critical in accreditation-centered interventions. Effectiveness in Rwanda’s accreditation scheme was attributed, in part, to the presence of both program engagement officers and an Accreditation Steering Committee, each of which monitored adherence to quality standards (Binagwaho et al. 2020).

Neither monitoring nor stakeholder incentivization was possible without adequate financial and human “resources.” The shortage of resources could be the result of the delayed payments from the contractor. For example, the performance of SLAs in Malawi was affected by late payment of bills by the MoH, coupled with other factors such as nonrevised prices, lack of transparency, and poor communication amongst stakeholders (Chirwa et al. 2013). Inadequate resources not only affected the program implementation itself but also compromised the program’s monitoring capabilities to refine the program for better outcomes. In Cambodian’s contracting study, the shortage of the budget for the Provincial Health Department to monitor the hospitals contributed to the irregular monitoring of the contracting effectiveness (Vong et al. 2018). The financial constraints would affect the overall health intervention performance.

Hospital public-private partnerships

Public-private partnership (PPP), in the form of an agreement between a public entity (e.g. the country’s MoH) and a private organization (e.g. private companies), provides a supplementary approach to delivering health services. By definition, each party under the agreement contributes resources and shares risks and rewards, and PPP involves the pooling of financial resources from the public and private sectors (Hrab 2004). This category includes two articles, and both are from Lesotho (Vian et al. 2017, Hellowell 2019) (Supplementary Table S3).

Design features

PPP leverages resources from the non-public sector to improve the coverage and/or quality of hospital services. Despite controversies due to increasingly high costs (Webster 2015), the PPP in Lesotho, whereby Lesotho’s MoH collaborated with a consortium of private companies, constructed a new hospital to replace an aging, government-run hospital (Vian et al. 2017, Hellowell 2019). Under the PPP contract, the private consortium was responsible for designing, building, partially financing, and equipping the new hospital. The private consortium maintained the hospital facilities and managed hospital operations during the implementation period with a unitary fee from the government. In the PPP hospital, the use of the facility and most all inpatient services were free, except for small copayments for some services.

Implementation features

“Government capacity” was reported as important for managing the PPP contract. The Lesotho MoH experienced limited capacity to plan, procure, manage, and pay for the PPP contract, which undermined the ability of the PPP to achieve greater health system objectives (Vian et al. 2017, Hellowell 2019). Due to weak monitoring and evaluation (M&E) capacity, the Government of Lesotho was unable to impose deductions and penalties on the private consortium. The “back-stop” arrangement, in which the private consortium contract with the Government would be terminated, should it fail to maintain accreditation, was the only attempt by the Government of Lesotho to monitor the PPP contract.

There was a pattern of higher-than-anticipated costs by the government and/or primary funding partner in the PPP contract in Lesotho. There was also a pattern of delays in payments of government-dedicated funding to their private partner. For example, the actual cost of the new privately run hospital was nearly two and a half times the amount that was originally considered affordable by the Government of Lesotho and development partners. Higher costs were a result of the number of treated patients exceeding expectations and the government’s inability to pay the fees on time, leading the private consortium to charge interest (Vian et al. 2017, Hellowell 2019).

The identified articles found “regulatory instruments,” “accountability, and monitoring mechanisms” to be necessary for an effective PPP. In Lesotho, the hospital PPP appeared to reduce corruption by controlling discretion and increasing accountability, transparency, and detection and enforcement (Vian et al. 2017). The PPP, with a broader focus on high-performing systems and normative guidance, was important to the creation of accountability. In turn, clear policies and guidelines and effective communication between contractual partners, combined with staff accountability and consistent feedback on performance, were crucial for a well-functioning PPP initiative.

Hospital provider payment changes

This category includes nine articles, eight of which study RBF/performance-based budgeting (PBB) and one that examines a diagnostic-related group (DRG) payment system in the Kyrgyz Republic. Countries studied under the RBF/PBB heading include Burundi, Iran, Lesotho, Zimbabwe, Uganda, Zambia, Senegal, and Mali (Supplementary Table S4).

Design features

Provider payment changes were enacted to stimulate providers’ behavior changes. Some RBF interventions studied were contracting pilots, and others were determined schemes. The design of RBF programs varied greatly across different countries, primarily, in (i) the type of services; (ii) the payment formula to the hospitals; (iii) the payment amounts; and (iv) the existence of other complementary programs.

Interestingly, several RBF programs were implemented with a strong focus on improving the quality of care. For example, in Iran, an RBF approach was used to create a new hospital performance grading scheme (Aryankhesal et al. 2013). In Burundi, an RBF scheme was put in place in which health facilities, including hospitals, were paid monthly on a fee-for-service basis for activities, in consideration of achievements on a quality checklist (Renaud 2013). In Senegal, the hospital performance contract was based on the auditing of 20 results, and the payment was made against the total score in the 20 indicators (Gueye and Kopp 2009). Lastly, in Zimbabwe, a Continuous Quality Improvement (CQI) program, which was applied to hospitals, was implemented as an arm of an already existing RBF program, where hospital staff was rewarded through their support of the implementation of quality improvement at hospitals and district-wide health outcomes (Gage et al. 2022).

The included article examining DRG-payment-based contracting changes is based in the Kyrgyz Republic (Bredenkamp et al. 2019). To streamline its primary health care provision in hospitals, the Kyrgyz DRG system had based its initial 28-category DRG system and grouping algorithm on previous efforts from the United States Health Care Financing Administration. Paired with performance-based provider incentives built on local data on costs and utilization of hospital services, the DRG-based system used the patient’s principal diagnosis for reimbursement.

Implementation features

For RBF interventions, maintenance of “incentive-based rationale,” an effective “monitoring” system, “stakeholder engagement,” and “technical and institutional capacity” were crucial to the effectiveness of the interventions.

On “financial incentives,” to ensure the incentives work in the desired direction, it was critical to set the financial incentives based on agreed and valid indicators. In Iran’s RBF hospital grading intervention, where hospital stay charges were based on the score of grading domains, it was reported that staff had little awareness of the grading standards and questioned the validity of the grading contents (Aryankhesal et al. 2013). There was even a disagreement between private and public hospitals on whether individual health workers were motivated by financial rewards received through the RBF component. Additionally, right incentives could enhance the adoption of other health interventions, as shown in the Kyrgyz Republic, where pairing the DRG introduction with performance-based bonuses for staff encouraged the adoption of DRG in the country (Bredenkamp et al. 2019).

The presence of an effective “monitoring” system in any provider payment intervention contributed to favorable outcomes. In Burundi’s RBF scheme, authors found a strong integration of the verification and monitoring system with the Burundi MoH regulatory system, which ensured a degree of confidence in the data produced (Renaud 2013). In the Kyrgyz Republic, complex data management and categorization would be impossible without an administrative unit to monitor and revise coding procedures. The mandatory health insurance fund, which introduced DRGs to the Kyrgyz system, performed the function of monitoring (Bredenkamp et al. 2019).

“Stakeholder engagement” was found to influence the implementation of RBF interventions. Although a wide range of stakeholders were involved in RBF implementation, the engagement of personnel at hospitals was of the most importance. In both Mali and Zambia RBF schemes (Zitti et al. 2019, Bergman et al. 2021), there was an insufficient involvement of some stakeholders, such as hospital administrators and managers, generally unaware of the process of monitoring and verification of the RBF system and its indicators. Mali’s RBF scheme showed that personnel’s understanding of the intervention and their commitment played a key role in effective implementation (Zitti et al. 2019). However, Zambia’s RBF scheme showed the importance of broader stakeholder engagement, which included development partners and the government. Zambia’s RBF was accused of being insufficiently planned before implementation. There were different perceptions on what levels of the health system the RBF would incentivize between donors and the government (Bergman et al. 2021), which necessitated increased communication between them.

Lastly, “technical and institutional capacity” was essential for proper provider payment reform implementation. Given the inherently technical nature of DRG-based payment systems—i.e. the use of coding for cost determination and the necessity of continued revision of diagnosis-related groups to reflect recent data—the authors noted that a key lesson learned from the Kyrgyz case was the need to strengthen the management capacity through institutionalizing DRG system management processes, and highlighted the need for consistent monitoring, evaluation, and updating of DRG-related data and grouping standards. Concerning RBF, the CQI initiative in Zimbabwe showed that the lack of institutional capacity at the national level to supervise, mentor, and monitor district and health facility teams to execute CQI processes contributed to the insufficient tracking of input and process indicators (Gage et al. 2022). In contrast, Uganda’s performance-based contracting intervention, with the improved capacity of the auditing team in their ability to successfully extract data from clinical registers, contributed to positive changes in performance (Ssengooba et al. 2012).

Hospital autonomy

Hospital autonomy is an organizational reform that can take many forms. It can involve providing hospital management with a certain level of autonomy in the preparation and execution of the public budget, in decision making related to resource mobilization (including, for instance, charging higher user fees in “private wards”), human resources, and in procurement and management of equipment, among others. Thus, hospital autonomy could overlap with many hospital initiatives, such as hospital decentralization, public financial management reform, etc. This category includes five articles based in Iran, Zambia, and Uganda (Supplementary Table S5): four were from Iran (Markazi-Moghaddam et al. 2014, Doshmangir et al. 2015, Doshmangir et al. 2016, Jafari et al. 2018), and one examined the programs in Zambia and Uganda jointly (Hanson et al. 2002).

Design features

In an autonomy program in teaching hospitals in Iran (Doshmangir et al. 2015, Doshmangir et al. 2016, Jafari et al. 2018), the Board of Trustees in Iranian teaching hospitals was responsible for governing the hospitals and deciding on the hospitals’ financial management. The hospital autonomy program created a new payment system for both public and teaching hospitals, which granted financial self-sufficiency to selected hospitals and gave authority to insurance organizations to purchase health services. Hospitals maintained contract-based relations with insurance organizations and received premiums from government budgets to provide packages of hospital inpatient services. Similar to the Board of Trustees in Iran, Hospital Management Committees and Hospital Boards were used in Uganda to be responsible for the management of the newly autonomous hospitals (Hanson et al. 2002).

Implementation features

The review shows that “managerial capacity” of implementers, adequacy of “financial resources,” and the presence of “enforcement mechanisms” of health laws and policies affect the performance of hospital autonomy in achieving its promises.

One essential factor in hospital autonomy reforms was “managerial capacity.” In Iran, a lack of hospital managerial capacity was found to slow down the reform. Hospital managers were reluctant to proceed with the reform and when initialized, the reform was likely to be obstructed by insurance organizations which often provided delayed and decreased payments to the hospitals. Similarly, in Uganda, weak management capacity at both the district and central levels in their new roles constrained the smooth implementation of decentralization procedures.

“Inadequate funding” from committed organizations, like with other hospital financing interventions, was seen to result in severe adverse effects on policy implementation. As stated by Hanson et al, effective decentralization of control over key resources is a key tool for successful implementation of an autonomy policy (Hanson et al. 2002). This lack of visibility in funding not only weakens a hospital’s ability to achieve financial autonomy with public finances. Sometimes, the funding was in the form of financial incentives.

Central to the implementation of hospital autonomy was the “enforcement of regulation.” This is particularly highlighted in Iran’s autonomy study, where there was a lack of ministerial supervision in policy implementation (forming leadership teams and defining communication routes), an absence of supporting hospitals against insurance organizations, and an inattention to the need for management and its stability (Doshmangir et al. 2015, Doshmangir et al. 2016).

Health insurance covering hospital services

This category includes eight articles that examine health insurance schemes covering hospital services in India (Kundu et al. 2018, Khetrapal and Acharya 2019), Kenya (Munge et al. 2018), Ghana (Atinga et al. 2012), Nepal (Khanal et al. 2023), Tanzania (Kigume and Maluka 2021), and Tunisia (Organisation Mondiale de la Santé 2020) (Supplementary Table S6).

Design features

Insurance schemes have become increasingly complex to achieve their targets. India’s national Rashtriya Swasthya Bima Yojana (RSBY) insurance scheme that covers hospital services (Khetrapal and Acharya 2019), for example, exhibited a design attempting to protect below-poverty line (BPL) individuals from catastrophic health expenditures. This insurance scheme has been expanded and remains in function today under the heading of a 2018 scheme, Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogaya Yojana (PMJAY), which built upon the RSBY foundations to expand hospital insurance coverage to some half a billion beneficiaries (Garg et al. 2019). The current PMJAY is the product of a decade of learning and adjusting RSBY features to meet the changing needs of BPL Indian households. In this review, we focused on the early years of RSBY to understand the design and implementation features that contributed to its eventual expansion.

The RSBY scheme combined several interventions studied in more depth in our report, including both contracting models and PPP. At one level, the Indian government (central and/or state) contracted an insurer, who was paid in government-sponsored premiums. The insurer, then, contracted hospitals (public and/or private) to offer inpatient services for its enrolled beneficiaries, reimbursing the hospitals with a “fixed amount per service type.” RSBY also stood as an example of using a PPP to provide both health insurance coverage and health services. Other schemes in Kenya, Ghana, Nepal, Tunisia, and Tanzania exemplified novel, and similarly complex, blends of financing mechanisms, enacting reforms in pooling, contracting, strategic purchasing, provider payments, benefit package creation, and more.

In the case of the National Health Insurance Scheme in Ghana, a social health insurance scheme implementing a specific DRG model, implementation issues stem from the decoupling of service purchasing and service provision. The major challenges confronting hospitals (e.g. logistical shortages; difficulties and delays in claims processing; patients’ long waiting hours; and overuse of hospital services) that resulted from the decoupling have been given critical attention (Atinga et al. 2012). In Nepal, poor budget absorption capacity was one of the major criticisms faced by the National Health Insurance Program (NHIP). The allocated budget for NHIP increased substantially between 2017/2018 and 2020/2021, while the disbursement was lower than the allocation (Khanal et al. 2023). Tunisia’s intervention concentrated on the transition from passive purchasing of hospital services to a more strategic approach that links the fund transfers to the performance and health needs of the population (Organisation Mondiale de la Santé 2020).

Implementation features

The effectiveness of health insurance interventions could be attributed to a series of interconnected implementation factors, including “resources,” “stakeholder and incentive alignment” with scheme conception (creating a larger public-private sector disconnect in practice), and “monitoring capacity.” Though some of the implementation factors (e.g. resources and stakeholder and incentive alignment) may be more likely to arise during the scheme’s infancy and thus have the potential to be addressed with the scheme’s progression, each is multidimensional and impactful in the context of insurance intervention functioning. That is, the mechanisms by which these factors produce less effective interventions were equally complex, with each factor affecting the others to create a negative feedback loop that impedes progress. “Resource availability and management” were necessary to (i) promote communication among stakeholders; (ii) generate incentives—for both beneficiaries and providers via sufficient benefit packages and affordable premiums, and timely provider payments, respectively; and (iii) establish monitoring/accountability mechanisms throughout the insurance scheme. As it became clear in the articles, where most schemes lacked control over resources, the opposite was also true: “inadequate” resource pooling and administration could contribute to—or work in alignment with–poor communication among key players, “dis”incentivization, and inhibited monitoring/accountability mechanisms, creating multi-fold consequences (Atinga et al. 2012, Munge et al. 2018, Garg et al. 2019, Mbau et al. 2020).

In the absence of sufficient sensitization and communication campaigns (from resource- or design-related issues), beneficiaries could be unaware of, and therefore fail to claim, their entitlements. Or, if beneficiaries’ needs were not explored and incorporated into benefit packages, entitlements could be less accessible and/or attractive to enrollees. Either may have decreased scheme enrollment from the general public. Kenya was one country that exemplified this phenomenon: the scheme’s overreliance on voluntary fund contributions created a lack of resources (Munge et al. 2018, Mbau et al. 2020). This, paired with poor communication regarding increased (and, thus, unaffordable) premium contributions and the National Hospital Insurance Fund benefit package contents, failed to incentivize beneficiary participation. As a result, the scheme saw inequity in benefit access with relatively low insurance enrollment across the population (Munge et al. 2018).

At the same time, “faulty communication” among “stakeholders” (providers, governments, external bodies, etc.) caused role confusion, which would in turn misalign incentives between public and private sectors and compromise care. In both India’s RSBY and multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB) schemes, an unclear definition of roles and responsibilities created disparities between the public and private sectors (Kundu et al. 2018, Garg et al. 2019). Whereas private RSBY contract providers sought to treat more patients to increase their profit, public providers wanted to use resources to enhance service provision (Garg et al. 2019). And, despite the private sector comprising a large share of Indian healthcare provision, the MDR-TB scheme saw both greater participation and performance in the public versus the private sector (Kundu et al. 2018). Tunisia also demonstrated inadequate involvement and incentivization of necessary stakeholders, creating a system in which the private sector flourished at the expense of the public sector (Organisation Mondiale de la Santé 2020). Regardless of the specifics in each setting, communication was generally insufficient to guarantee stakeholder buy-in, creating a cascade of effects among mixed schemes.

Resource unavailability inhibited “monitoring capabilities” in hospitals, further jeopardizing the quality of care especially if schemes did not invest in capacity- or infrastructure-building to complement insurance reforms. The resulting, insufficient service quality control was seen in the articles not only to further disincentivize beneficiary enrollment but also to permit (in Kenya) a loss of resources to fraud. Articles across “all” countries in this subsection mentioned weak accountability and mismanagement to an extent. For details, please see Supplementary Table S6.

Discussion

While existing reviews have often examined individual health financing interventions, this study takes a broader view by synthesizing common design and implementation factors across multiple hospital financing mechanisms in LLMICs. It shows that many factors along the policy design and implementation cycles affect the performance of the implementation, spanning from governance at the top level to the specific human, material, and financial resources for the implementation at the bottom. By examining a diverse range of factors, this study identifies five common elements that significantly influence the implementation of hospital financing interventions in LLMICs, which are “effective governance with strong accountability, participatory engagement of key stakeholders, design features (e.g. financial incentives and distribution of roles) tailored to a given context, availability of implementation resources and capacity, and strong M&E.” To translate these insights into practice, a checklist based on these common factors has been developed (see Box 1).

This study underscores the important role played by effective governance in facilitating hospital financing interventions. Interventions within health financing inherently carry a political dimension (Sparkes et al. 2019), and its successful realization mandates a strong political commitment from the top leadership—an indispensable factor across all phases of reform—from policy formulation, to adoption, and to implementation. For policy formulation, strong political commitment ensures that hospital financing interventions are on the leadership’s agenda—for instance, as part of the UHC agenda. During the implementation, it helps align stakeholders’ interests and secure the essential financial and human resources requisite for successful implementation. Contrarily, a lack of dedicated and strong leadership support would likely result in the poor performance of the intervention, as evidenced in the study in Mali (Zitti et al. 2019). The application of political economy analysis assumes significance as a tool to understand the dynamics of power and leadership, offering invaluable political insights in supporting the implementation of health financing interventions (Sparkes et al. 2019).

Our analysis found the significance of embracing a participatory approach throughout the design and execution phases of hospital financing interventions. Central to the participatory approach is the continuous and inclusive involvement of key stakeholders across the entire reform cycle, encompassing not only design and execution but also the stages of M&E. The more comprehensive the reform is, the more active engagement is needed. However, it should be noted that participatory engagement may be nuanced by the specific characteristics of different hospital financing reforms. The orientation of engagement is shaped by contextual factors and the specific barriers encountered during policy formulation and implementation. For instance, if a user fee exemption fails to boost the utilization of a specific service, emphasis on people expected to use the service may need to be prioritized for stakeholder engagement. Participatory engagement is not merely the provision of information. Rather, it is a deliberative process aimed at fostering consensus and a profound comprehension of both policy design and implementation nuances, so that implementers can provide swift support for the hospital financing interventions (Khalife et al. 2017).

Successful policy implementation stands contingent upon a well-constructed policy design such as financial incentives, ensuring key attributes of a hospital financing reform fit with the local context—population demands, disease burden, healthcare system capabilities, performance shortcomings, and sociocultural factors, among others. It should be noted that the design features of hospital financing reforms vary significantly. For instance, one of the key design features of health insurance is the premium rate. A challenge in setting premium rates is to ensure the premium is affordable for potential beneficiaries while sufficient to sustain the benefit package. One approach to ensure congruence with the local context is the use of locally collected information for program design. For illustration, this includes the utilization of existing cost data for developing hospital payment rates, and the incorporation of willingness-to-pay information to inform the establishment of insurance premiums (Zeng et al. 2017a, Behzad et al. 2022). In scenarios where limited data are available in LLMICs, it becomes crucial to glean insights from countries with similar economic contexts. Conducting a comprehensive feasibility assessment of proposed health financing interventions would help evaluate various policy alternatives, thereby fostering a swift adaptation of the selected intervention to the local context while ensuring the selected intervention is aligned with the UHC goals.

The performance of implementing health financing interventions hinges upon the availability of adequate financial resources and technical capacity, which necessitates meticulous planning and strategic allocation for its seamless execution. In instances where the required technical and financial prerequisites are not concurrently met, it is advisable to avoid the precipitous start of the reform process (Tama et al. 2018). Although governments assume a major role in mobilizing resources to facilitate the implementation of the reform, the resource encompasses diverse sources such as governmental budget allocation and/or external assistance from developmental partners. Equally important for implementing health financing interventions is the strong technical capacity of implementers. Among many technical constraints, a lack of public finance management capacity stands out as one of the major barriers, particularly in the context of contracting reforms where the timely disbursement of payments is instrumental.

Across all the hospital financing interventions we reviewed, M&E emerges as an essential element for successful implementation. As a continuous management process, M&E helps identify design flaws and implementation bottlenecks to be addressed by corrective measures. Within M&E, one of the core components is to develop validity indicators to be monitored/evaluated and appropriate mechanisms to conduct M&E activities. In some hospital financing interventions, such as RBF programs, the measurement of inputs and outputs is an integral part of the program as the achievement is tied to the payment. Countries could leverage such programs to strengthen M&E capacities across the health sector. It is important to note that building a strong M&E and health information system is not only “programmatic” for hospital financing but also hospital management and clinical governance. By using it for all these purposes, it would help mitigate the risk of gaming the system.

In LLMICs, where resource limitations prevail, the implementation of hospital financing interventions seldom operates in isolation; rather, it frequently becomes an integral component of broader healthcare reform initiatives within a country—a trait, we acknowledge, not well covered in this synthesis, because of the design of our study. Additionally, the landscape of hospital financing reforms within a nation is dynamic, and continuously evolving. Taking Rwanda as an example, with support from development partners, a community health insurance scheme that covered selected hospital services was launched in the early 2000s. Simultaneously, to increase health service coverage with priority interventions and quality of care, an RBF program was also launched as a part of the national health care reform (Meessen et al. 2006, Logie et al. 2008, Basinga et al. 2011, Saksena et al. 2011, Sekabaraga et al. 2011). With the nation’s economic growth and increased demand for high-quality hospital care, a hospital accreditation component was subsequently added to the RBF program (Umuhoza et al. 2022). The health financing reform dynamics are also observed in the evolution of RBF programs across different countries, where the incentivized services are gradually expanded from maternal and child health services at primary health centers to hospital services. The extension to hospital care is partially based on the successful RBF programs in the ambulatory setting. The expansion of health services from ambulatory to inpatient care may take a reverse pattern. In Cambodia, the successful HEF program that originally supported high-cost hospital services for the poor has led to the extension of its benefits package to cover the payment for primary health services in health centers (Jacobs et al. 2018), and progressively became a key pillar of the national health financing strategy toward UHC. Given the dynamic nature of health financing reforms, countries need to take a comprehensive, holistic, and long-term approach when designing their hospital financing interventions. This long-term view only strengthens the case for sufficient investment in participatory governance or M&E. As seen in the cases of India or Ghana described previously, constant policy learning is also key (Akhnif et al. 2019).

We found a limited number of papers about the implementation of hospital financing interventions in LLMICs, underscoring a significant paucity of empirical evidence within this domain and the deficiency in comprehensive insights to effectively inform and guide hospital financing practices in LLMICs. Documentation of some hospital financing interventions, such as hospital autonomy, is concentrated in a limited number of countries (e.g. Iran). Despite some attention given to charging private wards to raise revenues (Alam and Ahmed 2010, Singh et al. 2021b), we did not find studies describing the process of their introduction and implementation. Similarly striking is the limited number of scientific papers covering the application of schemes such as user fee removal or RBF schemes to hospital settings: this is in stark contrast to the massive attention given to the applications to health centers.

We acknowledge that many hospital financing questions cannot be answered through this study due to the inherent constraints. For example, how can an LLMIC achieve better implementation outcomes when facing both financial and technical constraints? Among the identified common contextual factors, do these factors work differently in different categories of hospital financing reforms? This study is not a comprehensive evaluation of all factors that may affect the implementation of hospital financing interventions. Several factors contribute to this limitation. Firstly, there are many hospital financing interventions with their own uniqueness. Although we aimed to synthesize the common factors across interventions, some critical factors specific to certain hospital financing interventions may have been inadvertently overlooked. Even in the literature providing relevant information, there is little information on how identified contextual factors are strengthened. More detailed analyses of the process to enhance contextual factors’ effectiveness should be provided. Secondly, we found limited literature assessing implementation aspects of hospital financing reforms. More implementation science studies are needed in this field. Additionally, many studies do not provide a comprehensive framework with sufficient details to systematically assess design and implementation factors. Developing a comprehensive framework (e.g. the relationship among key stakeholders) that focuses on hospital financing reforms should be prioritized. Thirdly, although we used a comprehensive algorithm to conduct a literature search using a combination of keywords, it is likely that we missed some relevant articles for synthesis. For example, none of the LLMICs studied for this report were American countries, which may be a result of the team’s choice to exclude articles in Spanish and Portuguese. Additionally, our review was restricted to studies specifically focused on hospitals. We excluded studies of interventions implemented across various types of health facilities, such as PBF programs or health insurance schemes, where the effects on hospitals could not be clearly distinguished. This might have resulted in the omission of some relevant information. Finally, this study does not capture the long-term view of the implementation of hospital financing interventions. Initial flaws of the implementation may have been progressively addressed, but not reported. While recognizing these constraints, this study serves as a steppingstone toward an enhanced understanding of design and implementation factors surrounding hospital financing interventions in resource-constrained settings.

Conclusion

Implementing hospital financing interventions is complicated in LLMICs. In this study, we found few successful stories in carrying out hospital financing interventions. Most studies that we identified focus on challenges and constraints encountered by LLMICs during the implementation. It does not mean that the hospital financing interventions are not “good.” Instead, it highlights the key design and implementation factors that need to be addressed for success. This study has affirmed the important roles played by governance, stakeholder engagement through participatory approaches, the meticulous design of scheme features, the availability of both financial and technical resources, and M&E, among others. These factors interact with each other and collectively emerge as primary determinants that profoundly influence the effective implementation of hospital financing interventions. Countries may have their unique challenges beyond what is summarized in this paper, and hospital financing policy designers and implementers should identify those challenges and address them with target management approaches. Though this study aids the understanding of common issues to be avoided in designing and implementing hospital financing interventions in LLMICs, the evidence on specifics across different categories of hospital financing implementations remains very limited, calling for more implementation studies guided by comprehensive theoretical frameworks to generate more concrete evidence.
