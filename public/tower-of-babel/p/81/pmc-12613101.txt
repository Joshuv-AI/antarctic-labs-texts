Obesity effects on the health-related quality of life in Japan: Implications from a cross-sectional study
Authors: Takako Kaneyasu, Hideki Murasawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesObesity is a global health problem owing to its association with various diseases, in turn requiring various treatment modalities, including expensive drugs and surgeries. In Japan, the obese population is relatively small, thus the impact of the disease is considered to be smaller than that in other countries. However, the impact of obesity and various comorbidities on health-related quality of life (HRQOL) in Japan remains clear.MethodsA cross-sectional (web-based) survey targeting individuals with obesity in Japan was conducted in this study. The survey employed two kinds of HRQOL questionnaires: the MOS 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey (SF-36) and EuroQoL-5 Dimension (EQ-5D). HRQOL scores were calculated for each body mass index category normal weight, and obese 1–3, classes. The effects of obesity and comorbidities on HRQOL were examined using a mediation analysis.ResultsOf the survey respondents, 4279 individuals meeting the eligibility criteria were included in the analysis. HRQOL scores generally showed an inverse U-shaped curve peaking at obese l, over weight. Although comorbidities significantly influenced HRQOL decline, obese 2 or higher demonstrated a significant direct effect on HRQOL.ConclusionWhile some findings of this study differ from conventional views in Japan and other countries, the impact of obesity on HRQOL may be consistent with findings in other countries when the mediating effects through comorbidities are considered.Keywords: Obesity, Body mass index, Health-related quality of life, Comorbidities

Introduction

The prevalence of obesity, defined as a body mass index (BMI) of ≥30 (kg/m2), has increased significantly worldwide. In the United States, obesity rates in adults increased from approximately 30.5 % in 1999–2000 to 42.4 % in 2017–2018, and this trend continued as of 2020 [1,2]. In Japan, obesity defined by the above international standard is affect less than 5 % of the population [3,4].

Obesity is a risk factor for hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes. It has also been associated with heart disease, cancer, and kidney disease [[5], [6], [7]], making it a major global issue [2]. In Japan, wide range of options are available for disease management, including surgery and medical agents [8]. However, some of these accessible modalities are expensive or require continuous administration, raising concerns about their impact on medical insurance finances. Owing to this, their cost-effectiveness has been examined [9,10].

When analyzing the cost-effectiveness of obesity treatment, the extent to which the decline in the quality of life (QOL) caused by the condition can be alleviated by treatment is one of the significant factors considered in treatment effectiveness (quality-adjusted life years: QALYs) [11]. The health-related QOL (HRQOL) used in the analysis should preferably be evaluated in clinical trials in each country [12,13]. However, it is often difficult to evaluate HRQOL in clinical trials owing to limitations in the number of participants, and results from other surveys frequently serve as a supplement. Previous reports examining the impact of obesity on HRQOL [14,15] have illustrated that HRQOL scores, such as EuroQoL-5 Dimension (EQ-5D) and SF-36 (MOS 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey), change in an inverse U-shape with increased BMI.

The effect of obesity on EQ-5D scores is influenced by comorbidities (diabetes, cancer, and kidney disease) [3,16,17]. In some countries, obesity was reported to affect HRQOL for overweight individuals (BMI ≥25 kg/m2) [17,18]; however, a study by Takahara et al. [19] reported that the decline in HRQOL due to obesity in Japan becomes significant with severe obesity (BMI ≥35 kg/m2) when the impact of comorbidities is excluded. This finding serves as a basis for the cost-effectiveness evaluation of publicly funded anti-obesity drugs in Japan [20]. Nonetheless, as their study focused only on patients with diabetes, further investigation is needed in broader populations to validate these findings.

This study evaluated how BMI (obesity) affects HRQOL in the presence of comorbidities using two types of HRQOL (EQ-5D and SF-36) scores.

Methods

Ⅾata collection

We conducted a web survey with Cross Marketing Inc., targeting people aged ≥25 years who were registered in the Disease Panel [21] and who had been hospitalized or visited a hospital for obesity within the last year or who were currently struggling with obesity. Participants responded to questions regarding background factors and HRQOL questionnaires posted on the website between July and August 2023. The background factors included age, gender, height, weight, marital status, working status, education, annual household income, and 12 kinds of diseases (diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, hyperuricemia, coronary artery disease, stroke, fatty liver, abnormal menstruation, knee arthritis, chronic kidney disease, sleep apnea syndrome, and cancer) and other comorbidities. The HRQOL questionnaires used were SF-36 [22] and EQ-5D-5 Level (5L) [23]. The eligibility criteria were follows: among those who consented to participate in the study, individuals who appropriately answered HRQOL-related questions and reported their height and weight correctly, and who had a standard (normal) weight or above. Participants who did not meet these criteria (e.g., those who provided the same value for all HRQOL questions, reported height under 120 cm, or reported a BMI derived from height and weight below 10 or above 300) and those falling into the underweight (BMI <18.5 kg/m2) were excluded from the study. The research protocol was approved by the Ritsumeikan University Research Ethics Review Committee (BKC-LSMH-2024-016) on September 12, 2024.

HRQOL questionnaires

The SF-36 version 2 (Japanese Acute) was used. It is one of the most widely used instruments to measure HRQOL, comprising 36 questions across 8 domains: physical functioning (PF), role limitations due to physical problems (RP), bodily pain (BP), general health perceptions (GH), vitality (VT), social functioning (SF), role limitations due to emotional problems (RE), and mental health (MH). Higher scores (0–100) reflect better perceived health. The scores were standardized to a mean of 50 and a standard deviation of 10, with >50 representing a better-than-average health status and <50 representing a poorer-than-average health status. The scores were summarized into mental component scores (MCS) and physical component scores (PCS). The SF-36 score is used to determine the effectiveness of healthcare interventions. However, it has limited application in economic evaluation because it represents the patient profile and not their preferences [22,24].

The EQ-5D-5L consists of five questions focused on mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each question has five options, and participants chose the option that best described their current situation. The results of the questionnaire were converted into an index value that reflected the preferences of the Japanese population. In principle, the value ranges from 0 to 1; the closer the obtained value is to 1, the higher the HRQOL [23,24].

Descriptive statistics using obese classes

BMI was calculated from height and weight; obese classes were defined as follows: 18.5 ≤ BMI <25, normal weight; 25 ≤ BMI <30, overweight (obese 1 class); 30 ≤ BMI <35, obesity (obese 2 class); and BMI ≥35, severe obesity (obese 3 class). Background factors are expressed as frequencies and percentages, and chi-square tests were performed for between-group differences (Table 1).

The relationship between obesity and HRQOL

HRQOL scores for each obese class are presented as means with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). The U-shaped relationship between obesity class and HRQOL was confirmed using regression analysis with HRQOL score as the dependent variable and BMI (squared continuous value) as the explanatory variable, controlling for any comorbidities, age and gender, marital status, working status, education, and income as covariates (coefficients and confidence intervals for the squared BMI by domain and composite score are shown in Table 2; the effects of variables on the composite score are shown in Supplementary Table S1). In Japan, obesity is more prevalent among men [25], and HRQOL is influenced by age and marital status [26]. Previous Japanese reports also accounted for the effects of age and gender alongside comorbidities [15,19]. Therefore, the primary mediation analysis included only age and gender as covariates. Furthermore, in the aforementioned regression analysis, variables other than comorbidities, age, and gender that showed significant influence were marital status, employment status, and educational background.

Causal mediation analysis

The effects of interventions or exposures often influence outcomes not only directly but also through specific mediating variables. These mechanisms are often shown in causal diagrams. Mediation analysis can be applied in determining the presence of an indirect or direct effect and estimating its magnitude [27,28]. The mechanism assumed in this study is shown in Fig. 1.

Path diagram for assessing obesity effects on HRQOL scores

EQ5D: EuroQoL-5Dimention, SF-36: MOS 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey.

In the mediation analysis, we investigated both the direct effect of obesity exposure or presence (normal weight vs. obese 1–3) on HRQOL scores (mental-MCS; physical-PCS; and overall health perception-EQ-5D scores), and the indirect effect mediated by any comorbidities. In the series of mediation analyses, the total effect (TE) = natural indirect effect (NIE, hereinafter indirect effects) + natural direct effect (NDE, hereinafter direct effects) was used [28]. Linear regression was applied for the outcome and mediator model in the analysis, but when an analysis included a binary mediator (such as presence of any comorbidity), logistic regression was applied for the mediator model.

Age and gender were chosen as covariates for the primary mediation analysis. When a direct effect of obesity on HRQOL was confirmed, a sub-analysis was conducted adding marital status, working status, and education as covariates. The results are shown in Supplementary Table S2. In the disease-specific mediation analysis, we followed the sub analysis and used age, gender, marital status, working status, and education as covariates. The findings are shown in Supplementary Table S3 and S4.

Analysis was performed using Stata v18.1 for Windows (StataCorp, TX, USA) with the mediate command employed for mediation analysis. Data management and figure/table creation was performed in Microsoft Excel (Office 2016 for Microsoft, WA, USA).

Results

Herein, we requested the participation of 9128 people who had registered on the survey panel as "hospitalized for obesity within the past year" or "currently struggling with obesity"; 4443 agreed to participate in the survey and responded with heights and weights from which BMIs could be calculated. Of them, 129 did not respond to the questions in the EQ-5D. One individual answered “1” to all the questions in the SF-36, and 5 incorrectly entered their height or weight. Twenty-nine people were excluded because they were underweight class. The final analysis included 4279 subjects.

The backgrounds of the subjects are presented in Table 1. The average age of subjects was 49.7 (±9.3) years, and 41.2 % were female. Of the total participants, 52.3 % were married, 71.9 % were employed, 43.2 % had at least a university education, and 39.4 % had a household income of approximately 30,000 US dollars or more (1 USD = 147.5 JPY as of January 2025). Overall, 45.5 % had no comorbidity, 21.0 % self-reported dyslipidemia, 17.0 % self-reported having diabetes, 3.8 % self-reported having cancer, and 1.4 % self-reported having chronic kidney disease.

An inverse U-shaped relationship between obesity and the two HRQOL scores was observed, as shown in Fig. 2A and B. This relationship was significant for all SF-36 domain scores except MH, SF, BP, and the MCS composite score (Table 2). The influence of each variable on the composite scores revealed that, after adjusting for comorbidities, age, and gender, marital status significantly affected MCS, employment status significantly affected PCS, and education level significantly affected EQ-5D scores (Supplementary Table S1).

The primary mediation analysis (Table 3) showed that obesity had a significantly positive direct effect on EQ-5D scores in obese 1, and a significantly negative effect in obese 2 and 3. Similarly, the direct effect of obesity on PCS was significantly positive in obese 1, and significantly negative in obese 2 and 3. The direct effect on MCS was not significant in any obese class, and only the indirect effect showed a negative impact.

Since no direct negative effect of obesity was observed on MCS, the sub-analysis examined whether the impact of obesity on PCS and EQ-5D scores varied by covariates. The results showed that the effect of obesity on PCS did not change even when employment status, marital status, and education were added as variables. However, the effect of obesity on EQ-5D scores was found to be attenuated when marital status and employment status were combined with education; the significant negative effect observed in obese 2 was no longer present, (Supplementary Table S2).

The HRQOL of individuals with any comorbidity generally declined at obese 2 and 3, which was attributable to direct effects (Table 3). However, no direct effect was confirmed in any obese class in the MCS (Table 3). The indirect effect of obesity varies by disease; the indirect effect on EQ-5D scores in individuals with diabetes was confirmed in obese 1 and 2 (Supplementary Table S3). Conversely, the indirect effect on EQ-5D scores for individuals with hyperlipidemia was confirmed in all obese classes (as in Supplementary Table S3).

The direct effect of obesity on EQ-5D scores was consistently confirmed in obese 2 and 3, regardless of any comorbidity (Supplementary Table S3). The direct effect of obesity for MCS was not consistently observed, as shown in Supplementary Table S4–1. The direct effect for PCS was observed in obese 2 and 3, regardless of any comorbidity (Supplemental Table S4–2).

Discussion

Reports indicating that HRQOL scores change in an inverse U-shaped relationship with obesity have been documented not only in Japan [15,19] but also in Asia and Western countries [14,[29], [30], [31]]. However, those peaks are occurred at normal weight 18.5 ≤ BMI <25), and the finding in this study that scores are higher in obese 1 (25 ≤ BMI <30) than in normal weight individuals. That was only observed in some of the reports by Takahara et al. (multivariate-adjusted analysis) [19] and Laxy et al. (Hispanic male population) [30].

The impact of obesity on HRQOL included not only direct effects of obesity but also indirect effects mediated by comorbidities. Indirect effects mediated by comorbidities were observed not only in MCS but also in PCS and EQ-5D scores, and were significantly negative compared to normal weight across all obesity levels (Table 3). However, the direct effect of obesity on EQ-5D scores and PCS was significantly positive in the obese 1, overweight, compared to normal weight. Consequently, the total effect at obese 1 showed no significant difference. This positive influence supports the observation that HRQOL generally peaked at obese 1. In contrast, the direct effect of obesity on EQ-5D scores and PCS was significantly negative for obese 2 and 3. The direct effect of obesity on EQ-5D was no longer significant in obese 2 depending on covariates, suggesting a similar situation may have occurred in the multivariate analysis by Takahara et al. [20].

With advances in obesity treatment, efforts are underway both domestically and internationally to evaluate and apply the decline in EQ-5D scores associated with weight loss [14,18,[30], [31], [32]]. In this study, a significant direct effect of obesity for EQ-5D scores was confirmed in obese 2 and 3, among people with any comorbidity (Supplementary Table S3). This was consistent with the Japanese report by Takahashi et al. (analysis adjusted for minimal factors) [19]. In contrast, obesity had a direct negative impact on EQ-5D scores only at obese 3 in Supplementary Table 2–1, which uses the same five covariates as Table S3. The indirect effect of obesity on EQ-5D scores also differed between Table S3 and Table S2-1. These differences would stem from Table S3 only including individuals with one of the 12 specific diseases and Table S2–1 including individuals with diverse disease backgrounds. The indirect effect of obesity on PCS varied by disease, similar to EQ-5D scores (Supplementary Table S4–1). However, overall, obesity negatively affect PCS, that was consistently observed in obesity grades 2 and 3. This indicates that obesity exerts a greater direct effect on PCS. These results indicate that excluding obesity without accounting for indirect disease effects would underestimate its impact on HRQOL. In other words, the effect of obesity on HRQOL should be considered present even at obese 2.

This study has a few limitations. First, it was based on a cross-sectional survey and, therefore, does not replace changes in HRQOL [32] due to changes in weight or BMI over time. Caution is required in interpreting causal relationships. Additionally, the SF-36 summary score was calculated using two components for comparison. Unlike other countries, the use of a three-component summary score is recommended for Japanese respondents [33]. Therefore, analysis using three components should also be considered in the future. The mediation analysis assumes that there are no unobserved variables [27,28]; to consider latent variables, it is necessary to examine variables and structures using structural equation modeling and then perform a mediation analysis. Lastly, comorbidity was reported spontaneously by the respondents in the cross-sectional survey of this study and was not evaluated by medical professionals. For this reason, careful consideration is required when using these findings for analyses demanding high precision, such as cost-effectiveness analyses. When HRQOL assessments in daily clinical practice become accessible to third parties, consideration of data-based analysis will be required.

Conclusion

This study found that the HRQOL of Japanese individuals generally follows an inverse U-shaped curve, peaking at obese 1, overweight, and increasing obesity. The decline in HRQOL associated with obesity was primarily due to its effects on physical health. The presence of comorbidities affected HRQOL, however, the impact of obesity was observed above obese 2. These results differ from some aspects from previous reports in Japan and other countries. However, when considering indirect effects mediators, the negative effect of obesity on HRQOL was similar to the results from other countries. While the effects of obesity may vary by region, caution is warranted regarding the decline in HRQOL.
