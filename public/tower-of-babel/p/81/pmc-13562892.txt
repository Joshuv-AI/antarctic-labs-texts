DJ-1-Binding Compound Improves Peripheral Nerve Injury in the Spinal Dorsal Horn by Restraining STAT3-Mediated Neurotoxic Reactive Astrocytes
Authors: Shengtao Wang, Jieyong Cai, Qingsong Zhou, Ming Zhao, Wei Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Astrocytes are significantly activated after peripheral nerve injury (PNI) and affect the nerve repair by mediating neuroinflammation. DJ-1 is highly expressed in astrocytes, and its binding compounds exert significant neuroprotective effects by regulating antioxidant and anti-inflammatory responses. This study is designed to investigate the therapeutic effects of DJ-1-binding compound P-DJ-1A in a rat PNI model induced by spared nerve injury (SNI) surgery. The results revealed that SNI surgery induced the aggregation of activated astrocytes and the phosphorylated form of STAT3 in the ipsilateral dorsal spinal horn (DSH), which was concentrated mainly in the projection area of the injured nerve. Moreover, SNI surgery led to the extensive induction of proinflammatory and neurotoxic mediators, including IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-18, and C3, while reducing the expression of TGF-β, IL-10, and S100A10. Treatment with P-DJ-1A or AG490 alleviated mechanical hyperalgesia and thermal hyperalgesia induced by SNI surgery, reduced “A1” astrocyte activation, and inhibited the expression of proinflammatory cytokines but increased the levels of TGF-β, IL-10, and S100A10. Mechanistically, P-DJ-1A or AG490 inhibited STAT3 activation and the NF-κB/NLRP3/IL-18 pathway. Collectively, the DJ-1-binding compound P-DJ-1A can alleviate SNI-induced injury by modulating neurotoxic astrocyte activation and inhibiting neuroinflammation, highlighting its potential as a novel therapeutic strategy.

1. Introduction

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) is a common clinical issue. This condition is usually associated with trauma and rarely caused by iatrogenic intervention, leading to the loss of nerve structure and/or dysfunction.1,2 In the United Kingdom, the annual incidence of this condition between 2005 and 2020 was 11.2 cases per 100,000 people (95% confidence interval: 10.9–11.6). 3 Pathologic factors such as penetrating injury, compression, stretching, and ischemia can cause partial or complete loss of motor and sensory function, limb disability, and neuropathic pain, resulting in severe and long-lasting functional and physiological impairments. 2 Although autologous nerve transplantation is commonly recognized as the best method for peripheral nerve repair, its intrinsic limitations have greatly limited its clinical application. 4 A nerve conduit technology scheme combining electrical stimulation, magnetic stimulation, and Schwann cells can serve as an effective strategy for repairing large-gap nerve injuries, providing a new approach for research on the repair of large-gap nerve injuries. 5 The regeneration process of severe injuries is complex and has limitations, which may lead to the aggravation of muscle atrophy. 6

Significant activation of microglia and astrocytes occurs in the spinal cord and brainstem after PNI. The variable, complex, and dynamic activation process of glial cells not only promotes the development of behavioral hyperalgesia but also facilitates peripheral nerve regeneration.7-9 As resident immune cells of the central nervous system, activated microglia play a pivotal role in the development of neuropathic pain following nerve injury. CD11b (αM integrin), a classic marker of microglial activation, regulates phagocytic function, cytokine release, and inflammatory responses.10,11 Studies have indicated that in multiple peripheral nerve injury models, including spared nerve injury (SNI), chronic constriction injury (CCI), and spinal nerve ligation (SNL), dense clusters of CD11b-positive cells form in the ipsilateral dorsal horn of the spinal cord. Moreover, microglial activation precedes the onset of pain behavior, and inhibiting microglial activation effectively alleviates neuropathic pain. 12

Astrocytes originate from different lineages and play crucial roles in an SNL model of neuropathic pain. 13 Mice with spinal cord injury (SCI)-induced damage exhibit significant pain symptoms and astrocyte activation in the lumbar segment. Selective depletion of resident astrocytes in the lumbar segment can alleviate neuropathic pain and activate microglia. 14 In a state of chronic pain, astrocyte proliferation in the spinal dorsal horn (SDH) is more prominent than microglial proliferation after nerve injury. 15 In an SNI mouse model, the SDH is associated with a reactive astrocyte phenotype, exacerbating neuropathic pain. Gpr37L1 has been identified as a specific marker of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) for spinal astrocytes. Knockdown of Gpr37L1 in astrocytes of the superficial dorsal horn can induce astrocyte proliferation and pain hypersensitivity, suggesting that modulating the activation of astrocytes by Gpr37L1 is a potential strategy to reverse neuropathic pain following nerve injury. 16 PNI can trigger astrocyte activation, accompanied by the upregulation of neurotrophic factors in the anterior horn of the spinal cord. 17 Therefore, mediating astrocyte activation in the early stage of PNI may contribute to nerve regeneration and the recovery of motor function.

DJ-1 is a protein encoded by the PARK7 gene with a molecular weight of 19.9 kDa. It was initially identified as an oncogene in 1997 and was later found to be associated with early-onset Parkinson’s disease (PD). 18 It plays a vital role in a range of neurological disorders, including Parkinson’s disease, 19 traumatic brain injury, 20 and SCI. 21 DJ-1 is widely expressed in multiple CNS cells (including astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes), with the highest expression level observed in astrocytes. 22 PARK7/DJ-1 is involved in maintaining the balance of the intestinal microbiota and regulating intestinal inflammatory responses; thus, it has emerged as a potential therapeutic target for central nervous system diseases. 23 SiRNA-mediated downregulation of DJ-1 expression enhances NLRP3 inflammasome-induced neuroinflammation. 24 In contrast, sodium benzoate administration increases DJ-1 expression and alleviates neurological dysfunction in a SCI model by inhibiting the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome complex mediated by the SOCS1/Rac1/ROS pathway. 25 DJ-1 is involved in a variety of cellular processes, including the regulation of neuronal apoptosis, 26 cellular oxidative stress, 27 mitophagy, 25 neuroinflammation, 28 and protein repair. 29 In addition to its direct antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, DJ-1 has been implicated in the regulation of key inflammatory signaling pathways, particularly the JAK2/STAT3 axis. STAT3 is a critical transcription factor that drives proinflammatory gene expression and promotes the development of neurotoxic “A1” astrocytes following nerve injury. 30 Choi et al reported that in models of neurological injury, STAT3 is activated within 3 hours post-injury, while pSTAT3 levels are significantly reduced in brain sections from DJ-1 knockout mice, accompanied by markedly diminished astrocytic proliferation. 31 These findings suggest that DJ-1 may act as a regulator of STAT3 signaling and astroglial reactivity. Therefore, pharmacological targeting of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway represents a rational approach for investigating the mechanisms through which DJ-1 regulates neuroinflammation.

The protein encoding DJ-1 against-1 (P-DJ-1A), also known as compound 23, can specifically target DJ-1. DJ-1 antibody-1, a DJ-1-binding compound, specifically targets DJ-1. Research has indicated that P-DJ-1A does not directly increase DJ-1 expression levels but instead protects DJ-1 activity through physical binding, thereby enhancing its inherent cell-protective function. 32 The cysteine residue at position 106 (C106) of DJ-1 is its most susceptible oxidation site, and P-DJ-1A specifically binds to this C106 site to inhibit excessive oxidation. 32 P-DJ-1A can cross the blood‒brain barrier (BBB), can act as a neuroprotective agent, and has potential for the treatment of PD. 33 In this study, the neuroprotective effect of a DJ-1-binding compound was investigated in a rat model of P via intrathecal infusion via an osmotic minipump. Mechanical allodynia, body weight changes, and histopathological alterations in lumbar spinal cord sections will be detected, and changes in astrocyte activation, neuronal damage, and demyelination will also be evaluated. This study provides a novel strategy for the treatment of PNI.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Animals

Adult male Sprague–Dawley rats (aged 8–10 weeks) with body weights ranging from 200–250 grams were used. All animals were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Room of Chengdu Medical College. After a 3-day acclimation period, the rats were randomly divided into groups and housed individually in an environment with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle. The room temperature was maintained at a constant 23±2°C, and the rats had free access to water and food. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the requirements of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health (NIH, USA), and the experimental protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Pidu District People’s Hospital (Approval No.20230242).

2.2. PNI Modeling

The rats underwent anesthesia via intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital. The spared nerve injury model, which was previously established in rats, 34 involved the following specific steps: A small incision was made in the lateral thigh skin to expose the sciatic nerve and its three branches, that is, the sural nerve, common peroneal nerve, and tibial nerve. The tibial nerve and common peroneal nerve were transected after fine ligation, while the sural nerve was preserved intact. During nerve ligation, rapid contraction and trembling of the ipsilateral hindlimb occurred, indicating successful establishment of the sciatic nerve injury model. For the sham operation group, the same surgical steps described above were performed, except that no ligation or transection was conducted. When the wound was sutured, the muscle layer was sutured with 6.0 silk thread, and the skin layer was sutured with 5.0 silk thread.

2.3. Drug Treatment

An intraperitoneal injection of a 1% sodium pentobarbital solution was used to induce deep anesthesia in the rats. After the L5 spinous process was removed, a PE-10 catheter filled with sterile normal saline was inserted into the intervertebral space between the L5 and L6 segments of the spinal cord. A sign of accurate catheter positioning was that bilateral hindlimb paralysis was immediately induced after the injection of 2% lidocaine, and this effect disappeared within 30 minutes. Animals with abnormal behaviors (such as paralysis or infection) were excluded. The tail flick response was used as an indicator of successful injection. The animals were allowed to recover for at least 4 days. 35 The protein encoding DJ-1 antigen-1 (P-DJ-1A) is a DJ-1-binding compound (catalog number: HY-137262; MedChemExpress, USA). It was dissolved in 1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). To investigate whether the effects of P-DJ-1A are mediated through inhibition of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, we used AG490 as a pharmacological tool to block STAT3 activation. The JAK2 inhibitor tyrphostin AG490 (catalog number: HY-12000; MedChemExpress, USA) was dissolved in 1% DMSO. P-DJ-1A (5 and 20 μg/10 μl) 33 or AG490 (1 μg/10 μl) 36 was administered via intraperitoneal injection at 24 hours and 1 hour before spinal nerve ligation (SNL) surgery, followed by once-daily injections for 2 consecutive days after surgery.

2.4. Behavioral Tests

Behavioral assessments were conducted following methods described previously. 37 All the rats were allowed to acclimate to the environment for a minimum of 3 days prior to baseline measurements. To evaluate mechanical sensitivity, the PWT was determined by applying von Frey filaments (Aesthesio, North Coast, USA) to the plantar surface of the hind paws. The updown method was employed to determine the 50% paw withdrawal threshold. 38 For the thermal sensitivity assessment, the PWL was measured with a plantar test apparatus (Model 7370, Ugo Basile, Comerio, Italy) following the procedure described by Hargreaves et al. 39 Throughout the behavioral tests, the operator was blinded to the experimental design and group assignments.

2.5. Cell Culture

Primary astrocyte culture and treatment were performed following previously described methods. 40 Spinal cord tissues were obtained from 1-day-old postnatal rats, cut into small pieces of approximately 1 mm3 using ophthalmic scissors, and mixed with an equal volume of 0.25% trypsin. The mixture was placed in a CO2 incubator for digestion at 37°C for 25 minutes, with intermittent shaking during the process. After digestion, the reaction was terminated by the addition of serum-containing complete medium (DMEM/F-12 + 10% fetal bovine serum + 1% P/S). The suspension was centrifuged, and the pellet was resuspended in complete medium. After repeated pipetting, the suspension was filtered through a 70 μm pore size cell strainer. The cell density was adjusted, and the single-cell suspensions were seeded into culture flasks. The flasks were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2. After 24 hours, the nonadherent cells on the surface were washed away with PBS, and the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium for continued cultivation. On the 3rd day, the cultures were shaken at 200 rpm for 20 minutes, after which the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium for further culture. This process was repeated on the 6th day. Immunostaining was performed using a rabbit anti-GFAP antibody (1:100; Thermo Fisher, 13--0300) to evaluate astrocyte purity, which was confirmed to be greater than 90%. All the experiments used cells from passages 6 to 8. To establish an in vitro inflammatory model, astrocytes were stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL) for 24 hours and subsequently treated with ATP (5 mM) for 30 minutes.

2.6. Cell Transfection

To verify the anti-inflammatory effect of DJ-1 in astrocytes, small interfering RNA (siRNA) was used to downregulate DJ-1 expression for subsequent analysis. The DJ-1 siRNA and negative control RNA used for transfection were purchased from Thermo Fisher. Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher, 11668030) was used to transfect the siRNAs into primary cultured astrocytes. At 48 hours post-transfection, the level of DJ-1 was detected via WB to confirm the transfection efficiency.

2.7. Immunofluorescence

Under deep anesthesia with isoflurane (2.5%), the rats were perfused intracardially with 0.1 M PBS, followed by 4% ice-cold paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS. Afterward, the L4‒L6 spinal cord segments were removed and postfixed in 4% PFA for 4 h and subsequently dehydrated in 30% sucrose solution overnight at 4°C. The collected spinal cord samples were cut into 20 μm thick sections with a cryostat (CM1900; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The frozen sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in 1× PBS supplemented with 3% donkey serum (Oumarsi, OMHD-E0074) and 0.3% Triton X-100 (Biosharp, P0096-100 ml). The primary antibodies used in this study included rabbit anti-DJ-1 (1:500; Thermo Fisher; MA5-32297), rabbit anti-GFAP (1:100; Thermo Fisher; 13-0300), and rabbit anti-CD11b (1:100; Thermo Fisher; PA5-79533) antibodies. The sections were first incubated with primary antibodies in the same buffer at 37°C for 30 minutes, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C. They were then washed three times in PBS (10 minutes per wash) and incubated with an Alexa 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:400; Beyotime, A0423) at 25°C for 1 hour. After additional PBS rinses, the sections were mounted, and alterations in the ipsilateral SDH were visualized via a confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, SP2 AOBS) using LCS Leica software for image acquisition.

2.8. Histological Examinations

Spinal cord sections (20 μm thickness) were prepared. Commercial staining kits, including a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit (#G1120; Solarbio, Beijing, China), luxol fast blue (LFB) (#G3240; Solarbio, Beijing, China) staining kit, and Nissl staining kit (#G1434; Solarbio, Beijing, China), were subsequently used to assess histopathological alterations, myelination, and neuronal degeneration status, respectively. All procedures were performed strictly under the instructions of the producers. Finally, a light microscope (IX53, Olympus, Japan) was used to observe the histopathological changes.

2.9. Western Blot

Spinal cord tissue blocks or PBS-washed cells were placed into centrifuge tubes together with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B), and the centrifuge tubes were placed in a TissueMaster™ tissue homogenizer (Beyotime, E6618) for homogenization. The mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4°C for 10 minutes to collect the supernatant, which was subsequently centrifuged again. The total protein concentration was determined via a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, P0010S). Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by SDS‒PAGE, and the proteins were subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. The protein membranes were subsequently washed with Western washing buffer (Beyotime, P0023B) for 1–2 minutes, followed by blocking with Western blocking buffer (Beyotime, P0023B) for 1 hour. Specific antibodies against target proteins (DJ-1, p-STAT3, STAT3, p-NF-κB, NF-κB, NLRP3, GFAP, CD11b, CD32, CD86, CD163, CD206, C3, and S100A10) were added. The antibodies used in the experiments included a rabbit anti-DJ-1 antibody (1:1000; MA5-32297; Thermo Fisher), a rabbit anti-STAT3 antibody (1:3000; MA1-13042; Thermo Fisher), a rabbit anti-NF-κB antibody (1:1000; MA5-53584; Thermo Fisher), a rabbit anti-NLRP3 antibody (1:1000; MA5-255; Thermo Fisher), a rabbit anti-GFAP antibody (1:5000; MA5-32078; Thermo Fisher), a rabbit anti-CD11b antibody (1:1000; PA5-79533; Thermo Fisher), a rabbit anti-CD32 antibody (1:500; PA5-105099; Thermo Fisher), a rabbit anti-CD86 antibody (1:5000; MA5-32078; Thermo Fisher), a rabbit anti-CD163 antibody (1:3000; MA1-13042), a rabbit anti-CD206 antibody (1:1000; MA5-16656), a rabbit anti-C3 antibody (1:1000; MA3-916; Thermo Fisher), a rabbit anti-S100A10 antibody (1:1000; PA5-95505; Thermo Fisher), and ox-DJ-1 (Cys106) (1:3000, MABN1773, Millipore). After blocking, the membranes were sequentially incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight and then with secondary antibodies (HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, dilution 1:1000; Beyotime, A0208) at room temperature. The protein signals were visualized via an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit and developed with X-film. The Western blot analysis was repeated 3 times, and the optical density values of the detected proteins were obtained via ImageJ software.

2.10. RT‒PCR

After the SDH tissues of the rats were collected, total RNA was extracted from the tissue blocks via the MagaBio plus Total RNA Purification Kit II (Bioer, BSC69), and its quality and concentration were determined via a NanoDrop Microvolume Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher). A cDNA First-Strand Synthesis Kit (Beyotime, D7178S) was used to synthesize the first-strand cDNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed via the SYBR Green method. Each PCR system consisted of 5 μL of cDNA, 2 μL of Green-High Rox Premix (2×) (Beyotime, D7265-5 ml), 0.8 μL of relevant primers (0.25 μM), and 2.2 μL of sterile distilled water. After the reaction system was added to a 96-well plate, it was run on a real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR instrument (Thermo Fisher, ABI Prism 7300) with the following program: 95°C predenaturation for 30 s; 40 PCR cycles (95°C for 5 s, 60°C for 30 s); and melting curve analysis (95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 1 min, 95°C for 15 s). Each sample was subjected to triplicate amplification in the same instrument to measure the mRNA levels of target genes (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-18, TGF-β, and IL-10). GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene. The sequences of the primers used were as follows:

2.11. Data Analysis

The results are presented as the mean ± SD, with p < 0.05 set as the threshold for statistical significance. All the statistical tests were completed using Windows GraphPad Prism version 10.4.2. For behavioral tests measured at multiple time points (baseline and at 1, 3, 7, and 14 days post-surgery), two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the effects of treatment, time, and their interaction. When a significant main effect or interaction was detected, Bonferroni post hoc correction was conducted for multiple comparisons between groups at each time point. For other experiments involving comparisons between two groups, Student's t test was used. For comparisons among multiple groups at a single time point, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test (for equal variances) or Dunnett’s T3 test (for unequal variances) was applied.

With the 50% PWT and PWL as indicators, behavioral tests were conducted 3 days before surgery (-3 d) and on the day of surgery to serve as baselines. 3.2. Activated Microglia and Astrocytes Are Detected in the SDH Following SNI

WB revealed that the expression of CD11b, a marker of microglia/macrophages, was significantly increased in the SDH of SNI rats but was continuously upregulated over time after modeling (Figure 2A). The immunostaining results for CD11b revealed that in the sham group, the CD11b-positive cells had small cell bodies with slender, highly branched processes distributed uniformly in the tissue. In the SDH of SNI rats at 14 days post-surgery, CD11b-positive cells presented significantly enlarged cell bodies, with processes becoming notably thicker, shorter, and more densely distributed (Figure 2B). WB was used to detect the protein expression levels of CD32, CD86, CD163, and CD206. The results demonstrated that the expression of CD32 and CD86 continuously increased over time in SNI model rats, whereas the expression of CD163 and CD206 first increased but then decreased over time (Figure 2C). Compared with that in the sham group, the expression of the astrocyte marker GFAP significantly increased in the SDH of SNI rats and was continuously upregulated over time after modeling (Figure 2D). GFAP immunostaining revealed that, in the sham group, the GFAP-positive cells presented small cell bodies with slender, dense, and highly branched processes, resulting in uniform fluorescent signals. In the SDH of SNI rats at 14 days post-surgery, GFAP-positive cells presented significantly enlarged cell bodies, with markedly thickened and elongated processes, and the overall fluorescence signal intensity increased (Figure 2E). WB was performed to detect the protein expression levels of C3 and S100A10. Compared with that in the sham group, the protein expression level of C3 in the SDH in the SNI model group significantly increased over time. The protein expression of S100A10 gradually decreased, with a slight increase at 14 days, but was still significantly lower than that in the sham group (Figure 2F). Compared with those in the sham group, the mRNA expression levels of the proinflammatory factors IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-18 in the SDH of SNI rats significantly increased and gradually increased over time. 3.3. P-DJ-1A Is Antinociceptive in SNI Rats

To evaluate the effect of the DJ-1-binding compound P-DJ-1A on SNI-induced allodynia in rats, behavioral tests for assessing mechanical allodynia were conducted on days 1, 3, 7, and 14 after surgery following treatment with P-DJ-1A or the JAK2 inhibitor AG490. For PWT (Figure 3A), two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant main effects of treatment (F3,20 = 64.04, P < 0.001) and time (F2.827, 56.53 = 600.5, P < 0.001) and a significant treatment × time interaction (F12,80 = 5.070, P < 0.001). Compared with the SNI+vehicle group, the P-DJ-1A group exhibited significantly greater PWTs at 7 and 14 days post-surgery (all P < 0.001), whereas the PWT of the AG490 group significantly increased (P < 0.001 at 7 days, P < 0.01 at 14 days). For PWL (Figure 3B), two-way repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant main effects of treatment (F3,20 = 13.47, P < 0.001) and time (F2.023, 40.46 = 407.7, P < 0.001) and a significant treatment × time interaction (F12,80 = 8.155, P < 0.001). Both P-DJ-1A and AG490 significantly increased the PWL compared with that in the SNI+vehicle group at 7 and 14 days post-surgery (P < 0.001). Next, histopathological changes, especially neuropathic damage, were detected using H&E staining, Nissl staining, and LFB staining. In the sham group, the tissue structure and morphology of the neurons were normal, and the Nissl bodies were clear. 4. Discussion

DJ-1 plays a crucial role in neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects in neurological diseases. Our study revealed that in the SDH of SNI model rats, the expression of DJ-1 increased in the early stage but decreased in the middle and late stages (7–14 days), whereas the inflammation-related proteins STAT3, NF-κB, and NLRP3 remained activated. The role of DJ-1 in the pathogenesis of PD has been extensively studied. Mutation or decreased expression of DJ-1 can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired autophagy, neuroinflammation, loss of the neurotransmitter dopamine, and cell death.18,25 In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), reduced DJ-1 expression is associated with the exacerbation of neuronal pyroptosis and neuroinflammation; by regulating the NF-κB/NLRP3/caspase-1 signaling pathway, DJ-1 alleviates AD-related pathological changes. 41 Owing to its ability to exert neuroprotective effects through mechanisms such as antioxidative stress, maintenance of mitochondrial function, and attenuation of neuroinflammation, DJ-1 has gradually been regarded as a novel target for the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases.42,43 Notably, decreased DJ-1 expression not only is associated with central neurodegenerative diseases but also may play a role in peripheral neuronal excitability and painful neuropathy. 44 A study by Lee 44 revealed that DJ-1 expression in mouse dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons controls cold hypersensitivity and neuropathy via TRPA1; targeting DJ-1 can alleviate symptoms associated with chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy (CIPN). DJ-1 agonists (e.g., betulin and cyclosporine A) can exert neuroprotective effects by upregulating DJ-1 expression and leveraging its antioxidative and mitochondrial function-regulating properties.45,46 P-DJ-1A (compound 23) is capable of binding to endogenous DJ-1 and exerting neuroprotective effects in PD and cerebral ischemia models, whereas these effects are abrogated in DJ-1 knockout animals.33,47 The results of this study suggest that targeting DJ-1 has potential as a therapeutic target for neuropathic pain.

The JAK/STAT pathway, an upstream signaling pathway for the secretion of many cytokines, is involved in mediating cell growth and death and plays a key role in the neuroinflammatory process. 48 NF-κB is a transcription factor that performs critical functions in immune regulation and inflammation and has become an important therapeutic target for various diseases. 49 The NLRP3 inflammasome is a key regulator of neuroinflammation; it exacerbates neuroinflammation by promoting the proliferation of astrocytes and microglia and the release of proinflammatory factors (such as IL-1β and IL-18). 50 Studies have reported that in microglia, DJ-1 inhibits the STAT1 pathway by promoting the interaction between SHP-1 and STAT1; it directly binds to the p65 subunit of NF-κB to suppress the activation of the NF-κB pathway, and the DJ-1‒NRF2 axis inhibits NLRP3 activation via TRX1. 51 Deficiency of DJ-1 leads to excessive phosphorylation of STAT1 in microglia and astrocytes, ultimately increasing the release of IFN-γ-induced proinflammatory factors. 52 In neurological diseases, astrocytes exhibit resistance to endoplasmic reticulum stress. These cells activate inflammatory responses by triggering the JAK1-STAT3 signaling pathway, resulting in the excessive production of proinflammatory factors such as IL-6, IL-12, and IL-23. 48 DJ-1 is highly expressed in reactive astrocytes, 22 and overexpression of DJ-1 in astrocytes can regulate the inflammatory response of astrocytes and exert neuroprotective effects in PD. 53 Consistent results were obtained in the present study: the administration of P-DJ-1A could sustainably upregulate the expression of DJ-1 in the SDH of SNI rats. By inhibiting the STAT3, NF-κB, and NLRP3 signaling pathways, P-DJ-1A suppressed the activation of microglia and astrocytes, reduced the polarization of M1-type microglia and A1-type astrocytes, and alleviated the neuroinflammatory response. Consistent results were also observed in in vitro cell experiments. In contrast, the knockdown of DJ-1 in primary cultured astrocytes not only induced spontaneous inflammation under basal conditions but also promoted the polarization of astrocytes toward the neurotoxic A1 phenotype under LPS/ATP stimulation, significantly increasing the release of proinflammatory factors (e.g., IL-1β and IL-6).

This study revealed that DJ-1 is a key endogenous inhibitor of peripheral neuropathic pain. It exerts a potent anti-inflammatory effect by inhibiting excessive activation of the STAT3/NF-κB-NLRP3 signaling axis and regulating the function of astrocytes (Figure 7). Exogenous enhancement of DJ-1 function may serve as a potential novel therapeutic strategy for peripheral neuropathic pain. Our study focused primarily on the function of DJ-1 in regulating neuroinflammation and did not investigate its role in antioxidative stress and the maintenance of mitochondrial function. In neuropathic pain, mitochondrial dysfunction and the activation of NADPH oxidases (e.g., NOX2 and NOX4) lead to the massive production of ROS.54,55 The excessive accumulation of ROS not only directly damages neurons but also acts as a signaling molecule to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB pathway, thereby exacerbating inflammation. 56 In turn, inflammatory factors can also induce the production of more ROS. Future studies may focus on the roles of DJ-1 in antioxidative stress and the maintenance of mitochondrial function to explore the comprehensive mechanism of DJ-1 in peripheral neuropathic pain.

4.1. Limitations of the Study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, while our loss-of-function experiments using siRNA knockdown demonstrated that endogenous DJ-1 is essential for restraining neurotoxic astrocyte polarization (Figure 6), gain-of-function evidence through direct overexpression of DJ-1 was not obtained in this study. Future investigations employing viral vector-mediated overexpression of DJ-1 in astrocytes or neurons, both in vitro and in vivo, would provide complementary causal evidence to further validate the protective role of DJ-1. Second, the sample size were not determined by power analysis. Although this sample size (n=6) is consistent with commonly used standards for neuropathic pain research and yielded statistically significant results, the lack of a prespecified power calculation may increase the risk of type II error and limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies with larger sample sizes and prospective power analyses could further validate the conclusions. Third, the immune response in the nervous system typically involves complex intercellular crosstalk. Studies have shown that in PD, DJ-1 in neurons inhibits the excessive activation of microglia and alleviates neuroinflammation by regulating chemokines such as CX3CL1. 43 Future studies could further explore whether DJ-1 is directly involved in the communication between nerve cells in peripheral nerve pain-related diseases. Forth, although P-DJ-1A has demonstrated significant neuroprotective effects in diseases such as PD, its toxic structure limits its clinical application. 57 Through in silico virtual screening, several candidate molecules (e.g., SS2) have been identified. These molecules can cross the BBB, exhibit good DJ-1 binding affinity, and have low toxicity, making them potential alternatives to P-DJ-1A. 57 Future research needs to further verify the neuroprotective effects of these candidate molecules in different disease models.

5. Conclusion

DJ-1-binding compounds can effectively alleviate SNI by upregulating DJ-1 expression, inhibiting the STAT3/NF-κB/NLRP3 axis and activating neurotoxic astrocytes, thereby suppressing neuroinflammation (Figure 7). This study provides a new reference for the treatment of neuropathic pain mediated by peripheral nerve injury.
