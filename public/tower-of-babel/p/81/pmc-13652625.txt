Intraparenchymal Occipital Cyst Formation after Contralateral Dural Arteriovenous Fistula Obliteration: A Case Report
Authors: Sho Hanai, Kiyoyuki Yanaka, Kuniyuki Onuma, Hitoshi Aiyama, Michihide Kajita, Eiichi Ishikawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Postoperative intracranial cyst formation is a rare complication. We report a case of an air-containing cyst in the contralateral left occipital lobe parenchyma after ligation of a dural arteriovenous fistula of the right transverse sinus. We reviewed related literature and discussed the mechanisms of this rare complication. A 59-year-old man presented with a right transverse sinus dural arteriovenous fistula with cortical reflux discovered incidentally during a brain checkup. The patient underwent surgical ligation of the draining vein combined with spinal drainage for intraoperative brain relaxation and to minimize retraction. The next day, there were no complications, and the subcutaneous drain was removed. Two days after surgery, right lower-quarter blindness appeared, and computed tomography showed the formation of a cyst in the contralateral left occipital lobe containing blood mixed with the cerebrospinal fluid and air. The fluid and air that accumulated in the epidural space and subcutaneously could have been drawn into the brain parenchyma through the dural tear on the surface of the left occipital lobe via the check valve mechanism and “reverse soda bottle effect,” resulting in cyst formation. The described complication appears to result from a coincidental combination of factors such as inadequate dural closure, unrecognized dural injury, and spinal drainage. Cyst formation after simple craniotomy is rare, but it can cause serious sequelae. For prevention, it is essential to understand its mechanisms and take appropriate measures.

Introduction

Postoperative symptomatic cyst formation is a rare complication in neurosurgery. 1 2 3 Most cysts develop after surgery for posterior cranial fossa lesions, 4 5 but rarely occur in supratentorial lesions. As we have previously reported, posterior cranial fossa cyst development is thought to be caused by the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) entering the brain from a nearby pseudomeningocele through a check valve mechanism. 6

Here, we present a case of a symptomatic cyst formed in the parenchyma of the contralateral occipital lobe after ligation of the draining vein of a transverse sinus dural arteriovenous fistula (dAVF). Most cysts that develop after surgery for posterior fossa lesions are filled with CSF, but in this case, the cyst was filled with CSF mixed with blood and even air. The cyst had an unusual clinical course, appearing on the side contralateral to the lesion on the second day after surgery. Postoperative cyst formation can lead to serious sequelae, so its prevention is extremely important, as is understanding the underlying mechanisms. We reviewed relevant literature and discussed the mechanisms of this rare cyst formation.

Case Description

A 59-year-old healthy man underwent a brain checkup and abnormal vascular shadows in the right occipital lobe were found on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). There was nothing remarkable in his medical or family history, and there were no abnormalities in his general laboratory tests, including blood coagulability. Angiography revealed the feeders from the right occipital artery and the petrosquamous branch of the right middle meningeal artery draining into the right occipital cortical vein with reflux ( Fig. 1 ). A diagnosis of dAVF of the right transverse sinus with intracranial reflux was made, and we decided to perform a craniotomy with ligation of the draining vein to prevent future bleeding.

Right carotid angiography: the isolated right transverse sinus is supplied from the right occipital artery and right middle meningeal artery (white arrow) draining into the cortical vein with reflux (white triangle).

We operated on the right occipital region with the patient in the prone position. A lumber spinal drain was prepositioned for brain relaxation and to minimize retraction. A square bone flap was cut using four burr holes with additional ones across the midline on the left side to expose the superior sagittal sinus. At that time, no apparent dural tear was noticed. The dura covering the right occipital lobe was incised, and the draining vein between the transverse sinus and the surface of the occipital lobe was identified and cauterized. The dura was carefully and tightly sutured with fine nylon threads. The bone flap was returned, and a draining tube was placed subcutaneously. Lumbar drainage was satisfactory, brain relaxation was achieved throughout the procedure, and the spinal drain was removed immediately after surgery.

The patient recovered well from anesthesia and spent the day after surgery in bed with only a mild headache. Head computed tomography (CT) performed on the day after surgery revealed no abnormalities and no apparent large subcutaneous hematoma ( Fig. 2 ). A mixture of blood and CSF with a total volume of 380 mL was drained through the subcutaneous tube, which was removed after CT examination.

Head computed tomography the day after surgery: no intracranial cyst formation and minimal subcutaneous hematoma.

On the second postoperative day, upon awakening, the patient noticed visual field abnormalities. Ophthalmologically, he developed right lower quadrant blindness. CT revealed a large cyst in the left occipital lobe, contralateral to the surgery site. The density within the cyst was slightly elevated and it appeared to be blood mixed with CSF. A small amount of air was also visible inside the cyst, and there was increased epidural and subcutaneous fluid accumulation in the right occipital area ( Fig. 3 ). On the second postoperative day, the intracystic fluid signal intensity was comparable to that of the CSF on T1-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery MRIs, but it was low on T2-weighted images, indicating contamination with blood components ( Fig. 4 ).

Head computed tomography on the second postoperative day: a cyst is formed in the left occipital lobe. Compared to the previous day, the volume of subcutaneous hematomas increased, and fluid also accumulated in the epidural space (white arrow). The density of the cyst contents is higher than that of the cerebrospinal fluid, and the cyst contains air (white triangle).

Magnetic resonance imaging on the second postoperative day: the intracystic fluid signal intensity is comparable to that of the cerebrospinal fluid on T1-weighted (T1WI) and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images but low on T2-weighted images (T2WIs), indicating contamination with blood components.

There were no symptoms other than visual field defects, and although the patient was treated conservatively, there was no worsening of the symptoms. The patient was discharged 19 days after surgery with right lower quadrant blindness. Six months later, his visual field impairment gradually recovered, and he could drive a car and perform his daily activities without difficulties. MRI showed that the cyst in the left occipital lobe had shrunk slightly.

Discussion

We reported a symptomatic cyst formation in the occipital lobe contralateral to the operated lesion after ligation of the dAVF draining vein. Initially, we suspected this cyst to be a remote hemorrhage caused by hemodynamic changes due to dAVF disappearance. However, since the imaging showed the mixture of CSF and blood, we considered this lesion to be a cyst rather than a hematoma.

Chronic supratentorial intracerebral cysts are frequently observed after bischloroethyl nitrosourea wafer implantation during the treatment of malignant brain tumors and after gamma knife application for arteriovenous malformations. 3 7 8 9 However, only isolated cases of supratentorial cyst formation in the acute postoperative period are known. 1 2 3 All of them occurred after tumor removal, and to our knowledge, we have described the first case of a cyst developed after vascular disease treatment.

Cyst formation in the acute postoperative period appears to be more common in the posterior fossa than in supratentorial lesions. In our previous report, 6 we suggested that the postoperative intracerebellar cyst could have been caused by the formation of a pseudomeningocele, 4 which is believed to occur when CSF leaks epidurally from a dural tear due to its incomplete closure. 10 The CSF then flows into the brain parenchyma via a check valve mechanism and forms the cyst. 4 The valve-like structure can develop from relocated brain tissue, restructured oxidized cellulose used for hemostasis during surgery, and the arachnoid membrane as a roof, leading to a one-way CSF flow forming the cyst. 11 In posterior fossa surgery, CSF leakage occurs more frequently due to the presence of a large adjacent cistern. 4 This appears to be one reason why cyst formation is more common in the infratentorial rather than in the supratentorial region.

In our case, the check valve mechanism was probably also involved in the fluid-filled cavity (cyst) formation. After removal of the subcutaneous drainage, fluid accumulated in the epidural and subcutaneous spaces and could leak into the parenchyma from the dural tear, which may have formed due to dural suture failure, leading to an extradural CSF flow. Although it was not noticed when the burr hole was made during the craniotomy, a small tear could appear in the dura covering the left occipital lobe, which created the inflow route. On the other hand, in this case, air was found inside the cyst, indicating pneumocephalus, which typically occurs in head trauma, during surgeries, or certain medical procedures. Two theories explain the mechanism of pneumocephalus development: check valve and inverted bottle mechanisms. 12 The first one describes the unidirectional air movement from the outside into the cranial cavity, where the air is trapped. According to the second theory, excessive CSF loss through physiological or spinal drainage during the Valsalva maneuver results in low intracranial pressure, the CSF drains from the dural tear, and the air is aspirated to compensate the low pressure. 12 In our case, the spinal drainage was performed during the surgery, which could have resulted in the pressure drop. However, the cyst developed only on the second day after surgery. Postoperatively, the subcutaneous fluid was continuously suctioned under negative pressure with a subcutaneous drain for one day, so the pressure both in the subcutaneous and epidural space was probably negative. The intracranial pressure was also low and the pressure gradient was probably in equilibrium. The gradient changed after we removed the subcutaneous drain, presumably allowing air and fluid to move intracranially. Dowd et al reported that if the brain tissue is tightly adherent to the dura mater at the fistula site, air may be able to bypass the subdural space and penetrate directly into the brain via the path of least resistance. 13 In our case, the cyst apparently formed in the brain parenchyma by the same mechanism caused by a combination of three factors: dural suture failure in the main surgical field, spinal drainage, and damage to the dura mater in the left occipital region outside the main surgical field. Without one of these factors, this complication would not have occurred. Watertight suturing of the dura mater in the operative field is of utmost importance to prevent such a complication. It is also essential to use special dural suture threads and various sealants for watertight dural closure and perform the Valsalva maneuver to ensure that there is no CSF leakage. Attention should be paid to areas other than the main surgical field to eliminate the possibility of dural damage or other problems during craniotomy. The volume of drained CSF should also be noted, as excessive intraoperative CSF loss can also cause low intracranial pressure, which may result in pneumocephalus. When using spinal drainage to achieve intraoperative brain relaxation and to minimize retraction, the changes in brain relaxation should be closely monitored and minimal drainage should be performed. If a cyst forms, conservative treatment should be done first, including resting with the head elevated with a compression bandage. If cyst enlargement or worsening of symptoms occurs, surgical treatment such as cyst drainage should be considered. 5

Conclusion

We reported intraparenchymal cyst formation, a rare postoperative complication caused by an unexpected combination of multiple factors including dural suture failure, unrecognized dural injury, and spinal drainage. It is essential to understand the pathogenesis and take appropriate measures for each cause to prevent this complication.
