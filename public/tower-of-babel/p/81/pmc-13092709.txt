Prodrug-integrated multifunctional liposome encapsulation enhances probiotic-based bacteriotherapy for inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Jianfeng Xin, Qing Guan, Yu Chen, Shijie Bi, Jiaying Zhang, Meng Li, Quan Liu, Wei Tang, Peng Wang, Jun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractProbiotic-based bacteriotherapies show promise for treating inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) by dynamically modulating the intestinal microenvironment. However, their clinical efficacy is hampered by poor gastrointestinal viability, limited intestinal retention, and low bioactivity. To overcome these challenges, we developed a prodrug-integrated multifunctional liposomal coating strategy to enhance probiotic efficacy. This liposomal coating protects probiotics from gastric acid, significantly improving their viability after oral administration. In the acidic stomach environment, protonation of amine groups on the liposomal surface increases the surface positive charge, enhancing probiotic adhesion to the negatively charged intestinal mucus through weakening electrostatic repulsion. Concurrently, PEG2000 chains further promote deep mucus penetration, facilitating enhanced colonization, prolonged intestinal retention, and reinforcement of epithelial barrier function. Additionally, idebenone (IDE), conjugated to the liposomal surface via a ROS-responsive linker, is selectively released in inflamed regions with elevated ROS levels, providing localized antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. In multiple colitis models, this engineered probiotic platform demonstrated significant prophylactic and therapeutic efficacy with favorable safety profiles, highlighting its potential as an effective strategy for IBD therapy.Keywords: Probiotics, Functionalized liposome, Idebenone, Bacteriotherapy, Inflammatory bowel disease

Introduction

Commensal bacteria, the most abundant organisms in the intestines, play a pivotal role in maintaining intestinal homeostasis and overall host health [1,2]. They inhibit pathogen colonization, strengthen the intestinal barrier, and regulate immune responses through interactions with epithelial cells and cytokine signaling [3]. Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), a chronic inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract, is driven by a multifactorial pathogenesis involving genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, immune dysregulation, and infections [[4], [5], [6]]. These factors contribute to gut microbiota dysbiosis, which in turn exacerbates inflammation, generates harmful metabolites, and impairs host metabolism, ultimately leading to a disrupted intestinal microenvironment characterized by oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and microbial imbalance [7,8]. Current therapies for IBD, such as antibiotics [9], nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [10], and immunosuppressive agents [11], primarily aim to control symptoms by alleviating inflammation. However, these treatments fail to restore the damaged intestinal microenvironment, ultimately contributing to disease recurrence.

As beneficial bacteria strains, probiotics hold considerable promise in restoring gut microbiota balance and promoting intestinal homeostasis for the treatment of IBD [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Nevertheless, their therapeutic efficacy remains limited by several challenges. After oral administration, probiotic strains exhibited poor survival due to the harsh gastric acidity and bile salts, leading to reduced viability [16]. In addition, rapid gastrointestinal transit limits their intestinal retention time, resulting in suboptimal bacterial abundance and therapeutic performance [17]. To address these limitations, recent advances in probiotic surface modification have introduced innovative strategies to enhance their resistance and mucosal adhesion capabilities [18,19]. Chemically synthesized coatings, such as norepinephrine [20], polydopamine [21], or polyethyleneimine [22], provide protective barriers against environmental stressors while promoting adhesion to the intestinal epithelial surface. Encapsulation technologies, utilizing materials like alginate or chitosan-based microcapsules, shield probiotics from gastric stressors and enable controlled release [[23], [24], [25]]. Moreover, genetic engineering enables the precise manipulation of surface proteins (e.g., S-layer proteins) to improve acid resistance and enhance mucus-binding affinity [26,27]. Despite these advancements, existing encapsulation strategies often form adherence to the intestinal surface without effectively penetrating the mucus layer, thereby hindering deep colonization and interaction with the mucosal environment [28]. Additionally, the intrinsic bioactivity of probiotics is often insufficient for rapidly counteracting oxidative stress and inflammation in the IBD-associated intestinal microenvironment, thereby limiting their therapeutic efficacy [29]. Thus, there is a compelling need to develop engineered probiotics that not only withstand external stresses and penetrate the mucus layer for durable colonization and barrier repair but also possess multifunctional bioactivities for restoring intestinal homeostasis and improving IBD outcomes.

To achieve these capabilities, we developed a multifunctional liposomal coating strategy for probiotic encapsulation in this study (Fig. 1). Liposomes, composed of a lipid bilayer and approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for use as nanovesicles in small-molecule drug delivery, are biocompatible, biodegradable, and non-toxic, making them ideal candidates for biomedical applications [30,31]. Here, we established a functionalized liposomal film containing PEG2000 chains and terminal amine groups on the surface of probiotics. This coating endowed the orally administered probiotics with enhanced resistance to the harsh conditions of gastric and intestinal fluids. In the acidic environment of the stomach, the terminal amine groups exposed on the liposomal coating would be protonated, thereby increasing the positive surface charge of the coated probiotics. This weakens electrostatic repulsion between probiotics and the negatively charged intestinal mucus layer upon reaching the intestines, thereby enhancing mucosal adhesion. Simultaneously, PEG2000, a hydrophilic and non-ionic polymer grafted onto the liposome, facilitates the diffusion of coated probiotics through low-viscosity pathways within the hydrophobic mucus network. This promotes deeper mucus penetration, increases intestinal retention time, and enhances probiotic colonization. Such colonization not only helps maintain a stable population of beneficial bacteria but also stimulates mucus secretion and upregulation of tight junction proteins, thereby reinforcing the intestinal epithelial barrier. To further endow the probiotics with therapeutic bioactivity, we incorporated idebenone (IDE), an analog of coenzyme Q10 with potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties [32], into the liposomal structure via a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive linker. In the inflamed intestinal microenvironment of IBD, characterized by elevated ROS levels, IDE is released in a controlled manner to mitigate oxidative damage and suppress inflammation. Additionally, sustained probiotic colonization contributes to local retention and targeted delivery of IDE, enhancing its intestinal bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy. Taken together, we presented a multifunctional IDE-loaded liposomal coating strategy for probiotic encapsulation. This platform confers protection against gastrointestinal stresses, facilitates mucus adhesion and penetration, prolongs intestinal retention, and delivers therapeutic agents in a targeted and responsive manner, offering a promising strategy for the effective treatment of IBD. Moreover, all components of the probiotic formulation are biocompatible, showing great safety profiles and strong potential for clinical translation.

Schematic illustration of the mechanism of DTI@EcN for IBD treatment. (A) Preparation process for DTI@EcN. (B) The mechanism of action of DTI@EcN. (I) Following oral administration, DTI@EcN passes through the stomach, where the DTI coating protects EcN from simulated gastric fluid (SGF)-induced damage and undergoes protonation. (II) Upon reaching the intestine, the protonated DTI weakens electrostatic repulsion with the negatively charged mucus layer, while PEG2000 incorporation improves mucus penetration, collectively promoting prolonged retention and effective colonization of the probiotics. (III) In the inflamed intestinal microenvironment with elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), the ROS-responsive linker within DTI is cleaved, enabling targeted release of the therapeutic agent to exert anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. (IV) Concurrently, EcN competitively inhibits pathogenic bacterial colonization and contributes to the restoration of intestinal homeostasis.

Materials and methods

Reagents

All reagents, obtained commercially, were used as received. Analytical TLC employed 0.25 mm silica gel plates (GF254), and column chromatography was carried out using 200–300 mesh silica gel. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker 400 MHz spectrometer. The selected chemicals and biological materials used in this research are as follows: 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide (EDCI, Macklin), 4-bromobutyric acid (Bidepharm), thiourea (Macklin), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, Macklin), hydrochloric acid (HCl, Macklin), trifluoroacetic acid (Bidepharm), idebenone (Bidepharm), hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide (Macklin), 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP, Macklin), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS, Macklin), 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA, Aladdin), DSPE-PEG2000-NH2 (Yusi), DSPE-PEG2000-FITC (Yusi), cholesterol (Yuanye), trypsin (Macklin), pepsin powder (Bidepharm), bile salts (Macklin), ampicillin (Bidepharm), lysin (Bidepharm), dextran sulfate sodium salt (DSS, MW 40,000, MP Bio), LB nutrient agar (Hopebiol), Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime), mouse IL-6, IFN-γ, IL-1β, and TNF-α uncoated ELISA kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and antigen retrieval solution (Servicebio). SGF was prepared by dissolving NaCl (2.0 g) and pepsin (3.2 g) in 1 L of deionized water, followed by pH adjustment to 2.0 with HCl and membrane filtration (0.22 μm). SIF was prepared by dissolving KH2PO4 (6.8 g) and trypsin (10 g) in 1 L of deionized water, followed by pH adjustment to 6.8 with NaOH and membrane filtration (0.22 μm).

Synthesis of 4-mercapto-butyric acid

To a stirred solution of 4-bromobutyric acid (5.0 g, 30.0 mmol) in 50 mL of ethanol, thiourea (3.4 g, 45.0 mmol) was added at room temperature, and the reaction mixture was stirred for 4 h under reflux. Then, 50 mL of NaOH (7.5 M) in ethanol was added, and the mixture was further stirred overnight under reflux. After cooling, the formed white precipitate was collected by filtration. Afterward, 4 M HCl was added to adjust the pH to 1.0. Then the crude product was extracted with DCM three times. After drying over anhydrous Na2SO4, the organic solution was concentrated in vacuo, yielding the product as a colorless oil: yield 2.5 g (70.0%); 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) δ 2.64-2.55 (m, 2H), 2.50 (t, J = 7.3 Hz, 2H), 1.93 (m, 2H), 1.34 (t, J = 8.1 Hz, 1H).

Synthesis of 4,4'-(propane-2,2-diylbis(sulfanediyl)) dibutyric acid (TK)

4-mercapto-butyric acid (3.5 mL) was dissolved in anhydrous acetone (6.1 mL). After adding trifluoroacetic acid (3 drops), the reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature overnight with nitrogen protection. Then, the mixture was cooled at −20 °C to form a colorless crystal, which was further washed with pre-cooled petroleum ether and water alternately and dried in a vacuum oven to afford the product TK: yield 5.5 g (58%) with a purity of 95.7% measured via HPLC. 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) δ 2.66 (t, J = 7.3 Hz, 4H), 2.49 (t, J = 7.3 Hz, 4H), 1.91 (q, J = 7.3 Hz, 4H), 1.58 (s, 6H).

Synthesis of 4-((2-((4-((11-(4,5-dimethoxy-2-methyl-3,6-dioxocyclohexa-1,4-dien-1-yl)undecyl)oxy)-4-oxobutyl)thio)propan-2-yl)thio)butanoic acid (TI)

To a stirred solution of TK (800 mg, 3.0 mmol) and idebenone (1072 mg, 3.2 mmol) in anhydrous DCM (25 mL) was added DMAP (700 mg, 6.0 mmol) and EDCI (1150 mg, 6.0 mmol). The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24 h. Then, 30 mL of DCM was added to the mixture, which was then washed with water and brine, dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and filtered. The solvent was concentrated under reduced pressure to give the crude product, which was purified by column chromatography on a silica gel column to generate the product TI as an orange oil: yield 752 mg (42.0%) with a purity of 97.8% measured via HPLC. 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) δ 4.05 (t, J = 6.7 Hz, 2H), 3.98 (s, 6H), 2.67-2.64 (m, 2H), 2.63-2.60 (m, 2H), 2.49-2.45 (m, 2H), 2.43-2.37 (m, 4H), 2.00 (s, 3H), 1.95-1.86 (m, 4H), 1.62-1.5 (m, 2H), 1.57 (s, 6H), 1.36-1.20 (m, 16H).

Synthesis of DTI

To a stirred solution of TI (30 mg, 0.05 mmol) in anhydrous DMSO was added NHS (15 mg, 0.13 mmol) and EDCI (25 mg, 0.13 mmol) under a nitrogen atmosphere at room temperature. After 2 h, DSPE-PEG2000-NH2 (50 mg) was introduced, and the reaction was allowed to proceed for 48 h. Subsequently, the crude product was purified by dialysis against 30% ethanol (three times one day) followed by distilled water (three times one day). The final product (DTI) was obtained as a solid after lyophilization. 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) δ 3.98 (s, 6H), 3.64 (m, 148H), 1.57 (s, 6H), 1.24 (m, 61H), 0.87 (t, J = 6.9 Hz, 6H).

IDE release assay

To evaluate the IDE release due to the cleavage of the ROS-responsive linker, both TI and IDE were incubated with hydrogen peroxide (100 μM), and the sample without hydrogen peroxide treatment served as a negative control. Employing high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), samples were characterized at the indicated time points.

Strains and culture conditions

The bacterial strains of EcN and STm used in this study were all cultured on LB agar plates. For subsequent experiments, bacteria were prepared in liquid LB medium with incubation at 37 °C for 12 to 18 h at a shaking speed of 225 rpm.

Preparation and characterization of DTI@EcN

To prepare the liposomal film, DTI and DSPE-PEG2000-NH2 at a ratio of 1:1 and cholesterol in a flask were dissolved in trichloromethane, which was evaporated by rotary evaporation to remove the solvent slowly, and a dry film was formed on the bottle wall. To encapsulate the liposomal film on the EcN surface, EcN was pretreated with calcium ions by suspending bacteria in calcium chloride solution (12.5 mmol/L) to increase the positive charges on the bacterial surface. Afterward, the bacterial suspension in ddH2O was added to the flask, and the mixture was placed on a shaker for 40 min. Following centrifugation at 3500 rpm, the bacterial pellet was washed twice with PBS, obtaining the DTI@EcN. The morphology of DTI@EcN was initially assessed by TEM. A sample aliquot was placed on a carbon-coated copper grid and, after thorough drying, was visualized under the microscope. The DTI@EcN was further characterized by fluorescence imaging. In the preparation process of DTI@EcN, FITC-labeled DSPE-PEG2000 with a 10% ratio to DTI was mixed. After preparing the DTI@EcN with similar procedures described above, the resulting DTI@EcN was imaged by fluorescence microscopy. Moreover, the colocalization analysis of FITC-labeled liposomal film and EcN was also performed by flow cytometry. In addition, the average size, size distribution, and zeta potential of DTI@EcN were determined with a dynamic light scattering (Malvern Zetasizer nano ZS, Great Britain).

Measurement of growth curves of EcN and DTI@EcN

To evaluate the influence of the DTI coating on the growth of EcN, DTI@EcN was suspended in LB medium with an initial OD600 value of 0.1, placed in a 96-well plate, and incubated at 37 °C with gentle shaking. Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring OD600 every 0.5 h for 12 h with a microplate reader, with uncoated EcN as the control.

Cell viability assay

To assess the impact of the preparation process on bacterial viability, DTI@EcN was resuspended in LB medium, mixed with a CCK-8 solution, and co-incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Then, the OD450 values were measured by a microplate reader. Uncoated EcN was used as a control.

In vitro antioxidant activity assay

The in vitro antioxidant activity of DTI@EcN was evaluated using a Transwell co-culture system. Briefly, DTI@EcN (1 × 106 CFU) was added to the upper chamber, while HCT116 cells were seeded in the lower chamber. Oxidative stress was induced in HCT116 cells by treatment with 100 μM hydrogen peroxide for 8 h. After treatment, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were gently washed three times with PBS. Subsequently, cells were incubated with 40 μM DCFH-DA in the dark for 30 min to detect intracellular ROS by fluorescence microscopy. Mitochondrial oxidative damage in HCT116 cells was further evaluated using a JC-1 staining assay. Treatment with CCCP (carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone), a mitochondrial depolarization inducer, was used as a positive control.

Resistance assay in vitro

The protective effect of the liposomal film on EcN was measured in different environmental conditions, including SGF (pH 2.0) supplemented with pepsin, SIF (pH 6.8) supplemented with trypsin, bile salts solution (0.5%), H2O2 solution (100 μmol/L), ampicillin solution (0.1 mg/mL), and lysin solution (10 mg/mL). Briefly, equal amounts of DTI@EcN and EcN were subjected to the aforementioned solutions and incubated at 37 °C under shaking at 225 rpm. Aliquots (50 μL) were withdrawn from each sample at predetermined time points, followed by PBS washing and plating on LB agar. Following 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, the colonies were quantified.

Antioxidant effect of the DTI coating

To evaluate the antioxidant effect of the DTI coating, DTI@EcN was suspended in the PBS solution containing H2O2 (1 mmol/L) and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After washing with PBS to remove the residual H2O2, the bacterial cells were treated with DCFH-DA solution at a final concentration of 50 μmol/L and incubated in the dark for 30 min. The fluorescence intensity in bacteria was detected by flow cytometry. EcN cells without DTI coating served as a control.

In vitro evaluation of mucus adhesion and penetration effect of liposome-coated bacteria

To assess the mucin adhesion and penetration capabilities of various bacterial formulations, a 0.5% porcine stomach mucin solution was prepared and mixed with different EcN formulations, including EcN, DSPC@EcN, DP@EcN, DPN@EcN, and DPNH@EcN. The mixtures were shaken and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, then centrifuged at 800 rpm for 5 min. The fluorescence intensity of the resulting sediment was measured using a microplate reader. Moreover, the capabilities were further evaluated by a transwell assay. Briefly, mucus was lightly spread on the filter membrane of the upper chamber, which was then placed into a well containing 200 μL PBS. Different EcN formulations (20 μL) were gently applied to the surface of the mucin layer. At predetermined time points, samples from the lower chamber were collected and plated on LB agar for colony counting. Additionally, artificial intestinal mucus was constructed to further evaluate the mucin penetration ability of bacteria. Briefly, mucin solution was prepared by dissolving it in PBS containing 0.05% agar and added into flow tubes. Various GFP-expressing EcN formulations were gently spread on the surface of the mucin, and bacterial penetration depth was monitored over time using a fluorescence excitation device (LUYOR-3421). Moreover, the in vivo penetration capability of DPNH@EcN was evaluated. Briefly, C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized and then administered 50 μL of GFP-expressing EcN or DPNH@EcN (1 × 108 CFU) via intra-cecal and intra-colonic injection. After 1 h of incubation, the intestinal segments were gently washed with PBS, followed by preparation of frozen sections for fluorescence imaging.

Evaluation of the adhesive effect of DTI@EcN in vivo

Animal protocols related to this study were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Ocean University of China. The mucoadhesive effect of DTI@EcN was first evaluated ex vivo. Briefly, freshly isolated intestinal tissues were washed, sectioned, and incubated with GFP-expressing EcN formulations for 1 h at 37 °C. After the incubation period, the intestines were washed with PBS to remove unbound EcN cells and subsequently subjected to IVIS imaging. Furthermore, the mucoadhesive effect of DTI@EcN was explored in vivo. Specifically, male mice (7 weeks old) were randomly assigned to four groups and fasted for 12 h with normal drinking water before administration of EcN formulations. Then, 100 μL of EcN formulations with bioluminescence, including EcN, DSPC@EcN, DP@EcN, and DPN@EcN (1 × 108 CFU in PBS), were orally administered, respectively. After that, the mice resumed normal eating and drinking. Bioluminescence was monitored in live mice at designated time points using an IVIS system. Following the final in vivo imaging at 24 h, the animals were euthanized, and the GI tracts were harvested for ex vivo imaging. To further evaluate the colonization of bacteria, mice were administered different bacterial formulations (1 × 108 CFU), and fresh fecal samples were collected at designated time points for fluorescence imaging.

Prophylactic efficacy of DTI@EcN against DSS-induced colitis

C57 mice (female, aged 6 to 8 weeks) were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5 per group): DSS−, DSS+, IDE, EcN, DPN@EcN, and DTI@EcN. Based on group assignments, mice were given various bacteria or drug IDE (bacteria dose, 5 × 108 CFU; IDE, 65 mg/kg) via oral gavage throughout the experimental period. Colitis was induced by administering 3% (w/v) DSS in the drinking water for five consecutive days starting on day 4. Body weights were recorded daily throughout the experiment. On day 11, the mice were euthanized. The colon tissues were isolated, and colon length was measured. Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for subsequent histological examination. Moreover, colon tissues were collected for further analysis.

Therapeutic efficacy of DTI@EcN against DSS-induced colitis

C57 mice (female, aged 6 to 8 weeks) were randomly divided into six groups: DSS−, DSS+, IDE, EcN, DPN@EcN, and DTI@EcN (n = 6). To induce colitis, 3% DSS was administered in the drinking water for the first 5 days, with the exception of the DSS− group. After that, the drinking water recovered to normal, and the mice were treated with different EcN formulations. Upon completion of the study, all mice were sacrificed, and colon lengths were determined. Colon tissues were collected for further analysis. In addition, mouse body weight was recorded daily.

Microbiome analysis

Cecal content samples were collected and immediately frozen at −80 °C for preservation. Bacterial DNA extraction was performed with the DNeasy PowerSoil kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the supplier's protocol. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), while its integrity was verified via agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified by PCR with universal primers 343F (5′-TACGGRAGGCAGCAG-3′) and 798R (5′-AGGGTATCTAATCCT-3′). Each 20 μL PCR reaction contained 10 ng of template DNA, 4 μL of 5 × Fast Pfu buffer, 2 μL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 μL of each primer (5 μM), and 0.4 μL of Fast Pfu polymerase, with the volume adjusted using deionized water. Amplification was conducted under the following thermal cycling protocol: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 27 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min and cooling to 4 °C. The resulting PCR products were separated on a 2% agarose gel, purified with Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter Co., USA), and quantified via the Qubit dsDNA assay kit. Sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw paired-end reads were processed through DADA2 within QIIME2 (version 2020.11) to filter low-quality sequences, perform denoising, merge reads, and remove chimeras using default parameters. Representative sequences for each amplicon sequence variant (ASV) were selected and taxonomically classified by alignment with the Silva database (v138).

Therapeutic efficacy of DTI@EcN against S. Typhimurium-induced colitis

To establish infection, male C57 mice (6-8 weeks old) first received streptomycin (100 μL of a 200 mg/mL solution) via oral gavage. After 24 h, the mice were subsequently inoculated with 1 × 109 CFU of S. Typhimurium pathogen. Following infection, mice were treated with EcN, DPN@EcN, DTI@EcN, and IDE for 4 days. Mouse fecal samples were collected daily. 50 mg of feces was resuspended in sterile PBS, and the suspension was centrifuged at 200 rpm to remove large debris. The resulting supernatant was serially diluted 10-fold with PBS and plated on MacConkey agar for quantification of S. Typhimurium and EcN. On the terminal day (day 5), mice were euthanized for collection of colon tissues and cecal contents. Body weight was tracked daily throughout the study.

Safety profiles of DTI@EcN in vivo

C57BL/6J mice (male, 6-8 weeks old) were administered PBS or DTI@EcN (5 × 108 CFU) daily for 15 consecutive days, and body weight was monitored and recorded throughout the experiment. Blood samples were collected on days 5, 10, and 15 and plated on LB agar to assess potential bacteremia. At the end of the experiment, blood samples were collected for complete blood count analysis. In addition, the colon and major organs were harvested and examined for any visible abnormalities. Moreover, to evaluate the metabolic safety of DTI@EcN, bacterial colonization in various organs was detected by plate counting.

Histopathological assessment

Histopathological analysis to assess the extent of colon damage was conducted following standard procedures for paraffin embedding and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Following fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, colonic tissues were processed through paraffin embedding, sectioning (4 μm), and H&E staining. The histopathological sections were scanned under a microscope. Subsequently, DSS-induced colon damage in each sample was evaluated in a blinded manner by an experienced pathologist according to the criteria detailed in Table S1.

Evaluation of ROS levels in colon tissues

A portion of the collected colon tissue was immediately immersed in DCFH-DA (50 μM) solutions for 2 h during mouse dissection. After washing three times with pre-cooled PBS, the tissue was embedded, and frozen sections were prepared. The frozen slides were then fixed with paraformaldehyde for 30 min, followed by three 5-min washes with PBS. Subsequently, the slides underwent Hoechst 33342 staining (15 min) prior to four final rinses with water. Dehydration was performed using four sequential ethanol washes (95%, 95%, 100%, and 100%) for 5 min each. Finally, the slides were mounted with cover slips and imaged using LSCM.

MPO assay

As a marker of neutrophil infiltration, colonic myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity was quantified to assess the severity of tissue inflammation. During animal experiments, the colons were isolated, feces were carefully rinsed with PBS, and the tissues were weighed. The samples were homogenized in PBS (pH 6.0) containing 0.5% hexadecyltrimethylammonium bromide. The homogenates were then subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles and brief sonication (60 Hz, 20 s), yielding a homogeneous suspension. The homogenates were centrifuged at 20,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the clear supernatant was collected. A 50 μL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a 96-well plate, to which was added 200 μL of dianisidine dihydrochloride (1 mg/mL) in PBS (pH 6.0) containing 0.005% (v/v) H2O2. Absorbance at 450 nm (OD450) was measured to quantify MPO activity via a microplate reader.

Immunofluorescence imaging of ZO-1 and Claudin-1

Colon tissues were immediately immersed in cold PBS after isolation from experimental mice. The tissues were then embedded in Tissue-Tek optimal cutting temperature (OCT) using cryomolds and stored at −80 °C. Following fixation with 4% PFA, the frozen sections underwent permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 and were then blocked with 3% BSA in PBST. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including ZO-1 rabbit polyclonal antibody and occludin monoclonal antibody. After the washing steps, the sections were treated with corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies: CoraLite488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) and CoraLite594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L). Finally, the slides were coverslipped with an antifade mounting medium containing DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology) and subsequently visualized under a fluorescence microscope.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The isolated colon tissues were carefully washed off with PBS to remove fecal residues before weighing. For quantification of colon tissue cytokines, samples were homogenized in Cell Lysis Buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. The homogenates then underwent three freeze-thaw cycles and a 30-s sonication to produce a uniform suspension. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 10,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C. Cytokine concentrations in the collected supernatants were determined with ELISA kits (Thermo Fisher) following the supplier's guidelines.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5). For comparisons between two groups, Student's t-test was applied, while one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's or Fisher's LSD post hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and the following significance levels were assigned: (ns) P > 0.05, ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of DTI@EcN

The probiotic of EcN, commonly used to regulate intestinal flora for IBD treatment, was selected as the model bacteria in this study. To conjugate idebenone (IDE) on the EcN surface and endow the bacteria with additional anti-inflammatory and antioxidative properties, a prodrug (DTI) was first synthesized by linking IDE to the phospholipid (DSPE) via a ROS-responsive linker (Fig. 2A). Briefly, 4-bromobutyric acid was used as the starting material and reacted with thiourea to give an intermediate 4-mercapto-butyric acid, which was then reacted with acetone to produce the ROS-responsive linker TK. The chemical structures of 4-mercapto-butyric acid and TK were confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (Fig. S1 and S2). Next, one carboxyl group of TK was coupled to the hydroxyl group of IDE under EDCI and NHS conditions to generate TI, which was further amidated using the other carboxyl group with the amine group in DSPE-PEG2000-NH2 to produce the final DTI conjugate. The purity of TK and TI was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Fig. S3), and the structures of TI and DTI were confirmed by 1H NMR and 13C NMR spectroscopy (Fig. S4–S6). To verify the ROS-responsiveness of the linker and the release of IDE under oxidative conditions (Fig. 2B), TI and IDE were both subjected to hydrogen peroxide conditions, and the resulting samples were analyzed via HPLC. As shown in Fig. 2C and S7, IDE was successfully released from TI, as evidenced by the matching peak time at 4.6 min between the TI + H2O2 and IDE + H2O2 samples, confirming the effective release of IDE under ROS stimulation.

Furthermore, to facilitate liposomal encapsulation on the bacterial surface and prepare DTI@EcN, EcN cells were pre-treated with a calcium chloride solution (Fig. 2D). Subsequently, DSPE-PEG2000-NH2 and DTI (1:1) were mixed in the presence of cholesterol to form a liposomal film, which was then hydrated with the EcN suspension to generate a liposome-coated bacterial formulation, referred to as DTI@EcN. The successful formation of the liposomal layer was first characterized by fluorescence imaging. As shown in Fig. 2E, fluorescence from FITC-labeled DSPE-PEG2000-NH2 was observed on the surface of EcN, indicating the existence of DSPE-based liposomes on the EcN surface. Flow cytometry analysis further verified the liposomal coating, as evidenced by a significant increase in fluorescence intensity in DTI@EcN compared to uncoated EcN (Fig. 2F). Additionally, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed a transparent outer shell surrounding the EcN cells, consistent with liposome encapsulation (Fig. 2G). The changes in the size and surface charge after liposomal coating were further evaluated by dynamic light scattering (DLS). As shown in Fig. 2H and I, after liposomal encapsulation, the size increased from 1196 to 1365 nm, and the zeta potential changed from −38.6 to −28.6 mV. The liposome coating remained stable in PBS for over 7 days, as evidenced by negligible changes in size and zeta potential (Fig. S8). Together, these results demonstrate that the liposome forms and encapsulates on the EcN surface. Moreover, the amount of IDE loaded on the EcN surface was quantified, revealing approximately 2.6 mg of IDE per 109 CFU of EcN (Fig. S9).

To assess whether the liposomal coating affects the growth and activity of bacteria, DTI@EcN was incubated in LB medium, and the growth curve was monitored over 12 h via OD600 values. As shown in Fig. 2J, DTI@EcN exhibited a similar growth rate to that of uncoated EcN, indicating that the liposomal coating had a negligible impact on the growth of EcN. Additionally, the viability of EcN following DTI encapsulation was assessed using a CCK-8 assay, which showed no significant difference between EcN and DTI@EcN, further confirming the biocompatibility of the DTI layer for bacterial coating (Fig. S10). Due to the IDE loading, the antioxidant capability of DTI@EcN was further evaluated under oxidative stress. Upon exposure to hydrogen peroxide, intracellular ROS levels were significantly lower in DTI@EcN compared to native EcN, indicating that IDE loading conferred antioxidative activity to the engineered bacteria (Fig. 2K and S11). Additionally, using a Transwell assay, DTI@EcN demonstrated protective effects on intestinal epithelial cells against oxidative damage, as evidenced by reduced intracellular ROS levels and alleviated mitochondrial oxidative stress, which can be attributed to the antioxidant capability of the conjugated IDE (Fig. S12 and S13). Overall, these results indicate that IDE loading endows the bacteria with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, facilitating the restoration of a healthy intestinal microenvironment.

In vitro resistance of DTI@EcN against environmental assaults

Next, we investigated the protective effect of the DSPE-based liposomal coating on EcN against various environmental challenges. First, the liposomal coating exhibited good stability under multiple conditions, including simulated gastric fluid (SGF) supplemented with pepsin to mimic the acidic gastric environment, simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) containing trypsin to mimic the intestinal conditions, and oxidative environments, which is essential for exerting its protective effects (Fig. S14 and S15). Given the extremely low survival rate of probiotics in the stomach after oral administration, we next evaluated whether the liposome coating could enhance the resistance of EcN to strong acidic environments. As shown in Fig. 3A, DTI@EcN exhibited significantly higher viability than uncoated EcN after incubation in SGF for 0.5 h, 1 h, and 2 h. Notably, all bacteria in the EcN group died by 2 h, while approximately 104 bacteria still survived in the DTI@EcN group, demonstrating the potent protective effect of the DSPE liposome coating against gastric acid. Moreover, the viability of DTI@EcN in the intestinal fluid (SIF) was evaluated. Unlike the bactericidal conditions in SGF, both DTI@EcN and uncoated EcN were able to grow in the SIF environment. However, the number of viable bacteria in the DTI@EcN group was significantly higher than in the uncoated EcN group, indicating the improved tolerance of DTI@EcN in the intestinal environment (Fig. 3B). Due to the antioxidant property conferred by IDE loading, the resistance of DTI@EcN to oxidative stress was further assessed by exposing the bacteria to hydrogen peroxide. As shown in Fig. 3C, DTI@EcN displayed markedly enhanced survival, with viable counts 3-4 times higher than those of uncoated EcN, highlighting its robust antioxidative capability imparted by the IDE-conjugated liposomal coating. Additionally, antibiotics are commonly administered to IBD patients to eliminate pathogenic bacteria. In this study, we also found that the DTI@EcN showed significantly enhanced resistance to the antibiotic ampicillin (Fig. 3D), which may help maintain probiotic abundance during antibiotic treatment. Furthermore, the protective effect of the DTI coating was further evaluated under other harsh conditions, including exposure to bile salts and lysozyme. As shown in Fig. 3E and F, the DTI coating substantially increased the viability of EcN under both conditions compared to uncoated EcN. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the DTI coating significantly enhances the resistance of bacteria to various external stressors, thereby improving their therapeutic potential and supporting long-term storage.

The mucosal adhesion and permeability capabilities of DPN@EcN

Furthermore, we investigated the effects of DSPE-based liposomal coating (DPN) on the mucosal adhesion and mucus permeability of EcN. To evaluate the contribution of the PEGylated surface to mucus penetration, EcN encapsulated with DSPC, a phospholipid structurally analogous to DSPE but lacking PEG chains, was used as a control (DSPC@EcN). Additionally, to elucidate the importance of the terminal amino group on DSPE, which would be protonated (-NH3+) in the gastric environment and facilitate electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged mucus for improved colonization, additional controls were designed: EcN coated with DP (DP@EcN, a PEGylated lipid lacking a terminal amino group) and DPNH@EcN, in which the amino groups were pre-protonated under acidic conditions. The zeta potential of DPNH@EcN was comparable to that of CaCl2-treated EcN (Fig. S16), confirming that protonation increased the positive surface charge following gastric transit. To assess whether the liposomal coatings enhanced mucin adhesion, a key factor for intestinal colonization and retention, GFP-expressing bacterial formulations were co-incubated with 0.5% porcine stomach mucin, and the fluorescence intensity in the mucin sediment was measured. As shown in Fig. 4A, PEG-containing liposomal coatings significantly enhanced mucin binding compared to uncoated EcN and DSPC@EcN, with DPNH@EcN showing the highest fluorescence signal, highlighting the synergistic role of PEG chains and protonated amino groups in promoting mucin interaction. To further demonstrate that protonation of amino groups enhances adhesion, DTI@EcN was pre-incubated under different pH conditions and subsequently incubated with porcine gastric mucin. The results showed that DTI@EcN pretreated under acidic conditions exhibited enhanced interaction with mucin, indicating that protonated amino groups play a key role in mediating the interaction (Fig. S17). To further evaluate bacterial permeability across the mucus barrier, a transwell model was employed. Bacterial formulations were placed in the apical chamber, and the number of bacteria that penetrated into the basolateral chamber was quantified by colony counting (Fig. 4B). It was found that PEG-containing liposome coatings significantly enhanced the transmucosal migration of EcN, as evidenced by the increased bacterial counts in the basolateral chamber compared to the uncoated EcN group. Notably, DPNH@EcN exhibited the strongest permeability, likely due to the presence of protonated amino groups on the liposomal surface (Fig. 4C and S18). Additionally, an artificial intestinal mucus agar model was established to further evaluate the permeation of probiotics after different liposomal coatings, using fluorescence imaging under a fluorescent protein excitation device. As shown in Fig. 4D and S19, DPNH@EcN achieved the greatest penetration depth within the artificial mucus layer, further confirming its superior mucus-permeating capacity among all tested formulations. Moreover, by administration of GFP-expressing bacterial formulations into the colonic region, the PEGylated liposome-coated bacteria exhibited markedly enhanced mucus penetration compared with unmodified EcN, further corroborating the mucus-penetrating capability conferred by PEG2000 modification (Fig. S20).

Furthermore, the mucoadhesive capability of the DPNH layer was investigated in an ex vivo model. GFP-expressing bacterial formulations were incubated with freshly excised mouse intestinal tissue for 1 h and imaged via IVIS. As shown in Fig. 4E and F, the DPNH@EcN group exhibited markedly higher fluorescence intensity compared to the other groups, indicating that the DPNH coating significantly enhanced bacterial adhesion to the intestinal mucosa. The in vivo retention time of DPN@EcN was further investigated by IVIS following oral administration. As shown in Fig. 4G and H, DPN@EcN consistently exhibited higher bioluminescence intensity and slower signal decay compared to other formulations. At 24 h, the signal intensity in the DPN@EcN group was approximately 2.3-, 8.4-, and 22.1-fold higher than in the DP@EcN, DSPC@EcN, and uncoated EcN groups, respectively (Fig. S21). These findings were further confirmed by ex vivo imaging of excised intestines (Fig. 4I and J), where DPN@EcN demonstrated the highest bioluminescence intensity, with 1.6-, 9.9-, and 31.1-fold higher signals than those of the DP@EcN, DSPC@EcN, and uncoated EcN, respectively. Moreover, the abundance of liposome-encapsulated EcN in feces was significantly higher than that of free EcN, further confirming that the coating strengthens the sustained presence and colonization of EcN in the gut (Fig. S22). Collectively, all the results demonstrate that the DSPE-based liposome layer containing PEG2000 chains and terminal amino groups substantially enhances bacterial mucoadhesion and mucus permeability, thereby improving intestinal colonization and prolonging retention. This coating strategy offers a promising approach to maximize the therapeutic efficacy of orally delivered probiotics.

Prophylactic and therapeutic efficacy of DTI@EcN against DSS-induced colitis

Next, given the multifunctional enhancements conferred to EcN by the DTI coating, including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, enhanced resistance to external stressors, improved mucoadhesion, and mucus permeation, we further evaluated the prophylactic and therapeutic efficacy in mouse colitis models. To assess the prophylactic efficacy of DTI@EcN, mice were pretreated with various EcN formulations throughout the experimental period, followed by the administration of DSS for 5 consecutive days, beginning on day 4, to induce colitis (Fig. 5A). Mouse body weight, a key indicator of disease severity, was monitored daily. As shown in Fig. 5B, all treatment groups showed attenuated weight loss compared to the DSS+ group, indicating the protective effects of both EcN-based formulations and the IDE drug. Notably, mouse body weight in most groups began to decline on day 5, while the DTI@EcN treatment showed delayed onset of weight loss until day 7 and exhibited the lowest weight reduction among all treatment groups, demonstrating the most potent prophylactic efficacy against DSS-induced colitis. In addition, colon tissues, which would be shortened due to the inflammation and tissue damage induced by colitis, were collected and imaged. As shown in Fig. 5C and D, DSS treatment caused a substantial reduction in colon length. In contrast, treatments with either IDE or EcN formulations led to partial recovery of colon length. The DTI@EcN group, in particular, demonstrated the greatest preservation of colon length and was comparable to the DSS- group, suggesting near-complete mitigation of DSS-induced tissue damage. To further evaluate colonic damage, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on colon sections. Representative histological images revealed that DSS treatment caused severe epithelial damage, characterized by crypt destruction, goblet cell loss, and extensive immune cell infiltration, whereas substantial improvements were found in all EcN and IDE treatment groups (Fig. 5E). Importantly, DTI@EcN treatment preserved epithelial integrity and markedly reduced inflammatory infiltration. To quantitatively evaluate tissue damage, a histological scoring system was applied (Table S1) based on the criteria of inflammation, ulceration, hyperplasia, lamina propria cell infiltration, and goblet loss [33]. The result showed that DTI@EcN achieved the lowest histopathology score among all treatment groups, with reductions of 43%, 60%, 56%, and 69% compared to DPN@EcN, EcN, IDE, and DSS+ groups, respectively, highlighting its superior therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 5F).

Furthermore, the expression of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and OCLN in colon tissues was investigated by immunofluorescence staining to evaluate intestinal epithelium barrier integrity, based on evidence that probiotic colonization in the mucus layer contributes to the regulation of intestinal homeostasis. As shown in the representative images (Fig. 5G and S23), EcN-based formulations, particularly DTI@EcN, markedly enhanced ZO-1 and OCLN expression compared to the DSS+ group. Quantitative analysis revealed that ZO-1 expression in the DTI@EcN group was 1.2-, 1.3-, 1.8-, and 2.0-fold higher than in the DPN@EcN, EcN, IDE, and DSS + groups, respectively (Fig. 5H). Similarly, OCLN expression was elevated by 1.3-, 1.6-, 1.9-, and 2.4-fold in the DTI@EcN group compared to the DPN@EcN, EcN, IDE, and DSS + groups, respectively (Fig. 5I), indicating that DTI@EcN most effectively restored colonic barrier function. Additionally, the oxidative and inflammatory status of colon tissues was assessed, given the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of IDE-loaded DTI@EcN. ROS levels, measured via DCF fluorescence imaging, were significantly reduced in the DTI@EcN group, showing a 16% decrease compared to the DSS+ group (Fig. 5J and K), demonstrating a strong ROS-scavenging activity. Myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity, a marker of neutrophil infiltration, was also significantly reduced in the DTI@EcN group and was comparable to that of the DSS− group, supporting its potent anti-inflammatory efficacy (Fig. 5L). Furthermore, levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α and IFN-γ, were significantly decreased in colon tissues after DTI@EcN treatment (Fig. 5M–P), further confirming the robust anti-inflammatory capability of DTI@EcN.

Next, the therapeutic efficacy of DTI@EcN against DSS-induced colitis was further evaluated. Mice were administered 3% DSS for 5 consecutive days to induce colitis, followed by treatment with various EcN and IDE formulations for 5 days (Fig. 6A). During the treatment period, the DTI@EcN treatment exhibited a slower rate of body weight loss compared to the other treatment groups. Notably, mouse body weight in the DTI@EcN group began to recover on the third day of treatment, while weight in the other groups continued to decline, indicating the superior therapeutic efficacy of DTI@EcN (Fig. 6B). Moreover, the average colon length in the DTI@EcN treatment group was significantly longer than in the other treatment groups (Fig. 6C and D), suggesting pronounced protection against DSS-induced colonic shortening. H&E-stained colon tissues confirmed these findings, revealing an almost intact colonic epithelium and minimal inflammatory cell infiltration in the DTI@EcN group (Fig. 6E). Quantitative histopathological analysis also showed the lowest damage score in the DTI@EcN treatment group, with 29%, 54%, 58%, and 66% reductions compared to the DPN@EcN, EcN, IDE, and DSS + groups, respectively (Fig. 6F). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining revealed elevated levels of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin in the DTI@EcN group (Fig. 6G and S24), with 1.3-, 1.7-, 1.6-, and 2.2-fold higher ZO-1 expression (Figs. 6H) and 1.1-, 1.3-, 1.4-, and 1.5-fold higher OCLN expression (Fig. 6I) than those in the DPN@EcN, EcN, IDE, and DSS + groups, respectively, indicating that DTI@EcN effectively preserved intestinal barrier integrity. The oxidative status of colon tissues was also assessed by measuring ROS levels. DCF fluorescence intensity (Fig. 6J) and its quantification (Fig. 6K) were significantly lower in the DTI@EcN group than in the other DSS-induced colitis groups, demonstrating a potent ROS-scavenging effect. Furthermore, MPO activity (Fig. 6L) and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, including IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ (Fig. 6M–P), were significantly reduced in the DTI@EcN group compared to other treatments, confirming its strong anti-inflammatory activity. Collectively, these results demonstrate the robust prophylactic and therapeutic efficacy of DTI@EcN in mitigating DSS-induced colitis. Using aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA) as a positive control, DTI@EcN demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy (Fig. S25), likely attributable to its multiple therapeutic mechanisms, including anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant, and microbiome-modulating effects.

Modulatory effects of DTI@EcN on the gut microbiome

Given the enhanced colonization and prolonged retention of EcN in the intestines after DTI encapsulation, the regulatory effects of DTI@EcN on gut microbiota were further investigated via a high-throughput 16S rDNA sequencing assay. The α-diversity of intestinal microbiota, represented by the ACE, Chao, and Shannon indexes, was first analyzed to assess microbial richness and diversity. As shown in Fig. 7A–C, DSS treatment showed the lowest OUT levels, whereas all EcN-based formulations increased the diversity. Notably, the DTI@EcN group showed the highest levels in all ACE, Chao, and Shannon indexes among all treatment groups, indicating a significantly improved richness and diversity of microbiota in mice with DTI@EcN treatment. The improved diversity in the DTI@EcN treatment group was further supported by the increased numbers of Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) compared to the DSS+ group (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, the β-diversity that reflects the similarity of communities revealed that the microbial community in the DTI@EcN group closely resembled that of healthy mice, suggesting an almost recovered intestinal microenvironment in mice after DTI@EcN treatment (Fig. 7E). Next, the bacterial composition analysis at the family level showed that DTI@EcN treatment significantly increased the abundance of beneficial families, including Lachnospiraceae (producers of short-chain fatty acids) [34] and Lactobacillaceae (which regulates intestinal pH, strengthens the barrier function, and maintains homeostasis) [35], while reducing pathogenic Enterobacteriaceae compared with the DSS + group (Fig. 7F) [36]. At the order level, the relative abundance of Desulfovibrionales and Enterobacterales (associated with barrier disruption and inflammation) [37,38] was significantly decreased, whereas Bacteroidales and Lactobacillales, which support intestinal homeostasis [39,40], were enriched in the DTI@EcN group (Fig. 7G–I). LEfSe analysis further confirmed an increased abundance of beneficial bacteria and a reduction in pathogenic taxa following DTI@EcN treatment (Fig. 7J). At the genus level, DTI@EcN markedly elevated the relative abundances of Muribaculaceae (involved in inflammation regulation) [41] and Prevotellaceae UCG-001 (a short-chain fatty acid producer) [42], while reducing Bilophila, which compromises tight junction integrity and mucus stability (Fig. 7K–M) [43]. Moreover, Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant associations between microbial abundance and inflammatory cytokines at the order level. Specifically, the abundance of Bacteroidales, a typical commensal bacterium [39], was negatively correlated with pro-inflammatory cytokines, whereas the abundances of Enterobacterales and Desulfovibrionales were positively correlated with these cytokines (Fig. S26). These results suggest that DTI@EcN can improve the intestinal microenvironment by modulating the gut microbiota, supporting microbiome regulation as a key mechanism underlying its therapeutic effects. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that DTI@EcN treatment enhanced beneficial bacterial populations, suppressed pathogenic species, and restored microbial richness and diversity, contributing to the therapeutic improvement of colitis.

Therapeutic effectiveness of DTI@EcN against Salmonella typhimurium (STm)-induced colitis

The enhanced mucoadhesion and mucus permeability of DTI@EcN may competitively inhibit pathogenic bacterial colonization, thereby mitigating pathogen-induced colitis. To explore this potential, we further evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of DTI@EcN in a murine colitis model induced by STm, a pathogen known for its microtubule protein secretion. The STm-induced colitis model was established as illustrated in Fig. 8A. Mice were pre-treated with streptomycin (100 μL of a 200 mg/mL solution) prior to oral STm administration, followed by treatment with various EcN and IDE formulations for 4 consecutive days. The inhibitory effect of DTI@EcN on STm colonization was assessed by quantifying STm levels in fecal samples during the treatment period. As shown in Fig. 8B, STm levels began to decline from the second day in all EcN-containing groups. Notably, DTI@EcN exhibited the most significant reduction, with markedly lower STm counts than all other groups by day 3. On the final day, fecal STm levels in the DTI@EcN group were reduced by 86%, 78%, 68%, and 48% compared to the STm+, IDE, EcN, and DPN@EcN groups, respectively (Fig. 8C), indicating robust suppression of STm colonization and proliferation. Since the cecum plays a key role in fecal formation, we quantified both STm and EcN in cecal contents. DTI@EcN treatment significantly reduced STm levels by 79%, 69%, 69%, and 18% (Fig. 8D), while enhancing EcN colonization by 4.6-, 4.3-, 2.7-, and 1.6-fold compared to the STm+, IDE, EcN, and DPN@EcN groups, respectively (Fig. 8E). These results further underscore the potent therapeutic effect of DTI@EcN against pathogenic infection.

Conclusions

IBD, characterized by chronic intestinal inflammation, is a multifactorial disorder associated with disrupted intestinal homeostasis, including oxidative stress, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and alterations in gut microbiota [44]. Although current therapies can temporarily alleviate symptoms, they fail to effectively restore the damaged intestinal microenvironment, often resulting in relapse. Bacteriotherapy using probiotics offers a promising alternative due to its capacity to dynamically modulate gut homeostasis [[45], [46], [47]]. First, probiotics can produce short-chain fatty acids, vitamins, and other metabolites that are beneficial for intestinal health. Second, their competitive colonization effectively prevents the invasion and establishment of pathogenic bacteria, thereby enhancing intestinal barrier function [48]. However, conventional probiotic treatments face several limitations, such as low viability under harsh gastrointestinal conditions, insufficient intestinal retention, and limited intrinsic bioactivity. Most probiotic strains are susceptible to gastric acidity, resulting in extremely low viability after oral administration [49,50]. Additionally, oxidative and inflammatory conditions, combined with competition from the resident microbiota, hinder effective colonization and reduce the abundance of probiotics in the gut. Although long-term probiotic supplementation may gradually influence the intestinal milieu, their modest bioactivity significantly restricts therapeutic outcomes [51]. Overcoming these limitations is critical for maximizing the full potential of probiotics in IBD treatment.

Given the therapeutic potential of probiotics and the limitations they face in IBD treatment, we developed a multifunctional drug-loaded liposomal coating strategy to enhance the efficacy of probiotics. This liposomal shell acts as a protective barrier, shielding probiotics from various hostile invasions, including simulated gastric and intestinal fluids (SGF and SIF), bile salts, antibiotics, ROS, and lysozyme. As a result, the coated probiotics demonstrated markedly improved viability under gastrointestinal conditions, which not only enhanced their modulatory activity in the gut but also facilitated better storage and transportation. Additionally, the liposomal design incorporated terminal amine groups and PEG2000 chains. The exposed amine groups become protonated in the stomach, thereby increasing electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged intestinal mucosal surface. This promoted improved adhesion and colonization on the mucus layer. Meanwhile, the presence of PEG2000 chains in the liposomal coating enhanced mucosal penetration, allowing the probiotics to infiltrate deeper into the mucus and achieve prolonged intestinal retention. To further augment therapeutic outcomes, we conjugated IDE, a molecule with potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, onto the liposomal surface via a ROS-responsive linker. In inflamed intestinal regions with elevated ROS levels, IDE was selectively released, delivering localized antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. This targeted release, combined with enhanced probiotic colonization, could increase IDE concentration specifically in the gut, thereby maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic exposure. Furthermore, in vivo studies using DSS-induced colitis models confirmed the platform's strong prophylactic and therapeutic potential, as evidenced by restored colonic architecture, improved expression of tight junction proteins, reduced ROS levels, and diminished inflammatory cytokine infiltration. Moreover, the developed probiotic system effectively suppressed the pathogen of STm colonization and the associated inflammatory response in STm-induced colitis models. Importantly, all components, including the liposome, IDE, and probiotic, have excellent biocompatibility, supporting the safety and translational potential of this platform.

Compared with existing microbial therapies, this multifunctional liposome-encapsulated probiotic system exerts multiple synergistic effects. It not only enhances probiotic resistance to the gastrointestinal environment but also leverages protonatable amino groups and PEG2000 on the liposomal surface to improve adhesion to and penetration of the intestinal mucus layer, thereby prolonging intestinal retention. Additionally, the platform enables responsive release of the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant drug idebenone at sites with elevated ROS, resulting in improved therapeutic outcomes. Overall, the resulting platform offers a promising strategy for enhancing bacteriotherapy in IBD treatment, with significant efficacy and a favorable safety profile.
