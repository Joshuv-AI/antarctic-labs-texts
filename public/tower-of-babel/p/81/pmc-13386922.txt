DNA demethylation of ANXA4 is associated with atrial fibrillation risk through myeloid immune mechanisms: evidence from Mendelian randomization and multi-omics analyses
Authors: Qingyi Zeng, Jingjing Xiang, Yilan Li, Mengjun Huang, Zhanglan Wang, Mengjiang Hu, Weihong Sun, Haiwen Li, Tao Xu, Nian Den, Zhengmei Zhou, Jin Chen, Jingjing Chen, Wei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is a common arrhythmia affecting millions of patients globally. While epigenetic modifications play a significant role in cardiovascular diseases, their contribution to AF remains incompletely understood. Annexin A4 (ANXA4), a calcium-dependent phospholipid-binding protein, is implicated in inflammation and immune-mediated diseases, but its epigenetic regulation and mechanistic role in AF pathogenesis have not been systematically explored.MethodsThis study employed a multi-omics approach, integrating Mendelian randomization (MR), DNA methylation quantitative trait loci (mQTL)-mediated analysis, bulk and single-cell transcriptomics, and clinical validation. Two-sample MR utilized plasma proteome quantitative trait loci data from 4759 Icelandic individuals and AF genome-wide association study summary statistics from FinnGen and OpenGWAS databases. mQTL-based mediation analysis leveraged data from 27,750 individuals, and ANXA4 promoter methylation in atrial tissue was assessed in the GSE62727 dataset. Clinical validation involved qPCR for ANXA4 mRNA and CpG sites cg11942603 and cg22792910 in peripheral blood from 130 participants (77 AF patients and 53 sinus rhythm controls). Additionally, ANXA4 protein levels in peripheral blood leukocytes were analyzed using Western Blotting. Machine learning models were used to assess diagnostic performance, and logistic regression analyzed clinical associations.ResultsMR analysis provided evidence consistent with genetically predicted higher ANXA4 levels increasing AF risk (odds ratio = 1.17, 95% CI 1.11–1.23, P = 1.73 × 10−10), with Bayesian colocalization supporting a shared genetic signal at rs17037076. mQTL-based mediation analysis indicated that hypomethylation at cg11942603 and cg22792910 significantly mediated the association between ANXA4 and AF. Peripheral blood and atrial tissue analyses confirmed lower methylation levels at cg11942603 in AF patients. qPCR confirmed significantly elevated ANXA4 mRNA expression in the peripheral blood of AF patients. Western Blot analysis further revealed a significant increase in intracellular ANXA4 protein levels within peripheral blood leukocytes of AF patients (P = 0.01). Single-cell RNA sequencing showed higher ANXA4 expression across various immune cell types in AF. Larger left atrial diameter, log-transformed NT-proBNP, and ANXA4 mRNA were independently associated with AF, while higher ANXA4 methylation was associated with lower odds of AF. Machine learning models demonstrated high discriminative ability for AF diagnosis (AUC 0.949–0.962).ConclusionsOur multi-omics approach provides evidence that hypomethylation at specific ANXA4 CpG sites is associated with increased gene expression and elevated AF risk. Increased ANXA4 mRNA and protein levels are associated with an enhanced immune response, particularly in myeloid cells, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic candidate to differentiate AF from sinus rhythm. Machine learning models further support the predictive value of ANXA4. Furthermore, decreased ANXA4 methylation levels were associated with TET2 upregulation. ANXA4 expression and promoter methylation may serve as potential diagnostic indicators and candidate targets for future precision medicine strategies in AF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13148-026-02131-y.Keywords: ANXA4, Atrial fibrillation, Epigenetics, DNA Methylation, Mendelian randomization, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Machine learning

Background

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a common cardiac arrhythmia that affects millions of patients worldwide and impairs quality of life [1]. Related studies have found that atrial fibrillation is associated with various factors, including physiological, psychological and environmental factors [2–4]. Despite the continuous development of treatment methods for AF in recent years, including drug therapy, electrical cardioversion, and catheter ablation, recurrence rates remain high and long‑term efficacy is often suboptimal [5]. This underscores the need to better understand the underlying mechanisms of AF and to identify novel, mechanism‑based therapeutic targets [6].

Current research indicates that the pathogenesis of AF involves complex biological processes, including electrophysiological remodeling, structural remodeling, and inflammatory responses [7]. Several studies have highlighted specific molecular pathways play a key role in the occurrence of AF, yet our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying AF remains incomplete [8].

In this context, the functional role of ANXA4 (Annexin A4) has garnered increasing attention. ANXA4 is a calcium-dependent phospholipid-binding protein implicated in membrane trafficking, signal transduction, and inflammatory modulation across diverse pathological conditions [9]. While prior investigations have documented significant correlations between ANXA4 expression and AF occurrence [10], the precise regulatory mechanisms governing ANXA4 in the cardiac context remain poorly defined [11]. Understanding the epigenetic regulation of ANXA4 and its downstream effector pathways may therefore provide mechanistic insights with potential translational relevance.

Here, we applied Mendelian randomization (MR), methylation quantitative trait locus (mQTL) mediation analysis, transcriptomic profiling, and single‑cell RNA sequencing to systematically investigate the role of ANXA4 in AF. By leveraging protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) data from a large Icelandic cohort together with AF genome‑wide association study (GWAS) data from the FinnGen and OpenGWAS databases, we assessed the relationship between genetically predicted ANXA4 levels and AF risk while minimizing confounding bias.

The primary objectives of this study were threefold: (1) to evaluate the potential causal relationship between ANXA4 and AF using genetically informed approaches; (2) to integrate DNA methylation data to identify specific CpG sites that may regulate ANXA4 transcription; and (3) to assess the clinical utility of ANXA4 expression and promoter methylation as potential diagnostic indicators in patients with AF. Through this integrated mechanistic investigation, we aim to provide novel insights into the epigenetic landscape of AF and to lay a foundation for precision‑medicine strategies targeting DNA methylation‑mediated immune dysregulation.

Methods

Data sources and ethical approval

This study integrated multiple publicly available genomic datasets and original clinical samples, all collected in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines. Protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) data were obtained from a large-scale plasma proteome study of 4,759 Icelandic individuals [12]. Genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics for AF were sourced from FinnGen (Release 10; https://r10.finngen.fi/), comprising 22,068 AF cases (electrocardiographically confirmed) and 116,926 controls. An independent validation dataset was obtained from OpenGWAS (ID: ebi-a-GCST006414), including 60,620 AF cases and 970,216 controls. DNA methylation quantitative trait loci (mQTL) data were acquired from the GoDMC database (http://mqtldb.godmc.org.uk), which aggregates genome-wide methylation profiles from 27,750 individuals of European ancestry. These substantial sample sizes provide ample statistical power to detect robust associations between genetic variants and phenotypes, thus ensuring the validity and statistical efficiency of our Mendelian randomization estimates.

Single-cell expression quantitative trait loci (sc-eQTL) data across 14 immune cell types were obtained from the OneK1K resource (https://onek1k.org/), encompassing approximately 1.27 million peripheral blood mononuclear cells from 982 donors.

Bulk RNA-sequencing datasets for AF were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), including GSE41177 [13], GSE31821, and GSE79768 [14]. Single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets were obtained from GEO accessions GSE261170 and GSE197518, comprising myocardial, endocardial, and peripheral blood samples from 24 AF patients and 5 sinus rhythm (SR) controls. All publicly available datasets used in this study received ethical approval from the respective institutional review boards.

For clinical validation, peripheral blood samples and associated clinical data were collected from 130 participants (77 AF patients, 53 SR controls) at the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University between September and November 2025. The ECG diagnostic criteria for AF were the disappearance of the P wave, replaced by fibrillation waves (f waves) of varying amplitudes and morphologies; an f-wave frequency of 350–600 times/min; and an irregular R–R interval. All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committee (Ethics Approval No. 383, 2025). All procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

To ensure the robustness of our findings and prevent bias, we systematically assessed dataset overlap. First, for the two-sample MR analyses, the exposure datasets (Icelandic pQTL cohort and GoDMC mQTL cohort) and the outcome datasets (FinnGen and OpenGWAS AF cohorts) are derived from geographically and demographically distinct populations, ensuring minimal to no sample overlap. Second, all incorporated GEO datasets originated from completely independent studies performed by different research institutions, with no shared patients across these distinct accessions. Finally, our local clinical validation cohort is a newly recruited, fully independent prospective sample that has zero overlap with any of the aforementioned public genomic or transcriptomic databases.

Mendelian randomization analysis

Mendelian randomization analyses were conducted using the `TwoSampleMR` R package (version 0.5.6). For pQTL-based MR, instrumental variables (IVs) were selected according to standard criteria: genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8), physical proximity to the gene locus (± 1000 kb), linkage disequilibrium threshold (r2 < 0.1), and instrument strength (F-statistic > 10). A slightly relaxed LD threshold (r2 < 0.1) was deliberately employed for molecular traits to retain a sufficient number of independent variants for essential pleiotropy sensitivity analyses. Five complementary MR methods were applied: inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode. For exposures with a single IV, the Wald ratio method was employed; for multiple IVs, IVW served as the primary analytical approach [15]. Causal estimates were corrected for multiple testing using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, with FDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Sensitivity analyses included Cochran's Q test for heterogeneity assessment [16], the MR-Egger intercept test to evaluate horizontal pleiotropy [17], and leave-one-out analysis to examine the influence of individual SNPs on overall causal estimates. Results were visualized using scatter plots, funnel plots, and forest plots.

Two-step Mendelian randomization for molecular mediation analysis

To investigate epigenetic regulation of ANXA4, we performed a two-step MR analysis. DNA methylation probes corresponding to ANXA4 were extracted from Illumina HumanMethylation450 BeadChip annotation files using the `minfi`, `IlluminaHumanMethylation450kanno.ilmn12.hg19`, and `GEOquery` R packages. Methylation-associated SNPs (mQTLs) meeting the same selection criteria as pQTL analyses were identified. Two-step MR was conducted: (1) mQTL to ANXA4 gene expression; (2) ANXA4 gene to AF risk. Mediation analysis was performed using the delta method [18], with the indirect effect calculated as β1 × β2, where β1 represents the effect of methylation on gene expression and β2 represents the effect of gene expression on AF risk. Standard errors were computed as SE(indirect) = √[β22 × SE(β1)2 + β12 × SE(β2)2]. The proportion mediated was defined as (β1 × β2)/β_total, where β_total is the total effect of methylation on AF.

Colocalization analysis

To further validate the causal relationship between ANXA4 and AF, we performed colocalization analysis utilizing plasma pQTL data specifically obtained from deCODE Genetics [12]. Bayesian colocalization was conducted using the coloc R package (version 5.2.3) [19] to evaluate whether specific SNPs in the pQTL dataset colocalize with AF-associated variants at the ANXA4 locus, thereby supporting a shared causal variant. The filtering criteria for selection included: a significance level of 5 × 10−8, a physical proximity of ± 10,000 kb, and a linkage disequilibrium threshold of 0.1. The posterior probabilities were calculated to determine the likelihood of colocalization, supporting the proposed mechanistic link between ANXA4 and AF risk.

Transcriptomic and immune infiltration analysis

Bulk RNA-sequencing datasets (including GSE41177, GSE31821, and GSE79768) were retrieved from the GEO database. Detailed information regarding the accession numbers, sample sizes, tissue sources, and specific applications for all accessed GEO datasets in this study is summarized in Supplementary Table S4. The bulk datasets were integrated and analyzed using the `limma`, `ggpubr`, and `pROC` R packages. Differential gene expression was assessed using criteria of |log2FC|≥ 0.5 and adjusted P < 0.05. Functional enrichment analyses were performed using KEGG pathway and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) [20, 21] to identify biological processes associated with ANXA4 expression. Immune cell infiltration was estimated using the CIBERSORT algorithm [22], which deconvolutes bulk tissue expression profiles into 22 distinct immune cell type proportions.

To establish causal relationships between ANXA4 and specific immune cell populations, single-cell eQTL data from 14 peripheral immune cell types (OneK1K resource) were used as exposures in MR analyses with AF as the outcome. Analytical procedures and sensitivity tests were identical to those described for pQTL-based MR.

Independent validation of DNA Methylation in atrial tissue

To validate ANXA4 methylation alterations in atrial tissue—the primary anatomical substrate of AF—we analyzed publicly available DNA methylation array data from the GSE62727 dataset (detailed in Supplementary Table S4), which includes atrial tissue biopsies from 7 AF patients and 4 sinus rhythm (SR) controls. Methylation levels (β-values) at CpG sites cg11942603 and cg22792910 were extracted and compared between AF and SR groups using independent-samples t tests and Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen’s d.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis

Single-cell RNA-seq data were obtained from GEO accessions GSE261170 and GSE197518 (Supplementary Table S4). The data were processed using the Seurat (version 4.3.0) and Harmony (version 0.1.0) packages in R. Quality control retained cells with > 50 detected genes and < 5% mitochondrial gene content; samples with fewer than 30 cells were excluded. Data were log-normalized, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were identified. Cross-sample integration was performed using the FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions, followed by batch correction with the Harmony algorithm. Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied for dimensionality reduction [23], and significant principal components were identified using the JackStraw procedure. Cells were clustered based on the top 30 principal components, and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used for two-dimensional visualization [24, 25]. Cell-type annotation was performed using canonical marker genes and reference datasets.

To explore the mechanistic role of ANXA4 in AF, we employed two complementary analytical strategies:

Strategy 1: Disease-state comparison (AF vs. SR atrial myocardium). We focused on atrial myocardial tissue samples from 20 AF patients and 5 SR controls (GSE261170, GSE197518) to identify disease-associated changes in cellular composition, signaling pathway activity, and metabolic profiles. This strategy aimed to characterize immune microenvironment remodeling at the primary anatomical site of arrhythmogenesis.

Strategy 2: Trans-tissue immune trafficking model (ANXA4-stratified analysis). To model immune cell migration from peripheral blood to cardiac tissue, we integrated a trans-tissue continuum dataset. To clarify the exact composition of this integrated dataset, it comprised paired subpopulations selectively extracted from the aforementioned single-cell resources: (1) 2 blood samples from AF patients, (2) 2 atrial endocardial samples from AF patients, and (3) 20 atrial myocardial samples from AF patients. By integrating these specific compartments, cells were stratified into ANXA4-high and ANXA4-low groups (median split) to investigate ANXA4-dependent regulation of immune cell trafficking, activation states, intercellular communication, and metabolic reprogramming across the “circulation–atrial endocardium–atrial myocardium” continuum.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

For Strategy 1 (AF vs. SR comparison), pseudotime analysis was performed to model disease-related transcriptional dynamics within AF myocardium. High ANXA4-expressing cells were designated as the trajectory root, allowing us to examine how cellular states and gene expression patterns evolve along a continuum anchored by ANXA4 upregulation.

For Strategy 2 (trans-tissue continuum), pseudotime analysis was designed with three complementary objectives [26–28]. First, we extracted monocytes and macrophages from the integrated blood–endocardium–myocardium dataset and applied DDRTree-based dimensionality reduction to assess whether these myeloid cells form a continuous trajectory across tissue compartments. Second, to capture global trans-tissue dynamics, we randomly sampled 10,000 cells from the entire continuum and reconstructed pseudotime trajectories using DDRTree, without predefining a root state. Third, we performed an additional trajectory analysis using Monocle3, specifying the 30 cells with the highest ANXA4 expression (≥ 75th percentile) as the root, in order to evaluate how ANXA4-high states align with the broader pseudotime structure.

Comparisons between these trajectories were used to assess the robustness of using high ANXA4-expressing cells as a reference state and to gain insight into the potential role of ANXA4 in disease progression. Pseudotime values were mapped onto UMAP coordinates using a blue-to-yellow color gradient. Gene expression dynamics along pseudotime were modeled using generalized additive models (GAMs, cubic spline with k = 5) with 95% confidence intervals.

Cell–cell communication and cellular metabolism analysis

For Strategy 1 (AF vs. SR comparison), intercellular communication networks were inferred using CellChat (version 1.5.0) [29] to compare ligand–receptor interaction probabilities between AF and SR atrial myocardium. Differential pathway activities were quantified as ΔProbability = Prob_AF − Prob_SR and visualized to highlight AF-associated changes in signaling.

For Strategy 2 (ANXA4-stratified analysis), cells were divided into ANXA4-high and ANXA4-low groups based on the median ANXA4 expression level. Ligand–receptor communication probabilities were computed separately for macrophages, monocytes, and cardiomyocytes in each group. Differential communication metrics were calculated as ΔProb = Prob_High − Prob_Low, and the top 20–40 ligand–receptor pairs with the largest absolute ΔProb values were displayed as heatmaps.

Metabolic pathway activity was assessed using single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) [30] based on KEGG metabolic gene sets. Spearman correlation analysis was used to evaluate associations between ANXA4 expression and pathway enrichment scores in monocytes, macrophages, and cardiomyocytes.

Clinical validation: gene expression and DNA Methylation

We recruited 130 participants (77 AF patients, 53 sinus rhythm controls) from the Cardiology Department. The 53 SR controls were selected from patients admitted to the same department during the identical period primarily for the management of hypertension. Importantly, these control patients presented with normal sinus rhythm on continuous ECG monitoring and were explicitly confirmed to have no prior history or current evidence of any arrhythmias. Inclusion criteria: The ECG diagnostic criteria for AF were the disappearance of the P wave, replaced by fibrillation waves (f waves) of varying amplitudes and morphologies; an f-wave frequency of 350–600 times/min; and an irregular R–R interval. Exclusion criteria: (1) known malignant tumours; (2) thyroid disease or severe hepatic and renal insufficiency; (3) concomitant acute and chronic infections and recent cerebrovascular diseases; (4) autoimmune diseases; (5) infectious diseases such as pneumonia and sepsis.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR

Peripheral venous blood was collected from all participants in EDTA anticoagulant tubes. Total RNA was extracted from whole blood using the RNAprep Pure High-Efficiency Blood RNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Reverse transcription was performed with the M5 Sprint qPCR RT Kit with gDNA Remover (Megafusion, Beijing, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was conducted using 2 × M5 HiPer SYBR Premix EsTaq with Tli RNaseH (Megafusion, MF787-01) on a QuantStudio™ Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The thermocycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 40 s. All samples were analyzed in technical triplicates. The specificity of the amplification was confirmed by melting curve analysis, which demonstrated a single dissociation peak for each target.

Primers for ANXA4 and the reference gene GAPDH were designed using Primer-BLAST, and sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S5. Relative ANXA4 mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2(−ΔCT) method, where ΔCT = CT(ANXA4) − CT(GAPDH).

Analysis of ANXA4 mRNA and DNA Methylation in peripheral blood

Genomic DNA was extracted from whole blood using the Blood Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen Biotech) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Bisulfite conversion was carried out with the DNA Bisulfite Conversion Kit (Tiangen Biotech). Methylation-specific PCR (MSP) primers targeting CpG sites cg11942603 and cg22792910 within the ANXA4 promoter were designed using MethPrimer based on the UCSC Genome Browser (hg19 assembly). Primer sequences are provided in Table 2. Commercially available methylated and unmethylated DNA standards (BioLab, BTN100936) were used as positive controls [31]. qPCR reactions were performed separately for methylated and unmethylated templates. Relative methylation levels were calculated as: methylation level = 2(−ΔCT_meth)/[2(−ΔCT_meth) + 2(−ΔCT_unmeth)], where ΔCT_meth = CT(methylated sample) − CT(methylated standard) and ΔCT_unmeth = CT(unmethylated sample) − CT(unmethylated standard) [32].

Protein extraction and western blotting

Total protein was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes (isolated via red blood cell lysis) using RIPA buffer. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ANXA4 (1:1000; ZenBio, R40125) and β-actin (1:5000; ZenBio, R380624) as the loading control. Following incubation with an HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature, protein bands were visualized using ECL and quantified via ImageJ.

Machine learning-based diagnostic modeling

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of ANXA4 expression and methylation alongside clinical variables, data preprocessing was first performed. Data quality checks confirmed no missing values across the 130 samples, allowing the full cohort to be retained. Continuous variables were standardized using the scale function.

To identify key diagnostic genes, a Random Forest (RF) model was initially constructed. The optimal number of decision trees (ntree = 500) was determined by minimizing the Out-of-Bag (OOB) error rate (11.96%), and feature importance was ranked using the Mean Decrease Gini index.

For clinical prediction, we utilized three machine learning algorithms: RF, LASSO regression, and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). For the clinical RF model, the dataset was randomly partitioned into a 70% training set and a 30% independent testing set using the caret package (seed = 123). LASSO regression was performed using the glmnet package, utilizing tenfold cross-validation to determine the optimal penalty parameter (λmin) for selecting non-zero features. Finally, an XGBoost model (nrounds = 100, objective = binary:logistic) was constructed using the top selected features. To enhance model interpretability, the shapr package was employed to calculate Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values via the empirical approach, quantitatively determining each feature's contribution to the AF diagnosis [33–36].

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1). Clinical data were analyzed using SPSS (version 22.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent-samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median [interquartile range] and compared using Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were compared using chi-squared tests. Clinical figures were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). All statistical tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.The research process is shown in Fig. 1.

Results

Identification of candidate genes via Mendelian randomisation and transcriptomic analysis, validated by clinical samples and colocalisation

Leveraging pQTL data from Icelandic individuals as the exposure and AF GWAS data from the FinnGen and OpenGWAS databases as the outcome, we performed a two-sample MR analysis. The intersection of these analyses identified four proteins with significant causal associations (IVW P < 0.05): MDM4, DEFB113, EPHA4, and ANXA4 (Fig. 2A–B). Comprehensive sensitivity analyses, including leave-one-out, scatter plots, funnel plots, and forest plots, are detailed in Supplementary Material 1.

Subsequently, we integrated three independent transcriptomic datasets (GSE31821, GSE41177, and GSE79768) to cross-validate differential expression in clinical AF samples. Among the MR-identified candidates, ANXA4 emerged as the sole gene exhibiting consistent and significant upregulation in AF patients compared to sinus rhythm (SR) controls (log2FC = 0.62, adjusted P = 0.0031; Fig. 2C). In the FinnGen cohort, ANXA4 demonstrated a robust genetically predicted association with AF risk, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.17 (95% CI 1.11–1.23, P = 1.73 × 10−10). Crucially, we observed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept P = 0.98) or heterogeneity (MR-Egger Q P = 0.19). Furthermore, ANXA4 retained genome-wide significance following FDR correction (FDR < 0.01). Steiger filtering analysis confirmed the causal direction, showing that the instrumental variables explained significantly more variance in ANXA4 levels than in AF risk (P < 0.01).

To corroborate these findings, clinical validation was conducted in an independent cohort (77 AF patients, 53 SR controls). qPCR confirmed significantly elevated ANXA4 mRNA expression in the peripheral blood of AF patients (P = 0.0032; Fig. 2D). Consistent with transcriptomic changes, Western blot analysis revealed a significant increase in intracellular ANXA4 protein levels within peripheral blood leukocytes of AF patients (P = 0.01; Fig. 2E). Finally, Bayesian colocalisation analysis using deCODE pQTL data revealed a high posterior probability of colocalisation for SNP rs17037076 (PP.H4 = 0.9612; Fig. 2F), confirming it as a shared causal variant regulating both ANXA4 expression and AF susceptibility.

Epigenetic regulation of ANXA4: from genetic inference to clinical validation

MR analysis identified three CpG sites (cg11072779, cg11942603, and cg22792910) significantly associated with both ANXA4 expression and AF risk (Wald ratio P < 0.05; Fig. 3A, B). Two-step MR mediation analysis further revealed that the causal effect of DNA hypomethylation (at cg11942603 and cg22792910) on increased AF risk was substantially mediated through the upregulation of ANXA4 gene expression. Specifically, ANXA4 expression accounted for 66.94% (95% CI 0.75–133.13%, P = 0.047) and 65.56% (95% CI 19.10–112.03%, P = 0.006) of the total effect of cg11942603 and cg22792910 methylation on AF risk, respectively (Table 1). These findings suggest that DNA demethylation at these regulatory loci is potentially associated with ANXA4 transcriptional activation in AF.

The wide confidence intervals observed in the mediation proportions reflect the inherent statistical uncertainty when calculating standard errors from two relatively small effect sizes using the delta method, suggesting that these mediation estimates represent potential trends rather than precise quantifications.

To validate these computational predictions, we designed MSP assays targeting cg11942603 and cg22792910 (Table 2). Successful amplification of both methylated and unmethylated DNA products was confirmed via agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 3C). Subsequent clinical validation in a cohort of 130 participants demonstrated that methylation levels at both CpG sites were significantly reduced in AF patients compared to SR controls (P < 0.001; Fig. 3D). Furthermore, analysis of myocardial single-cell data corroborated these findings, revealing significant hypomethylation at the ANXA4 promoter site cg11942603 in AF tissue (β value: AF = 0.59 ± 0.06 vs. SR = 0.67 ± 0.02; P = 0.006, Cohen’s d = −1.40), indicating a large effect size (Fig. 3E). Although cg22792910 also exhibited a trend towards reduced methylation in AF, the difference did not reach statistical significance (β value: AF = 0.67 ± 0.02 vs. SR = 0.70 ± 0.03; P = 0.186, Cohen’s d = −0.71). Collectively, these tissue-level findings provide direct evidence that specific demethylation of ANXA4-regulatory CpG sites occurs in the atrial myocardium of AF patients, establishing a tissue-specific epigenetic dysregulation within the arrhythmogenic substrate.

ANXA4 is enriched in immune-inflammatory pathways and causally associated with monocyte abundance

Given the established role of ANXA4 in immune regulation, we performed pathway enrichment and immune infiltration analyses. GSEA revealed that ANXA4-high samples were significantly enriched for Toll-like receptor signaling, TGF-β signaling, and glycine metabolism pathways (Fig. 4A), whereas ANXA4-low samples showed enrichment in oxidative phosphorylation and ribosomal pathways (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that ANXA4 participates in both inflammatory signaling and metabolic reprogramming.

CIBERSORT-based immune cell deconvolution demonstrated significant alterations in immune cell composition in AF patients compared to SR controls. Specifically, plasma cells and monocytes were elevated, whereas M2 macrophages were reduced in AF myocardial tissue (Fig. 4C, D). These findings implicate chronic immune activation and impaired anti-inflammatory responses in AF pathogenesis.

To explore genetically predicted associations between ANXA4 and specific immune cell types, we performed MR analysis using single-cell eQTL data from 14 peripheral immune cell populations. Genetically predicted ANXA4 expression was associated with increased monocyte abundance (IVW P < 0.05, OR = 1.13) and decreased memory B cell proportions (IVW P < 0.05, OR = 0.78; Fig. 4E). Sensitivity analyses confirmed the absence of significant pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept P = 0.51) or heterogeneity (MR-Egger Q P = 0.22), supporting a direct regulatory role for ANXA4 in myeloid cell biology. Detailed sensitivity plots are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Positive correlation between TET2 expression and ANXA4 in AF atrial tissue

To elucidate the enzymatic mechanism underlying ANXA4 demethylation, we examined the expression of TET2 (Ten-Eleven Translocation 2). Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of AF atrial samples stratified by ANXA4 expression revealed that TET2 expression was significantly elevated in the ANXA4-high group (log2FC = 0.487, P < 2 × 10−16), highlighting a significant co-expression pattern between TET2 and ANXA4 in these cell populations (Fig. 5). This relationship was observed across multiple cell types, with the strongest associations detected in myeloid lineages (monocytes: log2FC = 0.459, P = 0.173; macrophages: log2FC = 0.503, P = 0.043; combined myeloid cells: log2FC = 0.496, P = 0.024) (Fig. 5).

Investigation of the functional role and mechanisms of ANXA4 in single-cell datasets from atrial fibrillation patients

Building on the findings that ANXA4 is upregulated through demethylation and may contribute to AF via immune response pathways, we further explored its potential functional role in cardiac tissue using single-cell transcriptomics and two complementary analytical strategies.

Cellular composition, signaling pathways, and metabolic differences between AF and SR atrial myocardial tissue (analysis strategy 1: disease-state comparison)

We analyzed atrial myocardial tissue samples from 20 AF patients and 5 SR controls to characterize disease-associated immune microenvironment alterations at the primary site of arrhythmogenesis (Fig. 6A). Standardized residual analysis revealed significant differences in cell-type proportions, with marked increases in immune cell infiltration (monocytes, macrophages) in AF myocardium compared to SR (Fig. 6B). ANXA4 expression was significantly elevated across multiple cell types in AF, with the highest levels observed in monocytes, macrophages (Fig. 6C).

F Pseudotime trajectory analysis with ANXA4-high cells as root. G ANXA4 expression dynamics along pseudotime. H Metabolic pathway alterations along pseudotime in AF vs. SR

Intercellular communication analysis using CellChat identified significant upregulation of inflammatory signaling pathways in AF myocardium, primarily involving MHC-I, MIF, and GALECTIN pathways, while structural homeostasis pathways (collagen, laminin signaling) were downregulated (Fig. 6D). Pathway-specific activity analysis confirmed that MHC-I and MIF signaling were significantly enhanced in AF compared to SR (Fig. 6E), consistent with sustained activation of innate immune and inflammatory responses in AF patients.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis using ANXA4-high cells as the starting point revealed distinct temporal dynamics: early pseudotime was dominated by macrophages and monocytes from AF myocardium; intermediate stages showed monocyte differentiation with B cell infiltration; terminal stages exhibited adipocyte and fibroblast predominance (Fig. 6F). ANXA4 expression dynamics showed peak levels in early-stage macrophages/monocytes, declining in intermediate stages, followed by a second peak in late-stage adipocytes (Fig. 6G), consistent with a potential role of ANXA4 in both immune activation and tissue remodeling.

Metabolic pathway analysis identified three significantly upregulated pathways in AF: linoleic acid metabolism (pro-inflammatory mediators), phenylalanine metabolism (oxidative stress), and steroid hormone biosynthesis (pro-fibrotic signaling), collectively consistent with a microenvironment conducive to arrhythmogenesis (Fig. 6H).

Trans-tissue immune cell trafficking and ANXA4-dependent intercellular communication and metabolic reprogramming (analysis strategy 2: ANXA4-stratified trans-tissue continuum analysis)

To explore potential transcriptional state transitions across different anatomical compartments and investigate ANXA4-associated phenotypic changes, we integrated single-cell data from 2 AF peripheral blood samples, 2 endocardial samples, and 20 AF myocardial samples (Fig. 7A).

Trans-tissue continuity validation

Monocytes and macrophages from blood, endocardium, and myocardium aligned continuously along the same trajectory without obvious discontinuities, suggesting a persistent trans-tissue transition (Fig. 7B–D). ANXA4 maintained low-to-moderate expression throughout this trajectory, with scattered elevations particularly during the blood-to-myocardium transition, supporting the view that immune cells from these three compartments can be approximated as a unified continuum for analytical purposes.

Pseudotime trajectory dynamics

By not specifying a starting point for the pseudotime analysis, we observed that ANXA4 expression was relatively high at early pseudotime stages, which suggests a continuum of cellular states that may hypothetically align with immune cell distribution from peripheral circulation to cardiac tissue, though this requires longitudinal validation (Supplementary Material 3). Designating ANXA4-high cells as the trajectory root, we identified two principal developmental branches: (1) the main branch connecting endocardial cell populations (endothelial cells, cardiomyocytes); (2) a secondary branch extending to myocardial-resident cells (cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts). Key transition patterns included immune cell migration (blood monocytes to endocardium-localized subtypes during pseudotime 15–25) (Fig. 7E, F).

ANXA4 expression exhibited a bimodal distribution: an early peak in blood immune cells (lymphocytes, monocytes, macrophages) that may facilitate transendothelial migration, and a late peak in myocardial structural cells (fibroblasts, adipocytes) that may contribute to tissue remodeling (Fig. 7G).

ANXA4-dependent metabolic reprogramming

Stratified analysis by ANXA4 expression levels revealed cell-type-specific metabolic alterations (Fig. 8A, E, H):

ANXA4 expression level-dependent metabolic and communication network reprogramming. A Metabolic pathway correlations in macrophages (High vs. Low ANXA4). B–D Macrophage intercellular communication analysis. E Metabolic correlations in monocytes. F, G Monocyte communication analysis. H Metabolic correlations in cardiomyocytes. I, J Cardiomyocyte communication analysis

Macrophages: The pantothenate and coenzyme A (CoA) biosynthesis pathway exhibited negative correlation in ANXA4-high macrophages but positive correlation in ANXA4-low macrophages, suggesting that higher ANXA4 expression may be associated with a shift in metabolic flux away from oxidative metabolism toward pro-inflammatory pathways (Fig. 8A).

Monocytes: ANXA4-high monocytes demonstrated strong activation of pantothenate-CoA, amino sugar metabolism, and mannose biosynthesis pathways, all supporting fatty acid oxidation and prostaglandin synthesis—hallmarks of pro-inflammatory metabolic reprogramming (Fig. 8E).

Cardiomyocytes: ANXA4-high cardiomyocytes exhibited activation of amino acid metabolism, lipid biosynthesis, glycosphingolipid metabolism, and signal transduction pathways, whereas ANXA4-low cardiomyocytes displayed relatively suppressed metabolic activity (Fig. 8H).

ANXA4-dependent intercellular communication networks

CellChat analysis stratified by ANXA4 expression revealed profound alterations in intercellular signaling (Fig. 8B–D, F, G, I, J):

Macrophage-mediated communication: In the ANXA4-high group, MHC-I and MHC-II signaling from cardiomyocytes to macrophages was significantly enhanced. Macrophages transmitted signals to neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, cardiomyocytes, and B cells, which may amplify inflammatory responses and favor fibrotic remodeling (Fig. 8B–D).

Monocyte-mediated communication: The MIF signaling pathway emerged as a central hub, with multiple cell types (lymphocytes, neutrophils, macrophages) converging on monocytes via CD74 + CXCR4 and CD74 + CD44 receptor complexes. This pattern was consistent with AF vs. SR tissue comparisons, highlighting a potential role for ANXA4 in sustaining pro-inflammatory monocyte activation (Fig. 8F, G).

Cardiomyocyte-mediated communication: In ANXA4-high conditions, signal interactions with lymphocytes were enhanced, and cardiomyocytes transmitted signals to macrophages and fibroblasts, suggesting that higher ANXA4 expression may be linked to paracrine signaling pathways that favor myocardial fibrosis (Fig. 8I, J).

These convergent findings from AF versus SR comparison and ANXA4-stratified analyses support a model in which ANXA4 may orchestrate immune microenvironment remodeling through coordinated regulation of myeloid cell activation, metabolic reprogramming, and intercellular communication across tissue compartments.

Correlation analysis revealed biologically coherent relationships among key variables (Fig. 9A): ANXA4 mRNA was negatively correlated with its methylation level (r = − 0.42, P < 0.05), positively correlated with NT-proBNP (r = 0.38, P < 0.05) and LAD (r = 0.35, P < 0.05), and negatively correlated with LVEF (r = − 0.29, P = 0.05). Machine learning model performance

Three complementary machine learning approaches were employed:

Random Forest (RF): ANXA4 mRNA expression ranked first in variable importance (mean decrease in accuracy and Gini index), followed by NT-proBNP, LAD, and admission systolic blood pressure. The RF model achieved an AUC of 0.962 in tenfold cross-validation (Fig. 9D). Beyond the highly discriminative Area Under the Curve (AUC = 0.949–0.962), the clinical predictive models were systematically evaluated on the 30% hold-out test set (n = 38) to confirm their robustness. Based on the test-set confusion matrix, the Random Forest model yielded an overall Accuracy of 89.47% (95% CI 75.20–97.06%).

LASSO Regression: Optimal λ selection retained five core features: ANXA4 mRNA, NT-proBNP, LAD, admission systolic blood pressure, and ANXA4 methylation (Fig. 9C). The LASSO model achieved an AUC of 0.949 (Fig. 9E).

XGBoost-SHAP: SHAP value analysis confirmed that ANXA4 mRNA contributed the most to AF probability at the individual-sample level, with consistent positive directionality across all AF patients (Fig. 9F, G). NT-proBNP and LAD also contributed positively, whereas ANXA4 methylation contributed negatively, consistent with the "hypomethylation-high expression" phenotype.

These results indicate that ANXA4 expression and methylation status, in combination with cardiac structural and functional parameters, can provide robust discrimination between AF and SR, highlighting their potential as diagnostic and risk stratification candidates.

Discussion

Mechanistic role of ANXA4 demethylation in atrial fibrillation

In this study, we identified ANXA4 as a novel epigenetic regulator that may contribute to atrial fibrillation through myeloid immune activation and metabolic reprogramming. This finding underscores the importance of genetic variations in modulating the epigenetic landscape surrounding ANXA4.

Using integrated multi-omics approaches—incorporating Mendelian randomization, mQTL mediation, single-cell transcriptomics, and clinical validation—we provide evidence that DNA demethylation at specific ANXA4 CpG sites (cg11942603 and cg22792910) is linked to aberrant ANXA4 gene activation. Critically, all single-cell mechanistic analyses were performed on atrial tissue samples, ensuring direct relevance to the anatomical site of AF pathogenesis. Independent validation using the GSE62727 cohort confirmed significant hypomethylation at cg11942603 in atrial biopsies from AF patients, supporting the presence of epigenetic dysregulation at the arrhythmogenic substrate itself. Furthermore, our discovery that TET2 expression is significantly upregulated in ANXA4-high atrial cells suggests a potential mechanistic link that warrants further experimental investigation, as TET2 catalyzes the oxidation of 5-methylcytosine. The observed TET2–ANXA4 co-expression pattern, along with downstream proinflammatory myeloid phenotypes and intercellular signaling changes, may be associated with atrial inflammation and remodeling, though functional contribution remains to be proven.

Convergent evidence from dual single-cell analytical strategies

A key strength of this study is the adoption of a complementary single-cell analytical approach. Firstly, we found that immune microenvironment remodelling—characterised by increased myeloid cell infiltration and upregulation of the MHC-I/MIF signalling pathway—appears to be a prominent feature of AF. This finding aligns closely with recent proteomic studies that have revealed alterations in innate immune gene expression profiles associated with AF [11]. Importantly, the remodelling of the immune microenvironment is closely linked to ANXA4 expression, for which Mendelian randomisation studies have provided evidence consistent with a causal effect on AF risk [9].

Secondly, stratification analysis based on ANXA4 expression levels within the circulatory-endomyocardial-myocardial continuum indicated that these immune alterations correlate positively with ANXA4 expression. Cells exhibiting high ANXA4 expression demonstrated enhanced inflammatory signalling, metabolic reprogramming, and intercellular communication across tissue compartments. These findings are consistent with the relationships identified between various immune-related proteins and AF risk in whole-proteome Mendelian randomisation analyses [10]. Notably, the metabolic reprogramming characteristics observed in ANXA4 high-expressing cells are closely related to the electrophysiological abnormalities and structural remodelling seen in the pathophysiology of AF [1].

The convergence of these independent analytical methods reinforces the inference that ANXA4 may play a functional role in immune dysregulation in AF, rather than merely being associated with it. Specifically: (1) The comparison of disease states (AF vs. SR) identified immune activation as a pathological hallmark, consistent with findings from large-scale clinical studies linking AF to inflammatory markers; (2) The stratification analysis of ANXA4 (high vs. low expression within AF) demonstrated a dose-dependent relationship between ANXA4 levels and immune/metabolic phenotypes, supporting its potential as a therapeutic target; (3) The cross-tissue trajectory model illustrated transcriptional state transitions compatible with a hypothesized role for ANXA4 in the spectrum of immune cell activation across different tissue compartments, suggesting a multifaceted contribution to AF progression.

This dual-strategy design helps address the limitations of purely observational single-cell studies by providing a hierarchical framework that links disease association (AF vs. SR) to mechanistic hypotheses (ANXA4-dependent pathways). The findings corroborate recent large-scale proteomic Mendelian randomisation analyses, which similarly provided evidence consistent with a causal relationship between ANXA4 and AF risk, suggesting that these proteins could be promising therapeutic targets [11]. This robust evidence foundation provides a rationale for exploring targeted therapeutic strategies against ANXA4, which may offer novel treatment options for patients with AF.

Single-cell insights and immunometabolic reprogramming

Single-cell transcriptomics revealed that ANXA4 expression defines specific myeloid subsets exhibiting elevated inflammatory gene signatures, increased antigen presentation, and enhanced oxidative metabolism. Pseudotime trajectory analysis illustrated that ANXA4 upregulation may represent an early molecular event in the transition from homeostatic to proinflammatory states, accompanied by metabolic remodeling—particularly enhanced glycolysis and reactive oxygen species production—consistent with chronic cardiac inflammation profiles.

These observations provide mechanistic explanations for prior evidence linking mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress with AF pathogenesis [37, 38]. Our integrative analysis suggests that ANXA4 may act as an important node linking epigenetic remodeling, immune activation, and metabolic dysregulation in cardiac tissue.

The observation that TET2 expression is positively correlated with ANXA4 of ANXA4 demethylation adds a critical mechanistic layer to our understanding of epigenetic dysregulation in AF. TET2 catalyzes the conversion of 5-methylcytosine (5mC) to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC) and subsequent oxidized derivatives, ultimately leading to DNA demethylation and transcriptional activation. Our observation that TET2 expression is markedly elevated in ANXA4-high atrial cells suggests a feed-forward mechanism: inflammatory stimuli in the AF atrium may upregulate TET2, which in turn demethylates ANXA4 regulatory regions, amplifying ANXA4 expression and perpetuating immune activation.

Notably, TET2 mutations are common in clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential, a condition strongly associated with cardiovascular disease and inflammation [39]. Our findings raise the possibility that acquired or constitutional TET2 dysregulation may predispose to AF through ANXA4-mediated immune activation—a hypothesis warranting investigation in CHIP cohorts.

Furthermore, the convergence of demethylation, TET2 upregulation, myeloid infiltration, and MHC-I/MIF pathway activation across independent atrial tissue datasets is consistent with a multi-layered mechanistic network suggestive of potential mechanistic links. This epigenetic–immune–metabolic axis further supports the view that ANXA4 may function as a central integrator of arrhythmogenic remodeling processes.

Translational and clinical implications

First, the identified CpG methylation sites within the ANXA4 locus, validated in atrial tissue, may serve as chamber-specific candidate indicators for AF diagnosis and risk stratification. Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that log-transformed ANXA4 mRNA can serve as an independent risk factor, and that its methylation level can serve as an independent protective factor. Integration of ANXA4 methylation status with strongly predictive clinical parameters (NT-proBNP, LAD, LVEF) into machine-learning models achieved highly discriminative performance (AUC = 0.949–0.962). However, rather than serving as a ready-to-use clinical tool, these exploratory models primarily highlight that ANXA4 parameters can provide additive diagnostic value alongside classical clinical markers. Importantly, methylation-specific PCR assays targeting cg11942603 and cg22792910 can be performed on peripheral blood, enabling non-invasive monitoring.

Second, further investigation into the TET2-ANXA4 correlation could offer novel insights for therapeutic exploration. Future strategies might theoretically include: (a) TET2 inhibitors (e.g., Bobcat339) to prevent ANXA4 demethylation; (b) DNMT activators to restore repressive methylation marks [40]; (c) direct ANXA4 protein antagonists to block downstream immune signaling. Given that TET2 activity is modifiable by metabolic intermediates (e.g., α-ketoglutarate, succinate, fumarate), metabolic interventions targeting the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle may indirectly modulate ANXA4 expression [41].

Last, we utilized peripheral blood leukocytes as a non-invasive surrogate for atrial tissue because routine atrial biopsies are clinically unfeasible. Given our scRNA-seq findings that ANXA4 is predominantly expressed in myeloid cells, and since circulating monocytes infiltrate the myocardium to become pro-inflammatory macrophages during AF, ANXA4 upregulation in peripheral leukocytes effectively mirrors the systemic immune priming driving local atrial pathology. Methodologically, qPCR on total leukocytes (isolated via simple red blood cell lysis) was chosen as a highly sensitive tool to quantify intracellular mRNA changes. Although this isolation method is clinically straightforward, qPCR itself is time-consuming for large-scale screening. Because ANXA4 primarily acts intracellularly, future clinical translation will likely require rapid whole-blood flow cytometry.

Interpretation of genetic associations and mechanistic inference

A central finding is the Mendelian randomization–based evidence indicating a genetically predicted association between ANXA4 demethylation and AF risk, complemented by sc-eQTL-based inference suggesting that ANXA4 may influence peripheral blood monocyte abundance. These findings, together with single-cell transcriptomic profiling showing enriched MHC-I and MIF signaling in ANXA4-high myeloid cells, provide convergent evidence for an epigenetic-immune axis in AF pathogenesis.

However, it is important to acknowledge that our study does not include direct functional validation experiments demonstrating that ANXA4 demethylation or overexpression causally induces myeloid cell activation in vitro or in vivo. The causal inference is primarily derived from genetic and epigenetic epidemiological approaches (MR and mQTL-mediation) combined with observational single-cell transcriptomic associations. While MR provides genetically predicted associative evidence by leveraging germline genetic variation—thereby minimizing confounding and reverse causation—it does not replace experimental perturbation studies.Therefore, the current data do not definitively prove that ANXA4 methylation directly drives immune activation; rather, they highlight a robust genetic and transcriptomic association that warrants rigorous in vitro and in vivo functional testing.

The mechanistic link between ANXA4 upregulation and myeloid activation is supported by several lines of convergent evidence: Genetic evidence: MR suggests that genetically predicted ANXA4 levels are associated with increased AF risk and monocyte abundance in a manner consistent with a causal effect. Epigenetic mediation: mQTL-mediated MR indicates > 65% mediation through specific CpG sites. Disease-state correlation: AF vs. SR comparison shows coordinated immune activation. Dose-dependent relationships: ANXA4-high vs. low stratification within AF reveals graded immune/metabolic phenotypes. Trans-tissue dynamics: Pseudotime modeling shows ANXA4 expression peaks early in activation trajectories. Clinical concordance: Independent patient validation confirms hypomethylation and overexpression correlate with disease. Collectively, these findings highlight a potential role of ANXA4 in myeloid-associated AF pathogenesis, though direct causality requires further functional confirmation.

Limitations and future directions

While our atrial tissue-based analyses provide strong associative evidence and suggest potential causal relationships, direct experimental manipulation in atrial-specific models is still required: (a) Ex vivo human atrial tissue cultures: Fresh atrial appendage samples obtained during cardiac surgery could be treated with TET2 inhibitors or DNMT modulators, followed by assessment of ANXA4 methylation status, gene expression, and electrophysiological parameters. (b) Atrial-specific animal models: Tamoxifen-inducible, atrial cardiomyocyte-specific TET2 knockout mice or ANXA4 overexpression models subjected to rapid atrial pacing or angiotensin II infusion would directly test sufficiency. (c) Human iPSC-derived atrial cardiomyocytes: CRISPR/dCas9-TET2 fusion proteins targeted to ANXA4 CpG sites (cg11942603, cg22792910) would enable site-specific demethylation and functional assessment in a human genetic background. (d) Our study lacks direct functional validation. The observation that TET2 is co-expressed with ANXA4 only implies a correlation, not causation. Future experimental studies utilizing TET2 knockdown or ChIP-seq in atrial-specific models are essential to definitively determine whether TET2 acts as a direct upstream regulator of ANXA4 demethylation.

The trans-tissue trajectory analysis included 2 blood and 2 endocardial samples, which may limit detection of rare transitional cell states. Future studies with larger sample sizes and spatial transcriptomics would enhance resolution of anatomical immune cell niches. Another critical limitation concerns the pseudotime trajectory analyses used to evaluate the 'trans-tissue continuum'. It is important to emphasize that integrating in silico single-cell data from independent studies and heterogeneous sources cannot truly simulate the physical or temporal migration of immune cells. Analytical tools like Monocle are ideally suited for true longitudinal data. Because our incorporated datasets are inherently cross-sectional and derived from different time points and cohorts, the inferred trajectories represent a continuum of transcriptional states rather than definitive evidence of dynamic cellular migration. Consequently, these pseudotime findings must be interpreted with extreme caution as purely exploratory hypotheses. Future investigations utilizing spatial transcriptomics and true longitudinal tracking models (e.g., lineage tracing in animal models) are required to validly confirm the trans-tissue migration dynamics of ANXA4-associated immune cells.

Furthermore, several statistical limitations must be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size (N = 130) and data sparsity in certain clinical subgroups led to very wide confidence intervals in our logistic regression models (e.g., alcohol consumption) and mediation analyses. Consequently, the exact effect sizes of these variables should be interpreted cautiously. Second, while our machine learning models yielded exceptionally high AUC values (0.949–0.962) for AF prediction, these metrics likely overestimate the true predictive performance due to the inclusion of highly discriminative baseline clinical markers (such as LAD and NT-proBNP) and the potential for overfitting within a single-center cohort. The lack of an independent, external validation cohort limits our ability to confirm the generalizability of these models. Consequently, ANXA4 methylation should currently be viewed as an exploratory diagnostic candidate rather than an established clinical biomarker. Its true clinical robustness must be rigorously evaluated in future multi-center, large-scale prospective studies.

Conclusions

This study identifies a novel epigenetic mechanism in AF involving ANXA4 demethylation, which may contribute to myeloid immune activation and metabolic reprogramming. Our multi-omics approach provides evidence that hypomethylation of specific ANXA4 CpG sites is associated with increased gene expression and higher AF risk in a manner consistent with relationship.

The elevated ANXA4 levels correlate with heightened immune responses, particularly in myeloid cells, suggesting its potential value as a diagnostic candidate for distinguishing AF from sinus rhythm. Machine learning models further support the predictive value of ANXA4.

Additionally, we found that decreased levels of ANXA4 methylation are associated with upregulation of TET2. The exact functional consequence of this co-expression pattern on the inflammatory response in the myocardium requires further experimental investigation. This finding highlights the TET2-ANXA4 correlation as a potential area for future mechanistic exploration in AF.

In conclusion, our research advances the understanding of epigenetic factors in AF and provides a basis for developing innovative diagnostic and therapeutic approaches aimed at modulating gene expression to alleviate AF-related inflammation (Fig. 10).
