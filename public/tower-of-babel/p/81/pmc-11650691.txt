Assessment of nurses’ workplace silence behaviour motives: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Hanadi Mohammed Alhojairi, Naglaa Abdelaziz Mahmoud Elseesy, Sabah Mahmoud Mahran, Maram Ahmed Banakhar, Fatmah Alsharif
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe motives that lead to nurses’ workplace silence behaviour are essential to understand, as the failure to voice their concerns and maintain silence may cause various adverse outcomes, including burnout, turnover, job dissatisfaction, low productivity, and mental and moral distress. This study aimed to identify the level and influencing motives of the workplace silence behaviour among nurses at the National Guard Hospital in Madinah.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted in January and February 2023. The participants completed a questionnaire with two parts: the sociodemographic data form and the Workplace Silence Behavior Nursing Motives Scale. Data analysis was performed by descriptive statistics and the chi-square test.ResultsThe overall level for all workplace silence behavior motives is moderate (3.29 ± 0.69), with Avoidance Motives dimension receiving the highest scores (3.62 ± 0.75) and Organization-Oriented Motives dimension receiving the lowest (3.15 ± 0.86). Chi-square test showed that Saudi nationality (χ2 = 0.015, P = 0.019) and monthly income (χ2 = 0.045, P = 0.013) are factors in high-moderate levels of silence behaviour.ConclusionsThe overall level of workplace silence behaviour motives was found to be moderate, nurses have higher monthly salaries and non-Saudi nationals tend to have higher levels of workplace silence behaviour motives. The study results provide a reference for nursing managers to pay attention to nurses’ workplace silence behavior and take corresponding intervention measures.Keywords: Communication barriers, Nurses, Workplace silence, Interprofessional relations, Health personnel

Introduction

In contemporary healthcare management, there is widespread recognition that the primary determinant of organisational performance is human resources. They are crucial for the approval and sustainability of organizations within the competitive marketplace. The growing development of the healthcare industry forced administrators to need employees’ voluntary contributions, including their knowledge and suggestions to direct the decision making [1]. In the clinical nursing practice, the failure of nurses to voice their concerns and maintain silence can result in various negative outcomes, not only for patients but for themselves. These adverse consequences include greater intentions of leaving their current positions, decreased job satisfaction and performance, elevated emotional and moral distress, and increased burnout, which will ultimately weaken the ability of nurses to engage in innovative and creative practices [2,3]. Thus, to benefit from the diversity of nurses’ opinions, it is imperative to obtain a fuller understanding of the motives of silence [4].

Employee silence means when they withhold ideas, facts, and views deemed significant to their jobs and organizations because of various motives [5,6]. Pinder and Harlos [7] defined employee silence as the withholding of any form of genuine expression about the individual’s behavioural, cognitive, and/or affective evaluations of his or her organizational circumstances to persons who are perceived to be capable of effecting change or redress within the given context. At the same time, motive is also some reason offered as an explanation for the cause of an individual’s behaviours [8].

Currently, the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia is undergoing a major economic transformation that aligns with Vision 2030 and the Saudization programs. To do so, it is executing various initiatives across numerous sectors involving healthcare, including operational and structural modifications implemented to establish a comprehensive, effective, and interdependent health system that prioritizes optimal organizational performance and healthcare quality improvement [9]. Based on the 2021 statistical data published by the Saudi Arabia Ministry of Health (MOH), nurses comprise approximately 41.5% of the healthcare workforce [10]. Nurses are thus the predominant healthcare professionals in Saudi clinical settings, and their frontline role determines their extensive involvement in delivering patient care. Meanwhile, they have a significant impact on effecting changes and enhancing the work environment and healthcare services while ensuring the safety and well-being of patients. The other responsibility is an integral aspect of their professional duty as patient advocates and aligns with the nursing code of ethics [11]. There is also a concerted effort to achieve the magnet designation within the Saudi Arabian healthcare system. Attaining this recognition is a multifaceted and rigorous procedure that strongly emphasizes the empowerment of nurses across different levels of authority and requires the adoption of a structure to facilitate nurse-led decision-making [12]. It is necessary to acknowledge that—in spite of the significant value placed on the act of speaking up and reporting issues—numerous studies have provided evidence to suggest that many healthcare professionals, including nurses, frequently encounter obstacles that discourage them from expressing their concerns regarding patient care and nursing standards. Furthermore, even when they gather the courage to speak up, their concerns are often neglected or disregarded, which promotes a culture of employee silence within the healthcare industry [13,14].

Concerningly, the phenomenon of silence within the nursing profession can have detrimental effects on various aspects of nurses’ professional lives. For example, silence can significantly diminish nurses’ performance levels: when they choose to remain silent instead of voicing their concerns or suggestions, important issues may be unresolved, leading to suboptimal outcomes in patient care and negatively impacting nurses’ job satisfaction [15]. Moreover, silence among nurses can affect their civil behavior toward one another. By obstructing communication, silence makes it challenging for nurses to engage in respectful and beneficial interactions, which can, in turn, lead to a breakdown in teamwork and collaboration—both of which are crucial for providing high-quality patient care. This lack of open communication due to silence can decrease nurses’ job motivation. When nurses feel unable to express their ideas or contribute to decision-making processes, their sense of purpose and motivation may be diminished, detrimentally affecting their overall engagement and commitment to their profession.

In addition, nurses remaining silent in various situations can give rise to a range of interior conflicts within healthcare settings, hindering the progression of change and innovation while preventing the open exchange of ideas and the identification of potential areas for improvement [2]. The overall effectiveness and efficiency of healthcare systems may thus be compromised by silence. Silence motives can also contribute to increased stress levels among healthcare professionals. When nurses feel unable or unwilling to voice their concerns or suggestions, they may experience a sense of frustration and powerlessness. This can lead to a breakdown of morale within the nursing workforce, as nurses may feel undervalued or marginalized, affecting patient care quality. Given the many adverse consequences of silent behaviour motives, it is imperative to comprehensively examine silence in healthcare settings, particularly in nursing [[16], [17], [18]].

The tendency of nurses in Saudi Arabia to remain silent may be influenced by a complex interplay of cultural norms, organizational practices, research gaps, systemic issues, and economic factors. Addressing this issue comprehensively requires targeted research to identify specific barriers to develop appropriate corrective strategies. This study aimed to identify the level and influencing factors of the workplace silence behaviour motives among nurses and offer valuable insights to executives, directors, and managers by providing recommendations and implications that will help overcome the effect of nurses’ silence on their profession.

Methods

Study setting and participants

This study was conducted at the National Guard Hospital in Medina. The hospital is a non-profit, full-service facility with 348 beds and 478 registered nurses. The inclusion criterion held the status of a registered nurse working in the hospital, while the criteria for exclusion included nurses in managerial positions and nurses on vacation. The sample size for the study was calculated using the Epi Info program, a statistical software tool for epidemiology research created by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention based in Atlanta, Georgia. The degree of confidence was set at 95%, the power was 80%, and the margin of error was set at 5%. The minimum required sample size was calculated to be 214. The total population was determined to be 478 nurses, and a total sample of 327 nurses was obtained after considering non-respondents.

Study instruments

Sociodemographic data form

Sociodemographic data form was developed by the researcher, including gender, age, nationality, educational level, marital status, number of children, monthly income, type of residence and work years of nursing.

Workplace Silence Behavior Nursing Motives Scale

Aly et al.’s Workplace Silence Behavior Nursing Motives Scale (WSBNMS) was used to assess nurses’ workplace silence behavior motives [19]. Cronbach’s α coefficient for the entire scale is 0.84, with all items ranging from 0.83 to 0.94. The WSBNMS consisted of 36 items classified under six dimensions: Nurses’ Avoidance Motives (6 items), Nurses’ Belief Motives (5 items), Nurses’ Attitude Motives (3 items), Nurses’ Fear Motives (8 items), Nurses’ Management-Oriented Motives (6 items) and Nurses’ Organization-Oriented Motives (8 items). Each item was measured using a 5-point Likert scoring ranging from 1 (extremely disagree) to 5 (extremely agree), and then the total mean scores were categorized into three grades: mild (< 3), moderate (3 – 3.9) and high (≥ 4). The WSBNMS has been shown to perform strongly as a psychometric measure [19,20].

A panel of five faculty members from the Faculty of Nursing, King Abdulaziz University (KAU), four from the nursing administration specialty, and one from the psychiatry specialty validated the content of this study, assessing its content for accuracy, appropriateness, sentence clarity, item sequence, length, and overall appearance.

Before disseminating the final questionnaire, 10% of the total sample (n = 38) was included in a pilot study to examine the clarity, feasibility, and appropriateness of the data collection tools and estimate the required time for completing the questionnaires. All the questions and terminology were found to be straightforward. Finally, participants in the pilot study were excluded from the research sample. This was done to ensure the integrity of the research process, avoid biases associated with prior exposure, maintain objectivity in assessing the instrument’s performance, and ensure the generalizability of the findings.

According to the results of a test of the scale’s reliability, two subscale items, item 13 in Nurses’ Attitude Motives dimension and item 20 in Nurses’ Fear Motives dimension have an impact on the overall reliability of the scale, the remaining 34 items had a significant and positive correlation with the total degree of the scale, it ranged between (r = 0.929, P < 0.01) and (r = 0.449, P < 0.05). However, item 13 “Keep organizational confidentiality and secrecy” and item 20 “Events may grow bigger with involvement in argument situation” both negatively impacted the value of Cronbach’s α for the Nurses’ Attitude and Fear dimension; since the items had been excluded, the Cronbach’s α value for the Nurses’ Attitude Motives dimension improved from 0.56 to 0.70. Similarly, the Cronbach’s α value for the Nurses’ Fear Motives dimension improved from 0.66 to 0.88. Hence, it is necessary to reconsider or exclude both items from the study. We evaluated the scale’s reliability using Cronbach’s α and found a value of 0.94, indicating a high level of reliability.

Data collection

An electronic questionnaire was sent once by the chief nursing officer via email to the intended participants on behalf of the research team. Each participant independently completed the survey. Before data collection, participants were informed of the study’s goal, and they were provided with the researchers’ phone number and email address in case they had any questions or needed clarification. After completing the survey, a token of appreciation was provided in the form of a small gift to participating nurses. The data collection commenced on January 18, 2023, and concluded on February 14, 2023.

Statistical analysis

The sociodemographic data were presented using frequency and percentages, and the scores for Nurses’ Workplace Wilence Behavior Motives were presented by mean and standard deviation. The chi-square test was used to determine the relationship between the level of workplace silence behaviour motives and the sociodemographic data. A P-value of < 0.05 (two-sided) was used to indicate statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software version 26.

Ethical considerations

The nursing department ethics committee of the KAU in Jeddah granted approval for this study. Correspondingly, permission was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs (SP22M/140/08) and IRB Approval (IRB/2227/22). Permission was also granted from Dr. Nagah Abd El-Fattah Mohamed Aly, the developer of the WSBNMS.

The principle of voluntary participation in research ethics requires that people not be forced into participating in research, and participants have the right to withdraw from the study at any time. Thus, informed consent statements for cross-sectional surveys approved by MNGHA were attached as a picture in the electronic survey to ensure that participants understood their right to voluntary participation. After reading the attached consent form, the participant nurses had to choose between agreeing or disagreeing to participate in the study, thus signaling their informed consent.

Since this study addresses the phenomenon of silence at the level of nurses, which is considered especially controversial, several measures were undertaken to ensure that the rights of the participants were protected. The researcher explained that the participants’ confidentiality was protected as the researcher could only access the collected information. The data were obtained anonymously, no subjects’ personal identity information was known, such as their phone number or hospital/personal email. This was done to maximize the probability that the nurses would feel comfortable reporting their views about silent motives in the workplace.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

More than half of the nurses (56.6%) were between the ages of 31 and 40, with the majority of nurses (87.2%) being female and almost three-quarters (76.5%) being non-Saudis. Proximately two-thirds (61.8%) of participants were married; about 39.1% of nurses had no children; nurses earning 10,001 to 15,000 SAR per month constitute 42.3% of study participants. Concerning their educational background, almost two-thirds (62.1%) were bachelor’s degree holders. About 28.5% reported 11–15 years of nursing work experience, while 36.7% lived on a hospital campus (Table 1).

The overall level of the nurses’ workplace silence behavior motives is most often reported as moderate (54.4%) and somewhat less often reported as mild (31.5%), while the least common level was “high” (14.1%). Moderate levels of motives were the most common for all dimensions—nurses’ avoidance motives are most often moderate (44.6%), their belief motives also are (44.6%), as are their attitude motives (41.6%), their fear motives (41.3%), their management-oriented motives (46.2%), and their organization-oriented motives (42.8%). Notably, higher levels of silent behavior motives were observed for nurses’ avoidance motives (39.1%), attitude motives (31.8%), and nurses’ fear motives (30.3%). Management-oriented motives were least often reported as being at a high level (18.0%).

Factors influencing nurses’ workplace silence behaviour motives

The relationship between the level of workplace silence behaviour motives and the sociodemographic characteristics was examined using a chi-square test (Appendix A), which reveals that the level of each motive was significantly affected by non-Saudi nationality (χ2 = 0.015, P = 0.019) and monthly income (χ2 = 0.045, P = 0.013). Otherwise, no significant differences were observed between the workplace silence behavior motives by gender, age, educational level, marital status, number of children, type of residence, and work years of nursing.

Discussion

The nurses’ workplace silence behavior motives levels

More than half of the participants in this study had a moderate level of silent motives. This finding coincides with the results of Labrague et al. and Farghaly et al. [15,19] and is different from those of two Egypt-based studies by Abd et al. and Hassan et al. [20,21]. Çaylak et al. [22] found that nurses express a high rate of workplace silence, as nurses seek to avoid any conflicts with coworkers and superiors or being a part of a toxic workplace environment. They also found that prior negative experiences with speaking up, unfavorable feedback from management, and a lack of confidence in superiors are contributing factors [22]. In Jordan and Turkey, respectively, Alheet and Knoll et al. [23,24] found that nurses’ beliefs may be misinterpreted due to an absence of transparency among superiors, instances of oppressive supervision, and a tendency among superiors to suppress dissenting views.

The present investigation found that Nurses’ Avoidance Motives dimension has the highest mean. Notably, these findings did not align with the original scale study [19], which found that management-oriented motives were rated higher than other dimensions. However, Hassan & Ali [21] found comparatively higher means for motives associated with nurses’ attitudes.

The lowest average mean among all the dimensions was for Organization-Oriented Motives. This deviates from the findings of Parlar Kılıç et al. [25]. This Turkish study found that managerial and organizational reasons were critical factors affecting employee silence, turnover, and retention. Harmanci Seren et al. [11] found that healthcare employees in Turkey—physicians as well as nurses—remained silent mostly due to administrative and organizational reasons. The differences between the current study's results and those in the literature may be driven by many variables, such as the research context and socio-economic factors, as well as the measurement tools employed by the researchers. It is also plausible that the observed differences (e.g., organizational climate, workplace structures, institutional cultures, and operational policies and procedures) could be due to variations in the hospitals examined.

Regarding nurses’ avoidance behaviors, this study observed a tendency among nurses to avoid situations that may lead to conflict or negativity in the workplace. This aligns with the findings of Farghaly Abdelaliem and Abou Zeid [20], who revealed that nurses frequently choose to remain silent in various situations, primarily out of concern for potential harm to their interpersonal relationships with patients and colleagues, with such concerns constituting a significant factor influencing nurses’ decisions to withhold their opinions or concerns and the prevalence of silence within the healthcare setting. In Egypt-based research, Mahgoub et al. [26] emphasized the need to create a positive work environment that influences the level of job satisfaction, motivation, innovation, and engagement of nurses to improve their productivity, efficiency, and effectiveness in delivering high-quality patient care. In other words, fostering a sense of belonging, respect, and support is essential for promoting nurses’ well-being and job satisfaction. Zhang et al. [27] found that nurses in China who operate in unfavourable work environments tend to experience negative nurse outcomes. These outcomes may include a higher incidence of workplace violence, an elevated level of burnout, and an overall negative attitude toward work.

Notably, for the dimension of nurses’ avoidance motives, the lowest-scoring statement was, on average, “nurses avoid expressing ideas for self-protection,” which aligns with those of Manapragada and Bruk-Lee [28]. In a US-based investigation, they found that expressing one’s opinions or thoughts may impact how one is perceived by oneself or others, such that employees exhibit self-based safety silence due to potential adverse consequences, such as being viewed as bothersome or negative by their supervisors or colleagues. A lack of trust between employees and management, apprehension regarding potential negative repercussions for speaking up (e.g., social exclusion, ostracism, and disciplinary actions), employees’ perceptions of injustice and favouritism, and a reluctance to draw attention to oneself within the group and potentially challenge others’ cognitive superiority are the potential causal factors identified for this phenomenon. Relatedly, the breach of employees’ psychological safety may contribute to this issue, as noted by Jahanzeb et al. [29] Qi and Ramayah [30], in a study at a Chinese academic institution, confirmed these findings.

This type of silence is rooted in a sense of apprehension and may be classified as a form of defensive silence. According to Jahanzeb and Fatima [31], this form of silence has an adverse impact on the psychological well-being of employees while contributing to emotional exhaustion. Jahanzeb et al. [29] note that the practice of supervisor ostracism can also cause emotional exhaustion in employees, with their propensity to respond with defensive silence mediating this effect. Knoll et al. and Cho et al. [24,32] provided evidence supporting the existence of a relationship between quiescent silence and the well-being of employees, with this form of silence linked to discontentment, occupational turnover, and organizational performance.

Regarding the study results for nurses’ fear motives, the statement “seen as a mischief-maker person” had the highest mean, while “fear of not being accepted by others” had the lowest mean. The former statement could be construed as undermining nurses’ self-image—a significant concern for nurses (healthcare professionals who can enhance their patients’ self-esteem), as it may impact their self-concept and decision-making. The concept of “nursing self-image” refers to individual nurses’ professional qualities and behaviors that indicate their competence in the eyes of the general public and their colleagues. In a Croatia-based study, Fari et al. [33] demonstrated that nurses with a positive self-image are more likely to demonstrate greater confidence in their abilities. In contrast, nurses with a negative self-image may experience self-doubt and show reluctance to take risks. Moreover, they found that being labeled a troublemaker can harm an individual’s reputation and challenge their ability to garner credibility and reliance from peers and management.

This result may relate to the nurse’s desire for social acceptance and to fit in, as employees want to feel accepted and valued by their colleagues and supervisors. This finding is congruent with an Iranian study by Zamanzadeh et al. [34], as employees may feel more comfortable and confident in their work and feel a sense of belonging. This can reduce stress, increase job satisfaction, and increase motivation—eventually leading to better mental health, psychological safety, and well-being. In a Dutch study, Alingh et al. [35] identified a favorable relationship between team psychological safety and the propensity of nurses to voice their opinions. In other words, when nurses feel safer taking interpersonal risks, they will more frequently raise concerns about patient safety issues. Creating a friendly, welcoming, and conducive environment is essential to remove any barriers to speaking up.

For the dimension of Nurses’ Attitude Motives, the item “refuse disclosure of information that might harm the organization” obtained the highest mean in this study. This aligns with prior studies [15,36] suggesting that nurses tend to maintain their prosocial silence to protect the interests of their organization and colleagues.

The statement “nurses’ attitude is passive and taciturn” received the lowest score for the dimension of Nurses’ Attitude Motives. According to Howard and Holmes and Wang et al. [37,38], acquiescent silence is a behavior in which an individual displays apathy and disengagement from the organization by refraining from sharing any useful information or ideas. This behavior is characterized by a withdrawal from active participation and a passive attitude. This silence is seen as the least significant among the research participants. From a researcher’s standpoint, this might be attributed to nurses exhibiting a sense of organizational commitment. This concept can be defined as the degree to which employees are engaged in their organization and demonstrate a willingness to continue their involvement, ultimately leading to their active participation in the organization [39].

In this study, among all the items in Nurses’ Beliefs Motives dimension, the statement with the highest mean was “the nurse will be misunderstood, and nothing will change anyway”. This outcome may be linked with the discoveries of Abrams et al., Hassan and Ali, and Schwappach and Richard [21,40,41] indicating that healthcare professionals may believe that their speaking of concerns will have no effect, rendering it futile. Alternatively, they may perceive themselves as lacking the ability to influence the situation or experience a sense of powerlessness and defeat when attempting to voice their concerns due to the perception that their supervisors and upper management are uninterested in or dismissive of their perspectives. According to Knoll et al. [24], this results in social withdrawal, apathy, or reduced motivation due to a sense of futility and inefficacy.

Such results might be related to the phenomenon of social conformity. A study conducted in northern England by Fisher and Kiernan [42] highlighted how healthcare services have traditionally taken place within institutional settings emphasizing conformity and obedience as standard practices. Thus, it could be claimed that nurses may exhibit a proclivity toward social conformity, which refers to the inclination of individuals to conform to the norms, values, and behaviors of a particular group or society [43]. Furthermore, Nia et al. [44] find evidence that social conformity among employees has been found to have adverse effects, including but not limited to groupthink, suppression of individuality, a rise in the incidence of organizational absenteeism, decreased levels of organizational commitment, a decline in organizational citizenship behaviors, reduced job participation, a tendency toward obedient organizational silence, and a decrease in self-disclosure.

The item for management-oriented motives with the greatest mean score was “know the best for work and will make the right decision.” Accordingly, nurses in the targeted research setting exhibit considerable trust in their management and have confidence in their manager’s competence in making good decisions. This perception significantly influences the behavior of nurses and their desire to express any concerns they may have. It may either motivate them to voice their concerns or repress their desire to do so. The significance of trust between managers and employees in influencing employee behavior, performance, and engagement has been reinforced in prior research in South Korea and Iran [45,46].

Moreover, the behavior and attitudes exhibited by managers play a crucial role in the establishment and maintenance of a psychologically safe environment within an organization because they serve as role models for their subordinates. Their actions and attitudes can thus significantly influence the overall atmosphere and culture of the workplace. Alingh et al. and Hadi-Moghaddam et al. [35,47] highlight the significance of managers’ actions in fostering psychological safety by demonstrating honesty and integrity. When managers consistently display these traits, they set a positive example for their employees, emphasizing the importance of ethical conduct and trustworthiness. This, in turn, encourages employees to adopt similar behaviors, fostering an environment where individuals feel comfortable expressing their thoughts, ideas, and concerns without fear of judgment or reprisal.

The highest-scoring statements for organization-oriented motives were “most of my colleagues who speak out said they were often treated unfairly and said nothing was done” and “organization encourages strict hierarchical structure with a lack of open communication”. This finding could be related to nurses’ perceptions of organizational injustice in the selected hospital and is consistent with previous studies [48,49] that suggest an unfavorable relationship between organizational justice and organizational silence behavior among nurses. This implies that organizational justice plays a crucial role in shaping nurses’ tendency to remain silent in the workplace [16,41]. The promotion of silence among employees can be attributed to various organizational factors, such as a culture of injustice, inadequate organizational support, the absence of top management support, a climate that encourages silence, a hierarchical structure, an unfavorable leadership style, a lack of trust in supervisors, and communication opportunities.

Based on this study findings, the workplace’s organizational structure can be argued to impact nurses’ behavior significantly. This finding is consistent with those of Yalçn et al. [50]. Their Turkish study found that the tendency of nurses to refrain from speaking up is affected by the influence of hierarchical organizational structures on direct and vertical communication channels and that this occurrence has led to the adoption of lateral communication strategies. Individuals thus refrained from using lateral vocal expression due to the fear of damaging their interpersonal relationships and facing unfavorable consequences.

Likewise, the current study highlights the importance of open communication within the nursing profession. It has the potential to mitigate silence and enhance the quality of patient care. This statement aligns with the findings of Doo and Choi [51], who posit that enhancing organizational communication satisfaction may be a viable strategy for ameliorating the adverse consequences of organizational silence. Furthermore, Yalçın et al. [50] reveal that employee unwillingness to speak in organizational settings may be attributed to the prevailing organizational climate, particularly when it fails to promote transparent communication. Hence, facilitating the expression of nurses’ viewpoints is crucial in establishing a psychologically secure nursing workplace and a corporate atmosphere that fosters transparent communication.

Factors influencing the nurses’ workplace silence behavior motives

The study also revealed noteworthy associations between the nurses’ sociodemographic characteristics, such as nationality and financial income, and silent behavior. The findings indicate that non-Saudi nurses had a greater tendency toward silence than their Saudi colleagues. This occurrence could be elucidated by the fact that over three-quarters of the participant nurses in the selected setting are non-Saudi, predominantly hailing from Jordan, Egypt, Malaysia, and the Philippines. This might also explain the findings of nurses’ overall moderate level of silence, which can be attributed to the overrepresentation of non-Saudi nurses in the sample. This argument is substantiated by the 2021 report released by the MOH of Saudi Arabia, which declares that within the various governmental sectors, except for the MOH, the total number of nurses was 43,063. Yet, it is important to highlight that only 33% of these nurses were Saudi citizens [10]. Therefore, Saudi Arabia persists in its dependence on expat nurses to address the shortage in its nursing workforce.

Another explanatory factor could be the cultural differences among the nurses in the countries where this research was conducted. The relationship between culture and human behavior is inherently interconnected, with culture significantly impacting individuals’ perspectives, social norms, ethical principles, behavior, and communication [51,52]. Hofstede’s model provides a theoretical framework for understanding cultural differences between nations and further supports this view. This model includes six dimensions of national culture: power distance, individualism versus collectivism, masculinity versus femininity, uncertainty avoidance, long-term versus short-term, and indulgence versus restraint [53].

The power distance dimension—the degree to which individuals in a society who hold less power are willing to accept and anticipate an unequal distribution of power—is particularly noteworthy. The model developer employed a classification system wherein countries scoring below 50 were categorized as exhibiting a low-power distance culture, while those scoring above 50 were labeled as having a culture with a high-power distance. In societies with a high-power distance, individuals often resist raising issues or expressing their concerns to individuals in higher authority positions, reinforcing established hierarchies. Individuals frequently display a tendency toward conformity, passive acceptance, and silence to avoid conflicts [54,55]. Kumar and Budhwar [56], who aimed to assess cultural variations in Asian countries, found that such cultures exhibit a significant power distance, with subordinates tending to accept and anticipate a subservient dynamic with their superiors. According to Hofstede Insights, Asian nations with power distance index scores between 94 (the Philippines) and 100 (Malaysia) are regarded as having a high level of power distance, while Arab countries—Saudi Arabia, Jordan, and Egypt—have power distance index scores of 72, 70, and 80, respectively [57]. This aligns with the finding that East African and Arab cultures also frequently practice collectivism and higher power distance levels [58]. Such results are attributable to Saudi nurses potentially demonstrating more citizenship behavior than their foreign colleagues, which may foster a heightened level of responsibility and commitment among Saudi nurses and prompt them to surpass the expectations associated with their nursing role. Previous studies in Saudi Arabia support this finding by demonstrating that Saudi nurses display greater levels of organizational citizenship behavior than non-Saudi nurses [59,60]. Various other scholarly investigations have examined the inverse relationship between employees’ citizenship behavior and their inclination to remain silent, emphasizing its importance. For example, Manafzadeh et al. and Mashile [61,62] highlight the negative correlation between employees’ engagement in citizenship behavior and their tendency to withhold information.

Another noteworthy aspect to consider is the impact of job security on employee behavior, specifically their willingness to voice their opinions or remain silent. Per research conducted in Turkey, job security fosters a sense of belonging among an organization's members, resulting in a more relaxed work environment and better employee engagement [63]. Further, a recent investigation conducted by Oluwayemi et al. in Nigeria [64] concludes that job security is crucial in influencing employees' choices to express their opinions or maintain silence within their organizations.

The results may be explained by the fact that non-Saudi nurses can experience job insecurity, as non-Saudi nurses typically work on a contractual basis, which introduces the possibility of contract non-renewal. By contrast, Saudi nurses with tenured positions are less likely to have concerns about job loss. Non-Saudi nurses may thus exhibit a proclivity for maintaining silence to preserve their employment. This explanation aligns with the findings of Çaylak and Altuntaş [22]: The employment status of nurses and the absence of job security affect their behavior, as nurses in private hospitals who are employed contractually tend to exhibit more silence to safeguard their employment. Yang et al. [65] also find a notable impact of employment status on nurses’ tendency to remain silent—however, the identified effect is inverse of the previous findings. That is, they found that contract nurses and nurses on regular payroll actively expressed their opinions due to worries about the viability and security of their jobs and the desire for recognition and acknowledgment. The results of this study are consistent with those of Alsayed and West [66], who found, in a Saudi Arabian study, that foreign nurses expressed concern about the potential effects of Saudization initiatives—particularly those about the potential loss of employment and income.

The results support that nurses who receive a salary greater than average (> 10,001 SAR) tend to maintain silence. This finding may be elucidated by considering that a higher wage is often associated with more education and professional experience or higher-paying employment positions. Atalla et al. [67] also identified a positive correlation between nurses’ employment experience and elevated levels of silence among nurses. Similarly, Yang et al. [65] demonstrated that a greater degree of organizational silence was associated with higher levels of education and more prestigious job positions. Future research is recommended to compare larger sample sizes of nurses from the governmental and non-governmental healthcare sectors, which exhibit distinct organizational variations, and a qualitative or mixed-method methodology (e.g., interviews and focus groups). This is particularly important since silence is seen as a sensitive subject. Lastly, the phenomenon of nursing silence in relation to patients and organizational outcomes, such as sentinel events, mortality and morbidity rates, organizational commitment, and employee turnover, should be investigated.

Limitations

The limitations of this research must be acknowledged. First, the theoretical foundation suggests that large sample sizes are crucial for verifying the reliability and validity of any measurement tool in a new environment. In our study, although efforts were made to recruit a representative sample, the sample size could be too small for generalized conclusions from extensive psychometric testing. This constraint might impact the generalizability and robustness of our findings. Further, this hospital’s distinct culture, management procedures, and demographics may differ from those of other hospitals in Medina or Saudi Arabia. The results cannot be generalized to other Saudi hospitals as the study was conducted in one setting. Additionally, the research covered a sensitive topic, prompting nurses to respond cautiously, maybe with a social desirability bias. Finally, as the data were self-reported, they might be prone to response bias.

Conclusions

The findings of this research indicate that the levels of silent motives were generally moderate. Specifically, the motives related to nurses’ avoidance dimension were found to have the greatest mean score, while the organization-oriented motives had the lowest mean score. This research contributed to the identification of two sociodemographic characteristics that were shown to be associated with silence, such as a higher monthly wage and non-Saudi nationality.
