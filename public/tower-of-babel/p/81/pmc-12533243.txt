Functional personalized complex combination nano therapy for osteosarcoma
Authors: Orr Bar-Natan, Yuval Harris, Hagit Sason, Yosi Shamay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteosarcoma (OS) remains a challenging malignancy, particularly for metastatic cases, due to its heterogeneous genetic landscape characterized by lack of actionable oncogenic drivers and thus lack of personalized therapy. Though personalization of therapy based on functional assays using patient derived cells is emerging as a promising approach, it was not explored as a workflow for identifying patient specific drug cocktails. In this study we investigated a sequential drug screening approach for single and pair drugs which can be rationally used to identify 4 drug cocktails. We started with a systematic screening of 17 drugs from multiple classes across four osteosarcoma cell lines (U2OS, MG-63, SaOS-2, and K7M2), and observed differential drug responses among the cell lines. Interestingly, leading large language models (LLMs) failed to predict cell specific efficacy in OS cells while they were successful in KRAS mutation driven cells. Both SaOS-2 and K7M2 showed sensitivity to kinase inhibitors, particularly ponatinib, and we further explored their combinatorial therapeutic potential. We identified promising non-chemotherapeutic drug pairs, including trametinib-ponatinib and rapamycin-ganetespib and demonstrated potent synergistic effects. To mitigate dose limiting toxicities, drug pairs were formulated in polydopamine-stabilized nanoparticles and K7M2 murine models in vivo studies revealed that they preferably accumulated in tumors and were highly superior to the chemotherapeutic standard of care. Surprisingly, alternating administration of nanoparticle drug pairs was superior to concomitant regimen in both efficacy, survival and toxicity profiles. These findings strengthen a functional approach for combinatorial personalized treatment, potentially overcoming the limitations of current therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20222-3.Keywords: Osteosarcoma, Nanomedicine, Personalized medicine, Drug combinations, Large language models, PolydopamineSubject terms: Nanomedicine, Bone cancer

Introduction

Personalized cancer therapy has traditionally followed two distinct pathways: hypothesis-driven and data-driven approaches. Hypothesis-driven personalized medicine has demonstrated remarkable success in cancers with well-defined driver mutations, such as EGFR mutations in lung cancer1, HER2 amplification in breast cancer2, and BCR-ABL translocations in chronic myeloid leukemia3. In these cases, identifying a specific molecular alteration allows for targeted therapeutic intervention with predictable efficacy. However, many malignancies lack clear single-driver mutations and instead present complex genetic landscapes characterized by heterogeneity and redundant signaling pathways4. For these cancers, hypothesis-driven approaches often fall short, necessitating data-driven strategies that directly assess treatment responses rather than inferring them from genetic profiles5.

Osteosarcoma (OS) exemplifies this latter category. As the most common primary bone malignancy affecting children and young adults, OS represents the third most common cancer in adolescence with a peak incidence between ages 10–146,7. It typically affects long bones, such as the femur and tibia, demonstrating aggressive and rapid growth characteristics8. OS arises from sporadic mutations, primarily in tumor-suppressor genes including p53, RB, and BRCA1/2, rather than from consistent oncogenic drivers9. Diagnosis involves a multimodal approach including MRI, CT, PET, and biopsy10. While the 5-year survival rate for non-metastatic patients reaches approximately 70%, the remaining 30% experience relapse and metastasis, predominantly to the lungs. For these metastatic patients, the survival rate plummets to a mere 20%11.

Current standard treatments for OS include surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation. Surgical interventions aim to remove the entire tumor (limb salvage) or, when necessary, amputate the affected limb. Radiation therapy remains controversial due to its limited effectiveness and increased risk of complications12. Chemotherapy regimens typically incorporate doxorubicin, cisplatin, ifosfamide, and methotrexate13. Despite the introduction of these treatments, the prognosis for OS has remained largely unchanged over the past 4 decades14, highlighting the urgent need for innovative therapeutic approaches.

Since conventional chemotherapeutic agents present several limitations. They are often hydrophobic with poor bioavailability, distribute non-specifically throughout the body resulting in significant toxicity15,16, and follow standardized protocols rather than patient-specific regimens. These limitations highlight the need for personalized approaches tailored to individual patients’ tumors.

Functional personalized therapy has emerged as a promising data-driven alternative to traditional precision medicine for complex cancers like OS. Unlike genetic profiling-based approaches that infer treatment responses from mutational patterns, functional personalized therapy directly assesses drug responses through multiple experimental platforms17. These include ex vivo drug screening in both 2D and 3D cultures, patient-derived xenograft models, and innovative in vivo screening methodologies. This approach captures both genetic and non-genetic determinants of drug response, potentially leading to more accurate treatment selection for heterogeneous diseases like OS.

Kinase inhibitors have shown promise in treating sarcomas, especially in recurrent or unresectable cases where conventional chemotherapy proved ineffective. Tyrosine kinase receptors (TKRs) such as VEGFR, RET, and MET play crucial roles in the signaling pathways driving tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis. Multi-targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) like cabozantinib, regorafenib, and sorafenib have demonstrated clinical benefit by disrupting these pathways, resulting in reduced tumor progression and improved progression-free survival18. Clinical trials including CABONE19 and REGOBONE20 have reported partial responses and disease stabilization in patients with advanced sarcomas, indicating the potential of TKIs as valid therapeutic options when surgery and standard chemotherapy no longer present viable alternatives.

However, a significant challenge remains in identifying which patients will respond to specific kinase inhibitors. Recent clinical trials (NCT03919539, NCT04956198) have attempted to match patients with targeted therapies based on genomic profiling, but genetic markers alone often fail to accurately predict treatment responses. This limitation stems from the complex interplay between genetic and non-genetic factors influencing drug sensitivity, further emphasizing the need for functional approaches to treatment selection21.

While most functional personalized therapy research has focused on monotherapies, both research and clinical practice typically rely on treatment regimens combining multiple drugs. Indeed, treatments consisting of single agents are relatively rare (e.g., imatinib for CML and GIST22), with most cancer protocols incorporate two or three drugs, and some regimens utilize five or six agents (e.g., diffuse large B-cell lymphoma23 and multiple myeloma24 protocols). Beneficial drug combinations can act through various mechanisms, from targeting the same pathway vertically to affecting multiple targets across different cell types, resulting in additive or synergistic effects25.

The development of complex combinations involving four or more drugs represents a formidable challenge, often requiring years or decades of clinical optimizations. This challenge arises from the “combinatorial explosion” of possible drug combinations. For example, identifying an optimal 4-drug regimen from a pool of 30 candidates necessitates testing 31,000 different possibilities, including all possible pairs, triplets, and quadruple combinations. When attempting to assemble these combinations in a personalized manner, the complexity increases exponentially, making traditional clinical trial approaches impractical.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning techniques offer potential solutions to navigate this vast combinatorial space more efficiently. These computational approaches can analyze extensive datasets of drug interactions, molecular structures, and clinical outcomes to predict effective combinations while substantially reducing the number of experimental combinations requiring lab tests. AI algorithms can identify promising drug pairs based on complementary mechanisms of action, target pathway interactions, and historical efficacy data. Machine learning models may also optimize dosing ratios and administration timing, potentially accelerating personalized treatment protocol development. However, despite these advantages, significant challenges persist in translating AI-predicted combinations to clinical success26.

An additional setback is that many promising drug combinations fail clinical trials due to toxicity issues and insufficient therapeutic windows, challenges that nanomedicine approaches may help overcome. Polydopamine (PDA) has gained widespread use in biomedical research owing to its exceptional biocompatibility and strong binding affinity to various materials. Its applications span drug delivery, imaging, and tissue engineering. Research by Niezni et al. identified 1,1,2-Trimethyl-3-(4-sulfobutyl)benz[e]indolium (In820) as an effective comonomer for catecholamines, demonstrating that poly(dopamine)-poly(L-dopa)-co-In820 (PDA-PDO-In820 1:1:1) provides excellent stabilization for nanoparticles27. While some drugs do not readily form stable nanoparticles with PDA copolymer independently, stable formulations can be achieved through drug pairs exhibiting chemical synergy. When chemical synergy complements biological synergy, a “meta-synergy” effect emerges -a synergy of synergies- where combined effects yield significant therapeutic improvements28.

In this study, we focused on developing and evaluating complex combinations of tyrosine kinase receptor inhibitors for osteosarcoma treatment that extend beyond the predictive capabilities of current AI-based approaches. We formulated multiple therapeutic agents-including rapamycin, ponatinib, trametinib, and ganetespib- into polydopamine-stabilized nanoparticles. This multi-drug approach aims to simultaneously target diverse oncogenic pathways, thereby enhancing anti-tumor activity while minimizing resistance development. Our strategy demonstrates the potential of intricate combination therapies in personalized medicine to maximize efficacy while reducing drug resistance and adverse effects.

By bridging the gap between hypothesis-driven and data-driven approaches, our research provides a framework for developing complex combination nanotherapies for osteosarcoma, a disease where traditional personalized medicine approaches have proven insufficient. This work represents a step toward functional personalized medicine that considers the full complexity of cancer biology rather than reducing it to individual genetic alterations, potentially offering new hope for patients with limited therapeutic options.

Methods

Materials and reagents

All non-drug chemicals were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was purchased from Carlo Erba (Emmendingen, Germany). Drugs were purchased from LC-Laboratories (Woburn, MA) and MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ).

Polymerization process

Preparation of polydopamine and further nanoparticles was done as published by Niezni et al.27. Briefly, Dopamine, L-dopa and 1,1,2-Trimethyl-3-(4-sulfobutyl)benz[e]indolium, inner salt (In820) (6 mg each, Sigma-Aldrich) were dissolved in 3 mL of sodium bicarbonate buffer (0.1 M), with a final concentration of each monomer 2 mg/mL. The copolymers were shaken at 800 rpm for 4 h at room temperature and followed by dark storage for 7 days to permit aging before application.

Preparation of NPs

100 μl of drug or 200 μl of drug combination dissolved in DMSO (10 or 30 mg/ml) were added to a premade solution of 500 μl of sodium bicarbonate buffer (0.1 M) and 100 μl of a polymer solution (2 mg/ml). For some, more difficult formulations, the drugs were added to a mix of 400 μl bicarbonate buffer with 200 μl of the polymers. The NPs were cleaned by centrifugation (30,000 rpm, 15 min) and resuspended in 1 ml of DDW. The solution was then sonicated using Sonics’ Vibra-cell ultrasonic processor (20% amplitude, 5 s pulses) until the solution is homogenous.

Characterization of NPs

Size

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was used to measure size and size distribution. (Nano-ZS, Malvern Instruments, UK). The samples were prepared by diluting the NPs solution, 100 μl of the NPs solution is added to a cuvette with 1 ml of DDW. The size and distribution were calculated based on the intensity of laser light scattering by the particles (using the Stokes–Einstein relationship). The final size reported is an average of 3 independent measurements and the distribution and uniformity of the results is based on the polydispersity index (PDI).

Drug content

A waters acquity arc UHPLC system equipped with a PDA/UV detector was used for conducting the analysis. A CORTECS C18 (4.6 × 50 mm 2.7 μm) Column was used. The mobile phase system consisted of two solvents- A (H2O/acetonitrile/Trifluoroacetic acid, 95/4.9/0.1, v/v/v) and B (acetonitrile/H2O/Trifluoroacetic acid, 95/4.9/0.1, v/v/v), with the gradient varied according to the following program: 0 min (10% B), 3.5 min (95% B), 4.25 min (10% B). The analyses were performed at a flow rate of 0.4 ml/min, an injection volume of 132 μl, and a column temperature of 40 °C. UV spectra were collected from the PDA/UV detector with a wavelength range of 210–400 nm. The sample preparation was as follows: 50 μl from the particles’ solution were added to 450 μl mix of acetonitrile and ethanol (1:1). Calibration curves were created for each drug to calculate the total drug load in the particles, whether in combination or alone.

Cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryoTEM)

10 μl from the NPs solution is applied on carbon-coated TEM grid, excess solution is removed by Whatman paper and then plunged to a nitrogen-cooled liquid ethane. This rapid cooling (105 °C*S-1) prevents the formation of ice crystals on the grid. The samples are kept in liquid nitrogen until imaging. Samples’ imaging is carried out under Talos (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) transmission electron microscope (Technion center for electron microscopy of soft matter) and analyzed by ImageJ software (National Institute of Health, USA).

Cell cultures

The MG-63, U2OS, and SaOS-2 cell lines were kindly donated by the David Meiri laboratory. The K7M2 cell line was kindly donated by the Yuval Shaked laboratory. All cell lines are commercially available in ATCC. The MG-63 and U2OS cell line cultivated in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) containing 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), 1% Penicillin–Streptomycin (P/S) and 1% L-glutamine. The SaOS-2 and K7M2 cell lines cultivated in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% FBS, 1% P/S and 1% L-glutamine. Cells were cultured in 10 cm plates (Nunc, USA) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C, using a standard mixture of 95% air and 5% CO2. For passaging, cells were detached with trypsin/EDTA and subsequently replated.

Cell viability assays in 2D

5000 Cells were seeded in 96 well plates at a 30% confluency and allowed to adhere for 24 h. All drugs were dissolved in DMSO. Nanoparticles were suspended in DDW and were added to the wells after dilution with growth medium. Untreated cells (control) were used to establish 100% viability.

MTT viability assay

Cells were incubated for 4 h with 30 μl of the reagent (Glentham Life Sciences), followed by addition of 200 μl DMSO before measuring the fluorescence at 570 nm using plate reader (Synergy H1 microplate reader, BioTek).

Cell titer glow (CTG) viability assay

Cells were incubated with the reagent (Promega, USA) in 1:1 ratio in culture medium for 2 min in an orbital shaker to encourage cell lysis, the luminescence is recorded using plate reader (Synergy H1 microplate reader, BioTek).

Cell viability in 3D (tumor spheroids)

700 cells of K7M2 were seeded in round bottom ultra-low attachment plates (ULA, Corning, NY) and were incubated for 96 h for maturation. Drugs or nanoparticles were added in the appropriate dilution (in growth medium) and incubated for 72 h. The viability of the spheroids was quantified by Promega® CellTiter-Glo® 3D kit, according to the manufacture instructions. Spheroids size was monitored with LionHeart automated microscopy.

Synergy score calculations

The synergy between two drugs was tested using a concentration matrix in a 96well plate, both in 2D and in 3D. The more effective drug is diluted from left to right and the less effective drug is from top to bottom. The first concentration is 0.1 mg/ml and is diluted accordingly. The Synergy scores were calculated using the SynergyFinder tool (https://synergyfinder.fimm.fi/synergy/). The model quantifies the degree of synergy: ZIP the expected effect of two drugs assuming they do not affect each other. Scores results > 10 indicate the interaction between the two drugs are most likely synergistic, for results between -10 and 10 the interaction is most likely additive. Results < -10 the interaction is most likely antagonistic.

Animal ethics

All in vivo experiments were performed under protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Research Ethical Committee of the Technion—Israel Institute of Technology (ethics number IL0740620) and conducted in compliance with ARRIVE guidelines. All procedures were carried out in accordance with the relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations. All mice were housed in laminar-flow cabinets with filtered air, maintained under a 12 h light/dark cycle, with unrestricted access to food and water.

Polydopamine safety and toxicity study

Six-week-old female Balb/C mice (Envigo RMS) were randomly divided into four groups. Animals received intraperitoneal (I.P.) injections of trametinib–ponatinib–polydopamine or rapamycin–ponatinib–polydopamine nanoparticles (10 mg/kg), administered twice weekly. Body weight was recorded three times per week to monitor systemic tolerance.

Osteosarcoma xenograft experiments (K7M2 model)

Single-combination study

Six-week-old female Hsd:Athymic Nude-Foxn1nu mice (Envigo RMS) were injected subcutaneously with 1 × 106 K7M2 murine osteosarcoma cells suspended in 100μL of Matrigel (Corning, 1:1 ratio). Following engraftment, mice were assigned randomly into four groups (n = 10 tumors per group). Treatments were administered I.P. two to three times per week and included: trametinib–ponatinib (10–30 mg/kg), rapamycin–ganetespib (10–30 mg/kg), ponatinib (30 mg/kg), or rapamycin (10 mg/kg) formulated in polydopamine nanoparticles.

Complex-combination study

For combination therapy evaluation, the same xenograft procedure was used with 6-week-old female Hsd:Athymic Nude-Foxn1nu mice. The mice were randomized into four groups (n = 6 tumors per group) were treated I.P. with either (i) trametinib–ponatinib (10–30 mg/kg) plus rapamycin–ganetespib (10–30 mg/kg) nanoparticles, or (ii) a triple-chemotherapy regimen consisting of doxorubicin (5 mg/kg), cisplatin (3 mg/kg), and pemetrexed (10 mg/kg). Treatments were administered two to three times weekly. Tumor dimensions were measured with a digital caliper, and volumes calculated as (length × width2 × π/6). The maximum tumor burden allowed was 1.5 cm3. Mice were anesthetized with 5% isoflurane in oxygen and euthanized by CO₂ inhalation (12.5%), in accordance with institutional guidelines.

Histopathological evaluation

At study endpoint, major organs (liver, heart, kidneys, lungs, spleen) and tumors were harvested, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 24 h, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed. A board-certified veterinary pathologist, blinded to treatment allocation, systematically assessed five sections per organ per group for pathological alterations including hepatocyte degeneration, myocardial inflammation, renal glomerular/tubular changes, pulmonary congestion, and tumor necrosis. Tumor efficacy was assessed by architectural disruption and quantification of necrotic regions.

Nanoparticle biodistribution

Six-week-old female Hsd:Athymic Nude-Foxn1nu mice (Envigo RMS) were engrafted with K7M2 tumors as described above (n = 9 per group). At 24 h post-I.P. injection of 200μL (10 mg/mL) trametinib–ponatinib–polydopamine or rapamycin–ponatinib–polydopamine nanoparticles, organs (liver, kidney, lungs, heart, spleen, and tumors) were collected and imaged using an IVIS system (Xenogen Corp.). Fluorescence signals were captured at λex = 745 nm and λem = 840 nm, and radiance efficiency (photons·sec−1 cm−2) was quantified in tumor regions of interest using LivingImage V4.2 software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses for both in vitro and in vivo studies were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10). Group comparisons between treated and control samples were assessed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-tests, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. Each experimental condition was tested with at least two biological replicates to ensure reliable statistical interpretation. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (s.e.m.), with corresponding P values indicated in the main text and figure legends. All in vitro experiments were conducted with technical duplicates and repeated with at least one independent biological replicate, resulting in N ≥ 4 data points per condition. Survival outcomes in xenograft models were evaluated using the Mantel–Cox log-rank test. For animal studies, group sizes were n ≥ 3 mice per treatment arm, determined based on precedent and laboratory experience. Mice were randomly assigned to groups, and blinding was not employed during treatment or analysis. For histological evaluation, representative images were selected from at least five microscopic fields per tissue, and staining was duplicated with embedding performed for a minimum of two out of five tissue samples per group.

LLM predictions and evaluation

We evaluated the predictive performance of state-of-the-art large language models (LLMs) in classifying drug potency in osteosarcoma and KRAS-driven cancer cell lines29. The models tested included OpenAI’s GPT-4o and GPT-3.5-turbo (o3), Anthropic Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Google Gemini 1.5 Pro. All prompts were submitted via publicly accessible interfaces (chat.openai.com, claude.ai, and gemini.google.com), with temperature set to 0.1 to minimize output randomness. As direct seed control was not available through these interfaces, low temperature settings were used to ensure the most deterministic behavior possible.

Each prompt was submitted three times per model, with independent sessions used to capture stochastic variability. Responses were compiled and scored based on their ability to correctly classify drug potency across four osteosarcoma cell lines using a predefined four-tier scale (1–4). For each model, mean precision, recall, and F1-score were calculated. We also computed standard deviations and statistical significance for performance comparisons using Student’s t-test. All predictions used a zero-shot format, meaning no prior examples or reference classifications were provided within the prompt. results based on n = 12 total replicates (4 models × 3 submissions each).

The prompt used in this task was “i want you to try to predict and rank these drugs for potency in cell viability assay in osteosarcom cel line MG63, u2os, k7m2 and saos: Ponatinib Nintedanib Infigratinib Avaprtinib Saracatinib Lenvatinib Nilotinib Sorafenib paclitaxel rapamycin carfilzomib gemcitabine cabozantinib, trametinib, palbociclib, olaparib, vemurafenib. try classify to 1–4 classes of potency 4 is most potent and 1 is least potent and virtually like non treated control. don’t provide explanation, be succinct and concise. make a table where on the column its the drugs and the cell lines on the top and you put the numbers in the cells in the table”.

Results

An overview of the experimental strategy is illustrated in Fig. 1, which outlines our stepwise approach for developing functional personalized nanotherapy for osteosarcoma. The workflow includes in vitro drug screening on patient-derived osteosarcoma cells, synergy-based drug pair selection, polydopamine nanoparticle formulation, and in vivo evaluation of tumor targeting, therapeutic efficacy, and toxicity under different dosing regimens.

Schematic overview of the functional personalized nanotherapy workflow. Patient-derived osteosarcoma cells undergo high-throughput screening to assess drug responses in both 2D and 3D cultures. Potent drug pairs are identified based on synergy scoring and co-formulated into polydopamine-stabilized nanoparticles. These formulations are tested in murine xenograft models to evaluate tumor targeting, therapeutic efficacy, and toxicity. An alternating administration strategy is compared to concomitant dosing and standard-of-care chemotherapy to determine the optimal therapeutic approach.

Our study investigated functional personalized combinatorial therapy for osteosarcoma through comparative drug screening across multiple cell lines. To establish the foundation for personalized medicine approaches, we conducted systematic comparisons of drug responses in three human osteosarcoma cell lines (U2OS, MG-63, and SaOS-2) and one murine cell line (K7M2) using 2D cell viability assays. We evaluated a diverse panel of 17 drugs, encompassing both traditional chemotherapeutic agents and targeted kinase inhibitors, revealing distinct sensitivity patterns across the cell lines (Fig. 2). Using U2OS, the most studied OS cell line, as our reference cell line, we observed that chemotherapeutic agents dominated the high-efficacy responses, with paclitaxel and carfilzomib showing exceptional cytotoxicity, followed by gemcitabine. Among kinase inhibitors, only trametinib demonstrated notable effectiveness in U2OS cells (Fig. 2a). MG-63 cells exhibited a slightly broader response profile, maintaining sensitivity to paclitaxel and carfilzomib, but were much more resistant to gemcitabine than other cell lines. MG-63 also showed significant responses to targeted therapies, particularly palbociclib (CDK 4/6 inhibitor) and rapamycin (mTOR inhibitor) (Fig. 2b). In contrast, SaOS-2 cells displayed a very distinctive preference for kinase inhibitors, with ponatinib, nintedanib, and trametinib emerging as the most effective agents, suggesting a unique dependence on kinase-mediated pathways (Fig. 2c). The murine K7M2 cell line showed remarkable similarity to SaOS-2 in its enhanced sensitivity to kinase inhibitors, indicating potential conserved pathway dependencies across species (Fig. 2d). Notably, while the cell lines showed considerable heterogeneity in their responses to kinase inhibitors, the consistent effectiveness of carfilzomib and paclitaxel across all cell lines highlighted these agents as potential backbone therapeutics for combination strategies. These differential response patterns underscore the heterogeneous nature of osteosarcoma and validate the need for personalized therapeutic approaches. The varying sensitivities to kinase inhibitors suggest that molecular profiling could guide the selection of optimal drug combinations for individual tumors.

As the results of initial drug response were highly heterogenic between cell lines, we sought to compare it with current accumulated published knowledge of differential drug responses in osteosarcoma. We conducted an automated literature analysis using the SPIKE engine for extractive search, to examine drug-cell line frequency and relationships at sentence level30. Our analysis revealed significant disparities in research coverage across different cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S1a). While most drugs in our screen were frequently mentioned in publications with ‘osteosarcoma’ and well-studied cell lines like U2OS and MG63, there was notably less literature coverage for SaOS-2 and K7M2 cell lines. In fact, most of the effective drugs tested in our experiments were never published with these cells, emphasizing the novelty and unpredictability of the results.

We then asked whether this gap in knowledge could have been aided with the use of large language models (LLMs). Recent studies showed that LLMs can provide reasonable and useful treatment recommendations for medical conditions at least at human level and even better at some tasks31. To investigate how well can LLMs predict differential drug responses in osteosarcoma, we systematically evaluated the performance of leading LLMs (GPT-o3, GPT-4o, DeepSeek V3, Claude 4 Sonnet) across both osteosarcoma cell lines, U2OS, MG63, K7M2, SaOS-2 and KRAS-driven cancer cell lines, HCT116, KPL, CT26, as a control for cancer cells with an actionable mutation. We ranked the drug outcome in 4 categories of potency:1-no effect (> 0.01 mg/ml), 2 mild effect (0.001 mg/ml) 3 (0.0001 mg/ml) and 4- highly potent (0.00001 mg/ml). Each model was queried three times independently with the same prompt (see methods) to predict the 1–4 potency of each drug for each cell, and predictions were analyzed both individually and through majority vote consensus across all 12 predictions per drug-cell line combination (4 models × 3 repetitions). The results revealed striking differences in LLM prediction accuracy across models and across cancer types and drug classes, as can be seen in both aggregated majority vote analysis (Fig. 3) and for individual LLM models (Supplementary Fig. S1b). For chemotherapeutic agents (carfilzomib, paclitaxel, gemcitabine), LLMs demonstrated reasonable accuracy in both osteosarcoma cell lines (Fig. 3a) and KRAS-driven cancers and (Fig. 3b), likely leveraging well-established knowledge about cytotoxic mechanisms. In KRAS-driven cancers, LLMs also accurately predicted responses to kinase inhibitors, suggesting sufficient training data exists for these well-characterized systems (Supplementary Tables S1–S2). potential for computational approaches in guiding personalized therapy selection. However, LLM performance collapsed when predicting kinase inhibitor responses in osteosarcoma, particularly for cell line-specific sensitivities. The models completely failed to anticipate the distinctive high sensitivity of K7M2 and SaOS-2 cells to ponatinib, nintedanib, and olaparib—patterns clearly evident in our experimental data but absent from computational predictions. Quantitative analysis of individual models revealed consistently poor performance across all LLMs for osteosarcoma kinase inhibitors, with F1-scores ranging from 12.5 to 37.5% (Supplementary Tables S3–S4).

To understand mechanistically what the potential therapeutic targets for SaOS-2 and K7M2 are, we reviewed the literature and identified key commonalities and differences among various kinase inhibitors (Fig. 4a). We determined the efficacy of the drugs on specific targets using IC50 data obtained from Selleckchem.com. Ponatinib and nintedanib were found to share overlapping targets, including FGFRs, FLT3, and SRC, highlighting their broad-spectrum activity against signaling pathways critical for osteosarcoma growth and progression. In contrast, midostaurin specifically targeted FLT3 and SRC but lacked activity against FGFRs or PDGFRs, indicating a narrower but potentially synergistic range of inhibition. These observations underscore the importance of combining inhibitors with complementary target profiles to enhance efficacy and address the heterogeneity of tumor signaling networks. Moreover, targeting pathways like FGFR and SRC presents an opportunity for tailored therapies, particularly in osteosarcoma subtypes with upregulated expression of these receptors.

To investigate the mechanistic basis of ponatinib’s exceptional efficacy, we explored whether its potency stems from simultaneous inhibition of multiple targets or from unique binding characteristics. Given that ponatinib inhibits numerous cellular pathways—some overlapping with targets of other kinase inhibitors—we systematically tested various drug combinations designed to recapitulate ponatinib’s multi-kinase inhibition profile in 2D cultures of K7M2 cells (Fig. 4b).

We evaluated combinations of crenolanib with sorafenib, infigratinib, or both, as well as combinations of lenvatinib with midostaurin. Despite targeting similar kinase networks collectively, none of these combinations approached ponatinib’s remarkable cytotoxicity. The consistently superior efficacy of ponatinib (p < 0.001) compared to all tested combinations suggests that its potency may derive from unique binding characteristics, conformational effects on target kinases, or inhibition of undocumented targets rather than simply from its multi-target profile.

Given the high sensitivity of K7M2 cells to kinase inhibitors, we decided to expand this exploration with additional drugs (Fig. 5a) and identified ganetespib, as well as ponatinib from the previous screen as the most effective drugs. However, their structural properties drugs prevent efficient nanoparticle encapsulation, limiting their compatibility with this delivery method. To overcome this issue, we screened combinations of kinase inhibitors and discovered several promising pairings (Fig. 5b), including the potent combinations of trametinib with ponatinib and rapamycin with ganetespib.

We performed similar LLM analysis for the additional drugs and the drug combinations tested in Fig. 4 (Supplementary Fig. S2) and found that GPT-4 and DeepSeek achieved only 52.2% accuracy—essentially no better than random chance for many combinations (Supplementary Table S5).

To better mimic in vivo tumor conditions, we transitioned to 3D spheroid models, which more accurately recapitulate tumor architecture, oxygen gradients, and drug penetration barriers characteristic of solid tumors. Since K7M2 cells initially demonstrated suboptimal spheroid formation capability, we implemented a systematic enrichment protocol to develop spheroid-forming subclones.

Our spheroid enrichment methodology (Fig. 6a) involved a three-cycle selection process. We began by seeding K7M2 cells in flat-bottom ultra-low attachment (ULA) plates, followed by microscopic identification and selective harvesting of wells demonstrating superior spheroid formation. These selected spheroids were expanded in 24-well plates, with the process repeated three times to progressively enrich cells with enhanced spheroid-forming capacity. The micrographs clearly demonstrate the morphological improvement—from dispersed, loosely aggregated cells before selection to compact, well-defined spheroids after selection.

We tracked spheroid size dynamics over 5 days following treatment with various agents (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. S3). The control spheroids exhibited continuous growth, reaching approximately 550 μm in diameter by day 4. In contrast, trametinib and trametinib + ponatinib combinations demonstrated significant growth inhibition, reducing spheroid diameter to approximately 300 μm. Interestingly, while ponatinib alone showed moderate growth inhibition, rapamycin and rapamycin + ponatinib combinations exhibited intermediate efficacy. Carfilzomib, despite its potency in 2D models, showed diminished effectiveness in the 3D environment.

The comparative efficacy profiles (Fig. 6c, d) reveal that combinations containing ponatinib or ganetespib exhibited exceptional potency in 3D models, achieving nearly complete growth inhibition at concentrations above 10^-4 mg/ml. This suggests ganetespib maintains excellent penetration capabilities and target engagement within the complex 3D tumor architecture.

We conducted comprehensive synergy analysis using concentration matrices for each drug combination, presented as 3D response surfaces (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. S4). Each synergy landscape reveals distinctive interaction patterns that are highly concentration-dependent. The rapamycin + ganetespib synergy map exhibits a prominent peak at intermediate concentrations (approximately 1–10 ng/ml of ganetespib and 100–1000 ng/ml of rapamycin), with synergy scores exceeding 30, indicating strong synergistic interaction at non-equimolar ratios. In contrast, the rapamycin + ponatinib synergy landscape shows more modest synergy centered at higher concentrations of both drugs. The rapamycin + trametinib synergy map demonstrates a broad plateau of synergy across higher concentrations of rapamycin combined with intermediate trametinib concentrations. The ponatinib + ganetespib combination shows its highest synergy region at mid-to-high concentrations of ponatinib paired with lower concentrations of ganetespib. Notably, all four combinations exhibit diminished synergy or even antagonism at 1:1 concentration ratios, highlighting the critical importance of optimal dosing ratios for maximizing therapeutic benefit.

Maximum synergy scores across all tested combinations (Fig. 6f) showed that rapamycin + ponatinib demonstrated the highest synergy score (~ 12), followed by rapamycin + trametinib (~ 8) and rapamycin + ganetespib (~ 8), while trametinib + ponatinib, ganetespib + trametinib and ganetespib + doxorubicin showed moderate synergy scores of ~ 6 and ~ 4, respectively. Even the least synergistic combination, ganetespib + ponatinib, maintained a positive synergy score (~ 2), indicating that all tested combinations achieved at least additive effects.

After working with the spheroids, we expanded our research to using nanoparticles stabilized with co-polymers of polydopamine, L-Dopa and In-820 which will further be referenced to as PDA-820 NPs following a previously published method by Niezni et al. We successfully encapsulated the drug combinations into nanoparticles. We characterized the nanoparticles using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and found that all formulations were stable with sizes under 150 nm and a PDI lower than 0.2 (Fig. 7a). We also tested the encapsulation efficiency of the NPs using HPLC and saw high drug loading for each drug in the combinations (Figs. 7b and S5 and Table S6). The zeta potential measurements demonstrated that all formulations were either neutral or negatively charged. The three nanoparticle formulations containing ponatinib were neutral probably due to its positive charge neutralizing the negative charge of PDA copolymer, supporting their potential for stable dispersion and free circulation in the bloodstream (Fig. 7c). We imaged the nanoparticles using cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryoTEM) (Fig. 7d). We then tested the efficacy of the nanoparticles in vitro, both in 2D and 3D models (Fig. 7e,f).

Based on our earlier findings, we selected treatment combinations that demonstrated high efficacy, low toxicity, and strong synergy scores. Due to the limited efficacy of rapamycin + ponatinib at lower doses, we proceeded with trametinib + ponatinib and rapamycin + ganetespib combinations in PDA-820 NPs for further evaluation. We investigated two complex combination administration strategies—concomitant (simultaneous) versus alternating dosing schedules—comparing both to a common standard-of-care (SOC) chemotherapy which includes cisplatin, doxorubicin and pemetrexed. Initial in vitro testing in 2D cell cultures revealed significantly higher efficacy for both combination strategies compared to SOC (Fig. 9a, p < 0.001 by unpaired t-test), with concomitant administration showing slightly better cytotoxicity at lower concentrations. This pattern was similarly observed in 3D spheroid models (Fig. 9b), confirming that the improved efficacy persisted in more physiologically relevant tumor environments. We further assessed anti-tumor activity by tracking spheroid diameter changes over time after treatment with 1 × 10−5 mg/ml of each regimen. While control and SOC-treated spheroids continued growing throughout the observation period, both combination approaches effectively inhibited spheroid growth, with the concomitant regimen achieving the greatest size reduction by day 5 (Fig. 9c). Representative microscopy images documented these size differences for 96 h post-treatment (Fig. 9d).

Based on these promising vitro results, we designed a comprehensive in vivo treatment schedule (Fig. 9e) that precisely mapped administration of each therapy across 5 weeks. The alternating schedule systematically rotated between rapamycin + ganetespib and trametinib + ponatinib PDA-820 nanoparticles, while the concomitant schedule administered both combinations simultaneously.

In vivo efficacy studies in K7M2 xenograft models demonstrated that the alternating administration strategy significantly outperformed both concomitant dosing and SOC treatments. Tumors in the alternating group showed minimal growth (remaining below 500% of initial volume), while concomitant treatment showed initial efficacy but eventual tumor progression (Figs. 9f and S6e, p < 0.0001 by unpaired t-test). This translated to markedly improved survival rates in the alternating group (Fig. 9g, p = 0.0006 by Mantel-Cox analysis).

Importantly, body weight measurements indicated that the alternating regimen maintained better tolerability throughout the treatment period compared to concomitant administration, which showed fluctuating weight patterns suggesting periodic toxicity (Supplementary Fig. S6f). The SOC group exhibited significant early weight loss, contributing to its poor survival outcomes despite moderate tumor control.

Major organs and tumors were taken and analyzed for H&E staining to visualize the presence of pathological changes. As demonstrated in Fig. 10, Histological analysis revealed distinct tissue responses across the treatment groups. In the liver, both concomitant and alternating regimens showed mild hepatocyte degeneration, with alternating administration preserving hepatic architecture better than the concomitant regimen. The heart exhibited mild myocardial inflammation in the concomitant group, while the alternating strategy showed reduced disruption, suggesting improved cardiotoxicity profiles. In the kidneys, the concomitant regimen caused mild glomerular and tubular changes, whereas the alternating group displayed less nephrotoxicity. Lung histology indicated mild alveolar congestion in the concomitant group, which was less pronounced in the alternating regimen. Tumor sections revealed significant cell death and reduced cellular density in both treatment groups, with the alternating regimen showing a more controlled tumor response and less necrotic debris. The spleen in the SOC group displayed notable necrosis and lymphoid disruption, unlike the concomitant and alternating groups, where lymphoid tissue was better preserved. The NT group maintained normal histological features across tissues, confirming no treatment-related effects. These findings highlight the reduced toxicity and efficacy of the alternating regimen compared to the concomitant approach, while noting potential systemic damage in the SOC group.

Blinded histopathological evaluation revealed treatment-dependent differences in both tumor response and systemic toxicity. Tumor sections from alternating regimen groups showed pronounced architectural disruption and widespread necrosis, consistent with enhanced therapeutic efficacy. In contrast, standard and concomitant treatment groups exhibited more heterogeneous tumor morphology with less necrotic infiltration. Examination of major organs indicated minimal to mild hepatocyte degeneration and renal tubular alterations in most groups, with the alternating regimen demonstrating the lowest overall signs of systemic toxicity. No severe inflammatory infiltrates or structural damage were observed in cardiac or pulmonary tissues. These findings support the efficacy and relative safety of the alternating nanoparticle strategy (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Discussion

Science has traditionally been divided into two fundamental approaches: hypothesis-driven research, where investigations are guided by specific mechanistic theories, and data-driven discovery, where patterns emerge from comprehensive experimental observations without preconceived explanations. Osteosarcoma represents a challenging case for hypothesis-driven approaches due to its heterogeneous nature and lack of well-defined molecular drivers. Unlike breast cancer, which can be stratified into clinically relevant subtypes (ER+, HER2+, triple-negative) with targeted therapeutic strategies, osteosarcoma lacks established classification schemes that reliably predict treatment response. In this regard, osteosarcoma shares similarities with triple-negative breast cancer, where the absence of druggable targets necessitates broader screening approaches to identify effective therapies.

Our evaluation of large language models in predicting drug efficacy highlights important limitations of computational approaches in personalized cancer therapy. While LLMs have shown promising capabilities in medical diagnosis and treatment recommendation for well-characterized conditions, our findings reveal significant shortcomings when applied to heterogeneous malignancies like osteosarcoma. The poor predictive performance of all tested LLMs (Fig. 3) demonstrates that even advanced AI systems struggle with complex biological systems where clear molecular drivers remain undefined. This limitation was particularly evident in their failure to anticipate the distinctive kinase inhibitor sensitivity patterns in K7M2 and SaOS-2 osteosarcoma cell lines. Even more striking was the performance on drug combinations, where GPT-4 and DeepSeek achieved only 52.2% accuracy—essentially no better than random chance for many combinations.

In contrast, our functional testing approach yielded significant therapeutic insights that computational methods failed to predict. This systematic workflow begins with patient-derived cells cultured in both 2D and 3D formats, progresses through comprehensive drug sensitivity screening, identifies lead drug pairs with optimal synergy scores, and culminates in cocktail assembly and coformulation for in vivo testing. This data-driven methodology allowed us to discover unexpected efficacies, such as the remarkable synergy between ganetespib-rapamycin and trametinib-ponatinib combinations that no computational model predicted. Our findings align with Kornauth et al.17, who demonstrated that functional precision medicine provides clinical benefit in aggressive hematologic cancers by identifying unexpected drug sensitivities that genomic profiling alone could not predict. Similarly, Letai32 advocated for “functional precision medicine” wherein direct measurement of drug responses provides actionable insights beyond what inference from omics data can achieve. The workflow for identifying drug pairs yielded combinations of targets with complementary nodes in key survival pathways. Rapamycin inhibits mTOR, while ganetespib disrupts HSP90 client proteins, including AKT and CDK4, likely converging on mTOR signaling and promoting apoptotic stress. Similarly, trametinib blocks MEK–ERK signaling downstream of RTKs that are directly inhibited by ponatinib (e.g., PDGFR, VEGFR, FGFR), enabling vertical pathway suppression and limiting resistance through feedback loops.

Advanced concepts in complex combination nanotherapy

The superior efficacy of our alternating administration regimen likely stems from its ability to modulate evolutionary pressures on the tumor microenvironment. As Greaves and Maley33 demonstrated, cancer progression follows evolutionary principles where therapeutic interventions create selective pressures favoring resistant subpopulations. Our alternating strategy applies sequential selective pressures through different mechanistic targets (mTOR/HSP90 followed by MAPK/multi-kinase inhibition), preventing any single resistant population from gaining dominance. This aligns with Gatenby’s adaptive therapy concept (2020)34, which proposes maintaining stable tumor burden rather than pursuing maximum cell kill to preserve treatment-sensitive populations that suppress resistant subclones through competitive inhibition. The markedly improved survival in the alternating group (Fig. 9g) supports this theoretical framework, suggesting that temporally separated drug exposures create a more challenging environment for resistance development than simultaneous administration.

The reduced toxicity we observed with alternating administration parallels findings by Goldman and Zhao35 on pharmacodynamic separation in combination chemotherapy. By providing temporal spacing between different drug combinations, our approach creates “recovery windows” for normal tissues while maintaining continuous pressure on tumor cells. Chen et al.36 recently demonstrated this principle with immunotherapy combinations, showing that sequential rather than concurrent administration reduced immune-related adverse events while preserving antitumor efficacy. Our weight data (Supplementary Fig. 6c) showing stable body weights in the alternating group compared to fluctuations in the concomitant group provides empirical support for this concept in nanotherapy administration.

Our polydopamine nanoparticle formulations likely contribute benefits extending beyond simple drug delivery. Mitchell et al.37 demonstrated that nanoparticles can physically modify the tumor microenvironment, enhancing drug accumulation through alterations in tumor perfusion and penetration. Additionally, Shamay et al.38 showed that nanoparticle surface properties can influence interactions with tumor-associated macrophages, potentially reprogramming the immune microenvironment. The enhanced biodistribution we observed (Fig. 8b) suggests our formulations successfully leverage these principles. Furthermore, the stabilization provided by polydopamine likely creates what Azagury et al.28 termed “meta-synergy”—where chemical synergy in nanoformulation combines with biological synergy of the therapeutic agents, amplifying overall efficacy beyond what either component could achieve independently.

To place our nanoparticle platform in the context of current sarcoma nanotherapy efforts, it is important to compare PDA-820 with both earlier and more recent delivery systems. Foundational studies have explored various nanocarriers such as liposomes, PLGA particles, dendrimers, and metal-oxide nanoparticles for sarcoma applications. For example, Chen et al. developed mPEG-PLA nanoparticles for docetaxel delivery in sarcoma, achieving improved pharmacokinetics, higher tumor accumulation, and enhanced antitumor efficacy compared to free drug39. Çoban et al. developed targeted liposomes and nanocochleates for the co-delivery of imatinib and dexketoprofen in fibrosarcoma, achieving enhanced tumor suppression with their PEGylated nanocochleate formulation, but did not address tumor-specific activation mechanisms beyond passive targeting40. Cheng et al. reviewed recent advances in liposome and lipid nanoparticle strategies for cancer therapy, highlighting persistent challenges such as poor tumor penetration, rapid immune clearance, and manufacturing complexity41. Yuan et al. discussed similar challenges in the context of osteosarcoma42. Maleki et al. systematically reviewed the effects of doxorubicin- and methotrexate-loaded nanoparticles on osteosarcoma cells, providing a comprehensive overview of their cytotoxicity, apoptosis induction, and biocompatibility, and identifying gaps for future research43. Bo Li et al. developed MMP14-targeted, long-circulating liposomes co-loading cisplatin and mifamurtide, which demonstrated enhanced uptake and antitumor activity in osteosarcoma cells, providing a promising new approach for combination therapy44. More recently, efforts have been made by Zinger et al. to improve osteosarcoma nanotherapy through biomimetic leukosomes loaded with ponatinib, achieving improved immune evasion and inflamed endothelium targeting. While these biomimetic platforms reduced systemic toxicity and increased drug exposure in tumor tissue45, they did not explore programmed or alternating dosing strategies—an increasingly recognized approach for overcoming resistance in heterogeneous cancers.

Our PDA-820 system builds on these advances by integrating polydopamine’s favorable chemical properties—π–π stacking, hydrogen bonding, redox responsiveness, and strong bioadhesion—to enable stable co-loading of kinase inhibitors and programmable release in the tumor microenvironment. This formulation supports an alternating administration strategy that produced significantly superior tumor control compared to concomitant delivery or standard therapy in K7M2 models. Unlike previous studies, our approach addresses both the biological complexity of osteosarcoma and the therapeutic limitations of earlier NP systems by combining molecular synergy, temporal separation, and platform versatility. Collectively, these features position PDA-820 as a modular and clinically relevant nanotherapy capable of adapting to the dynamic and heterogeneous landscape of high-grade osteosarcomas.

The effectiveness of our multi-kinase inhibitor combinations addresses the fundamental challenge of tumor heterogeneity in osteosarcoma. Unlike molecularly-defined cancers with clear driver mutations, osteosarcoma exhibits both inter- and intra-tumoral heterogeneity that frustrates single-target approaches. Our formulations simultaneously engage multiple oncogenic pathways (mTOR, HSP90, MAPK, tyrosine kinases) in a coordinated manner, creating what Letai32 described as a “net of dependencies” that constrains tumor evolution more effectively than single-agent therapy. The differential sensitivities we observed across cell lines (Fig. 2) emphasize this heterogeneity, while our combination approach provides a rational strategy to address the diversity of molecular drivers simultaneously.

Advantages of multi-stage screening and future directions

The multi-stage screening approach we employed offers several advantages over purely computational methods. First, it captures both genetic and non-genetic determinants of drug response through direct observation rather than inference. Second, the progression from 2 to 3D cultures to in vivo models enables identification of treatments that maintain efficacy across increasingly complex biological systems. Third, the synergy scoring methodology helps overcome the combinatorial explosion challenge inherent in multi-drug regimens by prioritizing the most promising pairs for further development.

Furthermore, our nanoformulation strategy using polydopamine-stabilized particles addresses a critical translational barrier by enabling efficient delivery of complex drug combinations while mitigating individual toxicities. This addresses the “therapeutic window” challenge that has historically limited the clinical application of many promising drug combinations.

Looking forward, several innovative strategies could further enhance our approach. One of the most promising approaches to increase the relevance of the results are 3D Printed Patient Samples. The integration of 3D printed tumor models based on patient-specific imaging and biopsy data could revolutionize the preclinical testing phase. These bioengineered constructs would mimic the patient’s tumor microenvironment more precisely than conventional 2D or even 3D spheroid cultures. By incorporating patient-derived extracellular matrix components and stromal cells, such models would allow us to test alternating drug regimens in a setting that is highly reflective of in vivo conditions—potentially reducing reliance on animal models and accelerating the translation of our findings into clinical practice.

Another promising approach is Microfluidic Platforms for High-Throughput Screening. The development of microfluidic devices that simulate vascularized tumor microenvironments can facilitate rapid, high-throughput screening of numerous drug combinations and dosing sequences. These “organ-on-a-chip” systems would enable real-time monitoring of drug responses under dynamic flow conditions, thereby providing valuable insights into the kinetics of drug uptake, distribution, and clearance. Such platforms could be particularly useful in fine-tuning alternating dosing schedules, as they allow for precise control over exposure times and drug gradients.

Finally, a yet to be developed approach for Advanced AI for Drug Sequencing Optimization. Although our current study highlights the limitations of LLMs in predicting static drug efficacy, there is immense potential for advanced AI algorithms to optimize drug sequencing once they are integrated with real-time, high-dimensional data from functional assays. By incorporating data from 3D printed tumor models and microfluidic platforms, future AI systems could learn the optimal temporal sequences for alternating drug pairs—potentially identifying patterns and predicting resistance dynamics that are not apparent from static genomic or proteomic profiles. Machine learning could also help tailor the dosing ratios and timing to individual patient profiles, further personalizing the treatment approach.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings validate that a functionally driven, experimental approach to personalized therapy for osteosarcoma is not only necessary but also highly effective. The superiority of alternating drug pair administration in terms of both efficacy and safety underscores the importance of considering drug sequencing and timing in treatment design. By combining robust nanoformulation strategies with innovative in vitro and in vivo screening models, we have laid a strong foundation for future research. The envisioned integration of 3D printed patient models, microfluidic screening, and AI-driven optimization promises to further refine our approach, moving us closer to the ultimate goal of truly personalized, patient-centric cancer therapy. This integrated strategy, which leverages both empirical data and cutting-edge technology, could catalyze a paradigm shift in how we approach complex cancers like osteosarcoma—maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects. Our work clearly demonstrates that alternating drug pairs formulated into nanoparticles provide a distinct advantage and represents a compelling case for further investment in functional precision medicine.
