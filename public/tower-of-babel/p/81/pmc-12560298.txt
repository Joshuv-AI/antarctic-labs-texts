Bridging theory and practice: a qualitative interview study of barriers to and facilitators of research collaborations between academia and public health services in Germany
Authors: Laura Arnold, Franziska Vosseberg, Simon Bimczok, Helmut Brand, Timo Clemens, Jan M. Stratil, Simone Weyers, Dagmar Starke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence-informed decision-making (EIDM) is essential for developing and implementing health policies and public health measures that promote, maintain and protect population health. Effective EIDM requires equitable collaboration between academia and practice to foster bilateral knowledge translation and exchange (KTE). This study analyses the barriers and facilitators influencing engagement in such research collaborations within Germany’s public health service (PHS).MethodsWe conducted 23 key informant interviews with 24 experts engaged in or collaborating with Germany’s PHS to explore experiences with PHS research, knowledge transfer, collaboration and training. The interviews, conducted in pairs, were transcribed verbatim and analysed thematically via a coding frame developed through a deductive‒inductive approach. The COM-B Model of Behaviour Change was applied to categorize barriers and facilitators affecting individuals’ and organizations’ capability, opportunity and motivation to engage in academia‒practice collaboration.ResultsThe analysis revealed the importance of combined approaches that integrate informal networks, formal partnerships, research collaborations and capacity-building initiatives to foster local evidence ecosystems, ensuring bilateral KTE between academia and practice to support EIDM at the local level. Ten key barriers and 35 facilitators capable of addressing these challenges were identified. Strategies to address capability and opportunity barriers included workforce development with dedicated research time, on-the-job training, streamlined administrative processes, long-term funding mechanisms, strengthened relationships between academia and PHS and improved access to research infrastructure and local settings. Financial and educational incentives, adequate incentive structures, an evidence-informed culture within the PHS, and equitable partnerships based on mutual recognition were highlighted as essential motivators. Based on these findings, we developed a conceptual framework reflecting the interconnected nature of barriers and facilitators.ConclusionsSustainable academia‒practice collaboration is crucial for bridging the knowledge‒action gap and thereby strengthening EIDM in PHS. However, Germany’s PHS lacks institutionally anchored partnerships to strengthen bilateral KTE and EIDM at the local level. The proposed KTE framework provides actionable pathways to bridge gaps between academia and PHS, fostering sustainable and effective collaboration. It can be used to systemically assess factors influencing engagement in equitable partnerships and guide the development of solutions tailored to local needs, supporting EIDM processes at the local level.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12961-025-01413-w.

Background

The systematic use of high-quality research evidence is essential for developing and implementing health policies and public health measures that promote, maintain and protect population health. Public health practitioners and organizations are urged to base decisions and programs on the best available evidence on intervention effectiveness while also taking into account factors such as acceptability, affordability, context, equity, feasibility, public opinion, political will and sustainability—an approach known as evidence-informed decision-making (EIDM) [1–3]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), EIDM is a structured approach involving the identification, appraisal and application of evidence via systematic, replicable methods [1]. This is often described as a two-way, iterative process involving several steps [4]: (1) identifying relevant evidence; (2) synthesizing this evidence to form a robust decision-making foundation; and (3) utilizing it in practice while accounting for cultural, economic and social contexts. The benefits of EIDM include increased effectiveness, efficiency and equity of health policies and interventions; more efficient use of scarce resources; reduced research waste; greater transparency and accountability; minimized harm and improved health outcomes [5–8].

For the EIDM to function efficiently and effectively, evidence must address local needs and offer applicable, actionable solutions. Achieving this requires two-way knowledge translation and exchange (KTE) within equal partnerships rather than one-way transfers from academia to practice [9, 10]. Such bilateral approaches depend on active support from all organizations and stakeholders involved in conceptualization, policy development, and the implementation of public health. At the organizational level, integrating EIDM into daily operations requires sustainable collaboration among academia, the government and administration to ensure bilateral KTE processes that align research with policy questions, address practical needs and conduct and communicate evidence in meaningful ways [11–13]. At the individual level, researchers, practitioners and decision-makers must understand the operational contexts and infrastructures of academia, government and administration, as well as the public health and health policy systems in which evidence translation occurs [14–16]. To implement effective KTE processes in sustainable academia‒practice partnerships, sufficient capability, opportunity and motivation for both public health professionals and organizations is essential.

These aspects are particularly relevant at the municipal level, where local health authorities (LHAs), with their in-depth understanding of local conditions, play a crucial role in implementing EIDM processes [17]. Embedded in local governments, LHAs are uniquely positioned to adapt evidence to meet community needs and preferences [18, 19]. While national and federal bodies provide essential support through evidence generation, guideline development, training and consultation, LHAs are often tasked with tailoring these resources to address localized challenges. However, limited capacity, resources and expertise often hinder LHAs’ ability to integrate EIDM into local public health policies and interventions [20]. In Germany, 377 LHAs coordinate a broad range of public health operations, including health promotion, disease prevention and health protection [21]. However, their organizational structures and autonomy vary significantly [22]. State-organized LHAs operate under standardized directives, offering consistency but limiting their decision-making autonomy, whereas LHAs embedded within districts and independent city administrations enjoy greater autonomy but content with greater structural diversity.

Strategic collaborations between academia—comprising universities and research institutions—and LHAs offer promising solutions to enhance EIDM processes at the local level [23–26]. These partnerships can enable bilateral KTE, fostering continuous interaction to ensure that evidence is generated in alignment with the needs of local practice and remains both relevant and applicable [27–29]. They can support the development of guidelines and standard operating procedures tailored to municipal and LHA contexts, bridging the gap between evidence generation and practical implementation. While LHAs are not expected to generate evidence themselves, they play a crucial role in adapting generated evidence to develop and implement tailored solutions.

Despite this potential, sustainable academia‒practice partnerships are rare. Most local collaborations are limited to infrequent, short-term and topic-specific projects [30], and empirical research on barriers and solutions for establishing long-term partnerships remains limited. Within Germany’s Public Health Service (PHS), challenges such as staff shortages, resource constraints and bureaucracy hinder collaboration further [31–33]. Although barriers within academia are also acknowledged, they are less frequently documented in the scientific literature. These challenges have yet to be empirically examined in Germany’s federally structured public health system. Existing studies on barriers to and facilitators of local research collaborations originate exclusively from outside Germany [17, 34, 35], with some focusing on specific interventions such as knowledge brokering [36–38] or academic health departments [24, 39]. Crucially, no evidence defines the specific requirements for public health professionals and organizations to effectively engage in bilateral KTE within academia‒practice partnerships to strengthen EIDM at the local level.

To address this gap, we conducted qualitative interviews to identify barriers and facilitators influencing engagement in research collaborations between academia and practice within the PHS in Germany, aiming to strengthen evidence-informed practices at the local level. The COM-B model [40], a widely recognized framework for behavior change, guided our contextualization of findings. By integrating capability (C), opportunity (O) and motivation (M) as causal components of behaviour (B), the model provides a structured approach to assess the requirements needed to sustain academia‒practice partnerships. Effective collaboration requires three elements: capability in terms of necessary skills and knowledge to engage in cooperation (for example, scientific and administrative competencies); opportunity in terms of external conditions that make cooperation feasible (for example, available funding, supportive administrative policies); and motivation in terms of the drive and willingness of both individuals and institutions to pursue collaborative activities (for example, incentives). Although the COM-B model was initially developed to explain individual behaviour change, it is also well suited for assessing organizational dynamics [4, 12]. By applying the COM-B model to the context of local academia‒practice partnerships, we aim to clarify the conditions necessary for health professionals and organizations to engage in such partnerships. This will be accomplished by exploring the following research questions (RQs):What types of exchange and collaboration exist between academia and PHS practices?What are the rationales of collaboration in academia and PHS practices?What barriers hinder and which facilitators support academia‒practice collaboration?

Methods

We conducted 23 key informant interviews (KIIs) with 24 experts working in or with the PHS in Germany. This work is part of the EvidenzOEGD study, which examines institutional collaborations within Germany’s PHS to develop and implement novel training approaches that qualify public health researchers and practitioners for KTE processes at the local level [41]. This study focuses on the forms, barriers and facilitators of sustainable research collaboration. We followed the COREQ reporting guidelines [42]; a completed checklist is available as a supplementary file (Supplement A).

Sample

Participants were purposefully selected using criterion-based sampling. Inclusion criteria were professional affiliation with academia and/or the PHS and direct experience with collaborative research or practice projects. To capture diverse perspectives, we sought variation across administrative levels (state, federal and local), professional backgrounds and varying levels of experience, aiming for a balanced distribution across levels. All PHS levels were included to gain a comprehensive view of contextual conditions. No formal exclusion criteria were applied. Participants were identified through the professional networks of the EvidenzOEGD study consortium and expanded via snowballing. Recruitment occurred from December 2021 to March 2022. Each candidate received an email invitation detailing the study’s aims, data collection procedures and data protection measures. Once written consent was obtained, interviews were scheduled at the participants’ convenience.

Data collection

A semistructured interview guide, informed by a literature review and input from the study consortium, was developed to explore PHS research, knowledge transfer, collaboration and qualifications (Supplement B). Following pilot testing with two participants and minor adjustments, interviews were conducted from January 12, 2022, to May 03, 2022. Most interviews were conducted with researcher pairs (LA, SB or SW) via an online conference tool (Webex and Zoom). Only participants and researchers were present and no follow-up or repeated interviews were conducted. Records were transcribed verbatim (LA, SB, DD, SG, JP and LU) via a content‒semantic approach [43]. Participants were offered the opportunity to review and comment on their transcripts; however, none chose to do so. Audio recordings were deleted after the transcripts were pseudonymized. The interviews were conducted in German and English, with the analysis being performed in the original languages. The quotes were translated into English via neural machine translation (DeepL SE) and large language models (ChatGPT) and were subsequently verified by researchers (LA, FV and ML).

Analysis

Pseudonymized transcripts [36] were analysed using Mayring’s qualitative content analysis method [44, 45]. The coding template was developed through a deductive-inductive approach. The a priori coding frame was constructed in iterative brainstorming sessions within the research team (LA, SB, FV, SW), complemented by feedback from the EvidenzOEGD steering group and external experts. The coding scheme for collaboration forms and types drew on prior mapping of cooperation structures in the German PHS [46], while the scheme for relevant skills and competencies was informed by the results of a prior scoping review [47]. Core meta-aspects identified included forms and types of collaboration, facilitating and hindering factors and relevant skills and competencies for sustainable collaboration. We furthermore used the COM-B model as the theoretical framework to identify barriers and facilitators influencing individuals’ and organizations’ engagement in academia‒practice collaboration. Barriers were defined as factors hindering engagement, whereas facilitators enhanced it. Following Michie et al. [40], the factors were categorized into three domains:Capabilities: Internal factors that can affect collaboration engagement. For individuals, this can include relevant skills and knowledge and communication abilities; for organizations, this can involve adequate institutional capacity, such as well-qualified personnel with dedicated time.Opportunities: External conditions that can enable collaboration, given that capability is present. For individuals, this can include supportive regulatory conditions, access to resources and networks; for organizations, it can involve streamlined administrative processes, favourable legal frameworks and effective interinstitutional partnerships.Motivation: Intrinsic and extrinsic drivers that can impact engagement. For individuals, this can encompass personal interest, recognition and perceived public health impact; for organizations, motivation can be derived from strategic goals and anticipated benefits, such as enhanced capacity and service delivery. Eternal factors such as standards, guidelines and funding incentives can further shape motivation.

The coding frame was piloted with one interview, refined and then applied to all transcripts. Interviews were coded independently by two researchers (LA, LR and FV) using MAXQDA Analytics Pro 2022 (VERBI Software GmbH). New concepts emerging during coding were incorporated into the final coding template (Supplement D). Discrepancies were resolved through discussion (LA, FV). After coding two-thirds of the transcripts, no additional themes emerged, leading the study team to conclude that data saturation had been reached. In accordance with the study design, all transcripts were coded and no further participants recruited.

Ethics and quality assurance

The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethics approval from the Ethics Committee of Heinrich-Heine-University Duesseldorf (protocol codes 2021-1646, 2021-1646.1).

To mitigate potential biases, each research step involved multiple researchers to ensure intersubjectivity. Following a reflexive approach, the interdisciplinary research team (LA, SB, DS, FV, SW) brought together diverse professional perspectives, with three external experts (HB, TC, JMS) providing independent reviews for a more balanced analysis [48]. As detailed in Supplement C, the team members involved in coding and content analysis had extensive methodological experience in qualitative research and diverse academic backgrounds.

Since the PHS in Germany, as in other European countries, has traditionally been strongly medicine-oriented, particular attention was paid to including a wide range of professions in the expert panel to avoid one-sided interpretations. Another concern identified early on was the risk of overlooking organizational barriers in PHS due to limited real-world practice insights – a common issue in academic settings. To address this, the research team was deliberately balanced with members who had long-standing experience in PHS, academia, or both. All team members were aware that their current or previous roles could shape their perspectives during analysis. This was addressed in regular team discussions, independent reviews by external experts and equal collaboration between early-career and senior researchers, ensuring integration of perspectives without long-standing professional experience. These measures aimed to support critical assessment of methodologies and interpretations, balancing academic and practice-oriented viewpoints and enhancing the study’s overall credibility.

Study participants

Twenty-four experts (12 women, 12 men) participated in 23 KIIs, with no participants refusing or dropping out. The interview durations ranged from 31 to 56 min.

At the time of data collection, 16 participants were employed in the PHS, eleven were affiliated with academic research institutions and three held dual affiliations. Two participants had transitioned to academia after several years in the PHS, and four had transitioned in the opposite direction. Types of exchange and collaboration (RQ1)

The interviewees described various types of exchange and collaboration between academia and PHS, drawing on both their direct experiences and their views on how these interactions should ideally be structured. These types were classified by form (formal vs. informal) and focus (research vs. education/training).

Informal (non-formalized) forms of exchange, often mentioned in the context of networks such as the German Network of Young Professionals in Public Health [49], the Zukunftsforum Public Health [50] and the Competence Network Public Health COVID-19 [51], were valued for fostering relationship-building and rapid knowledge exchange (see Supplement E, interview participant 01 and quote 1; in the following quote I01.Q1). However, concerns emerged regarding their fragility (I16.Q2), reliance on personal commitment (I13.Q3) and perceived limitations in impact (I08.Q4). Additionally, participants highlighted quality control issues, particularly during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, when the lack of safeguards increased the risk of spreading unreliable information (I19.Q5). To address these issues, some interviewees recommended developing a cohesive system of support by formalizing networks and partnerships:Essentially, a system of support needs to be developed. We need to identify the areas where collaboration between academia and practice in PHS needs a significant leap forward and then specifically promote those areas, ensuring that they work together in a networked manner. The outcome would then be a system that is implementable and useable for PHS (I13.Q6).

The interviews revealed that most formal collaborations between academia and LHAs focused on local research collaboration and educational capacity-building initiatives, which often overlap in practice. Research collaborations typically fell into one of three categories: research with the PHS, research about the PHS and research for the PHS (I09.Q7). The majority of the research collaboration mentioned fell under the first category, research with the PHS, although the level of LHA involvement varied significantly. While some projects demonstrated active collaboration, with LHAs contributing to design and implementation, others relegated LHAs to a more peripheral role, such as data providers or facilitators, without substantial influence over research priorities or methods (I01.Q8). Furthermore, most formal collaborations were topic-specific and time-limited and were often constrained to individual projects rather than forming part of a sustained or institutionalized partnership.

Most capacity-building initiatives mentioned were designed to strengthen PHS representation in academic programs and equip students and young professionals with the practical skills needed for careers in PHS. The participants emphasized the importance of experiential learning opportunities and interdisciplinary collaboration as key strategies to bridge the gap between academic training and the operational demands of PHS. These initiatives included activities such as joint thesis supervision (I03.Q9), teaching collaborations (I18.Q10), teaching-research projects (I06/07.Q11), clinical placements, internships and job shadowing for students (I18.Q12), as well as staff rotation, exchanges and secondments (I12.Q13).

Not yet implemented forms of exchange and collaboration highlighted by interviewees included academic health departments dedicated to research and teaching (I12.Q14, I04.Q15), appointing knowledge brokers to facilitate local evidence transfer (I15.Q16, I15.Q17) and creating bridged professorships to link academic chairs and LHAs (I13.Q18). A coordinated infrastructure led by academies for PHS (I04.Q19) or state health departments (I13.Q20) was suggested to support these initiatives.

Rationales for engaging in formal research collaborations (RQ2)

A common rationale for engaging in formal research collaborations, expressed by both academia and PHS interviewees, was to increase the practical relevance of academic research for PHS (I01.Q21, I12.Q22, I12.Q23) and to strengthen the evidence base for identifying and addressing complex public health problems on site (I24.Q24). PHS involvement in academic research was also seen as enhancing the credibility and impact of research findings, giving more weight to recommendations:Certainly, it is also beneficial if the public health authorities are involved [as a partner], [...] [as] this can enhance the credibility of the message […]. It also likely adds more weight to the recommendations if the local or state public health authorities have contributed. It may also be less easily ignored by political decision-makers than when it is just coming from ‘science’ (I01.Q25)

Additionally, the interviewees highlighted the mutual benefits of access to data and infrastructure, with the PHS providing valuable local data and close community engagement and academia offering expertise and resources for data analysis (I10.Q26).

Further rationales for PHS professionals included applying research findings in daily practice and decision-making (I15.Q27), addressing research questions relevant to PHS (I01.Q28) and improving the PHS image through practical insights such as rotations that offer internal insights (I22.Q29). Academics were driven by the desire to increase the practical applicability of their work, gain firsthand insights (I08.Q30) and support PHS structural development through scientific methods (I02.Q31).

Barriers and facilitators to cooperate and engage in PHS research collaborations (RQ3)

The interviews identified a range of factors influencing research collaboration between academia and PHS. Using the COM-B model [40], these factors were classified into three domains—capability, opportunity and motivation—affecting individuals (for example, public health professionals in academia and the PHS) and organizations (for example, universities, research institutes and public health authorities).

The analysis revealed 10 key barriers and 35 facilitators, summarized in the Knowledge Translation and Exchange (KTE) framework developed in this study (Fig. 1). Meta-facilitator: leadership

(1) Human resources

Several interviewees highlighted the lack of dedicated staff hours for research as a significant barrier to collaboration within the PHS (I01.Q32). This longstanding issue has been exacerbated by austerity measures, staff reductions and difficulties in recruiting staff qualified for scientific work within the PHS in recent years (I18.Q33). Among others, this was attributed to a focus on medical professions, creating entry barriers for professionals without medical degrees (I02.Q34) and the perceived unattractiveness of the local PHS as an employer due to limited career opportunities and a lack of relevance within the broader health system (I08.Q35, I08.Q36).

Similar challenges were reported for academia, where the availability of dedicated staff positions was regarded as limited due to reliance on third-party funding and a lack of permanent positions (I13.Q37). One interviewee stated:In research, the principle is as follows: Follow the money - This applies not only in research but also in the future. If there is no funding for PHS research, then essentially, we can hardly carry it out. We are all relatively poorly equipped in regard to staff positions at universities, that is, available positions (I02.Q38)

To address these issues, participants suggested allocating dedicated research time by reducing duties and integrating research activities into existing roles (I19.Q39). They recommended diversifying recruitment strategies by including multi-professional teams and opening PHS positions to those with public health qualifications, thereby expanding the pool of eligible professionals and alleviating personnel shortages (I04.Q40, I04.Q41). In this context, the current modernization efforts initiated at the political level in response to the COVID-19 pandemic were viewed as an important “window of opportunity” (I17.Q42). Establishing research units and fixed research staffing ratios within LHAs (I05.Q43) and creating explicit PHS-relevant funding calls (discussed in further detail in key theme 2) were also proposed to increase research capacity and overcome the shortage of personnel in both academia and the PHS.

(2) Knowledge, skills and competencies

Another barrier was the lack of scientific literacy among PHS employees, particularly at the local level, which was considered essential for academia‒practice collaboration in general and related knowledge translation activities in particular. According to the interviewees, this included a lack of skills related to a critical assessment and interpretation of scientific evidence (I05.Q44), especially evidence syntheses (I14.Q45, I15.Q46), as well as communicating research findings (I05.Q47). In contrast, a barrier to cooperation on the side of academia was a reported lack of practical knowledge of PHS administrative procedures, reporting channels and decision-making processes (I08.Q48). For both academia and PHS practices, a lack of knowledge and mutual understanding of the other systems was regarded as a barrier to collaboration (I02.Q49).

The participants emphasized the need for mutual understanding of the differing practices, operational modes and working conditions between academia and PHS (I15.Q50). Training PHS professionals in evidence utilization, policy advising and communication of research findings was considered essential (I15.Q51, I21.Q52), as was equipping academics with the skills needed for scientific work within the PHS (I05.Q53). Both scientific and administrative literacy are seen as prerequisites for effective collaboration (I24.Q54), with familiarity in policy frameworks such as the public health action cycle [52] valued for providing a structured approach to public health advocacy (I08.Q48, I18.Q55). However, for this to be successful, LHAs must act as knowledge brokers, translating evidence into local strategies, provided that the necessary evidence is available (I09.Q56).

On-the-job training was recommended to convey the relevant skills and competencies (I14.Q57). Additionally, staff rotations (I03.Q58), secondments (I12.Q13) and joint posts (I21.Q59) were seen as viable options for enhancing practical experience across both sectors.

To institutionalize the relevant knowledge, the interviewees suggested recruitment strategies that prioritize competencies over professional titles (I04.Q40). The accumulation of capabilities at the institutional level was seen as essential for ensuring a comprehensive and effective organizational skill set and promoting interdisciplinary collaboration relevant for aligning interests and fostering collaboration (I01.Q60, I05.Q61).

(3) Bureaucratic hurdles

Bureaucratic hurdles on both sides, academia and PHS, complicated collaboration efforts: Restrictive data protection regulations and comprehensive ethics applications added complexity to data sharing and joint analysis, making collaboration disproportionately difficult for both parties (I15.Q62). For the PHS, legal and administrative constraints limited its capacity to engage in research collaborations alongside its routine operations, such as service delivery and regulatory duties, leaving little room for individual or institutional research activities (I14.Q63, I02.Q64). Compliance with strict regulatory frameworks often required lengthy approval processes and coordination efforts (I15.Q62). Federal structures were described as adding further complexity through unclear responsibilities and fragmented procedures (I06/07.Q65). As a result, interviewees often found it easier to conduct research within academia, using the PHS as a source of information rather than as a full collaboration partner:[…]. A major challenge in this collaboration has always been the bureaucratic hurdles that need to be overcome. Sometimes it was on the university side, and other times more on the side of the [PHS]. It was often easier to run projects as university projects, where we used the PHS as a source of information rather than as a full cooperation partner because it saved much of the bureaucracy. […] In many cases, it was just truly challenging to get to the right people, who themselves had to go through multiple levels of approval. Therefore, instead of constantly having to go through networks to connect with the right people at the [institution], it was often easier to keep things internally (I15.Q62)

The respondents highlighted the need to streamline bureaucratic processes and ensure dedicated time, allowing PHS professionals to engage in research collaborations without being overwhelmed by routine tasks (I19.Q66, I11.Q67). Integrating research tasks into job profiles was seen as essential for aligning scientific work with daily operations (I13.Q68). Strong leadership support was highlighted as crucial for the success of these efforts (I23.Q69).

(4) Financial and technical constraints

Barriers to collaboration within the PHS included a limited research infrastructure, particularly in rural municipalities (I18.Q70). This involved limited access to data analysis software (I05.Q71, I10.Q72), reference management tools (I22.Q73), scientific literature (I14.Q74, I19.Q75) and mechanisms for ethical approval for applied PHS research (I21.Q76). The participants emphasized the need for strong leadership support to secure adequate resources, noting that severe underfunding often confines staff to routine tasks and stifles innovation. They also noted that some leadership practices, such as maintaining tight control over activities, may inadvertently hinder progress by discouraging critical reflection on existing structures or potential structural changes (I05.Q77).

The dependence on project-based external funding further limited opportunities and motivation for collaboration on both sides, with concern over the lack of continuity (I08.Q78). For academia, third-party funding was a key driver: while insufficient funding restricted opportunities (I02.Q79), securing it facilitated cooperation and increased motivation to engage in practice-oriented PHS research (3.8). However, the administrative burden of applying for grants discouraged engagement on both sides. Academia often finds the process too cumbersome given the comparatively small funding amounts (I03.Q80), compounded by perceived challenges in securing funding for applied research (I03.Q81). On the other hand, PHS, particularly LHAs, often lack the capacity and specialized expertise to manage complex funding applications (I22.Q82, I01.Q83).

Substantial public investment and long-term strategic funding were seen as essential for supporting joint PHS research and ensuring sustainable collaboration (I04.Q84). While existing project-based funding was valued, its short-term focus was seen as insufficient (I13.Q85). Instead, participants suggested long-term funding mechanisms that prioritize applied research with measurable impact and effective implementation of research findings (I13.Q86). To secure PHS participation, joint funding models that mandate active collaboration were suggested by participants (I16.Q87, I15.Q88, I13.Q89). One interviewee proposed an iterative approach, where PHS professionals contribute to setting research priorities, collaborate on methodologies and assist in interpreting and disseminating findings to ensure their relevance and practical applicability (I15.Q90).

(5) Relationships and networks

The interviewees noted a lack of personal relationships, exchange opportunities and institutionalized collaboration between academia and the PHS, with most connections relying on individual efforts. Without structured exchange opportunities, individuals had to build these connections on their own, often depending on their superior mandate, scientific background and personal engagement (I12.Q91, I02.Q92).

The participants highlighted the value of informal exchange opportunities to build personal connections (I16.Q93). However, to promote structured exchanges on emerging topics and research needs, interviewees recommended establishing structured communication channels between PHS and academia (I03.Q94, I18.Q95), alongside institutionally anchored, long-term collaborations that are independent of short-term third-party funding cycles and supported by leadership (I13.Q96). Practical experience in both fields was deemed essential (as also discussed in key theme 1, barrier 2), and the interviewees suggested the introduction of bridged positions such as knowledge brokers as mediators between academia and the PHS (I10.Q97, I15.Q98) and the use of bridged professorships in terms of joint appointments between universities and state public institutions to enhance evidence generation and translation at a systemic level (I13.Q99, I04.Q100).

(6) Divergent operational modes and access

The participants noted different operational approaches between academia and PHS. In some aspects, these differences were seen as complementary: the PHS has direct access to data, settings and political connections essential for developing locally relevant strategies and policies (I08.Q101, I23.Q102) but lacks operational and research autonomy because of its integration within local administration and dependence on (local) politics (I24.Q103, I22.Q104, I08.Q105). In contrast, academia has more autonomy but limited access to local data and settings (I04.Q106, I04.Q107). However, the interviewees noted that these differences also create barriers, particularly due to a lack of common language, different timeframes and specific legislative demands within public administration (I14.Q108, I21.Q109, I24.Q110). For example, the time frame under which PHS practitioners must act and require evidence was reported as often being at odds with the time frame perceived as necessary in academia to provide reliable evidence:Political demands [within the PHS] are “to have the measure immediately, to implement it immediately and to have implemented everything within 3 months” so that the minister or mayor can adorn themselves with it. The requirements that come from academia, which we have valid findings on, for example, effectiveness or barriers, take more time than is often the case in practice (I13.Q111)

Facilitating the translation of research findings into language relevant for politics and decision-makers was deemed essential (I15.Q115), as was fostering a general understanding of the scientific and administrative processes and their inherent time constraints.

Key theme 3—motivation

Most interviewees characterized joint research collaborations as mutually beneficial for academia and PHS, describing them as a win‒win‒win scenario (I01.Q116): Collaborations were seen as a way to increase motivation across employees, health authorities and research institutions in various ways. For PHS professionals, key motivations included access to research infrastructure, scientific approaches and skill development, potentially leading to better pay and career progression. Academics, in turn, were motivated by gaining access to relevant research questions, data and practical insights, increasing the (local) applicability of their work. For LHAs, specialized expertise and research infrastructure from collaborations were viewed as means to improve their capacity to address PHS-relevant research questions, enhance evidence-based practices and increase the quality of service delivery. This could, in turn, enhance reputation and employability, helping to alleviate human resource constraints (as discussed in key theme 1, barriers 1 and 2). For academia, access to concrete research questions and data, along with the promotion of applied research with practical relevance, were strong motivational factors.

Despite these recognized benefits, the interviewees also identified several demotivating factors that affected their engagement in research collaborations. (7) Reward imbalances in continuous professional development (CPD)

The lack of well-qualified staff within the PHS emerged as a key barrier to research capability (key theme 1, barriers 1 and 2). While continuous professional development (CPD) and practical experience were noted as potential mitigators, participants noted that inadequate salary adjustments and limited opportunities for career progression following additional responsibilities or qualifications discouraged further education or research collaboration (I22.Q117, I01.Q118). One interviewee even raised concerns that health authorities might restrict CPD to prevent staff turnover (I01.Q119). The lack of structured career advancement for non-medical professionals was seen as another demotivating factor (I10.Q120). In academia, career progress has been discussed mainly in relation to the lack of opportunities for pursuing a medical doctorate within the PHS (I10.Q121).

Financial and educational incentives, such as appropriate remuneration and clear career paths for all relevant professions within the PHS, were seen as crucial for fostering motivation to engage in CPD and maintain a well-qualified PHS workforce (I01.Q122). Additionally, opportunities to gain postgraduate qualifications such as doctorate or habilitation for academia and PHS (I04.Q123) and to ensure structured CPD and attractive career opportunities for all academic professions within the PHS (I14.Q124) were suggested. These facilitators were also seen as crucial retention strategies for enhancing the employability of PHS, ensuring that professionals remain motivated to pursue long-term careers within the public health sector (I10.Q125).

(8) Conflicting incentive structures

Conflicting incentive structures between academia and PHS were seen as another key barrier: While academia focuses on gaining (international) scientific reputation by publishing in high-impact, English-language journals (I13.Q126), PHS needs to prioritize practical, locally relevant evidence, ideally summarized in the German language, to inform relevant stakeholders, including policymakers and citizens (I09.Q127, I10.Q128, I18.Q129, I01.Q130). This misalignment in output expectations, combined with a lack of incentives for translating and disseminating research results into practice (I18.Q131), discourages engagement in collaboration. The impact factor-driven academic incentive system further exacerbates these issues by marginalizing PHS-centric research (I12.Q131, I15.Q132, I23.Q134), particularly those specific to rural areas:Scientists usually look for projects that can be published internationally. That’s their must—it is their incentive system [...]. In addition, if you imagine there’s a tiny local health authority [in a rural district] that has issues with two schools or has a question, no scientist is interested in that because it does not bring anything. No one can conduct a large study with just two schools and publish this (I13.Q135)

No one can conduct a large study with just two schools and publish this (I13.Q135)

The interviewees suggested prioritizing ‘real-world relevance’ and rewarding dissemination and implementation of research in academic performance evaluations as ways to increase academia’s extrinsic motivation to engage in research collaborations with the PHS or conduct PHS-relevant research (I15.Q136, I18.Q137). They also emphasized the importance of recognizing and funding translational tasks as a ‘third mission’ in academia, including media appearances and the development of practical tools, to better align academic incentives with PHS needs (I20.Q138, I13.Q139) and stressed the importance of teaching the essential skills required for this work (I20.Q140).

(9) Evidence-informed culture within the PHS

The participants from academia and the PHS expressed concerns that the PHS self-perception as an enforcement agency, which focused primarily on regulatory public health functions, fostered a task-oriented culture (I04.Q141). This mindset was seen as hindering forward-thinking developments, complicating research efforts for intrinsically motivated staff (I22.Q142) and limiting the implementation of EIDM processes needed for advancing evidence-based practice at the local level (I05.Q143). Many PHS professionals viewed research activities as outside their core responsibilities, lacking awareness of their potential benefits for daily practice (I18.Q144).

In this context, interviewees again emphasized the need to equip PHS professionals with skills and competencies to apply empirical methods for evidence use, rather than expecting them to conduct research for evidence generation, as highlighted in key theme 1, barrier 2. They highlighted the importance of building infrastructure that provides locally relevant evidence, such as checklists and implementation guidelines, to support evidence-informed policymaking. According to one interviewee, these tools would enable LHAs to identify and implement evidence-based policy options in their context (I09.Q145).

Development of an evidence-informed culture within the PHS was seen as a key motivational facilitator for promoting EIDM processes and fostering critical discussions. This culture was deemed crucial for addressing emerging public health challenges and preparing PHS for the future (I04.Q146). (10) Mutual recognition and equitable partnerships

Finally, the participants identified a lack of mutual recognition between academia and PHS as a strong demotivating factor, especially when contributions were perceived as undervalued or misunderstood. PHS professionals often feel reduced to the role of data providers rather than equal partners (I15.Q150). Meta-barriers and facilitators

During the development of the KTE framework, two meta-criteria were identified as overarching factors that indirectly shape the conditions for effective collaboration rather than directly impacting the collaboration itself. These criteria addressed broader systemic issues and prerequisites, creating either enabling (meta-facilitator) or inhibiting (meta-barrier) environments, thereby playing a crucial role in the success of academia‒practice partnerships.

Supportive leadership has emerged as a meta-facilitator that is essential for establishing sustainable collaborations by addressing key barriers and creating supportive conditions. Within the PHS, supportive leadership was seen as critical for securing dedicated research time (key theme 1, barrier 1), fostering relevant skills and competencies (key theme 1, barrier 2), reducing bureaucratic hurdles (key theme 2, barrier 3), promoting intersectoral networks and partnerships (key theme 2, barrier 5), aligning operational modes (key theme 2, barrier 6) and supporting career advancement opportunities and rewards (key theme 2, barrier 7). Leadership also played a key role in strengthening evidence-based approaches, fostering an evidence-informed culture within PHS (key theme 3, barrier 9) and encouraging equitable collaboration between academia and practice (key theme 3, barrier 10).What you need, of course, is the will to do scientific work on the part of superiors. If there is no interest, no matter how willing you are, if you are not supported, then it will fizzle out. […] If you are not given time off or are not supported, […] then it is difficult to make progress (I11.Q163)

While supportive leadership was frequently highlighted in the context of the PHS, its role within academia was not mentioned by participants.

Conflicting system logics were identified as a meta-barrier, with operational disparities between academia and PHS significantly impacting the capability, opportunity and motivation for sustainable collaboration. Systemic differences manifested across several domains, including limited system knowledge (key theme 1, barrier 2), complex administrative processes (key theme 2, barrier 3), differing operational modes with distinct timeframes, priorities and languages (key theme 2, barrier 6) and divergent reward structures and incentives (key theme 3, barrier 8). For example, academia focuses on long-term research cycles with rewards tied to publications, whereas PHS prioritizes practical, immediate solutions for local challenges that inform policy and legislation. A lack of understanding, acknowledgment and alignment of these differences can severely hinder the development of effective collaborative structures.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the requirements for public health professionals and organizations to effectively engage in bilateral KTE within academia‒practice partnerships to strengthen EIDM at the local level. We analysed forms of exchange and collaboration, rationales for engagement and barriers and facilitators affecting partnerships. This discussion expands on these findings to consider broader implications, propose practical solutions and outline actionable strategies for enhancing EIDM through collaboration between academia and practice.

Types of exchange and collaboration needed for local academia‒practice partnerships

We identified two types of exchange—informal networks and formal partnerships—and two forms of collaboration—research collaborations and capacity-building initiatives—as crucial for establishing long-term partnerships between academia and the PHS.

Interviewees considered informal networks and exchange opportunities as essential for fostering innovation and knowledge sharing between academia and practice. This aligns with expert opinions on their role in promoting mutual understanding, respect and trust [35], as well as with empirical evidence of their contribution to rapid knowledge exchange during crises [53–57]. However, without institutional support, we found these networks to remain fragile and reliant on personal commitment. In line with the literature [26], interviewees underscored the value of formalizing such networks (for example, through memoranda of agreements or contracts [34]) to improve reliability and clarify expectations.

Formal research collaboration between academia and PHS involved research with, about and for the PHS. The most commonly reported form was research with the PHS. Research about and for the PHS has occurred far less frequently, highlighting a gap in the academia-practice landscape. In collaborations focusing on research with the PHS, LHA involvement ranged from active participation in codeveloping research questions to peripheral roles as data providers or facilitators. Contrasting these findings with the participation ladder from Wright et al. [58, 59] indicates that many collaborations exhibit lower levels of participation, where LHAs are engaged but lack direct influence over decision-making, providing input or logistical support without control over research priorities or methods. Arnstein describes this level as ‘tokenism’, where participation appears inclusive but lacks meaningful agency [60]. Progressing to higher levels would require equitable partnerships, with LHAs actively being involved in research efforts alongside academia. Further research should systematically assess participation levels in existing academia‒practice collaborations to identify steps for fostering more equitable partnerships, ensuring that research aligns with PHS priorities and generates evidence applicable to local settings as demanded by several interviewees and experts in the field [32].

Capacity-building initiatives were highly valued by interviewees and public health education experts [24, 39], who emphasized the importance of ‘real-world ‘ exposure for broadening the understanding of public health issues, gaining institutional insights and developing skills through active engagement. In line with the literature, such initiatives are regarded as vital for building collegiality and trust, two relational factors identified as essential foundations for future organizational collaboration [29]. Furthermore, they were seen as crucial for fostering familiarity with the distinct system logics of academia and practice, as well as equipping professionals with the skills and competencies needed to engage in bilateral KTE approaches at the local level [29]. Interviewees highlighted the importance of bridging this gap through a variety of teaching formats and experiential learning opportunities, including joint thesis supervision, teaching collaboration, placements and staff rotations. The forms and potential of these diverse teaching formats to strengthen collaboration have been analysed in previous work [47].

On the basis of the interviews, a combined approach that integrates both types of exchange (formal and informal) and both forms of collaboration (research and capacity building) was identified as particularly beneficial for establishing collaborative infrastructures between academia and the PHS. This finding resonates with the WHO concept of local evidence ecosystems [1], which builds on comprehensive methodological work (for example, in the field of evidence synthesis [61, 62]) and conceptual development in KTE for EIDM [63–65]. According to the WHO, evidence ecosystems emphasize the joint generation, synthesis and utilization of evidence for informed decision-making and practice. Figure 2 illustrates how the intersection of the identified types of exchange and collaboration can be understood as foundation of local evidence ecosystems, bridging the two distinct systems of academia—focused on generating knowledge—and practice—focused on translating evidence into action. To foster these linkages and address gaps between research generation and application, interviewees highlighted the importance of positions and institutions located at these intersections. Examples include academic health departments, knowledge brokers and bridged professorships, which are also recognized in the literature as valuable approaches to overcoming barriers to research collaboration [26, 38, 66]. Rationales to engage in research collaborations

The main rationale for engaging in academia‒practice collaboration was enhancing the credibility, impact and practical application of research evidence at the local level. Interviewees further emphasized the desire to apply research findings in practice, address PHS-relevant questions and gain practical insights. In particular, the latter was seen as a way to improve the PHS image by familiarizing students and young professionals with its tasks and services.

These rationales align with longstanding calls for stronger collaboration between academia and PHS in Germany, as expressed by the scientific community [31, 67], the PHS [68] and expert committees advising German health policy [32, 33]. However, these calls haven largely emphasized organizational-level benefits, while overlooking individual-level benefits such as career advancement, skill development and academic recognition for the people participating in such collaborations. According to the interviews, giving greater attention to these individual-level rationales could enrich discussions on the broader benefits of institutionalized collaboration. As they are closely tied to motivational aspects, addressing them—for example through stronger incentives, recognition and joint visions—appears to be a promising way to ensure sustainable collaboration, as further discussed in Motivational aspects.

Strategies to overcome key barriers and strengthen local research collaboration

To understand the mechanisms behind sustainable research collaboration at the local level, we used the COM-B model [40] to categorize the factors influencing engagement at both the individual and institutional levels. Across the three core dimensions—capability, opportunity and motivation—we identified ten key barriers and 35 facilitators. These were integrated into the Knowledge Translation and Exchange (KTE) framework developed in this study (Fig. 1). The framework is intended to guide public health professionals from academia and practice through a systematic assessment of barriers and facilitators in their respective settings. By doing so, they can develop strategies tailored to their situation and strengthen EIDM at the local level through bilateral KTE. Conceptualized as a ‘spinning wheel’, the framework can be used to illustrate how facilitators address multiple barriers. It thus offers actionable pathways to bridge gaps between academia and the PHS and provides a dynamic tool for fostering sustainable and effective collaboration.

In the following, we discuss strategies to address the ten key barriers to academia–practice collaborations in Germany’s PHS along the dimensions of capability, opportunity and motivation, situating our findings within the current discourse. Secs. "Added value of the KTE framework" and "Transferability of findings" then examine the added value of the KTE framework and the transferability of the results to other contexts.

Capability aspects

Barriers affecting the ability of individuals and organizations to engage in collaborations included (1) limited human resources, linked to a lack of dedicated staff hours and recruitment challenges in the PHS, as well as a lack of permanent positions in academia and (2) insufficient knowledge, skills and competencies for joint PHS research and KTE (Fig. 1).

To address key barrier (1) human resources shortage within the PHS, the interviewees emphasized the need to diversify the PHS workforce and prioritize competencies over titles. These recommendations align with calls from young professionals and experts [31, 69–73] and reflect similar developments in the United Kingdom. There, public health specialist training was opened to non-medical professionals [74], alongside the introduction of the United Kingdom Public Health Register to ensure professional standards and establish training equivalence [75, 76]. In contrast, public health specialist training in Germany remains restricted to medical professionals, although efforts are underway to develop curricula for training non-medical professionals in essential public health functions [69, 77].

To address key barrier (2), interviewees suggested, in line with the literature, capacity-building initiatives focused on evidence utilization, policy advising and (research) communication [78, 79]. Cross-sector training programs and on-the-job learning were recommended to help professionals navigate both scientific and administrative processes and better understand the different system logics. Such approaches, also endorsed by experts in the field, were seen as crucial for fostering long-term partnerships and mutual understanding [24, 80–82].

As a solution to both capability aspects, the interviewees proposed establishing local PHS research units modeled after academic health departments (AHDs). Developed in the United States in the 1990s, AHDs aim to address systemic challenges by institutionalizing collaboration between academic research and public health practices [17]. According to the literature, their flexible structure enables them to provide practical research opportunities, field experience and workforce development for PHS [26]. In Germany, similar organizational concepts were recommended in 2023 by an expert panel advising the nationwide Public Health Service Reform Pact launched in 2021 [32, 33]. In 2024, Lower Saxony became the first federal state to establish a local AHD [83], with other states planning similar initiatives [84]. Taken together, these developments suggest that AHDs can address systemic barriers at the intersection of workforce development, collaboration and evidence use. While their long-term impact on academia‒practice collaboration in Germany remains to be evaluated, AHDs represent a promising meta-level intervention to foster sustainable and effective partnerships.

Opportunity aspects

Opportunities for individuals and organizations to engage in research collaboration were constrained by four key barriers: (3) bureaucratic hurdles, (4) financial and technical limitations, (5) a lack of relationships and networks and (6) divergent operational modes (Fig. 1).

To address key barrier (3), bureaucratic hurdles, interviewees strongly emphasized the need to streamline administrative processes and to embed research activities into job roles to support EIDM at the local level. While the international literature highlights the benefits of streamlining processes [20, 85], integrating research activities into job profiles has received little attention to date. From an academic perspective, this may appear straightforward, as job postings are often tied to specific research projects. In Germanys PHS, however, such integration is often complicated by structural and legal requirements. With few exceptions, such as health reporting and outbreak investigation, research activities are not a legally defined responsibility of LHAs in Germany [86]. Moreover, job postings and role descriptions must be aligned within administrative hierarchies and comply with collective agreements. Otherwise, positions risk being classified into lower pay grades [87], which was highlighted as undermining the recruitment of qualified staff and potentially threatens the effort-reward balance [88], an important motivational aspect. Overall, these findings indicate that legally anchoring research activities as a core responsibility for public health professionals working at the academia-practice intersection, supported by leadership to that enables a balance between routine operations and research engagement, is essential to strengthening academia-practice collaboration [13, 89–91].

Our findings align with the literature in emphasizing that long-term structural funding is essential to address the resource shortages identified as key barrier (4) [31–33, 67, 92]. In Germany, PHS-related topics, particularly those focusing on the local level, remain underrepresented in academic research because of limited institutional representation and targeted funding [32]. While the German Innovation Fund for Healthcare Development allocateds €200 million annually [93], no comparable funding existed for PHS research. Existing PHS-specific funding opportunities were limited and often topic specific [94–97]. Moreover, interviewees noted that LHAs often lack the capacity and experience to apply for research grants, whereas academic institutions often find the application process cumbersome, especially for smaller, locally focused projects. Taken together, these findings suggest that overcoming funding-related barriers requires significant public investment and the establishment of a supporting infrastructure, including training and institutional resources for both LHAs and academia, as recommended by experts in the field [31].

Our findings indicate that a lack of personal relationships, exchange opportunities and institutionalized collaboration—identified as key barrier (5)—further aggravates the complexity of transferring evidence between academia and practice. Consistent with prior research [10, 35, 89], interviewees emphasized that sustainable collaboration requires moving beyond isolated projects toward institutional anchoring and structured communication channels. Two intermediary approaches appear particularly promising: knowledge brokers and bridged professorships. The former, widely discussed internationally but not yet embedded in Germany’s PHS, are positioned at the interface between academia and practice, where they can align operational modes, translate between academic and practice-oriented language, and facilitate bilateral knowledge transfer [12, 91, 98–101]. Their effectiveness, however, seems contingent on factors such as organizational research culture, trust, role clarity and managerial support [37, 38], highlighting the need for further investigation. Bridged professorships, by contrast, have already been introduced in several German federal states. As joint appointments between universities and health authorities, they aim to foster the exchange of research questions, the co-development of practical solutions and the application of innovative methods [66], while also providing space for critical reflections within health administrations [102]. Given the historical underrepresentation of PHS-relevant research in German academia, recent initiatives to expand bridged professorships signal a shift toward stronger structural integration [103–106]. Yet their reliance on university proximity limits their applicability for smaller, rural LHAs, underlining the importance of additional approaches—such as knowledge brokers—to strengthen collaboration in less well-connected regions.

Within key barrier (6), colliding institutional logics, interviewees identified differing operational modes of academia and PHS as a major cause of friction—an observation consistent with prior research [98, 107, 108]. Academia typically focuses on long-term, systematic research aimed at evidence generation, whereas the PHS prioritizes immediate, actionable outcomes. These differences manifest in priorities (for example, research rigor versus practical applicability), language (for example, academic jargon versus policy communication) and timelines (for example, lengthy research cycles versus rapid responses), complicating sustainable collaboration with the intention of joint generation, synthesis and utilization of actionable evidence [109]. In line with the literature [110, 111], our findings suggest that addressing these tensions requires ongoing alignment of structures and processes, coupled with the development of a mutual understanding and common language to bridge organizational boundaries. As outlined in Types of exchange and collaboration needed, joint capacity-building initiatives appear particular promising in fostering these aspects.

Motivational aspects

The motivation of individuals and organizations to engage in research collaborations was impacted by a broad range of factors, including (7) reward imbalances in CPD, (8) conflicting incentive structures, (9) a weak evidence-informed culture within the PHS and (10) a lack of mutual recognition (Fig. 1). To overcome these key barriers, interviewees recommended systemic changes to foster an environment that values collaboration and supports evidence-informed practice at the local level.

To address key barrier (7), reward imbalances in CPD, interviewees emphasized the need for financial and educational incentives, such as fair remuneration and clear career paths, to motivate individuals and enable organizations to pursue training and engage in academia‒practice collaboration. An imbalance between effort and reward is well known to reduce motivation and job satisfaction [112–114], potentially discouraging engagement in further training as well as research activities beyond daily duties. Accordingly, numerous position papers and expert recommendations call for improved reward structures for both medical and non-medical professionals, along with the development of attractive postgraduate qualification programs [31, 70, 115–118]. While CPD opportunities for medical staff are long established in Germany [119, 120], there is still a lack of standardized CPD formats for other academic professions. A first pilot program, developed, tested and evaluated within a federal funding initiative [121], demonstrated promising approaches in a local academia–practice collaboration [46]. Experiences from the planned scale-up phase remain to be seen.

Within key barrier (8), conflicting incentive structures, interviewees identified academia’s emphasis on high-impact publications and grant acquisition as misaligned with the PHS need for locally relevant, actionable evidence. According to the literature, this misalignment reduces researchers’ motivation to pursue locally focused, rapid-response projects that do not advance academic careers, while discouraging LHA practitioners when research outputs lack timely, actionable insights [107, 108, 122]. To overcome this barrier, interviewees and experts suggested a shift away from the ‘publish and run’ model by prioritizing real-world relevance, rewarding research implementation and recognizing translational tasks as legitimate ‘third missions’ in academia [10, 79, 122].

To address the weak evidence-informed culture within the PHS, identified as key barrier (9), our findings highlight the need to foster an institutional culture that encourages critical discussions and reflective practices—both considered essential for advancing EIDM processes and promoting research collaborations. Participants and organizational behaviour experts emphasized the critical role of leadership in shaping a climate where engagement in research collaborations and evidence-based practice is valued, rewarded and supported [7, 123].

Finally, a lack of mutual recognition and equitable partnerships, identified as key barrier (10), was cited as a major demotivator. Participants emphasized that equal partnerships require mutual recognition and suggested external incentives, such as funding structures requiring active PHS involvement. Yet, while such mechanisms remain under-represented in previous debates and position papers [31, 32], funding requirements alone are unlikely to resolve the deeper challenge of establishing genuine equality and mutual respect in academia‒practice partnerships. As Wright’s participation ladder illustrates, mandating joint grant applications risks tokenistic involvement without meaningful influence [58, 59]. To avoid this, deliberate efforts addressing both extrinsic and intrinsic motivations are necessary to ensure active involvement and shared decision-making. Interviewees stressed that successful partnerships should be grounded in common goals and a shared vision of public health, driven by the intrinsic motivation to ‘make a difference’—a motive also highlighted by young professionals [115]. Interdisciplinary training programs emerged as a promising strategy to cultivate a common identity rooted in the core public health values, which, as supported by the literature, are best acquired through practical training and workplace experiences [24].

Added value of the KTE framework

The KTE framework, developed in this study, provides a structured approach to assessing barriers and facilitators of sustainable collaboration between academia and (local) PHS. In its intended application, the KTE framework addresses an important gap in the extensive KTE literature. A focused body of evidence has examined the use of research evidence in (local) public health decision-making [124–128] and identified facilitators and barriers at both individual and organizational levels [15, 79, 129]. Among others, these studies highlight the importance of local-level transferability, the value of brokered relationships (for example, knowledge brokers or embedded researchers), the need to reform academic incentive structures and the benefits of capacity building for both evidence generators and users. While these insights align closely with our findings, the KTE frameworks adds a distinctive component: it functions as a structured starting point for systematically assessing barriers and enhancing facilitators to derive strategies to strengthen local academia-practice collaboration.

Thereby, the KTE framework also complements existing approaches that promote the uptake of research in policymaking, such as the SPIRIT Action Framework [130], which provides a structured method for selecting and testing strategies to increase evidence use in policy, and the SUPPORT tools [131, 132], which guide policymakers and their advisers in applying evidence in health decisions. Measuring and supporting the use of research in policy is complicated by the heterogeneity of policy processes, the scarcity of relevant evidence and the influence of external factors beyond policymakers’ control [15, 129, 133]. This underscores the importance of assessing not only the engagement with evidence, but also the capacities of individuals and organizations in doing so [12]. Established instruments such as Staff Assessment of enGagement with Evidence (SAGE), Seeking, Engaging with and Evaluating Research (SEER) and Organisational Research Access, Culture and Leadership (ORACLe) [134] offer valuable measures of research engagement and use. However, none systematically analyse barriers specific to academia–practice collaboration. While many studies emphasize the importance of the local level as a key setting for public health operations [100, 135], and others highlight the relevance of collaborative structures to enable bilateral KTE [136–138], there is a lack of research that explicitly addresses this intersection. The KTE framework helps to fill this gap by providing a practice-oriented tool for assessing and addressing collaboration barriers at the local level, thereby supporting more sustainable partnerships between academia and the PHS.

Transferability of findings

Given the twofold nature of our study—both identifying relevant barriers and facilitators of collaboration between academia and local PHS in Germany and developing a framework to systematically assess these determinants, we see opportunities for transferability in both respects.

While some solutions discussed in the context of Germany’s federal public health service system may have limited applicability in more centralized systems, we consider many findings as being likely generalizable. Challenges such as insufficient dedicated staff hours or shortages of qualified personnel are broadly relevant [12, 15, 139]. Even barriers rooted in the strong orientation of the German PHS toward medical professionals, which is rooted in traditional population medicine, echo debates in other countries, such as the United Kingdom, where widening access for academic professions and expanding qualification programs have taken place [75]. Furthermore, countries with devolved or partially devolved local evidence ecosystems have explored approaches such as AHDs and knowledge brokers [36–39], which were also proposed by our study participants. The alignment of these strategies across different settings supports the transferability of our findings to other contexts with comparable systems.

More context-specific and therefore likely less transferable are the bureaucratic hurdles shaped by differing system logics between academia and practice, as well as within the PHS itself, spanning national, state and local levels. To address this, the KTE framework is explicitly designed to capture relevant contextual conditions and system characteristics. We therefore assume that, even if not all barriers are directly transferable across different public health settings, the systematic analysis of potential barriers using the KTE framework will provide a valuable resource for public health professionals engaged in bilateral KTE processes.

Strengths and limitations

Our study provides the first systematic analysis of the barriers and facilitators impacting sustainable research collaboration between academia and PHS in Germany. Its key strengths include the novel approach of contrasting identified barriers with potential solutions on the basis of a deductive-inductive coding method, blending theory-driven and data-driven insights. The application of the COM-B model offers a structured framework to assess how capability, opportunity and motivation influence engagement.

However, several limitations to this work should be noted. First, the findings are based on participants’ self-reported perceptions, without external validation from stakeholders such as local politicians or funding agencies. Nevertheless, the diversity of participants, with experience across different administrative levels, provided a wide range of perspectives and the saturation of the analysis enhanced the relevance of the findings. The study’s integration into the larger EvidenzOEGD research project allowed for valuable feedback from expert discussions and a multi-professional stakeholder workshop [47], improving the validity of the results. Second, data collection occurred during the fifth wave of the COVID-19 pandemic in Germany [140], potentially causing participants to prioritize pandemic-related challenges over long-term collaboration issues. However, most participants saw the pandemic as a window of opportunity to address existing inadequacies and push for PHS research reforms. Finally, as public health researchers, our involvement may have influenced our responses. To minimize bias, we employed interviewers with varied professional backgrounds and levels of expertise, conducted independent double coding and held regular reflexive discussions within the study team and with external experts. While bias cannot be fully ruled out, we believe that these measures ensure the credibility and reliability of the findings.

Conclusions

Sustainable research collaboration between academia and practice is crucial for bridging the knowledge‒action gap and thereby strengthening EIDM. Within this study, we developed a conceptual framework to systemically assess the factors influencing engagement in equitable PHS research collaborations. The proposed KTE framework offers actionable strategies to mitigate capability and opportunity barriers, including developing a well-qualified workforce with dedicated research time, offering on-the-job training, streamlining bureaucratic processes, improving long-term funding mechanisms, strengthening relationships between academia and PHS and facilitating access to research infrastructure and local settings. It also emphasizes the importance of providing financial and educational incentives, ensuring adequate incentive structures, fostering an evidence-informed culture within the PHS and cultivating equitable partnerships on the basis of mutual recognition as vital motivators. Thus, balancing the distinct systems of academia and PHS is crucial, aligning routine responsibilities with research activities in PHS and ensuring adequate incentives for academia to maintain research relevance while addressing both academic and practice needs and goals.

Promising approaches for implementing these strategies include bridging roles, such as knowledge brokers and bridged professorships, and organizational structures such as AHDs. Supported by leadership and integrated into interconnected systems, these strategies can support the development of local evidence ecosystems that support PHS-relevant research and EIDM processes at the local level.

Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of these approaches to improve research collaboration between academia and practice in various contexts to assess their applicability and impact on public health systems. The KTE framework developed in this study provides a solid theoretical foundation for such investigations, particularly for methods that establish and sustain local evidence ecosystems and evaluate proposed solutions.
