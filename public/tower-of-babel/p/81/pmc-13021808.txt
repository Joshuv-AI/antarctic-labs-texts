Multiscale environmental analysis on autotrophic euglenid communities: insights from DNA metabarcoding
Authors: Katarzyna Jankowska, Maja Łukomska-Kowalczyk, Rafał Milanowski, Bożena Zakryś
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFreshwater ponds are increasingly recognized as biodiversity hotspots for microorganisms, yet remain understudied compared to larger aquatic systems. Among protist communities, autotrophic euglenids play a significant role, especially in eutrophic, shallow water bodies. Despite their importance, they are understudied and often overlooked, mostly due to difficult morphological identification and the lack of relevant determination literature, as well as their unusual DNA structure and still unresolved taxonomy. To date, no comprehensive study has used metabarcoding to investigate ecological patterns of euglenids in freshwater ponds. This study employs high-throughput sequencing using euglenid-specific primers targeting the 18S rDNA V2 region, enabling detailed group-focused analysis focused on their ecology. It includes 190 samples from freshwater ponds collected over three years across three regions of Poland. Environmental parameters including physicochemical parameters of water, surrounding land use, temporal variation and spatial distribution were analysed in relation to euglenid read counts. Alpha diversity was assessed using global models, while Canonical Correspondence Analysis was employed to investigate how environmental conditions shape the composition of euglenid communities. Our findings highlight ecological variability within the group and shows the importance of species-specific approaches in studies of microbial ecology. This study provides the first integrative framework combining metabarcoding and ecological data to determine the drivers of euglenid diversity in freshwater pond ecosystems.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00248-026-02721-6.Keywords: Autotrophic euglenids, Biodiversity, Freshwater ecosystems, Protists, Shallow water bodies, V2 18S rDNA metabarcoding

Introduction

Although freshwater represents only a small fraction of Earth’s total water – less than 1%, compared to 97% in saltwater – freshwater bodies contain a vast diversity of microorganisms [1], play a vital role in the water cycle, and serve as crucial indicators of environmental change [2, 3]. In recent years, scientists have shown growing interest in the diversity of unicellular organisms inhabiting freshwater, and their ecological importance is gaining wider recognition. As a result, the structure and dynamics of freshwater microbial communities – especially in small, lentic ponds [4] – along with their high species richness, are becoming increasingly important subjects of research [5, 6].

A significant component of these freshwater ecosystems is a common, cosmopolitan, yet understudied group of protists: the autotrophic euglenids. The literature on this group in ecological context remains limited [7–9]. However, the rapid advancement of molecular techniques, integrated with morphological observations, has significantly improved euglenid taxonomy [10]. Emerging studies are substantially enriching sequence databases, enhancing our understanding of phylogenetic relationships within the entire group, while species delimitation within the Lepocinclis Perty, Phacus Dujardin, Discoplastis Triemer, and Strombomonas Deflandre genera among others is shedding new light on euglenid taxonomy [11–16].

Along with the growing understanding of the significance and specific taxonomic relationships within euglenids, their biotechnological potential has become increasingly apparent across the health, energy and food sectors [17]. These organisms have shown promising prospects in water quality monitoring, as a nutritional source and for tolerance to heavy metals, while also creating potential threats such as toxic blooms or the presence of parasitic species [18–21]. For instance, euglenid blooms occurrence is well-documented and reported in numerous studies over the decades [22–26] and can have substantial impact on the environment, including ecosystem instability and mass fish mortality [27]. Conversely, certain species forming dense red blooms such as Euglena sanguinea Ehrenberg produce euglenophycin, a toxin that has demonstrated potential anti-cancer properties [28]. The potential usefulness of euglenids proves that the comprehensive understanding of their diversity and ecology is necessary in order to manage their harmful properties, as well as fully exploit their biotechnological benefits.

Given the need to collect comprehensive data on the environment in which euglenids appear, Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) techniques, particularly metabarcoding [29], appear promising for addressing their biodiversity [30]. However, existing metabarcoding studies tend to underrepresent euglenids [31–33]. This underrepresentation is attributed to the highly variable and long 18S rDNA fragment, a common target for eukaryotic microorganism studies. To address this challenge, finding a more optimal region of the DNA for the identification of euglenid species was essential. After comparison, the V2-V3 and V4 regions of 18S rDNA emerged as promising candidates based on their ability to amplify euglenid species [34]. Although the V4 region is commonly used in protist biodiversity studies, it was found to be unsuitable for euglenids due to the excessive length of their sequences, which prevents effective amplification with short-read sequencing platforms like Illumina MiSeq [35]. Ultimately, the V2 region was selected as the optimal barcode. Combined with improvements in biomass harvesting, DNA isolation, and amplification techniques [36], this approach resulted in robust and reliable amplification of euglenid sequences [35]. A comprehensive survey was conducted across 16 small eutrophic waterbodies located in three regions of Poland, involving thorough sampling and detailed assessment of euglenid species. The ponds varied in size, trophic status, surrounding land use, and general environmental conditions, providing a diverse range of habitats. These contrasting characteristics contributed to the high species richness observed – some sites yielded over 100 distinct euglenid taxa over the three-year sampling period. This paper builds upon and complements the study by Jankowska et al. [35] and focuses on the biodiversity and distribution of autotrophic euglenids together providing a comprehensive overview of their ecology.

Given the described tools, we aimed to investigate the following aspects of autotrophic euglenid ecology:

spatial and temporal dynamics of euglenid communities,

Materials and methods

Sampling overview

Sample collection was carried out across 16 freshwater sites located in three regions of Poland (Fig. 1; Table S1). Sampling was conducted during the euglenid vegetation season with four sampling events (May to October) over a three-year period (2017–2019), which resulted in a total of 192 samples. Details regarding measurements of water parameters, sampling strategy and subsequent laboratory workflow are described in Jankowska et al. [35] and summarized in Fig. 2.

Map of the sampling sites and main study areas in three regions of Poland: Masovia (yellow), Masuria (green), and Pomerania (blue). Sampling points are indicated and labelled with their respective names. The inset shows the location of Poland within Europe

Schematic overview of the methodological workflow, including measurements of water parameters and laboratory procedures leading to metabarcoding data output

In the previous work, studied water bodies were classified by type (park pond, village pond, farm pond, field pond) [35]. To improve accuracy of their description, we additionally categorized the sites based on their surroundings, size and trophic state (Table S1). Pond surroundings were classified based on the dominant land cover type in the immediate vicinity of each sampling site. For each site, a 100-m buffer was created, and the proportion of different land cover types within that buffer was quantified using orthophotomaps from the Polish Geoportal (https://www.geoportal.gov.pl/pl/dane/ortofotomapa-orto/). To summarize variation in land cover composition across sites, we performed non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity. The analysis was conducted in R version 4.5.1 [37] using the metaMDS() function from the vegan package [38], with sites treated as observations and land cover types as variables. From the resulting ordination, we extracted NMDS coordinates for both sites and land cover types. We then calculated the Euclidean distance between each site and each land cover type in the NMDS space. Each site was assigned to the land cover type to which it was closest, provided the distance was less than 0.3. Sites that were not within this threshold for any single land cover type were classified as mosaic. This approach resulted in four final pond surroundings categories: grassland, cropland, urban, and mosaic. In addition to pond surroundings, site size was characterized by surface area and classified into small (< 1,000 m2), medium (1,000–10,000 m2), and large (> 10,000 m2). The trophic state of each water body was estimated using the mean Carlson’s trophic state index (TSI), calculated from all phosphorus (P) measurements collected at that site [39]. Based on the range of TSI values in our dataset (66 to 102), we classified the sites into three categories: eutrophic (TSI < 80), moderately hypereutrophic (80 ≤ TSI < 90), and highly hypereutrophic (TSI ≥ 90). As there is no universally accepted classification for such high TSI values, these thresholds were defined specifically for this study to reflect the distribution of our data (Table S1).

Extraction and amplification of DNA

DNA extraction and amplification were performed according to the protocols described in Jankowska et al. [35]. The 18S rDNA V2 region was targeted using specifically designed primers as in Gumińska et al. [36] (see Fig. 2, Table S2).

Sequencing and data processing

Sequencing was done in external facility (Genomed, Warsaw, Poland) using the Illumina MiSeq platform with 2 × 300 bp paired-end reads. Raw reads can be found in SRA database (NCBI) with PRJNA1020135 accession number [35].

Initial data processing steps were performed using cutadapt tool and DADA2 plugin in QIIME2 once, as described in Jankowska et al. [35]. They included demultiplexing, trimming (to a final amplicon length of 293 bp), quality filtering, denoising and chimera removal, which resulted in obtaining amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). A correction for migration of reads between samples was applied. The total number of identified ASVs reached 5211. After quality control and filtering to include only ASVs classified as Euglenida, 4418 of them remained. Taxonomic classification was conducted using trained machine learning naïve Bayes classifier (confidence = 0.7, classify-sklearn: Qiime2). Later ASVs were agglomerated at the species level using tax_glom function from the phyloseq package [40], resulting in species-level operational taxonomic units (OTUs).

The database used for taxonomy assignment was created with the SILVA_132_rep_set_all_99 database [41]. The sequences from the Euglenaceae, Eutreptiales and Phacaceae families were removed from the dataset and replaced with 940 verified 18S rDNA euglenid sequences representing 260 species. The sequences were then trimmed with the primers used for the amplification of the barcodes. The database is available online with the third version created for this paper (10.18150/9MKYCN). We have also treated as species: 22 Trachelomonas Ehrenberg strains in spite of them being delimited solely based on molecular data with an unknown morphology (labelled Trachelomonas sp.1-sp.22), one Cryptoglena Ehrenberg strain (labelled Cryptoglena sp.1), one Euglena Ehrenberg strain (labelled Euglena sp.1) and one Eutreptia Perty strain (labelled Eutreptia sp.1), as well as one Lepocinclis variety [Lepocinclis oxyuris var. helicoidea (C.Bernard) Zakryś & Chaber]. For the loricate taxa, we follow names that have been verified through recent taxonomic revision [42].

The data processing concluded with rarefaction to a minimum depth of 22,938 reads. All downstream analyses were conducted on these rarefied data. Read counts are presented in Table S3.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis (starting with rarefaction of reads) was conducted using R programme (version 4.5.1) [36]. Data was imported using the qiime2R package [43]. Microbial data processing was handled using the phyloseq [40] and speedyseq [44] packages. Data manipulation and visualization were performed using the tidyverse packages [45], including ggplot2, dplyr and others. For statistical modeling, linear mixed models were implemented using glmmTMB [46], with model diagnostics via DHARMa [47], model selection using MuMIn [48], and post hoc comparisons through emmeans [49], multcomp [50] and multcompview [51], and visualization of predicted effects with ggeffects [52]. Collinearity was assessed using VIFs calculated with the car package [53]. Ordination and community analyses were performed with vegan [38]. Community structure differences were further explored with pairwise PERMANOVA tests using the pairwiseAdonis package [54]. Indicator species were identified using the indicspecies package [55]. Maps were created using rnaturalearth [56], rnaturalearthdata [57], and sf [58] packages. Additional packages used in the study include corrplot [59], ggthemes [60], ggrepel [61], reshape2 [62], e1071 [63], viridis [64], patchwork [65] and cowplot [66]. Final graphical adjustments of figures were performed using Inkscape (v. 0.91).

Due to the specific requirements of the conducted statistical analyses, NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻ + NO₂⁻ and Mn measurements were removed from the dataset, as they contained a high proportion of zero values. In addition, temperature data were excluded due to noticeable sampling time-dependent variation.

Alpha diversity metrics were calculated from metabarcoding data, including overall taxonomic richness (number of taxa detected per sample), genus-specific richness, and genus-level abundance (read counts) (Table S3). Alpha diversity was quantified using the Observed metric from the phyloseq package, which represents species richness based on the number of unique taxa detected per sample. Shannon diversity measure was also calculated and taken into consideration. However, since the data is focused on a single taxonomic group (Euglenida), variation in evenness is less informative than variation in overall richness. Thus, we focused on species richness as a more interpretable alpha diversity measure.

To address multicollinearity among the environmental parameters, we initially assessed pairwise correlations using the corrplot function in R based on samples with complete data (n = 151). Variables with Pearson correlation coefficients above 0.8 were considered highly collinear and candidates for removal. Based on this criterion, total phosphorus (P) and PO₄3⁻ was excluded from further analysis due to its strong correlations with other phosphorus fractions (e.g., dissolved and particulate P), which were retained to enable more specific interpretation. As mentioned total phosphorus is also the parameter used to calculate the TSI. Additionally, we calculated a carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C:N ratio) from organic carbon (OC) and total nitrogen (N) concentrations, which replaced the individual variables in further analyses. This transformation reduced multicollinearity and provided a more integrated view of nutrient availability.

To evaluate how various environmental and sampling factors influenced taxonomic richness, we used a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a negative binomial distribution (nbinom2) and a log link, fitted using the glmmTMB package, to account for overdispersion in the richness data. Preliminary analyses indicated that sampling site was the factor explaining the largest portion of variation in alpha diversity. To quantify this, a GLMM with a negative binomial distribution was fitted, with site as a fixed effect and year as a covariate. The full model (site + year) had the lowest AIC (1360.9) and the highest R2 (0.48), indicating the best fit. A model with site as the only predictor explained nearly as much variance (R2 = 0.45; AIC = 1365.5), whereas a model with year as the only predictor accounted for minimal variation (R2 = 0.03; AIC = 1443.3). Although year explained little variance, its inclusion slightly improved model performance. Based on these results, site was treated as a random effect in all models evaluating other variables (e.g., region, trophic state, pond surroundings) to account for site-level variation while focusing on other predictors. Model fit was assessed using residual plots and overdispersion tests. Each explanatory variable (sampling site, pond surroundings, trophic state, site size, season, and year) was assessed in a separate model. As mentioned, for models testing region, season, trophic state, site size and pond surroundings, site was included as a random effect to account for repeated sampling at the same locations over time. Year was included as a fixed effect to control for inter-annual variation, as it had too few levels to be modelled reliably as a random effect [67]. When site was the main explanatory variable, it was modelled as a fixed effect to compare differences between specific sites, with year included as a fixed effect, but without including site as a random effect. For models assessing the effect of year, year was modelled as a fixed effect without additional control variables, and site was included as a random effect to account for spatial structure. This modelling approach allowed us to isolate the effects of each factor while appropriately accounting for the hierarchical and temporal structure of the data. To assess the contribution of each predictor, three models were compared: one including both site and year, and two simpler models including only site or only year. Model comparisons were performed using AIC and likelihood ratio tests (LRT) to determine the best-fitting model [68].

We estimated marginal means (EMMs) using the emmeans() function on the response scale (type = "response"), allowing richness to be presented in its original count units for all predictor models. Pairwise contrasts were computed as ratios on this scale. Pairwise comparisons among all levels of each predictor were adjusted using Tukey’s method to control the family-wise error rate for multiple testing. To summarize group differences, we used Compact Letter Displays (CLDs) generated by the cld() function based on Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons (α = 0.05).

To investigate the relationships between species richness and the physico-chemical parameters of water, we again applied generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) using the glmmTMB package in R. We specified random intercept for site and treated year as a covariate in the same way as in previous mixed models. Predictor selection was conducted using dredge() on the full GLMM fixed-effect structure, and model importance was evaluated using summed Akaike weights. Then we applied the ggpredict() function which generates adjusted predictions for each predictor while holding other variables constant at their mean values. All environmental variables were standardized (scaled to mean = 0 and standard deviation = 1) prior to analysis.

For the richness models, multicollinearity among environmental predictors was assessed using generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF), calculated from a linear model that reflected the fixed-effects structure of the full GLMM. All GVIF values (computed using the car::vif() function in R, based on the complete set of ten environmental variables) were below 10, indicating acceptable collinearity.

Beta diversity was investigated using Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA) [69], based on a subset of five environmental variables: pH, electrical conductivity, carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (C:N ratio), dissolved organic carbon (DOC), and the TSI. CCA was selected as the ordination method due to the long gradient length (~ 7.6) observed in the data, which indicates unimodal species responses along environmental gradients. Selection of final predictors was based on the combination of ecological relevance and statistical contribution (indicated by ChiSquare and F-values) from a full model including complete set of 10 environmental variables. Although all variables were statistically significant, those with the strongest explanatory power and ecological interpretability were retained. Multicollinearity was assessed using the vif.cca() function from the vegan package. All VIF values were below 5, confirming low collinearity among the selected predictors. Log transformation (log(x + 1)) was applied to sequence read counts via decostand() function from the vegan package to reduce the influence of large values and skewed distribution. The association between species composition and environmental gradients was assessed using permutation-based ANOVA (999 permutations), applied to the CCA model. The explanatory power of the model was quantified using the coefficient of determination (as computed with RsquareAdj()). In addition, p-values for each environmental variable were obtained to evaluate their individual significance within the constrained ordination.

Ellipses on the CCA plots represent 90% confidence intervals based on a multivariate t-distribution. This level was chosen to reduce visual overlap and highlight trends in group dispersion. During the calculation of robust covariance estimates (used for ellipse fitting), a convergence warning was issued due to the presence of an extreme but valid outlier. To assess its influence, we repeated the analysis with the point removed. The overall pattern and structure of the results remained qualitatively similar, indicating that the outlier did not substantially affect interpretation. Consequently, the full dataset, including the outlier, was retained for final analyses to preserve data completeness.

To assess the impact of rarefaction, we compared ordinations with unrarefied, Hellinger-transformed counts using a Procrustes rotation. The ordinations were highly congruent (Procrustes correlation = 0.956, p = 0.001), indicating that rarefaction did not alter overall ecological patterns. Therefore, all CCA results presented are based on the rarefied data, ensuring consistency across analyses.

In parallel, total species richness, genus-specific species richness, and the proportional abundance of individual genera relative to the total community in each sample were calculated. For loricate taxa, numbers of OTUs for Strombomonas and Trachelomonas were summed, as Strombomonas is species-poor and generally rare; considered alone, its influence might not be significant. These variables were subsequently passively projected onto the CCA ordination using the envfit() function from the vegan package [38]. The strength and significance of environmental vectors fitted using envfit() were assessed with permutation-based correlation tests, which produced R2 values and associated p-values for each variable.

We assessed the effect of categorical environmental variables (site, region, pond surroundings, season, site size, trophic state, and year) on community composition using Pairwise permutational multivariate analyses of variance (PERMANOVA), implemented via the adonis2 function from the vegan package. Distance matrices were computed from log-transformed sequence read counts (as described for CCA model) using the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity. Each variable was tested separately. To verify the assumption of homogeneity of multivariate dispersion, we applied the betadisper function, followed by a permutation-based ANOVA (anova) to test for significant differences in within-group dispersion. For all categorical variables, we then performed pairwise PERMANOVA comparisons using the pairwise.adonis2 function with 999 permutations to identify specific group pairs with significantly different community structures. To control for multiple testing, p-values from these pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

Indicator species analysis was conducted on metabarcoding data from 190 samples using multi-level pattern analysis (multipatt() function from the indicspecies package). The analysis aimed to identify indicator species across seven spatial, temporal and environmental categories: region, site, season, year, trophic state, site size, and pond surroundings. We used duleg = TRUE option to report only single-group indicator species, ensuring clearer ecological interpretation and avoiding mixed-group associations. Significance testing was based on 999 permutations. The indicator value statistic (stat) was calculated using the IndVal.g method [55], which combines species specificity (A) and fidelity (B) as their geometric mean. Only species with a p-value < 0.05 and an overall statistic > 0.6 were retained for interpretation. For each category, the three species with the highest stat values were selected, while the species with the highest specificity (A) and fidelity (B) were retained individually. In cases where two species had identical or nearly identical A or B values, both were included.

Results

General trends in community structure

Across the 190 studied samples, a total of 198 species belonging to 13 genera were identified.

Richness of euglenid species ranged from 3 to 64 species, with a mean of 26.1 (SD = 11.4) and a median of 24, with the interquartile range (IQR) equal to 14. The distribution of richness was moderately right-skewed (skewness = 0.84) (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). These values indicate considerable variation in species richness with a few samples showing particularly high euglenid diversity, reflecting the heterogeneity of the studied environments.

The rarefaction curves for majority of the samples reached the asymptotes well before the applied sequencing depth threshold, indicating that the chosen sequencing depth (22,938) has captured the majority of present species (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

To explore how environmental factors structure euglenid communities, we analysed species composition using CCA and fitted genera-level richness and relative abundance with envfit (Fig. 3).

As a result, the overall CCA model was significant based on permutation tests (p = 0.001, Table S4). The first four constrained axes were statistically significant (p = 0.001 for each, Table S5), with CCA1 explaining the largest portion of the constrained variation (4.48%) followed by CCA2 (2.27%) resulting in a cumulative constrained variance of 6.75% (Table S6).

Overall, the CCA model explained 11% of the total variation in species composition, with an adjusted R2 of 7.8%, indicating that the measured environmental variables moderately accounted for the observed community changes (Table S4).

All environmental variables included in the analysis were significant (p = 0.001 for all in permutation test). The variable contributing most to the explained variation was conductivity (constrained inertia = 0.16), followed by the TSI (constrained inertia = 0.14) (Table S4). Their importance was also reflected by their relatively long vector lengths (conductivity = 0.77, TSI = 0.80), indicating strong directional influence on community structure (Table S7). The remaining variables presented similar, moderate constrained inertia (0.10 for pH, 0.11 for DOC and 0.12 for C:N ratio, Table S4). As for the vector length, C:N ratio presented the highest value (0.97), while the values for pH and DOC were lower (0.54 and 0.69, respectively) (Table S7). When tested individually, each variable also explained a significant proportion of the variation (p = 0.001), with R2 values ranging from 0.018 (pH) to 0.039 (C:N ratio) (Table S4).

Envfit analysis was used to examine the relationships between species richness and relative abundances of specific genera with the community ordination derived from the CCA. The most of the richness metrics for particular genera (e.g., Euglena richness) as well as their relative abundances showed significant correlations with the ordination axes, indicating that these biological variables reflect important ecological gradients captured by the environmental variables (Table S8). Specifically, species richness calculated for genera separately showed significant association with the community structure for 10 out of 12 measured variables with R2 values ranging from 0.05 (Phacus species) to 0.41 (combined Trachelomonas and Strombomonas species). As for the relative abundance of specific genera, the analysis resulted in 8 out of 12 significant variables, presenting numbers from 0.6 for Euglenaformis M.S.Bennett & Triemer abundance to 0.29 for combined Trachelomonas and Strombomonas abundance (Table S9).

The vectors displayed in the ordination plot indicate that several genera may be linked to specific environmental gradients in the studied waterbodies. The number of species from specific genera in the sample shows associations with various environmental parameters, although in many cases, these relationships are not clearly defined. Species richness of Flexiglena Zakryś & Łukomska, Euglenaformis, Lepocinclis, Phacus and Cryptoglena is positively correlated with a higher carbon-to-nitrogen (C:N) ratio and lower pH (Fig. 3A). In contrast, Colacium Ehrenberg species richness appears to be associated with lower C:N ratios and higher pH. A greater number of Euglenaria Karnkowska, E.W.Linton & Kwiatowski and Discoplastis species is typically observed in samples with higher TSI and conductivity, whereas lower values of these parameters are generally associated with more species from the combined Trachelomonas and Strombomonas group. Additionally, elevated levels of DOC are linked to greater species richness in Euglena. In most cases (except for Flexiglena, Colacium and combined group of Trachelomonas and Strombomonas, there appears to be no direct correlation between the number of species and the abundance of distinct genera. As illustrated in Fig. 3B, the relative abundance of Flexiglena increases in samples with a high carbon-to-nitrogen (C:N) ratio and low pH. In contrast, the abundance of Colacium, Monomorphina Mereschkowsky, and Discoplastis tends to be associated with higher pH and lower C:N ratios. Furthermore, the abundance of Euglena and Euglenaria shows a positive correlation with elevated conductivity and TSI, while negative correlation was observed for the abundance of Euglenaformis, as well as the combined abundance of Trachelomonas and Strombomonas.

Notably, the abundance vectors for Lepocinclis and Phacus were not significant, even though both genera are represented by high read counts. This suggests that their lack of significance cannot be attributed to low number of reads, but may instead indicate a genuinely weak or absent relationship between their abundances and the environmental gradients captured in the ordination.

Indicator species analysis, conducted across seven categories, identified the top single-group indicators, considering species with p < 0.05 and stat > 0.6. The “mosaic” pond-surroundings group was excluded to maintain interpretive clarity. In result, total of 109 indicator species met the selection criteria, with the highest number of significant indicator species found at the individual site level (n = 51), followed by region (n = 22), indicating strong spatial structuring of species distributions. Among environmental conditions, trophic state (n = 17) and pond surroundings (n = 10) supported a substantial number of indicators, suggesting that nutrient availability and the characteristics of surrounding habitats are important determinants of species composition. Fewer indicators were associated with site size (n = 8), while temporal variables such as season and year yielded only one and zero indicator species, respectively, implying temporal community stability (Fig. 4, Tables S10-11).

Spatial patterns

Region

Species richness varied significantly across the geographical regions of Poland included in this study (LRT = 10.81, df = 2, p = 0.004 in comparison with null model, Table S12).

The highest average richness was recorded in Masuria, followed by Masovia, while the lowest values were observed in Pomerania, with median values reflecting the same pattern. Despite having fewer samples compared to Pomerania and Masovia (48 vs. 71 for both), Masuria exhibited the highest total species richness (151 species), whereas Pomerania had the lowest (136 species) (Table 1, Fig. 5A).

The model assessing variation in species richness included region as the main fixed effect and year as a covariate, with site specified as a random effect to account for local variation. The marginal R2 of the model was 0.244, indicating that region and year together explained approximately 24% of the variance in richness, while the conditional R2 increased to 0.419 when accounting for site-level random effects (Table S13).

The lowest modelled richness levels were observed in Pomerania, followed closely by Masovia, with both regions grouped together in the CLD analysis ("a"), indicating no significant difference between them. In contrast, Masuria exhibited substantially higher richness, forming a distinct group ("b") and differing significantly from both Pomerania and Masovia (Fig. 5A, Table 1). Specifically, richness in Masuria was 1.67 times higher than in Pomerania (p < 0.001) and 1.47 times higher than in Masovia (p = 0.01) (Table S14).

Overall, both observed and modelled data reveal significant geographical patterns in species richness, with Masurian sites exhibiting notably higher alpha diversity compared to Pomerania and Masovia.

The species composition of autotrophic euglenids in the studied samples differ between the three regions of Poland included in the study. Based on PERMANOVA, region accounted for a modest but statistically significant portion of community variation (R2 = 0.110, p < 0.001). Environmental differences likely contribute to these patterns. The Masurian region, characterized by a higher carbon-to-nitrogen (C:N) ratio and lower pH, showed a higher number of species in the genera Flexiglena, Euglenaformis, Lepocinclis, Phacus, Cryptoglena, and summed Trachelomonas and Strombomonas, as well as increased relative abundance of Flexiglena, Euglenaformis, and summed Trachelomonas and Strombomonas (Fig. 6A, 7A). In Pomerania, a greater number of Colacium species was observed, along with a higher abundance of Colacium and Monomorphina. These patterns may be associated with the region’s higher pH and lower C:N ratio. The Masovian region can be characterized by the elevated levels of DOC, conductivity, and TSI, coinciding with a higher percentage of Euglena and Euglenaria. Additionally, more species were recorded from these genera, along with species from Discoplastis.

Regional comparison of indicator species highlighted pronounced taxonomic differences, identifying 22 taxa with strong indicator values (stat > 0.6, p < 0.05), with Masovia supporting six indicator species, Masuria 15, and Pomerania one (Table S10, Fig. 4). Masuria had the highest number of exclusive indicator taxa, suggesting a distinct and relatively consistent species composition. This pattern is further supported by the top indicators: Lepocinclis gracillicauda (Deflandre) Zakryś & Łukomska, Trachelomonas compacta Middelhoek, and Lepocinclis fominii (Y.V.Roll) Zakryś & Łukomska, all specific to the Masuria region. Additionally, species showing perfect exclusivity were Lepocinclis pseudofominii Zakryś & Łukomska and Trachelomonas nepos (Dreżepolski) Zakryś, Fells & Łukomska (= T. zuberi var. nepos Dreżepolski) for Masuria, and the species exhibiting the highest fidelity was Flexiglena variabilis (G.A.Klebs) Zakryś & Łukomska, occurring consistently across Masurian sites. These patterns emphasize the distinctiveness of the Masurian assemblage of euglenids relative to other regions, as well as more generalised species composition in Pomerania, which is consistent with its intermediate ecological role, as supported by other results presented.

Sampling site

Alpha diversity measure revealed substantial variation in richness across the sampling sites (Table S12). Species counts per sample ranged from a minimum of three species (recorded in individual samples from PO_Jec and PO_Kop) to a maximum of 64 species in a single sample from MU_Tal. Site-level mean richness ranged from 14.75 species at MO_Ceg to 38.5 species at MU_Tal (Table S17). Similarly, the site-level median richness was lowest at MO_Ceg and highest at MU_Ur19, indicating differences not only in average richness but also in variability among sites. When considering total species richness per site (the number of unique species recorded across all samples collected at each location) values ranged from 38 to 106 species, with the highest richness observed at MU_Mik and the lowest at MO_Ceg (Fig. 8). These findings indicate that certain sites consistently support more diverse communities, while other sites exhibit lower and less variable richness.

As illustrated in the ordination plot (Fig. 9), several environmental parameters appear to be associated with species composition across the different water bodies. In particular, site MU_Ur19 is characterized by a relatively high C:N ratio and low pH, conditions that occur with an elevated species richness of genera such as Flexiglena, Euglenaformis, Lepocinclis, Phacus, Cryptoglena and the combined taxa of Trachelomonas and Strombomonas. By contrast, at site PO_RMl the C:N ratio is comparatively low while pH is higher which corresponds with enhanced richness of Colacium. Moreover, other environmental variables – DOC, conductivity, and TSI – increase in the direction of sites MO_Ceg and MO_IK1, which appears to be related to higher richness of Euglena and Discoplastis.

The number of indicator species per site (based on the threshold of stat > 0.6 and p < 0.05) varied considerably, with MU_Ur19 presenting the highest number (n = 15), followed by MO_INo (n = 6), MO_IK1 and PO_RMl (n = 5 each) and MU_Gra (n = 4) (Table S11, Fig. 4). Other sites had only one to three indicator taxa that met this criterion. The site PO_Per was excluded from the stat > 0.6 analysis as none of its significant indicators exceeded this threshold.

Several species were identified as strong indicators of particular sites. Discoplastis gasterosteus (Skuja) Zakryś & Łukomska from PO_RMl exhibited the highest overall association, with high specificity and perfect fidelity (Table S11). Trachelomonas manschurica Skvortzov and Trachelomonas charkowiensis Svirenko, both from MU_Ur19, showed strong site associations, with manschurica consistently present across all replicates. In PO_RMl, Lepocinclis tripteris (Dujardin) B.Marin & M.Melkonian and Discoplastis adunca (J.Schiller) Triemer also demonstrated strong indicator values, both with perfect fidelity, contributing to their high overall association with this site.

The highest specificity was found for Phacus anacoelus A.Stokes in association to MO_IK1, Trachelomonas glebis Zakryś, Fells & Łukomska (= T. granulata Svirenko) to MO_INo, and Phacus smulkowskianus (Zakryś) W.-H.Kusber to PO_Ryb1, all presenting perfect scores and indicating their exclusive occurrence at these sites. In total, nine species displayed perfect fidelity across the sites. Besides the ones included as species presenting highest stat values, this group contains taxa such as Euglena geniculata Dujardin (MO_Ceg), Phacus mangini M.Lefèvre (MO_IK1), and Euglena pseudoviridis Chadefaud (MO_IK1), reflecting highly consistent presence across all replicates. Despite not being a universal category for indicator species analysis, site-level approach allowed the identification of species restricted to specific ponds, with some sites exhibiting multiple strong indicator species.

Temporal patterns

Season

Seasonal differences in species richness were minimal and statistically non-significant in this study (Table S12). Mean and median richness were comparable across spring, summer and fall (Table 1). Total richness was also consistent: 167 species in summer, 164 in fall, and 161 in spring. Notably, despite the larger number of samples collected in summer, total richness did not increase substantially, suggesting that most taxa were already captured with a smaller sampling effort, or that summer communities may contain fewer unique species overall (Table 1, Fig. 5B).

The mixed-effects model including season as a fixed effect, year as a covariate, and site as a random effect resulted in no significant influence of season on richness, with a very low marginal R2 (0.028; Table S13). Estimated marginal means of richness across seasons were nearly identical and no pairwise contrasts were significant (Table 1, Table S14). The Compact Letter Display grouped all seasons together ("a"), confirming the absence of meaningful seasonal variation (Fig. 5B), which is also showed by pairwise comparisons. Overall, these results could indicate that local alpha diversity remained consistent throughout the vegetative season, with no pronounced dynamics.

Season explained a small fraction of the variance in species composition (R2 = 0.017), and this effect was not statistically significant (p = 0.153; Table S15). Dispersion did not differ among seasons. The CCA plot shows strong overlap of ellipses for all seasons, consistent with the no significant differences in community composition indicated by the PERMANOVA (Fig. 6B, 7B). Given that neither the effect size nor any pairwise differences were significant, detailed pairwise comparisons were not conducted. These results indicate minimal or no seasonal effect on beta diversity in the studied.

Seasonal variation had a limited influence on the distribution of indicator species, with only a single taxon showing strong indicator value (stat > 0.6, p < 0.05) (Table S10, Fig. 4). Trachelomonas sp. 11 was identified as characteristic of summer, showing moderate consistency within summer samples but low exclusivity to this season.

Year

Alpha diversity varied moderately across the three sampling years, although overall model support for a year effect was weak (Table S12). Mean and median richness was highest in 2017, and lower in both 2018 and 2019 (Table 1). Median richness values followed a similar trend. Total species richness was also slightly higher in 2017 (171 species) compared to 2018 (162) and 2019 (157), although differences were relatively small (Fig. 5C).

The mixed model assessing year effects included year as a fixed factor and site as a random effect. The marginal R2 was low (0.025), indicating that year explained just 2.5% of the variance in richness, while the conditional R2 was substantially higher (0.422), highlighting strong site-level variation (Table S13). Estimated marginal means (EMMs) of richness on the response scale revealed modest but significant interannual differences. Specifically, richness was significantly higher in 2017 compared to both 2018 and 2019 while 2018 and 2019 did not differ from each other. Pairwise contrasts confirmed these patterns, and CLD groupings reflected the same results, with 2017 in group "b" and 2018–2019 in group "a" (Fig. 5C, Table 1, Table S14). These results suggest that local richness was higher in 2017, before declining to similar levels in subsequent years.

Year explained a small but significant fraction of variation (R2 = 0.019, p = 0.038), while dispersion was not significantly different among years (Table S15). This suggests subtle temporal shifts in community composition across the sampling years, without differences in variability among groups influencing the result. Pairwise comparisons revealed no significant differences (Table S16). The CCA plot shows strong overlap of ellipses for all years, with a slight shift in the 2017 ellipse, consistent with the minimal differences detected by the PERMANOVA (Fig. 6C, 7C). Although year was identified as a significant factor influencing beta diversity, its overall effect size was too small to reveal clear relationships with environmental variables or biological patterns such as the abundance or richness of specific genera.

Indicator species analysis did not reveal significant differentiation among years, with no taxa meeting the thresholds for strong indicator values (stat > 0.6, p < 0.05).

Environmental patterns

Trophic state

Species richness showed a marginal association with trophic state across the study sites (LRT = 4.99, df = 2, p = 0.083, Table S12). Although the effect did not reach statistical significance at the model level, clear trends in both raw richness and estimated marginal means point to biologically meaningful differences. The highest average richness and median was observed in eutrophic ponds, followed by moderately hypereutrophic ponds (TSI 80–90), while the lowest richness was recorded in highly hypereutrophic ponds (TSI > 90) (Table 1). Despite having the smallest number of samples (n = 46), eutrophic ponds harboured high total richness (154 species), nearly matching moderately hypereutrophic ponds (n = 84, 155 species), and exceeding highly hypereutrophic ponds (n = 60, 132 species) (Fig. 5D, Table 1).

The generalized linear mixed model, which included trophic state as a fixed effect and year as a covariate, with site as a random effect, had a marginal R2 of 0.141, indicating that trophic state and year together explained ~ 14% of the variance in richness (Table S13). Estimated marginal means of richness adjusted for year further supported this trend: eutrophic ponds had the highest estimated richness, moderate hypereutrophic ponds were intermediate, and highly hypereutrophic ponds the lowest (Table 1).

While the overall model was not statistically significant, compact letter display grouped highly hypereutrophic ponds (TSI > 90) into category "a", moderately hypereutrophic ponds (TSI 80–90) into "ab", and eutrophic ponds into "b", suggesting overlapping but distinct richness patterns. Pairwise comparisons revealed a statistically significant difference only between eutrophic and highly hypereutrophic ponds (ratio = 1.48, p = 0.047), while other comparisons were non-significant. These findings suggest that while trophic state may not be a strong predictor of richness at the model level, eutrophic conditions appear to support significantly greater alpha diversity than more heavily enriched systems (Fig. 5D, Table 1, Table S14).

Trophic state explained 10% of the variation in community composition (p < 0.001), but significant differences in dispersion were also detected, indicating heterogeneous within-group variability (Table S15). Pairwise comparisons further revealed that eutrophic ponds significantly differed from both highly hypereutrophic and moderately hypereutrophic ponds, while there was also a smaller but significant difference between the two hypereutrophic categories (Table S16). The observed separation among trophic categories aligns with the CCA ordination and overlaid ellipses, which suggest that highly hypereutrophic ponds and eutrophic ponds differ in species composition (Fig. 6D, 7D). Moderately hypereutrophic ponds exhibit an intermediate profile, sharing characteristics of both groups. Overall, community composition was significantly, though moderately, associated with pond trophic state, but a significant dispersion test suggests within‑group heterogeneity may also contribute.

The ordination plot (Fig. 6D, 7D) demonstrates that variation in species composition across water bodies is associated with multiple environmental parameters. Samples from sites with higher trophic status mainly align along the axis defined by conductivity and TSI. At those sites, richness of Euglena, Euglenaria and Discoplastis was elevated, along with increased abundance of the first two genera. A moderate association with elevated DOC was also observed. In contrast, less enriched sites tend to exhibit lower conductivity and TSI, together with higher richness and abundance of combined Trachelomonas and Strombomonas, and increased abundance of Euglenaformis. Furthermore, eutrophic ponds appear to be characterized by high C:N ratio and low pH, which is also related to elevated richness of Flexiglena, Euglenaformis, Lepocinclis, Phacus, Cryptoglena, and the combined Trachelomonas and Strombomonas genera. Other patterns in the data appear too complex to be reliably interpreted based mainly on this plot.

Indicator species analysis across trophic states revealed strong community differentiation, identifying 17 taxa with strong indicator values (stat > 0.6, p < 0.05), with eutrophic ponds supporting 11 indicator species, hypereutrophic (TSI 80–90) ponds two, and hypereutrophic (TSI > 90) ponds four (Table S10, Fig. 4). Eutrophic ponds had the highest number of exclusive indicator taxa, suggesting that intermediate nutrient conditions strongly shape assemblages of euglenids. Top indicators included Phacus pleuronectes (O.F.Müller) Nitzsch ex Dujardin, Flexiglena variabilis, and Trachelomonas sp. 11. Species showing high exclusivity in terms of trophic state were Trachelomonas oblonga Lemmermann and Euglenaformis parasitica Kato, Yahata & T.Nakayama for eutrophic conditions. In terms of consistent presence, Flexiglena variabilis demonstrated strong fidelity to eutrophic ponds. In summary, eutrophic ponds supported more and stronger indicator species compared to hypereutrophic ones, reflecting habitats supporting a greater number of taxa.

Site size

Analysis of alpha diversity revealed no significant relationship between site size and species richness (Table S12). Mean species richness was marginally higher in large ponds compared to small and medium ponds, with a similar pattern observed for median values – large ponds exhibiting the highest richness, small ponds intermediate, and medium ponds the lowest values (Table 1). These statistics indicate relatively consistent alpha diversity across pond size categories, with no evidence of a clear trend associated with increasing or decreasing pond size. Despite differences in sample sizes medium (n = 96), small (n = 48), and large (n = 46) the total species richness and variability appeared similar among groups (Fig. 5E).

The model evaluating richness included site size as the main fixed effect, year as a covariate, and site as a random effect to account for site-level variation. The marginal R2 was relatively low (0.037), suggesting that pond size and year (as a covariate) explained only a small fraction of the variance in richness (Table S13). Estimated marginal means of richness did not differ significantly between large, medium, and small ponds (Fig. 5E, Table 1). Similarly, pairwise comparisons revealed no significant contrasts between groups (Table S14).

Site size accounted for approximately 7.4% of the variation in species composition and was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Dispersion was homogeneous among size categories, supporting valid interpretation of differences (Table S15). Pairwise comparisons further revealed significant differences among all size groups, with the greatest divergence observed between the large and small groups, indicating a size‑related gradient in community composition (Table 14). However, the CCA ordination (Fig. 6E, 7E) shows a less distinct pattern than suggested by the pairwise tests. The ellipses for small and medium sites overlap substantially and the ellipse representing large sites shows only partial separation. This indicates that although site size categories differ significantly in species composition, these differences are rather gradual and only faintly reflected in the ordination space.

The relationship between species composition and site size does not present a clear, consistent pattern (Fig. 6E,7E). Large sites tend to support a wider spectrum of beta‑diversity and are associated with more variable environmental conditions – especially in terms of pH and carbon-to-nitrogen (C:N) ratios. In contrast, small and medium-sized sites exhibit more constrained and homogeneous community structures, with most samples associating with elevated DOC, conductivity, and TSI. However, attributing richness and abundance vectors directly to site size categories may be misleading since no clear, consistent trend emerges from the data.

Variation in pond size was associated with distinct indicator species, identifying seven taxa with strong indicator values (stat > 0.6, p < 0.05), with large and medium ponds contributing two each, and small ponds three (Table S10, Fig. 4). Top indicators included Monomorphina pyrum (Ehrenberg) Mereschkowsky in small ponds, Trachelomonas papillifera (T.G.Popova) Zakryś, Fells & Łukomska (= T. intermedia var. papillifera T.G.Popova) in medium ponds, and Phacus pleuronectes in large ponds.

Species showing high exclusivity included Discoplastis excavata (Schiller) Zakryś & Łukomska for small ponds and Euglena geniculata for medium ones. As for fidelity, Monomorphina pyrum exhibited strong value for small ponds. These patterns highlight the role of pond size in structuring euglenid communities, although no single pond size stood out as clearly structuring the community.

Pond surroundings

Species richness differed significantly between sites with different dominant land cover type in the immediate pond surroundings (LRT = 11.98, df = 3, p = 0.007; Table S12). The highest average and median richness was recorded at cropland sites, followed by grassland, urban, and mosaic landscapes, which showed the lowest values (Table 1). Total species richness across all samples in each group ranged from 81 species (n = 36) in mosaic areas to 162 (n = 83) in grassland, indicating broader ecological differences (Fig. 5F). Notably, while direct comparisons of total richness across groups should be interpreted with caution due to differences in sample size, cropland and mosaic sites had the same number of samples (n = 36), yet cropland sites exhibited substantially higher total richness (138 species vs. 81 in mosaic areas). This contrast further highlights the lower diversity associated with mosaic landscapes.

The model assessing species richness included pond surroundings as a fixed effect, year as a covariate, and site as a random effect. The marginal R2 was 0.266, indicating that pond surroundings and year together explained approximately 27% of the variance in richness (Table S13). Estimated marginal means (EMMs) of richness, adjusted for year and expressed on the response scale, revealed significant differences among land cover types. The highest modelled richness was found in cropland and grassland sites, both grouped together in CLD group “a”. Urban sites were not significantly different from any of the other land cover types and formed group “ab”, indicating statistical overlap with both lower- and higher-richness categories. The lowest modelled richness occurred in mosaic areas, forming a distinct group (“b”) in the CLD. Pairwise comparisons indicated that cropland sites were nearly twice as rich as mosaic sites, and grassland sites were also significantly richer. These findings suggest that the type of land use around ponds influences species richness, with natural or agricultural settings supporting higher diversity compared to urban or fragmented mosaic landscapes (Fig. 5F, Table 1; Table S14).

Pond surroundings explained 10.6% of the variation in community composition (p < 0.001), but a significant dispersion test indicates heterogeneous within-group variation, which may influence the results (Table S15). The highest R2 values were observed for urban-mosaic, cropland-mosaic, urban-cropland, and grassland-mosaic pairs. These distinctions partially align with visual patterns from the CCA ordination and overlaid ellipses – cropland and grassland sites are compositionally distinguishable, although with some overlap (Fig. 6F, 7F). Notably, the urban-mosaic pair exhibited the largest R2, even though this was not clearly reflected in the ordination plots. High R2 for this pair likely reflects the combination of tightly clustered urban sites and more unevenly dispersed mosaic sites, which amplifies the PERMANOVA effect (Table S16).

Cropland‑associated samples were linked with higher DOC, elevated C:N ratios, increased conductivity, and higher TSI values (Fig. 6F, 7F). In cropland sites, richness of Euglena, Euglenaria and Discoplastis was elevated, along with increased abundance of Euglena and Euglenaria. By contrast, grassland sites generally exhibited lower DOC, conductivity, C:N ratio, and TSI values. While richness of genera in grassland did not show consistent patterns, abundances of combined Trachelomonas and Strombomonas, Euglenaformis, Colacium and Monomorphina tended to be elevated. In mosaic and urban sites, community composition more closely corresponded with lower C:N ratios and higher pH; in these sites, abundances of Monomorphina and Colacium, together with Discoplastis, Euglena, and Euglenaria, were comparatively higher, and richness of Discoplastis, Euglenaria, and Colacium also increased.

Analysis of indicator species across ponds with different surrounding land uses identified 10 taxa with strong indicator values (stat > 0.6, p < 0.05), with cropland ponds contributing four, grassland one, mosaic three, and urban two (Table S10, Fig. 4). The top indicator species were Phacus circumflexus Pochmann in urban ponds and Trachelomonas compacta and Lepocinclis fusiformis (H.J.Carter) Lemmermann in cropland ponds. Species showing high exclusivity to particular pond surroundings followed the same pattern, with Discoplastis excavata associated with cropland ponds and Trachelomonas niezabitowski (Dreżepolski) Zakryś, Fells & Łukomska (= T. hispida var. niezabitowskii Dreżepolski) with urban ponds. Fidelity analysis indicated Trachelomonas compacta and Phacus circumflexus as strongly consistent indicators within the cropland and urban habitats, respectively. These patterns reflect the influence of land use on euglenids community composition, with cropland ponds showing stronger structuring effect than the other types of surrounding.

Physicochemical drivers of alpha diversity

To examine how abiotic factors relate to alpha diversity, we fitted a generalized linear mixed model with negative binomial distribution, as in the previous analyses. Species richness was used as the response variable, with standardized continuous predictors and a categorical year as fixed effects, and site (n = 16) as a random intercept. Model selection identified a top model (lowest AICc) that included four predictors: C:N ratio, dissolved phosphorus (DP), dissolved oxygen (O₂), and TSI (Table S18). After examination of predictor importance with summed Akaike weights we found that, in addition to those four, pH also had strong support (weight = 0.58; Table S19). Therefore, the final model included five scaled environmental predictors: C:N ratio, DP, O₂, TSI, and pH (Fig. 10).

Predicted values of species richness (solid lines) with 95% confidence intervals (shaded red ribbons) are shown across five key environmental predictors: pH, dissolved oxygen (O₂), dissolved organic carbon (DOC), TSI (trophic state index), and C:N ratio. Raw data points are overlaid on the plot. The model was fitted using standardized predictors using negative-binomial GLMM with a random intercept for site, holding other predictors at their mean values. The x-axis represents the observed range of each predictor in original units

The model explained 31.1% of the variation in species richness with fixed effects alone and 34.3% when including the site-level random effect (Table S20). The random intercept variance was very low (Var = 0.00003, SD = 0.005), indicating that differences among sites contributed little additional variation. Among predictors, TSI had the strongest negative effect on richness, followed by dissolved oxygen, both associated with lower species richness. Dissolved phosphorus and C:N ratio showed positive effects. Although pH exhibited moderate relative importance across candidate models, its averaged coefficient was small and statistically non-significant. We therefore retain pH in our final model to maintain ecological completeness, while noting its weak and uncertain influence. Overall, the model suggests that measured environmental variables contribute to variation in species richness, while differences among sites appear minimal in this analysis. However, a substantial portion of variation remains unexplained, and other site-level factors may still be important.

Predicted species richness estimates exhibit varying trends across different environmental predictors.

For pH, species richness declined from approximately 27 species at pH 7.1 to ≈22.5 species at pH 9.8. Similarly, richness decreased with dissolved oxygen, from ≈29 species at 1.5 mg/L to ≈19 at 19.2 mg/L. In contrast, dissolved phosphorus showed a rise in richness – from ≈24 at 0.01 mg/L to ≈39 species at 1.78 mg/L. A comparable positive relationship was observed for C:N ratio, where species richness increased from approximately 22 species at a ratio of 1.7 to ≈32 at 13.4. Finally, TSI exhibited the steepest decline: predicted richness fell from ≈38 species at an index of 50.4 to ≈18 species at 114. These predictions, supported by 95% confidence intervals, are illustrated in Fig. 15 and presented in Table S21.

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive, long-term analysis of autotrophic euglenid diversity in freshwater ponds using metabarcoding approach. By combining species-level data with detailed environmental and physicochemical measurements across three regions of Poland, we were able to investigate both the richness and composition of euglenid communities in relation to multiple ecology drivers, while also detecting indicator species. Previous studies, mostly focusing on general protist communities, have typically used universal primers [70–72] or relied on light microscopy observation [73], which limits the taxonomic resolution and may underrepresent specific groups such as euglenids. While this approach offers a general understanding of community composition and ecological patterns, it does not give detailed insights into specific groups or the environmental factors that affect them. This can be misleading, as even among a relatively small group of euglenids, ecological preferences can vary substantially. As a result, the lack of detailed data makes it difficult to understand how specific species or entire groups respond to environmental changes or to identify reliable indicator species, constraining broader climate impact assessments. Our methodological focus on autotrophic euglenids provides insights that are not accessible through broader metabarcoding approaches; therefore, the two approaches are not directly comparable.

Results in this work highlight a clear hierarchy in drivers of euglenid biodiversity in small ponds: geographical context, particularly at the site scale, followed by environmental factors including trophic state, pond surroundings, site size, and continuous abiotic variables, with temporal factors presenting comparatively minor influence. Nevertheless, even with this apparent gradient of influence, a substantial portion of microbial variability remains unexplained, which is consistent with previous studies focused on ponds that also report pronounced spatial and environmental effects alongside unexplained variation – likely due to unmeasured variables, biotic interactions, or unpredictable factors [74, 75].

Geographical context emerged as the primary determinant of community composition, with local site-specific factors being the strongest contributors, followed by regional patterns. In our work we collected samples from three regions of Poland, each approximately 200–300 km apart in the straight line distance, with the sampling sites within each region ranging from 200 m to 30 km. Regional contrasts in species richness were evident, with Masuria exhibiting substantially higher richness and a greater number of indicator species. Although ponds within a region often shared similar species composition, spatial proximity did not fully account for these similarities, as some distant ponds also showed comparable assemblages. These results are consistent with the idea that while many microbial taxa (and euglenids among them) are considered cosmopolitan, the “everything is everywhere, but the environment selects” hypothesis [76] only partially applies. It is consistent with the recent research, which supports the view that most protists are not in fact globally widespread, which is reflecting the “moderate endemicity model” [77]. True cosmopolitan distribution appears rare among protists, with less than 1% of OTUs found globally in oceanic [78] or soil [79] environments. However, the variation of abiotic factors across different environments are also influencing protist communities. Since the terrestrial environments are far more diverse in terms of parameters than marine, the influence of geographic distance is much less pronounced here [80]. For euglenids, cell size (approximately 10–100 µm) likely limits passive dispersal, especially for species exceeding 20 µm [81]. Zoochory, particularly via waterbirds, is likely the main dispersal mechanism for both short- and long-distance dispersion, supporting our observations of sudden large population appearances of species such as Euglena mazurica [82, 83]. In this context, studies conducted in Poland can provide significant insights as it lies along migratory routes used by birds of passage travelling from far regions of Europe, Africa and Asia. Its geographic location provides suitable environment to observe introduction and spread of euglenid species.

Environmental factors – considered in the context of broader categories such as trophic state, pond surroundings, and pond size, as well as continuous abiotic measures – affected euglenid biodiversity, though less strongly than geographic factors. We identified five key continuous variables influencing species richness: TSI, dissolved oxygen, dissolved phosphorus, C:N ratio, and pH, which confirms part of the patterns reported in previous research. Broader metabarcoding studies [4, 70] found total phosphorus and pH important for protist community composition, which is consistent with our CCA results. Notably, conductivity was found to be a significant factor influencing beta diversity of euglenid communities in our work, whereas it was reported as non-significant in the findings of Bock et al. [70]. Light microscopy studies [22, 27] focusing on euglenid blooms suggested that euglenids favour lower pH and dissolved oxygen but higher nutrient concentrations. Our model confirms similar trends for dissolved oxygen and nutrients, with pH effects weak, but still consistent with previous studies. However, in our study we observed that higher trophic levels were associated with a lower number of euglenid species, as well as indicator species from this group. This suggests that while the nutrient level may support the growth of euglenids, it does not necessarily promote greater species richness. Additionally, in highly enriched systems, large amounts of nutrients can favour a few tolerant, opportunistic species while excluding more sensitive taxa, reducing compositional complexity [84]. The observed beta diversity structure is therefore consistent with ecological responses to progressive eutrophication. Importantly, most of our sampled water bodies were eutrophic and hypereutrophic ponds. This selection was motivated by the fact that euglenids are relatively rare in mesotrophic and oligotrophic waters, making eutrophic ponds the more appropriate habitats to study their diversity [7]. It is also important to note that our work is focused exclusively on euglenids and conducted with metabarcoding methods. As a result, we did not collect data concerning their volume, biomass or relative abundance to other groups of protists in specific samples. Pond surroundings also influenced communities: ponds in cropland or grassland areas tended to exhibit higher species richness compared to urbanized or mixed-use ponds, yet species composition varied subtly, reflecting differences in nutrient input, water chemistry, and anthropogenic impacts. Additionally, pond size showed measurable but smaller effects on the diversity patterns, with larger sites supporting slightly higher diversity and smaller ponds often harboring fewer species. Our findings are consistent with previous studies on small lakes and ponds, where factors such as pond area influenced microbial communities [85]. Larger ponds often provide more stable conditions, while smaller ponds tend to show higher variability in community composition, possibly due to limited habitat heterogeneity. These results highlight that, while environmental factors can modify community composition and richness, their effects interact with geography and dispersal dynamics, producing complex, pond-specific patterns.

Among the variables considered, temporal factors, such as season and year, explained the least variation in euglenid biodiversity, although some patterns were detectable. Species richness and composition did not differ substantially across seasons during the vegetation period of euglenids. According to the literature, seasonal dynamics in temperate zones are well documented, with richness typically increasing from early spring to summer and gradually declining through summer and fall ([86] and our observations). The absence of a clear pattern in our study may indicate that we captured only a limited portion of the full seasonal cycle, or that seasonal variation is substantially reduced in shallow ponds, where stratification can occur multiple times per year [87]. In addition, while higher water temperatures and increased light intensity can elevate euglenid abundance [18, 88], our metabarcoding approach likely captures rare species and extracellular DNA, masking subtle seasonal fluctuations. Similarly, interannual variation was limited; the three-year dataset revealed mostly consistent richness and composition across years, further emphasizing the dominance of spatial and environmental drivers over temporal ones. This contrasts with broader protist research, where seasonal and annual dynamics can significantly influence alpha and beta diversity [87].

This type of research is especially critical in the context of climate change, as freshwater ecosystems are among the most vulnerable to environmental fluctuations [89, 90]. The impacts of climate-driven changes, combined with direct anthropogenic stressors, are particularly pronounced in small freshwater bodies, yet research on such habitats remains limited – especially in the context of protist metabarcoding [91]. Despite their ecological importance and wide distribution [4, 92], existing studies provide insufficient insight, and this research gap extends to other microalgal groups, including dinoflagellates, chlorophytes, diatoms, and cyanobacteria. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms of climate- and human-induced changes, particularly in communities of eukaryotic microorganisms, is essential for predicting ecosystem responses and developing effective conservation strategies.

Conclusion

Our study provides a comprehensive assessment of the drivers shaping euglenid biodiversity in small freshwater ponds. Across 190 samples from three regions of Poland, we found a clear hierarchy of influences on community composition and species richness, with geography exerting the strongest effect. Site-level variation emerged as the dominant factor, followed by regional differences, highlighting the spatial structuring of euglenid assemblages. Environmental factors, including trophic state, pond surroundings, and site size, also played important roles, though their effects were generally more moderate. Temporal variables, such as season and year, had comparatively minor influence, indicating that communities remained largely stable over time.

Environmental gradients, such as C:N ratio, pH, conductivity and phosphorus metrics, were closely associated with species richness. Similarly, the summed richness and abundance of specific genera reflected these patterns, highlighting the key role of local habitat characteristics in shaping community structure. At the species level, several taxa served as indicators of particular conditions, further emphasize the patterns observed in both alpha and beta diversity.

Despite identifying these major drivers, a substantial portion of variation in euglenid diversity remained unexplained, suggesting that additional biotic or microhabitat factors may be important. Overall, our integrative approach combining long-term, species-specific metabarcoding with detailed environmental data provides a robust framework for understanding protist ecology in small ponds and offers a valuable template for future studies targeting microbial communities in shallow freshwater ecosystems.
