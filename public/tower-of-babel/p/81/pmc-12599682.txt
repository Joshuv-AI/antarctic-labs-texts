Exploring Perineal Reconstruction: Incisional Negative Pressure Wound Therapy for Perineal Flaps—A 2-Case Report
Authors: Viviana Gómez-Ortega, Daniela Escobar-Domingo, David Camacho-Obando, Oscar Mejía-Segura, Santiago Toledo, Anwar Medellín, Daniel Sanabria
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary:Managing perineal defects presents a clinical challenge requiring a multidisciplinary approach for optimal outcomes. These defects stem from various causes, and their associated complications significantly affect patient morbidity and quality of life. Incisional negative pressure wound therapy (iNPWT) serves as an adaptable adjunct for postreconstruction management across different anatomical areas. We present 2 clinical case reports of patients with perineal defects who underwent comprehensive management, including surgery, antibiotic therapy, and iNPWT. Case 1 involved a 64-year-old woman with advanced neoplastic disease complicated by a rectovaginal fistula. Case 2 featured a 63-year-old man with severe perianal and scrotal infection. Both patients underwent multiple surgical procedures and iNPWT as part of their treatment. Successful wound healing was achieved in both cases, accompanied by resolution of infections and a significant reduction in complications. These cases demonstrate the potential benefits of iNPWT as adjunctive therapy for managing perineal defects. Incorporating iNPWT into the treatment of perineal defects resulting from complex pathologies represents a positive advancement toward enhancing outcomes.

Managing perineal defects poses a clinical challenge, typically necessitating a multidisciplinary approach to achieve satisfactory outcomes. These defects arise from diverse etiologies such as colorectal resections, trauma, obstetric injuries, and necrotizing infections such as Fournier gangrene. Complications associated with perineal surgery and reconstruction significantly impact patient morbidity and quality of life.1 The choice of surgical technique for perineal defect closure is critical and ranges from primary closure to the use of complex flaps, depending on factors such as defect size, location, tissue viability, and comorbidities.

Incisional negative pressure wound therapy (iNPWT), with its mechanisms of protection against infections, reduction of wound tension, drainage of fluids, and enhancement of microcirculation and lymphatic drainage, has emerged as a valuable addition to the armamentarium for managing perineal wounds. At the cellular level, iNPWT promotes cell proliferation and modulates the inflammatory response by reducing inflammatory mediators, which supports a more favorable environment for tissue repair. Additionally, iNPWT increases arterial and subcutaneous oxygen partial pressure, thereby improving oxygen delivery to the wound bed, which is critical for effective healing.2 On a tissue level, the therapy helps protect surgical wounds from external contamination, maintains wound edge approximation, and equalizes mechanical strain across the wound site. These combined effects contribute to accelerated wound healing, reduced complications, and improved surgical outcomes.1,2

CASE PRESENTATION NUMBER 1

A 64-year-old woman presented with a history of perianal pain and discharge. Colonoscopy revealed high-grade dysplasia with glandular extension and a neoplastic mass involving the rectum and vagina, complicated by a rectovaginal fistula. Surgical interventions included laparoscopic procedures, colostomy, and subsequent abdominoperineal resection with vaginectomy, followed by closure using V-Y flaps and iNPWT. DISCUSSION

Patients with malignant pathologies or progressive skin infections often require surgical procedures involving extensive tissue resections, which are associated with high rates of mortality, morbidity, complications, and substantial coverage defects.3 Additionally, patients with a history of malignancies frequently undergo radiotherapy, which can increase the risk of complications in surgical wounds due to altered tissue perfusion in irradiated areas.

Vacuum-assisted closure or negative pressure wound therapy has been introduced as an adjunctive treatment modality for perineal defects. It entails applying negative pressure to the wound via specialized dressing, promoting wound healing, and minimizing skin defects.4 Combining negative pressure wound therapy with surgical debridement and broad-spectrum antibiotics has demonstrated promising results in improving outcomes for patients with infectious pathologies while preparing the defect for definitive reconstruction.5

Scalise et al6 conducted a systematic review to investigate the effect of iNPWT on the healing of surgical wounds by primary intention, revealing reduced rates of infection, seroma, and hematoma with its use. Aljoaib et al7 conducted a meta-analysis highlighting the effectiveness of iNPWT in reducing surgical site infections in colorectal surgery. Cahill et al,8 in their systematic review, noted a significant reduction in perineal complications following abdominoperineal resections when iNPWT was used, detecting only a 9% incidence of surgical site infections.

However, there is limited evidence regarding the use of incisional negative pressure therapy in the reconstruction of large tissue losses in the perineal region following conditions such as Fournier gangrene and infiltrating squamous cell carcinoma, which are anatomically challenging and associated with high mortality rates. One study reported positive outcomes in terms of hospital stay for a patient with Fournier gangrene treated with iNPWT.5 Another study suggested that iNPWT could effectively manage reconstruction after infiltrating squamous cell carcinoma by protecting against fecal discharge and reducing recovery time.9 Both studies reported no complications such as dehiscence, infection, reintervention, or bleeding following reconstruction with iNPWT.

Advances in technology enable more precise and controlled postoperative care for immediately reconstructed patients, particularly those undergoing local/regional flaps or primary closure in reconstructive plastic surgery. The use of iNPWT has shown potential to reduce complications by 74% during hospitalization, decrease hospital stay duration by 27%, and lower the incidence of wound dehiscence by 78%.10

CONCLUSIONS

The complexity of managing perineal defects highlights the necessity for a comprehensive treatment approach. Incorporating iNPWT into treatment protocols marks a significant advancement in improving patient outcomes. As demonstrated in our cases, iNPWT effectively reduced the risks of dehiscence and surgical site infections, which are common complications in these procedures when iNPWT is not used. However, further research is needed to develop standardized protocols and fully clarify the benefits of iNPWT in complex perineal reconstructions.
