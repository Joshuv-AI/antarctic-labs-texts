Synthetic cervical spine radiographs: expert validation and transfer learning for low-data settings
Authors: Austin A Barr, Brij S Karmur, Anthony J Winder, Eddie Guo, John T Lysack, James N Scott, William F Morrish, Muneer Eesa, Morgan Willson, David W Cadotte, Michael M H Yang, Ian Y M Chan, Sanju Lama, Garnette R Sutherland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundApplication of machine learning (ML) in neuroimaging is often constrained by the difficulty of assembling, sharing, and utilizing large, high-quality datasets. Synthetic data may address limitations in traditional data-sharing by enabling privacy-preserving image generation at scale. Blinded expert validation involved six neuroradiologists and two spine fellowship-trained neurosurgeons reviewing 50 randomly selected image quartets, each containing one real and three synthetic images. Experts attempted to identify the real scan and rated the realism of each image on a four-point Likert scale. To assess privacy preservation and potential memorization, we performed a real-synthetic nearest-neighbor search using vision transformer image embeddings ranked by cosine similarity. We then generated pseudolandmarks for our curated synthetic dataset and trained a landmark-localization backbone. External evaluation was performed on the Cervical Lateral X-ray 34-point (CLX-34) dataset using a directly matched 22-landmark task, comparing fine-tuning from the synthetic backbone and de novo training on increasing amounts of CLX-34 data.ResultsExperts correctly identified the real image in 29.0% of trials, with low inter-rater agreement (Fleiss’ κ = 0.061). Background

Machine learning (ML) is increasingly applied to neuroimaging research; however, progress can be constrained by the availability of large-scale training data [1, 2]. Patient volumes are often limited [3, 4], institutional data are heterogeneous and frequently incomplete [5], and the labor required to curate, standardize, and de-identify raw data is substantial [6]. Privacy and regulatory constraints further complicate data sharing [7–9], which may otherwise be used to compile multi-institutional datasets: a recent case study documented an average of eight months to receive neuroimaging data from cross-institutional requests [10]. Similarly, published peer-reviewed neurosurgical research rarely provides access to source data [11]. Collectively, these factors limit the availability of neuroimaging data for model training and external validation [12, 13].

Synthetic data has emerged as a pragmatic complement to real-world data (RWD) under these constraints. Generated data that are not tied to real patient records but preserve the salient properties or statistical structure of RWD can be produced at scale and shared without regulatory restrictions [14, 15]. Synthetic tabular data has increasingly been used to overcome limitations in the availability of perioperative RWD [16–18]. For example, high-fidelity tabular neurosurgical data was used to support privacy-preserving data sharing for ML model training and clinical analyses in place of RWD [16]. Synthetic imaging data is advancing along a similar trajectory [19, 20], albeit at a slower pace, due to the added complexity of achieving visual fidelity.

One method for generating synthetic images is a generative adversarial network (GAN) [21], which employs two competing networks to improve image fidelity. Early evaluations of GAN-based synthetic imaging data have demonstrated significant fidelity in expert reviews [22, 23]. Much of this work, however, has been concentrated on chest radiography, where datasets such as MIMIC-CXR-JPG (377,110 images) and CheXpert (224,316 images) enable large-scale training and evaluation [24, 25]. In contrast, neuroimaging datasets are typically much smaller and limited in availability [1, 2]. This ‘abundance irony’ underscores why neurosurgical and neuroimaging research stands to benefit disproportionately from synthetic data.

Another method of synthetic image generation is the denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) [26]. The DDPM generates images by iteratively denoising random noise, producing high-quality and diverse synthetic images. Emerging evidence suggests that DDPM-generated images exhibit superior quality and fidelity to those produced by GANs [20, 27]. Yet, the application of DDPMs to generate new samples of synthetic neuroimaging data, rigorous assessment of image realism, and demonstration of downstream utility toward ML tasks remain largely unexplored.

In this study, we investigated whether a DDPM can generate realistic synthetic lateral cervical spine radiographs when trained on the open-access Cervical Spine X-ray Atlas (CSXA) of 4,963 radiographs [28, 29]. We first conducted a multi-expert blinded validation and memorization audit of generated radiographs. We then tested whether a synthetic-pretrained landmark-localization backbone could support transfer learning on an external dataset under low-data settings. We hypothesized that experts would be unable to distinguish real from synthetic images, and that synthetic initialization would improve downstream landmark localization when external training data were limited.

Methods

Real-world data source and image pre-processing

Training images for the generative model were drawn from CSXA, an open-access dataset of 4,963 lateral cervical spine radiographs with accompanying vertebral landmark metadata [28, 29]. All CSXA radiographs were included and standardized to 256 × 256 pixels prior to model development. Associated landmark coordinates were transformed using the same geometric operations such that the saved keypoints remained spatially aligned with the preprocessed images. The CSXA annotation scheme comprised 23 vertebral keypoints, including the inferior endplate corners and centroid of C2 together with the vertebral-body corners of C3 through C7. Negative scans (i.e., cortical bone rendered dark) were also intensity-inverted to homogenize the coloration of training data.

DDPM implementation and training

A DDPM training and sampling setup was implemented in Python using code adapted from the serag‑ai “I‑SynMed” repository (https://github.com/serag-ai/I-SynMed) [30]. To optimize the generative model hyperparameters, the CSXA dataset was randomly divided into training and validation partitions with 4,219 (85%) and 744 (15%) images, respectively. Hyperparameters included batch size (8), learning rate (5e-5), and number of training steps (up to 160,000). Validation loss was monitored at ‘model checkpoints’ saved every 2,000 training steps.

For final large-scale sampling, a separate DDPM instance was retrained on 100% of the available CSXA images to maximize data utilization. During this final training run, distributional similarity between real and synthetic images was monitored with Fréchet inception distance (FID), which compares the statistical properties of deep feature representations (computed with a pretrained Inception network) between the two sets of images [30, 31]. FID was computed between the 4,963 real radiographs and 7,250 synthetic images generated at intervals of 20,000 training steps. Training and sampling were performed on institutional NVIDIA A100 or V100 GPUs.

Synthetic dataset preparation and post-processing

Synthetic image sets of 1,000 radiographs were generated for each of three selected DDPM checkpoints. These checkpoints were chosen on the basis of the loss trajectories and FID behavior. Following generation, synthetic images were standardized to left-facing orientation to ensure consistent anatomical alignment. A manual post-generation review was then conducted to exclude any samples with implausible anatomy (e.g., pharynx and trachea posterior to the spinal column, inverted anterior and posterior spinal column elements, etc.). For blinded expert validation, 50 synthetic radiographs were randomly sampled from each checkpoint and paired with 50 randomly selected real CSXA radiographs. These images were organized into quartets containing one real radiograph and three synthetic radiographs, one from each checkpoint.

In parallel, larger synthetic sampling jobs were performed from the same checkpoints, pooled, and manually screened with the same exclusion criteria to create the final publication-scale dataset. Each large-scale sampling job was conducted within the maximum institutional runtime limit of 24 h.

Memorization audit

To assess for memorization, a near-duplicate search of all generated images was conducted using the open-source imagededup library (https://github.com/idealo/imagededup) with vision-transformer embeddings. Nearest neighbors were ranked by cosine similarity, and the 100 most similar real-synthetic pairs were extracted. Two authors (AAB and BSK) independently performed side-by-side visual review of these 100 pairs, adjudicating for explicit memorization. Explicit memorization was defined a priori as near-identical vertebral contours, soft-tissue silhouettes, or acquisition artifacts beyond plausible sampling variability.

Blinded expert validation

Synthetic image realism was further evaluated in a blinded expert study involving eight clinicians: six neuroradiologists and two spine fellowship-trained neurosurgeons, from the University of Calgary (Calgary, Canada) and the University of Toronto (Toronto, Canada). Using the Qualtrics survey platform (Qualtrics, Provo, UT), each expert reviewed 50 image quartets presented sequentially and, for each quartet, identified the image they believed to be real and assigned realism scores to all four images on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (unrealistic) to 4 (fully realistic).

Statistical analysis for synthetic image validation

Identification accuracy was summarized as the proportion of quartets in which the real image was correctly identified, aggregated across raters. Inter-rater agreement for the identification task was quantified with Fleiss’ κ [32]. Mean realism ratings for the real images were compared with those of the three synthetic groups using paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.2, The R Project for Statistical Computing), and rating distributions were visualized using violin plots generated with Matplotlib (https://matplotlib.org).

Training of the baseline synthetic landmark model

To create the synthetic landmark-localization baseline for the transfer experiments, a heatmap-regression landmark detector was first trained on real CSXA landmark annotations and then used to generate pseudolandmarks for the synthetic dataset. The CSXA corpus was partitioned into 3,475 (70%) training, 744 (15%) validation, and 744 (15%) testing images. An initial Spatial Configuration Network (SCN) was trained from random initialization on the 3,475 real image-landmark pairs and used to infer pseudolandmarks for all curated synthetic radiographs. No manual or algorithmic quality-control filtering of the pseudolandmarks was applied. A second SCN was then trained from random initialization on the synthetic image-pseudolandmark pairs, and the resultant model weights were saved as the baseline synthetic landmark-localization backbone.

The model was implemented using the Landmarker toolkit (https://github.com/predict-idlab/landmarker) as a 2D SCN with one input channel and 23 output channels corresponding to the 23 CSXA keypoints [33–35]. Gaussian target heatmaps were generated with σ = 3, and optimization used stochastic gradient descent with learning rate 1e-6, Nesterov momentum 0.99, and weight decay 1e-3. The loss function was GaussianHeatmapL2Loss, with α = 5. Validation was performed at intervals corresponding to 3,475 training images, and early stopping was triggered after eight consecutive validation checks without improvement, at which point the best-performing weights were retained. During evaluation, predicted heatmaps were converted to landmark coordinates, and per-landmark Euclidean errors were squared and averaged to compute subjectwise mean squared error (MSE).

External transfer learning evaluation

External transfer learning was evaluated on the open-access Cervical Lateral X-ray 34-point (CLX-34) dataset [36], which contains 1,307 radiographs with 34 annotated keypoints. Since the CLX-34 keypoints did not directly correspond to the CSXA annotations, the transfer analysis was restricted to a matched 22-keypoint subset consisting of the inferior corners of C2 and the superior and inferior vertebral-body corners C3 through C7. The CSXA C2 centroid was excluded because no corresponding keypoint was provided in CLX-34 metadata. CLX-34 images and annotations were preprocessed to the same 256 × 256 pixel geometry as the CSXA data, and images lacking one or more of the required 22 landmarks were excluded. The remaining images were then randomly divided into a fixed 70:30 development-to-test split, and the development set was subsampled sequentially in increments of 100 images to generate nested training subsets for fine-tuning.

For each CLX-34 training subset, two landmark-localization paradigms were compared on the same held-out external test set. In the fine-tuning arm, the baseline synthetic SCN weights were used to initialize the model and the network was fine-tuned on increasing subsets of the CLX-34 development set. In the de novo arm, the same architecture was trained from random initialization on the identical CLX-34 subset. Before fine-tuning, the 23-output synthetic-trained model was adapted to a 22-output external model by removing the excluded centroid channel from the output layers while preserving the remaining pretrained parameters. A zero-shot experiment was also performed in which the adapted synthetic backbone was evaluated directly on the held-out CLX-34 test set without any CLX-34-specific fine-tuning. All CLX-34 experiments used the same SCN/Landmarker training configuration described above. Mean test-set MSE was summarized for each training-set size and used to compare the learning efficiency of synthetic initialization against de novo training.

Ethical considerations

The CSXA is an open-access dataset released under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) [28]. We have made our large-scale pooled dataset available for inclusion with this publication, consistent with principles of open science and the permissive terms of the CC BY 4.0 license. As our study team conducted a blinded review of images, and there were no human participants beyond co-authors, formal institutional review board (IRB) approval was not required.

Results

DDPM training dynamics and checkpoint selection

Training and validation losses for the DDPM converged between model weight checkpoints 30 and 40, diverging thereafter (Fig. 1). FID computed on batches of 7,250 synthetic radiographs continued to decrease over training (Fig. 2), which indicated ongoing improvement in sample quality despite the loss divergence. On this basis, checkpoints 34 and 40 were selected to represent the interval of apparent loss-convergence, and checkpoint 80 was retained to assess whether longer training improved subjective image realism despite the divergence in loss behavior.

Blinded expert validation

Across 400 quartet judgements (eight raters with 50 quartets per rater), experts identified the real image in 29.0% of trials. A sample quartet is provided in Fig. 3. Inter-rater agreement for the identification task was low (Fleiss’ κ = 0.061). Mean realism ratings were 3.323 for real images and 3.228, 3.258, and 3.320 for synthetic images sampled from checkpoints 34, 40, and 80, respectively (Fig. 4). Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests showed no statistically significant differences between real and synthetic mean ratings (unadjusted p = 0.128, 0.236, 1.000; Holm-adjusted p = 0.383, 0.471, 1.000; two-sided).

Memorization audit and dataset curation

The memorization audit identified the 100 real-synthetic image pairs with the highest vision-transformer cosine similarity. Two reviewers independently found no visually explicit memorization among these top-similar pairs according to the predefined criteria. The top 100 most similar real-synthetic pairs are available at (10.6084/m9.figshare.30436465) [37].

The initial synthetic pool comprised 27,000 lateral cervical spine radiographs and was reduced to 20,063 scans after exclusion of 6,937 images with gross anatomical abnormalities on manual review. The resulting large-scale dataset is available at (10.6084/m9.figshare.30436465) [37]. A greater proportion of exclusions arose from earlier DDPM checkpoints, consistent with a higher prevalence of implausible anatomy in samples generated earlier in training. The specific counts of excluded images from each checkpoint are detailed in Table S1.

External transfer learning

Of the 1,307 CLX-34 radiographs, 59 were excluded for incomplete keypoint metadata, leaving 874 scans in the development set and 374 in the held-out test set. In the zero-shot setting, the synthetic backbone achieved a mean MSE of 124.73 on the external test set. After fine-tuning on CLX-34 data, mean test-set MSE decreased to 31.78 (n = 100), 12.97 (n = 200), 10.10 (n = 300), 9.74 (n = 400), 7.49 (n = 500), 8.56 (n = 600), and 4.37 (n = 700). The corresponding de novo model trained from random initialization achieved mean MSE values of 84.33, 55.84, 34.30, 27.21, 14.17, 7.42, and 7.42, respectively. Fine-tuning from the synthetic backbone therefore outperformed de novo training at six of the seven matched training-set sizes, specifically at n = 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 700, with the largest absolute benefit observed in the most data-constrained settings (Fig. 5).

Discussion

This study produced two principal findings. First, a DDPM trained on a modest training corpus generated synthetic images that subspecialist reviewers could not reliably distinguish from real radiographs. Identification accuracy was near chance, inter-rater agreement on real-image selection was low, and subjective realism ratings did not significantly differ between real and synthetic images. Second, the synthetic data demonstrated downstream utility for transfer learning. A synthetic-pretrained model improved landmark localization on an external dataset under data-constrained conditions.

Anatomically faithful synthetic neuroimaging data offer a practical solution to longstanding limitations in RWD. Neurosurgical patient volumes are often small and unevenly distributed across sites, constraining the assembly of large, representative training sets [1–4]. By contrast, once a DDPM is trained, synthetic images can be generated at scale, enabling greater availability of open-access datasets for academic research and ML model development. Clinical data are also heterogeneous and frequently incomplete [5, 6], whereas synthetic images are generated in standardized format and can augment dataset sizes. The labor necessary to de-identify RWD and coordinate data-sharing agreements is significant [6–9]. Synthetic images, which are not tied to specific individuals, can reduce this burden and lower the privacy risks of sharing data between sites. Synthetic datasets may be generated from institutional data and shared in place of RWD to support large-scale, multi-institutional dataset curation. Synthetic datasets may also be generated to preserve the informational value of clinical data beyond the confines of data retention periods.

This work adds to a growing body of evidence demonstrating the fidelity, utility, and privacy of synthetic data. Similar to past evaluations of GAN-based chest X-ray images [22, 23], our evaluation of DDPM-generated spine radiographs demonstrated high expert-perceived realism under blinded review. Our study expands on this literature in several ways. First, while chest X-ray pipelines have been trained on datasets numbering in the hundreds of thousands (e.g., MIMIC-CXR-JPG > 370k; CheXpert > 220k) [24, 25], neuroimaging datasets are typically several orders of magnitude smaller. Accordingly, we achieved comparable expert-perceived realism despite training on a much smaller corpus (< 5k lateral radiographs). Importantly, our nearest-neighbor audit found no visually explicit reproduction, mitigating concerns that high realism merely reflected memorization or overfitting despite the smaller training corpus. Second, we broadened our blinded validation beyond radiologists alone to a multi-expert evaluation that included spine fellowship-trained neurosurgeons as well. Third, we used a head-to-head design that allowed reviewers to compare one real image with three synthetic images within the same quartet rather than rating images in isolation. This comparative format likely increased sensitivity to subtle inconsistencies across images, yet accuracy and inter-rater agreement remained low.

Beyond situating our results within the landscape of synthetic imaging data, it is instructive to note parallel progress in synthetic tabular neurosurgical data. Recent work has demonstrated that synthetic cohorts can preserve outcome-relevant signals for real-world analyses and expand dataset size for model training [16]. Our findings extend this utility to radiographic transfer learning. Synthetic pre-training substantially accelerated learning and improved downstream performance, mitigating challenges often posed by the limited availability of large annotated clinical imaging datasets.

The external benchmark itself was deliberately challenging. The CLX-34 cohort included a heterogeneous mix of spinal pathology, surgical hardware, and differing acquisition contexts, including flexion and extension views—none of which were comparatively represented in the CSXA training corpus. In addition, CLX-34 was developed around a 34-keypoint schema which did not directly match the CSXA annotation space [28, 36]. This heterogeneity likely contributed to the weak zero-shot performance. However, it also makes the low-data fine-tuning gains more meaningful because they were demonstrated under realistic sources of radiographic variation across datasets and centers, rather than on a narrowly standardized holdout. These findings suggest that either a shareable synthetic corpus or a pretrained backbone can provide substantial utility for ML tasks in low-data settings.

Beyond research and ML model development, shareable datasets may provide educational opportunities to create large-scale teaching banks for trainees to more readily practice radiograph interpretation. In addition to trainee education, synthetic images can provide examples of pathological and non-pathological images for patient education (e.g., to demonstrate the difference between lateral cervical spine X-rays of a degenerative and a normal spine). Our release of a pooled synthetic lateral cervical spine dataset aims to catalyze these use cases [37].

Despite the promising results, a few limitations must be considered. First, all CSXA images were cropped and resampled to 256 × 256 pixels, which simplified modeling but may have reduced fine anatomical detail and altered aspect ratios. Second, FID is not a radiology-native metric and may miss clinically meaningful differences even when global image distributions appear similar. Third, pseudolandmarks for the synthetic dataset were generated automatically from a model trained on real CSXA labels, and no manual quality-control filtering was applied at the pseudolandmark stage. Labelling errors in this intermediate step could have constrained the downstream benefit of synthetic pretraining.

A further practical limitation is that curating the released synthetic dataset still required manual quality control to remove gross anatomical abnormalities. Even so, this workflow remained considerably simpler than negotiating a formal data-sharing agreement for data transfer. This framework may also shorten the time to collaboration relative to conventional cross-center data sharing [10]. Nevertheless, future work should automate image screening (e.g., anomaly detection/quality assessment models) such that large-scale synthetic dataset and model release becomes less dependent on manual review. Additional investigation is also warranted to characterize patterns of DDPM-generated anatomical anomalies and to determine the extent to which DDPMs preserve the prevalence and distribution of pathological anatomy present in training data.

Finally, while our negative nearest-neighbor audit reduces concern that reviewer-level realism simply reflected memorization of the training set, it cannot prove the complete absence of privacy risk. Future work should incorporate more rigorous privacy auditing, including adversarial stress tests across varying training-set sizes and sampling settings, and should contextualize findings within evolving privacy and regulatory landscapes.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that DDPM-generated lateral cervical spine radiographs can achieve expert-perceived realism while also supporting a practical downstream ML application. Synthetic images trained from an open cervical spine atlas were not reliably distinguished from real scans, and synthetic pretraining improved external landmark localization in low-data settings. These findings support synthetic radiographs as both a shareable research asset and a useful pretraining resource for ML applications otherwise constrained by limited data availability.
