The effect of herd-level passive immunity in dairy farms on survival probability during the first year of life
Authors: Shuji KAYASAKI, Rika FUKUMORI, Shin OIKAWA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examined the impact of passive immunity at the herd level on survival probability during the first year of life for successful passive immunity (SPI) farms (n=19), <20% of the proportion of newborn calves with serum immunoglobulin (Ig) G concentrations of <10.0 g/L, and failure of passive immunity (FPI) farms (n=20) with ≥20%. The proportions of serum IgG concentrations on SPI farms were clearly shifted toward the high-concentration strata compared with FPI farms. SPI farms showed significantly (P<0.001) higher survival probability than FPI farms. These results suggest that having the proportion of higher serum IgG concentrations at the herd level may increase survival probability.

Newborn calves lack placental antibody transfer and have underdeveloped immune systems, making them vulnerable to various infectious diseases. Therefore, absorption of immunoglobulin (Ig) from colostrum is essential for postnatal immune acquisition. This protects calves from exposure to common pathogens until their immune systems become partially functional, typically 2 to 4 weeks after birth [3]. Since the G fraction constitutes the most abundant Ig in colostrum, accounting for 85–90% [5], the blood IgG concentration in newborn calves is used as an indicator for evaluating the transfer of passive immunity. At the individual level, higher serum IgG concentrations in calves have been reported to correlate with increased non-diseased probability and survival probability [9, 16]. Calves with serum IgG concentrations below 10 g/L or equivalent total protein concentrations are referred to as having failure of passive immunity (transfer), and they frequently experience morbidity, mortality, and culling [4, 8, 9, 14,15,16, 18]. At the herd level, stratification based on the serum IgG (total protein) concentration has been reported to reduce these risks, depending on the proportion within each stratum [8,9,10, 20]. Studies on the transfer of passive immunity at the herd level, as well as medium- to long-term studies including the post-weaning period, are scarce. Our previous study [8] concerned the transfer of passive immunity at the herd level, but it was a short-term investigation conducted during the first month after birth. Therefore, this study was conducted to evaluate the effectiveness of passive immunity at the herd level from the perspectives of survival probability during the first year of life and serum IgG concentration distribution.

This study was conducted as a retrospective study. It targeted 39 dairy farms in Teshikaga-cho, eastern Hokkaido, where Holstein cows are the main breed and more than 50 calves are expected to be born annually. The actual annual number of Holstein calves born ranged from 32 to 430 per farm, with 18 free-stall farms and 21 tie-stall or stanchion farms. At the farms, calves were fed colostrum at least once within 24 hr after birth was confirmed. Colostrum was defined as the first milk expressed after calving (including frozen and pasteurized milk) or commercially available colostrum replacer. To understand the transfer of passive immunity in calves, we used apparently healthy, untreated 381 Holstein heifers and bull calves from single pregnancies. Additionally, the veterinarian assessed the health status during blood draw. Normal suckling was observed, and individuals with abnormally small body size or abnormalities in the umbilicus, feces, and respiration were excluded. The information on the causes of death was obtained from the database of the Livestock Mutual Aid System of the Agricultural Mutual Aid Association, and information on the date of death from birth to 365 days of age was obtained from the Cattle Individual Identification Information Search Service (https://www.id.nlbc.go.jp) of the National Livestock Breeding Center. In this study, death includes both natural death and euthanasia when an animal loses its breeding value. All animals were treated appropriately following the Laboratory Animal Control Guidelines of Rakuno Gakuen University, which essentially conform to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health in the United States [17].

Based on the diagnostic criteria for transfer of passive immune at the herd level [10], tested calves were at least half a day past their first milk feeding after birth and blood was drawn between 18 hr and 5 days after birth. The number of blood samples was also based on the same criteria [10], with a minimum of 12 samples per farm as a baseline; however, on farms with fewer than 100 calves born annually, 6 to 8 samples were obtained. Blood collection was conducted from December 2019 to April 2020. Almost samples were obtained by March, except a few ones. Centrifuged serum was stored at −20°C until analysis.

The procedure for analysis of serum IgG concentrations was as follows. After first seeding polystyrene microwell strips with sheep anti-bovine IgG (1 mg/mL) (Bethyl Laboratories, Inc., Montgomery, TX, USA) diluted 100-fold in IgG buffer (50 mM Tris, 0.14 M NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 8.0), the strips were coated by reaction at room temperature for 1 hr. They were then washed five times by hand and blocked with blocking solution (50 mM Tris, 0.14 M NaCl, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 8.0) for 30 min. The serum was thawed at room temperature and immediately placed on ice after thawing and manipulated. It was diluted 200,000-fold in ELISA coating buffer (carbonic acid-bicarbonate buffer, pH 9.6), seeded with a standard (Bovine IgG, Bethyl Laboratories, Inc.), and reacted for 1 hr, and then washed five times by hand. Sheep anti-bovine IgG HRP-conjugated antibody (Bethyl Laboratories, Inc.) diluted 100,000- to 150,000-fold was applied and allowed to react at room temperature for 1 hr, then washed five times by hand. TMB (Sera Care Life Sciences, Inc., Milford, MA, USA) was applied and allowed to react for 15 min in the dark, and then the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (iMark, Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA).

For the transfer of passive immunity, serum IgG concentration stratification was performed using the criteria established by Lombart et al. [9] with concentrations ≥25.0, 18.0–24.9, 10.0–17.9, and <10.0 g/L. Herd-level evaluation was conducted using the criteria established by Kayasaki et al. [8]. Namely, farms were classified as those with successful passive immunity (SPI) (n=19) if the proportion of serum IgG of <10.0 g/L was below 20%, and those with failure of passive immunity (FPI) (n=20) if the proportion of serum IgG <10.0 g/L was 20% or more.

The causes of death were classified with reference to Kayasaki et al. [7], ensuring that there was no overlap between individuals. Deaths with no history of veterinary treatment were classified as unknown deaths. Cases with a history of treatment were classified as digestive diseases (including abnormal stool such as diarrhea), respiratory diseases (involving abnormal lung sounds on auscultation), concurrent digestive and respiratory diseases (involving both), or other diseases (if none of the above applied). Antibiotics were used in all cases of treatment.

This study used Excel Statistics ver. 4.09 (SSRI, Tokyo, Japan), a dedicated program for statistical analysis. The statistical significance level was set at less than 5%. Intergroup comparisons were performed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Kaplan-Meier method (generalized Wilcoxon test, Gehan-Breslow) for survival probability.

Regarding the number of individuals (proportion) by serum IgG concentration, on SPI farms, there were 99 (53%) with levels ≥25.0 g/L, 28 (15%) with those of 18.0–24.9 g/L, 46 (25%) with levels of 10.0–17.9 g/L, and 13 (7%) with <10.0 g/L. On the FPI farms, these figures were 45 (23%) ≥25.0 g/L, 18 (9%) at 18.0–24.9 g/L, 52 (27%) at 10.0–17.9 g/L, and 80 (41%) with levels of <10.0 g/L. Significant differences between the SPI and FPI farms were observed in these numbers of individuals (P<0.001).

On SPI farms, female was 141 (76%) and male was 45 (24%); annual number of births ≥100 was 105 (56%) and <100 was 81 (44%); blood draw ≥3 days of age was 75 (40%) and <3 days of age was 111 (60%). On the FPI farms, female was 141 (72%) and male was 54 (28%); annual number of births ≥100 was 109 (56%) and <100 was 86 (44%); blood draw ≥3 days of age was 83 (43%) and <3 days of age was 112 (57%). There were no differences between herds regarding sex, annual number of births, and days until blood draw.

Lombert et al. [9] proposed herd-level criteria to reduce calf mortality and morbidity based on the serum IgG concentration. These criteria require that the proportion of calves with serum IgG concentrations ≥25.0, 18.0–24.9, 10.0–17.9, and <10 g/L be >40%, 30%, 20%, and <10%, respectively. In this study, these overall criteria were not met. When restricted to the SPI farms, the corresponding proportions were 53%, 15%, 25%, and 7%, respectively. Except for cows in which the serum IgG level of 10.0–17.9 g/L became slightly greater, the samples largely met the criterion established by Lombert et al. [9]. In other words, this indicated that SPI farms with a proportion of serum IgG of <10.0 g/L below 20% was synonymous with meeting the criteria [9]. Given that half of the farms in this study were SPI farms, it was considered that achieving SPI status at the herd level was by no means impossible.

In this study, the survival probability showed that the vast majority of deaths occurred within the first 30 days of life, with deaths after 30 days being occasional (Figs. 1, 2). This trend is common across many regions [1, 2, 6, 9, 11,12,13, 16, 19, 21], suggesting that the first month of life is the most critical period for growth. Furthermore, among the rearing management practices during the early lactation period, becoming FPI farms due to colostrum feeding was a factor contributing to the high mortality and culling risk during the first month of life [8]. In other words, colostrum feeding was thought to significantly affect survival probability during the first year of life.

Serum IgG concentrations showed a tendency for survival probability to increase as they reached higher levels (Figs. 1, 2, Table 1). These results were consistent with other studies [4, 8, 9, 14,15,16, 18], indicating that disease resistance increases proportionally with the serum IgG concentration. Regarding the transfer of passive immunity at the herd level, it was suggested that reducing the size of the lowest stratum (serum IgG <10 g/L) and increasing that of the strata above it would be effective.

During the first month of life in this study, digestive diseases were the primary causes of death, and this trend was consistent across all calves on the farms [7]. Furthermore, calves that died of digestive diseases during this period tended to have serum IgG concentrations below 10 g/L, and most of them were from FPI farms (Table 2). This suggests that FPI farms were likely operating in environments with an elevated risk of infectious disease. Even among individuals with serum concentrations of IgG ≥10 g/L, SPI farms showed higher survival probability than FPI farms (Fig. 2, Table 1). Furthermore, individuals with serum IgG levels of <10 g/L originating from SPI farms showed no significant reduction in survival probability compared to those with IgG ≥10 g/L on SPI farms (Fig. 2, Table 1). These results indicate the herd safety of being born on SPI farms and underscores the importance of controlling passive immunity at the herd level. It should be noted that after one month of age, the number of unknown deaths without treatment increased, and the cause of death could not be determined. On the other hand, a characteristic of colostrum feeding at FPI farms was a tendency toward lower reserves of frozen stored colostrum and higher frequency of colostrum replacer use [8]. Regarding colostrum replacers, it was deemed necessary to verify the conditions under which high serum IgG concentrations of 10 g/L or more can be achieved.

This study examined the effect of herd-level passive immunity on survival probability during the first year of life. When the criterion of a serum IgG level of <10.0 g/L in below 20% of newborn calves was met on a farm, the strata with higher serum IgG concentrations became larger, leading to increased survival probability. By implementing this population strategy, dairy farms can increase survival probability during the rearing period and expect economic benefits.
