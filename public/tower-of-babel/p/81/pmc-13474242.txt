Serum and local vancomycin concentrations following intrawound application in elective lumbar spinal fusion: A secondary pharmacokinetic analysis of the vancomycin arm of a prospective randomized controlled trial
Authors: Lukas Florian Panzenböck, George Abdel Nour, Damian Leo Dürrschnabel, Matthias Pallamar, Petra Krepler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntrawound vancomycin powder is widely used as an adjunct to systemic prophylaxis in spine surgery, yet prospective pharmacokinetic data are limited and concerns about systemic absorption persist. We report the prespecified pharmacokinetic endpoints of a randomized controlled trial, quantifying serum and local wound-drain vancomycin concentrations after intrawound application.MethodsThis is a secondary pharmacokinetic analysis of the vancomycin arm of a prospective, patient-blinded, randomized controlled trial. The 140 patients in this arm underwent elective 1- or 2-level posterior lumbar interbody fusion (August 2015–April 2019) and received 1 g intrawound vancomycin powder additional to routine i.v. cefuroxime prophylaxis. Serum vancomycin was measured on postoperative days 1, 2, 3, and 5 and wound-drain concentrations at 24 and 48 hours.ResultsSerum vancomycin was available for 106 to 110 of 140 patients on days 1 to 2 and remained negligible (day 1: 0.10 ± 0.48 µg/mL; day 5: 0.00 µg/mL); it was detectable in 7 of 106 patients (6.6%) on day 1, and no value exceeded 4.20 µg/mL. Wound-drain concentrations (104–105 of 140 patients) were supratherapeutic (day 1: 969.33 ± 798.08 µg/mL; day 2: 709.01 ± 470.23 µg/mL), with 100% of samples above the MRSA MIC₉₀ (2 µg/mL).ConclusionsIn elective lumbar fusion, 1 g intrawound vancomycin produced supratherapeutic local wound-fluid concentrations with negligible systemic absorption, supporting its rationale as an adjunct to systemic prophylaxis.

Introduction

Surgical site infections (SSIs) remain a significant complication following lumbar spinal fusion surgery, occurring in 2% to 12% of cases despite standardized perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. SSIs lead to prolonged hospitalization, additional surgical procedures, increased healthcare costs, and diminished patient outcomes. The causative organisms are predominantly gram-positive, with Staphylococcus aureus—including methicillin-resistant strains (MRSA)—as the most frequently isolated pathogen [1,2].

Systemic perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis with first- or second-generation cephalosporins represents the current standard of care [5]. However, systemic administration may not achieve adequate local tissue concentrations at the surgical site, particularly in avascular areas such as bone graft and instrumentation surfaces [2,3]. This pharmacokinetic limitation provides the rationale for local antibiotic delivery as an adjunct strategy.

In this context, intrawound application of vancomycin powder has gained increasing popularity as an adjunct to systemic prophylaxis in spine surgery. Multiple retrospective studies and meta-analyses have suggested a reduction in SSI rates with local vancomycin application [[1], [2], [3], [4],6]. However, heterogeneous study designs and variable dosing protocols limit the comparability of existing data.

Despite growing clinical adoption, pharmacokinetic data from prospective randomized trials remain limited. Concerns regarding systemic absorption persist and limit broader adoption [7]. Characterizing the pharmacokinetic profile—specifically the magnitude and duration of local versus systemic vancomycin concentrations—is a prerequisite for the rational use of this technique. The parent randomized controlled trial was primarily powered to detect a difference in surgical-site infection rate between intrawound vancomycin and standard systemic prophylaxis; those efficacy results, together with the safety and long-term outcomes of intrawound vancomycin application, are being reported in separate manuscripts. The present report is a prespecified secondary pharmacokinetic analysis restricted to the vancomycin arm.

The objective of this study was to quantify serum and local wound drain vancomycin concentrations following intrawound application of 1 g vancomycin powder in elective PLIF surgery.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study reports a prespecified secondary pharmacokinetic analysis of the vancomycin arm of the iVRCT trial (Intrawound Vancomycin Randomized Controlled Trial), a prospective, randomized controlled trial conducted at the Department of Spine Surgery, Orthopedic Hospital Speising, Vienna, Austria, between August 2015 and April 2019. The pharmacokinetic outcomes reported here were prespecified in the trial protocol. Primary efficacy outcomes (surgical-site infection rate, depth and pathogen) and the safety and long-term outcomes of intrawound vancomycin application are outside the scope of this pharmacokinetic report and will be reported in separate manuscripts. The trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT02631408) and received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the City of Vienna (EK 14-117-0814).

Patient population

Adult patients (age ≥18 years) scheduled for elective primary 1- or 2-level lumbar spinal fusion for degenerative disc disease, spondylolisthesis (Grade I or II), or lumbar spinal stenosis were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included revision surgery, multilevel procedures (≥3 levels), acute trauma, active or previous spinal infection, documented allergy to vancomycin or cefuroxime, immunosuppressive therapy, and chronic corticosteroid use. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrolment.

Randomization and blinding

Patients were randomized in a 1:1 ratio using computer-generated block randomization (block size 5) to either the vancomycin group or the control group (not included in this report). This trial was patient-blind: participants were blinded to treatment allocation. Outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group allocation throughout the study period. Surgeons were not blinded due to the inherent nature of the intervention (topical application of powder). Blinding was maintained until completion of the 1-year follow-up period.

Intervention

Patients in the vancomycin group received 1 g of vancomycin powder (Vancomycin Xellia, Xellia Pharmaceuticals, sterile, preservative-free vancomycin hydrochloride) applied directly into the surgical wound immediately before layered closure. The powder was distributed evenly over the posterior spinal elements, implants, and surrounding soft tissue under direct visualization by the operating surgeon. All patients in both groups received standard intravenous perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis consisting of 3 g cefuroxime (in-house standard at time of the trial) administered 30 minutes before skin incision. No extended postoperative antibiotic prophylaxis was administered.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed as posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) using a standard midline posterior approach by experienced spine surgeons (>10 years of experience). Pedicle screw instrumentation with cage-based interbody reconstruction was employed uniformly across all cases. Interbody cages were composed of either polyetheretherketone (PEEK) or coated PEEK material. Autograft was used to fill the intervertebral space surrounding the interbody cage. The surgical technique was homogeneous across the study cohort.

Wound drainage protocol

Two subfascial wound drains were routinely placed in all patients at the conclusion of surgery. The drains were maintained for a fixed period of 48 hours postoperatively, irrespective of drainage volume. At 24 hours postoperatively, 1 drain bag was exchanged; the vancomycin concentration measured from this bag constitutes the Day 1 drain sample, representing the 0 to 24-hour postoperative interval. At 48 hours postoperatively, both drains were removed; the vancomycin concentration was measured from the newer bag, representing the 24 to 48-hour interval (Day 2 drain sample).

Pharmacokinetic monitoring

Serum vancomycin concentrations were measured on postoperative days 1, 2, 3, and 5. Blood samples were drawn in the morning (between 6:00 and 8:00 AM) and analyzed using validated immunoassay techniques (detection limit: 0.29 µg/mL). Drain fluid vancomycin concentrations were analyzed as described above using the same immunoassay methodology. Following a protocol amendment, day-3 and day-5 blood sampling was discontinued from subject 265 onward—day-3 and day-5 serum vancomycin having been consistently negligible in the earlier participants—to reduce the phlebotomy and administrative burden, while day-1 and day-2 serum sampling and the wound-drain sampling were retained unchanged. Consequently, the 19 vancomycin-arm patients enrolled from subject 265 onward did not contribute day-3 or day-5 serum samples.

Outcome measures

The primary pharmacokinetic outcomes were: (1) serum vancomycin concentrations over time (Days 1, 2, 3, 5); and (2) drain fluid vancomycin concentrations (Days 1 and 2). The secondary outcome was the proportion of patients with any detectable serum vancomycin (≥0.29 µg/mL) and the proportion of wound drain samples exceeding the MIC₉₀ for MRSA (2 µg/mL).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Vancomycin concentration data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.x; SciPy and NumPy libraries). Two-tailed p-values <.05 were considered statistically significant. Serum results below the assay's limit of detection (0.29 µg/mL) were included in all analyses as 0 (undetectable); the denominator at each time point is the number of patients in whom the assay was performed.

Results

Completeness of pharmacokinetic data

The number of patients contributing pharmacokinetic data at each time point is summarized in Table 2. Serum was available for 106, 110, 92 and 85 of the 140 patients on days 1, 2, 3 and 5 (75.7%, 78.6%, 65.7% and 60.7%), and wound-drain fluid for 104 and 105 (74.3% and 75.0%). The lower day-3/day-5 completeness is largely by design: day-3 and day-5 sampling was discontinued from subject 265 onward (Methods), so the 19 patients enrolled thereafter contributed no such samples; among the 121 earlier patients, day-3/day-5 completeness was 76.0% and 70.2%. The remaining gaps reflect logistic sampling failures.

Patients with versus without an available sample did not differ significantly in any recorded baseline or surgical characteristic (all p > .05; Supplementary Table S1); a clinically driven sampling bias is therefore unlikely, although unmeasured factors cannot be excluded.

Discussion

This prespecified secondary pharmacokinetic analysis of the vancomycin arm of the BLINDED trial demonstrates that intrawound application of 1 g vancomycin powder in elective PLIF produces high local wound concentrations with negligible systemic absorption. Serum levels remained below the detection threshold in the majority of patients at all time points, while drain fluid concentrations exceeded the MIC₉₀ for MRSA by more than 2 orders of magnitude on postoperative day 1.

Comparison with published literature

The present findings are consistent with prior pharmacokinetic studies of intrawound vancomycin. In posterior instrumented thoracolumbar fusion (2 g; n = 178), Sweet et al. [1] reported mean drain concentrations of 1,457 µg/mL on the day of surgery, declining to 128 µg/mL by postoperative day 3, while serum vancomycin was undetectable (assay limit 0.6 µg/mL) in 80% of patients. Armaghani et al. [8] (pediatric deformity; 1 g; n = 25) measured mean drain concentrations of 403 µg/mL on postoperative day 0 and mean serum levels of 2.5 µg/mL. Gans et al. [9] (pediatric deformity; 0.5 g; n = 87) found serum vancomycin undetectable (<2.0 µg/mL) in all but 1 patient. In a double-blind randomized controlled trial of posterior lumbar interbody fusion (1 g; n = 78), Han et al. [10] reported a median drain concentration of 674 µg/mL on postoperative day 1 with serum vancomycin undetectable (<0.24 µg/mL) through postoperative day 5. Murphy et al. [11] (instrumented spinal fusion; 1 g; n = 24) similarly found negligible serum uptake (23 of 24 patients <3.3 mg/L), and Schär et al. [12] reported negligible systemic uptake after suprafascial application in the VANCO trial. In the nonspine setting, Johnson et al. [13] reported higher serum levels peaking at 4.7 µg/mL at 12 hours after 2 g intrawound vancomycin in total joint arthroplasty (n = 34), with mean drain concentrations of 922 µg/mL at 3 hours declining to 207 µg/mL at 24 hours.

The serum concentrations reported by Armaghani et al. [8] (mean 2.5 µg/mL; pediatric deformity; n = 25; 1 g) and Johnson et al. [13] (peak 4.7 µg/mL at 12 hours; total joint arthroplasty; n = 34; 2 g) exceed those measured here. Four factors differ between these studies and the present cohort: dose (2 g vs. 1 g), application compartment (subfascial spinal placement onto bone graft and instrumentation vs. peri-articular placement; suprafascial application by Schär et al. [12] likewise yielded undetectable serum levels), sampling time (12-hour peak vs. first sample at 18–24 hours), and patient population (pediatric body composition in Armaghani et al.). These factors cannot be separated with the available data.

Clinical implications

The pharmacokinetic profile—high local, negligible systemic concentrations—supports the rationale for intrawound vancomycin as an adjunct to systemic prophylaxis. In wound-drain fluid, vancomycin concentrations exceeded the MRSA MIC breakpoint (2 µg/mL) by a mean factor of 485 on day 1 and 355 on day 2 and remained above this breakpoint in all sampled patients throughout the 48-hour drain period. These measurements confirm that a large amount of drug is present and continues to elute into the wound fluid during this interval. They do not, however, establish the concentration achieved at the bone-graft or instrumentation surface, where surgical-site infection frequently originates; drain-fluid concentration represents pooled, solubilized drug and may not reflect the heterogeneous distribution of drug across the surgical bed. The present data should therefore be interpreted as evidence of high local drug availability rather than as a direct measure of the bactericidal concentration achieved at any specific tissue surface.

The mean serum concentration was 0.10 µg/mL on postoperative day 1 and declined to 0.02 µg/mL on day 2 and to undetectable levels by day 5; no individual value exceeded 4.20 µg/mL—well below the lower bound of the therapeutic monitoring range for systemic vancomycin. Intrawound application of 1 g vancomycin powder therefore does not produce measurable systemic exposure in elective primary PLIF.

Local elution depends on the volume and composition of the wound fluid. Drain output did not correlate with drain concentration (Fig. 3), indicating that elution is not drainage-volume-dependent under the conditions of elective primary PLIF. Effects of larger hematoma or dead-space volumes, as in revision or multilevel surgery, were not assessed.

Concerns have been raised that the supratherapeutic local concentrations produced by intrawound vancomycin may approach or exceed in vitro cytotoxicity thresholds. Goldschmidt et al. [14] reported dose-dependent dural-fibroblast cytotoxicity over a range (40–4,000 µg/mL) that overlaps with the drain concentrations measured here, and Eder et al. [15] raised analogous concerns regarding osteoblast function and bone remodeling at high local doses. In vivo, however, Mendoza et al. [16] found that intrawound vancomycin at clinically used doses did not impair fusion or radiographic bridging-bone formation in a rat spinal model. Consistent with the latter, no local complications attributable to vancomycin—wound dehiscence, irritation, necrosis, or impaired healing—were recorded in the present cohort during the initial follow-up period of 1 year. In patients who subsequently underwent revision or other surgery at the same site, the surgical bed showed no atypical macroscopic appearance, no excessive scarring and no increased tissue friability.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the prospective randomized patient-blind design, dual monitoring of serum and wound drain vancomycin concentrations with a standardized fixed drain protocol, and the large sample size (n = 140) compared with prior pharmacokinetic studies. The standardized 48-hour drain protocol and fixed blood sampling time points reduce variability in concentration measurements.

Several limitations apply. Incomplete pharmacokinetic data—with serum vancomycin measurements available for 60.7% to 78.6% of patients—may introduce selection bias. The fixed 48-hour drain protocol precluded assessment of vancomycin concentrations beyond this time point. Vancomycin concentrations were measured in drain fluid rather than directly in tissue, which represents pooled solubilized drug and may not reflect the heterogeneity of drug distribution across the entire wound; microdialysis would be required to characterize tissue concentrations directly. The study was performed at a single center only.

Future directions

Several questions remain for future investigation. First, dose-response studies comparing different intrawound vancomycin doses (eg, 0.5 g vs. 1 g vs. 2 g) could help define the optimal dose that maximizes local concentrations while maintaining negligible systemic absorption. Second, extended pharmacokinetic monitoring beyond 48 hours—using alternative sampling methods such as microdialysis—would clarify how long local bactericidal concentrations persist after drain removal. Third, the pharmacokinetic behavior of intrawound vancomycin in revision surgery, where tissue vascularity and wound characteristics differ from primary procedures, warrants separate investigation. Finally, multicenter prospective studies with standardized pharmacokinetic sampling protocols would strengthen the evidence base for dosing recommendations across different surgical indications.

Conclusions

Intrawound application of 1 g vancomycin powder in elective PLIF surgery produced supratherapeutic local concentrations (mean drain: 969.33 µg/mL on day 1, 709.01 µg/mL on day 2) with negligible systemic absorption (mean serum: 0.10 µg/mL on Day 1, 0.00 µg/mL on day 5). Only 6.6% of patients showed any detectable serum vancomycin on day 1, and no patient exceeded 4.20 µg/mL at any time point. These findings characterize the pharmacokinetic profile of intrawound vancomycin in lumbar spinal fusion.
