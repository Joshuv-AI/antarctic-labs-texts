Physician burnout in Maryland: young female physicians at highest risk during COVID-19
Authors: Shannon Hritz, Andrea Flores Umanzor, Jimin Hwang, Catherine Johannesen, Padmini Ranasinghe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo examine the prevalence of burnout, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) among Maryland physicians during the COVID-19 pandemic and identify demographic and professional characteristics associated with increased risk.MethodsA cross-sectional, online survey was conducted between December 2020 and February 2021 using the Maryland Medical Society (MedChi) physician database. A total of 773 physicians participated, excluding 24 physicians in training. Participants reported demographics, practice characteristics, and COVID-19 exposure. Screening tools for burnout were the Maslach Burnout Inventory, depression with the Patient Health Questionnaire-2; and PTSD with the Short PTSD Rating Interview. Data were analyzed using t tests, chi-squared tests, and logistic regression.ResultsThe burnout screening was positive, and as reported by most respondents, notable rates of depression and PTSD were also observed. These outcomes were more prevalent among female-identifying physicians, those in primary care, physicians with fewer years of practice, and those with direct exposure to COVID-19 patients. Burnout was associated with higher rates of depression, PTSD, and intent to leave medicine.ConclusionsThe COVID-19 pandemic significantly impacted the mental health of Maryland physicians, particularly among specific high-risk groups. These findings highlight the need for systemic interventions to reduce occupational stress and support physician well-being. Future research should explore long-term mental health outcomes and evaluate systemic and tailored interventions.

Introduction

Burnout is a state of emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and decreased sense of personal achievement linked to emotionally demanding work. While burnout is distinct from psychiatric illnesses, such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and substance use disorders, these entities have overlapping risk factors, symptoms, and sequelae. Physicians have been identified as a group particularly vulnerable to burnout, as well as depression, suicide, PTSD, and substance use disorders [1–5]. Physician burnout is of utmost concern, as it has been linked to harmful outcomes on patient care and healthcare system costs. Physicians experiencing burnout are more likely to commit medical errors, have lower patient satisfaction scores, and could be included in a malpractice lawsuit [6]. There are also decreased job retention rates among physicians experiencing burnout, which can strain the healthcare system through reduced access to care and high costs associated with turnover [6].

Before the onset of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), occupational stressors, including excessive workloads, burden of administrative tasks and associated inefficiency, work–home conflicts, and perceived loss of autonomy, were known to contribute to burnout and depression among physicians [7], with female gender and early career stage portending particular risk. Since the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, physicians have faced new challenges related to limited resources, longer or more unpredictable shifts, disruptions to social support mechanisms, occupational hazards, witnessed morbidity and mortality, and paucity of knowledge about the disease itself [6].

A study estimated that 62.8% of physicians surveyed in 2021 had experienced one or more symptoms of burnout, an increase from prior estimates of 38.2% in 2020 and 43.9% in 2017 [8,38]. In the face of this growing threat, it is important to understand how factors contributing to burnout and psychiatric sequelae have changed since the onset of COVID-19. More recent data from the American Medical Association (AMA) indicates that physician burnout rates have declined since their peak in 2021; however, the prevalence of burnout remains significant, as reflected by the latest 48.2% of physicians reporting at least one symptom of burnout in 2023.

The COVID-19 pandemic significantly intensified physician burnout, exposing and amplifying pre-existing stressors such as high workloads, emotional exhaustion, and systemic inefficiencies. Understanding these trends highlights the pandemic’s lasting impact on healthcare professionals, emphasizing the urgency to address burnout with sustainable solutions. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the rates of burnout, depression, and PTSD among physicians in Maryland and to identify high-risk characteristics among those who screened positive.

Despite growing national and international awareness of physician burnout during the COVID-19 pandemic, few studies have examined the combined burnout, depression, and PTSD within specific state contexts. This study addresses that gap by focusing on these issues within the state of Maryland’s physicians, providing critical insights into the unique stressors, demographic risk factors, and mental health challenges faced in primary care. These findings are essential for developing effective, tailored strategies to support physician well-being and promote a resilient healthcare workforce moving forward.

Methods

The Maryland physician health during COVID-19 survey

A one-time, online cross-sectional survey to assess physician well-being was developed and administered to physicians in Maryland within the Maryland Medical Society (MedChi) between December 2020 and February 2021. Links to the survey were distributed through email to about 10,000 physicians within the MedChi database. This study focused on covariates such as age, race, and medical specialty to establish associations with mental health outcomes through a hypothesis-driven approach. Primary care was highlighted due to its frontline role during the COVID-19 pandemic. Age and race were included to control demographic confounders.

The survey included questions about demographics (age, gender, ethnicity, marital status, children) and information about practice-setting characteristics (training status, specialty, years in practice, medical school setting, practice setting, use and frequency of telemedicine, and involvement in the care of patients with COVID-19). All participants self-identified under demographics that fit them best when this study was conducted.

Sample selection and exclusion criteria

The study’s sample selection consisted of physicians who are members of Maryland State Medical Society and have their email addresses in the system. Inclusion criteria were broad, requiring only that participants be physicians with access to the online survey, implying internet access and English proficiency.

Survey instrument development

The survey instrument was developed using validated screening tools to assess key mental health outcomes among physicians during the COVID-19 pandemic. Burnout was measured using single-item measures derived from the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI), focusing on emotional exhaustion and depersonalization [9,10,37]. Depression symptoms were screened using the Patient Health Questionnaire-2, which evaluates anhedonia and low mood over the past 2 weeks but does not provide a clinical diagnosis [11]. Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) was assessed using the Short PTSD Rating Interview (SPRINT), an 8-item tool with high sensitivity (∼83%) and specificity (∼92%) [12]. These tools were selected for brevity and prior validation in physician populations, ensuring practicality for the online format. Demographic and practice-setting questions were added to account for confounders.

This study was conducted fully following the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants before their involvement, ensuring voluntary participation and the right to withdraw at any time. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at Johns Hopkins School of Medicine, and measures were taken to protect participant confidentiality and data privacy. The research was designed to minimize risks and contribute to the understanding of physician well-being, aligning with the Declaration’s emphasis on ethical research practices and societal benefit.

Outcome measures

The main outcomes were the proportion of participants who screened positive for burnout, depression, or PTSD according to the survey instrument. Participants were also asked whether they intended to leave their career in medicine and if they regretted becoming a doctor.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical information of the survey participants were presented as mean and standard deviation for continuous variables and percentage for categorical variables. Clinical characteristics of the participants were compared by exposure to patients with COVID-19 and gender with unpaired t-test and Pearson’s chi-squared test for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Comparisons between demographic and practice-setting groups were also made across the three outcome measures of burnout, depression, and PTSD. Multiple logistic regression was performed to identify the association between exposure to COVID-19 patients or gender and burnout, depression, and PTSD, adjusting for age, race, and specialty (primary care). Statistical significance was defined as two-tailed p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.0.4 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

Missing data was excluded in the analysis, and no imputation was performed. Logistic regressions were performed on complete datasets per model.

Twenty-four physicians who were in training were excluded from this analysis. Gender distribution was nearly equal (50.7% female, 49.3% male). The majority of respondents were White (73.1%), followed by Asian (11.6%), Black (8.8%), and other races (6.4%), with Hispanic ethnicity representing 3.7% of the sample. Most physicians were married (80.8%) with children (85.5%), and among married respondents, 52.6% had a spouse working full time.

Primary care specialties comprised 40.4% of the sample, and the majority (69.6%) had more than 20 years of practice experience. Regarding practice settings, half (50.1%) were partners while 39.0% were employed physicians. Patient populations were distributed across urban (35.9%), both urban and rural (37.3%), and rural (12.5%) settings.

Nearly two-thirds (65.7%) had treated COVID-19 patients, and 64.0% worked primarily in outpatient clinic settings post-pandemic. Overall, physicians reported low levels of regret about becoming a doctor (mean 0.5 ± 0.8), though 12.9% expressed intent to leave medicine.

Table 2 shows the demographic and clinical practice characteristics of the participants according to psychiatric outcomes (i.e. burnout, depression, and PTSD). The overall rates of burnout, depression, and PTSD were 58.0%, 12.4%, and 18.9%, respectively. 12.9% of respondents reported intent to leave a career in medicine. Physicians who reported burnout were more likely to be female (61.5% vs. 49.4%, p = 0.03), working in primary care (44.1% vs. 35.3%, p = 0.016), and seeing patients with COVID-19 (69.4% vs. 61.3%, p = 0.021). Burnout was less common in respondents with age >66 (19.9% vs. 41.2%, p < 0.001) and >20 years in practice (61.2% vs. 80.5%, p < 0.001). Physicians who screened positive for burnout reported an intent to leave their medical career more than those who did not (18.1% vs. 5.4%, p < 0.001) and had higher rates of positive screens for PTSD (28.5% vs. 5.2%, p < 0.001) and depression (19.7% vs. 2.3%, p < 0.001).

Burnout risk factors were identified with univariable and multivariable logistic regression (Table 4). In univariate regression, younger age, fewer years in practice, spouse working full-time, primary care practice setting, and care of patients with COVID-19 were associated with burnout. Physicians working in primary care and physicians seeing patients with COVID-19 had higher rates of burnout with odds ratio (OR) 1.45 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.07–1.96) and 1.44 (95% CI 1.06–1.95), respectively. Conversely, respondents with children reported less burnout, with an OR of 0.46 (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.29–0.72). Physicians who were not seeing patients reported less burnout with an OR of 0.3 (95% CI 0.15–0.61).

Discussion

This study aimed to characterize the prevalence of burnout, depression, and PTSD in a physician population in Maryland in the setting of the COVID-19 pandemic. An overwhelming 58% of physicians in this population reported burnout between December 2020 and February 2021, like an estimated 62.8% [13] nationwide in 2021. The prevalence of depression and PTSD in our population was 12.4% and 18.9%, respectively, higher than the national year-prevalence of depression and PTSD in the United States adult population in 2021 (8.4% for depression and 3.6% for PTSD, respectively) [14,15].

The prevalence of burnout observed aligns closely with prior national estimates, underscoring the widespread impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on physician mental health. The finding that female and younger physicians face disproportionate risk is consistent with reports highlighting gender and career-stage related disparities in occupational stress [13]. Our results contribute to a new, state-level context, pertaining to vulnerability of primary care physicians, which has been studied less in previous Maryland-focused research.

This study demonstrated that self-reported female physicians are at significant risk for burnout and PTSD. Female physicians in our cohort were younger, more often in primary care, and more likely to have full-time working spouses. Prior work highlights that female physicians spend 8.5 more hours weekly on household duties than their male peers, with fertility challenges [16] and pay gaps [17] further exacerbating stress. Early-career physicians faced similar burdens, consistent with literature tying burnout to debt, limited autonomy, and work–home conflicts [16,18].

Oftentimes, female physicians must complete medical training during their peak reproductive years [8]. One survey found that 72% of female physicians attributed their obstetric issues to the demands of medical training and job-related fatigue [17]. These fertility challenges may also contribute to higher levels of burnout in female physicians, as psychological stress is common in women struggling to conceive [16]. Prior work has suggested that conflict between work and family responsibilities disproportionately impacts female physicians [16,18]. Additionally, our study suggests that female physicians were more likely to have partners with full-time employment, suggesting that having a partner who was employed full-time likely contributed to female physicians devoting substantially more time to domestic duties than their male counterparts [19]. Interestingly, having children was protective against burnout in our overall population, with Table 4’s predictor suggesting a statistically significant p value (OR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.29–0.72, p = 0.0007). Exploratory analyses of our data set, prompted by this finding, suggest that female participants were more likely to have younger children with age <15, which may be a surrogate of increased personal responsibilities and should be further explored.

Increased rates of burnout and PTSD among female and early career physicians are driven by multiple factors, including greater work–life imbalance, financial pressures [20], pay disparities [21–23], inequities in career advancement [23,24], and domestic responsibilities. Further research is needed to better understand how workplace, societal, and economic factors impact physician well-being across different groups, and to examine how burnout and related mental health issues develop over time among physicians.

Female physicians within our study population were also more likely to work in primary care, a field that is known to have a high risk for burnout [25]. Particularly in the era of COVID, primary care physicians were part of the front line in distributing accurate information and faced barriers to specialty referrals, making them vulnerable to increased burnout [25].

One study found that moral distress from COVID-19, triggered by fear of infecting others, its negative impact on family, and work-related concerns, increased the likelihood of PTSD and burnout [39]. This distress may explain the increase in burnout seen among primary care physicians during the pandemic [26,38,39].

While COVID-19 patient care was associated with burnout in unadjusted analysis, this link dissipated after accounting for age, specialty, and race. This may suggest the pandemic amplified pre-existing systemic issues, rather than acting as an independent driver. Staffing shortages in the non-physician healthcare workforce persist [27], highlighting inefficiencies in the workplace. It is also possible that assimilating new practice-relevant information in response to COVID-19 has led to increased stressors on physicians and the overall healthcare system. Further research may investigate the transformations within our healthcare system post-COVID-19. Such studies should aim to identify system-level risk factors and interventions to mitigate physician burnout in the future.

Strengths and limitations

This study had several strengths. This survey was administered to MedChi, a large and established medical society whose members have varying ages, specialties, experience, and practice settings. Our study population was reflective of the national average age for practicing physicians in the US, listed as 53.2 years in one estimate [28]. The proportion of survey respondents identifying as female was greater than the 36.3% of physicians in the US workforce who are female, as estimated by the AAMC in 2019 [29]. Further, this study utilized validated screening tools for burnout, depression, and PTSD.

Despite this, there are several limitations that warrant consideration. First, this study could harbor bias. It is possible that MedChi’s membership does not represent all Maryland physicians. It is also possible that the physician experience of the COVID-19 pandemic within Maryland may not be generalizable to the broader physician population based on differences in mask mandates, vaccine uptake, and state reimbursement models [11,40,41]. This may seem to limit the ability of this study to capture the experience of physicians in other regions, but the trends were overall similar to what has been described by other groups [30].

The sample may favor physicians who respond to email surveys and or those engaged with MedChi. Furthermore, this survey did not specifically inquire about military service status, limiting our ability to gauge differences based on military service. The response rate of this study was low at 7.73%, which raises the possibility of response or non-response bias. It is possible that we did not observe differences in adjusted models due to the small sample size. Gender categories were binary, and we are therefore unable to assess the stresses or differential outcomes physicians identify as nonbinary. Finally, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to infer causality; longitudinal studies are needed to assess how physician mental health evolves beyond the pandemic.

Potential future interventions

A meta-analysis found that, largely, physician burnout is due to system inefficiencies [31], emphasizing the pressing need for systemic changes, including flexible work hours, salary transparency, leadership support, and opportunities for professional and personal growth in the workplace. These systemic efforts complement possible interventions at the interpersonal and individual levels, which are noted to be effective in burnout reduction [17]. Interventions targeting personal mindset and resiliency skills, have shown improvement in compassion, professional fulfillment, and engagement [32–36]. Because medical societies are comprised of physicians of varying ages, specialties, and demographic characteristics, medical societies can facilitate the development and uptake of such programs. Pandemics, natural disasters, and other critical events highlight the importance of collaboration between medical societies and the healthcare system for the well-being of their employees and affiliates.

Conclusion

In summary, this study highlights elevated levels of burnout among female and early-career physicians in the state of Maryland during the COVID-19 pandemic. Older age was associated with reduced risk, while direct COVID-19 exposure was not independently predictive of psychological distress after accounting for other factors. These findings emphasize the need for both individual and system-level strategies to support physician well-being, with particular emphasis on high-risk groups. Future research is needed to understand the longitudinal impact of COVID-19 on burnout, depression, and PTSD. Research exploring the natural history of burnout and how it may evolve to include comorbid depression and PTSD is also needed to understand whether there may be high-risk periods to target intervention. This study identified that early career female physicians have a higher risk for burnout, and further research is needed to understand how interventions might be tailored to this group.
