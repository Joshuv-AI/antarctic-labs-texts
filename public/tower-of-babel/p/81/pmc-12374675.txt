Viewpoint: childhood developmental disability: recognising the primary role of the family
Authors: Peter Rosenbaum
Published: 2025

Background: our traditional approach to child-onset disability

Our longstanding approach to ‘developmental disability’ has been to focus, quite reasonably, on the child: promoting early detection of developmental impairments, assessing the issues, formulating diagnoses and recommending interventions. We have always recognised, but have likely just taken for granted, that ‘children’ live in ‘families’. We have expected the adult caregiving members of the family to follow our well-intentioned advice and have often judged them critically when they did not. Based on >30 years of research, this Viewpoint offers a personal perspective that argues for an active expansion of our scope of practice to situate ‘family’ (including siblings) as the essential focus of all our work in child health.

Rationale for recognising the centrality of family

Consider four realities about our field.

The first is self-evident: children never seek our help or advice on their own. They always come to our attention with adults—people in the role of parents/caregivers—who bring their concerns about their child to us. Whether the parents themselves have initiated contact with services or been prompted to do so by others’ observations, it is parents and families who ‘present’ their issues to us.

Second, in the 21st century, our ideas about the field of childhood disability have been considerably influenced (and expanded) by WHO’s ICF (International Clasification of Functioning, Disability and Health)) framework for health and CanChild’s animation of these concepts with its F-Words for Child Development. These expansions are the focus of a recent essay designed to explain why and how we need to reframe our thinking.1 In ICF terms, it is a statement of the obvious that ‘family’ is the essential human ‘environment’ of all children. Many people recognise that parents are the world’s experts on their child2: while professionals bring important perspectives and expertise to the issues that affect children’s health and development, family is the constant in their own child’s life and must be respected as such.

Third, considering this scenario within traditional medical terminology and arguing by analogy, the child can be thought of as the family’s ‘presenting complaint’: parents’ concerns are about a ‘part of themselves’ (their child). We take a history (from parents), we assess and evaluate their ‘complaint’ (their child), and we then report our findings, our formulations and our recommended interventions… to families! It is families who are expected to ‘manage’ and ‘comply’ with our best advice….

Fourth, there is considerable evidence of the impact—on parents—of raising children with any long-term condition affecting their child’s health, development or behaviour.3 Both clinical and epidemiological research documents the significantly greater physical and mental health ‘morbidity’ experienced by these parents than by comparable parents raising typically developing children. It is self-evident that the well-being of children impacts the well-being of their parents—but the opposite is equally true and important. Thus, while striving to support parents to promote their child’s development, hopefully improving their well-being as their children thrive and develop, we must also explore how we can offer parent-and-family-directed services addition to those needed by the child, targeted to the needs of the adults and siblings around the child with the developmental challenges. (This expectation means that we also need to be aware of the myriad realities—eg, poverty, poor transport, community attitudes/culture, leisure opportunities, access to natural environments, quality of education/support—that may all have profound impacts on family well-being.)

For all these reasons, we argue to expand our focus on ‘childhood disability’ and set a collaborative and respectful tone with families right from the earliest months of their journey in the health system.

Opportunities to address these ideas and emerging evidence

As argued above, 21st-century thinking about developmental ‘disabilities’ presents a changing—an expanding—landscape within which we can offer our perspectives and advice on long-term childhood issues.1 How this is to be addressed and accomplished may present challenges to both frontline practitioners and the healthcare settings in which we work. There is, however, some low-hanging fruit to help us get started, with evidence to support the impact on families of the application of these approaches to service delivery.

First, most (perhaps all) people working in the field of child health, and most child health organisations, would expect (and claim) to be ‘family-centred’ and to offer ‘family-centred service’ (FCS). What does FCS mean, and what does it look like? The author and his colleagues have been exploring these questions in our research programmes for 30 years. We have published concept papers on what FCS means; created and validated measures of parents’ experiences of FCS4 and whether and how it ‘matters’ to parents5; and begun to identify some of the structural and systemic barriers that make delivering FCS challenging. A more detailed overview of these ideas has recently been included in a book on childhood disability.6

Second, the well-documented impact of long-term childhood conditions on parental well-being compels us to ask what factors might contribute to this ‘morbidity’ and whether some of these stresses might potentially be moderated or even prevented. While these issues are the focus of early explorations, and much work remains to be done, work by the author and his colleagues in Canada and Australia shows what can be done for parents.

The ENabling VISions And Growing Expectations (ENVISAGE) programme for families, co-designed and co-facilitated by service providers and parents, offers 5-hour-long virtual workshops for small groups of parents of young children with any kind of developmental concern.7 The programme—what one parent called ‘Early Intervention for Families’—presents parents with new ways of thinking about health (ICF and F-Words); a focus on family, including siblings; a workshop called ‘Parenting is a dance led by the children’; a session on the importance of self-care by parents; and a workshop to discuss how to use these ideas to support collaborating, communicating and connecting with others. Considerable work is underway in the ENVISAGE programme with analogue versions for service providers, First Peoples (in Australia) and an integrated version for parents and service providers together (currently being analysed).

In summary, what can we do to bring families into the centre of our programmes and services?

Broadly speaking, I believe we can and must expand our thinking, our training and our ways of working with families—respecting their voices, their knowledge of their child and family, their expressed needs and their values. At an organisational level, our services need to see and support the importance of family as our focus. That means encouraging a family-focused approach to what we do and how we do it in terms of the nature and focus of clinical encounters. It also means staffing our programmes appropriately with resources of psychosocially trained professionals to complement the ‘developmental’ therapists and doctors whose roles are so vital. It means walking the talk about truly being family-centred!

Ultimately, we need to emphasise family well-being as a goal every bit as important for children’s well-being as the best of our developmental interventions to promote children’s functioning. Finally, we must seize every opportunity to study the impact—on children, on parents, on service systems—of any expansions or shifts in emphasis we make in our service programmes. There remains much to be learned!
