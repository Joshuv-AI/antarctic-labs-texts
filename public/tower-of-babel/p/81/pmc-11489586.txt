High-frequency shear wave MR elastography of parotid glands: custom driver design and preliminary results
Authors: Vitaliy Atamaniuk, Jun Chen, Marzanna Obrzut, Kevin J. Glaser, Łukasz Hańczyk, Andrii Pozaruk, Krzysztof Gutkowski, Bogdan Obrzut, Wojciech Domka, Richard L. Ehman, Marian Cholewa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Parotid glands are one of the most common sites for salivary gland tumors. Conventional imaging techniques have limited usefulness in the quantitative assessment of the parotid glands, making it difficult to differentiate between healthy tissue and tumors, as well as between benign and malignant tumors. Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) is a non-invasive technique that may potentially overcome these limitations. Nevertheless, due to the size of the parotid gland, increased elastographic resolution is required. This may be achieved by applying shear waves at higher frequencies. However, it also results in stronger attenuation, making the illumination of the parotid challenging. Here, we describe a novel passive driver tailored to the anatomy of the human face, which minimizes the distance shear waves need to travel from the source to the area of interest and thus decreases shear wave attenuation, making high-frequency shear wave MRE feasible.

Introduction

Parotid glands are the most common site for benign salivary gland tumors (up to 70%) and the second most common location for malignant salivary gland tumors (34 to 42%, depending on the tumor type)1. The diagnosis of parotid tumors can employ several imaging tests, including ultrasound (US), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), as well as physical examination by an otolaryngologist palpating the parotid gland, and fine needle aspiration (FNA) cytology, a subtype of biopsy.

Although the majority of parotid tumors are benign, the treatment often necessitates surgery to remove part or all of the parotid gland due to the limited reliability of existing diagnostic techniques in distinguishing between benign and malignant parotid tumors. The variability in malignant tumor morphology and the dependence of FNA cytology specificity and sensitivity on the experience of the operator and pathologist contribute to potential misclassifications2–4.

To enhance the accuracy of parotid disease diagnosis, one potential avenue is to use FNA biopsy with ultrasound guidance. Through this approach, an experienced clinician and cytopathologist can distinguish between benign and malignant tumors with more than 80% accuracy. However, this procedure is invasive and may lead to complications such as hemorrhage, cellulitis at the puncture site, or even facial nerve injury5–8. Some even believe that a biopsy can spread cancer cells and should not be performed.

The introduction of a non-invasive diagnostic method capable of reliably differentiating between benign and malignant parotid tumors would represent a significant advancement in otolaryngology. This could substantially improve patient comfort and reduce the number of unnecessary biopsies and parotidectomies. The evolving technology of magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) holds promise as a potential solution.

MRE is an imaging technique that allows for the quantitative assessment of the biomechanical properties of soft tissues. Currently most widely used to detect and stage liver fibrosis, MRE has emerging applications in evaluating other organs, including the spleen, brain, kidneys, pancreas, uterus, muscles, and prostate9–16.

The value of MRE stems from its unique capability to assess tissue biomechanical parameters, specifically the complex shear modulus or its magnitude, also known as stiffness. The stiffness value of normal and pathological tissue can vary by over 5 orders of magnitude, providing excellent contrast, which is not achievable with conventional imaging techniques like US, CT, or MRI17. A comparable technique to MRE is US shear wave elastography (US-SWE). However, cross-sectional MRI images acquired during an MRE exam offer superior delineation and evaluation of the anatomic features of the parotid gland and the facial nerve7.

The principle of operation of MRE involves three basic steps: (1) inducing shear motion inside the tissue of interest using an external vibration source or intrinsic body motion; (2) imaging the propagating shear waves at different phases using a motion-sensitized MRI sequence; (3) applying an inversion algorithm of choice to the wave data to generate stiffness maps, also referred to as elastograms.

Given the specific location and shape of the parotid glands, conventional, relatively large drum-like or pillow-like MRE passive drivers may not be optimal. Several studies have indicated that a custom transducer tailored to the anatomy of the examined organ is necessary for higher reliability of results18–21.

Moreover, since parotid glands are relatively small structure, a higher shear wave frequency, and hence a shorter wavelength, is required for better spatial resolution of elastographic details. Increased wave frequency, however, results in stronger attenuation, necessitating the passive driver to be located as close to the examined organ as possible.

This study aimed to design and construct a dedicated, patient-friendly passive driver fitted to the human face that enables high-frequency MRE of parotid glands. The performance of this driver was then evaluated using both commercially available 2D MRE and research-only 3D vector MRE.

To fulfill all criteria, a stethoscope-like shape for the passive driver with vibrating pads at its two ends was chosen. This design allowed simultaneous induction of shear waves in both parotid glands. The driver was connected to the active acoustic generator using the same plastic tubing as the one used in a conventional liver MRE driver. It was fixed to the patient using elastic cords, similar to those in surgical masks, or hook and loop straps, ensuring ease of use and fixation. The stethoscope-like shape facilitated the direct placement of vibrating pads over the parotid glands, maintaining constant contact with the patient’s face.

Driver assembly

The novel passive driver for parotid MRE was first demonstrated at the International Society for Magnetic Resonance in Medicine 2023 Annual Meeting in Toronto, Canada22. The base of the driver, an 80 cm long polyvinyl chloride tube, was connected to the vibrating part made of a shorter tube (35 cm long) to give the driver its distinctive stethoscope-like shape (Fig. 1). The ends of the shorter tube were welded together, with the tube slightly flattened for easy inflation and deflation. A polypropylene air filter was placed inside to prevent wall adhesion and allow free airflow.

A plastic T-shaped connector was used to connect the vibrating pads to the longer tube. A flexible plastic rod (2 mm in diameter) inside the longer tube prevented kinking. Elastic cords or hook-and-loop straps were attached to the vibrating pads for easy fixation over the patient’s face. A custom 3D-printed plastic connector was employed to connect the passive driver to the main tubing, ensuring its compatibility with the commercially available active driver used in most MRE centers.

Subjects & study protocol

This prospective study received approval from the institutional review board, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant before enrollment. All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Real-world testing of the designed driver involved three healthy volunteers. One participant, a 45-year-old male, underwent 3D vector MRE of the parotid glands on a 1.5T whole-body Signa HDxt scanner (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA). Two participants, both males aged 26 and 29, underwent 2D MRE on a 1.5-T whole-body unit MR OPTIMA MR360 Advance (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA).

The passive driver was positioned on the subject’s face with vibrating pads directly over the parotid glands. Head & neck coils were utilized to image shear wave propagation within the salivary glands. For additional patient comfort and better adherence of vibrating pads to the face, memory foam blocks were placed between the patient’s head and coil case.

The active driver, located in the technical room, generated acoustic vibrations at different frequencies (60, 90, and 120 Hz for 3D vector MRE, and 60, 80, 100, and 120 Hz for 2D MRE) to assess shear wave propagation quality at varying frequencies. Additionally, to compare the designed driver’s performance with the common approach for MRE of the head, one volunteer underwent an additional 2D MRE exam with the same excitation frequencies and a pillow-like driver typical for brain MRE placed under the head.

For 3D vector MRE, the study protocol included acquiring scout scans, head and neck T2-weighted fat-suppressed anatomical images, and MRE data. A motion-sensitized spin-echo echo-planar imaging (SE-EPI) MRI sequence with trapezoidal motion-encoding gradients (MEGs) matching the oscillating mechanical vibrations from the active driver was used for shear wave propagation data acquisition. The imaging parameters for 3D vector MRE are presented in Table 1. Two different variations of a 3D direct inversion algorithm were tested for processing the 3D wave data – algorithm A, originally developed for liver MRE, and algorithm B originally developed for brain MRE. Algorithm A, the “liver” inversion algorithm, is described by Allen et al.23 Algorithm B, the “brain” inversion algorithm, is described by Lan et al.24 Both methods estimate the vector curl of the 3D wavefield and use a direct inversion of the Helmholtz wave equation using the curl to determine the complex-valued shear modulus. The primary difference between the two algorithms is that the “brain” algorithm does not use the 20 3D directional filters that are used in the “liver” algorithm. That was a design choice to control the kernel size, or processing footprint, of the inversion for brain data to emulate the localized and adaptive processing25. To evaluate the viscoelastic properties of the parotid gland, the real and imaginary components of the complex shear modulus—storage modulus (G’), related to tissue elasticity, and loss modulus (G’’), related to viscous loss—along with the magnitude of the complex shear modulus (|G*|), were calculated. The magnitude of the complex shear modulus is referred to as the “stiffness” (µ).

Due to its wider availability, the feasibility of parotid MRE was also explored using 2D scalar MRE pulse sequence and processing software commonly used for liver MRE in which the wave information for each slice is processed independently of the other slices, and only 1 component of the vector motion (the “motion-encoding direction”) is measured and processed per acquisition. The imaging protocol for 2D MRE included acquiring scout images, T1- and T2-weighted images, and gradient-recalled echo-based (GRE) MRE data. The imaging parameters for 2D MRE exams are summarized in Table 2. A conventional, commercially available 2D multimodal direct inversion (MMDI) algorithm designed for liver MRE was used to generate maps of stiffness (µ or |G*|). Even though it operates on each slice independently, and only uses one component of the vector motion for processing, MMDI still calculates the complex-valued shear modulus, but in most applications it only provides the stiffness (i.e., the magnitude of the complex shear modulus) as an output to the user. The maps of the tissue stiffness distribution are commonly referred to as elastograms.

Image analysis

Shear wave propagation images underwent visual analysis to assess the depth of wave penetration and the reliability of inversion. Two-dimensional masks, in the form of polygonal regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to the parotid glands, were manually drawn on anatomical images by a radiologist with over 20 years of experience. The ROIs were designed to be as large as possible while avoiding artifacts or low-shear wave amplitude areas. The measured viscoelastic properties of the parotid gland were then averaged separately for the left and right glands, as well as collectively for both glands.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of the MRE data acquired in this study was performed using the open-source JASP Statistical Software version 0.18.2.0 (University of Amsterdam, Amsterdam, The Netherlands; https://jasp-stats.org, accessed on January 5, 2024). Median values with interquartile ranges (IQR) for storage modulus, loss modulus, and/or shear stiffness were calculated for all subjects. The within-subject coefficient of variation - a ratio of IQR to the median - was computed to estimate the reproducibility of the results obtained with the novel driver. All calculations were performed separately for the left and right parotid glands and for both glands combined.

Results

The novel driver demonstrated effective performance in both 2D and 3D vector MRE. However, measurable shear waves were obtained only at frequencies ranging from 60 to 100 Hz in the case of 2D MRE, whereas all 3D vector MRE examinations were successful. In comparison, the 2D MRE exam with a pillow-like driver was successful only for a setup with 100 Hz vibration and motion encoding gradient applied in the X direction; other exams failed due to insufficient shear wave amplitude (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The assessment of biomechanical tissue properties through palpation has long been a part of diagnostic protocols, particularly for superficial organs like the parotid glands. While effective for qualitative assessments, palpation is also highly subjective. The introduction of biopsy improved diagnostic accuracy, but invasiveness and potential complications have driven the search for non-invasive, quantitative methods for parotid evaluation.

Magnetic Resonance Elastography (MRE), is now regarded as being as accurate as biopsy in detecting and staging liver fibrosis and has been applied to assess various organs, including the brain, spleen, and kidneys26–28. However, applying MRE to evaluate the biomechanical properties of the parotid gland poses challenges due to its location and size (according to one study of 100 healthy volunteers29, the parotid gland had median (interquartile range) dimensions of 36.15 (12.45) x 16.95 (4.00) x 43.60 (10.10) mm for the anteroposterior length, paramandibular depth, and cradio-caudal height, respectively). Due to the small size of the parotid, the shear wave propagation may be significantly affected by geometric and boundary effects, so even the best stiffness estimates would be biased to some unknown degree.

The use of an appropriate driver that provides sufficient penetration of the shear waves while being easy to use and patient-friendly may be a key to the successful MRE examination of the parotid. Moreover, the assessment of the whole gland may be rarely needed, and the main focus would be evaluating parotid tumors, the common pathology affecting salivary glands30. These lesions are often as small as 5 mm or less in diameter, so increased elastographic resolution is required. One way to achieve that is by applying high-frequency vibrations to the tissue of interest, which results in shorter shear waves and hence more accurate inversion.

The drivers described in the literature operate in the frequency range of 25 to 80 Hz and are typically placed under the patient’s head or neck21,31. The novel driver was designed for direct placement against the patient’s face over the parotid glands to minimize shear wave attenuation. This approach made achieving adequate shear wave illumination of the parotid feasible at frequencies as high as 100 Hz in 2D MRE and 120 Hz in 3D vector MRE, providing more reliable performance compared to drivers placed under the patient’s head.

Another benefit of the novel driver is its versatility, as it is equipped with the same connector as the one used in the conventional FDA-approved MRE setup. Therefore, any clinical or research center using such a system may perform MRE of the parotid gland with this driver. However, commercially available 2D MRE may not be the best choice for high-frequency MRE, lacking sufficient motion detectability for tiny displacements obtained with vibration frequencies higher than 100 Hz. A research-only 3D vector MRE, where motion sensitivity can be increased by adding more MEG cycles, is preferable.

The measured values of parotid gland stiffness are consistent with those reported by other groups (at lower vibration frequencies) and fall within the expected range for this organ21,31. However, a large-cohort, multicenter study is needed to establish normative stiffness values for the parotid gland.

This study has several limitations. The MRE data acquisition approaches are inconsistent, and the sample of subjects studied is very small. However, this pilot study aimed to test the feasibility of the technique and the performance of the novel driver rather than define clinically relevant values of biomechanical properties of the parotid. Therefore, it was necessary to test as many imaging setups as possible with a limited number of available volunteers. Additionally, the results obtained with two 3D vector MRE inversion methods significantly differ from each other, and it is yet to be established which method to use.

In conclusion, the developed passive driver made high-frequency MRE of the parotid gland feasible; however, 3D vector MRE should be used to ensure the reliability of the results. This study provided preliminary normative measurements of parotid stiffness that may be useful in designing future studies, including those aimed at characterizing parotid tumors.
