Circadian regulation of cardiovascular function: from physiology to clinical implications
Authors: Deeksha Malhan, Stefano Ministrini, Jesse Chih-Wei Lin, Chao-Lun Lai, Angela Relógio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCircadian rhythms play a crucial role in maintaining cardiovascular homeostasis, orchestrating fluctuations in blood pressure, heart rate variability, endothelial function, and systemic vascular tone. Disruptions of the circadian clock -arising from ageing, genetic predisposition, or environmental and lifestyle factors- can significantly elevate the risk of cardiovascular diseases, including hypertension, atherosclerosis, heart failure, and arrhythmias. This review examines the role of circadian regulation in cardiovascular physiology, from molecular clock networks and clock-controlled gene regulation to systemic cardiovascular physiology and clinical translation. We discuss how circadian disruption affects blood pressure, heart rate variability, and vascular health, through mechanisms including inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic dysregulation. We further position circadian biology within the broader context of cardiovascular ageing and the molecular mechanisms that drive age-associated cardiovascular decline.Special attention is given to the role of inflamm-ageing in promoting atherosclerosis and acute cardiovascular events, as well as the impact of desynchrony between central and peripheral clocks on disease severity. Additionally, we highlight the circadian regulation of genes implicated in cardiovascular ageing and disease. Finally, we explore emerging research on the clinical implications of circadian misalignment, with a focus on therapeutic strategies such as chronotherapy, time-restricted eating, and physical activity. By integrating current evidence, this review provides a comprehensive perspective on leveraging circadian rhythms for the prevention and management of cardiovascular diseases.Keywords: Circadian rhythms, Ageing, Hypertension, Cardiovascular system, Circadian clock

Background

Beyond its spontaneous electrical and mechanical activity [1], cardiac function is tightly coordinated by circadian rhythms—endogenous time-keeping mechanisms that regulate physiological processes to align with the 24-hour day-night cycle [2, 3]. The core of the circadian system is the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus, which synchronises peripheral clocks present in nearly all cells, including cardiomyocytes, through the regulation of the autonomous nervous system [4]. The coordinated activity of the central and peripheral clocks synchronizes various aspects of cardiovascular physiology, such as heart rate, blood pressure, and myocardial metabolism, with predictable changes in behavioral and metabolic demands, optimising cardiovascular function in anticipation of daily activities [5].

At the molecular level, the cellular circadian clock is governed by a network of core clock genes and proteins that form transcriptional-translational feedback loops (TTFL) to generate 24-hour rhythmicity. The heterodimer complex of circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (CLOCK) and basic helix-loop-helix aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator-like 1 (BMAL1) binds to E-box elements in the promoters of target genes, activating the transcription of negative regulators such as Period 1/2/3 (Per1/2/3) and Cryptochrome 1/2 (Cry1/2), as well as a wide array of clock-controlled genes (e.g. DBP, PPARA) that govern various physiological functions including metabolism, immune responses, cell cycle progression, and hormone regulation. As PERs and CRYs proteins accumulate, they form complexes that inhibit the CLOCK-BMAL1 function, repressing their own transcription. Additionally, nuclear receptor subfamily 1 group D1/2 (NR1D1/2) and receptor tyrosine kinase-like orphan receptor A/B/C (RORA/B/C) fine-tune this system by regulating BMAL1 expression – NR1D1/2 acts as a repressor, while RORA/B/C serves as an activator. Altogether, these interconnected feedback loops ensure the precise 24-hour oscillations of the circadian clock [6]. This clock machinery synchronises cellular functions with the external light-dark cycle, regulating a wide range of physiological processes across the body, including metabolism, hormone secretion, and cardiovascular function. Specifically within the heart, the circadian clock governs rhythmic variations in myocardial contractility, electrical conduction, and energy metabolism, ensuring optimal performance throughout the day. The rhythmic expression of these clock genes and proteins in cardiomyocytes and vascular cells ensures that cardiovascular function follows a daily pattern, with blood pressure, heart rate, and myocardial efficiency typically peaking during the day and declining at night [7].

Addressing circadian dysregulation is crucial in managing and preventing cardiovascular diseases, as disruptions in circadian rhythms—whether due to lifestyle factors such as shift work, irregular sleep patterns, or ageing—are increasingly recognised as contributors to conditions such as hypertension, arrhythmias, heart failure, atherosclerosis, and ischemic heart diseases. Integrating circadian biology into cardiovascular therapy strategies highlights the importance of maintaining proper circadian rhythms to safeguard heart health and mitigate the detrimental effects of circadian misalignment.

As the global population ages, the prevalence of cardiovascular diseases is rising, making it crucial to understand the impact of age-related disruption to circadian rhythms on cardiovascular ageing. Indeed, ageing is associated with a weakening of circadian rhythms [8]. In the heart, this circadian disruption can lead to impaired myocardial metabolism, reduced cardiac output, and a higher risk of arrhythmias [9]. Additionally, ageing affects the synchronisation between the central SCN pacemaker and peripheral clocks in the heart, leading to further circadian misalignment [10]. This circadian dysfunction in the ageing heart is thought to exacerbate the progression of cardiovascular diseases [11].

In heart failure, for example, the disruption of circadian gene expression has been linked to impaired cardiac metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction, which can accelerate disease progression [12]. Similarly, circadian misalignment in hypertensive patients can lead to abnormal blood pressure variability, increasing the risk of cardiovascular events [13]. Moreover, arrhythmias, including atrial fibrillation, exhibit circadian patterns with higher incidence during certain times of the day, underscoring the influence of circadian regulation on the electrical activity of the heart [14].

This review explores the intricate relationship between circadian rhythms and heart health, focusing on the importance of the circadian clock in regulating cardiac function in mammals. In addition to summarizing current physiological and clinical evidence, we provide a comprehensive overview of core clock genes and clock-controlled genes involved in cardiovascular function and disease.We examine how circadian dysregulation contributes to cardiovascular ageing and its role in heart-related pathologies such as heart failure, hypertension, and arrhythmias. Finally, the review discusses emerging therapeutic strategies targeting circadian mechanisms to improve heart health and reduce the impact of cardiovascular diseases, particularly in ageing populations. Understanding the role of circadian rhythms in the heart, at a systems-level regulation, opens new avenues for innovative approaches, such as chronotherapy to cardiovascular care.

The circadian clock: a key regulator of cardiovascular health

Circadian rhythms and regulation of heart physiology

The circadian clock ensures the timely regulation of numerous physiological processes, including sleep/wake cycles and cardiovascular function [15]. Circulatory parameters, such as cardiac output, heart rate, and blood pressure, fluctuate with daily activity, increasing during wakefulness and decreasing during rest or sleep, following the physiological demands of nutrients and oxygen delivery and waste exchange [16, 17]. Under normal conditions, both heart rate and blood pressure exhibit daily fluctuations, with a decline during the night by approximately 10% of blood pressure and 16% of heart rate [18, 19]. Blood pressure regulation involves dynamic circadian control, not merely lower blood pressure at night. The central circadian clock in the SCN orchestrates various peripheral clocks and hormonal systems to create daily blood pressure rhythms through neural, endocrine, and renal pathways. Circadian fluctuations in vascular tone, renal sodium handling, autonomic nervous system activity, and hormonal secretion (e.g., melatonin, aldosterone) all contribute to time-dependent regulation of blood pressure beyond simple day-night differences [20–22]. Furthermore, coronary blood flow exhibits a circadian rhythm under normal conditions, with fluctuations aligned to the day-night cycle. Studies in humans and animals demonstrate that coronary flow velocity and myocardial perfusion peak during the active/awake phase and decrease during rest/sleep phases, reflecting the circadian regulation of cardiac metabolism and vascular tone [23–25]. This rhythmic pattern supports the heart’s fluctuating demand for oxygen and nutrients that vary over the day. In disease states such as ischemic heart disease or heart failure, the normal circadian pattern of coronary blood flow can be disrupted. This disruption may contribute to increased susceptibility to cardiovascular events, which are known to occur more frequently at specific times of day, particularly in the morning. Circadian misalignment affects endothelial function, vascular tone, and inflammatory processes that regulate coronary flow, exacerbating disease severity.

Biological sex is a significant determinant of the circadian regulation of cardiovascular physiology and pathology. Sex differences influence key cardiovascular parameters, including heart rate, blood pressure, and heart rate variability (HRV), which follow distinct circadian patterns in males and females [26]. For instance, females generally exhibit higher resting and active heart rates across the circadian cycle and different HRV profiles compared to males [27]. Moreover, females tend to have higher nocturnal blood pressure and show sex-specific circadian gene expression that affects cardiac function. These variations contribute to differing cardiovascular risk profiles and disease progression patterns between sexes [28].

The circadian regulation of cardiac function involves an intricate cross-talk between the sympathetic nervous system (SNS), parasympathetic nervous system (PNS), renin-angiotensin system (RAS), and other hormones [29, 30], as discussed below. The SNS is pivotal for the circadian modulation of heart rate, contractility, and blood pressure. It operates primarily through the release of norepinephrine, which binds to adrenergic receptors [31]. In rodents, this interaction activates signalling pathways like the cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB), which adjust clock gene expression by inducing PER1/2 [32, 33]. On the other hand, core clock genes such as Cry1 and Cry2 modulate the secretion of RAS and aldosterone levels, thereby contributing to circadian variation of blood volume in rodents [34, 35]. This rhythmic activity of the RAS could play a crucial role in the timing of cardiovascular events and efficacy of targeted medications in humans, such as angiotensinogen-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blocker [36]. Thyroid hormones, especially T3 and T4, control metabolic rates and body temperature, indirectly influencing circadian rhythms. They modulate gene expression through thyroid hormone receptors, impacting cardiac contractility and metabolic processes [37, 38].

In addition to endocrine influences, intrinsic autonomic mechanisms also display robust circadian patterns that shape cardiovascular function. HRV exhibits strong circadian rhythmicity, reflecting the autonomic nervous system’s dynamic modulation over the 24-hour cycle. HRV typically peaks at night during rest, when parasympathetic activity dominates, and declines during daytime, when sympathetic tone increases. This pronounced circadian pattern captures the fine autonomic tuning of cardiac function across sleep-wake phases [39–42].

In contrast, cardiac output displays more modest and less well-defined circadian variability, as it depends on both heart rate and stroke volume. Stroke volume and vascular tone are influenced by numerous physiological factors that change more gradually and less rhythmically than autonomic input [43]. Consequently, the circadian fluctuations in cardiac output are smaller in magnitude and more strongly shaped by physical activity and metabolic demands than by intrinsic circadian modulation.

Overall, HRV provides a sensitive and immediate window into circadian autonomic regulation, whereas cardiac output integrates multiple determinants and exhibits subtler circadian variations. This makes HRV one of the most robust non-invasive biomarkers of circadian automonic regulation.

Also, other hormones known to follow a 24-hour rhythm, like cortisol, growth hormone (GH) and melatonin, influence cardiac output and blood pressure levels. Cardiovascular effects of cortisol and GH are well-established, basically consisting of increased blood pressure as a result of increased heart rate, increased blood volume and arteriol vasoconstriction [44, 45]. Melatonin is produced by the pineal gland and it regulates the circadian rhythm in the central nervous system, by maintaining sleep and preventing awakenings [46]. However, it could also have a direct effect on clock genes in cardiomyocytes through melatonin receptors MT1 and MT2. Indeed, in vitro evidence using rat cardiomyocytes suggested that melatonin stabilises the circadian rhythm of cardiomyocytes by inducing Nr1d1 [46]. Thyroid hormones control metabolic rates and body temperature, indirectly influencing circadian rhythms and cardiovascular function [37, 38]. The circadian clock also regulates platelet formation and function among healthy subjects, with activation markers peaking in the morning [47] (Fig. 1).

Furthermore, glucocorticoids and glucocorticoid receptor (GR) activity significantly influence circadian cardiovascular functions by modulating metabolic and mitochondrial processes in a time-dependent manner. Studies in rodents demonstrate that light-phase glucocorticoid administration (e.g., prednisone at ZT0) enhances cardioprotective effects, improving mitochondrial function and energy metabolites like NAD + and ATP, while dark-phase dosing (ZT12) blunts these benefits, highlighting the critical interplay between GR signalling and circadian rhythms in mammalian cardiovascular health [48, 49]. These observations highlight the relevance of a well-regulated circadian system in cardiovascular physiology, and its dysregulation is associated with heart diseases (Fig. 1). For instance, a dysregulation of daily sympathetic variability might lead to systemic arterial hypertension and coronary artery disease (CAD), due to excessive nighttime sympathetic activity [50, 51]. Similarly, a circadian dysregulation of the RAS can promote adverse cardiac remodelling and fibrosis due to uncoordinated fluctuations in angiotensin II levels, eventually leading to chronic heart failure in humans [12]. Furthermore, epidemiological studies show that circadian misalignments are one of the potential mechanisms linking hyperthyroidism to supraventricular tachyarrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation [52, 53]. Finally, low levels of melatonin production, especially during critical nighttime periods, have been associated with acute coronary syndromes [54, 55]. Moreover, the risk of adverse cardiovascular events, such as myocardial infarction and stroke, exhibits a circadian pattern, with a higher incidence in the early morning hours when blood pressure and heart rate increase [2]. Such pathological phenotypes of the cardiovascular system due to alterations in circadian rhythms will be discussed in detail in the next sections.

Molecular insights into circadian gene expression and cardiovascular function

Core clock genes like Bmal1, Clock, Per1/2/3, Cry1/2, Nr1d1/2, Rora, and Npas2 play a crucial role in the maintenance of cardiovascular homeostasis, with cell- and tissue-specific profiles (Fig. 2; Table 1). Circadian changes in clock gene activity have been observed in the hearts of humans, rodents and non-human primates [56–59]. Bmal1 knockout in mouse cardiomyocytes leads to reduced glucose utilisation, dilated cardiomyopathy, and altered blood pressure rhythms [60, 61], while Clock mutation in mouse cardiomyocytes affects heart rate and triglyceride metabolism [61, 62]. Per1/2 mediates the effects of sympathetic tone by negatively regulating cardiomyocyte proliferation, and the double knockout of Per1/Per2 in mice resulted in an increased heart size and cardiomyocyte proliferation [33, 63]. Cry1/2 mutations impact blood pressure regulation, Nr1d1/2 expression is linked to the regulation of cardiomyocyte size and function in cardiomyocyte-specific Bmal1 knockout mice [64], and cardiomyocyte-specific Rora knockout mice showed impaired mitophagy [65], leading to apoptosis. On the other hand, a large number of genes expressed in the heart exhibit 24-hour rhythmic expression controlled by core clock genes; these genes are known as clock-controlled genes (CCGs). This rhythmic regulation enables the heart to adapt its biological functions to daily fluctuations in physical activity, blood pressure, and metabolic demands (Table 2). Results of microarray sequencing in hearts of mice kept in light/dark cycles vs. constant dim light showed that around 10–15% of heart genes change their activity depending on the time-of-day [71]. Similarly, rhythmic patterns were observed in cardiac and vascular tissues of baboons for a total of 671 genes in the aorta and 2314 genes in the heart, showing rhythmic changes. Compared to the heart, the aorta showed a more complex and less consistent oscillation pattern, with bimodal gene expression peaks [72]. Interestingly, circadian regulation also extends to genes involved in vascular development. A study using microarray and RNA-seq data from 12 mouse tissues reported that genes related to the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) pathway in heart exhibited circadian rhythms [73]. In particular, at dusk, the expression of Vegfa and Kdr (the principal membrane-bound receptor) was low, while Flt1 (a decoy receptor) was high, reflecting the circadian modulation of angiogenesis [73]. Furthermore, cardiomyocyte-specific CCGs play a key role in heart development and function. An example is NKX2.5, essential for cardiac development, as it plays a central role in the regulatory pathways of second heart field (SHF) development [74]. NKX2.5 is among the earliest markers of cardiac mesoderm and is crucial for potentiating Wnt signalling during cardiogenesis by regulating the expression of Rspo3 gene, and mutations in NKX2.5 are associated with arrhythmia [75–77]. HIFs are transcriptional regulators and enable cells to adapt to hypoxia conditions, common in cardiovascular diseases [78–80]. There are two main isoforms: HIF-1α and HIF-2α. Under normal oxygen levels, HIF-α subunits are degraded, but during hypoxia, they stabilize, translocate into the nucleus, and dimerize with constitutively expressed HIF-β. This HIFα/β complex binds hypoxia-response elements (HREs) in target gene promoters to activate transcription of genes involved in angiogenesis, metabolism, erythropoiesis, and cell survival [81]. In cardiovascular medicine, HIFs play a critical role in ischemic conditions—such as myocardial infarction and peripheral artery disease—by promoting vessel growth through factors like VEGF, improving oxygen supply to tissues [82]. They contribute to ischemic preconditioning, whereby brief episodes of ischemia induce protection against subsequent injury, partly via HIF-driven expression of protective enzymes and metabolic adaptation. Moreover, HIFs regulate endothelial metabolism, favouring glycolysis during hypoxia to maintain energy production despite limited oxygen. Their roles extend to chronic cardiovascular pathologies where HIFs influence tissue remodelling, inflammation, and repair. Dysregulated HIF signalling is implicated in vascular diseases like atherosclerosis and pulmonary hypertension [83]. Therapeutically, pharmacological modulation of HIF pathways is being explored to enhance tissue tolerance to ischemia and improve outcomes in cardiovascular disease, highlighting HIFs’ relevance linking oxygen sensing with circadian cardio protection mechanisms [84]. Beyhond its established role in oxigen sensing , HIF1A plays a critical role in the function and survival of cardiomyocytes, especially under hypoxic conditions [82]. It also regulates endothelial cell adaptation by activating gene-signalling cascades that promote endothelial migration, growth, and differentiation, thereby supporting the overall cardiovascular environment during low oxygen conditions [85]. Moreover, HIF-1α signaling regulates blood pressure by modulating nitric oxide (NO) production. While HIF-1α promotes NO synthesis in pro-inflammatory macrophages, HIF-2α suppresses it in anti-inflammatory macrophages. This differential regulation of NO by HIF-1α and HIF-2α ensures a balanced vascular response to inflammation and oxygen availability. HIF1A is also an upstream regulator of PER2, a core clock element [86]. Eckle et al. [87]. in 2012 demonstrated that signalling through the Adora2b receptor stabilizes the circadian protein Per2 during myocardial ischemia, which promotes cardio protection by enhancing glycolysis and stabilising the hypoxia-inducible factor Hif1a. Per2-deficient mice showed larger infarct sizes, impaired metabolic adaptation to ischemia, and loss of cardio protection normally induced by ischemic preconditioning. Light exposure further stabilised Per2, enhancing glycolysis and providing time-specific cardio protection. Subsequent work in 2013 showed that hypoxia activates transcriptional programs that increase extracellular adenosine production and signalling, including Sp1-dependent induction of CD39 and HIF-mediated upregulation of CD73 and the Adora2b receptor [88]. This coordinated adenosine pathway activation enhances Adora2b availability during ischemic stress, thereby supporting Per2 stabilization and the associated metabolic and cardioprotective adaptations. A recently published study by Ruan W, et al. revealed that the core circadian transcription factor BMAL1 regulates circadian-dependent myocardial injury by forming a transcriptionally active heterodimer with HIF2A in a diurnal manner [89]. This BMAL1/HIF2A complex controls the circadian variation in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury by modulating gene expression rhythms in heart tissue. Notably, the study provides the first cryo-EM structure of the BMAL1/HIF2A complex bound to DNA, showing structural rearrangements within BMAL1 that enable cross-talk between circadian rhythms and hypoxia signalling. Furthermore, the work identified Areg as a rhythmic transcriptional co-target of the BMAL1/HIF2A complex, critical for regulating daytime variations of myocardial injury. Areg mediates cardio protection, and targeting the BMAL1–HIF2A–AREG pathway offers potential for clock-based therapies, with effectiveness depending on circadian phase. This study provided a mechanistic explanation for time-of-day differences in myocardial injury severity, with the BMAL1/HIF2A complex integrating circadian and hypoxia signals to drive protective rhythmic gene programs [89]. Klf15 plays a critical role in the heart’s metabolic adaptation to nutrients, and it also acts as an inhibitor of cardiac hypertrophy [90, 91]. Klf15 is also shown to depict a circadian pattern under the control of BMAL1 in healthy mice heart [91]. NOTCH3 is crucial for cardiovascular health, through the maintenance of vascular integrity and blood pressure [92, 93]. Moreover, elevated NOTCH3 expression is reported in the vascular smooth muscle cells from lung biopsies of pulmonary arterial hypertension patients [94]. Furthermore, circadian clock proteins BMAL1 and CLOCK regulate cardiac metabolism and repair, while Notch signalling controls heart development and remodelling, suggesting indirect crosstalk between NOTCH3 and circadian pathways in cardiomyocyte growth and differentiation [95]. Angiotensin II plays a crucial role in maintaining heart health by regulating blood pressure and fluid balance, thereby ensuring optimal blood flow and reducing the strain on the heart [96]. PRKCA is crucial for cardiovascular health as it regulates cardiac contractility, calcium signaling, and endothelial function [97, 98]. Furthermore, PRKCA is involved in an additional photic input pathway to the mammalian circadian clock, complementing the well-known CREB-mediated transcriptional activation of Period genes [77]. Pre-clinical evidence has shown that the histone-deacetylase Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) binds the CLOCK-BMAL1 complex in a circadian manner, suggesting that its protective role towards atherosclerosis, endothelial dysfunction and ischemia/reperfusion damage could have a circadian rhythm too [99–101]. PPARA is also regulated by the CLOCK-BMAL1 complex; in turn, it upregulates BMAL1 in a positive feedback mechanism. Beyond its well-known effects on lipid metabolism and inflammation, PPARα worsens the effects of ischemia/reperfusion damage in myocardial infarction [102]. PGC1A is essential for the mitochondrial biogenesis in human and mouse cardiomyocytes [103], and it is also regulated by the circadian clock [104]. PPARγ (PPARG) is expressed in vascular cells with a measurable circadian pattern, peaking before the core clock gene Bmal1. Activation of PPARγ directly stimulates Bmal1 expression, establishing PPARγ as a peripheral regulator of the vascular clock. Vascular-specific deletion of PPARγ in mice disrupted the rhythmic expression of Bmal1 and other clock genes, impairing circadian variations in blood pressure and heart rate [105]. Beyond gene regulation, PPARγ integrates environmental cues, circadian timing, and vascular function, linking metabolism with circadian control in cardiovascular tissues. Other CCGs that have an essential role in heart development, cardiomyocyte trophism and angiogenesis: Ahr and Sik1 are involved in the regulation of cardiomyocyte size and hypertrophic response to chronic stress [106, 107]. The AHR complex cross-talks with the biological clock playing a key role in homeostatic processes [108]. CEBPB plays a crucial role in regulating cardiac growth and regeneration in adult mammalian hearts, acting as a repressor of cardiomyocyte growth and proliferation under normal conditions [109]. LMNB1 regulates the timing of epicardial cell migration during embryonic heart development. Lmnb1 knockout (Lb1-null) murine embryos exhibited delayed migration and nuclear morphology changes, potentially impacting cardiac development [110]. Finally, Flt1 is a key regulator of angiogenesis in vitro inhibiting blood vessel formation [111].

Ultimately, many CCGs are involved in energy metabolism and oxidative stress. Furthermore, GH receptor (GHR) signalling protects endothelial cells by reducing oxidative stress damage [45]. Similarly, Nrf2 (also known as Nfe2l2) is one of the main hypoxia-responsive genes, protecting cardiac cells from oxidative stress-induced DNA damage [130], while COQ7 is essential for the antioxidant Co-enzyme Q10 production, which supports mitochondrial electron transport and ATP production [131], while NFKB1 controls inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, promoting the expression of inflammatory factors during reperfusion injury [132]. Notably, all these genes—GHR, NFE2L2, COQ7, CEBPB, LMNB1, FLT1, and NFKB1—exhibit circadian expression in the healthy cardiovascular system (in at least one human tissue: aorta, artery coronary, artery tibial, or heart atrial) as well (source: CircaDB http://circadb.hogeneschlab.org/;). Circadian dysregulation in ageing: links to heart health and disease

The cardiovascular system is particularly vulnerable to the detrimental effects of ageing, which represents the main risk factor for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular accidents. Ageing of the cardiovascular system is characterised by the occurrence of eight main pathophysiological features, which appear very early in life (beginning from 25 years of age) at the molecular level, and then slowly progress to the overt disease. According to Ministrini et al. these features develop over a long time span, following a specific temporal pattern: the first feature is endothelial dysfunction, followed by atherosclerosis, arterial stiffness, pro-thrombotic state, susceptibility to ischemia/reperfusion damage, aortic valve calcification, myocardial stiffness and finally heart failure [100]. However, this process is not the simple result of a passive exhaustion of homeostatic mechanisms and the subsequent accumulation of cellular and molecular damage over time. Conversely, current knowledge reveals the existence of molecular pathways opposing ageing, as well as biochemical processes actively promoting its progression. According to Lόpez-Ótin et al. [133], ageing is characterised by the co-occurrence of 12 independent hallmarks: genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis, disabled macroautophagy, deregulated nutrient-sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, altered intercellular communication, chronic inflammation, and dysbiosis. Among the 12 hallmarks of ageing, eight molecular hallmarks serve as common denominators in cardiovascular ageing. These include impaired macroautophagy, disrupted proteostasis, genomic instability—particularly clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential—epigenetic modifications, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, dysregulated neurohormonal signaling, and inflammation [134]. All these hallmarks associated to cardiovascular ageing can affect the central and peripheral clocks (Fig. 3), leading to the ageing-related circadian rhythm dysregulation. In turn, circadian misalignment can enhance the progression of ageing hallmarks, thus exerting a gero-promoting effect [135].

Sex-dependent mechanisms also shape the response to cardiovascular stroke and the progression of diseases such as myocardial infarction, hypertrophy, and heart failure. Interactions between the circadian clock and sex hormones, such as estrogen and progesterone in females, modulate cardiac and vascular physiology in a sex-specific manner. Recent study by Haider A et al. depicted that age-related cardiac changes are more pronounced in women when compared to men, likely reflecting a greater decline in sex hormones [136]. Females often demonstrate greater resilience to certain circadian disruptions, which may contribute to less adverse cardiovascular ageing compared to males [28]. These biological differences impact disease severity and symptom presentation, underscoring the importance of sex as a biological variable in cardiovascular research.

Age-related heart disease

The most relevant age-related heart disease is coronary artery disease (CAD), with its chronic and acute ischemic complications, such as angina pectoris and myocardial infarction (MI), respectively [137]. Indeed, due to its high metabolic demand, the heart is one of the organs which is most affected by ischemic damage. Impaired autophagy and mitochondrial dysfunction make cardiomyocytes more vulnerable to ischemic injury [138], whilst ageing of the systemic vasculature increases left ventricular afterload, leading to compensatory left ventricular hypertrophy and subsequent increased oxygen demand [139]; at the same time, myocardial microcirculation becomes progressively incompetent [140, 141]. Finally, the ageing myocardium is not only more prone to ischemic injury, but it also shows a reduced potential for functional recovery [142]. In summary, ageing weakens the heart’s ability to handle and recover from ischemic damage due to changes in cardiomyocytes, blood vessels, and circulation.

Atherosclerosis and inflamm-ageing

Atherosclerosis, a focal inflammatory disease of the large arteries, is an age-dependent disease and a primary cause of CAD [143]. The age-related dysregulation of the immune system, named inflamm-ageing, is a key process implicated in age-related cardiovascular disorders [144, 145]. Overall, ageing is characterised by an increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, extracellular matrix proteases, neutrophil extracellular traps, and reactive oxygen species [146–149]; at the same time however, there is a reduced efficacy of microbial killing and an increased trend towards autoimmunity [150, 151]. Altogether, these mechanisms contribute to atherosclerosis and blunted repair after acute and sub-acute ischemic damage [152–154].

Circadian rhythm has a strong influence on inflammation and the effect of this interplay in the onset and progression of atherosclerosis has been extensively reviewed [155]. Furthermore, metabolic disorders promoting atherosclerosis, such as dyslipidemia and insulin resistance, are also affected by circadian rhythm [156, 157]. More recently, the detrimental effect of ageing on the circadian rhythm of human immune cells has been explored [158–160], suggesting that circadian rhythm disruption could be a key mechanism in inflamm-ageing, and therefore in atherosclerosis and its ischemic complications. At the same time, inflammation can directly influence the rhythmical expression of clock genes, such as Per1/2, Bmal1, and Rora [161, 162], in both human and rat cells suggesting a feedback between inflammation and circadian rhythm disruption. In particular, chronic inflammation can suppress clock gene expression, worsening circadian misalignment, circadian rhythms disruption exacerbates inflammatory signalling increasing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines.

A similar bidirectional interplay was also observed between ageing and pro-atherosclerotic metabolic disorders [163, 164] in both human and rat cells.

Acute cardiovascular events

Not only is atherosclerosis influenced by circadian rhythm, but also its acute complications such as MI, sudden cardiac death and stroke. Indeed, these events occur more frequently in the early morning [165, 166], following the circadian peak of glucocorticoids, catecholamines, blood pressure, blood viscosity, platelet reactivity, and prothrombotic soluble factors [167–172]. Furthermore, chronic circadian rhythm disruption, as observed in night shift workers, is associated with an increased incidence of cardiovascular disease [173]. Beyond occurrence, circadian rhythm has been observed to affect also MI severity in animal models [174], likely by mouse Per2-mediated mechanism [175]. However, this observation was not confirmed in epidemiological studies [176].

Other age-related cardiac diseases

Beyond being a risk factor for ischemic heart disease, age affects several other cardiac diseases, including calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD), amyloid cardiomyopathy, and arrhythmias [177–179]. The pathophysiology of CAVD is still largely unclear and no direct relationship between circadian rhythm and aortic valve leaflets calcification is currently known. However, this condition shares some pathologic features with atherosclerosis [180], namely immune cell infiltration, failure in the resolution of inflammation and dysregulation of the phosphate-calcium metabolism [181, 182]. Therefore, the facilitating effect of ageing and circadian rhythm disruption could have a similar role in this setting, too. Furthermore, subjects with aortic valve diseases display a reduced diurnal variation of blood pressure, due to the reduced nocturnal modulation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system [183]. Amyloid cardiomyopathy is the cardiac manifestation of systemic diseases characterised by the deposition of amorphous protein material originating from the bloodstream, because of pathologic overproduction, reduced disposal or misfolding [178, 184]. Amyloid cardiomyopathy is characterised by myocardial stiffening and a reduced diurnal variation of blood pressure [185].

Finally, elderly population are more vulnerable to heart rhythm disturbances, due to three main pathologic mechanisms: (i) progressive fibrosis of the conduction system, (ii) disturbances of intracellular Ca2+ metabolism and (iii) alterations of the autonomous nervous system [179, 186, 187]. As a result, ageing is a predisposing factor for bradyarrhythmias, such as sick sinus syndrome and atrio-ventricular blocks [188, 189], as well as tachyarrhythmias, such as ventricular tachycardia, paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia, and most importantly atrial fibrillation (AFib) [190, 191]. Heart rhythm has a clear circadian rhythm, characterised by the reduction of heart rate during the night, due to both intrinsic cardiac and neurogenic factors [192]. Indeed, pre-clinical models show that myocardial-specific knockout of core clock genes, such as Clock or Bmal1, reduces the diurnal variation of heart rate, without suppressing it completely [193]. In particular, genetic deletion of Bmal1 induces a nocturnal increase of heart rate alongside an increased occurrence of sinus pauses, resembling the features of spontaneous ageing [194]. Interestingly, in vitro and in vivo studies associated BMAL1 deficiency to accelerated cellular senescence, reduced autophagy, and mitochondrial dysfunction [195–197], which represent three different hallmarks of ageing [133]. Altogether, this evidence suggests that a reduction of BMAL1 transcriptional activity may be involved in ageing-related changes in heart rhythm, with special regard to sinus bradyarrhythmias. AFib is the most common arrhythmia among the elderly population [198] and AFib-related cardioembolism is the main cause of acute ischemic stroke [199]. Like acute ischemic events, incidence of AFib shows a circadian preference [200, 201], likely due to diurnal fluctuations of circulating catecholamines [202]. Interestingly, in patients with paroxysmal AFib, those presenting a circadian recurrence of episodes (i.e., during either daytime or nighttime) had a reduced risk of progression to permanent AFib [203], suggesting that a preserved circadian rhythm constitutes a protective factor. Consistently, a reduced mRNA expression of key clock genes, such as Bmal1, Cry, and Rora, in peripheral white blood cells of patients with AFib are associated with a higher risk of high-rate episodes [204].

Ageing and heart failure

The common final stage of the above-described heart diseases is heart failure (HF), one of the main causes of morbidity and mortality among the elderly [205, 206]. Irrespective of the underlying pathology, the ageing myocardium undergoes predisposing structural and functional changes that can be summarised in (i) myocardial stiffening, (ii) eccentric left ventricular thickening, especially in the interventricular septum, and (iii) reduced responsiveness to the β-adrenergic stimulation [207–209]. The combination of these features explains the epidemiological prevalence of HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) over HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) among the elderly [210]. The role of chronobiology in the pathophysiology and natural course of HF has been recently reviewed elsewhere [12]. As reported above, in vitro and in vivo studies identified BMAL1 deficiency as a potential determinant of accelerated ageing, especially in the cardiovascular system. Consistently, myocardial-specific Bmal1 knockout mice develop myocardial hypertrophy and interstitial fibrosis, resembling the features of spontaneous ageing [211].

In conclusion, circadian rhythm and cardiovascular ageing show a complex interplay with a bidirectional influence at the molecular and physiological levels. In this setting, BMAL1-regulated pathways seem to stand at the intersection between circadian rhythm perturbations and multiple features of cardiovascular ageing.

Circadian disruption in heart-related conditions: impact on hypertension and other heart pathologies

Hypertension

Hypertension affects more than one billion people globally and is a major risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, making it a critical public health challenge [212]. In most individuals with essential hypertension, the primary hemodynamic abnormality is elevated total peripheral resistance, not a primary increase in heart rate or cardiac output. Hypertension is thus framed as a multisystem circadian–neurohumoral disorder involving vascular resistance, renin–angiotensin activity, sympathetic rhythmicity, and renal circadian regulation, with changes in heart rate and cardiac output acting as secondary modulators rather than primary drivers. This disease is heavily influenced by the synchronization of circadian rhythms with physiological regulatory systems, significantly impacted by thyroid hormone levels, the RAS, and interactions with the SNS. Hypothyroidism generally increases diastolic blood pressure by raising systemic vascular resistance [213], while hyperthyroidism can boost systolic blood pressure by accelerating heart rate and cardiac output, also affecting the RAS [214]. Typically, RAS activity peaks in the morning, with angiotensin II levels expected to decrease at night [215]. However, disruptions in circadian regulation can prevent these levels from falling, leading to non-dipping hypertension and heightened nighttime blood pressure. Angiotensin II activates intracellular pathways through AT1 receptors, enhancing calcium signaling which may activate kinases such as CaMKII [96]. These kinases can phosphorylate core circadian proteins like CLOCK and BMAL1 [216], shifting gene expression patterns that regulate vascular tone and sodium handling in the mouse kidneys, and complicating nighttime blood pressure control [217]. Furthermore, angiotensin II boosts norepinephrine release in human subjects from sympathetic nerve endings and the adrenal medulla, increasing SNS activity [218]. This surge in norepinephrine affects the phosphorylation of circadian proteins like PER1/2/3 and CRY1/2 via the cAMP and PKA pathways, potentially shifting circadian rhythms and altering the timing of blood pressure regulation throughout the day. These effects have been reported in mouse models, primary cell cultures, and in vitro biochemical assays [219, 220]. Additionally, angiotensin II heightens overall sympathetic output from the brain’s cardiovascular control centers [221] and diminishes baroreflex sensitivity [222], sustaining elevated blood pressure in rodents. In cases of hypertension in humans, persistent heightened SNS activity at night can inhibit the usual nocturnal dip in blood pressure [223], leading to a non-dipping hypertension profile associated with increased cardiovascular risks [224]. Moreover, reduced melatonin production, particularly common in elderly population [225] or night-time workers [226], can weaken suppression of nocturnal sympathetic activity, disrupting normal reductions in night-time blood pressure [227]. Recently, time-restricted eating (TRE) has emerged as a promising strategy for lowering blood pressure, particularly in overweight, obese, pre-diabetic, and shift-working populations, independent of weight loss [228]. Furthermore, the benefits of TRE in regulating blood pressure appear to be more pronounced in the cases where meal timing is aligned with natural circadian rhythms and supported by clock-gene–mediated regulation of blood pressure [228].

Atrial fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation (AFib), a highly prevalent cardiac rhythm disorder, significantly impacts public health as it affects millions worldwide and increases the risk of stroke, heart failure, and other cardiovascular complications [229]. Crucially, circadian genes such as Clock, Bmal1, Per, and Cry influence the expression and function of key ion channels in mouse models and in vitro cardiomyocytes vital for maintaining the heart’s electrical stability [230]. Disruptions in these genes among different pre clinical models (mouse and rat) can alter the normal rhythmic expression of potassium and calcium channels, leading to atrial electrical instability and predisposing to arrhythmias [231]. Additionally, circadian rhythms regulate HRV and cardiac cells’ refractory periods in humans [232]. Misalignment in these rhythms can disrupt the timing of electrical repolarization in the atrial myocardium, increasing the risk of developing reentrant electrical circuits or premature heartbeats [192], which are common pathways to AFib. Furthermore, the SNS exacerbates these conditions by enhancing heart rate and myocardial contractility. These effects, typically more pronounced during the day, can become pathological if circadian control is compromised, further contributing to AFib onset in human and other preclinical models such as dogs or rabbits [233]. Similarly, the RAS impairs cardiac function in mouse and rat models through Angiotensin II, which not only affects myocardial remodeling and fibrosis [234], thereby increasing atrial susceptibility to arrhythmias. In addition, Angiotensin II, modulates ion channel and gap junction protein expression, altering electrical impulse propagation in isolated human atrial myocytes ex vivo [235]. Moreover, both the SNS and RAS can influence the expression and function of circadian clock genes, as demonstrated in murine and other rodent models, including transgenic mice, as well as in human studies, creating a feedback loop that further destabilizes circadian control of cardiac physiology [236, 237]. Indeed, elevated levels of catecholamines and angiotensin II can alter the phosphorylation states and nuclear translocation of circadian proteins like CLOCK and BMAL1 in mouse [238], thereby exacerbating the rhythm disruptions. Additionally, thyroid gland disorders — both hypo- and hyper-thyroidism — affect cardiac heart’s electrophysiology. Hyperthyroidism increases cardiac sensitivity to catecholamines, thus predisposing to supraventricular tachyarrhythmias, while hypothyroidism changes the electrophysiological properties of cardiomyocytes, modifying the expression of ion channels and prolonging the action potential duration, thus predisposing to ventricular arrhythmias [239]. These multifactorial influences highlight the critical role of maintaining circadian rhythm integrity to effectively manage and mitigate the progression of AFib.

Heart failure

Heart failure, affecting approximately 26 million people globally, is on the rise due to increase in ageing population worldwide and improved management of other cardiovascular conditions [240]. It is essential to understand how circadian dysregulation contributes to the development and progression of heart failure, as disruptions in biological rhythms can exacerbate the condition. Initially, chronic activation of the SNS serves as a compensatory response in heart failure, but it ultimately becomes detrimental, causing heart muscle hypertrophy and arrhythmias both in humans and preclinical models [241]. This harmful effect is largely due to elevated norepinephrine levels in humans and animal models, which damage cardiac myocytes through mechanisms such as oxidative stress and production of harmful catecholamine metabolites [242], further exacerbating myocyte injury and promoting fibrosis [243]. Similarly, chronic activation of the RAS worsens heart failure by promoting cardiac remodelling and fibrosis [240], primarily through angiotensin II [231], which induces hypertrophy of cardiomyocytes and stimulates the production of TGF-β and other profibrotic cytokines in the heart [241]. Furthermore, studies on human and rodents showed that inefficient mitochondrial metabolism can increase the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), contributing to oxidative stress and cellular damage, including the mitochondria [244], which can lead to cell death and trigger fibrotic responses [245]. Circadian genes like NR1D1 and NPAS2 significantly influence heart failure progression through their regulation of cardiac metabolism and response to stress [12]. NR1D1 gene regulates lipid and glucose catabolism for the heart’s energy supply and is controlled by CLOCK and BMAL1 as part of the circadian clock’s negative feedback loop. Dysregulation of NR1D1 leads to imbalances of mitochondrial metabolism, resulting in energy depletion and oxidative stress, precursors to myocardial fibrosis in human tissues and rodents [246]. Additionally, NPAS2, a transcription factor related to CLOCK, regulates the heart’s response to stress and metabolic shifts. In tissues where CLOCK is less prevalent, such as the heart, NPAS2 can substitute for or work alongside CLOCK, forming complexes with BMAL1 to regulate circadian rhythms and carry out cardiac function. Impaired NPAS2 function can result in inadequate responses to metabolic stress, exacerbating conditions that favour fibrotic processes in mammals [246, 247]. The dysregulation of these circadian genes disrupts the heart’s circadian response to energy demands and recovery, crucial for maintaining cardiac output and adapting to physiological stresses. Integrating circadian biology into therapeutic strategies for heart failure underscores the importance of maintaining circadian rhythm integrity to enhance cardiac health and mitigate heart failure progression both in humans and animal models [248].

Atherosclerosis and ischemic heart diseases

Atherosclerosis and ischemic heart diseases, including sudden cardiac death, are major public health concerns globally [249, 250]. Atherosclerosis, characterised by the formation of inflammatory plaques in the arterial walls, can lead to significant reductions in blood flow, culminating in ischemic heart diseases. These conditions are principal causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, underscoring the importance of understanding their underlying mechanisms and management strategies. Endothelial cells in blood vessels and components of the coagulation system, governed by core clock genes such as Clock and Bmal1, play a pivotal role in maintaining vascular homeostasis with supporting evidence primarily from vascular endothelial cells and additional contributions from other vascular cell types, including smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts [251, 252].These cells’ intrinsic circadian clocks regulate crucial functions such as the release of nitric oxide (NO) and tissue factor, which are involved in vascular tone and hemostasis in humans and rodents [252]. Circadian control of physiological processes is crucial in the development of atherosclerosis and the progression of ischemic heart diseases, including sudden cardiac death. Dysfunctions in circadian genes in mice, such as Per2 mutations, can impair NO production, essential for vasodilation, particularly during periods of increased physical activity. These mutations affect NO signaling by altering post-translational regulation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and increasing cyclooxygenase (COX)-derived vasoconstrictors, leading to vascular stiffness and promoting atherosclerotic changes in humans and animals, despite unchanged eNOS expression [253]. Moreover, these genes control the inflammatory response timing in endothelial cells, and their dysregulation can induce a pro-inflammatory state, increasing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and adhesion molecules like vascular cellular adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) and inter-cellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) [252]. This facilitates the recruitment of inflammatory cells, exacerbating vascular inflammation and plaque development in humans [254]. Additionally, disruptions in circadian rhythms affect lipid metabolism, leading to atheorgenic dyslipidemia characterised by elevated low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and triglycerides, as reported through preclinical models and in vitro studies [255]. Circadian variations also regulate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) involved in the breakdown of extracellular matrix components in plaques, with abnormal regulation potentially causing plaque instability and increasing the risk of myocardial infarction in humans and animals [253, 256]. Furthermore, the endothelial pro-thrombotic factors, like plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) show circadian expression, with elevated levels in the early morning enhancing the risk of thrombosis, a critical factor in the onset of myocardial infarction and sudden cardiac death in humans [257]. Therefore, reinforcing circadian regulation could provide a strategic approach to mitigate the progression of atherosclerosis and its severe thrombotic consequences in humans and animals [258].

Circadian timing in cardiovascular treatment: the promise of chronotherapy

Circadian-based treatment strategies aim to align medical interventions with the body’s natural biological rhythms, with the goal of optimising therapeutic efficacy and minimising adverse effects [259]. This approach has been tested in some clinical scenarios, including the treatment of cardiovascular diseases, yielding promising results.

Most of the studies listed, reported positive impact on health and cardiovascular disease management via BP regulation in their respective cohorts. In hypertension management, published results suggested that nighttime administration of antihypertensive medications can improve 24-hour blood pressure control and potentially reduce cardiovascular risk [260]. For instance, taking angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEis) at bedtime has been associated with better myocardial remodeling in hypertensive patients and in those with pre-existing heart conditions.

Antihypertensive therapy is highly effective in preventing disability and death, with most evidence based on morning dosing. Night-time hypertension, nondipping BP, and morning BP surge are linked to higher cardiovascular risk, indicating that the main goal of antihypertensive treatment should be to achieve adequate blood pressure control over the entire 24-hour period. While bedtime dosing of antihypertensive drugs has been proposed as potentially more effective, existing studies have major limitations and high risk of bias. Until results from ongoing high-quality trials are available, morning dosing with long-acting medications to achieve full 24-hour BP control remains the recommended strategy [261]. The HYGIA chronotherapy trial, which suggested bedtime dosing of antihypertensive drugs reduces cardiovascular risk, has raised serious methodological concerns, regarding randomization, data collection, and monitoring [262, 263]. Given these issues, the study’s findings should be interpreted with caution, as routine bedtime dosing may be unsafe—especially for older patients or those at risk of excessive night-time BP lowering. Current evidence does not support changing standard practice, and antihypertensive medications should only be timed differently when specifically indicated.

Beyond hypertension, circadian based strategies has demonstrated benefits in other cardiovascular conditions [248]. In patients with end-stage renal disease, nocturnal hemodialysis has been linked to improved blood pressure control and regression of left ventricular hypertrophy compared to conventional daytime therapy [248, 221]. Additionally, research has been carried out exploring circadian based strategies for coronary heart disease and its associated risk factors [264].

Importantly, the sex differences influence the timing and effectiveness of therapeutic interventions, including chronotherapy, TRE, and physical activity regimens aligned with circadian rhythms. Recognising sex as a critical modulator of circadian cardiovascular biology is essential for developing personalised treatment strategies that optimize efficacy and minimize risks based on individual circadian and sex-specific profiles. Thus, the integration of biological sex in circadian studies of cardiovascular disease reveals profound impacts on physiological regulation, molecular mechanisms, and clinical outcomes. Future research and clinical applications should consider these sex differences to enhance prevention, diagnosis, and treatment approaches in cardiovascular medicine.

While chronotherapy offers promising benefits, recent clinical trials on hypertension management have reported conflicting results [272, 273], highlighting the need for further research to establish definitive guidelines. Nonetheless, its potential to enhance cardiovascular disease management without adding significant strain to healthcare systems makes it an area of continued interest and investigation.

Summary and future outlook

The heart’s circadian rhythm is a fundamental aspect of its physiological regulation, influencing the whole cardiovascular function, including heart rate, blood pressure, and myocardial metabolism. The synchronisation of these internal rhythms with the body’s external environment ensures that cardiovascular functions are optimised for the different demands of the day and night cycles. This review highlights the importance of circadian rhythms in maintaining cardiovascular health and the significant consequences when these rhythms are disrupted, particularly in ageing and in the context of heart-related pathologies.

Circadian rhythms play a crucial role in the heart by regulating the timing of various physiological functions, from cellular metabolism to the contractile activity of the heart muscle. This temporal organization allows the heart to meet the fluctuating demands of the body, such as increased cardiac output during the day and recovery and repair during the night. The disruption of these rhythms, however, can have severe implications, particularly as the heart ages. With age, the robustness of circadian rhythms diminishes, leading to a misalignment of physiological processes that can exacerbate cardiovascular ageing and increase susceptibility to disease. This review has emphasised how circadian dysregulation in the ageing heart contributes to conditions such as hypertension, arrhythmias, heart failure, and atherosclerosis.

In addition to the ageing process, circadian dysregulation plays a critical role in various heart-related pathologies. Heart diseases, including myocardial infarction, heart failure, and stroke, often exhibit distinct circadian patterns in their onset and severity, underscoring the importance of circadian rhythms in the progression of these diseases. The loss of synchrony between the heart’s internal clock and external environmental cues can lead to detrimental outcomes, making it essential to explore strategies that can restore or maintain circadian alignment. Chronotherapy, which involves timing medical treatments to align with the body’s circadian rhythms, represents a promising approach for improving cardiovascular outcomes [274, 275]. For example, administering medication to regulate blood pressure or carrying out interventions addressing myocardial infarction at specific times of day may enhance efficacy and reduce side effects; however, recent clinical trials have reported mixed results, highlighting the need for further research to establish clear guidelines and optimal timing strategies. Recent experimental and clinical studies suggest that the extent of myocardial injury during cardiac surgery varies with the time-of-day, reflecting circadian regulation of ischemia–reperfusion tolerance [276]. A randomised trial in patients undergoing aortic valve replacement found that surgeries performed in the afternoon were associated with reduced perioperative myocardial injury and fewer adverse cardiac events compared with morning procedures [277]. This effect was linked to circadian differences in clock gene expression and molecular pathways regulating cardiac susceptibility to injury. However, subsequent larger retrospective studies have failed to consistently confirm a time-of-day effect on surgical outcomes, possibly due to confounding factors such as patient selection and clinical practices [278]. These findings suggest that while circadian biology influences myocardial injury risk, further investigation is needed before definitive clinical recommendations on surgery timing can be made. Nonetheless, these insights underscore the potential importance of circadian mechanisms in modulating cardiac injury and recovery.

Interpretation of circadian clock function within cardiovascular tissues is still limited by the fact that most experimental data come from whole-body or tissue-level models rather than cell-specific manipulations. In vivo studies rarely isolate the contributions of distinct cardiac or vascular cell types, and human studies lack cellular resolution altogether. These constraints mean that current conclusions about cell-type-specific circadian mechanisms should be viewed as provisional. Despite the growing understanding of the connection between circadian rhythms and cardiovascular health, significant gaps remain in our knowledge. Future research should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying circadian regulation in the heart and identifying how these processes become disrupted with ageing and disease. Advances in chronobiology could lead to the development of more effective therapeutic strategies that target the circadian clock to prevent and manage heart-related pathologies. Furthermore, personalised approaches that consider individual circadian profiles may hold the key to optimising cardiovascular health and treatment outcomes.

In conclusion, the interplay between the heart’s circadian rhythms, ageing, and heart-related pathologies is a critical area of research with significant implications for improving cardiovascular health. Maintaining circadian rhythm integrity is essential for preventing cardiovascular ageing and manageing heart disease. This review has provided an overview of the current understanding of the heart’s circadian rhythms, their disruption in ageing, and the role they play in heart-related pathologies. Future research should further elucidate the mechanisms underlying circadian dysfunction in the ageing heart and develop strategies to restore circadian alignment through chronobiological interventions and time-based therapeutic approaches. Ultimately, progress in cardiovascular medicine will depend not only on identifying novel therapeutic targets, but also on determining when these targets are biologically most amenable to intervention, thereby integrating circadian timing into precision cardiovascular care.
