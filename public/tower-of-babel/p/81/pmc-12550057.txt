A nonlinear stiffness softening mechanism for low-bias, high-sensitivity MEMS accelerometers with extended dynamic range
Authors: Peyman Firoozy, Milad Seifnejad Haghighi, Mikhail Kanygin, Philip Ferguson, Behraad Bahreyni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The small dimensions of microfabricated devices present challenges in applications such as inertial sensing, where a larger proofmass is necessary for enhanced sensitivity. An effective approach to addressing the limitations of linear sensing is to use nonlinear mechanisms that adapt the device’s response according to different operating conditions. This paper introduces a new nonlinear spring mechanism for use in microsensors that harnesses the buckling phenomenon to achieve stiffness softening. The proposed mechanism utilizes a micro-arm to apply an eccentric axial load to an inclined beam, causing it to buckle in a controlled manner under a specified load. Once buckled, linear springs dominate the response of the system. We demonstrate that this method results in a smaller bias displacement compared to previously reported techniques based on snap-through behaviour, leading to potential reductions in device size and improvements in operational range. The behaviour is analytically modelled and verified through simulations. A prototype device was designed and microfabricated to experimentally validate the design principles. Compared to pre-curved nonlinear springs, the proposed design results in an 11-fold reduction in bias force, a 100-fold reduction in bias displacement, and a reduction in mechanical stiffness by a factor of 520. These results were verified through experiments conducted on a microfabricated accelerometer with an on-chip optical interferometer. Introduction

Nonlinear spring mechanisms, including geometric anti-springs (GAS) and bistable pre-curved beams, have garnered significant interest in precision sensing applications due to their capability to reduce the effective spring constant of suspension structures. These mechanisms are particularly valuable in developing high-resolution Micro-Electro-Mechanical Systems (MEMS) accelerometers for applications such as navigation1, structural health monitoring2, gravity measurement3, seismic sensing4, and space exploration5, which require low noise, low power consumption, compactness, and cost-effectiveness for wide-scale deployment. In these applications, sensitivity, noise, and measurement range are the key performance parameters of the sensor. Several strategies have been explored to enhance sensitivity, such as increasing proofmass size6–8, employing optical sensing schemes9–11, and reducing stiffness10,12–15. However, these approaches require precise matching of the positive and negative spring constants, where even minor discrepancies can prevent the system from achieving near-zero stiffness.

Achieving high sensitivity for MEMS accelerometers is often at the cost of dynamic range due to the inherent tradeoff between sensitivity and working range. Consequently, the most sensitive devices exhibit dynamic ranges as low as few \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{mg}$$\end{document}mg, severely limiting their application. To enhance the dynamic range, many researchers have adopted closed-loop systems in MEMS accelerometers, which apply a feedback force to counteract the inertial forces acting on the proofmass using electrostatic26 or electromagnetic actuation27. However, MEMS accelerometers with high sensitivity typically use large proofmasses. As a result, high acceleration inputs require a substantial feedback force to balance the inertial force, necessitating either a high actuation voltage or a large array of actuation capacitors28. Nevertheless, this approach presents significant design, fabrication, and control challenges, especially when integrating more proofmasses into a single module. In comparison, optical MEMS accelerometers have gained significant attention due to their high displacement accuracy and inherent robustness against electromagnetic interference, making them particularly suitable for sensing in harsh environments29.

To address the trade-off between sensitivity and dynamic range, this work introduces a new nonlinear spring mechanism based on an inclined beam that buckles under a preset axial load. Theoretical modeling and finite element analysis demonstrate that adjusting the inclined beam angle and the length of the connected micro-arm can significantly reduce the bias force and displacement to achieve stiffness softening regime. A prototype device, in the form of a spring accelerometer, was designed, microfabricated, and characterized using an on-chip optical interferometer to validate the design principles and models. The proposed structure can be incorporated into high-performance micromachined sensors that need to operate in the presence of a strong DC interference, such as gravimeters. Its small bias displacement enables the use of alternative device interfaces, such as capacitive sensing, for operation.

Buckled beam structures are attractive in microsystem designs due to their simplicity and ability to generate large displacements with minimal restoring force. As shown, the force-displacement curve for each beam angle and micro-arm length exhibits two distinct regions. The initial portion corresponds to a low-sensitivity regime, where the structure behaves as a stiff spring with minimal displacement. The mild nonlinearity observed in this region arises from the eccentric loading, which introduces an initial deflection even before the critical buckling threshold is reached. Once the applied force exceeds the critical value, the system transitions into a high-sensitivity regime, where the effective stiffness drops significantly. The force–displacement curve for each beam angle is primarily influenced by two parameters. First, as evident from Eq. (7), the buckling threshold force is proportional to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sin \theta$$\end{document}sinθ; therefore, adjusting the angle \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta$$\end{document}θ directly affects the axial force required to initiate buckling. Second, since the structure is intended to be fabricated on anisotropic silicon, varying the beam angle relative to the crystal orientation also changes the Young’s modulus. These two factors together alter the required bias force and influence the overall shape of the force–displacement curve. Implementation of the proposed nonlinear spring in a MEMS sensor

Figure 3a shows the schematic of a micromachined spring accelerometer based on a pair of the proposed nonlinear spring mechanism. A proofmass is suspended by two sets of nonlinear springs on the top corners and two linear springs on the bottom corners. The system is designed to be symmetrical and move only in the vertical direction (sensing direction) by increasing torsional and rotational stiffnesses. The modal analysis of the design reveals the shapes and resonant frequencies of different modes, showing that the first resonant frequency corresponds to the sensing direction and is significantly lower than the others. Discussion

The experimental results validate the effectiveness of the proposed nonlinear spring mechanism in achieving stable stiffness softening with low bias force and displacement. Table 1 summarizes the performance comparison of the proposed nonlinear spring mechanism integrated into an optical MEMS prototype and existing nonlinear MEMS accelerometers. Most MEMS sensors listed in Table 1 utilize pre-curved beams to enhance mechanical sensitivity, which typically requires large bias forces and displacements. At such low bias displacements, capacitive sensing becomes a viable readout mechanism for the proposed structure. However, achieving the required sensitivity would require a large number of comb fingers, which introduces both design and fabrication complexity. Additionally, capacitive sensors are vulnerable to particulate contamination; any particle lodged between comb fingers could shorten the structure or render the device inoperable. As a proof of principle, using an optical interface allowed us to maintain focus on the mechanical performance of the device. A limitation of using curved beams is the sudden transition between bistable positions, which results in a limited dynamic range. Our proposed nonlinear mechanism shows extended linear behavior after buckling, which results in a large dynamic range of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$167\,\mathrm{mg}$$\end{document}167mg (limited by the buckled beams touching the frame of the device). These improvements enable a die area reduction by an order of magnitude, resulting in a compact \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$11\times 11\,{\mathrm{mm}}^{2}$$\end{document}11×11mm2 form factor compared to other works, such as21 and23 which report die dimensions of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$25\times 25\,{\mathrm{mm}}^{2}$$\end{document}25×25mm2 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$20\times 20\,{\mathrm{mm}}^{2}$$\end{document}20×20mm2, respectively. This smaller size enables scalable mass production, simplifies die handling during microfabrication and testing, enhances application flexibility, and facilitates easier integration into various systems. Supplementary Note 4 shows pictures of the proposed prototype microfabrication at the wafer level. Although the resonant frequency of our fabricated MEMS prototype is higher than the designs reported in Table 1, as discussed in Fig. 3e, our design can achieve a resonant frequency of 4.3 Hz by selecting longer linear springs while maintaining a small bias displacement. Also, the utilized high-sensitivity optical readout interferometer compensates for the low mechanical sensitivity of the MEMS sensor. This work demonstrates a promising approach for advancing MEMS sensor design, particularly in applications requiring high sensitivity, large dynamic range, and miniaturization. Materials and methods

Fabrication process flow

The proposed optical MEMS prototype is fabricated in the SFU 4D LABS cleanroom. The fabrication process flow, which is based on a Silicon-On-Insulator (SOI) wafer, is shown in Fig. 7a–f. The external dimension of the chip is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$11\,\mathrm{mm}\times 11\,\mathrm{mm}$$\end{document}11mm×11mm. The fabricated chip comprises two layers: a device layer with a thickness of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$100\,\mathrm{\mu m}$$\end{document}100μm that is used to etch the proofmass, springs, and optical and electrical features, and a handle layer with a thickness of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$575\,\mathrm{\mu m}$$\end{document}575μm which is partially kept and used for an additional mass. The fabrication begins with the proper cleaning of the SOI wafer. Then, a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$$\end{document}2μm layer of silicon dioxide is thermally grown on top of the device layer (a). Through a photolithography process, the oxide layer is patterned to be used as the hard mask for the subsequent silicon etching. The exposed area of the oxide layer is etched using Reactive-Ion-Etching (RIE) (b). The same process is used to etch the backside oxide layer (c) followed by etching the handle wafer through a Deep-Reactive-Ion-Etching (DRIE) step (d). Then, the patterned frontside oxide layer is used as a masking layer to etch the device silicon layer in another DRIE step (e). Finally, the device is released via vapor hydrofluoric acid (VHF) to release the moving parts from the buried oxide (BOX) layer (f). Once the fabrication is completed, the MEMS prototype is mounted in customized packaging for performance testing, as shown in Fig. 7g. Optical intensity calibration

Figure 8a shows the schematic of the optical intensity calibration setup. In this experiment, as shown in Fig. 8b, a step-shaped sample with the same material as the proofmass was fabricated using DRIE with a step height of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim 100\,\mathrm{\mu m}$$\end{document}~100μm to calibrate the optical intensity reflection amplitude. As shown in Fig. 8a, the sample is bonded to a sample carrier, which is attached to a micro-positioner. The micro-positioner is located under a vertical stand, where a fiber is properly aligned to the sample using a fiber holder. The distance between the fiber end face and the sample surface, acting as a reflector, can be adjusted. Initially, the fiber faces a flat surface of the sample, establishing the reference distance. By adjusting the micro-positioner, the distance between the fiber and the sample changes when the fiber faces the step-shaped region. The output voltage corresponding to different positions of the sample is shown in Fig. 8c, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0\,{\rm{V}}$$\end{document}0V indicates the fiber facing the flat surface of the sample, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$672\,\mathrm{mV}$$\end{document}672mV corresponds to the fiber being within the step-shaped region. This results in an intensity calibration factor of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$102\,\mathrm{\mu m}/672\,\mathrm{mV}$$\end{document}102μm/672mV. This factor is used to obtain the proofmass displacement to an external acceleration. Then, the silicon sample was positioned in a stationary setup with a fixed fiber facing it to measure the displacement noise floor of the optical sensor.
