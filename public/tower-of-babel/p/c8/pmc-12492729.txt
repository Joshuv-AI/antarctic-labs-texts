Improving silage characteristics in high-moisture grape residues using cotton stalk as a moisture regulator
Authors: Ke Xu, Junyu Zhang, Aireti Mirizati, Bate Bayin, Xubiao Han, Lingling Su
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Grape branches and leaves are promising feedstock for high-quality silage, yet their high moisture content limits effective fermentation. This study aimed to investigate the effect of cotton stalk addition on fermentation quality and microbial characteristics of high-moisture grape branches and leaves silage, providing a basis for the efficient utilization of these resources as animal feed. The experiment included six treatments with different levels of MG (cotton stalk) : 0% (MG0), 10% (MG10), 20% (MG20), 30% (MG30), 40% (MG40), and 50% (MG50). After 60 days of silage fermentation, increasing MG proportion significantly reduced silage moisture content (P < 0.05), while pH and acetic acid (AA) levels significantly increased (P < 0.05). Bacterial community analysis revealed that the dominant genera were mainly Lentilactobacillus, Bacteroides, unclassified_rumen_bacterium, and Romboutsia. Among these, Lentilactobacillus predominated in MG20 group, with a relative abundance of 92.15%. Correlation analysis indicated that Lentilactobacillus was significantly negatively correlated with potentially undesirable bacteria such as Romboutsia and unclassified_Clostridia_UGG_014 (P < 0.01). Network analysis showed that addition of MG altered the bacterial community structure, with MG20 group exhibiting the highest mean degree and graph density, suggesting enhanced microbial interactions. In conclusion, incorporating cotton stalk can significantly improve fermentation quality of high-moisture grape branches and leaves silage while optimizing its microbial community. The best fermentation performance was achieved with 20% cotton stalk addition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04374-w.

Introduction

The rapid development of livestock industry has resulted in a considerable scarcity of high-quality roughage. In response to this challenge, sustainable utilization of unconventional feed resources have emerged as crucial strategies to reduce feed costs and address the forage deficit for cattle and sheep [1]. Grape (Vitis vinifera L.), one of the world’s major fruit crops, presents a promising opportunity in this context. According to the 2023/24 report by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA), global grape production reaches approximately 27.9 million tons annually. China leads global production, contributing 13.5 million tons per year —accounting for 48% of the total. Grape branches and leaves, the primary by-products of grape cultivation, are not only rich in essential nutrients but also contain various bioactive compounds, including polyphenols and phytosterols. These compounds exhibit functional properties, such as anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects [2–4]. Previous studies have demonstrated that nutritive value and palatability of grape branches and leaves can be significantly enhanced through the silage fermentation process, with or without the addition of silage additives [5]. However, their high moisture content—often reaching 90%—poses significant challenges during ensiling. Specifically, excessive moisture can lead to the loss of soluble nutrients, dilution of lactic acid (LA) concentration, and delayed pH reduction. These conditions favor the growth of undesirable microorganisms, such as Clostridia and Enterobacter, potentially reducing fermentation quality or causing silage failure [6, 7].

As one of the world’s leading cotton producers, China generates large quantities of cotton stalk annually. Recent studies have confirmed that cotton stalk is a nutrient-rich agricultural by-product and is suitable as roughage for cattle and sheep [8, 9]. Moreover, its safety for sheep feeding has been well-established [10]. The addition of moderate levels of cotton stalk to sheep diets has shown no adverse effects on production performance [11]. However, its high lignin content leads to a tough texture and poor palatability, limiting its direct use as roughage [12, 13]. Microbial fermentation can soften stalk, improve palatability and digestibility, and degrade free cotton phenols, thereby reducing toxicity [14, 15]. Being abundant and cost-effective, cotton stalk represents a valuable feed resource whose rational utilization can reduce resource waste and lower production costs.

Incorporating low-moisture materials during ensiling is an effective moisture regulation [16–18]. We hypothesized that adding cotton stalk to fresh grape branches and leaves can effectively balance moisture content, thereby inhibiting undesirable microorganisms and improving overall silage fermentation quality. To date, limited research has explored co-fermentation of cotton stalk and grape branches and leaves, and optimal mixing ratio remains unclear. Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate fermentation quality, bacterial community composition, and network structure of silages prepared using different ratios of cotton stalk and grape branches and leaves, in order to determine optimal ratio. The results will provide a scientific basis for efficient and sustainable utilization of grape by-products as animal feed.

Materials and methods

Raw materials silage preparation

The grape branches and leaves, as along with cotton stalk, were collected from Hami City, China. The grape material used was Purple Sweet A17 variety, while the cotton stalk belonged to Yuan Cotton No. 8 variety. Both were harvested in June 2023. Grape branches and leaves were collected During summer pruning stage, and were immediately chopped into uniform segments of 2~ 3 cm. Six treatments were formulated by incorporating naturally dried cotton stalk into fresh grape branches and leaves at different ratios: 0% (MG0), 10% (MG10), 20% (MG20), 30% (MG30), 40% (MG40), and 50% (MG50). The experiment followed a completely randomized design with three replicates per treatment. A quantitative microbial silage inoculants were added to all groups, and silage moisture content was adjusted to approximately 65% in all groups except MG0, MG10, and MG20. Details on microbial inuculants and their functions are provided in Supplementary Table 2. After thorough mixing, the material was sealed in vacuum silage bags using a vacuum packing machine and stored in a cool, dark environment for 60 days [19].

Fermentation quality analysis

A 20 g sample of each silage was homogenized with 180 ml of sterile water and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h. The mixture was then filtered through four layers of sterile gauze, ant the filtrate was used for fermentation quality measurements. The pH was measured using a glass electrode pH meter (Eutech™ pHTestr30 pH, USA). Concentrations of lactic acid (LA), acetic acid (AA), propionic acid (PA), and butyric acid (BA) were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) [20].

Silage moisture content (MC) was calculated using the following equation:

MC (%) = ((W₁ − W₂)/W₁) × 100%,

where W₁ is the fresh weight and W₂ is the constant dry weight obtained after air-drying in the shade.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted using the TGuide S96 DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, CHN) according to manufacturer’s instructions. The V3-V4 region of 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using following primers:

Forward (F): 5′-ACTCCTACGGGGAGGCAGCA-3′.

Reverse (R): 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′.

The PCR was performed following the protocol described by Klindworth et al. [21]. The PCR products were purified using the Omega DNA Purification Kit (Omega Inc., Norcross, GA, USA).The purified products were then sequenced using paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. The sequence data were deposited in NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject accession number PRJNA1229765.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance ANOVA in SPSS 26.0 software, with Tukey’s post hoc test used for multiple comparisons. Results are presented as means ± Standard Error of the Mean (SEM). Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05, and differences were deemed nonsignificant at P > 0.05. Alpha diversity indices (Shannon and Chao1) were calculated using R software and the “vegan” package to compute these indices, which are commonly used to assess species richness and diversity. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed with Canoco 5.0, and PERMANOVA was conducted using R software. Bacterial community network structures were visualized using Gephi v0.9.2, based on Pearson correlations (R > 0.8, P < 0.05) among the top 50 genera. Topological properties of each network were calculated accordingly. BugBase was used for phenotypic prediction of bacterial communities, and figures were generated using Origin 2024 and Excel 2019.

Bacterial diversity and dominant genera composition of silage

As shown in Fig. 1A, and B, both Chao1 and Shannon indices initially decreased and then increased with higher levels of cotton stalk, reaching their lowest values in MG20 group. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed that bacterial community structure varied significantly with the proportion of cotton stalk, with MG20 group clearly separated from other treatments (Fig. 1C). This separation was further supported by PERMANOVA results (P = 0.003) (Supplementary Table 1).

As illustrated in Fig. 2A, the dominant bacterial genera included Lentilactobacillus, Bacteroides, unclassified_rumen_bacterium, and Romboutsia. The relative abundance of Lentilactobacillus and unclassified_rumen_bacterium followed an increasing and then decreasing trend as cotton stalk content increased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2B, and D). Lentilactobacillus reached its peak relative abundance of 92.15% in MG20 group, while unclassified_rumen_bacterium peaked at 24.33% in MG30 group. Conversely, Bacteroides and Romboutsia exhibited an opposite trend, first decreasing and then increasing, both reaching their lowest relative abundances (less than 1%) in MG20 group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2C, and E).

Correlation between fermentation parameters and bacterial genera

Figure 3 presents the Spearman correlation analysis between fermentation indicators and bacterial genera. The pH and AA were significantly positively correlated with Lentilactobacillus and negatively correlated with Romboutsia, unclassified_Clostridia_UGG_014, unclassified_rumen_bacterium, and MC (P < 0.01 or P < 0.05). This indicates that silage moisture regulation boosts lactic acid bacteria growth, thereby increasing acetic acid and curbing undesirable microbes. Additionally, significant inter-genus correlations were detected: Lentilactobacillus was negatively correlated with Romboutsia, Peptococcus, and unclassified_rumen_bacterium (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01), whereas positive correlations existed among most other dominant genera, indicating that relationships among genera are primarily characterized by coevolution.

Discussion

Effects of cotton stalk on the fermentation quality of high-moisture grape branches and leaves silage

The pH value is a key indicator of silage fermentation quality, with optimal silage typically exhibiting pH values between 3.8 and 4.2 [22]. In this study, when cotton stalk was added at 20–40%, silage pH remained within the optimal range (3.87–3.96). However, MG0 and MG10 groups displayed significantly lower pH values, approximately 3.4. A strong negative correlation was observed between pH and moisture content (MC) (P < 0.01), consistent with findings by Du and Chen et al. [7, 16].Excess moisture enriches spoilage bacteria, accelerating the decomposition of organic matter and acid production, which can result in abnormally low pH values [23]. Among organic acids, lactic acid (LA) and acetic acid (AA) are primary metabolites influencing silage quality. LA rapidly lowers pH, inhibiting undesirable microbes, while AA enhances aerobic stability, minimizing nutrient losses. Our results showed both AA content and pH significantly increased with higher cotton stalk levels (P < 0.05). Given that cotton stalk contains lower water-soluble carbohydrate (WSC) levels than grape residues, increasing its proportion elevates buffering capacity of the mixture [24]. This pattern was reflected in the higher pH and AA concentrations observed. The reduction in WSC likely favored heterofermentative lactic acid bacteria (LAB) over homofermentative species, explaining the increase in AA content and lack of significant change in LA [25]. Since AA has a higher pKa value (4.8) compared to LA (3.9), its accumulation contributes to slower pH reduction [26]. Therefore, these findings suggest that the optimal cotton stalk addition to improve fermentation quality ranges from 20 to 40%.

Effects of cotton stalk on bacterial community composition of high-moisture grape silage

During successful siklage fermentation, LAB typically dominate microbial community, leading to a simplification of community structure and a reduction in microbial diversity [26]. The Chao1 and Shannon indices, which measure richness and diversity respectively [27], reached their lowest values in MG20 group (P < 0.05), indicating a highly specialized microbial environment.

The dominant bacterial genera identified were Lentilactobacillus, Bacteroides, unclassified_rumen_bacterium, and Romboutsia. Notably, Lentilactobacillus was the most abundant in MG20 group. This genus is heterofermentative and capable of converting LA into AA and 1,2-propanediol, which enhances aerobic stability and suppresses spoilage bacteria [28]. The higher buffering capacity resulting from cotton stalk addition likely facilitated the growth of Lentilactobacillus [24]. Correlation analysis revealed a significant negative relationship between Lentilactobacillus and both Romboutsia and unclassified_Clostridia_UGG_014 (P < 0.01). These clostridial taxa are known to produce butyric acid and biogenic amines, which degrade feed quality [29, 30]. Thus, conditions of MG20 group favored the dominance of beneficial LAB while suppressing undesirable genera. This microbial shift led to a simplified bacterial community structure and enhanced fermentation quality. Moreover, Bacteroides showed a positive correlation with AA and a negative correlation with MC (P < 0.01). As a genus that produces AA through anaerobic fermentation, its growth may be restricted in high-moisture environments due to oxygen availability [31]. his suggests that moisture indirectly regulates AA production by shaping bacterial community dynamics.

Effect of cotton stalk on microbial network structure and functional prediction

From an ecological standpoint, each fermentation bag represents as a complex anaerobic micro-ecosystem [32]. The microbial network structure reflects community complexity and functional interactions, both of which influence fermentation efficiency and nutrient preservation [33]. For example, Li et al. [34] showed that additives such asPiper sarmentosum can significantly reshape microbial networks and improve silage outcomes. Du et al. [7] demonstrated that silage inoculated with LAB developed a symbiotic network primarily dominated by Lactobacillus plantarum, which regulated microbial community structure and metabolic pathways, ultimately improving silage fermentation quality. Our results showed MG20 group exhibited the lowest number of nodes but the highest average degree (5.217) and graph density (0.237), indicating a highly interconnected and synergistic microbial community. Such structure facilitates efficient metabolite exchange and enables dominant LAB, such as Lentilactobacillus, to effectively inhibit harmful bacteria and enhance silage stability [28, 35]. While this functional homogenization (i.e., a reduction in microbial functional diversity leading to community simplification) may improve fermentation performance, it may also reduce community resilience, making system more vulnerable to environmental fluctuations (e.g., changes in pH or substrate availability) [36, 37].

BugBase functional predictions further revealed that MG20 group had significantly higher abundances of aerobic and mobile-element-containing bacteria, and lower abundances of facultatively anaerobic, Gram-negative, and potentially pathogenic bacteria compared to other groups (P < 0.05). This pattern reflects a more stable and less pathogenic microbial environment. Notably, higher moisture levels in MG0 and MG10 groups likely increased dissolved oxygen, promoting the growth of facultatively anaerobic bacteria. These microbes compete with LAB, leading to protein degradation and the accumulation of toxic metabolites, ultimately reducing silage quality [38]. Additionally, the acidic environment in these groups may support the proliferation of acid-resistant Gram-negative bacteria [39].

Conclusion

The current study illustrates that the addition of cotton stalk can significantly enhance fermentation quality of high-moisture grape branches and leaves silage. Specifically, when cotton stalk was incorporated, the beneficial genus Lentilactobacillus spp. became predominant, leading to a simplified microbial community and stronger synergistic interactions among bacterial taxa. This microbial restructuring contributed to improved fermentation stability and silage quality. In summary, adding 20% cotton stalk into high-moisture grape branches and leaves silage is an effective strategy to optimize both fermentation characteristics and microbial community structure. These findings suggest that mixed fermentation using complementary plant residues holds considerable potential for enhancing the utilization of unconventional high-moisture feed resources.
