Upper tract urothelial carcinoma mimicking emphysematous pyelonephritis: a diagnostic challenge case report
Authors: Omar Al Ayoubi, Grace Tannous, Shaza Saba, Mohammad Alaa Aldakak, Mohammad Salman Otahbashi, Osama Harouk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Upper tract urothelial carcinoma is a rare malignancy arising from the renal pelvis or ureter, accounting for 5–10% of urothelial carcinomas. Common clinical manifestations include hematuria and flank pain; however, many patients present with invasive disease at the time of diagnosis due to the absence of characteristic early symptoms. Fever may occur in association with infection or neoplastic fever, which can complicate the diagnostic process.Case presentation:A 47-year-old male with a history of recurrent nephrolithiasis and multiple prior right-sided urological interventions presented with persistent fever, right flank pain, and malaise. Despite broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy and nephrostomy drainage, there was no clinical improvement. Non-contrast computed tomography demonstrated obstructive nephrolithiasis with intraluminal gas within the right renal collecting system, raising suspicion of emphysematous pyelonephritis. Subsequent ultrasound showed similar findings with cortical thinning. Due to persistent sepsis and failure of conservative management, a right nephrectomy was performed. Histopathological examination revealed a high-grade urothelial carcinoma with extensive necrosis and local extension beyond the renal capsule. Immunohistochemistry showed positivity for alpha-methylacyl-CoA racemase, cytokeratin 7, and GATA-binding protein 3, with negative paired box gene 8 staining, confirming urothelial carcinoma. The patient was started on gemcitabine and cisplatin chemotherapy and remained clinically stable during approximately 3 months of follow-up.Conclusion:This case highlights a rare presentation of upper tract urothelial carcinoma mimicking emphysematous pyelonephritis, leading to a delayed diagnosis. Persistent or atypical upper urinary tract infections should raise suspicion for an underlying malignancy.

Introduction

Upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) is a relatively rare subtype of urothelial malignancy that originates in the renal pelvis or ureter, accounting for approximately 5–10% of all urothelial carcinomas (UCs) and around 10% of kidney cancers, with a reported 5-year survival rate of less than 60%[1–3]. Common clinical manifestations of UTUC include hematuria, flank pain, clot colic, palpable abdominal mass, systemic symptoms, or incidental detection on imaging studies[4]. Due to the absence of clear characteristic clinical symptoms in the early stages, approximately 60% of UTUC cases present with invasive disease at the time of diagnosis. Fever is frequent in cancer patients, most commonly due to infection, though neoplastic fever occurs in approximately 27% of cases. Differentiating it from infectious fever is often challenging and may lead to misdiagnosis[5]. Here, we report a case of a 47-year-old male who presented with fever, leukocytosis, and purulent nephrostomy drainage, initially suspected to have emphysematous pyelonephritis. Nephrectomy revealed an unexpectedly high-grade upper tract UC. Case presentation

A 47-year-old nonsmoking male presented to the Urology Department after experiencing complaints of right flank pain, generalized malaise, and persistent fever.

The patient had a long-standing history of recurrent nephrolithiasis with multiple prior urological interventions on the right side. These included open renal stone removal approximately 20 years ago, followed by multiple right ureteroscopic lithotripsy procedures (approximately six sessions) using laser and lithoclast for ureteral stones, the most recent of which was performed in 2018. Additionally, the patient underwent transurethral cystolithotripsy for a bladder stone in 2024. The patient had no history of diabetes mellitus and no history of chronic medication use.

Approximately 1 month prior to presentation, he developed persistent right flank pain and fever and received intravenous antibiotics elsewhere without clinical improvement. During that evaluation, laboratory tests showed leukocytosis and elevated C-reactive protein, consistent with systemic inflammation.

A subsequent non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scan was performed during the initial evaluation in the setting of an acute presentation with suspected severe infection and obstructive uropathy to avoid a delay in management. Contrast-enhanced imaging was deferred at that stage due to the urgent clinical condition and the primary diagnostic focus on infection rather than underlying malignancy. The scan revealed moderate dilation of the right renal collecting system with a 23 × 17 mm stone located in the lower pole of the right kidney (Fig. 1A), along with intraluminal gas within the collecting system, raising suspicion of emphysematous pyelonephritis (Fig. 1B). A nephrostomy tube was subsequently inserted at that time; however, persistent purulent drainage (100–200 mL/day) continued, and the patient failed to improve clinically, prompting referral to our center.

On admission to our institution, the patient appeared ill and febrile, with right flank tenderness. Abdominal examination revealed mild distension, decreased peristalsis, and costovertebral angle tenderness without palpable masses. A subsequent ultrasound performed at our institution demonstrated gas in the collecting system and thinning of the renal cortex, raising suspicion for emphysematous pyelonephritis or severe chronic kidney infection.

The initial clinical suspicion of emphysematous pyelonephritis was based on the prior CT findings, which demonstrated gas within the right renal collecting system in the setting of obstructive nephrolithiasis. This was further supported by a renal ultrasound performed at our institution, which showed similar findings of intraluminal gas, in addition to clinical and laboratory evidence of severe infection.

Given the failure of antibiotic therapy, persistent purulent drainage, and the patient’s deteriorating clinical condition with ongoing severe symptoms, the decision was made to proceed with nephrectomy (Fig. 2). The surgery was technically challenging due to extensive adhesions from prior surgeries. Histopathological examination of the excised kidney revealed a high-grade malignant neoplasm measuring 13 cm in greatest dimension, with extensive necrosis (Fig. 3A and B), involving the entire kidney and showing extension beyond the renal capsule into the perinephric tissue, renal sinus, and pelvicalyceal system, in addition to lymphovascular and perineural invasion. These findings are consistent with at least a pathological stage pT3 disease according to the TNM classification. No additional postoperative cross-sectional imaging was performed at the time of reporting due to limited local resources and management decisions guided by histopathological confirmation and the patient’s clinical condition. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed, and the tumor showed focal positivity for alpha-methylacyl-CoA racemase (AMACR), cytokeratin 7 (CK7), and GATA-binding protein 3 (GATA3), while paired box gene 8 was negative. These IHC findings, in combination with the morphologic patterns, confirmed the diagnosis of UC.

The patient was started on gemcitabine and cisplatin combination chemotherapy as part of the standard treatment for UC, and close clinical follow-up was implemented. At the time of writing, the patient has been followed for approximately 3 months and has remained clinically stable, with a plan for radical nephroureterectomy (RNU) once medically optimized.

Discussion

UC of the upper urinary tract, involving the renal pelvis and ureter, is considered an uncommon malignancy. It constitutes fewer than 1% of all genitourinary neoplasms and comprises approximately 5% of all urothelial tumors[7,8]. In addition, UC occurs approximately three times more frequently in men than in women[8].

Several risk factors for kidney cancer have been well established, including cigarette smoking, hypertension, obesity, acquired renal cystic disease, the use of analgesics, genetic predisposition, and specific occupational exposures[8]. In addition to these factors, a meta-analysis demonstrated a significant correlation between a history of kidney stones and the two most prevalent kidney cancers, renal cell carcinoma (RCC) and UC, indicating an overall 1.76-fold increased risk for RCC and a 2.14-fold increased risk for UC among individuals with a history of kidney stones compared to those without such a history[8]. Furthermore, this increased risk is likely due to stone-induced irritation and recurrent infections, as kidney stones may alter the local microenvironment through chronic irritation and infection, which in turn promotes urothelial cell proliferation and malignant transformation[8]. This association is relevant to our case, as our patient’s history of recurrent nephrolithiasis may have contributed to UC development, in line with evidence linking chronic stone disease to higher urothelial cancer risk.

Clinically, upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) most frequently presents with hematuria, reported in 70–80% of cases[9,10], while flank mass presentation is less common[11]. In contrast, our patient presented with right flank pain, persistent fever, and features suggestive of emphysematous pyelonephritis, which initially obscured the underlying diagnosis of UC. Several reports have similarly described atypical UTUC presentations in which infection-like or inflammatory features initially obscured the underlying malignancy. Cheng et al reported a case of high-grade UTUC accompanied by persistent hyperthermia that was initially difficult to distinguish from infectious fever[5]. Similarly, Guo et al described several cases of UTUC presenting with hydronephrosis, infection-like clinical and radiological findings, and persistently elevated inflammatory markers, leading to delayed recognition of the underlying malignancy until postoperative pathological examination[12]. These findings are consistent with our case, where chronic infection masked an underlying aggressive UC.

UTUC may also be detected incidentally on imaging in asymptomatic patients. The presence of constitutional symptoms, such as weight loss, fatigue, fever, or night sweats, should raise suspicion for metastatic disease and a poorer prognosis[11].

The definitive preoperative diagnosis is usually made by fine-needle aspiration biopsy; however, current guidelines do not recommend routine biopsy for suspected renal malignancies due to limited accuracy and invasiveness, as surgical resection is often required regardless of biopsy results[13,14]. Therefore, imaging plays a central role in evaluation. CT findings of intrarenal UC typically include a filling defect in the renal pelvis, with irregular or narrowed collecting system structures and urothelial wall thickening[15,16]. However, these imaging findings are not specific, and several benign and malignant conditions may mimic UTUC on CT urography. Reported differential diagnoses include blood clots, suburothelial hemorrhage, renal papillary necrosis, inflammatory urothelial thickening, urogenital tuberculosis, fibroepithelial polyps, RCC, and renal lymphoma. Differentiation may require correlation of imaging findings with clinical, laboratory, and sometimes pathological data to avoid misdiagnosis and inappropriate management[17].

In the present case, imaging revealed moderate dilation of the collecting system, a 23 × 17 mm stone, and the presence of gas within the right renal collecting system. These findings were suggestive of a severe infectious and obstructive process, which initially led to a clinical suspicion of infection rather than an underlying malignancy.

Histological examination alone may be insufficient for accurately diagnosing urothelial lesions. IHC staining helps confirm tumor grade, but single markers can be inconclusive, so using multiple IHC markers enhances accuracy, particularly in difficult cases[18]. In support of this approach, a previous study reported that among the four IHC markers evaluated – CK20, CD44, AMACR, and p53 – AMACR demonstrated the highest sensitivity, specificity, and diagnostic odds ratio for detecting UC[18]. Additionally, GATA3, a transcription factor regulating cell differentiation and proliferation, is frequently expressed in urothelial and breast epithelial cells and serves as a valuable biomarker to distinguish UC from other malignancies[19,20]. This was evident in our case, where positivity for AMACR, CK7, and GATA3 confirmed high-grade UC, highlighting the value of using multiple IHC markers.

For patients with localized high-risk UTUC, RNU with bladder cuff excision remains the gold-standard surgical treatment, with lymphadenectomy considered in selected cases[21]. Additionally, adjuvant platinum-based chemotherapy has been shown to improve disease-free survival in patients with locally advanced disease following complete lymphadenectomy[22]. In our patient, nephrectomy was performed initially due to persistent infection, failure of medical management, and the severity of the suspected emphysematous pyelonephritis. This was followed by gemcitabine and cisplatin combination chemotherapy, with a planned RNU once the patient becomes medically optimized, in line with treatment strategies reported in the literature.

Conclusion

This case highlights a diagnostic challenge arising from overlapping features of severe renal infection and underlying malignancy. Emphysematous pyelonephritis was initially suspected based on imaging findings, while the underlying UC was only diagnosed after nephrectomy. The case underscores the importance of considering malignancy in patients with atypical or non-resolving infectious presentations of the upper urinary tract.
