Monoclonal gammopathy of clinical significance: a multisystem review
Authors: Manraj Singh Sra, S. Vincent Rajkumar, Shaji Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Monoclonal gammopathy of clinical significance (MGCS) is a general term that encompasses a spectrum of non-malignant clinical disorders that are directly caused by monoclonal immunoglobulins (monoclonal proteins) produced by clonal plasma cell proliferative disorders. It is best considered as a collection of specific disorders that represent non-malignant progression of monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS). This review provides a comprehensive overview of MGCS, highlighting its pathophysiology, diagnostic approach, clinical manifestations, and current management strategies across multiple organ systems. The spectrum of MGCS includes specific renal, neurological, cutaneous, hematological, and ocular diseases, as well as multisystem disorders such as POEMS syndrome, Schnitzler syndrome, cryoglobulinemia, and TEMPI syndrome. The diagnosis of MGCS is often challenging as it requires establishing a causal link between a monoclonal protein and the specific disease entity or clinical manifestation. Treatment strategies vary by disease and target the underlying monoclonal protein-producing clone or are directed to limit the effects of the monoclonal protein. As clinical trials remain limited, current management relies on mechanistic insights and observational studies. Increased awareness of MGCS and its organ-specific clinical features is crucial for providing timely diagnosis and delivering targeted interventions that may reverse or halt disease progression.

Introduction

Clonal plasma cell disorders (monoclonal gammopathies) are associated with the secretion of abnormal monoclonal immunoglobulins, commonly referred to as monoclonal proteins (M proteins). They span a wide spectrum of clinical disorders from the classic premalignancy, monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) to overt malignancy, such as multiple myeloma, Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia, and solitary plasmacytoma. Monoclonal proteins secreted in clonal plasma cell disorders are usually harmless and lack affinity to self-antigens (autoantibody characteristics) in most individuals. However, in some people, monoclonal proteins in clonal plasma cell disorders can cause organ damage through a variety of pathogenetic mechanisms resulting in single- or multi-organ disorders that are collectively referred to as monoclonal gammopathy of clinical significance (MGCS) when occurring as a non-malignant progression of MGUS [1]. Note that specific disorders included under the term MGCS may also occur in patients with an active plasma cell malignancy such as multiple myeloma, and for purposes of classification, when this occurs it is referred to as multiple myeloma plus AL amyloidosis, or multiple myeloma plus monoclonal gammopathy-associated peripheral neuropathy, etc., rather than MGCS.

The concept of MGCS addresses a gap in the classification of monoclonal plasma cell disorders, where patients develop organ damage attributable to a monoclonal protein, despite having an underlying clonal population that does not meet the diagnostic criteria for overt malignancy, such as multiple myeloma. MGCS is not a specific disease but an overarching classification term encompassing several specific non-malignant disorders that are causally related to MGUS (Table 1; Fig. 1). Each MGCS disorder requires evidence of causal relationship to the monoclonal protein, and many have specific diagnostic criteria that need to be fulfilled. Importantly, the term MGCS raises awareness among clinicians across disciplines that MGUS can lead to serious non-malignant disorders independent of the risk of malignant progression to myeloma or related disorders. The pathogenesis of these disorders is varied, with some disorders resulting from direct deposition of monoclonal immunoglobulins or their fragments, some due to complement activation, and some due to autoantibody characteristics of the secreted monoclonal protein [2]. This review provides an overview of MGCS, encompassing its pathophysiology, classification, clinical manifestations across organ systems, diagnostic evaluation, and current management strategies. We review the spectrum of MGCS, including specific renal, neurological, cutaneous, hematological, and ocular diseases, as well as selected multisystem disorders such as POEMS syndrome, Schnitzler syndrome, cryoglobulinemia, and TEMPI syndrome. Although AL amyloidosis is part of the MGCS spectrum, it is a well-established entity that is covered elsewhere and is beyond the scope of this review.

Monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance (MGRS)

Monoclonal gammopathy of renal significance (MGRS) is the earliest defined clinical entity under MGCS and includes several specific renal disorders that are caused by monoclonal immunoglobulins (Table 2) [3, 4]. It remains one of the most well-characterized of the organ-specific MGCS syndromes with clear pathogenetic evidence and diagnostic criteria for each entity. Classification and pathogenesis

Disorders that are encompassed by MGRS are classified based on the type of deposits and their ultrastructural characteristics: organized deposits, unorganized (amorphous) deposits, or lesions without detectable deposits [4]. Organized forms are further classified into fibrillar, microtubular, or crystalline. The specific renal disorders associated with each category are summarized in Table 2. This classification highlights the diverse direct and indirect mechanisms through which monoclonal proteins cause renal injury. Direct injury results from glomerular or tubulointerstitial deposition of monoclonal immunoglobulins, which initiates local inflammation and tissue damage [5, 6]. In some cases, these immunoglobulins function as autoantibodies, targeting antigens such as PLA2R or type IV collagen. This leads to immune complex formation and complement activation [7]. In contrast, indirect mechanisms are implicated in conditions such as thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) and C3 glomerulonephritis (C3GN), where monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits are not observed. In these cases, the monoclonal protein acts as an auto-antibody against complement regulatory proteins, resulting in uncontrolled activation of the complement pathway and subsequent renal injury [8].

Clinical features

Approximately 10% of patients with MGUS develop one of the MGRS disorders, placing the estimated prevalence of MGRS at 0.32% among individuals over 50 years of age and 0.53% among those over 70 years [9, 10]. The clinical presentation (nephrotic syndrome, a mixed nephritic-nephrotic syndrome, acute kidney injury, progressive renal insufficiency, etc.) depends on the specific underlying renal pathology [11]. For example, monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition disease (MIDD) involves the glomeruli and typically manifests as nephrotic syndrome. In contrast, proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits (PGNMID) often presents with nephritic-nephrotic features. In both scenarios, glomerular involvement leads to proteinuria, hematuria, hypertension, and ultimately, renal dysfunction. Acute kidney injury is characteristic of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), while progressive renal decline is commonly associated with tubulointerstitial involvement, as seen in light chain proximal tubulopathy (LCPT) or MIDD.

Diagnosis

The diagnosis of MGRS involves two essential components: identification of a specific renal lesion that is causally related to monoclonal gammopathy (e.g., MIDD, PGNMID, etc.), and confirmation of underlying MGUS. The recently developed Mayo Clinic MGRS Prediction Tool can aid in triaging patients for a kidney biopsy [12]. The tool incorporates eight variables: presence of diabetes, free light chain ratio, 24-hour urinary protein excretion, positive urine protein electrophoresis or immunofixation electrophoresis, serum creatinine level, complement C3 level, presence of hematuria, and systolic blood pressure. At a 10% probability threshold, the tool demonstrates a 98.9% sensitivity and a 50.5% specificity. The biopsy specimens should undergo light microscopy followed by immunofluorescence staining for light chains, heavy chains, and immunoglobulins. Electron microscopy is also recommended to confirm ultrastructural features consistent with specific MGRS disorders.

To detect the underlying monoclonal gammopathy, patients should undergo evaluation with serum and urine protein electrophoresis, immunofixation, and serum-free light chain assays. Once a monoclonal gammopathy is confirmed, further investigations are necessary to identify the responsible clone, including peripheral blood flow cytometry, bone marrow biopsy, and imaging studies. Bone marrow studies should include immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, cytogenetic analysis, and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) to ascertain the nature of the clone. In cases of suspected Waldenstrom macroglobulinemia or IgM MGUS, IgM quantification and testing for the MYD88 mutation is also recommended [4]. Additionally, PET-CT may be useful for identifying FDG-avid lymph nodes that can be targeted for biopsy.

Treatment

There is limited evidence to guide the optimal treatment of the diverse spectrum of disorders classified under MGRS. The fundamental principle of therapy is the eradication of the pathogenic clone producing monoclonal immunoglobulin. Given that the primary objective is to preserve renal function, early initiation of therapy is recommended for patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) stages 1 to 3. In contrast, for patients with CKD stage 4 or end-stage renal disease (ESRD), treatment should be considered only if they are candidates for renal transplantation or if there are coexisting extra-renal manifestations [13–15].

Bortezomib or anti-CD38 monoclonal antibodies, such as daratumumab, are used to target plasma cell clones. Venetoclax has demonstrated efficacy in patients with the t (11;14) translocation. For IgM-secreting clones, rituximab is the agent of choice if bone marrow studies confirm CD20 expression in the clonal cells. Medications that are renally excreted, such as lenalidomide and melphalan, as well as nephrotoxic agents like carfilzomib, should generally be avoided due to the risk of worsening renal function. In cases of progressive kidney failure, the timely initiation of dialysis is essential. Kidney transplantation may be considered in selected patients; however, this option requires strict control of the underlying plasma cell or B-cell clone. If the clonal disease is not adequately suppressed, the risk of disease recurrence following transplantation approaches 90% [16, 17].

Outcomes in MGRS vary depending on the underlying renal lesion. In lesions, such as proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits (PGNMID), suppression of the pathogenic clone may lead to partial or complete remission and stabilization of renal function. In the absence of treatment, MGRS frequently progresses to ESRD, and a subset of patients may eventually develop overt hematologic malignancies. Therefore, all patients with MGRS should be evaluated for therapy. Treatment decisions should be individualized, considering the patient’s age, comorbid conditions, and overall functional status.

Monoclonal gammopathy of neurologic significance (MGNS)

Patients with MGUS have a 2.7-fold increased risk of developing peripheral neuropathy, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 7% [18]. Peripheral neuropathy is observed in approximately 31% of patients with IgM monoclonal gammopathy, compared to 14% in those with IgG and 6% with IgA monoclonal gammopathies, though it is likely unrelated in the vast majority of these patients [19].

All patients suspected to have peripheral neuropathy that is potentially related to monoclonal gammopathy should undergo a thorough evaluation for alternative causes since most associations are coincidental rather than a true causal relationship. These include diabetes mellitus, micronutrient deficiencies, HIV infection, hepatitis C virus infection, and other systemic disorders. This evaluation is particularly relevant for patients with IgG or IgA paraproteinemia, as these are less commonly associated with monoclonal gammopathy-associated neuropathy. Nerve conduction studies (NCS) and electromyography (EMG) help characterize the type and distribution of nerve involvement. Additional investigations may include serologic testing for anti-myelin-associated glycoprotein (anti-MAG) antibodies, anti-ganglioside antibody panels, serum cryoglobulins, and evaluation for POEMS syndrome, including serum vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) levels and a comprehensive endocrine panel. Nerve biopsy is not routinely recommended due to the significant risk of iatrogenic nerve injury. The most commonly encountered IgM-associated peripheral neuropathy is Distal Acquired Demyelinating Symmetric polyneuropathy with monoclonal gammopathy (DADS-M) [20, 21]. DADS-M is a slowly progressive, symmetrical, length-dependent, demyelinating sensory neuropathy that typically begins in the lower extremities [22]. The underlying pathogenesis involves IgM antibodies directed against MAG or other components of peripheral myelin. Clinically, patients present with prominent sensory ataxia, while distal motor weakness tends to be relatively mild. High titers of anti-MAG antibodies and the presence of the MYD88 L265P mutation are frequently observed in these patients [23]. NCS reveals a demyelinating pattern, including reduced conduction velocities and prolonged sensory and motor latencies. Patients without gait impairment are typically managed conservatively with physical therapy [24]. In individuals with significant functional impairment, first-line therapy often includes rituximab, although high-quality evidence supporting its efficacy remains limited [24, 25]. Additional therapeutic options may include intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), corticosteroids, ibrutinib, or combination regimens involving rituximab, cyclophosphamide, and prednisolone [26].

Another IgM-associated MGCS neuropathy is CANOMAD, which stands for Chronic Ataxic Neuropathy with Ophthalmoplegia, M-protein, Agglutination, and Disialosyl antibodies. This is a rare disorder characterized by IgM monoclonal gammopathy in the absence of anti-MAG antibodies. Instead, affected individuals typically exhibit anti-ganglioside antibodies, particularly anti-GD1b (disialosyl) antibodies [27]. Clinically, CANOMAD presents with a distal sensory peripheral neuropathy accompanied by prominent sensory ataxia. Ophthalmoplegia and bulbar symptoms, such as dysarthria or dysphagia, are frequently observed. Cold agglutinins may also be present in some patients. NCS shows a mixed axonal and demyelinating pattern of neuropathy. Treatment options include IVIG and rituximab, both of which have demonstrated clinical benefit in reported cases [22]. POEMS-associated peripheral neuropathy is discussed in the section on multisystem manifestations.

Sporadic late-onset nemaline myopathy associated with MGUS (SLONM-MGUS) is a rare, late-onset myopathy characterized by predominantly limb-girdle and axial weakness involving both upper and lower limbs [28]. The condition is often underdiagnosed because serum CK levels are frequently normal, EMG findings may be myopathic or mixed, and a muscle biopsy is required for diagnosis. Case series suggest that a significant proportion of patients with negative whole-exome sequencing may have SLONM [29]. Patients with SLONM and MGUS more commonly exhibit bulbar weakness and respiratory failure [30]. SLONM-MGUS is also associated with a lower likelihood of clinical improvement and maintenance of ambulatory status. The IgG kappa M-protein is the most commonly detected subtype in these patients. In reported case series, treatment approaches for SLONM-MGUS have included immunosuppression with or without plasmapheresis or plasma exchange, as well as chemotherapy with or without autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT). Higher response rates have been observed with chemotherapy-based approaches, suggesting an important role of the M-protein in disease pathogenesis [31].

Monoclonal gammopathy of cutaneous significance

The clinical features of disorders that are encompassed by the term monoclonal gammopathy of cutaneous significance fall under four distinct pathogenic mechanisms, each leading to a unique disease [32]. First, direct dermal or follicular deposition of the monoclonal protein leads to conditions such as macroglobulinosis-related dermopathy (macroglobulinoderma), and follicular hyperkeratotic spicules. Second, intravascular precipitation of the paraprotein results in thrombotic or vasculitic lesions, as observed in type I and type II mixed cryoglobulinemia. Third, monoclonal antibodies with specific biological activity may cause disorders such as acquired C1 inhibitor deficiency-related angioedema or anti-flavin xanthoderma. Fourth, excess cytokine produced by the underlying neoplastic clone drives paraneoplastic syndromes such as POEMS, Schnitzler syndrome, TEMPI syndrome, and systemic capillary leak syndrome. In these cases, cutaneous manifestations are primarily mediated by dysregulation of cytokines such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), interleukin-1β, or interleukin-6, rather than direct deposition of monoclonal protein. Treatment options for MGCuS are guided by the specific underlying pathology. Group I disorders are managed with plasma cell–directed therapies when symptoms are attributable to conditions such as Waldenström macroglobulinemia, AL amyloidosis, or plasmacytomas. Treatment strategies for multisystem disorders are discussed later in the section on multisystem disorders. In Group II, scleromyxedema has shown the most consistent response to monthly IVIG, with adjunctive use of corticosteroids, thalidomide, or proteasome inhibitors for more aggressive or neurocutaneous variants [33–35]. Scleredema may respond to IVIG and corticosteroids, while plane xanthoma is typically managed with surgical or laser excision for isolated plaques, and systemic plasma cell–directed therapy when lesions are diffuse. Necrobiotic xanthogranuloma is generally treated with IVIG, antimalarial drugs, intralesional triamcinolone, lenalidomide and proteasome inhibitors [36]. Corticosteroids remain the mainstay of treatment for pyoderma gangrenosum (systemic administration) and Sweet syndrome (intralesional or systemic use) [37–39]. Both erythema elevatum diutinum and subcorneal pustular dermatosis are typically responsive to dapsone therapy [32, 40, 41].

Cold agglutinin disease

Monoclonal gammopathy–associated cold agglutinin disease is a form of hemolytic anemia caused by an IgM autoantibody produced by an underlying low-grade B-cell/lymphoplasmacytic clone. This autoantibody agglutinates red blood cells at cold temperatures and activates the classical complement pathway [42, 43]. Clinically, patients present with chronic hemolysis that is often exacerbated by cold exposure, leading to symptoms such as fatigue, pallor, acrocyanosis, and jaundice. Diagnostic findings include a direct Coombs test positive for complement component C3 on red blood cells, an elevated cold agglutinin titer (typically ≥1:64) [44]. Rituximab monotherapy is the first-line treatment [45]. In refractory cases, combination regimens incorporating bendamustine, ibrutinib, or venetoclax may be considered. Recently, sutimlimab, a monoclonal antibody targeting C1s, has been approved for use in CAD. It functions by inhibiting the classical complement cascade initiated by IgM autoantibodies, thereby preventing hemolysis [46].

Acquired von Willebrand Syndrome (AvWS)

Patients with MGUS may develop bleeding that resembles congenital von Willebrand disease, despite having no personal or family history of bleeding disorders [47]. In monoclonal gammopathy–associated AvWS, the monoclonal immunoglobulin binds to von Willebrand factor (VWF), promoting its accelerated clearance through the reticuloendothelial system [48, 49]. This results in a reduction of high-molecular-weight VWF multimers and impaired platelet adhesion. Clinically, patients present with easy bruising, mucocutaneous bleeding, or excessive bleeding during surgical procedures. Laboratory findings typically include low levels of VWF antigen and decreased ristocetin cofactor activity [50]. For minor bleeding, the first-line treatment is 1-deamino-8-D-arginine vasopressin (DDAVP). If active bleeding is present, IVIG is recommended [51]. DDAVP is generally ineffective in controlling major bleeding; therefore, in such cases, IVIG combined with VWF concentrates constitutes first-line therapy. For patients with severe or refractory bleeding who do not respond to standard treatments, clone-directed therapy using agents such as lenalidomide, thalidomide, or bortezomib may be considered. These therapies may offer long-term remission but should be initiated with caution due to their potential adverse effects.

Monoclonal gammopathy of thrombotic significance (MGTS)

Monoclonal gammopathy of thrombotic significance (MGTS) is a recently recognized prothrombotic disorder in which a monoclonal immunoglobulin, typically of the IgG subtype, exhibits autoantibody activity against platelet factor 4 (PF4), leading to platelet activation and thrombus formation [52]. The underlying pathogenic mechanism parallels that of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) and vaccine-induced immune thrombocytopenia and thrombosis (VITT).

Clinically, patients present with recurrent, unprovoked thromboses, often occurring at atypical sites such as cerebral venous sinuses or splanchnic veins. Thrombocytopenia is a common finding, although in MGTS cases that resemble VITT, the thrombocytopenia may be transient and detectable only during thrombotic episodes [53]. Laboratory testing in MGTS frequently yields positive results on heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) assays, including the PF4-heparin ELISA and the platelet serotonin-release assay, even in the absence of prior heparin exposure [52, 54]. Unlike HIT and VITT, MGTS often presents as a chronic, atypical, and anticoagulation-refractory thrombotic disorder [53]. This distinction is clinically important, as standard anticoagulation therapy may be inadequate for managing thrombosis in MGTS. In order to diagnose and treat MGTS, specialized laboratory evidence for the monoclonal protein autoantibody activity against PF4 is needed. Although data on MGTS treatment are currently limited to case series, several therapeutic strategies have been explored. These include IVIG, plasmapheresis, and clone-directed therapies such as bortezomib, daratumumab, and dexamethasone. Further studies on antibody-depleting agents, such as efgartigimod and imlifidase are needed [45].

Monoclonal gammopathy associated ocular diseases

Although relatively uncommon, monoclonal gammopathies can affect the eyes, most frequently in the form of paraproteinemic keratopathy (PPK). In PPK, monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits accumulate within the cornea, resulting in corneal opacities and visual disturbances [55]. Patients may present with symptoms such as blurred vision, glare, or photophobia. On slit-lamp examination, the cornea often reveals crystalline or patchy deposits, typically arranged in polygonal, powdery, or diffuse stromal patterns [56]. In the early stages, visual acuity is usually preserved, as deposits tend to begin in the peripheral cornea. However, as the deposits extend toward the central cornea, corneal thickening and opacification develop, eventually leading to vision loss. Beyond corneal involvement, retinal manifestations in MGCS may include neurosensory retinal detachment and autoimmune retinopathy. Additionally, monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition has been observed in the anterior lens capsule and Descemet’s membrane.

Evaluation of PPK may involve corneal biopsy or aqueous humor sampling to confirm the presence of monoclonal immunoglobulin deposition [57]. In asymptomatic patients with mild, non-progressive corneal deposits, observation with regular ophthalmologic follow-up is appropriate. However, if corneal deposits are progressing or encroaching on the visual axis, systemic therapy should be initiated. First-line treatment typically involves a bortezomib-based regimen, which has demonstrated favorable hematologic and ocular responses [58].

Monoclonal gammopathy associated multi-system disorders

Besides unique primarily organ-confined diseases discussed above, MGUS is causally related to a variety of multi-system MGCS disorders (Table 1). Many of these are well established in medicine (AL amyloidosis, MIDD), and although they conceptually represent classic MGCS, in clinical practice, there is seldom any confusion about the causal relationship to the underlying MGUS. Some of the key MGCS multi-system disorders are discussed below.

POEMS

POEMS syndrome is a paraneoplastic multisystem disorder associated with an underlying clonal plasma cell proliferative disorder. The acronym POEMS stands for polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, monoclonal protein, and skin changes [59]. Among these features, the predominant clinical manifestation is peripheral neuropathy, which is typically symmetrical, length-dependent, and sensorimotor. Onset is usually acute or subacute, often leading to significant functional impairment [60]. Motor involvement in POEMS syndrome may include foot drop and distal muscle atrophy. When peripheral neuropathy is the sole initial presenting feature, it is frequently misdiagnosed as chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP). NCS reveals an axonal or mixed axonal-demyelinating pattern. On physical examination, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, and lymphadenopathy are commonly observed. Endocrinopathies are present in the majority of patients and may involve the gonadal, thyroid, adrenal, and glucose-regulatory axes. Dermatologic manifestations include hyperpigmentation, hypertrichosis, glomeruloid hemangiomas, acrocyanosis, and sclerodermoid skin changes. Extravascular volume overload is a hallmark feature, presenting as peripheral edema, ascites, or pleural effusion. Papilledema occurs in approximately one-third to one-half of cases. Additional clinical features include pulmonary hypertension, digital clubbing, unintentional weight loss, and an elevated risk of thrombotic events. The underlying pathophysiology of POEMS syndrome involves the overproduction of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which promotes increased vascular permeability and angiogenesis [61–63]. Serum or plasma VEGF serves as a sensitive and specific diagnostic biomarker, and its levels are also valuable for monitoring disease activity over time [64]. Additionally, patients with elevated baseline IL-6 levels have inferior event-free survival and overall survival compared with those with normal IL-6 levels [65]. IL-6 is a particularly important prognostic marker among patients who have not achieved a partial hematologic response or better.

Therapeutic strategies in POEMS syndrome are guided by the extent of disease and the presence or absence of bone marrow involvement. Patients with one or two isolated sclerotic bone lesions and no evidence of marrow infiltration are typically managed with involved-field radiation therapy [59]. In contrast, patients with disseminated skeletal lesions or bone marrow involvement require systemic therapy. In transplant-eligible patients, high-dose melphalan followed by autologous stem cell transplantation is effective [66, 67]. Other systemic therapeutic options include lenalidomide, bortezomib, and thalidomide [59]. Corticosteroids may provide transient symptomatic relief but are not sufficient as monotherapy. In refractory cases, daratumumab-based regimens have demonstrated clinical activity [68–71]. Given the elevated risk of thrombotic complications in POEMS syndrome, some centers recommend prophylactic anticoagulation, typically with low-molecular-weight heparin and a single antiplatelet agent, until serum VEGF levels fall below 1000 pg/mL [72].

A recently recognized cause of death in patients with POEMS syndrome is the capillary leak phenotype (CLP). CLP is characterized by treatment-resistant third spacing, clinically presenting as refractory ascites, pleural or pericardial effusions, or anasarca, which ultimately progresses to renal failure and cardiopulmonary arrest. Patients with CLP have significantly poorer outcomes, with a median time to death of 2.5 years, compared to 12 years in patients who die from non-CLP causes [73].

Schnitzler syndrome

Schnitzler syndrome is a rare, late onset autoinflammatory disorder characterized by a recurrent urticarial rash and an associated monoclonal gammopathy. Interleukin-1 (IL-1) plays a central role in the pathogenesis of the disease [74]. The precise role of the monoclonal protein in Schnitzler syndrome remains unclear. It is hypothesized that the monoclonal gammopathy may be causal, a secondary phenomenon, or merely a coexisting feature arising from shared pathogenic mechanisms such as inflammasome dysregulation [74].

The monoclonal gammopathy in Schnitzler syndrome is most commonly of the IgM isotype, with IgM kappa being particularly characteristic [75, 76]. Additional clinical features often include recurrent fevers, lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, and arthralgia or arthritis. Diagnosis is based on the Strasbourg criteria, which require the presence of both a chronic urticarial rash and a monoclonal gammopathy [77].

IL-1 blockade is the most effective and rapidly acting treatment for Schnitzler syndrome. Administration of anakinra, an IL-1 receptor antagonist, typically results in symptom resolution within hours. However, the symptoms usually recur within days of discontinuing therapy [76]. Newer IL–1–targeted agents, including canakinumab and rilonacept, have also demonstrated clinical efficacy and may offer more sustained disease control [78, 79]. Important complications associated with Schnitzler syndrome include the development of AA amyloidosis and lymphoproliferative disorders, underscoring the need for long-term monitoring and disease-modifying therapy [80, 81].

Type I and II cryoglobulinemia

Cryoglobulinemias are a group of disorders characterized by the precipitation of immunoglobulins at temperatures below 37 °C. Type I cryoglobulinemia involves a single monoclonal immunoglobulin, most commonly IgM, and is typically associated with an underlying lymphoproliferative disorder. In contrast, type II and type III cryoglobulinemias are referred to as mixed cryoglobulinemias, consisting of both IgG and IgM components. While type I and most type II cryoglobinemias are driven by underlying clonal B-cell or plasma cell proliferative disorders, type III is generally associated with polyclonal immune activation, often in the context of infections or autoimmune diseases [82]. The clinical manifestations of cryoglobulinemia are diverse, but most commonly involve the skin, presenting as palpable purpura, Raynaud’s phenomenon, distal ulcers, and livedo reticularis. Peripheral neuropathy, renal involvement, and arthralgias are also frequently observed. There are important differences in presentation across cryoglobulinemia subtypes. Type I cryoglobulinemia is more commonly associated with cold-induced symptoms, including skin necrosis and ulceration. In contrast, types II and III (mixed cryoglobulinemia) more frequently present with palpable purpura, arthralgias, and glomerulonephritis, reflecting immune complex–mediated vasculitis [83]. Accurate measurement of cryoglobulins is essential for diagnosis, and meticulous handling of the sample is critical to avoid false-negative results. Blood samples should be collected and centrifuged at 37 °C to prevent premature precipitation of cryoglobulins. Following centrifugation, the serum should be stored at 4 °C for a minimum of 8 days to allow for the detection of delayed precipitates, which may be missed with shorter incubation periods [83]. Treatment of cryoglobulinemia is directed at the underlying clonal disorder responsible for cryoglobulin production. Prednisolone or rituximab are used in cases with an IgM isotype or a CD20-positive B-cell clone [84, 85]. Patients should be educated on the avoidance of cold exposure.

TEMPI syndrome

TEMPI syndrome is an ultra-rare plasma cell disorder, first described in 2011, with only 49 cases reported worldwide as of January 2025 [86]. The acronym TEMPI refers to a pentad comprising telangiectasia, erythrocytosis with elevated erythropoietin levels, monoclonal gammopathy, perinephric fluid collections, and intrapulmonary shunting. Most patients initially present with telangiectasia, erythrocytosis, and monoclonal gammopathy. Due to the erythrocytosis, these cases are frequently misdiagnosed as polycythemia vera and inappropriately managed with phlebotomy, which does not address the underlying clonal disorder [87]. Perinephric fluid collections and severe hypoxia due to intrapulmonary shunting are late-stage manifestations. Additional reported features include venous thrombosis, intracranial hemorrhage, and ascites [88]. No single immunoglobulin isotype has been consistently linked with TEMPI syndrome. However, a recent review reported that IgG kappa was detected in 17 of 25 cases [89]. The exact mechanism by which the monoclonal protein or plasma cell clone leads to the constellation of symptoms observed in TEMPI syndrome remains unknown. It is hypothesized that the plasma cells may secrete circulating factors, such as macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), or that the monoclonal protein may act as an autoantibody [87, 90].

Treatment of TEMPI syndrome is directed at the underlying plasma cell clone. Therapeutic agents such as bortezomib, daratumumab, lenalidomide, and autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) have demonstrated clinical efficacy in the majority of reported cases [91–94]. Plasma cell–directed therapy leads to rapid clinical improvement in over 80% of patients. However, due to the extreme rarity of the disease, there is limited data on long-term outcomes, and further research is needed to establish optimal treatment duration and monitoring strategies.

AESOP syndrome

AESOP (Adenopathy and Extensive Skin Patch Overlying a Plasmacytoma) syndrome is a rare paraneoplastic entity characterized by a slowly expanding erythematous or violaceous cutaneous patch directly overlying a solitary bone plasmacytoma, accompanied by locoregional lymphadenopathy [95]. The syndrome predominantly affects males (~90%) with a mean age of onset of 55 years.

Two morphological skin variants have been described: a classic variant presenting as a smooth, shiny, bright-red patch with visible underlying vasculature, typically over the sternum or anterior chest; and a morphea-like variant presenting as a brownish-purple indurated plaque with a “peau d’orange” appearance, preferentially located over the lower ribs and hypochondrium. Histopathology reveals dermal capillary proliferation, mixed inflammatory infiltrate, and mucin deposition, though findings are nonspecific. Regional lymph node biopsies show nonspecific hyperplasia or Castleman-like changes in approximately 40% of cases. The underlying plasmacytoma is most commonly located in the ribs (55%) or sternum (20%), with lambda light-chain restriction in over 80% of cases and an associated monoclonal gammopathy (predominantly IgG lambda) in over 70% [96]. Sensorimotor neuropathy is present in approximately 70% of patients, and roughly 40% fulfill diagnostic criteria for POEMS syndrome at diagnosis. A substantial proportion of untreated patients subsequently develop POEMS syndrome [97].

Treatment of the underlying plasmacytoma with radiation therapy leads to regression of skin lesion and lymphadenopathy within months. AESOP syndrome should be considered in patients presenting with an expanding erythematous patch over a bony prominence, as early recognition may identify POEMS syndrome at a curable stage.

Monoclonal gammopathy–associated capillary leak syndrome

Systemic capillary leak syndrome (SCLS) is characterized by a triad of intravascular hypovolemia, hemodynamic instability, and systemic edema. SCLS can occur in association with infections, autoimmune disorders, hematologic malignancies, or drug exposure. A subset of patients exhibit a monoclonal protein and a relapsing–remitting clinical course; these patients are classified as having monoclonal gammopathy–associated capillary leak syndrome (MG-CLS), also known as Clarkson disease [98]. The exact pathophysiological role of the monoclonal protein in MG-CLS remains unclear. However, recent studies suggest that intrinsic functional abnormalities of the vascular endothelium contribute to disease pathogenesis [99–101]. Evidence from genome-wide association studies further suggests that patients with MG-CLS may be genetically predisposed to exaggerated responses to common stimuli [98, 102]. The diagnosis of SCLS requires at least one episode in which all three of the following are present: systolic blood pressure <100 mmHg, hemoglobin above the normal range for age and sex, and albumin below the normal range for age and sex [103]. IgG kappa is the most commonly associated monoclonal protein in MG-CLS.

The acute phase of the illness resembles a state of distributive shock and is primarily managed with fluid resuscitation. Given the rarity of the disease, there are no established guidelines to direct optimal fluid resuscitation in these patients. However, a strategy focused on permissive hypotension with frequent reassessment may be preferable to aggressive fluid resuscitation because excessive fluids may increase the risk of compartment syndrome and worsening systemic edema [104, 105]. The acute phase may last from days to weeks and is followed by a post-leak phase, during which extravasated fluid returns to the intravascular compartment. Management of this phase requires close cardiovascular monitoring and often diuresis. IVIG has been reported to be effective in the management of acute flares of MG-CLS in some case series [106, 107]. The most important intervention to improve the prognosis of patients with MG-CLS is maintenance therapy with IVIG, which reduces the rates of relapse and improves overall survival [108, 109].

Conclusion

MGCS, and its subtypes such as MGRS, MGNS, etc. are not unique disease entities, but important concepts and classifying systems that highlight that monoclonal proteins secreted by clonal plasma cell disorders can lead to organ damage and specific diseases independent of their inherent risk of progression to malignancy. Often, it is the onset of an MGCS disorder that reveals the underlying monoclonal gammopathy for the first time, but these disorders can also arise months or years later in patients who are followed for MGUS. MGCS disorders should be considered in patients who have underlying MGUS and unexplained organ dysfunction fitting the clinical presentation of one of the many disorders known to be causally related to MGUS. We discourage the use of terms like “MGCS” or “MGRS” in patient care unless there is a clear specific disorder that is identified and confirmed. For instance, unless there is biopsy proof of PGNMID, MIDD, AL amyloidosis, etc., the term “MGRS” should not be applied. Thus, patients with proteinuria and MGUS may possibly have an MGRS disorder, but unless the specific disorder is established (e.g., PGNMID), they should not be classified as having MGCS or MGRS. This rigor is essential to avoid over-treatment with powerful immunosuppressive agents used to eradicate the underlying clone. In some MGCS disorders such as monoclonal gammopathy-associated neuropathy, there are no good diagnostic tests to definitively conclude causal association. In general, we refrain from therapy unless on follow-up the neuropathy is serious, progressive, without other cause, and the risk-benefits are well explained to the patient. In fact, we seldom offer clone-directed therapy in these instances because most patients do not fit these requirements, and the results of intervention are not impressive. Overall, the message is that each entity listed in Tables 1–6 are unique disorder, and each has to be diagnosed and managed uniquely. For established MGCS disorders such as PGNMID, MIDD, C3 glomerulonephritis, etc., early recognition of MGCS is crucial and relies on maintaining a high index of suspicion. Timely initiation of therapy can halt disease progression and, in many cases, reverse organ damage. The diagnosis and management of MGCS requires close collaboration between hematologists and organ-specific specialists, combining clone-directed therapies with supportive interventions.
