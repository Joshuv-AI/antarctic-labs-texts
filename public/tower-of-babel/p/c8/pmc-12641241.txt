AP‐1 Is an Initial Convergent Transcriptional Response in Lymphatic Endothelium to VEGF‐C or TNFα
Authors: Ewa A Kreft, Zuzanna J Juśkiewicz, Brant E Isakson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe lymphatic vasculature plays a central role in resolving inflammation by draining interstitial fluid, immune cells, and inflammatory mediators. While vascular endothelial growth factor C (VEGF‐C) is a well‐established driver of lymphangiogenesis, the effects of chronic pro‐inflammatory cytokines on lymphatic remodeling remain incompletely defined.MethodsHere, we investigated how tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) compares with VEGF‐C in regulating lymphatic endothelial structure and function using human dermal lymphatic endothelial cells (HDLECs).ResultsIn tube formation and spheroid sprouting assays, both VEGF‐C and TNFα supported early lymphangiogenic events, promoting robust sprouting and network development within 24 h. However, when pre‐formed microvascular networks were challenged, prolonged TNFα exposure triggered progressive destabilization, characterized by tube fragmentation, reduced proliferation, and impaired metabolic activity, whereas VEGF‐C preserved network stability. Mechanistically, both VEGF‐C and TNFα induced rapid phosphorylation of p38 MAPK and upregulation of Activator Protein‐1 (AP‐1) transcription factor subunits within 30 min, suggesting a convergent early transcriptional response. Bulk RNA‐sequencing confirmed shared induction of AP‐1 family genes (FOS, JUN, ATF), highlighting AP‐1 as a candidate regulator of the transition between adaptive and maladaptive outcomes. We propose that transient AP‐1 activation promotes pro‐lymphangiogenic programs, while sustained TNFα signaling redirects AP‐1 activity toward stress, growth arrest, and apoptosis, leading to lymphatic regression.ConclusionThese findings identify TNFα as a temporally bifunctional regulator of lymphatic endothelial fate.

Introduction

Inflammatory responses are orchestrated by multiple mechanisms, with the vasculature playing a central role in both their initiation and resolution. While blood vessels primarily regulate leukocyte recruitment and tissue perfusion [1, 2], the lymphatic vasculature is essential for clearing interstitial fluid, trafficking immune cells, and draining inflammatory mediators to restore tissue homeostasis [3, 4]. Given its multifaceted role, the lymphatic endothelium emerges as a critical regulator of inflammatory balance [5, 6]. Particularly important are the initial capillary lymphatics, which remain in direct contact with inflamed tissues. Their unique structure consisting of thin, permeable endothelial walls with button‐like junctions renders lymphatic capillaries particularly sensitive to inflammatory cues and allows entry of exogenous agents, including pathogens such as bacteria and viruses [7, 8].

During inflammation lymphatic vessels exhibit structural and functional plasticity. One immediate response is the dilation of existing vessels, which enables the accommodation of the increased interstitial fluid accumulation in inflamed tissues [9, 10]. Beyond this acute adaptation, inflammation frequently induces lymphangiogenesis—the expansion and remodeling of lymphatic networks—which serves a reparative purpose by facilitating immune surveillance, promoting antigen transport to draining lymph nodes, and accelerating the clearance of excess cytokines, chemokines, and cellular debris [11]. Together, these processes act to restore tissue homeostasis and promote the resolution of inflammation. Moreover, their dysfunction has profound consequences: compromised drainage and impaired immune trafficking fuel edema, prevent resolution, and promote chronic inflammation, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting the lymphatic vasculature [12, 13].

Despite growing recognition of the lymphatic vasculature as a central regulator of inflammation, beyond the well‐described lymphangiogenic responses, far less is known about how persistent inflammatory stimuli influence the structure and function of capillary lymphatic vessels. Furthermore, the molecular cues that determine whether remodeling promotes vessel formation or drives pathological disintegration remain poorly defined. In particular, the impact of persistent exposure to pro‐inflammatory cytokines on lymphatic structure and function is poorly understood. Among these mediators, Tumor Necrosis Factor Alpha (TNFα) is of special relevance, as it is chronically elevated across a broad spectrum of inflammatory disorders, including rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis, atherosclerosis, and cancer [14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. TNFα is a validated therapeutic target in several of these conditions [19, 20, 21, 22]. Through its effects on endothelial barrier function [23] and junctional organization [24], TNFα has the potential to shift lymphatic remodeling away from pro‐resolving adaptation toward destabilization and chronic dysfunction. Dissecting molecular mechanisms that govern this shift is critical for understanding how the lymphatic vasculature moves from supporting resolution to sustaining inflammation.

In this study, we investigated how TNFα compares to Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor C (VEGF‐C), a well‐established driver of lymphangiogenesis [25], in both the induction of new lymphatic growth and the maintenance of established microvascular networks. Here, we demonstrate that VEGF‐C and TNFα exert similar initial effects on lymphatic capillary micronetworks, which may converge to mediate these early events through the transcription factor Activator Protein 1 (AP‐1).

Methods

Cell Culture

Primary human dermal lymphatic endothelial cells (HDLECs; Promocell, C‐12217) were maintained in Endothelial Cell Growth Medium V2 (ECMV2, Promocell, C‐22022) supplemented with the manufacturer's recommended growth factors and penicillin/streptomycin (ThermoFisher Scientific, 15140122). Cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 on gelatin‐coated culture dishes (Bovine Gelatin, Sigma, G9391). Experiments were performed using cells between passages 2 and 10.

Tube Formation Assay

HDLECs were harvested, counted, and resuspended at 5 × 105 cells/mL in basal ECMV2 supplemented with 1% fetal bovine serum (FBS, R&D Systems, S12450). A total of 2.5 × 104 cells in 50 μL suspension were seeded onto pre‐solidified Matrigel (Corning, CLS354234‐1EA) and cultured for 24 h under control conditions (1% FBS), with recombinant human VEGF‐C (400 ng/mL, Proteintech, HZ‐1336), or recombinant human TNFα (10 ng/mL, Proteintech, HZ‐1014). Tube networks were imaged using an Olympus CKX53 inverted microscope (Olympus Life Science, Japan) with a 4× objective. Tubular networks were quantified by skeletonization of the images in ImageJ2 (version 2.16.0) [26] and then analyzing the skeletons in R Studio (R version 2023.09.1 + 494), using openly accessible scripts available on GitHub [27].

Tube Maintenance Assay

HDLECs were seeded onto Matrigel and cultured for 24 h in basal ECMV2 supplemented with 1% FBS to allow formation of tubular networks, following the same procedure as the tube formation assay. After 24 h, the established networks were imaged using an Olympus CKX53 inverted microscope at 4× to record baseline morphology. Networks were then stimulated by adding 50 μL of medium containing either vehicle control, VEGF‐C (400 ng/mL), or TNFα (10 ng/mL) directly onto the pre‐formed tubes. Cultures were imaged after 24 h, re‐stimulated under the same conditions, and incubated for an additional 24 h before imaging. Tubular networks were quantified as previously described, and changes were calculated relative to the baseline morphology measured before the first stimulation.

Spheroid Sprouting Assay

HDLEC spheroids were generated by seeding 1500 cells per hanging drop in complete ECMV2 supplemented with methylcellulose (4000 cP viscosity, Sigma‐Aldrich, M0512) and incubated at 37°C in a humidified incubator overnight. The following day, spheroids were embedded in a 3‐dimensional matrix consisting of collagen type I (Rat Collagen I, Corning, 354236) and methylcellulose, supplemented with phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA, ThermoFisher Scientific, 356150010). After solidification, matrices were overlaid with basal ECMV2 containing 10% FBS (control), VEGF‐C (400 ng/mL), or TNFα (10 ng/mL). Matrices were maintained in culture for 24 h, and sprout development was captured at 10× magnification with an Olympus CKX53 inverted microscope. The number of sprouts and their lengths were quantified using ImageJ2 and averaged across all spheroids per condition.

Immunofluorescence Staining

HDLECs were seeded onto gelatin‐coated 13‐mm sterile coverslips (6 × 104 cells per well) in 24‐well plates and cultured in complete ECMV2 to ~80% confluence. Cells were serum‐starved for 24 h in basal ECMV2 supplemented with 1% FBS, then stimulated with VEGF‐C (400 ng/mL), TNFα (10 ng/mL), or vehicle control for 30 min, 24 h, or 48 h. For the 48‐h condition, cells were restimulated 24 h after the initial stimulation. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma‐Aldrich, 158127) for 15 min at room temperature, washed with 1× PBS (ThermoFisher Scientific, 70011044), and permeabilized/blocked for 2 h in 1× PBS containing 2% BSA (Fisher Scientific, BP1600‐100) and 0.5% Triton X‐100 (Thermo Scientific, A16046.AE) under gentle agitation. Coverslips were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti‐Ki67 antibody (clone 1O15, ZooMAb Rabbit mAb, Sigma‐Aldrich, ZRB1007‐25UL, 1:100) diluted in blocking buffer. The following day, samples were rinsed four times in 1× PBS (5 min each) and incubated for 2 h at room temperature in the darkness with Donkey anti‐Rabbit IgG Secondary Antibody Alexa Fluor 568 (ThermoFisher, A10037). Coverslips were washed four times with 1× PBS (5 min each), counterstained with 1:1000 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI, 5 mg/mL stock; Invitrogen D1306), washed in 1× PBS, and mounted with a droplet of ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant with DNA Stain DAPI (Fisher Scientific, P36931) on a microscope slide. Images were acquired using the FLUOVIEW 3000 confocal microscope (Olympus Life Science) with the 40× water immersion objective. Five random fields per coverslip were imaged. The proportion of Ki67+ nuclei (Ki67+/DAPI+) was quantified and averaged per coverslip, which was considered one biological replicate.

Viability Assay

HDLECs were seeded in gelatin‐coated 96‐well plates at 5 × 103 cells per well in 200 μL complete ECMV2 and grown to ~80% confluence. Cells were serum‐starved for 24 h in basal ECMV2 and then stimulated with vehicle control, VEGF‐C (400 ng/mL), or TNFα (10 ng/mL) for 30 min, 24 h, or 48 h. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Proliferation Kit I (Roche, 11465007001) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) was added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37°C in the dark, followed by the addition of solubilization solution. Absorbance was measured at 560 nm using a FLUOStar Omega microplate reader (BMG Labtech, Germany), and values were corrected by subtracting blank readings.

Protein Collection and Western Blotting

HDLECs were seeded in gelatin‐coated 10‐cm dishes and grown to approximately 80% confluence in complete ECMV2. Cells were serum‐starved for 24 h and then stimulated for 30 min with either vehicle control, VEGF‐C (400 ng/mL), or TNFα (10 ng/mL). After stimulation, cells were collected in ice‐cold protein lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Sigma‐Aldrich, P8340 and P0044), vortexed for 30 s, and centrifuged to remove debris. Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher, 23225). Equal amounts of protein were mixed with Laemmli buffer (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, 161‐0737), separated by SDS‐PAGE using 4%–12% Bis‐Tris precast gels (Invitrogen, WG1401BOX), and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Genesee, 84‐876). Membranes were blocked in 5% BSA in TBST and incubated overnight at 4°C with phospho‐p38 MAPK (Thr180/Tyr182) antibody (D3F9, XP Rabbit mAb, Cell Signaling Technology, 4511, 1:500). Following washing, membranes were incubated with anti‐rabbit IRDye 800CW secondary antibody (LI‐COR, 926‐32 211) at room temperature for 1 h, washed in TBST, and imaged using a LI‐COR Odyssey infrared scanner (LI‐COR, USA). To assess total p38 levels, the same membranes were stripped and reprobed with p38 MAPK antibody (D13E1, XP Rabbit mAb, Cell Signaling Technology, 8690, 1:500), followed by incubation with the same secondary antibody and imaging process as previously described. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ2.

RNA Isolation and Sequencing

Cells were cultured and stimulated under the same conditions as those used for protein collection. Total RNA was isolated using the Quick‐RNA MiniPrep Kit (Zymo Research Corporation, 50‐444‐597) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration was measured, and samples were submitted to Novogene (USA) for bulk RNA sequencing on the NovaSeq X Plus platform (paired‐end 150 bp). Data processing, quality control, and differential expression analyses were performed using RStudio and the BIOMEX platform [28].

Statistical Analysis and Data Visualization

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.5.0, GraphPad Software, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SEM. The number of independent biological replicates (n) is indicated in the figure legends; technical replicates were averaged prior to analysis unless stated otherwise. Differences between groups were assessed using one‐way ANOVA followed by Holm–Šidák's multiple comparisons test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Unless stated otherwise, the graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism.

Results

To evaluate the functional response of HDLECs to VEGF‐C and TNFα, we first employed a Matrigel tube formation assay (Figure 1A). After 24 h of stimulation, neither VEGF‐C nor TNFα impaired tube formation; instead, representative images demonstrate both conditions supported robust network development (Figure 1B). To further assess whether VEGF‐C or TNFαcan actively promote a pro‐lymphangiogenic phenotype, we next performed a spheroid sprouting assay (Figure 1C) with representative images in Figure 1D. Quantitative analysis of tube formation confirmed that total tube length did not differ significantly between groups (Figure 1E), indicating that TNFα does not suppress lymphangiogenic capacity at this time point. Quantitative measurement of the VEGF‐C and TNFα‐induced sprout number and length both demonstrates significant increases compared to control (p < 0.05), with differences between VEGF‐C and TNFα (Figure 1F,G). These results suggest that TNFα, like VEGF‐C, supports early lymphangiogenic events.

VEGF‐C and TNFα both drive lymphatic endothelial sprouting. Schematic representation of the tube formation assay (A) with corresponding representative images (B, scale bar: 1 mm). Schematic of the spheroid sprouting assay (C) with representative images (D, scale bar: 500 μm). Quantification showed no significant differences in tube formation (E, n = 4 biological replicates), indicating that TNFα does not inhibit network formation, while spheroid sprouting (F, G, n = 5 biological replicates) revealed comparable induction of sprout number and cumulative length by TNFα and VEGF‐C. Data were analyzed by one‐way ANOVA with Holm–Šidák's post hoc correction.

Because our interest was primarily focused on the fate of established lymphatic microvessels, we next adapted the tube assay to stimulate pre‐formed networks (Figure 2A). Unlike the pro‐lymphangiogenic effect observed at early time points, prolonged exposure to TNFα markedly destabilized these networks. After 48 h of treatment, average tube length was reduced by 28.9% compared to vehicle and by 37.6% compared to VEGF‐C (p < 0.05) (Figure 2C). Morphological assessment further revealed extensive thinning, fragmentation, and collapse of TNFα‐treated structures, whereas VEGF‐C preserved network organization comparable to control (Figure 2D–F). Notably, at 24 h no consistent structural differences were detected (Figure 2B), Together, our data suggest that chronic, rather than acute, TNFα signaling is required to trigger vascular disintegration. This delayed yet reproducible phenotype across independent replicates highlights a time‐dependent destabilizing influence of TNFα on lymphatic endothelial networks, in contrast to the stabilizing role of VEGF‐C.

TNFα promotes regression of established lymphatic tube networks in contrast to VEGF‐C. Schematic representation of the tube maintenance assay (A). Quantification of tube length at 24 h (B, n = 3 biological replicates) and 48 h (C, n = 3 biological replicates) showed significant regression in TNFα‐treated networks compared with VEGF‐C or vehicle control. Representative images of the tube formation assay at time 0 (D), 24 h (E), and 48 h (F). One‐way ANOVA with Holm– Šidák's post hoc analysis was applied for statistical evaluation.

To determine whether this structural collapse was associated with alterations in proliferative capacity or cell health, we next examined the expression of the proliferation marker Ki67 and MTT viability. Ki67 analysis revealed that at 30 min, proliferative indices were comparable between groups (38.99% in Control, 48.9% in VEGF‐C, 40.31% in TNFα; Figure 3A,B). By 24 h, Ki67+ fractions had slightly declined in all groups without reaching significance (36.17% in Control, 32.25% in VEGF‐C, 24.56% in TNFα; Figure 3C,D). However, by 48 h, TNFα‐treated cells exhibited a significant reduction in proliferation (18.49%), compared to 26.51% in Control and 38.90% in VEGF‐C (Figure 3E,F). This progressive decline indicates a selective suppression of cell proliferation under prolonged TNFα exposure. Consistently, MTT assays showed no significant changes at 30 min or 24 h, but by 48 h, a marked reduction in absorbance was observed in TNFα‐treated cells relative to both Control and VEGF‐C (Figure 3G–I), indicating impaired metabolic activity. Taken together, these data suggest that TNFα exerts a delayed cytotoxic effect, manifested as reduced proliferation and viability, which correlates with the morphological collapse observed in tube maintenance assays.

Statistical significance was assessed using one‐way ANOVA with Holm– Šidák's post hoc correction (B, 30 min, n = 4 biological replicates; D, 24 h, n = 4 biological replicates; F, 48 h, n = 5 biological replicates). MTT assay results at the corresponding time points (G, 30 min; H, 24 h; I, 48 h, n = 6 biological replicates each) revealed a significant decrease in cell viability at 48 h, as determined by one‐way ANOVA with Holm–Šidák's post hoc correction.

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying these temporal differences, we first analyzed phosphorylation of p38 MAPK—a canonical mediator of TNFα signaling—after 30 min of stimulation. Both VEGF‐C and TNFα induced a comparable increase in phosphorylated p38 relative to Control (0.40 in Control vs. 0.77 in VEGF‐C and 0.76 in TNFα, p < 0.05) (Figure 4A,B). This shared activation provides a plausible explanation for the similar short‐term phenotypes, including intact tubular networks, preserved proliferation, and viability.

Both TNFα and VEGF‐C trigger phosphorylation of p38 MAPK within 30 min. Representative Western blot of phosphorylated p38 MAPK (p‐p38) in HDLECs treated with VEGF‐C or TNFα for 30 min (A). Densitometric quantification of Western blots is expressed as the ratio of p‐p38 to total p38 (B, n = 3 biological replicates). To address what may be mediating an initial convergent pathway between VEGF‐C and TNFα (and possibly the divergent pathway after 24 h), we performed bulk RNA sequencing after 30 min of stimulation to capture early transcriptomic signatures (Figure 5A). Differential expression analysis revealed 71 upregulated and 40 downregulated genes in Control versus VEGF‐C, and 83 upregulated and 50 downregulated genes in Control versus TNFα (Figure 5B,C). While many of the differentially expressed genes were linked to well‐established proinflammatory pathways (Figure S1), our focus turned toward transcriptional regulators that might explain the divergent long‐term outcomes. This led us to the AP‐1 complex, a dimeric transcription factor typically composed of members of the FOS, JUN, and ATF families [29, 30]. Notably, transcripts encoding several AP‐1 subunits all exhibited an upward expression trend in both VEGF‐C– and TNFα‐stimulated cells (Figure 5D,E), with many of the AP‐1 components showing significant increases.

Given AP‐1's proven roles in balancing pro‐survival responses with stress‐induced apoptotic signaling [31, 32, 33], its early induction provides a compelling mechanistic link. We propose that AP‐1 activity may initially promote a pro‐lymphangiogenic phenotype in response to both VEGF‐C and TNFα, thereby explaining their early similarities. However, in the context of chronic TNFα signaling, AP‐1–driven transcription may gradually shift toward a program favoring growth arrest, reduced metabolic activity, and apoptosis, resulting in the structural disintegration of lymphatic microvascular networks (Figure 6). This model underscores a critical temporal dimension in cytokine signaling, where early convergence gives way to later divergence in lymphatic endothelial cell fate.

Discussion

A key mediator across diverse inflammatory conditions is TNFα [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 34, 35, 36] whose effects on the vasculature are context and time‐dependent, sometimes promoting adaptive lymphatic remodeling [37, 38], yet in other settings driving vessel regression and dysfunction [39]. Our initial assays revealed that TNFα, similar to VEGF‐C, supported tube formation and spheroid sprouting within the first 24 h (Figure 1). These results demonstrate that TNFα does not suppress lymphangiogenic capacity at early time points, consistent with previous reports that inflammatory cytokines can induce sprouting in lymphatic endothelial cells [40, 41]. However, when pre‐formed lymphatic networks were challenged, chronic TNFα exposure caused a progressive collapse of tubular structures, whereas VEGF‐C preserved network stability. Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant reduction in tube length only after 48 h of TNFα treatment, highlighting a time‐dependent destabilizing effect (Figure 2). Our findings demonstrate that while TNFα supports early lymphangiogenic events comparable to VEGF‐C, prolonged exposure destabilizes established networks, underscoring its dual role in lymphatic microvascular remodeling. Although both the tube formation and spheroid sprouting assays represent three‐dimensional models of lymphangiogenesis, they assess distinct cellular processes. Tube formation on Matrigel reflects rapid network organization within a highly permissive, growth factor–enriched environment, whereas the spheroid sprouting assay recapitulates endothelial polarization, invasion, and directed migration into a collagen matrix. Under these respective conditions, TNFα fails to suppress tube formation within the nutrient‐rich environment but elicits a pro‐sprouting response comparable to that induced by VEGF‐C. Collectively, these observations highlight the context‐dependent nature of TNFα‐mediated lymphatic endothelial responses, emphasizing that the two assays capture complementary, rather than overlapping, mechanisms of lymphangiogenic regulation. These observations were made in an in vitro model, which captures direct endothelial responses but does not encompass the additional layers of immune and stromal regulation that could potentially affect the lymphatic remodeling in vivo. For instance, in the case of inflammatory arthritis, TNFα can stimulate myeloid cells such as osteoclast precursors to produce VEGF‐C via NF‐κB signaling, thereby increasing lymphatic vessel density in inflamed joints [42]. However, the effects of anti‐TNF therapies further highlight this complexity: while blockade of TNFα has been associated with enhanced lymphatic density and function in arthritic joints [43], it attenuates lymphangiogenic remodeling in other contexts, such as chronic airway inflammation, particularly in TNFR1‐deficient or anti‐TNF–treated models [44]. Moreover, our findings are consistent with those of Kemp et al., who examined the prolonged effects of multiple proinflammatory mediators (including TNFα, interferon‐γ (IFNγ), and thrombin) on lymphatic endothelial tubular structures in collagen and fibrin matrices. They showed that the destabilized phenotype could be pharmacologically rescued with, that is, FISTchSB2K—a combination therapy including cAMP upregulator, p38 MAPK inhibitor, tubulin deacetylase (HDAC6) inhibitor, as well as ALK1 and ALK2 inhibitor [39, 45], underscoring the translational potential of targeting inflammatory mediators to preserve lymphatic microvasculature in chronic disease.

Our findings that TNFα‐treated HDLECs preserve metabolic activity at early timepoints, which is significantly reduced by 48 h in both cases, align with foundational evidence that endothelial susceptibility to TNFα is highly context‐dependent. While TNFα alone often induces only modest apoptosis, the blockade of NF‐κB or inhibition of protein synthesis unmasks robust caspase‐dependent death in primary endothelial cells, highlighting the requirement for survival signaling to counterbalance TNFα's pro‐apoptotic effects [46, 47, 48, 49]. Mechanistically, TNFα activates caspase‐8 with downstream mitochondrial involvement, which is opposed by NF‐κB–inducible Bcl‐2 family proteins, such as A1 [50, 51]. Consistent with this, protective pathways (e.g., PI3K/Akt, ERK, VEGF, NO/cGMP) have been shown to mitigate TNFα‐induced loss of viability [52, 53, 54, 55, 56], whereas ROS‐linked signaling promotes cytotoxicity [57]. Importantly, the temporal relationship between TNFα and VEGF‐C signaling provides a mechanistic framework for these observations. In tumor models, TNFα frequently acts upstream to induce VEGF‐C transcription via NF‐κB within ~24–48 h and to increase VEGF‐C secretion (including in cervical cancer cells at 48 h) [41, 58], while also recruiting macrophages that serve as secondary VEGF‐C sources in vivo. Moreover, VEGFR3 signaling is necessary for TNFα‐driven lymphangiogenesis, as VEGFR3 blockade abolishes this effect [40]. At the endothelial level, TNFα pretreatment is required to induce VCAM‐1 and reveal VCAM‐1–dependent permeability to tumor‐conditioned media, indicating an explicit order requirement with TNFα preceding permeability‐inducing cues [59]. Acutely, TNFα disrupts LEC junctions over hours in human monolayers [24], whereas VEGF‐C has likewise been reported to compromise lymphatic barrier integrity, though the kinetics of this response remain unresolved [60]. Taken together, these data support a temporally ordered model in which early TNFα exposure preserves endothelial viability through NF‐κB–dependent survival programs—thereby permitting or priming subsequent VEGF‐C‐mediated proliferative and remodeling responses—whereas sustained TNFα stimulation progressively shifts the balance toward apoptotic and stress pathways, consistent with the late reductions in proliferation and metabolic activity observed in our HDLEC assays. However, it is essential to note that most prior work that aimed at describing cellular responses to TNFα exposure has been conducted in blood endothelial cells, and whether these observations fully translate to lymphatic endothelium remains a far less studied area. Our observations in HDLECs provide an important new insight, suggesting the late reduction in proliferation and metabolic viability observed in our assays likely reflects the progressive dominance of apoptotic over survival pathways during sustained TNFα exposure.

Mechanistically, both VEGF‐C and TNFα elicited rapid p38 MAPK phosphorylation (Figure 4), providing a plausible basis for their shared early phenotype of proliferation and network integrity. Yet, sustained p38 activation is known to drive endothelial apoptosis by phosphorylation and downregulation of anti‐apoptotic Bcl‐xL—an effect blocked by p38 inhibition in prior studies [61]. This shared activation provides a possible explanation for the similar short‐term phenotypes; however, it also raises the question of how such convergent signaling at early time points diverges into opposite outcomes upon prolonged stimulation. One possible explanation is that divergent outcomes might arise from differences in signaling context and feedback regulation. While VEGF‐C appears to counterbalance this stress pathway by co‐activating pro‐survival cascades such as PI3K/Akt and ERK [62, 63], TNFα couples p38 to JNK and NF‐κB signaling in a manner that progressively favors apoptosis once transient NF‐κB–mediated protection wanes [64, 65]. More work on these pathways is warrented.

Consistent with the p38 phosphorylation, we show upstream signaling events converge at the transcriptional level, where bulk RNA‐seq data revealed early induction of AP‐1 family transcription factors (Figure 5E) in response to both stimuli. AP‐1 is recognized as a context‐dependent regulator: transient activation can drive migration and proliferation, whereas persistent or dysregulated activity often tilts toward apoptosis and growth inhibition [66]. Moreover, AP‐1 has emerged as a regulatory node that integrates mechanical and inflammatory inputs to shape endothelial fate. In blood vascular endothelium disturbed flow activates AP‐1 through multiple upstream sensors, including integrin α5β1–PKN1, GRK2, and Piezo1, which converge on histone modification, metabolic coupling, and chromatin remodeling to drive immediate early FOS/FOSB and JUN expression [67, 68, 69]. Beyond inflammation, subunit‐specific roles link JunB to sprouting and tube morphogenesis [70], FOSL1 to adhesion and migration [71, 72], and AP‐1 broadly to VEGF‐driven endothelial proliferation [73]. Whether these mechanisms translate to lymphatic endothelium remains unclear; however, JunB has been shown to regulate vascular sprouting and lymphatic development in zebrafish [74], while broader reviews highlight AP‐1's dual roles in balancing survival and death programs [66, 75]. This positions AP‐1 as a temporal transcriptional switch initially enabling lymphangiogenic programs shared by both VEGF‐C and TNFα, but under prolonged TNFα exposure, shifting toward stress‐induced, cytotoxic gene networks, thus explaining the divergence in outcome.

In conclusion, our data identify TNFα as a temporally bifunctional regulator of lymphatic endothelial fate. Short‐term exposure converges with the effects of VEGF‐C to promote sprouting and network formation, whereas sustained signaling suppresses proliferation, diminishes metabolic activity, and promotes vascular regression. This transition is linked to shared early activation of p38 MAPK and AP‐1, followed by a divergence in downstream transcriptional programs that bias VEGF‐C toward survival and TNFα toward stress and apoptosis. These findings may help reconcile contradictory views of TNFα as both a pro‐ and anti‐lymphangiogenic factor and underscore that the duration and context of cytokine signaling—rather than its identity alone—determine endothelial outcomes. We propose a duration‐dependent signaling switch in which TNFα initially supports lymphatic adaptation but, when sustained, redirects AP‐1–driven transcription toward stress and apoptosis. Future studies employing pathway‐specific analyses will be essential to define the molecular divergence between TNFα and VEGF‐C signaling and to evaluate whether therapeutic modulation of AP‐1 could preserve lymphatic integrity during chronic inflammation.

Perspectives

This work positions AP‐1 as an early, convergent transcriptional node in lymphatic endothelium, showing that VEGF‐C and TNFα initially trigger overlapping programs before TNFα drives a time‐dependent shift toward endothelial destabilization and apoptosis. The work integrates RNA‐seq with 3D spheroid sprouting assays to connect transcriptional dynamics to functional remodeling of lymphatic vessels. The work reframes inflammatory lymphatic remodeling as a temporal signaling problem, suggesting that targeting AP‐1 timing could yield more precise strategies for edema, chronic inflammation, or tumor‐associated lymphangiogenesis.
