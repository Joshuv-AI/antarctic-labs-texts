Spatial Activity Patterning and Topological Defect Transport in Acoustically Energized Active Liquid Crystals
Authors: Antonio Tavera‐Vázquez, Paul F. Nealey, Alexey Snezhko, Andrey Sokolov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPatterning activity in space is expected to transform active nematics from chaotic dynamic fluids into reconfigurable materials capable of controlled transport of topological defects. Although some of these capabilities have been investigated in computational models, the experimental realization has been limited by the challenges of reliable generation and modulation of spatiotemporal activity patterns. Here, we report a versatile approach for spatial activity patterning in a quasi 2D acoustically powered active liquid crystal with activity controlled solely by the local confinement height. We demonstrate that linear gradients and step‐like variations in the local channel height set the local activity level in the active liquid crystal and govern the behavior of topological defects. Based on our findings, we realized activity patterns that support a sustained antiparallel transport of +1/2 and ‐1/2 topological defects. A minimal agent‐based model confirms that the observed topological defect transport arises from geometry‐induced spatial modulation of activity rather than from direct geometric rectification of defect motion. Our findings establish a scalable strategy for programming the dynamics of active nematic materials, and advance the development of active microfluidic architectures that utilize topological defect transport for information processing.

Introduction

New material functionalities, such as autonomous microscopic motion, targeted cargo transport, self‐healing, adaptive reconfiguration, and even logic operations, can be programmed by tuning how energy is injected and dissipated at small scales [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Developing robust strategies to control how external stimuli or forces drive materials out of equilibrium is essential for unlocking the practical potential of active matter. Among the most promising approaches is the spatiotemporal modulation of energy input, which can break symmetries and steer active systems toward directed transport, dynamic pattern formation, and adaptive responses [7, 8, 9]. Recent advances across biological and synthetic platforms have demonstrated that local activity can be modulated via external cues, such as electromagnetic fields, chemical gradients, and heat, with light‐based approaches showing particular promise due to their high spatiotemporal resolution [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Another elegant experimental example is the use of patterned orientational fields and topological defects in DSCG liquid crystals to direct the motion and spatial organization of active bacteria [19].

In active nematic systems, spatial activity modulation has emerged as an effective strategy for directing topological defect dynamics. While experimental demonstrations employing light‐responsive microtubule–molecular motor assemblies have shown feasibility of the approach [20, 21, 22, 23, 24], many of the most compelling effects, such as long‐range defect transport, ratcheting flows, and logic operations, remain largely limited to computational modeling [23, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31].

Translating these behaviors into robust experimental implementations remains challenging due to inherent limitations of biological active matter systems, including variability in material properties, photo‐bleaching, degradation of protein components, slow defect dynamics, interfacial stability, and restricted dynamic range of light‐controlled activity [24, 32, 33, 34, 35]. Together, these limitations have restricted experimental studies of defect‐based transport largely to proof‐of‐principle demonstrations.

In this work, we demonstrate a robust approach for activity patterning and controlling topological defect dynamics in an active liquid crystal. We utilize acoustically energized lyotropic chromonic liquid crystal (LCLC), specifically Disodium Cromoglycate (DSCG), confined within soft polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) microchannel of a variable height. Acoustic excitations of LCLCs are capable of generating extensile stresses at the microscale, making the material intrinsically active and leading to the emergence of undulation instability, proliferation of topological defects and chaotic flows [36]. As a fully synthetic system, acoustically powered active liquid crystals offer exceptional material stability, tunable viscosity, and elasticity, and the ability to accommodate a wider range of activities. Our approach leverages the sensitivity of active stress generation to local channel height, allowing spatial modulation of activity through controlled variations in channel thickness. Using this strategy, we demonstrate the generation and guided transport of topological defects in geometrically patterned microfluidic circuits. Specifically, we realized annular channels with periodically modulated height profiles. Upon excitation, the system exhibited patterned generation of topological defects and their sustained, directional transport. The observed topological defect dynamics is captured by a minimal phenomenological agent‐based model. In this model, defects are treated as particles interacting with each other, as demanded by the elasticity of the underlying nematic phase [37, 38, 39]. The simulations not only reproduce the experimentally observed topological currents, but also reveal the microscopic mechanisms underlying the directed motion of non‐motile negative defects. The directed transport emerges from the interplay of activity gradients, periodic redistribution of topological charges, and pairwise defect interactions. The results validate a new strategy for topological control in active matter systems, offering a scalable route toward microfluidic architectures with programmable topological defect transport.

Results and Discussion

Spatial Activity Patterning

In our experiments, lyotropic chromonic liquid crystal (DSCG) is confined within microchannels formed between a glass slide and a patterned PDMS layer. The PDMS structures are cast from resin molds fabricated by two‐photon polymerization, enabling precise control over local channel height, see Materials and Methods for details. The system is driven by two ultrasonic transducers glued to the glass substrate and energized at a frequency of f=24 kHz. The experiments were performed at room temperature (∼ ).

To investigate how confinement geometry modulates activity, we examined four basic microchannel profiles shown in Figure 1a such as a step‐like height discontinuity, a continuous constant height gradient, and two circular wells featuring either a shallow center or a shallow outer ring. These structures have lateral dimensions in the range of 300–400 μm and depth variations from 1 to 10 μm. DSCG maintains quasi 2D nematic ordering at these thicknesses [40], enabling the optical characterization of the local director field and the order parameter described in Ref. [36] (see Supporting Information for details).

Regions of reduced retardance (black‐to‐white colors) correspond to thinner confinement. In the step‐like structures, the retardance maps exhibit sharp transitions, whereas the gradient structure displays a smooth spatial variation. In the circular wells, the thickness changes are radially symmetric. Superimposed director fields reveal stronger distortions and higher average vorticity in thinner regions, reflecting enhanced activity, see Figure 1c.

To quantify spatial variations in activity more directly, we computed the time‐averaged angular velocity magnitude of the director field, A=⟨∣ω∣⟩Δt, over sequences filmed at 10 fps for 8 min (Figure 1c). While this is an indirect measure of activity, we demonstrated previously [36] that ⟨|ω|⟩ is consistent with the expected behavior of active nematics across different activity levels. We employ this quantity because it can be measured at high spatial resolution to characterize local activity. Activity maps, quantified by A=⟨∣ω∣⟩Δt, consistently show that shallower regions act as strong activity sources, with step‐like changes in the discontinuous profiles and smooth gradients in the sloped geometry. Activity also tends to be low near PDMS boundaries, likely due to boundary anchoring, hydrodynamic flow constraints and suppressed vibration of the microchannel walls.

We also extract 1D activity profiles from these maps by averaging along the directions where height does not change. For the step‐like and gradient channels, activity is averaged along y‐direction (Figure 1c) and plotted as a function of the x‐coordinate (Figure 2a,b). For the circular wells, activity is averaged azimuthally and plotted against radial distance from the center (Figure 2c,d). Across all geometries, the measured activity profiles closely follow the imposed height modulation, demonstrating that channel geometry provides a reliable and tunable mechanism for spatial control of activity in acoustically energized active liquid crystals.

Mechanism Behind the Activity Patterning

In our experimental system, ultrasound waves generate high frequency vibration of the glass slide and apply pressure alternating in time to the LC in the channel. Given the long acoustic bending wavelength of ∼20 mm in glass for the frequency of f=24 kHz (see Supporting Infomation for details) compared to the 0.3−0.4 mm size of the channel, the pressure gradient across the channel can be neglected and approximated as uniform in space (see Figure 2d). The emergence of active dynamics in acoustically energized lyotropic chromonic liquid crystals in thin films is driven by the generation of active extensile stress [36, 41]. This stress originates from vertical vibrations of the confining surfaces, which induce periodic compressions and expansions of the LC film. In each oscillation cycle, the distance between the top and bottom surfaces decreases; the LC undergoes vertical compression accompanied by lateral expansion, and vice versa during decompression. A schematic representation of the underlying mechanism behind activity modulation via channel thickness is shown in Figure 2e,f. Current understanding of the microscopic mechanisms translating these deformations into active stresses is inspired by the tumbling behavior of DSCG molecules under shear [42]. Vertical compression of the horizontally aligned molecular aggregates displaces them along their local orientation due to the system–s highly anisotropic viscosity [43], effectively inserting them between one another. This arrangement sets up conditions for on‐course collisions during a subsequent vertical expansion (horizontal compression) phase (see Figure 2e), if time‐reversal symmetry is broken, for example, due to thermal noise or small inertial effects. These collisions produce a local extensile stress, resulting in the observed active behavior.

Inside a flat channel, the magnitude of compression is uniform across the whole LC film, except in areas near the wall. However, in a channel with spatially modulated thickness, the relative compression varies with the channel depth. In our system, the top and bottom surfaces of the channel move up and down as a rigid body, with negligible bending over the lateral scale of several hundred microns corresponding to a typical field of view. Correspondingly, the relative decrease in the film thickness per oscillation is more pronounced in thinner regions than in thicker ones. As a result, aligned molecular aggregates in thin regions are more tightly squeezed together during each cycle, increasing the number of inter‐aggregate collisions and thereby generating larger active stresses. A quantitative dependence of the activity on the channel height is illustrated in Figure 2b (the activity is measured in terms of a time‐averaged angular velocity magnitude of the director field) for a set of acoustic field amplitudes. We observed the same qualitative behavior for other channel profiles tested, including multi‐step and saw‐tooth geometries: the local channel thickness sets the local activity. The driving frequency mainly changes the maximum reachable activity through the resonance response of a given experimental cell, without altering the overall behavior. At the same time, near the channel perimeter, oscillation amplitude is suppressed by the vertical walls, leading to a reduction in local activity in their vicinity (see Figure 1b,c), contrary to what occurs at the internal vertical walls, where the activity is enhanced (see Figures 1b,c, and 2d).

Topological Defects Under Spatially Modulated Activity

Acoustically energized active LC demonstrates the emergence of topological defects, occurring as ±1/2 disinclinations in the nematic director field. The defects serve as markers of nematic field orientational disorder and also act as dynamic agents that drive flows, mediate turbulent states, and organize large‐scale patterns in the active medium [1, 31, 44, 45, 46, 47].

The behavior of activity‐induced topological defects strongly depends on local activity and, as a result, channel thickness patterning provides a spatial control of the defects behavior. Figure 3 demonstrates distribution of topological defects and generation rate in two representative cases: step‐like modulated and continuously varied activity. In the step‐like geometry (Figure 3a–c), the thinner region, which has larger measured activity, exhibits an increase in both defect density and generation rate relative to the thicker region with lower activity. However, rather than changing abruptly at the step, the defect densities are slightly smoothed over a distance of about 30–40 μm. The smooth transition arises from the diffusive motion of the defects. By contrast, when the thickness varies continuously Figure 3d–f, both the defect density and generation rate closely follow the height gradient, rising linearly toward the thinner side of the channel. In both scenarios, the defect statistics clearly mirror the spatial patterning of activity programmed by the channel geometry. The results provide direct confirmation of theoretical predictions from continuum and numerical studies, which established that both defect density and defect pair creation rate scale approximately linearly with activity [1, 48]. Previously, we demonstrated this relationship by tuning activity with the amplitude of the acoustic wave [36]. Here, the same behavior is recovered by imposing spatial variations in activity via channel height.

Topological Charge Currents

Motivated by the results, we asked whether spatial patterning of activity could serve as a design principle for the directed topological defect transport. We addressed this question by designing a PDMS chip featuring multiple annular channels of a radius 250 μm with thickness modulated in a sawtooth pattern from 1 to 6 μm, as shown in Figure 4a. We introduced the channels with both clockwise and counterclockwise directions of the sawtooth pattern (we define the direction of the round channel as the one in which the thickness of the channel increases gradually and then decreases sharply) on each chip to negate the influence of possible external interference on topological currents.

The channel at the top (bottom) channel exhibits the It=‐39 ±14 μ/s (It=30 ±10 μ/s) flow of topological charges in CW directions. Scale bar is 100 μm. Once the channels are energized by the acoustic wave, the sawtooth patterns of the channel thickness induce spatial variations in activity (see Movie S5 and Figure 4b). Topological defects are generated in pairs in thinner, high‐activity regions. These defects subsequently propagate along the ring, including into regions of lower activity, and eventually annihilate. By tracking the motion of individual topological defects, we calculated the net flow of positive Ip and negative In charges, as well as the total topological current It=Ip−In. The flow is defined as a sum of tangential velocities of defects averaged in time over the course of the experiment (several minutes).

A nonzero net current It in 14 out of 21 rings was observed. In the remaining seven channels, the activity was too low to sustain persistent defect motion, and the measured topological current fluctuated around zero within the standard deviation. Notably, in all 14 active channels, the direction of the topological charge current follows the orientation of the channel and is opposite to the direction of the slow gradient of activity. The emergence of a net topological current is also evident from the histograms of tangential velocities of +1/2 and −1/2 defects shown in Figure 4c. Although the distributions peak at zero, corresponding to non‐motile defects, they are asymmetric, with mean velocities shifted toward positive or negative values depending on the channel orientation and the topological defect charge.

The observation of directed topological charge flow in channels with asymmetrically modulated activity profiles raises an important question: whether the emergence of net current is a consequence of activity gradients alone, or if it is influenced by the asymmetric geometry of the confinement. Similar rectification effects have been previously reported in systems of self‐propelled particles, including bacteria, where spatial asymmetry in channel walls or embedded obstacles converts chaotic motion into persistent current [49, 50, 51, 52, 53]. In recent simulations of active nematics interacting with an array of asymmetric obstacles [29], a directed motion of topological defects was also observed. However, such rectification occurs when the geometric features are comparable in size to the characteristic length scales of the system. In our experiments, the period of the sawtooth modulation ranges from 100 to 200 μm, while the height difference across each tooth is only about 5 μm. This results in a very shallow slope, making direct geometric rectification of chaotic motion unlikely on the relevant defect‐travel length scale. Moreover, as shown in Figure 4d, most topological defects do not traverse multiple teeth but are instead generated and annihilated within a single modulation period. Therefore, their motion is unlikely to be influenced by the geometric asymmetry or sharp corners (see Supporting Information for details). An additional argument against geometry‐induced rectification flows is that, at these channel thicknesses, the dynamics of the liquid crystal is effectively 2D [36].

The primary mechanism behind the emergence of topological current in our system is the asymmetric spatial patterning of activity imposed by the channel height profile, rather than direct geometric rectification by the channel shape itself. The drift of self‐propelled positive topological defects against the activity gradient has been previously reported in simulations and experiments [23, 25, 30, 31], supporting this hypothesis. However, what mechanism drives the directed motion of the non‐motile negative topological defects in the opposite direction?

To elucidate the mechanism behind the motion of non‐motile defects, we performed numerical simulations of defect dynamics in a ring with spatially patterned activity. To disentangle the contributions of 3D confinement from those of activity patterning, we constructed a minimal 1D phenomenological model. Rather than solving the full hydrodynamic equations of active nematics, we treat topological defects as quasi‐particles that can emerge and annihilate in pairs, move along a circular path, and interact according to the rules. The model is intentionally formulated in an effective 1D ring geometry to isolate the minimal ingredients required for the emergence of a directed topological current. Specifically: (i) defect pairs are generated at a rate proportional to the local activity above the threshold required for defects unbinding and generation of chaotic flows [36, 45, 54, 55]; (ii) annihilation occurs at a rate proportional to the local defect density; (iii) positive defects are self‐propelled, with a speed proportional to the local activity [25, 45]; and (iv) pairwise interactions between defects are incorporated via an effective mobility parameter μ, following previous theoretical models [25, 45], in which two defects separated by a distance r attract or repel each other via an overdamped force proportional to ±μ/r. To better match our experimental observations, we also include delta‐correlated Gaussian noise in the defect velocities. While this minimal model does not aim to reproduce the full dynamics of active liquid crystals quantitatively, and notably excludes hydrodynamic effects and defect advection by flow, it assesses whether the observed directional transport of topological defects can arise from the interplay between spatially modulated activity, pairwise interactions, and stochastic dynamics. The model also neglects the full nematic elasticity beyond effective pairwise interactions and any feedback of defect motion on the local activity field, and therefore should be viewed as a minimal phenomenological framework aimed at identifying qualitative mechanisms rather than providing a quantitative description. A detailed description of the model, including all parameters, is provided in the Supporting Information.

While being highly simplified, this model, nevertheless, captures the major qualitative features of the experimentally observed azimuthal defect dynamics. In the model, the activity profile is modulated as a function of the angular coordinate α according to A+B·mod(Nα,2π) (see Supporting Information for details), where N=8 for the results presented below.

Figure 5 illustrates two distinct regimes of the defect dynamics: one without defect interactions (μ=0), and another with weak non‐zero pairwise interactions (μ=0.005). In both scenarios, defect pairs are generated preferentially in the regions of high activity. In the non‐interacting regime (μ=0), only self‐propelled positive defects drift along the activity gradient, contributing to the variation in topological charge Figure 5a,b and to the net defect current, Figure 5c. The velocity histogram of the positive defects is slightly asymmetric, while the negative defects exhibit motion solely due to stochastic noise, Figure 5d. Notably, the direction of the net current in this regime matches the experimental observations. A higher rate of defect pair generation elevates the steady‐state defect density and boosts the magnitude of the total current in the system.

In the regime of weakly interacting defects (μ=0.005), the inclusion of pairwise interactions gives rise to the directed motion of negative defects in the opposite direction to the current of positive defects (Figure 5g), while also enhancing the asymmetry in the velocity distribution of the positive defects (Figure 5h). This effect may arise from attractive interactions between positive defects slowly traversing low‐activity regions and negative defects accumulated at activity nodes. According to the simulation results, the average radial velocity of negative defects and the total topological current grow with μ (see Figure S3), highlighting the role of defect interactions on the formation of a topological charge current.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we demonstrate activity patterning through the local confinement height modulation in acoustically powered lyotropic chromonic liquid crystals. This approach provides a reliable method for the spatial patterning of activity in active liquid crystals and for orchestrating topological defect dynamics. By benchmarking simple confinement geometries such as linear gradients, sharp steps, and circular rings, we show that channel height directly sets the local activity level and defect behavior. Leveraging the sensitivity of active stress generation to local channel height, we demonstrate the generation and guided transport of topological defects in geometrically patterned microfluidic circuits. The observed topological defect dynamics is captured by a minimal phenomenological agent‐based model, confirming that directed topological transport originates from geometry‐induced activity patterning rather than from direct geometrical rectification of defect motion. The main physical insight is that local channel height controls local activity, which in turn governs defect generation and directed transport. In this way, confinement‐controlled activity patterning provides a simple design principle for programming topological transport in acoustically powered liquid crystalline systems. The ability to encode non‐equilibrium functions directly into fabricated geometry introduces a new design pathway for active materials design, advancing the prospect of defect‐based transport and information processing in scalable active microfluidic circuits.

Experimental Section

Sample Preparation

We used disodium Cromoglycate (DSCG) from Sigma–Aldrich with a purity of 98% without further purification. An aqueous dispersion of DSCG at a concentration of 13 wt.% was prepared in deionized water. At this concentration, DSCG exhibited a nematic phase at room temperature. A small droplet of such LC (∼ 0.2μl) was placed on a glass slide with two transducers attached to it and then covered by a structured PDMS film.

The PDMS channels (Sylgard 184, Dow Chemical Company, USA) were developed from resin‐printed molds using a two‐photon 3D lithography (Photonic Professional GT from NanoScribe, Germany). The molds were developed and cured with a UV chamber (Curebox CB‐4051, Wicked Engineering, USA) for 30 min at 30∘C to enhance their strength and release mechanical stresses. Subsequently, the PDMS negative mold was obtained by mixing the elastomer with a curing initiator at a 10:1 ratio and pouring it onto the photo‐resin microstructures. The uncured mixture was contained within insulation tape covered with a glass slide to ensure flat side and upper PDMS boundaries, and a mold thickness of 1 mm. To speed up the PDMS curing process, we baked the sample for 2 h at 65∘C at heating and cooling rates of ∼0.7∘C/min to prevent aggressive temperature fluctuations that could crack the resin mold. After curing, the PDMS negative mold was peeled off the substrate.

The acoustic field was generated by two ultrasonic transducers (H2KLPY11000600, DigiKey) attached to a microscope glass slide, and manipulated by a function generator (Agilent 33210a) as sketched in Figure S1.

Optical Phase Retardance

To capture the local orientation of the director field and optical retardance, the LC film was illuminated with monochromatic (590 nm) circularly polarized light and captured by a digital camera equipped with a polarized image sensor (Sony IMX250MZR). The experimental setup was detailed in Ref. [36]. In brief, an array of four polarizers, oriented every 45 out of phase simultaneously, and coupled to the camera sensor, captures the degree of polarization altered by the local alignment of the LC. Using Jones calculus, considering the nematic LC as a linear phase retarder, the optical phase retardance can be written as Γ=2πΔnd/λ, with d the LC layer thickness, λ the wavelength of the monochromatic light, and Δn the birefringence value defined as Δn=ne−n0, where ne and n0 are the extraordinary and ordinary refractive indices of the DSCG mesogens, respectively. Δn≈−0.016 for the DSCG concentrations, we used in this work [56].
