Imidazole propionate ameliorates lipid metabolism in adipocytes to attenuate high-fat diet-induced obesity via PPAR signaling pathway
Authors: Caiyu Lin, Zhou Peng, Juan Du, Xiaoyu Shan, Zhongxiao Zhang, Liling Xu, Shan Huang, Jianfang Gao, Xirong Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity is a global health concern linked to metabolic disorders and gut microbiota dysbiosis, particularly under high-fat diet (HFD) conditions. This study explores the role of imidazole propionate (ImP), a histidine-derived microbial metabolite, in regulating lipid metabolism and the development of obesity.MethodsMale C57BL/6 mice were fed either a chow diet or HFD for 15 weeks, followed by plasma metabolomic analysis, which revealed significant downregulation of ImP in obese mice. Functional assays were performed using zebrafish larvae and human adipocytes, with lipid accumulation assessed via Nile Red and Oil Red O staining. Transcriptomic sequencing and KEGG pathway analysis were used to investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms.ResultsImP treatment notably reduced lipid accumulation in both zebrafish larvae and human adipocytes. RNA-seq and protein expression analyses revealed that ImP suppressed peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) pathway key components, such as FABP4, ACSL4, and CEBPα.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that ImP attenuates lipid accumulation by inhibiting the PPAR signaling pathway. As a gut microbial metabolite, ImP may offer therapeutic potential in preventing or treating HFD-induced obesity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02768-3.

Background

Obesity is primarily described by excessive fat tissue and is often accompanied by comorbidities, such as cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes (T2D), stroke, and hypertension [1–4]. As a result, finding effective treatments for obesity has become a critical health priority. The rise of the Western diet has contributed to the growing prevalence of obesity, with high-fat diets (HFD) emerging as a significant cause [5]. HFD, which typically consists of fat intake exceeding 35% of total caloric intake, leads to gut microbial changes, a condition known as dysbiosis [6]. Gut dysbiosis induced by HFD is characterized by a decline in beneficial taxa and an expansion of proinflammatory microorganisms. The gut microbiota plays a key role in regulating the metabolic activities of its human host by producing bioactive metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), lipopolysaccharides (LPS), and secondary bile acids [7, 8].

SCFAs act as signaling molecules via binding to G protein-coupled receptors, enhancing intestinal barrier integrity and suppressing adipose tissue inflammation [9]. In contrast, LPS can enter the bloodstream under HFD conditions, activate Toll-like receptor 4, and promote insulin resistance via NF-κB-mediated inflammation [10]. Secondary BAs also impact metabolic health by modulating the farnesoid and/or Takeda G protein-coupled receptor 5 signaling pathways, contributing to glucose homeostasis and energy expenditure [11].

Additionally, recent studies have highlighted the role of human gut microbial aromatic amino acids and their metabolites in regulating lipid accumulation, intestinal immune responses, and lipid absorption, all of which help prevent obesity [12]. Although studies have demonstrated that the gut microbial metabolites could reduce obesity, the specific microbial metabolites that mitigate HFD-induced obesity remain incompletely understood. Despite evidence that gut microbial metabolites can reduce obesity, the specific microbial metabolites responsible for alleviating HFD-induced obesity remain incompletely understood.

We performed a differential analysis of the plasma metabolome in mice fed a HFD compared to those on a regular chow diet. Our results revealed that histidine metabolism was the most significantly downregulated pathway among the top 25 differential metabolites. Imidazole propionate (ImP), a metabolite derived from histidine metabolism, was notably reduced in the HFD group. ImP is produced by gut microbiota and has the chemical formula C6H8N2O2 with a molecular weight of 140.14. The safe dose of ImP for zebrafish and human adipocytes was determined, and then ImP treatment was performed to explore its effects on HFD-induced obesity. We first determined the safe dose of ImP for zebrafish and human adipocytes before administering it to assess its impacts on HFD-induced obesity. This study is the first to show that ImP reduces lipid accumulation in both zebrafish and adipocytes, suggesting its capacity, as a novel therapeutic approach, to prevent obesity.

Methods

Animal model

The Animal Ethics Committee at Shanghai Tongren Hospital (Reference number A2023-079-01, dated: 08/16/2024) obtained ethical approval for all animal experiments. 4 weeks old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Shanghai JieSiJie Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (China) and maintained under controlled conditions at 20–26 °C with a 12 h light/dark cycle. They were provided with food and water ad libitum. Before the experiment, all mice were acclimated for one week on a chow diet (12.4% kcal from fat, XTI01FZ, Xietong, China). Following the acclimation period, the mice were randomly assigned to receive either the chow diet or HFD (60% fat, XTHF60, Xietong, China) for 15 weeks. Weekly body weight measurements and blood samples were collected for metabolomic profiling throughout the study.

Zebrafish rearing and treatment

Zebrafish model and treatment

This study utilized Tu strain zebrafish larvae sourced from the China Zebrafish Resource Center. Zebrafish were housed at the Model Animal Research Center, Hongqiao Institute for International Medical Sciences, Shanghai Jiao Tong University. ImP was dissolved in ultrapure water and allowed to diffuse into the water column, where the zebrafish embryos absorbed it. For each experimental group, approximately 30 embryos were collected and exposed to various ImP concentrations (0, 50, 100, 250, and 500 µmol/L) daily for 3 days. The survival rate of embryos was assessed at 3 days post-fertilization (dpf). No drug toxicity was observed at ImP concentrations up to 500 µM, so 250 µM was selected for subsequent experiments. Zebrafish larvae were randomly assigned to different experimental groups. A diet-induced obesity (DIO) model was established by feeding 4 dpf larvae a HFD prepared by diluting clotted cream (Devon Cream Company; composition: 55.0 g fat, 35.5 g saturated fat, 2.2 g sugar, 1.6 g protein, trace salt) with a 1:10 (v/v) ratio.

During 4–6 dpf and 8-15 dpf, the larvae were subjected to ImP-treatment for 6 h, along with the HFD or a control diet. Larvae were kept at 28 °C under standardized lab conditions with a 14 h light and 10 h dark cycle. The Animal Ethics Committee of Tongren Hospital, China, approved the use of animals in this study (Reference number A2023-079-01, dated: 08/16/2024).

Staining of zebrafish larvae lipid

Oil Red O staining was performed on fixed Casper zebrafish. Briefly, the Casper zebrafish was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, 3 times 1× PBS wash (5 min each), 30 min incubation in isopropanol (60%), and then stained with Oil Red O solution (0.3% Oil Red O in 60% isopropanol) for 3 h to assess its lipid accumulation. Subsequently, zebrafish were washed three times with 60% isopropanol (5 min each), switched in 1× PBS. The number of larvae showing low, medium, and high lipid accumulation was counted for each experimental group (n = 50–59 per group), as described in Karla Misselbeck et al. [13].

For Nile Red staining, 0.5 µg/mL dye was added to zebrafish larvae for 30 min (dark) [14]. After two washes with E3 fish water, larvae were imaged under a laser confocal microscope (Leica SP8 SR, Germany) or a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Cell culture and treatment

We obtained human preadipocytes (HPA-v, Cat.7210, ScienCell) from ScienCell Research Laboratories. Preadipocytes were cultured in PAM (ScienCell, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS at 37 °C and 5% CO₂ until confluence was achieved. Differentiation was induced by adding a cocktail of 0.5 µM insulin, 0.5 mM 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine, 1 µM rosiglitazone, and 1 µM dexamethasone to DMEM/F12 medium (Sigma, USA) for four days. After that, the cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 medium with 0.5 µM insulin for an additional 4 days. During the differentiation process, experimental cells were treated with ImP dissolved in ultrapure water, while control cells received only ultrapure water. The culture medium, supplemented with 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, was refreshed every 2 days.

Cell viability assay

The safe concentration of ImP for adipocytes was determined using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, China). Preadipocytes were seeded at a density of 3.0 × 10³ cells in 100 µL of medium in a 96-well plate and incubated overnight. The cells were then applied with ImP at 0, 1, 10, 100 M, or 1000 µM for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. After the designated treatment period, CCK-8 (10 µL) was put into each well for incubation (5% CO₂, 37 °C), and 450 nm-absorbance was measured via a microplate reader (Tecan, China).

Oil red O staining and TG measurement

Adipocytes were fixed 4% paraformaldehyde fixation, and then underwent twice-washing in double-distilled water to remove any residual fixative. The cells were subsequently stained with a 3:2 mixture of Oil Red O solution (ScienCell, USA) and water for 0.5 h. Following staining, cells were rinsed five times with distilled water and incubated in PBS for imaging using a fluorescence microscope. For lipid accumulation quantification, the stained lipids were extracted by adding 100% isopropanol and centrifuging at 10,000 rpm for 3 min at room temperature. The OD520 of the supernatant was recorded on a microplate reader (Tecan, China).

To measure intracellular triglyceride (TG) levels, a commercial assay kit (Applygen, China) was used. Followed by twice PBS washes, the cell lysis buffer was added to adipocytes for 10 min, then centrifuged (2,000 rpm, 5 min) to remove the lipid layer. TG levels were quantified from the diluted supernatant following the kit’s protocol.

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was obtained from cultured samples using RNAeasy™ isolation reagent (Vazyme, China) and quantified with a Nanodrop 2000 UV–Visible spectrophotometer. The first-strand cDNA reverse transcribed from RNA (1 µg) works as a template. For qPCR with SYBR™ at conditions: 95 °C for 1 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 1 min. Data were determined by 2−ΔΔCt, with PPIA RNA expression as the internal control. See Table 1 for primers.

Western blot assay

Adipocytes were lysed in RIPA buffer with 50X protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, China). Protein was quantified using a BCA Protein Assay Kit. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, followed by transfer to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) and blocked for 25 min in QuickBlock™ blocking buffer (Beyotime, China). Next, they were overnight applied with primary antibodies: PPARγ (Abcam, ab272718, 1:1000, rabbit monoclonal), FABP4 (Abcam, ab92501, 1:1000, rabbit polyclonal), ACSL4 (Abcam, ab155282, 1:20000, rabbit monoclonal), CEBPα (Abcam, ab40764, 1:1000, rabbit polyclonal), AMPKα (CST, 2532S, 1:1000, rabbit monoclonal), P-AMPKα (CST, 50081S, 1:1000, rabbit monoclonal), P-4EBP1 Thr37) (Proteintech, 81812-4-RR, 1:2000, rabbit monoclonal), and β-actin (Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig, 1:20000, mouse polyclonal). Subsequently, membranes were washed with TBST buffer and incubated with secondary antibodies. After additional washing with TBST, the membranes were developed using ECL (Tanon, China) and quantified with ImageJ software.

Untargeted UPLC–LC/MS analysis

Metabolomic profiling was performed using four UPLC-LC/MS methods. For sample preparation, methanol extraction (1:4 ratio) was followed by vortexing for 3 min, centrifugation for 10 min (4,000 × g, 20 °C), and nitrogen drying of 100 µL supernatant aliquots before reconstitution. The method parameters were as follows: (1) RP-Positive mode: BEH C18 column, mobile phase: water/methanol with 0.05% perfluoropentanoic acid (PFPA) and 0.1% formic acid (FA), ESI+; (2) RP-Negative mode: BEH C18 column, mobile phase: water/methanol with 6.5 mM NH₄HCO₃, pH = 8, ESI−; (3) RP-Positive (Modified) mode: BEH C18 column, mobile phase: water vs. methanol/acetonitrile/water with 0.05% PFPA and 0.01% FA, ESI+; (4) HILIC-Negative mode: BEH Amide column (2.1 × 150 mm), mobile phase: water/acetonitrile with 10 mM ammonium formate, ESI−. Metabolomic data were analyzed by Calibra Lab (China).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistics were analyzed using SPSS 16.0 and GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Comparative data were collected through the students’ t-test. P <.05 means statistical significance.

Results

Long-term HFD feeding induces extensive metabolic reprogramming

To investigate diet-induced metabolic alterations associated with obesity, we first established a mouse model of obesity by feeding a HFD. Mice (4 weeks old) were fed for 15 weeks with either Chow or HFD, and their body weight was measured weekly (Fig. 1A). HFD-fed mice gained approximately 30% more weight than those on the Chow diet (Fig. 1B). Additionally, HFD mice showed increased lipid accumulation in both epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) and inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT) compared to the Chow-fed group (Fig. 1C). To assess metabolic changes in these mice, we conducted metabolomic profiling on plasma samples using UPLC–LC/MS analysis. Orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) of differential metabolites revealed significant differences between the Chow and HFD groups. Notably, lipid-related metabolites such as N-palmitoyl-sphingadienine, sphingomyelin, and 1-stearoyl-2-arachidonoyl-GPI were identified (Fig. 1D). The above analyses selected variables with a variable VIP score > 1.5 and a P <.05 as biomarker candidates, obtaining 446 upregulated and 46 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 1E). Further analysis revealed that lipids and amino acids were the most prevalent classes among the differential metabolites, representing 43.37% and 26.77%, respectively (Fig. 1F). HFD significantly disrupts histidine metabolism

To identify potentially beneficial metabolites, we focused on the downregulated metabolites in the HFD group. KEGG pathway analysis revealed that HFD feeding predominantly affected 25 metabolic pathways, including histidine metabolism, glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism, as well as valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis (Fig. 2A). Previous studies have highlighted the protective role of histidine metabolism in the gut microbiota, especially in obesity and obesity-related metabolic disorders [15, 16]. Consequently, we performed a differential analysis of metabolites in the histidine metabolic pathway. We observed several downregulated metabolites in HFD, with ImP ranking first based on the fold-change (FC) value (Fig. 2B). The chemical formula of ImP is C6H8N2O2 (Fig. 2C). These results suggest that ImP, a downstream metabolite of histidine metabolism, was present at lower concentrations in HFD mice. We subsequently investigated the effects of ImP in zebrafish larvae and human adipocytes.

ImP ameliorates lipid accumulation in zebrafish larvae

To examine how ImP affects HFD-caused obesity, we first established a DIO zebrafish model by feeding high-fat cream to 4 dpf larvae. The cytotoxicity of ImP was evaluated by treating zebrafish embryos with various concentrations (0, 50, 100, 250, and 500 µM). No significant toxicity was observed at any concentration up to 500 µM (Fig. 3A, B). Based on these findings, we selected 250 µM ImP for subsequent experiments. Next, ImP (250 µM) was applied to zebrafish larvae for 6 h, and then either an HFD or control diet from 4 to 6 dpf (Fig. 3C), which revealed that ImP treatment significantly reduced lipid deposition in HFD-fed larvae. Quantitative analysis confirmed these results (Fig. 3E). Similarly, Oil Red O staining performed at 7 dpf demonstrated that ImP treatment notably decreased lipid accumulation (Fig. 3F and G). Furthermore, zebrafish larvae were exposed to HFD and ImP therapy from 8 to 15 dpf, with lipid accumulation evaluated at 15 dpf. ImP intervention significantly alleviated diet-induced lipid deposition, further supporting its role in modulating lipid metabolism in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 1). These findings collectively suggest that ImP mitigates HFD-induced lipid accumulation in zebrafish larvae.

ImP inhibits lipid accumulation in human adipocytes

While this study demonstrated the health benefits of ImP in zebrafish larvae, we further explored its effects by culturing adipocytes in vitro. To assess the impact of ImP on cell viability, we exposed preadipocytes to increasing concentrations of ImP (0, 1, 10, 100, and 1000 µM) for 24, 48, and 72 h, using a CCK8 assay (Fig. 4A). At 100 µM, no significant changes in cell viability were observed. However, treatment with 1000 µM ImP resulted in a marked decrease in cell viability at 48 h. Consequently, we selected 100 µM ImP for subsequent experiments. To investigate the possible anti-adipogenesis of ImP, treatment was carried out in the process from preadipocytes into mature adipocytes. Oil Red O staining revealed that ImP-treated adipocytes were smaller than those in the control, with a substantial reduction in lipid droplet accumulation (Fig. 4B). Quantitative analysis of Oil Red O staining confirmed that ImP treatment significantly inhibited adipocyte lipid accumulation compared to the control group (Fig. 4C). Consistent with this, intracellular TG content was significantly reduced following ImP treatment (Fig. 4D). Further investigation through qPCR showed a significant downregulation of key transcription factors involved in adipocyte differentiation and the downstream genes they activate in ImP-treated cells (Fig. 4E). Together, these results suggest that ImP not only inhibits lipid accumulation in zebrafish larvae but also exerts similar effects on adipocytes, suppressing adipogenesis and lipid accumulation in vitro.

ImP ameliorates lipid accumulation via the PPAR signaling pathway

To investigate the underlying mechanism, we performed RNA sequencing to analyze adipocyte mRNA expression in the ImP and control groups. 446 DEGs were observed in mature adipocytes, 152 up- and 294 downregulated in the ImP group (Fig. 5A). Heatmap results further confirmed these findings (Fig. 5B). KEGG pathway result revealed significant enrichment of the PPAR pathway (Fig. 5C). A closer examination of the DEGs within the PPAR signaling pathway showed that genes were sorted based on correlation P-values. The top 10 DEGs were represented in a hierarchical clustering heatmap (Fig. 5D, E). Among these, the expression levels of ME1, CPT1B, ACADL, ACSL4, FABP7, APOC3, UCP1, and HMGCS2 were all significantly reduced in the ImP-treated group, while APOA1 and SCD5 were increased. We also assessed protein levels in the ImP-treated group and observed a significant reduction in the expression of key proteins (Fig. 6A, B). To exclude potential off-target of ImP, we examined the phosphorylation of AMPK and 4EBP1 in adipocytes (Supplementary Fig. 2). No significant changes were observed, indicating that the metabolic improvements induced by ImP are likely independent of AMPK or mTORC1 pathways. These results suggest that ImP reduces lipid accumulation through modulation of the PPAR signaling pathway.

Discussion

Accumulating evidence suggests that HFD-induced changes in gut microbiota disrupt metabolic balance, potentially contributing to obesity development. In this study, we investigated how HFD alters the metabolome in obese mice and identified specific impaired metabolic pathways. Among the 25 pathways, histidine metabolism was the most significantly affected. Given the increasing recognition of interactions between the host metabolism and gut microbiota, we observed a marked decrease in ImP, a downstream product of histidine metabolism, in HFD compared to the chow group. Our data showed that ImP reduced lipid accumulation in zebrafish models and demonstrated strong efficacy in treating adipocyte obesity. Additionally, RNA sequencing of DEGs in the HFD group revealed that ImP mitigates lipid accumulation during adipose differentiation by downregulating the PPAR signaling pathway.

Histidine metabolism has been verified in gut microbiota-host interactions associated with metabolic disorders like obesity and diabetes [15, 17]. Histidine, an essential amino acid, exhibits proton-buffering capacity, metal ion-chelating ability, and antioxidant activity, owing to the unique chemical properties of its imidazole ring. These features suggest its potential as a therapeutic nutritional supplement in treating metabolic syndrome, cardiac surgery patients, and immune-related disorders [18]. Dietary histidine intake has been inversely linked to metabolic syndrome [19]. In HFD-induced obese rats, histidine supplementation reduced adipose tissue inflammation and improved metabolic parameters [20]. Similarly, a clinical study demonstrated that histidine enhances insulin resistance and reduces body mass index (BMI) by suppressing systemic inflammation in obese women with metabolic syndrome [21].

Meanwhile, histidine supplementation improved diet-induced-only fatty liver and decreased de novo lipogenesis by promoting the transcripts of genes related to mitochondrial function and fatty acid transport and upregulating the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 [15, 22]. Histamine, produced in response to immunological stimuli, acts on the hypothalamus to suppress appetite and promote adipocyte lipolysis by activating the H1 receptor [22]. Additionally, carnosine, synthesized from histidine and β-alanine, reduces cholesterol and triglyceride levels in the cardiac and hepatic tissues of mice [23]. Our preliminary data also reveal significant downregulation of histidine metabolism in HFD-induced obesity, which aligns with previous findings. Notably, another metabolite of histidine, ImP, was found to be closely linked to HFD-induced obesity.

ImP is a metabolite produced by gut microbiota through histidine metabolism, rather than being synthesized by the human body or directly ingested. Key enzymes converting histidine into ImP include histidine ammonia-lyase (HAL) and urocanate reductase (UrdA) [24]. This conversion mainly occurs in the gut, where HAL transforms intestinal histidine into urocanate, which is then catalyzed into ImP by UrdA. While ImP is a histidine derivative, its levels are regulated mainly by enzymatic activity and gut microbiota composition [25]. UrdA, in particular, exhibits optimal activity at neutral pH [26]. ImP was initially identified as a microbial-derived metabolite that impairs insulin signaling by directly inhibiting the insulin receptor substrate-PI3K-AKT pathway [27]. However, its role in anti-adipogenic effects remains unclear. ImP has also been shown to reduce mitochondrial reactive oxygen species and mTORC2 activity to alleviate atopic dermatitis-like skin lesions [28]. Yet, it inhibits wound healing in T2D by targeting S1P transport mediated by SPNS2 [29]. In contrast, our study demonstrates that ImP inhibits lipid accumulation in adipocytes and zebrafish larvae. These findings challenge previous reports linking higher circulating ImP levels with increased BMI in vivo [30] and showing elevated ImP levels in response to high-fat diets [31]. One proposed mechanism is that ImP promotes adipocyte differentiation by suppressing AMPK (Thr172) phosphorylation [32]. This discrepancy may stem from differences in study populations, as prior research primarily focused on T2D patients, whereas our study investigated obesity models. Additionally, RNA sequencing and KEGG pathway analysis revealed that ImP mitigates lipid accumulation through the PPAR signaling pathway.

The PPAR pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating metabolism. PPARs include PPAR-α, PPAR-β, and PPAR-γ, each with distinct tissue distribution patterns and regulatory mechanisms. These receptors are integral to developing chronic diseases, modulating key targets, transcription factors, and other signaling pathways. Upon activation, PPARs heterodimerize with the retinoid X receptor and bind to peroxisome proliferator-responsve elements within the promoter areas in target genes, thus regulating transcription of target genes [33]. PPAR-α is predominantly expressed in the liver and muscle, regulating fatty acid oxidation [34]. PPAR-β is widely expressed across tissues and affects energy expenditure and insulin sensitivity. PPAR-γ has an extensive expression in adipose and critically regulates lipid accumulation and lipogenesis [35]. Gut microbiota-derived butyrate has been shown to suppress inflammation in visceral adipose tissue (VAT) and promote the expansion of VAT ST2+ Treg cells by activating PPARγ [36]. Additionally, PPARs regulate the expression of metabolic genes through histone modifications, including acetylation, deacetylation, and methylation. For instance, PPARγ recruits histone acetyltransferases such as p300/CBP to establish H3K27ac marks at adipogenic loci (e.g., FABP4, ADIPOQ), facilitating chromatin relaxation and transcriptional activation [37]. It has also emerged that PPARγ antagonizes Polycomb-mediated gene silencing by reducing H3K27me3 deposition at adipogenic loci (CEBPα) via EZH2 inhibition [38]. This study identified that ImP inhibits lipid accumulation by downregulating PPARγ and its downstream target proteins within the PPAR signaling pathway. This finding suggests a novel mechanism through which ImP may regulate metabolic processes.

Study strengths and limitations

This study demonstrated that ImP, a gut microbiota-derived histidine metabolite, inhibits lipid accumulation, with the PPAR signaling pathway acting as a key downstream effector. ImP emerged as a promising therapeutic agent for HFD-induced obesity, likely exerting its effects through modulation of the PPAR pathway. However, there are limitations. While we showed that ImP suppresses lipid accumulation by targeting PPARs in adipocytes, the full range of downstream effectors in the PPAR pathway remains unexplored. Future studies will clarify how ImP influences adipose differentiation via the PPAR signaling pathway.

Conclusions

ImP, a downstream metabolite of histidine metabolism, inhibits lipid accumulation in zebrafish larvae and adipocytes by modulating the PPAR signaling pathway. ImP can be considered as a potential candidate to prevent or treat obesity.
