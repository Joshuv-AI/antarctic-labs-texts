Metabolic signature across Life's Essential 8 profiles is heterogenous and may predict cardiovascular disease and mortality
Authors: Ian M Pollack, Jiaxuan Duan, Justin Swanson, Hongtian Wang, Kevin Kip, Steven E Reis, Chris McKennan, Anum Saeed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLife's Essential 8 (LE8) is an American Heart Association framework of modifiable behaviors and factors for measuring cardiovascular health. However, the underlying metabolic signature of individual LE8 metrics is less known. The purpose of this study is to identify the metabolites associated with all 8 components of LE8 and evaluate association with adverse cardiovascular events.MethodsModified baseline LE8 metrics were collected and metabolomic profiling (Metabolon, Inc) was completed in the Heart SCORE study participants. For each LE8 component pathway enrichment was assessed using Fisher's exact test across metabolite subpathways. A metabolic age (metAge) was derived via elastic net regression using 987 plasma metabolites, predicting chronological age while adjusting for sex and race. Cox proportional hazard ratios (HR) were used to assess the association between metAge and ASCVD or major adverse events (MAE).FindingsAmongst 1,218 participants (mean age 59.1 ± 5.1 years; 67.1% women and 39.2% self-identified as Black), distinct metabolite patterns linked to different modified LE8 components were identified. Notably, favorable exercise scores had significant associations with plasmalogen metabolites. Optimal sleep health was most strongly associated with lower activity of the phosphatidylethanolamine pathway. In adults aged 65 years and older, higher accelerated metAge was associated with increased risk of ASCVD (HR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.00–1.26, two-sided p = 0.059; prespecified one-sided p = 0.030) and MAE (HR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.01–1.18, two-sided p = 0.028; prespecified one-sided p = 0.014) after multivariable adjustment, with effect estimates suggesting higher risk per one-year increase in accelerated metAge.InterpretationAll modified LE8 components show distinct metabolic patterns underscoring the biological heterogeneity of cardiovascular health behaviors. Notably, metAge was independently associated with ASCVD and MAE than many LE8 variables in older adults.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease prevention, Metabolomics, Atherosclerosis, Life's essential 8, Aging

Research in Context

Evidence before this study.

We searched PubMed and Web of Science from database inception to March 31, 2026, without restrictions using combinations of terms including “metabolite(s)”, “Life's Essential 8”, “biomarker(s)”, “cardiovascular disease”, “ASCVD”, and “metabolomics” to identify studies 1) evaluating metabolic profiles associated with cardiovascular risk factors or behaviors, 2) metabolite informed predictors of biological age, and 3) metabolic signatures associated with cardiovascular disease outcomes. Existing studies have demonstrated that circulating metabolites are associated with individual cardiovascular risk factors and that metabolic signatures may improve risk prediction beyond traditional clinical models. Emerging work has also introduced metabolite-based measures of biological aging that correlate with cardiovascular disease and mortality.

Added value of this study.

This study provides a comprehensive characterization of the metabolic signatures associated with all eight components of LE8 within a community-based cohort with long-term follow-up. By correlating individual health behaviors and clinical factors to distinct metabolic pathways, we demonstrate substantial biological heterogeneity underlying cardiovascular health in a diverse population. Additionally, we develop a measure of biological aging (metAge) that integrates. We show that metAge is independently associated with incident atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and major adverse events, even after adjustment for traditional risk factors. The prognostic value is particularly pronounced in older adults, a population in which conventional risk prediction is often less reliable.

Implications of all the available evidence.

These findings support the role of metabolomics in capturing biological processes not reflected in traditional risk models and suggest the potential for improving risk stratification, particularly in older adults. The identified metabolic pathways provide insight into mechanisms linking optimal versus poor cardiovascular health across all components of LE8. Incorporating metabolite-informed measures such as metAge could enhance precision risk assessment. Overall, this work advances understanding of the metabolic basis of cardiovascular health and has implications for improving prevention of ASCVD.

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally, necessitating implementation of early preventive strategies [1]. In 2022, the American Heart Association(AHA) introduced Life's Essential 8 (LE8) as a comprehensive framework for understanding cardiovascular health, composed of collective health behaviors and factors [2]. However, the biological pathways through which these components influence disease risk are not fully understood, limiting the ability for precision prevention.

Using biomarkers of subclinical injury in addition to traditional risk factors of cardiovascular disease is an important advance in incremental risk assessment improvement for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Recent data also show that metabolic pathway abnormalities, examined by metabolomic profiling, are associated with cardiovascular risk [[7], [8], [9]]. Circulating metabolites reflect integrated responses to lifestyle, environmental exposures, and subclinical disease processes, and they may, therefore, serve as sensitive indicators of cardiovascular vulnerability. Indeed, there is a growing interest in “metabolic age”, a composite biomarker derived from metabolomic data that reflects one's physiologic age [10]. However, molecular heterogeneity and metabolic differences across individual components of LE8 metrics are not fully identified across heterogenous cohorts.

Leveraging the longitudinal Heart Strategies Concentrating on Risk Evaluation (Heart SCORE) longitudinal community study [8,11], we characterized the metabolic profile associated with LE8 and developed a metabolomic age metric (metAge) capturing cumulative biological stress. We further examined whether metAge was independently associated with, and predictive of, incident atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and major adverse events (MAE) beyond LE8 metrics.

Methods

Study cohort

The Heart SCORE study began in 2003 as a community-based research study of disparities in ASCVD conducted in Allegheny County, Pennsylvania [8,11]. Detailed methods of cohort recruitment and retention have been previously published [8,11]. Briefly, men and women, between ages 45-75, were recruited through community-based initiatives and the cohort underwent comprehensive demographic, anthropometric, genetic, serologic and metabolomic phenotyping at baseline visit (2003-05). The cohort has been followed up annually for CVD and other adverse events till date.

Life's essential 8 definitions

Using previously published [11,12] baseline surveys and assessments in the Heart SCORE study, we scored modified LE8 categories across the study population. LE8, as defined by the AHA consists of dietary habits, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep quality, BMI, cholesterol levels, blood glucose, and blood pressure, which collectively define cardiovascular health [2]. Each component was assessed using a standardized method, with variations in scoring based on available self-reported behaviors or clinical measurements. The modified LE8 scoring system is detailed in Supplemental Table 1.

Dietary habits, exercise habits, and sleep quality were assessed using validated self-reported questionnaires [8,11]. Diet was evaluated using two key questions from the PrimeScreen questionnaire rather than DASH or MEPA adherence as in LE8: “How many servings of dark green leafy vegetables do you consume each week?” and “How many servings of citrus fruits do you consume each week?”. Responses were categorized into five frequency groups, with higher intake contributing to higher scores. Physical activity was assessed through self-reported engagement in sweat-inducing workouts and overall activity levels, categorized into four levels from sedentary to strenuous rather than minutes of moderate or vigorous exercise as in LE8. Sleep health was quantified using a composite sleep score derived from five questions related to sleep difficulties, ranging from 0 to 25, where lower scores indicate better sleep quality [13]. Sleep quality was measured using the validated PSSQ-I questionnaire, while LE8 measures sleep quantity.

For the remaining components: nicotine exposure was categorized based on smoking history, distinguishing between never, former, and current smokers, which differs from LE8's five groups. BMI was treated as a continuous variable, with lower values indicating better health. Lipids, glucose, and blood pressure were categorized into graded risk levels, with point reductions applied for individuals receiving medication treatment [[14], [15], [16]].

Covariates

Methods for covariate assessment has been previously detailed [8]. Briefly, baseline evaluation included assessment of demographics (age, sex, race), anthropometric measurements, and LE8 metrics (smoking status, diabetes, blood pressure [BP], lipid levels, BMI, diet, physical activity, and sleep). Diabetes mellitus was defined as a fasting serum glucose level ≥126 mg/dL or non-fasting serum glucose level ≥200 mg/dL, a self-reported physician diagnosis of diabetes, or use of hypoglycaemic medication. Participants were classified as “never,” “former,” or “current” smokers from self-reported information. Trained technicians measured BP following a standard protocol. Fasting venous lipid profile was measured using standard enzymatic methods at the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center clinical laboratory.

Metabolomics

Metabolomic profiling in the Heart SCORE study was completed on plasma (987 metabolites) from 1218 participants obtained in the fasting state at the baseline visit and has been previously reported [8]. Plasma samples were run across the Metabolon Inc. (Durham, NC) Precision Metabolomics discovery platform. Log-transformed integrated metabolite intensities were used in all analyses. As we have done previously, we used the Metabolomics Standard Initiative to classify metabolites as annotated (level 1 or 2; identified or putatively annotated compounds), semi-annotated (level 3; putatively characterized compound classes), and nonannotated (level 4; unknown compounds) [8].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was a composite of ASCVD mortality and nonfatal ASCVD events, defined as nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), acute ischemic syndrome, coronary revascularization (percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass graft). Our secondary outcome was major adverse events (MAE) including non-fatal/fatal MI, revascularization, ischemic stroke and all-cause death. All events were tabulated by annual telephonic or in-person follow up, confirmed by hospital record review, and adjudicated by staff cardiologists. Mortality was ascertained and classified as ASCVD death by reviewing death certificates and hospital records [8,11]. Outcomes were analyzed until December 31, 2021 with a mean of 10.2 years (sd = 3.4 years) follow up period.

Statistical analysis

Baseline comparisons

Baseline characteristics between Black and White participants are compared using the Welch Two Sample t-test and Pearson's Chi-squared tests for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. To assess the presence of unequal variances formally, we applied Levene's test for homogeneity of variances prior to conducting the Welch t-test.

Metabolomic signature of Life's essential 8

To characterize the metabolomic profiles associated with each modified LE8 component, we regressed log-transformed metabolite levels onto each component separately, adjusting for age, sex, and race (White or Black). Participants were categorized according to whether they were in the highest or lowest quartile of each modified LE8 component. Metabolite-level significance was determined using a false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.05 across most modified LE8-like components, with the exception of sleep, for which a more lenient exploratory threshold of FDR <0.10 was applied. The relaxed criterion for sleep reflects the lower signal abundance and higher biological variability observed in sleep-related metabolites and was intended for exploratory analysis.

Following identification of metabolites associated with each modified LE8 component, pathway-level enrichment was performed as an exploratory analysis to identify broad metabolomic patterns and generate biological hypotheses. Metabolites were annotated by sub-pathway, and Fisher's exact test was used to assess whether each sub-pathway was overrepresented among metabolites associated with each modified LE8 component. Sub-pathway enrichment results were considered exploratory at FDR <0.25. These enrichment analyses were intended to provide biological context for metabolite-level associations rather than definitive mechanistic evidence.

Estimating metabolic age (metAge)

We trained a metabolite-based predictor of chronological age using elastic net regression. Log-transformed integrated metabolite intensities were used as input features. Metabolites with missing values in 50% or more of participants were removed, and remaining missing values were imputed using the mean value of each metabolite. All metabolite features were centered and scaled to have mean 0 and variance 1 before model fitting. Chronological age was modeled as the outcome, with sex and race included as unpenalized covariates and metabolite features included as penalized predictors.

Participants were randomly partitioned into training and test sets using a 75%/25% split. Model tuning was performed only in the training set. We used an elastic net mixing parameter of α = 0.5 and selected the penalty parameter λ using 5-fold cross-validation, choosing the λ that minimized cross-validated mean squared error. The 5-fold cross-validation procedure was used solely for λ selection. After λ was selected, the model was refit in the full training set, and predictive performance was summarized separately in the training and test sets (R2 = 0.84 and R2 = 0.83, respectively). No bootstrap resampling was used for model tuning or performance estimation.

After evaluating model performance, the final metAge score used in downstream association analyses was obtained by refitting the same elastic net procedure in the full cohort to maximize precision. This full-cohort refit was used to derive metAge scores for downstream analyses and did not contribute to the reported training- or test-set performance metrics. Cardiovascular outcomes were not used in model training, tuning, or selection. metAge was defined as the predicted chronological age from this model, and accelerated metAge was calculated as metAge minus chronological age, with positive values indicating an older metabolomic profile than expected based on chronological age.

Association of metAge with cardiovascular outcomes

To assess the prognostic value of metAge for ASCVD and MAE, we used a Cox proportional hazards model to regress these onto metAge while adjusting for demographic variables (chronological age, sex, race) and clinical risk factors defined by the pooled cohort equations (PCE): baseline estimated ASCVD risk, hypertension category, systolic blood pressure, smoking status, diabetes, fasting glucose, total and HDL cholesterol, body mass index (BMI), diet quality, physical activity, and sleep health [17]. Since chronological age was included in the model, the coefficient for metAge is interpretable as the log-hazards ratio corresponding to a one-year increase in accelerated metAge.

Primary hypothesis and testing

Given our a priori directional hypothesis that higher metAge increases ASCVD and MAE risk, one-sided tests were used only for the metAge coefficient in the Cox models. All other statistical tests and reported p-values (including those accompanying 95% confidence intervals in figures) are two-sided unless otherwise specified.

Results

A total of 1218 participants’ data was included in the study whose baseline characteristics are described in Table 1. The mean age of the cohort at baseline was 59.1 ± 5.1 years with 25% of the population being 65 years or older. Within the cohort, 67.1% were women and 39.2% self-identified as Black. Approximately 10% were smokers, 9% of the participants had diabetes, 43% had hypertension, and 9.5% of the participants had a sedentary lifestyle.

Discussion

In this community-based cohort of primarily White and Black individuals with longitudinal follow-up, we identified distinct metabolic profiles associated with each component of LE8, suggesting candidate biological pathways related to cardiovascular health. Furthermore, leveraging these metabolomic profiles, we developed a predictive aging measure, metAge, which demonstrated prognostic value for ASCVD and MAE among individuals aged 65 and older. To our knowledge, such an integration of metabolic correlates of all LE8-like components into a unified biomarker of biological aging in a racially balanced longitudinal cohort of Black and White participants has not been previously reported.

The exploratory metabolic signatures observed across modified LE8 components suggest biological heterogeneity and complexity beyond traditional clinical cardiovascular health measures. Many identified metabolic pathways (such shingolipids and ceramides) have previously demonstrated adverse associations with ASCVD. While the exact mechanisms are still unknown – both ceramindes and sphingolipids are postulated to alter inflammatory burden in atherosclerosis thereby contributing to ASCVD events [[18], [19], [20]]. Our results show an inverse association with sphingolipids and ceramides with optimal non-HDL-C however, a similar direction correlation with sphingolipids and no smoking habit-revealing the heterogeneity of cardiovascular health metabolites. Additionally, plasmologens have been shown to have an inverse relationship with ASCVD likely though anti-inflammatory effects [8,[21], [22], [23]]. These findings reinforce the concept that individual health behaviors and factors are linked to distinct yet overlapping metabolic pathways. Additionally, while some of the metabolites have weak effect sizes, when the results are combined, stronger effect sizes were seen. However, the pathway enrichment findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Although several enriched sub-pathways are biologically plausible and consistent with prior metabolomics literature, these analyses do not establish causal mechanisms. Rather, they provide biological context for the observed metabolite-level associations and identify candidate pathways that warrant replication and mechanistic evaluation in future studies.

Current approaches to cardiovascular risk prediction rely largely on clinical risk factors [17,24], which may not fully capture genetic, environmental, and physiologic processes underlying disease [25,26]. This limitation is particularly evident in older adults, where traditional risk frameworks perform less consistently [27]. As a result, polygenic and metabolite-informed models have emerged as complementary approaches to refine ASCVD risk estimation [28,29]. Deng et al., previously showed that a higher LE8 score and its metabolic signature may be associated with coronary heart disease in a study of low-income Black and White Americans [28]. We extend these findings by evaluating all LE8 domains and demonstrating prognostic relevance in a geographically different, biracial U.S. cohort. More specifically, whereas prior work evaluated a metabolite signature of the aggregate LE8 score, metAge was developed as a metabolite-based estimate of chronological age and then assessed for longitudinal associations with ASCVD and MAE, with the clearest associations observed among adults aged ≥65 years. While the risk stratification potential of metAge is promising and may identify high risk individuals, the clinical utility of the framework must be tested in larger and prospective cohorts.

Another notable observation in our data is the association metAge and cardiovascular outcomes which persisted beyond LE8-like components. More specifically, whereas prior work evaluated a metabolite signature of the aggregate LE8 score [28], metAge was developed as a metabolite-based estimate of chronological age and then assessed for longitudinal associations with ASCVD and MAE, with the clearest associations observed among adults aged ≥65 years. Furthermore, in sensitivity analyses excluding lifestyle variables, the association between metAge and cardiovascular outcomes was modestly strengthened in adults aged 65 or greater. This suggests that metabolic profiling captures biological variation beyond established cardiovascular health metrics used for risk assessment. While LE8 provides a comprehensive clinical framework for assessing cardiovascular health, these results hypothesize that metabolite-based measures may reflect physiologic processes not fully captures by these domains alone, especially in older adults, in whom cardiovascular risk and subclinical disease are more heterogenous [30]. Given the inherent measurement error and subjectivity of self-reported lifestyle factors, as well as their limited consistency in real-world clinical settings, metAge may capture latent biological signals integrating both behavioral and clinical influences. While the risk stratification potential of metAge is promising and may identify high risk individuals, the clinical utility of the framework must be tested in larger and prospective cohorts.

Limitations

Our study has some notable limitations which must be addressed. First, tobacco exposure, diet, sleep, and exercise were measured at baseline using available data in the Heart SCORE study as described in prior work [11,12]. Second, the sample size of the Heart SCORE cohort is modest relative to the high dimensionality of the metabolomic data and the breadth of analyses across eight modified LE8 domains. Although metabolite-level analyses were adjusted for multiple testing using FDR thresholds, the possibility of false discoveries remains, particularly for weaker signals and exploratory pathway enrichment analyses. In addition, pathway enrichment analyses used relaxed FDR thresholds, particularly for sleep-related metabolites and sub-pathway enrichment. Therefore, the identified metabolite signatures and enriched sub-pathways should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive mechanistic evidence. Replication in larger independent cohorts will be important to confirm the robustness and generalizability of these metabolic patterns. Third, metabolic profiling was performed at a single baseline timepoint, limiting the ability to assess longitudinal trends. Fourth, the cohort was derived from a single geographic region with limited racial diversity other than White and Black participants, which may limit generalizability to broader populations. Fifth, frozen plasma samples were used for analysis, which may have affected measured concentrations of metabolites and lipids. However, we used contemporary quantitative -omic techniques, which are unlikely to be altered by storage [31]. Lastly, as shown in Supplemental Fig. 1, hypertension stage 2 showed a protective predictive association with ASCVD. This finding is unexpected, but may reflect treatment effects i.e. individuals with severe hypertension are more likely to receive intensive management.

Conclusions

In this community-based cohort, distinct metabolic signatures were identified across all modified components of LE8, highlighting the biological heterogeneity underlying cardiovascular health. A metabolite-derived aging metric (metAge) independently predicted atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and major adverse events including mortality in older adults. These findings suggest metabolic signature of individuals lifestyle may yield greater risk assessment potential than the LE8 associated behavioral risk factors only. Future studies can prospectively examine the role of metAge d in cardiovascular risk assessment.
