A filtering-enhanced MIMO antenna architecture for next-generation multi-user satellite communication
Authors: Dhaou Bouchouicha, Wyssem Fathallah, Amal Al-Rasheed, Musa Safiki Adubango
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this paper, we present an innovative approach to interference mitigation in ultra-wideband (UWB) antennas through passive filtering techniques. The proposed design focuses on selective frequency rejection to ensure signal integrity across the 3–9 GHz spectrum. By implementing a dual-notch configuration, unwanted interference is effectively suppressed. Specifically, the first notch band, covering the 3.4–3.7 GHz range (WiMAX band), is achieved by embedding a complementary split-ring resonator (CSRR) within the radiating patch. The second notch band, covering the 5.8–7.2 GHz range (WLAN/Wi-Fi band), is realized using a split-ring resonator (SRR) etched in the ground plane. The design process begins with the development and optimization of a basic UWB antenna, validated through reflection coefficient and gain analyses. The CSRR cell is introduced in the radiating element to generate the first notch without degrading the wideband performance, while the SRR is strategically placed in the ground plane to establish the second notch. The resulting dual-notch configuration successfully reduces interference while maintaining strong performance across the remaining UWB bandwidth. The study is further extended to a multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) antenna system comprising two elements. To enhance overall performance, particular attention is given to minimizing mutual coupling, a critical factor for high efficiency and signal integrity in MIMO configurations. An isolating structure is integrated between the antennas, significantly reducing mutual coupling and preventing undesired signal interference. These enhancements enable the MIMO system to support multiple simultaneous data streams, making it well-suited for modern wireless technologies, including 5G, Wi-Fi 6, and other high-data-rate applications.Keywords: UWB antenna, Dual-notch filter, CSRR, SRR, Frequency rejection, MIMO, Mutual coupling, WiMAX, WLANSubject terms: Engineering, Physics

Introduction

The rapid growth of modern wireless communication systems has created a strong demand for compact, high-performance antennas capable of operating across multiple frequency bands while maintaining strong immunity to interference. In response to these requirements, filtering antennas, which integrate filtering and radiation functions into a single radiating structure, have attracted significant research attention in recent years1–3. By eliminating external RF filters, filtering antennas offer reduced system size, lower insertion loss, simplified front-end architecture, and improved overall efficiency.

Several techniques have been developed to realize filtering behavior within antenna structures. Filter synthesis–based approaches directly combine classical filter theory with antenna design, enabling accurate placement of transmission zeros and tailored frequency responses4,5. In parallel, defected microstrip structures (DMS) and defected ground structures (DGS) have been widely employed to create stopband characteristics by perturbing the surface current distribution on the feed line or ground plane6–8. These methods are particularly attractive due to their simplicity, low cost, and ease of integration into planar antenna designs.

To further enhance filtering selectivity and radiation performance, advanced configurations such as multilayer structures and substrate-integrated waveguide (SIW) technologies have been introduced9–11. These approaches provide improved bandwidth control, reduced loss, and higher quality factors, making them suitable for high-frequency and millimeter-wave applications. More recently, frequency-reconfigurable filtering antennas using active components such as PIN diodes and varactors have been reported, allowing dynamic control of operating bands and notch frequencies to support multiple wireless standards, including Bluetooth, UMTS, WiMAX, and Wi-Fi12–14.

Filtering antennas play a vital role in numerous applications, including cognitive radio systems, where dynamic spectrum access is required, as well as in dense electromagnetic environments such as WLANs, UAV-based radar platforms, and military communication systems15,16. In these scenarios, compactness, spectral selectivity, and robustness against interference are critical performance metrics.

A particularly challenging domain is ultra-wideband (UWB) antenna systems, which operate over an extremely wide frequency range and are therefore highly susceptible to interference from coexisting narrowband services17. This challenge has become increasingly severe in next-generation multi-user communication systems due to growing spectral congestion. For instance, the 5G New Radio (NR) bands n77 (3.3–4.2 GHz) and n78 (3.3–3.8 GHz) partially overlap with satellite C-band allocations, leading to potential uplink and downlink interference. Similarly, Wi-Fi 6/6E and WLAN systems, operating in the 5.15–5.925 GHz range, overlap with frequencies allocated to fixed satellite services (FSS), which can significantly degrade system performance through reduced channel capacity, increased bit error rates, and compromised quality of service18,19.

To mitigate these coexistence issues, band-notched UWB antennas have been extensively investigated. Conventional solutions, such as etched slots, parasitic elements, or resonant stubs, can generate single or multiple notch bands; however, they often suffer from limited notch controllability, increased design complexity, or degradation of radiation characteristics20,21. These limitations highlight the need for compact UWB antenna designs that provide precise and stable interference suppression without sacrificing wideband performance.

Motivated by these challenges, this paper proposes a compact dual-notch UWB antenna that integrates passive resonant filtering structures to selectively suppress interference in the 3.7–4.2 GHz band (5G NR and satellite C-band overlap) and the 5.7–6.2 GHz band (Wi-Fi 6/6E). The proposed design maintains stable impedance matching and radiation characteristics over the 3–9 GHz UWB range.

Furthermore, to meet the requirements of modern high-capacity wireless systems, the proposed antenna is extended to a multiple-input multiple-output configuration. Since mutual coupling remains a major challenge in compact MIMO systems, an embedded isolation structure is introduced to enhance port isolation and improve overall diversity performance. Consequently, the proposed dual-notch UWB MIMO antenna is well suited for next-generation wireless applications, including 5G networks, Wi-Fi 6 systems, radar, and satellite communications.

Finally, Section “Conclusion and futures work” concludes the paper and outlines future research directions.

Antenna design and optimization

Ultra-wideband (UWB) monopole antennas have been extensively investigated with diverse geometrical configurations, including rectangular, square, circular, elliptical, triangular, and hexagonal shapes. Among these, hexagonal monopole antennas are particularly notable for achieving wide impedance bandwidths, often exhibiting return losses better than 10 dB, which makes them highly suitable for UWB applications. Additionally, modifications such as rectangular slots or resistive-loaded elements have been applied to hexagonal patches or to the ground to further improve performance for many applications. Also, the analysis of surface current distributions proves to be a powerful tool for identifying the active radiating regions of the antenna and guiding the optimization process, as multiple current modes along the patch periphery contribute significantly to the broadband characteristics.

In this work, a printed regular hexagonal monopole antenna is designed on an FR4 substrate (εr = 4.4, h = 1.6 mm, tan δ ≈ 0.02) with the target lower edge frequency f1 = 3.5 GHz. Following classical antenna theory, the total effective length L and width W of the hexagonal patch are initially determined as the equations given in22.

From the calculated design parameters, the side length of the hexagonal patch was obtained as Lp = 8 mm. This gives an effective height of H = 12 mm from the ground plane edge to the top of the radiator. The equivalent cylindrical radius was determined as r = 6 mm, leading to a total diameter of Dp = 16 mm. The overall substrate size was fixed to L × W = 31 × 18 mm2, providing a compact footprint relative to the free-space wavelength at 3.5 GHz (λ₀ ≈ 85.7 mm). The microstrip feed line was designed with a width of Wf = 3 mm to achieve a characteristic impedance of 50 Ω. On the reverse side of the FR4 substrate (εr = 4.4, h = 1.6 mm, tan δ = 0.02), a rectangular ground plane of dimensions Lg × Wg = L × W = 31 × 18 mm2 was implemented, with a clearance of S = 0.5 mm from the feed point. These proportions resulted in a radiator electrically equivalent to approximately half a wavelength in length, elevated by about a quarter wavelength above the ground plane, ensuring proper impedance matching and wideband behavior, as shown in Fig. 1.

This optimal choice of Lg is critical for shaping the current distribution and controlling the effective coupling of the antenna, thereby supporting a broad and stable bandwidth while maintaining compatibility with the frequencies intended for subsequent rejection. The results, illustrated in Fig. 4, indicate that Lg = 15.5 mm achieves the best compromise, providing the widest operational band while maintaining S11 below − 10 dB. This careful optimization confirms the sensitivity of the antenna performance to subtle changes in the ground plane length and demonstrates the necessity of a detailed parametric approach to achieve the desired broadband characteristics.

To further enhance the bandwidth of the proposed antenna, we adopted a different approach by introducing a V-shaped slot in the ground plane. This slot is located at the extremities of the ground plane to perturb the current distribution and effectively increase the operating bandwidth. The dimensions of the slot were determined based on a parametric study: the width xf and height yf of the V-shaped slot were varied to evaluate their impact on the reflection coefficient and bandwidth. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the parametric study ranged xf = yf from 0.5 mm to 3.5 mm while keeping yf fixed at 3 mm. As observed from the results, increasing xf generally expands the operational bandwidth; however, a compromise is required between maintaining S11 < − 10 and achieving the desired frequency coverage. The optimal values were selected as xf = 3mm and yf = 3 mm, providing the best trade-off between return loss and bandwidth.

Finally, by etching a V-shaped slot into the ground plane, the antenna achieves the widest performance, covering 3.0 to 9.0 GHz, i.e., 6 GHz of bandwidth, which represents a fractional bandwidth of ≈ 100% around 6 GHz.

The V-slot ground further maximizes this effect, extending the bandwidth and improving impedance matching, making it highly suitable for ultra-wideband (UWB) applications. Overall, the bandwidth improves by nearly 200% from the first to the third case, clearly demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed ground plane design.

UWB antenna integrated with a filter cells

In ultra-wideband (UWB) communication systems, the antenna is typically designed to operate over a very broad frequency range, covering several gigahertz of spectrum. However, this wide coverage inevitably overlaps with other existing wireless services, which can introduce severe interference. In particular, the Wi-Fi bands at 3.4–3.7 GHz (WiMAX 2) and 5.925–7.125 GHz (WLAN/Wi-Fi) are of major concern. These bands are heavily occupied by terrestrial communication systems, which generate strong signals that may saturate the receiver or degrade the performance of UWB-based applications. For satellite communication, where the received signals are often extremely weak due to the long propagation path, such interference can be especially detrimental, leading to loss of link reliability and reduced quality of service. Therefore, it is crucial to incorporate filtering mechanisms directly into the antenna design, typically in the form of band-notched structures or embedded filter cells. By rejecting these narrow frequency bands while maintaining wideband performance elsewhere, the proposed UWB antenna ensures robust operation, minimizes external interference, and guarantees the reliability required for satellite communication applications.

Therefore, we propose to introduce a slot in one of the active regions in order to selectively disturb the current circulation at the undesired frequency. More specifically, the solution investigated consists of implementing a complementary split-ring resonator (CSRR) of square shape etched in the ground plane or in the resonator ring, characterized by a gap of width g1 = 1 mm, an outer dimension f1 = 6 mm, and a trace thickness e1 = 0.5 mm .All the dimensions of the proposed CSRR structure are provided in Fig. 10.

The effective perimeter of the CSRR is designed to resonate at 3.5 GHz, so that the resonator produces a strong local resonance which alters the impedance and generates a stop-band centered at 3.5 GHz. The expected effect is a strong concentration of currents along the edges of the CSRR at , accompanied by a clear peak in S₁₁ (rising toward 0 dB) and a strong suppression of transmission (S₂₁ − 30 dB) within the targeted band, while preserving good impedance matching and radiation performance across the remaining UWB spectrum, as illustrated in Fig. 11.

To highlight the effect of this cell, Fig. 13 compares the reflection coefficient (S11) of the antenna without and with the CSRR. It is evident that the majority of the unwanted band is effectively rejected when the CSRR is inserted, confirming its filtering capability. However, a slight transmission persists at the extreme edges of the stopband due to the relatively weak capacitive coupling of the CSRR at those frequencies. This behavior indicates that the resonant response of the cell is strongest near the center of the rejection band, where the electric field is most concentrated, and gradually decreases toward the band edges.

Another square ring resonator SRR with a narrow gap has been integrated into the proposed UWB antenna to filter the band WiFi centred at . This resonator acts as a stop-band filter, creating a strong notch that rejects the undesired frequency while preserving the remaining UWB operation. The equivalent circuit of the resonator can be modeled as a parallel LC resonant circuit, where the inductance L is associated with the current path along the ring and the capacitance C arises from the gap between the ends of the ring.

The next step, after designing an effective resonator cell capable of creating a stop-band at the WiMAX frequency, is to determine the optimal placement of this cell on the antenna. To achieve this, the surface current distribution at 6.6 GHz was simulated for the original UWB antenna. A parametric analysis of the gap g₂ was first carried out using the S11 and S12 responses (Fig. 16) in order to accurately position the stopband. The optimized value of g2 ensures that the rejection band is well centered and exhibits stable filtering characteristics. Subsequently, the effect of the gap g2 on the antenna performance was investigated.

Simulated responses of the proposed antenna with SRR: (a) S11 and (b) S12for different gap parameters g₂.

The results show that increasing g2 leads to a wider transmission bandwidth and a stronger isolation (lower S12), which reflects a higher coupling between the SRR and the radiator. To achieve a suitable compromise between a sufficiently wide passband and acceptable isolation, a value of g2 = 1.2 mm was selected. This configuration provides balanced performance, with S12 maintained below − 35 dB and S11 remaining close to 0 dB within the passband, as illustrated in Fig. 16. These parametric optimizations confirm that the combined selection of g₂ for stopband centering and g2 = 1.2 mm for transmission control allows the SRR structure to effectively reject the second band while maintaining low coupling in the desired transmission region.

Following the principle that a notch resonator should effectively interact with the antenna without excessively disturbing the feed, the resonator was positioned near this low-current zone in the center. This placement ensures that the resonator induces a strong local resonance to reject the undesired band, while avoiding excessive perturbation of the feed and the UWB passband. The position along the y-axis was further fine-tuned through a parametric study, optimizing the distance between the feed line and the resonator, which was set to 0.5 mm for optimal coupling.

Figure 17 illustrates the studied antenna with the inserted resonator cell, while Fig. 18 compares s11 before and after insertion. It is evident that, after the placement of the resonator, currents are strongly concentrated on the stop-band cell at 6.6 GHz, creating a local resonance that effectively blocks radiation in this band. The corresponding S-parameter analysis confirms this behavior, as the simulated S11 clearly shows that the WiMAX band is successfully rejected, while the remaining UWB spectrum remains virtually unaffected. This approach demonstrates both the principle and effectiveness of the stop-band cell placement in achieving targeted frequency rejection without degrading the overall antenna performance.

UWB antenna integrated with two filter cells

To realize selective band rejection while preserving the ultra-wideband (UWB) characteristics, two resonant filter cells were integrated into the final antenna: a split-ring resonator (SRR) and a complementary SRR (CSRR). As illustrated in Fig. 19, these cells are inserted respectively on the radiating patch and the ground plane to create two independent stopbands within the UWB operating range. Their purpose is to suppress the first unwanted band around 3.7–4.2 GHz and the second unwanted band around 5.7–6.2 GHz, corresponding to common interference sources such as WiMAX and WLAN. In the final design, the placement of the two filter cells remains essentially fixed: the CSRR is kept at its optimal location on the ground plane, while the SRR stays in its initial position on the patch. However, it was observed that the presence of the CSRR slightly influences the rejection band generated by the SRR. To compensate for this coupling effect, the SRR was slightly shifted (environ 2 mm) along the y-axis, without any significant change to its geometry. This minor adjustment preserves the precise position of its stopband.

Analysis of ultra-wideband MIMO system with integrated filters and isolation technique

The antenna technology has become a cornerstone for next-generation multi-user satellite communication systems, particularly when combined with ultra-wideband operation. By employing multiple transmit and receive elements, MIMO systems exploit spatial diversity and multiplexing to significantly enhance spectral efficiency, link reliability, and data throughput, which are critical for satellite networks supporting a large number of simultaneous users. In the context of UWB, the wide operating bandwidth enables the accommodation of diverse services such as high-speed broadband, Internet of Things (IoT) connectivity, and low-latency data links, while also improving frequency reuse and interference mitigation. For satellite applications, MIMO-UWB architectures allow dynamic beamforming and adaptive coverage, ensuring robust communication even in the presence of channel impairments like rain fading, polarization mismatch, or multipath effects in ground terminals. Furthermore, MIMO facilitates multi-beam payloads and multi-spot coverage, enabling satellites to serve dense user populations with high throughput and flexible resource allocation. As a result, the integration of MIMO with UWB technology provides a scalable and energy-efficient solution to meet the growing demands of next-generation satellite broadband, non-terrestrial 6G networks, and emerging multi-user applications such as real-time video streaming, machine-to-machine communication, and global IoT services.

Figure 26 shows the proposed MIMO antenna prototype, where two identical radiating elements are positioned in close proximity and individually fed to allow independent excitation. The overall dimensions of the structure are Ls2 et Ws2 (76 × 30 mm2), demonstrating a compact layout suitable for next-generation multi-user satellite communication applications.

Configuration of the proposed MIMO antenna showing two identical elements in front and back views.

As observed from the current distribution in Fig. 27, the excitation of the first antenna element induces surface currents in the adjacent second element, resulting in localized high current concentration. This phenomenon indicates the presence of mutual coupling between the two elements, which arises primarily due to electromagnetic near-field interactions and capacitive/inductive coupling between closely spaced radiators. Such coupling can affect the overall antenna performance, including impedance matching, radiation pattern distortion, and isolation levels, highlighting the importance of careful element spacing and isolation techniques in the MIMO design.

Distribution current of the MIMO system antenna at 5.5 GHz.

To enhance the isolation of the proposed UWB MIMO antenna, two parasitic structures were integrated between the two radiating patches: an inverted-U element and a closed-square ring, as illustrated in Fig. 28. Figure 29 shows the S-parameters of the proposed MIMO antenna system. It is evident that the two elements exhibit high isolation in the rejection bands, with S12 values ranging between − 25 dB and − 35 dB, whereas in the remaining frequency range, the isolation is comparatively lower, between − 7 dB and − 25 dB. This behavior can be explained by the electromagnetic coupling between closely spaced elements, which is naturally stronger outside the rejection bands. The introduction of the isolation structures, such as element parasite, effectively suppresses the mutual coupling in the targeted bands, thereby enhancing the isolation.

Proposed UWB MIMO Antenna Structure with Integrated Isolation Elements.

Comparison of S-Parameters of the Proposed UWB MIMO Antenna without and with Isolation.

The first element, the inverted-U, serves as the primary electromagnetic barrier. Its vertical length Lp1 (≈ 31.5 mm) was initially estimated from the effective wavelength at the antenna’s center frequency and then optimized through parametric analysis to generate a stable resonance across the operating band. Its horizontal width Lp2 (≈ 17.5 mm) controls the lateral spacing of the U-shaped arms and directly influences the rejection bandwidth.

To further reinforce this effect, a second parasitic structure in the form of a closed-square ring was positioned above the inverted-U. This ring behaves as a complementary resonant cell that couples to the near fields and suppresses additional coupling paths between the two antennas. Its internal width Lp3 (≈ 4 mm) and peripheral length Lp4 (≈ 2 mm), ensure a slightly higher resonant frequency and introduce an additional capacitive effect that strengthens the isolation mechanism.

Parametric optimization of the vertical placement and the dimensions Lp1, Lp2, Lp3, and Lp4 enabled the achievement of high isolation while maintaining a reflection coefficient S11 below − 6 dB over the entire UWB operating range. Simulation results confirm that this dual-parasitic configuration provides an isolation S12 ranging from − 20 to − 55 dB within the passband, while preserving stable gain and radiation patterns. This combined approach offers a compact and effective solution for extending the isolation bandwidth and suppressing mutual coupling in UWB MIMO antenna systems.

Figure 30 illustrates the simulated surface current distribution of the proposed UWB MIMO antenna at the operating frequency. A clear concentration of high-density currents (represented by the red regions) can be observed not only on the excited radiating element but also along the added isolation structures. In particular, a significant portion of the induced current is diverted toward the inverted-U parasitic strip and the closed-ring element located between the two antenna elements. This redistribution effectively reduces the direct coupling from the active antenna to its adjacent element by confining and rerouting the surface currents through the isolation network. The strong current presence on these parasitic elements validates the effectiveness of the proposed isolation technique, demonstrating that the added structures successfully suppress mutual coupling and enhance the isolation performance across the wide operating band.

Similarly, in the H-plane (φ = 90°), the measured pattern closely follows the simulated curve with only minor variations in gain magnitude that can be attributed to measurement tolerances and connector losses. The consistency observed in both planes validates the reliability of the numerical model and highlights the capability of the antenna to maintain its radiation characteristics at the target frequency of 5.5 GHz (see Fig. 34).

The low ECC translates into a high Diversity Gain (DG) of over 9.9 dB, approaching the theoretical maximum of 10 dB (Fig. 37).

The DG values are derived from the far-field–based ECC, highlighting the benefit of low correlation for improving system diversity performance. Calculations are performed for the antenna ports to assess the system’s capability to minimize interference and maximize diversity, ensuring reliable MIMO operation.

The Total Active Reflection Coefficient (TARC), evaluated using far-field radiation patterns for all ports, demonstrates excellent impedance matching (TARC < − 10 dB) across most of the operating band, with minor increases observed near the filtered rejection bands (Fig. 38). This behavior reflects intentional spectral shaping by the dual-notch resonators, which maintain high performance while selectively rejecting undesired bands.

Channel Capacity Loss (CCL), also computed from the far-field patterns, remains below 0.5 bits/s/Hz over the operational band (Fig. 39), except for slight increases in the rejection bands. These small rises are consistent with the corresponding TARC increases, demonstrating that the antenna maintains regulatory compliance while providing sharp band rejection.

The Mean Effective Gain (MEG) quantifies the average received power at each antenna element relative to the incident power in a multipath environment. Calculated from the far-field radiation patterns, Fig. 40 presents the MEG values for each port remaining below 0.5 across the entire band, confirming balanced MIMO performance and uniform radiation characteristics.

These results validate our antenna dual capabilities: maintaining near-ideal MIMO performance while offering sharp out-of-band rejection through controlled degradation in specific parameters. Also, these results confirm that the proposed design provides a tangible improvement in communication system performance, supporting its relevance for multi-user satellite communication applications.

Conclusion and futures work

This paper presented a compact dual-notch ultra-wideband (UWB) antenna and its extension to a MIMO configuration, integrating passive resonant filtering structures to effectively mitigate interference from coexisting wireless systems. The proposed design achieves selective rejection of the 3.7–4.2 GHz band associated with 5G NR and satellite C-band services, as well as the 5.7–6.2 GHz band corresponding to Wi-Fi 6/6E, while maintaining stable UWB performance over the 3–9 GHz frequency range. To address the challenges of compact MIMO systems, an embedded isolation structure was introduced to reduce mutual coupling between antenna elements. Both simulated and measured results demonstrate good agreement, confirming effective impedance matching, high radiation efficiency, enhanced isolation, and low envelope correlation coefficient. These characteristics validate the suitability of the proposed antenna for modern wireless applications requiring high data rates and robust interference suppression. The compact size, simple planar structure, and low-cost fabrication make the proposed antenna well suited for integration into next-generation wireless devices, including 5G systems, Wi-Fi 6 networks, radar, and satellite communication platforms. Future work will focus on extending the proposed concept toward tunable and reconfigurable filtering mechanisms, as well as scaling the design to larger MIMO arrays. The integration of adaptive techniques such as beamforming and machine learning–based optimization also represents a promising direction for developing intelligent and self-optimizing antenna systems for future wireless networks.
