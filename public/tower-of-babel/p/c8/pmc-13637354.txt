Differential expression of the C-Myc gene in intestinal-type and diffuse-type gastric cancer and its clinical significance
Authors: Lingzhi Lian, Xuewei Wang, Jingying Shen, Pingping Hu, Qinglu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the differential expression of the c-Myc proto-oncogene in intestinal-type gastric cancer (ITGC), diffuse-type gastric cancer (DTGC), and gastric mucosal intestinal metaplasia (IM), and to assess its clinical significance.MethodsA total of 262 archived paraffin-embedded gastric tissue specimens were obtained from Putuo Hospital, Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, collected between January 2016 and December 2024. The specimens included 30 samples of normal gastric mucosa (NM), 180 of IM (subclassified as mild [n = 60], moderate [n = 60], and severe [n = 60]), 30 of ITGC, and 22 of DTGC. C-Myc protein expression was evaluated by immunohistochemistry using the EnVision™ system and categorized as low (< 15% positive cells), moderate (15%-40%), or high (≥ 40%). Statistical analyses were performed using the Mantel-Haenszel trend test, Kruskal-Wallis test, Duncan’s multiple comparison test, and chi-square test.ResultsC-Myc expression differed significantly among gastric mucosal lesions (P < 0.001). Moderate IM exhibited higher c-Myc expression than NM, DTGC, and severe IM (P < 0.05). High c-Myc expression was more frequent in ITGC (26.67%) than in DTGC (4.55%), NM, or severe IM (P < 0.05). Among IM subtypes, types I and II IM demonstrated elevated c-Myc expression compared with NM and DTGC (P < 0.05), while type III IM exhibited lower expression levels. Patients with ITGC were significantly older than those in other cohorts (P < 0.05). Female predominance was observed in the NM group, whereas male predominance was observed in the ITGC group.ConclusionC-Myc expression shows stage-specific heterogeneity during gastric carcinogenesis. The significantly higher c-Myc expression in ITGC compared with DTGC supports the molecular basis of the Lauren classification and may aid in the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05113-9.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a malignant tumor with a high global incidence and mortality rate, characterized by a complex regulatory network involving multiple genes and signaling pathways in its development. According to the classic Lauren classification, GC is categorized into intestinal-type gastric cancer (ITGC) and diffuse-type gastric cancer (DTGC), which differ markedly in epidemiological features, pathological morphology, and molecular mechanisms [1]. ITGC occurs more frequently in older adults from high-incidence regions and typically develops through a stepwise carcinogenic sequence: chronic gastritis → atrophic gastritis → intestinal metaplasia (IM) → dysplasia. This process is closely associated with Helicobacter pylori infection and environmental risk factors. In contrast, DTGC tends to occur in younger individuals, exhibits strong invasiveness and a strong genetic predisposition, and is generally associated with a poor prognosis.

This molecular heterogeneity not only affects clinical treatment strategies but also reflects intrinsic differences in the mechanisms of oncogene activation and tumor suppressor gene inactivation between the two histological types [2, 3]. The c-Myc proto-oncogene, a key transcription factor that regulates the cell cycle, metabolism, apoptosis, and differentiation, is frequently aberrantly activated in various solid tumors. Its oncogenic effects are primarily mediated through chromosomal translocation, gene amplification, or abnormal protein overexpression resulting from dysregulation of upstream signaling pathways, ultimately promoting uncontrolled cell proliferation and resistance to apoptosis. Recent findings from single-cell transcriptomics and whole-exome sequencing analyses suggest that c-Myc may serve as a critical driver in the progression from IM to ITGC, exhibiting a trend of progressively increased expression during malignant transformation [4–6]. However, the comparative expression patterns of c-Myc across the complete spectrum from normal mucosa through IM subtypes to the two Lauren types of gastric cancer (ITGC and DTGC) have not been systematically evaluated. Moreover, existing studies have reached divergent conclusions regarding c-Myc expression in DTGC versus ITGC, and the relationship between c-Myc expression and different IM subtypes (complete vs. incomplete, small intestinal vs. colonic) remains poorly defined [2, 3, 7]. Unlike previous reports that focused on isolated comparisons between two or three groups, the present study is specifically designed to systematically characterize the c-Myc expression profile across the entire pathological continuum within a single cohort.Clarifying these differential expression patterns is essential for understanding whether c-Myc-driven oncogenesis is specific to the intestinal-type carcinogenic pathway or also relevant to the diffuse-type, which could inform subtype-specific diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Therefore, a systematic analysis of the expression pattern, regulatory mechanisms, and clinical significance of c-Myc—a key driver gene in gastric carcinogenesis—across different Lauren subtypes is essential. To bridge this gap, the present study, encompassing 262 archived specimens, is the first to comprehensively evaluate c-Myc expression not only across ITGC and DTGC, but also across the entire spectrum of IM severities (mild, moderate, severe) and IM subtypes (types I, II, and III) within a single cohort.

Materials & methods

Study subjects and reagents

A total of 262 formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) gastric mucosal specimens were retrospectively selected from the pathology archives of Putuo Hospital, Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, collected between January 2016 and December 2024, using a stratified sampling method based on the following inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria: (1) pathologically confirmed gastric mucosal specimens with adequate tissue for immunohistochemical analysis; (2) complete clinical data including age and sex; (3) no history of preoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Exclusion criteria: (1) specimens with extensive necrosis or poor tissue integrity; (2) patients with multiple primary malignancies; (3) cases with equivocal pathological classification.

This study was carried out in compliance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, and was initiated on June 1, 2025, following ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Putuo District Center Hospital, Shanghai (Putuo Hospital, Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine; Ethics Approval No.: PTEC-A-2025-8(S)-1).

The final cohort comprised 30 cases of chronic non-atrophic gastritis with minimal histological alterations, serving as the non-intestinal metaplasia/non-neoplastic gastric mucosa (NM) reference group; 180 cases of intestinal metaplasia (IM), classified by pathological severity into mild (n = 60), moderate (n = 60), and severe (n = 60) grades; 30 cases of intestinal-type gastric cancer (ITGC); and 22 cases of diffuse-type gastric cancer (DTGC).

The study population consisted of 125 males and 137 females aged 28–89 years, with a mean±standard deviation of 63.46 ± 10.73 years.

Key reagents included a ready-to-use rabbit monoclonal anti-human c-Myc antibody (clone EP121, Product No. GT220607) and the EnVision™ horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-polymer detection system (Beijing Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), an Alcian blue-periodic acid-Schiff (AB-PAS) staining kit and high-iron diamine (HID) reagents (Shanghai Suhai Medical Devices Co., Ltd.), and analytical-grade reagents including citrate buffer (pH 6.0), phosphate-buffered saline, 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen, hematoxylin, graded alcohols, xylene, and neutral mounting medium (all analytical grade).

Staining procedures

FFPE tissue sections, 4–5 μm thick, were processed according to standardized laboratory protocols.

For AB-PAS staining, deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were incubated in 1% AB solution (3% glacial acetic acid, pH 2.5) for 30–60 min, rinsed, oxidized in 0.5%-1% periodic acid for 5–10 min, and incubated in Schiff reagent in the dark for 15–30 min. The sections were then rinsed in sulfite solution, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted.

For AB-HID staining, deparaffinized/rehydrated sections were incubated in freshly prepared HID solution for 12–24 h, rinsed, counterstained with 1% AB (3% glacial acetic acid, pH 2.5) for 30–60 min, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted.

For c-Myc IHC, antigen retrieval was performed using the EnVision™ system by high-temperature incubation in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 121 °C for 2 min. The sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity, blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 min, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody, a ready-to-use rabbit monoclonal anti-human c-Myc antibody (clone EP121, Product No. GT220607; Beijing Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). This was followed by incubation with the HRP-polymer conjugate for 30 min at room temperature. DAB was used as the chromogen for 3–5 min, and the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, differentiated, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted.

Evaluation criteria

IM subtypes were classified based on mucin histochemistry combined with histomorphological features.

Type I, or complete IM, was characterized by AB-positive, PAS-negative, and HID-negative staining, with epithelium resembling small intestinal mucosa containing goblet, absorptive, and Paneth cells.

Type II, or incomplete small intestinal IM, showed AB-positive, PAS-positive, and HID-negative staining, with goblet and columnar cells secreting neutral mucins and few Paneth cells.

Type III, or incomplete colonic IM, exhibited AB-positive, PAS-negative, and HID-positive staining, with columnar cells producing sulfomucins, distorted glands, and an absence of Paneth cells.

C-Myc expression was semi-quantitatively evaluated by two independent, blinded pathologists who examined five randomly selected high-power fields (×400) per section. The percentage of c-Myc-positive cells was categorized as low (<15%), moderate (15%-40%), or high (≥ 40%).These threshold values were established based on our previously published work using the same standardized immunohistochemical protocol in gastric intestinal metaplasia specimens [8], in which the same cut-offs were applied to ensure methodological consistency across studies. The cut-offs were further optimized according to the distribution characteristics of c-Myc-positive cells observed in our pilot experiments on gastric mucosal specimens, and are consistent with semi-quantitative grading systems commonly employed in gastric cancer immunohistochemistry studies [7]. For quantitative comparisons, each level was assigned an ordinal value: low = 1, moderate = 2, high = 3. Mean scores±standard deviation were then calculated for each group and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Duncan’s multiple comparison test. This semi-quantitative approach is consistent with previously reported gastric cancer immunohistochemistry studies employing graded scoring systems [7].Any discrepancies in evaluation were resolved by joint review and consensus between the two pathologists.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (version 26.0). Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean±standard deviation or median with interquartile range and analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, Duncan’s multiple comparison test, or Mantel-Haenszel trend test. A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.Given that intestinal metaplasia is a recognized precursor of ITGC but not DTGC, linear trend analyses (Mantel-Haenszel test) were also performed separately for the biologically relevant sequences: (i) NM → mild IM → moderate IM → severe IM → ITGC, and (ii) NM → type I IM → type II IM → type III IM → ITGC.

Patient characteristics

A total of 262 patients were analyzed across the NM reference group (n = 30), IM groups subclassified by severity (mild, moderate, severe; n = 60 each) and by subtype (type I, n = 37; type II, n = 97; type III, n = 46), ITGC (n = 30), and DTGC (n = 22). Detailed demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1. Briefly, low c-Myc expression predominated in the NM (80.00%) and DTGC (81.81%) groups, whereas moderate expression was most frequent in moderate IM (45.00%). High c-Myc expression was most frequent in ITGC (26.67%) and notably low in DTGC (4.55%) (Table 2). The ITGC group had the highest median age (71.5 years), significantly exceeding all other groups (P < 0.05), while the NM group had the lowest median age (49.5 years). Sex distribution showed female predominance in the NM group and male predominance in the ITGC group; other groups were broadly balanced (Table 1).

Distribution and linear trend of c-Myc expression

c-Myc expression distribution first was compared across the broad pathological categories of NM, IM, and GC (including both ITGC and DTGC). The groups differed significantly (Pearson χ² =12.861, P = 0.012), but no linear trend was detected (Mantel-Haenszel χ² =1.703, P = 0.192). Because IM is a recognized precursor of ITGC but not DTGC, two additional trend analyses were performed after excluding DTGC. Significant group differences were observed in the NM-mild-moderate-severe IM-ITGC sequence (χ² =24.548, P = 0.002) and in the NM-type I-type II-type III IM-ITGC sequence (χ² =19.151, P = 0.014). No significant linear trend was found in either of the two biologically relevant sequences (χ² =2.683, P = 0.101; χ² =2.306, P = 0.129, respectively) (Table 2).

Differences in c-Myc expression levels

The distribution of c-Myc expression scores across all lesion groups is illustrated in Fig. 2. The Kruskal-Wallis test indicated significant overall differences among groups (P < 0.01). Duncan’s multiple comparison test revealed that moderate IM (mean score 1.88) and ITGC (1.83) had significantly higher c-Myc levels than NM (1.27), DTGC (1.23), and severe IM (1.48) (P < 0.05). Mild IM (1.67) also showed higher levels than NM and DTGC (P < 0.05). When stratified by IM subtype, type I IM (1.84), type II IM (1.70), and ITGC (1.83) exhibited significantly higher scores than NM (1.27) and DTGC (1.23), while type III IM (1.50) showed an intermediate level. No significant difference was observed between NM and DTGC. The detailed numerical data and statistical parameters are provided in Supplementary Table S1.Regarding the rates of high c-Myc expression (≥ 40%), we observed notable variation across subgroups: ITGC showed 26.67% (8/30), type I IM showed 24.32% (9/37), type II IM showed 16.50% (16/97), and type III IM showed 6.52% (3/46). Given the relatively small sample sizes in some of these subgroups, the precision of these point estimates is limited, and the corresponding 95% confidence intervals are expected to be wide. Thus, these rates should be interpreted with appropriate caution, and the differences between subgroups should be viewed as exploratory rather than definitive.

Differences in age and gender distribution

The Kruskal-Wallis test showed significant age differences among groups (P < 0.01). ITGC had the highest median age (71.5 years), which was significantly higher than that of all other groups. NM had the lowest median age (49.5 years), significantly lower than the IM subgroups and DTGC. No significant age differences were observed among the mild, moderate, and severe IM, or among type I, II, and III IM, and DTGC.

Gender: The chi-square test indicated no overall significant gender difference (P = 0.09). However, females predominated in the NM group (standardized residual, SER = 2.1), while males predominated in the ITGC group (SER = 2.2). Other groups exhibited approximately balanced gender ratios (Table 3).

Discussion

This study revealed heterogeneity in the c-Myc expression pattern during the progression of gastric mucosal lesions.To our knowledge, this is the first head-to-head comparison of c-Myc expression encompassing all key steps of the gastric carcinogenesis sequence—ranging from normal mucosa through severity-graded and subtype-classified IM to the two distinct Lauren histological types—within a single large cohort. In the normal gastric mucosa group, low c-Myc expression predominated (80.00%). As the severity of IM increased, the proportion of moderate c-Myc expression peaked in the moderate IM group (45.00%), significantly exceeding that observed in the mild and severe IM groups. This finding suggests that c-Myc may play a critical regulatory role during the intermediate stage of IM [9]. However, the proportion of high c-Myc expression in severe IM was only 6.67%, which was lower than that in moderate IM.

Several potential biological mechanisms may explain this stage-specific decline. First, during progression from moderate to severe IM, intestinal epithelial cells undergo more complete differentiation toward mature phenotypes, typically accompanied by reduced expression of proliferation-driving oncogenes as cells exit the cell cycle [10]. Second, sustained c-Myc overexpression in advanced premalignant lesions may activate tumor suppressor checkpoints—notably the p53 and p16INK4a/Rb pathways—exerting negative selective pressure against high c-Myc-expressing clones [11]. Third, epigenetic modifications (promoter hypermethylation or histone deacetylation) and microenvironmental changes (alterations in inflammatory cytokine profiles or Wnt/β-catenin signaling attenuation) may further contribute to transcriptional silencing of MYC in late-stage IM [12, 13]. The interplay among these mechanisms likely underlies the observed drop in c-Myc expression from moderate to severe IM.

The proportion of high c-Myc expression in ITGC (26.67%) was significantly higher than that in NM, DTGC, and severe IM, indicating that c-Myc overexpression may be a key driver of ITGC development [7, 14, 15]. In contrast, DTGC exhibited predominantly low c-Myc expression (81.81%), in sharp contrast to ITGC, thereby confirming fundamental molecular and mechanistic differences between these two GC types [16, 17].The mechanistic basis for this differential expression pattern warrants further elaboration. First, E-cadherin (CDH1) dysfunction is a hallmark of DTGC; germline or somatic CDH1 mutations lead to loss of cell adhesion and trigger epithelial-mesenchymal transition, shifting the malignant phenotype toward reliance on transcription factors such as Twist and Snail rather than c-Myc-driven proliferation [18, 19]. Second, although the Wnt/β-catenin pathway canonically activates c-Myc in ITGC, constitutive β-catenin stabilization in the stomach has negligible oncogenic effects and requires MYC co-activation to induce tumor formation [20], suggesting Wnt pathway activation alone is insufficient to drive DTGC pathogenesis. Third, DTGC harbors recurrent genomic alterations such as the CLDN18-ARHGAP26 gene fusion, which promotes RHOA activation and downstream FAK and YAP-TEAD signaling [21]. Collectively, these molecular distinctions—E-cadherin loss, differential Wnt dependency, and RHOA/FAK/YAP-driven oncogenesis—underpin the low c-Myc expression in DTGC and support the biological rationale for the Lauren classification.

With respect to IM subtypes, c-Myc expression in type III (incomplete colonic) IM (6.52%) was significantly lower than that in type I (complete) IM (24.32%) and type II (incomplete small intestinal) IM (16.50%). This observation differs from previous reports [22] and warrants further investigation into the correlation between c-Myc expression and IM subtype differentiation.

The differential c-Myc expression observed across IM subtypes may reflect distinct biological properties. Type I (complete) IM, which retains small intestinal differentiation with well-organized goblet, absorptive, and Paneth cells, showed the highest proportion of high c-Myc expression (24.32%). Type II (incomplete small intestinal) IM exhibited an intermediate level (16.50%), while type III (incomplete colonic) IM—which has traditionally been considered to carry the highest gastric cancer risk—unexpectedly showed the lowest high-expression rate (6.52%). This finding suggests that c-Myc overexpression may be more closely associated with the early and intermediate stages of IM progression rather than the advanced colonic-type phenotype. The stage-specific activation of c-Myc in distinct IM subtypes warrants further investigation in prospective studies with detailed molecular characterization.

Our study demonstrated a significant difference in c-Myc expression between ITGC and DTGC, consistent with previous findings [17, 23–25]. The high c-Myc expression rate in ITGC (26.67%) was markedly greater than in DTGC (4.55%), providing new evidence supporting the molecular basis of the Lauren classification. ITGC, which is often associated with Helicobacter pylori infection and environmental factors, follows a multistep progression from chronic inflammation → IM → dysplasia → carcinoma. Progressive c-Myc activation may play a pivotal role in this carcinogenic sequence [26]. Conversely, DTGC, characterized by greater invasiveness and greater genetic susceptibility, shows low c-Myc expression, indicating that its pathogenesis likely depends on alternative oncogenic mechanisms (e.g., E-cadherin mutations) or on the invasion of tumor suppressor genes [27–29].

Interestingly, across all three analytical strategies—whether analyzing broad pathological categories (NM‒IM‒GC) or restricting to the biologically relevant NM‒IM‒ITGC sequences (by severity or by subtype)—the Mantel-Haenszel test revealed no significant linear trend in c-Myc expression (P = 0.192, P = 0.101, and P = 0.129, respectively; Table 2). This consistent absence of a linear trend suggests that c-Myc regulation during gastric carcinogenesis is not a monotonic, stepwise increase but rather involves stage-specific activation, with expression peaking in moderate IM and ITGC. The finding that DTGC exhibits predominantly low c-Myc expression further underscores the fundamental molecular divergence between the two Lauren subtypes and supports the biological rationale for excluding DTGC from precancerous linear models based on the IM pathway.The cellular microenvironment and crosstalk may influence heterogeneity in signaling pathway expression across different disease stages.

The normal gastric mucosa group had the lowest median age (49.5 years), which was significantly lower than that of all IM groups and the DTGC group. In contrast, the ITGC group exhibited the highest median age (71.5 years), consistent with epidemiological evidence showing that ITGC is more prevalent in older adults [30]. This likely reflects its prolonged, multistage precancerous progression.From a mechanistic perspective, age-related changes may directly influence c-Myc regulation. Studies have demonstrated that induction of c-Myc expression by growth factors such as EGF decreases with cellular senescence, accompanied by alterations in c-Myc-specific DNA-binding proteins [31]. Additionally, age-associated epigenetic alterations may modulate MYC promoter accessibility [4]. The accumulation of genetic and epigenetic changes over decades likely creates a permissive microenvironment for c-Myc activation in the aging gastric mucosa.

No statistically significant age differences were observed among IM subgroups and DTGC (median ages 64.0–67.0 years; P>0.05), suggesting that DTGC may exhibit a distinct age distribution pattern not solely attributable to cumulative age-related effects. Regarding gender, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated that male gender was strongly associated with c-MYC positivity (OR 8.83; 95% CI 5.74–13.56; P<0.01) [32]. Mechanistically, estrogen has been shown to induce c-Myc via an upstream enhancer activated by the estrogen receptor and AP-1 [33], while androgen receptor signaling may also cross-talk with c-Myc regulation [34]. These hormonal pathways may contribute to the observed male predominance in ITGC.

Mechanistically, elevated c-Myc expression in ITGC may result from activation of the upstream Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which promotes c-Myc gene amplification or enhances protein stability [35]. In contrast, low c-Myc expression in DTGC may be related to E-cadherin deficiency, which leads to loss of cell adhesion and induction of epithelial-mesenchymal transition, thereby shifting the malignant phenotype to reliance on other transcription factors such as Twist and Snail [28, 36, 37]. Furthermore, the observed association between IM subtypes and c-Myc expression suggests that sulfated mucus secretion in type III IM may inhibit c-Myc expression by altering the cellular microenvironment [38], providing a potential new direction for studying IM-related carcinogenesis.

This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective single-center study, our findings may be subject to selection bias, and the limited subgroup sizes—particularly for DTGC (n = 22) and type I IM (n = 37)—may reduce statistical power and increase the risk of type II error. Nonetheless, the total cohort of 262 specimens represents one of the larger IHC-based studies in this field, and the significant differences observed in key comparisons (P<0.05) suggest that the main findings are robust. Future multi-center studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate our subgroup-specific findings. Second, the NM reference group comprised chronic non-atrophic gastritis rather than truly healthy mucosa. Chronic non-atrophic gastritis may involve low-grade inflammation that could elevate baseline c-Myc expression, potentially leading to underestimation of c-Myc upregulation in IM and ITGC. Conversely, the absence of a significant difference between NM and DTGC should be interpreted with this baseline in mind. Future studies incorporating truly healthy mucosa would help establish a more accurate baseline.

Beyond its biological implications, c-Myc immunohistochemistry may complement emerging multimodal risk stratification frameworks for gastric cancer. Recent studies have demonstrated the utility of integrating radiomics, digital pathology, and clinical data through artificial intelligence-based models such as the multimodal Radiomics-Clinical Integrated Risk Stratification Assessment (RSA) model for predicting recurrence and peritoneal metastasis in gastric cancer [39, 40]. As a widely available protein-level marker, c-Myc IHC could be incorporated into such multimodal frameworks—alongside imaging, histopathology, and transcriptomic signatures [41]—to enhance risk stratification, particularly for distinguishing intestinal-type from diffuse-type gastric cancer. However, this potential application remains investigational and requires prospective validation.

Additionally, future studies should incorporate quantitative PCR and Western blotting to validate c-Myc at both mRNA and protein levels, fluorescence in situ hybridization for MYC gene amplification assessment, and targeted sequencing of upstream pathways (APC, CTNNB1, TP53) to elucidate regulatory networks [23].

Conclusions

This study systematically analyzed c-Myc expression in gastric mucosal lesions using IHC. The key findings are as follows: C-Myc expression exhibited heterogeneity during the progression of gastric mucosal lesions, showing a significant increase in moderate IM and ITGC stages but remaining low in DTGC. C-Myc expression was closely associated with the Lauren classification, with a significantly higher expression rate in ITGC than in DTGC, providing new evidence for molecular and mechanistic differences between these subtypes. C-Myc expression also correlated with IM subtypes, patient age, and gender, although the specific regulatory mechanisms require further investigation. These findings reveal distinct c-Myc expression patterns between ITGC and DTGC, supporting the molecular heterogeneity underlying the Lauren classification. The differential expression across IM subtypes provides new perspectives on the biological progression of gastric precancerous lesions. c-Myc may serve as an adjunct marker for distinguishing ITGC from DTGC; however, it is important to emphasize that c-Myc has not yet been validated for independent clinical decision-making. Its potential clinical application requires further validation in larger, multi-center cohorts with standardized detection methods, inclusion of intraepithelial neoplasia and early gastric cancer specimens, and long-term prognostic follow-up data. At present, c-Myc IHC should be considered a research tool rather than a clinically actionable biomarker, and prospective studies are needed before any clinical use can be recommended.
