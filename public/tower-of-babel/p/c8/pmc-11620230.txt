Balancing growth and preservation: Unravelling Africa's carbon-economic nexus through the environmental kuznets curve
Authors: Claudien Habimana Simbi, Fengmei Yao, Jiahua Zhang, Dagmawe Tenaw, Juvens Sugira Murekezi, Mchura Tereza Magati, Hubert Hirwa, Ali Salem Al-Sakkaf, Alexis Kayiranga, Jia Peng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAfrica is vulnerable to the challenges of global climate change, which poses a significant obstacle to achieving economic development while protecting the environment. Despite numerous Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) studies, evidence remains inconclusive, especially in African countries. This study delves into the carbon-economic nexus within the EKC framework across 46 African countries from 1990 to 2020. Utilizing advanced econometric techniques such as Auto-Regressive Distributed Lag (ARDL) modeling based on the Pooled Mean Group (PMG) estimator, Common Correlated Effects Pooled Mean Group (CCE-PMG), appropriate U test, and Dumitrescu and Hurlin causality test, the research uncovers significant insights into how GDP, energy consumption, and urbanization impact CO2 emissions across different income groups. The findings strongly support the EKC hypothesis for lower-middle-income (LMI) and upper-middle- and high-income (UM&HI) countries, where economic growth initially worsens environmental degradation but eventually leads to improvements as incomes rise. However, in low-income (LI) countries, the study reveals a U-shaped relationship, suggesting that early economic growth may reduce emissions, but continued development could lead to increased environmental stress. Energy consumption is identified as a persistent driver of CO2 emissions across all income groups, while urbanization shows mixed impacts, with negative environmental consequences in LI countries and potential environmental benefits in LMI and UM&HI countries. These findings carry important policy implications for African countries as they seek to balance economic growth with environmental preservation. The research calls for targeted policies that promote green technologies, enhance energy efficiency, and implement sustainable urban planning to ensure that Africa's development trajectory is environmentally sustainable.Keywords: Africa, Climate change, CO2 emissions, Economic growth, Environmental kuznets curve (EKC), Urbanization

Introduction

Africa, the World's second-largest and most densely populated continent, is undergoing a significant transformation characterized by economic growth, urbanization, and technological advancement [[1], [2], [3]]. However, this progress is accompanied by pressing challenges related to environmental sustainability, particularly the increasing carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions driven by industrial activities, increasing energy demand, and expanding transportation needs [[4], [5], [6]].

For several reasons, Africa is a critical region in the global fight against climate change. Despite contributing less than 4 % to global CO2 emissions, the continent is disproportionately vulnerable to climate change impacts due to limited adaptive capacity, heavy reliance on climate-sensitive sectors such as agriculture and fisheries, and widespread poverty [[7], [8], [9]]. The effects of climate change in Africa are already evident, with increased frequency and intensity of droughts, floods, and heatwaves threatening food security, water resources, and livelihoods across the continent [3,10]. Moreover, Africa's rapidly growing population and urbanization rates mean that the demand for energy, infrastructure, and resources will continue to rise, potentially leading to increased emissions if not managed sustainably.

Africa's unique position in the global climate agenda presents both challenges and opportunities. The continent is rich in renewable energy resources, including solar, wind, and hydroelectric power, which, if harnessed effectively, could support sustainable development while contributing to global efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions [11]. Investments in green technologies and sustainable practices in Africa can drive a low-carbon development pathway, providing a model for other developing regions [12]. Furthermore, Africa's biodiversity and vast natural landscapes, such as the Congo Basin (the World's second-largest rainforest), play a crucial role in global carbon sequestration, making their preservation vital in the fight against climate change. Africa's climate actions have global significance, mainly as the continent is home to some of the World's fastest-growing economies and cities. How Africa balances economic development with environmental sustainability will set an important precedent for other emerging regions. Successful implementation of climate resilience and mitigation strategies in Africa could catalyze broader global climate action and foster international cooperation, particularly in achieving the goals of the Paris Agreement and the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals.

Despite these opportunities, the relationship between economic development and environmental sustainability in Africa remains complex and poorly understood. Africa's economic landscape has grown substantially, surpassing the global average of other emerging and developing countries (excluding India and China), with notable performers including Rwanda, Tanzania, Ethiopia, Ghana, Benin, and Côte d'Ivoire [13]. Projections indicate that economies such as Nigeria, South Africa, and Egypt are poised to maintain their global rankings, with significant economic prowess expected by 2050 [14,15]. However, despite these economic achievements, many African countries remain highly vulnerable to the global impacts of the climate crisis [7,8,12]. For instance, 79 African cities, which account for 48 % of the total African GDP, are among the World's 100 fastest-growing cities but are deemed to be highly vulnerable to natural disasters and climate change [5,16]. The Global Climate Risk Index for 2021 highlights the disproportionate impact of climate-related events on African countries, with several ranking among the most affected globally [10]. Despite Africa's relatively modest contribution to global CO2 emissions compared to more industrialized regions, its vulnerability to climate change is exacerbated by limited adaptive capacity and heavy reliance on natural resources [[7], [8], [9]]. Understanding the relationship between environmental degradation (CO2 emissions), economic expansion, and energy utilization in Africa is crucial for policymakers seeking to achieve sustainable development goals while preserving natural ecosystems.

The Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) theory offers a framework to analyze the relationship between economic growth and environmental quality, suggesting an inverted U-shaped pattern wherein environmental degradation worsens before improving as economies mature [17,18]. The core idea behind the EKC theory revolves around the concept of “grow first and clean up later,” where countries prioritize economic growth initially and address environmental concerns as their economies settle [[19], [20], [21]]. While numerous recent studies have examined the relationship between economic development and environmental degradation within the EKC framework [[22], [23], [24]], empirical evidence remains inconclusive, particularly within the context of African economies.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the carbon-economic nexus within the EKC framework across 46 African countries, categorized by income levels, from 1990 to 2020. We employ various econometric methods on panel data to achieve our objectives, including Auto-Regressive Distributed Lag (ARDL) modeling, which is adaptable for uncovering relationships among variables over both the short and long term, irrespective of their integration status [25,26], Pooled Mean Group (PMG) estimation to mitigate heterogeneity bias [27,28], and the Dumitrescu and Hurlin causality test to investigate potential causal connections between the variables [29]. Our objective is to provide empirical insights into the applicability of the EKC hypothesis and the implications of economic growth, energy consumption, renewable energy consumption, and urbanization on CO2 emissions within the African context. By considering Africa's unique structural, historical, political, and demographic context, we seek to contribute new insights to the literature on the dynamics of the carbon-economic nexus in Africa.

Literature review

The EKC theory suggests a non-linear relationship between per capita income and environmental decline [17,30,31]. Initially, as a country's income increases, resource-intensive economic activities lead to environmental degradation. However, beyond a certain income threshold, societies become more environmentally conscious, leading to a decline in environmental deterioration [18,32]. While comprehensive reviews of EKC literature exist [[32], [33], [34]], our focus is to critically examine the application of the EKC hypothesis in African countries, seeking to enhance our understanding of the income-environment nexus on the continent. The EKC has been extensively studied in various contexts, but the applicability of the EKC hypothesis in Africa remains underexplored [35,36]. Conflicting empirical evidence exists regarding CO2 emissions patterns in African countries, with some following the expected trajectory under the EKC framework, while others deviate due to unique economic structures, energy sources, and policy approaches [20,37,38]. The reliance on fossil fuels for energy generation exacerbates emissions in Africa, posing challenges to sustainable development and environmental preservation [39,40].

Several studies provide empirical support for the EKC hypothesis in African contexts; for instance, Ref. [20] investigated 16 West African countries from 1990 to 2018, revealing compelling evidence for the EKC hypothesis, especially in lower-middle-income and low-income countries. Similarly, ref. [41] confirmed the validity of the EKC hypothesis for ten middle-income Sub-Saharan African countries between 1971 and 2012. Ref. [42] demonstrated the EKC's validity in the context of deforestation across 45 African countries from 1990 to 2016. Ref. [43] also found a positive and nonlinear association between environmental quality and human capital, technology, and urbanization in 38 African countries from 2000 to 2018.

However, conflicting findings challenge the universality of the EKC hypothesis in Africa. Ref. [44] found no evidence supporting the EKC hypothesis in five African countries, regardless of the economic growth trajectory. Ref. [23] similarly observed a lack of support for the EKC hypothesis in Sub-Saharan Africa across six regions from 2000 to 2018. Moreover, studies by Refs. [[45], [46], [47]] revealed the absence of the EKC hypothesis in selected West African countries, further questioning its applicability. Inconsistencies in findings are often attributed to inadequate econometric methods [41,48], highlighting the need for rigorous analytical approaches. Despite a substantial body of research, a consensus on the validity of the EKC hypothesis in Africa remains elusive [41,49,50]. Scholars advocate for examining the EKC hypothesis at regional or global scales within climate change rather than solely focusing on individual countries [19,48,51]. This broader framework considers factors beyond income alone, such as energy consumption, renewable energy adoption, and urbanization, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the income-environment nexus in diverse economies.

Africa's contribution to global CO2 emissions, historically relatively limited due to lower economic growth and energy consumption than developed regions, is undergoing a notable shift [4,52,53]. Rapid urbanization, industrialization, and infrastructure development contribute to rising emissions, highlighting the continent's growing carbon footprint [54,55]. Energy access disparities, with millions lacking electricity, resulting in a reliance on inefficient and carbon-intensive energy sources, further exacerbating emissions [[56], [57], [58]]. Despite varying emissions patterns across African countries, addressing the drivers of CO2 emissions is crucial for sustainable development and climate action [38,59].

Moreover, Africa faces unique challenges, such as significant disparities in energy access, high poverty levels, susceptibility to climate change impacts, and reliance on fossil fuels for energy generation [4,60,61]. These challenges underscore the importance of effective policies, governance, and investment in clean energy to mitigate emissions while promoting sustainable development [[62], [63], [64]]. Strengthening institutions and governance frameworks are essential to achieving emission reduction targets and sustainable economic growth [50,65].

Fundamental panel econometric tests

Cross-sectional dependence test

A common issue in panel data analysis is a correlation among panel units, known as cross-sectional dependence. This correlation can arise from standard global shocks, unobserved common factors (spillover effects), spatial dependence, idiosyncratic pairwise dependence in the disturbances, and interactions within socio-economic networks [9,67]. Considering cross-sectional dependence when selecting other econometric tests is crucial because neglecting it can lead to biases, inconsistency, and inefficiency in standard panel estimators. In this study, the CD test, proposed by Refs. [68,69] was employed to assess the presence of cross-sectional dependence among the selected nations. Panel unit root test

In panel data analysis, unit root testing is essential for determining the stationarity of the investigated variables. Traditional unit root tests, such as the Im, Pesaran, and Shin (IPS) test [70], rely on the assumption of cross-sectional independence among panel units. However, this assumption may not hold in many real-world scenarios, mainly when dealing with data from countries or regions that are economically or geographically interconnected. The presence of cross-sectional dependence (CSD) can lead to biased and inconsistent estimates, making first-generation unit root tests inadequate for such datasets. Pesaran [71] introduced the cross-sectional augmented IPS (CIPS) unit root test to address this limitation. The CIPS test extends the IPS framework by incorporating cross-sectional dependence into the unit root testing procedure. This is achieved by augmenting the standard Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) regressions with the cross-sectional averages of both the dependent variable and the lagged differences. This modification allows the test to account for cross-sectional dependence while accommodating heterogeneity across panel units.

This test is particularly suited for heterogeneous panels where the presence of CSD cannot be ignored. By employing the CIPS test, this study ensures that the unit root properties of the variables are assessed more accurately, even in the presence of interdependencies among the African countries in the panel. The CIPS test has been widely adopted in empirical research, particularly in studies involving macroeconomic and financial data [[71], [72], [73]]. This study applies the CIPS test to each variable in the dataset, considering both constant and trend specifications, with appropriate lag length selection based on the general-to-specific approach. The results of the CIPS test guide the determination of the order of integration for each variable, which is essential for subsequent econometric modeling, such as the ARDL framework.

Appropriate U‒test

One potential challenge arises from the significant non-essential collinearity observed in equation (3) between GDP's linear and quadratic terms. Merely relying on the significant and negative coefficient of the quadratic term may not suffice to identify an inverted U-curve relationship. Ref. [82] highlights that this condition is unreliable and should not be considered a definitive indicator of an inverted-U-shaped relationship. To ascertain the presence of an actual inverted U shape in the modified EKC analysis, this study employed the appropriate U test, as suggested by Ref. [83] to address this issue.

Ref. [83] introduced a three-step process for assessing an inverted U-shaped relationship. Firstly, the quadratic coefficient must be statistically significant and exhibit the expected positive sign. Secondly, the slope should demonstrate sufficient steepness at both ends of the relevant data range. Lastly, the data range under consideration should encompass the inflection point. Notably, an inverted U-shaped correlation typically manifests as a negative slope at the upper limit of the data range and a positive slope at the lower limit, both of which necessitate statistical significance. Analysis of ARDL–PMG estimations

The ARDL (Autoregressive Distributed Lag) estimation provides valuable insights into the long-term relationship between CO2 emissions, GDP per capita, and other influencing factors across 46 African countries from 1990 to 2020. Using the PMG (Pooled Mean Group) estimator for this particular panel ARDL model, supported by Ref. [80] test confirming consistency in long-term coefficients across countries highlights the robustness of our analysis. The PMG estimator is renowned for its reliability and efficiency in handling heterogeneous panels, making it a suitable choice for this study. The high p-values from the Hausman test further affirm the appropriateness of the PMG estimator for this analysis.

The results derived from the PMG estimation, as presented in Table 5, illuminate the enduring relationship between economic growth and environmental impact (specifically CO2 emissions) in African countries. Key variables such as GDP per capita, energy consumption (EC), and urbanization (URB) exhibit significant coefficients, underscoring their lasting impact on CO2 emissions. For the entire sample of 46 African countries, the positive coefficient for GDP (lnGDP: 3.065, p < 0.05) combined with the negative coefficient for the squared GDP term (lnGDP2: 0.169, p < 0.01) supports the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) hypothesis. This suggests that as income levels rise, CO2 emissions initially increase, reaching a peak before declining at higher income levels (approximately $8467.58 GDP per capita in this study). These findings are consistent with previous research by Refs. [20,89], who found a significant correlation between GDP and CO2 emissions, support the EKC hypothesis in the sub-Saharan African countries. In addition, as shown in Table 5, the post-regression U test proposed by Ref. [83] strongly support the presence of an EKC in the relationship between GDP and CO2. The positive slope at the lower bound indicates that economic growth leads to increased environmental degradation at lower income levels. However, as GDP rises beyond the turning point, the negative slope at the upper bound suggests that further economic growth begins to reduce environmental degradation.

However, the results for low-income (LI) countries present a different picture. Here, the U-shaped relationship, indicated by a negative GDP coefficient (lnGDP: 3.429, p < 0.01) and a positive squared GDP coefficient (lnGDP2: 0.333, p < 0.05), suggests that initial economic growth reduces CO2 emissions, but this trend reverses as income continues to rise. This pattern may reflect the early stages of industrialization in these countries, where economic activities are often accompanied by limited environmental regulation and reliance on environmentally harmful practices, leading to increased degradation as development progresses [20,86].

In contrast, the lower-middle-income (LMI) and upper-middle- and high-income (UM&HI) countries exhibit a clear EKC pattern (Fig. 1). For LMI countries, the positive relationship between GDP and CO2 emissions (lnGDP: 4.517, p < 0.01) and the negative relationship with its square (lnGDP2: 0.268, p < 0.01) confirms that as these countries grow economically, they reach a point where further growth begins to yield environmental benefits. A similar pattern is observed in UM&HI countries, where the coefficients (lnGDP: 5.101, p < 0.05; lnGDP2: 0.226, p < 0.05) suggest that these countries have advanced to a stage where economic growth is associated with environmental improvements, likely due to higher investments in sustainable technologies and more stringent environmental regulations [32,90].

Energy consumption (lnEC) consistently shows a positive and significant relationship with CO2 emissions across all income groups, highlighting the persistent environmental cost of increased energy use. This result underscores the need for energy policies that promote efficiency and the adoption of renewable energy sources to mitigate the negative environmental impacts of economic growth in Africa [6,91,92]. Urbanization (lnURB) exhibits varied impacts on CO2 emissions depending on the income group. For the entire sample, the slightly negative but insignificant impact (lnURB: 0.153, p < 0.10) suggests that urbanization may contribute to environmental improvements in some contexts, possibly due to better infrastructure and access to cleaner technologies. However, in LI countries, urbanization has a positive and significant impact on CO2 emissions (lnURB: 0.883, p < 0.01), indicating that rapid urbanization, without adequate planning and infrastructure, can exacerbate environmental stress [93]. Conversely, in LMI and UM&HI countries, urbanization contributes positively to environmental outcomes, as indicated by the negative coefficients (lnURB: 0.379 and −0.741, respectively). This suggests these countries may benefit from more effective urban planning and better access to environmentally friendly technologies.

Regarding short-run dynamics, the error correction term (ECT) is negative and significant across all income groups, indicating a stable long-run relationship between the variables. This implies that any short-term deviations from the equilibrium are corrected over time, reinforcing the stability of the long-term EKC relationship in African countries [27]. The short-run coefficients for GDP and its square are generally insignificant, suggesting that immediate changes in income have a limited impact on CO2 emissions. This finding aligns with the notion that the effects of economic growth on the environment are more pronounced over the long run as countries implement comprehensive environmental policies and technologies [72,94]. The short-run impact of energy consumption remains positive and significant, highlighting the immediate environmental consequences of increased energy use. The impact of urbanization in the short run is mixed, with some countries experiencing environmental benefits while others face increased environmental stress. For example, in LI countries, the short-run coefficient for urbanization is negative and significant (ΔlnURB: 2.067, p < 0.05), suggesting that urbanization may lead to short-term environmental improvements through more effective land use and resource management [93]. The U test results corroborate the EKC hypothesis across different income groups, although the specific turning points and slopes vary. This variability suggests that the EKC relationship is sensitive to the level of economic development and the structural characteristics of the countries involved [95,96].

Robustness check using CCE–PMG method

The robustness check using the Common Correlated Effects Pooled Mean Group (CCE-PMG) method provides further insights into the relationship between economic growth, energy consumption, urbanization, and CO2 emissions in African countries. The CCE-PMG estimation accounts for potential cross-sectional dependence across countries, making the results more reliable in addressing the common shocks that could affect multiple countries simultaneously.

Table 6 shows that the long-run coefficients from the CCE-PMG analysis largely align with the EKC hypothesis, particularly for lower-middle-income (LMI) and upper-middle- and high-income (UM&HI) countries. Specifically, for all countries combined, the positive coefficient for GDP (lnGDP: 1.426, p < 0.05) and the negative coefficient for the squared GDP term (lnGDP2: 0.096, p < 0.05) suggest an inverted U-shaped relationship between economic growth and CO2 emissions, consistent with the EKC hypothesis. For low-income (LI) countries, the robustness check reveals a U-shaped relationship, with a negative coefficient for GDP (lnGDP: 1.879, p < 0.01) and a positive coefficient for squared GDP (lnGDP2: 0.150, p < 0.01). This result contrasts with the EKC hypothesis and suggests that CO2 emissions decrease with initial economic growth in LI countries but increase again as income continues to rise. This pattern could be due to the early stage of industrialization in these countries, where economic activities may still heavily rely on environmentally detrimental practices [89,97].

In LMI countries, the CCE-PMG results reaffirm the EKC hypothesis, with a significant positive GDP coefficient (lnGDP: 3.352, p < 0.01) and a negative squared GDP coefficient (lnGDP2: 0.229, p < 0.01). Similarly, for UM&HI countries, the positive coefficient for GDP (lnGDP: 5.196, p < 0.05) and the negative coefficient for its square (lnGDP2: 0.255, p < 0.05) indicate that these countries have reached an income level where further economic growth is associated with environmental improvements. The energy consumption (lnEC) results remain consistent across all country groups, with positive and statistically significant coefficients, highlighting the persistent role of energy consumption in contributing to environmental degradation. This consistency across both PMG and CCE-PMG results underscores the need for energy policy reforms focusing on efficiency improvements and adopting renewable energy sources to mitigate the environmental impacts of economic growth in Africa.

Urbanization (lnURB) presents a more complex picture. The CCE-PMG results indicate that urbanization is associated with increased CO2 emissions in the long run for all countries combined (lnURB: 0.696, p < 0.01) and for UM&HI countries (lnURB: 1.100, p < 0.01). These findings differ from the PMG results, where urbanization had a negative or mixed impact on CO2 emissions. The positive impact of urbanization in the CCE-PMG results suggests that rapid urbanization in African countries, particularly in more developed economies, may lead to significant environmental challenges, likely due to insufficient infrastructure, governance, and planning to support sustainable urban growth [98].

The short-run dynamics reflected in the error correction terms (ECT) remain negative and statistically significant across all country groups, similar to the PMG results. This consistency indicates a stable long-run equilibrium relationship between the variables, with any short-term deviations quickly corrected. The short-run coefficients for GDP and its square are primarily insignificant, implying that immediate income changes do not substantially impact CO2 emissions. However, the positive and significant short-run coefficients for energy consumption across all country groups highlight increased energy use's immediate negative environmental impact, further reinforcing the need for energy transition strategies in Africa [98]. The U test results continue to support the existence of the EKC in different income groups, although the turning points and slopes exhibit slight variations compared to the PMG results.

In short, the robustness check using the CCE-PMG method confirms the general findings of the EKC hypothesis for African countries, particularly for LMI and UM&HI countries. However, the positive impact of urbanization on environmental degradation observed in the CCE-PMG results, especially in UM&HI countries, suggests that rapid urbanization may still pose significant environmental challenges.

Impulse response and variance decomposition results

To further analyze how one variable responds to innovations or shocks in another variable and to validate the estimation results' consistency, this study presents the impulse response and variance decomposition findings for CO2 emissions in Africa, as outlined in Ref. [104]. Fig. 2 shows that the reaction of CO2 emissions to its shock starts high and continuously decreases over time, though it remains positive throughout. This indicates that a shock to CO2 emissions positively affects future emissions, but the impact gradually diminishes [105]. There is an immediate increase in CO2 emissions following a shock in GDP, which stabilizes at a higher level than initially. This suggests that economic growth leads to increased emissions, and the effect stabilizes without reverting, highlighting a sustained positive relationship. The response to a shock in GDP squared shows a slight initial increase, then stabilizes. This indicates the non-linear effects of economic growth on emissions, where the squared term of GDP (possibly representing acceleration in economic activities) initially boosts emissions but then plateaus, reflecting diminishing marginal effects. CO2 emissions respond positively and significantly to a shock in energy consumption, with the response increasing over time. This reflects a strong and growing impact of energy consumption on emissions, underlining the role of energy sector dynamics in environmental outcomes. Urbanization initially decreases CO2 emissions slightly, reversing into a slight increase before stabilizing. This could reflect complex dynamics where urbanization initially might lead to efficiencies and reduced emissions per capita. Still, as urbanization progresses, the aggregate effects on emissions become neutral or slightly positive due to increased economic activities and possibly inadequate urban planning. These responses suggest that GDP growth and energy consumption are critical drivers of CO2 emissions in Africa, with both showing a substantial and increasing impact over time. The effects of urbanization are less pronounced, indicating potential areas for policy intervention [106]. The impulse responses underscore the importance of sustainable growth strategies that incorporate energy efficiency, renewable energy expansion, and smart urban planning to mitigate the environmental impact of economic and demographic changes.

Concluding remarks

Conclusions

This study comprehensively analyzes the relationship between carbon-economic nexus in 46 African countries through the lens of the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) hypothesis. By employing the ARDL-PMG and CCE-PMG estimation techniques, we uncover the relationship between CO2 emissions, economic growth, energy consumption, and urbanization from 1990 to 2020. The results robustly support the EKC hypothesis for lower-middle-income (LMI) and upper-middle- and high-income (UM&HI) countries, where economic growth initially worsens environmental degradation but eventually leads to improvements as GDP per capita increases. However, the findings for low-income (LI) countries diverge, showing a U-shaped relationship, indicating that environmental degradation initially decreases but worsens as these economies grow.

Energy consumption consistently emerges as a significant driver of CO2 emissions across all income groups, underscoring the pressing need for energy policy reforms focusing on efficiency and adopting renewable energy sources. Urbanization presents a more complex impact, varying across income levels, with LI countries experiencing negative environmental consequences due to rapid urbanization, while LMI and UM&HI countries appear to manage urban growth more effectively, leading to environmental benefits.

The causality tests reveal a bidirectional relationship between GDP and CO2 emissions and energy consumption and CO2 emissions, further emphasizing the interconnection of economic activities and environmental outcomes. The impulse response and variance decomposition analyses confirm the lasting influence of economic growth and energy consumption on CO2 emissions, highlighting these factors as critical drivers of environmental change in Africa.

Policy implications and limitations

The study's findings have significant policy implications for African countries as they strive to balance economic growth with environmental sustainability. The evidence supporting the Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) hypothesis for lower-middle-income (LMI) and upper-middle- and high-income (UM&HI) countries suggests that these nations are at a stage where continued economic growth can lead to environmental improvements. This underscores the importance of policies that promote green technologies, sustainable infrastructure, and cleaner industries to decouple economic growth from carbon emissions. However, for low-income (LI) countries, where the relationship between GDP and CO2 emissions is U-shaped, early-stage environmental regulations and sustainable development planning are critical to preventing the negative impacts of industrialization and urbanization.

Energy consumption is identified as a consistent driver of CO2 emissions across all income groups, highlighting the urgent need for energy policies that focus on improving efficiency and accelerating the transition to renewable energy sources. As energy demand is expected to rise with economic development, African countries must adopt sustainable energy practices to avoid carbon-intensive growth paths. Urbanization presents mixed outcomes, with rapid urban growth in LI countries contributing to environmental stress, while in LMI and UM&HI countries, it may support positive environmental outcomes. Thus, sustainable urban planning and infrastructure development are essential to managing urbanization's environmental impact across the continent.

Despite the valuable insights provided by this study, there are limitations to consider. The analysis focuses on CO2 emissions as the primary environmental indicator, which may not capture other critical environmental issues in Africa, such as biodiversity loss and water scarcity. Data limitations and the complexity of modeling the relationships between economic growth and environmental degradation in developing countries also present challenges. Policymakers should interpret the findings with these limitations in mind, ensuring that policy responses are tailored to the diverse economic and environmental contexts across Africa, and future research should aim to address these gaps for a more comprehensive understanding of the carbon-economic nexus in the region.
