A prospective observational cohort study comparing perioperative outcomes, complications, and quality of life between surgical procedures for female stress urinary incontinence
Authors: Jiali Jiang, Tangxiaoman Wang, Qiuya Deng, Yurong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare perioperative outcomes, complications, and quality of life between tension-free vaginal tape obturator (TVT-O) and retropubic tension-free vaginal tape exact (TVT-E) procedures for female severe stress urinary incontinence (SUI).MethodsThis prospective cohort study enrolled women undergoing surgery for severe stress urinary incontinence (December 2023-February 2025). Patients were grouped by procedure (TVT-O vs. TVT-E). We compared perioperative data, complications, quality-of-life scores (ICI-Q-SF, IIQ-7, PISQ-12 at 6 and 12 months), and 12-month objective cure rates. Kaplan-Meier curves assessed cumulative recurrence.ResultsOf 120 patients enrolled (TVT-O: 66; TVT-E: 54), baseline characteristics were comparable (P > 0.05). Operative time was significantly shorter in the TVT-O group (P < 0.001), with no significant differences in blood loss, catheterization time, or hospital stay. Complication rates were similar (TVT-O: 21.21% vs. TVT-E: 18.52%, P = 0.710), including specific complications like groin pain. Both groups showed significant postoperative quality-of-life improvements (P < 0.001), but between-group differences were not significant. At 12 months, objective cure rates and cumulative recurrence-free survival were comparable between groups.ConclusionBoth TVT-O and TVT-E are safe and effective for severe SUI, significantly improving quality of life. Introduction

Stress urinary incontinence (SUI), defined as involuntary urine leakage during increased intra-abdominal pressure such as coughing, sneezing, or exercise, is the most common subtype of urinary incontinence in women and significantly impairs quality of life [1, 2]. The condition is primarily associated with dysfunction of urethral and pelvic floor support structures caused by factors including childbirth, aging, and obesity [3].

Since Ulmsten et al. first reported retropubic tension-free vaginal tape (TVT) in 1996, it has rapidly become a standard surgical treatment for SUI due to its advantages of minimally invasive, confirmed efficacy and rapid recovery [4]. To further reduce the risks of bladder, vascular, and bowel injuries associated with the TVT procedure, Delorme described a transobturator route for tape placement in 2001 [5]. Subsequently, in 2003, de Leval introduced the tension-free vaginal tapeobturator (TVT-O) procedure, an inside-out transobturator technique that avoided the retropubic space, theoretically reducing the risk of pelvic organ injury [6].

Currently, retropubic vaginal tape exact (TVT-E) and TVT-O are widely used surgical techniques for female SUI. Previous randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses have demonstrated comparable therapeutic efficacy and quality-of-life improvement between the two procedures [7–9]. However, their complication profiles differ. TVT-E is associated with a higher risk of bladder perforation and hematoma [10, 11], whereas TVT-O more commonly causes postoperative groin or thigh pain [7].

Although numerous studies have compared the efficacy and safety of TVT and TVT-O, the majority have enrolled a mixed population of patients with mild to moderate SUI, with relatively few comparative studies specifically targeting patients with severe SUI (defined as 1-hour pad test ≥ 10 g). Patients with severe stress urinary incontinence may have more severe pelvic floor dysfunction, and their perioperative outcomes and complication risks may also differ from those of patients with mild to moderate SUI. Furthermore, prospective observational studies enable more standardized data collection and reduce recall bias, thereby providing important complementary evidence to randomized controlled trials [12].

Based on this, the present study employed a single-center prospective observational cohort design to compare perioperative outcomes, complication profiles, and postoperative quality-of-life improvements between TVT-O and TVT-E procedures in the treatment of female severe SUI, aiming to provide a clinical reference for surgical decision-making in patients with severe SUI.

Methods

Research design and objects

This study was a single-center prospective observational cohort study. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committees of Yongzhou Central Hospital (Approval No. 2023120302). Consecutive patients with severe SUI who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and were scheduled to undergo surgical treatment at our hospital between December 10, 2023, and February 1, 2025, were enrolled. All patients were informed of the study details and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The patients were divided into two groups according to the surgical procedure received: the TVT-O group and the TVT-E group. The choice of surgical procedure was based on patient preference and the surgeons’ clinical recommendation; consequently, a natural disparity in sample sizes between the two groups was anticipated, reflecting the distribution of surgical techniques in real-world clinical practice. No statistically significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics between the two groups (P > 0.05), indicating that the groups were comparable (Table 1).

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) diagnosis of severe SUI according to the diagnostic criteria of the International Continence Society (ICS) [13], specifically: ①objective demonstration of involuntary urine leakage during increased intra-abdominal pressure induced by standardized cough stress testing and/or Valsalva maneuver; ②meeting the objective diagnostic criteria for severe SUI, defined as a 1-hour pad test≥10 g; ③urodynamic findings consistent with SUI, with bladder capacity > 300mL, post-void residual<100mL, absence of intrinsic sphincter deficiency, and absence of voiding dysfunction; (3) undergoing primary mid-urethral sling surgery via the vaginal route.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) predominant symptoms of urgency urinary incontinence, or a diagnosis of overactive bladder (OAB) without adequate behavioral and pharmacological management; (2) presence of pelvic organ prolapse requiring concomitant surgical correction (pelvic organ prolapse quantification [POP-Q] stage ≥ III); (3) history of previous anti-incontinence surgery, urethral surgery, or pelvic reconstructive surgery; (4) concomitant neurological disorders that could potentially affect the surgical procedure or its assessment; (5) pregnancy, lactation, or planned pregnancy within one year; (6) presence of untreated urinary tract infection or coagulation disorders preoperatively (Fig. 1).

Surgical procedures

All patients underwent vaginal irrigation with 0.5% iodophor one day prior to surgery. All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the lithotomy position. The tape devices used were the TVT-O and TVT-E systems (Tiloop, Germany). All surgeries were performed by the same team of senior surgeons.

(1) TVT-E group (n = 54): Bilateral abdominal skin exit points were marked 2 cm lateral to the midline, just above the superior border of the pubic symphysis. After inserting a Foley catheter and emptying the bladder, a hydrodissection was performed by injecting normal saline into the paraurethral space to create a fluid cushion. A 1.5 cm midline longitudinal incision was made in the anterior vaginal wall, approximately 1.0 cm from the external urethral meatus. The incision was extended bilaterally by blunt dissection towards the mid-urethra. A catheter guide was inserted into the bladder and used to deviate the bladder to the contralateral side. The needle with the attached tape was introduced through the vaginal incision and advanced behind the pubic bone, exiting through the previously marked skin incision. The procedure was repeated on the contralateral side. A cystourethroscopy was performed to confirm the absence of lower urinary tract injury before the needles were removed. The tape was adjusted to lie loosely (tension-free) beneath the mid-urethra, and the excess tape ends were cut. The vaginal incision was closed with 2 − 0 absorbable suture, and an iodophor-soaked gauze pack was placed in the vagina.

(2) TVT-O group (n = 66): A 1.5 cm midline longitudinal incision was made in the anterior vaginal wall, approximately 1.0 cm from the external urethral meatus. Blunt dissection was carried out to the posterior aspect of the mid-urethra. The needle with the attached tape was inserted through the vaginal incision and advanced laterally, following the groove of the helical wing guide, to traverse the medial aspect of the obturator foramen and the adductor muscles, exiting through the skin at the inner thigh. The procedure was repeated on the contralateral side. The tape was adjusted to lie loosely (tension-free) beneath the mid-urethra, and the excess tape ends were cut. The vaginal incision was closed with 2 − 0 absorbable suture. The skin exit points on the inner thighs were dressed, and an iodophor-soaked gauze pack was placed in the vagina.

Outcome measures and data collection

All data were prospectively collected by designated personnel at enrollment and at each follow-up time point, and were entered into an electronic database.

Primary and secondary endpoints were predefined before data analysis. The primary endpoint of this study was the objective surgical efficacy at 6 and 12 months postoperatively (classified as cure, improvement, or failure). The secondary endpoints included perioperative outcomes, intraoperative and postoperative complications, and patient-reported quality of life measures.

Baseline characteristics included age, body mass index (BMI), pregnancy history, parity, and menopausal status.

Perioperative parameters included operative time, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative hospital stay, indwelling catheterization duration, and post-void residual urine volume measured by bladder ultrasound immediately after spontaneous voiding.

Intraoperative complications included bladder injury, urethral injury, and vascular injury, among others.

Postoperative complications included voiding dysfunction, de novo urgency urinary incontinence, tape erosion, and groin/inner thigh pain, among others.

Groin/inner thigh pain was assessed using the visual analogue scale (VAS, 0–10), where 0 indicates no pain and 10 indicates the worst imaginable pain. The incidence of pain, VAS scores, and clinical outcomes were recorded.

Quality of life assessment: Validated Chinese versions of the following questionnaires were independently completed by patients at enrollment (preoperatively) and during outpatient follow-up visits at 6 months and 12 months postoperatively. The International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire Urinary Incontinence Short Form (ICI-Q-SF) was used to evaluate the impact of urinary incontinence symptoms on patients’ quality of life. This questionnaire comprises items addressing the frequency of leakage, the amount of leakage, and the interference with daily life, with total scores ranging from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate more severe symptoms and poorer quality of life [14, 15]. The Incontinence Impact Questionnaire-7 (IIQ-7) was employed to assess the impact of urinary incontinence on patients’ daily activities, social interactions, emotional well-being, and physical activity. This instrument consists of 7 items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 21; higher scores reflect a greater impact of incontinence on life [16, 17]. The Pelvic Organ Prolapse/Urinary Incontinence Sexual Questionnaire-12 (PISQ-12) was utilized to evaluate the effect of urinary incontinence on sexual function. This questionnaire contains 12 items, with total scores ranging from 0 to 48, where higher scores indicate better sexual function [18, 19].

Efficacy endpoints

Follow-up assessments were conducted through outpatient clinic visits or telephone interviews at 6 and 12 months postoperatively. According to criteria established in previous studies [20], objective efficacy was classified as cure, improvement, or failure. Cure was defined as complete resolution of symptoms, with no urine leakage during activities that increase intra-abdominal pressure, such as coughing, sneezing, or straining. Improvement was defined as symptomatic amelioration, with a ≥ 50% reduction in urine leakage on the 1-hour pad test compared to preoperative values. Failure was defined as no improvement or worsening of symptoms, with a < 50% reduction in urine leakage on the 1-hour pad test compared to preoperative values. The efficacy rate was calculated as follows: (number of cured cases + number of improved cases) / total number of cases × 100%.

Follow-up

All patients underwent follow-up assessments at 6 and 12 months postoperatively, conducted via outpatient clinic visits or telephone interviews. Follow-up assessments were conducted through outpatient clinic visits or telephone interviews. At each follow-up time point, patients were preferentially evaluated in outpatient clinics, while telephone interviews were used for patients who were unable to return for in-person visits. At the 6-month follow-up, 112 patients completed outpatient evaluations and 8 completed telephone interviews. At the 12-month follow-up, 96 patients underwent outpatient assessments and 24 completed telephone follow-up. Outpatient evaluations included inquiry regarding subjective symptoms, physical examination, and administration of the ICI-Q-SF questionnaire assessment, etc. Follow-up assessments were performed by dedicated research personnel who were not involved in the surgical procedures and were blinded to group allocation to minimize measurement bias. The follow-up period ended on February 28, 2026, by which time all patients had completed at least 12 months of follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0 software. Continuous variables were first tested for normality. Data following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using the independent samples t-test. Data not following a normal distribution were expressed as median and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages (%) and compared between groups using the chi-squared test (χ² test). The Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed to analyze urinary incontinence recurrence (defined as failure to achieve cure or improvement), and comparisons between groups were performed using the Log-rank test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 120 eligible patients with severe SUI were enrolled in this study, comprising 66 patients in the TVT-O group and 54 patients in the TVT-E group. No significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics between the two groups, indicating good comparability (Table 1).

Perioperative outcomes

The operative time was significantly shorter in the TVT-O group compared to the TVT-E group, with a statistically significant difference observed (P < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were found between the two groups regarding intraoperative blood loss, indwelling catheterization duration, or mean length of hospital stay (P > 0.05 for all). No cases of major hemorrhage (blood loss > 200 mL) or urethral injury were observed intraoperatively (Table 2).

Risk of recurrence

The follow-up period was 12 months. During follow-up, a total of 5 patients (7.58%) in the TVT-O group experienced recurrence (defined as treatment failure), compared with 3 patients (5.56%) in the TVT-E group. The Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis demonstrated that the 12-month cumulative recurrence-free survival rate was 92.42% in the TVT-O group and 94.44% in the TVT-E group, with no statistically significant difference observed between the two survival curves (log-rank χ² = 0.235, P = 0.628) (Fig. 2).

Discussion

This prospective observational cohort study compared perioperative outcomes, complications, and postoperative quality of life between the TVT-E and TVT-O procedures in women with severe SUI. Our findings demonstrate that both the TVT-O and TVT-E procedures are safe and effective in this specific patient population. Notably, the TVT-O group had a significantly shorter operative time compared to the TVT-E group, while the two groups showed comparable results in terms of other perioperative parameters, complication rates, quality of life improvements, and therapeutic efficacy.

Regarding operative time, the present study found that the TVT-O procedure was significantly more expeditious, with a mean difference of approximately 9 min. This difference is primarily attributable to the distinct surgical approaches of the two techniques. The TVT-E procedure employs a retropubic route, which routinely necessitates cystoscopy to exclude bladder perforation, thereby prolonging the operative time. Although the operative time was statistically shorter in the TVT-O group, the absolute difference was relatively small and should be interpreted cautiously from a clinical perspective, particularly because all procedures were performed under general anesthesia. Nevertheless, in high-volume surgical settings, even modest reductions in operative duration may still contribute to improved operating room efficiency and workflow management. This advantage of TVT-O persisted in our cohort of patients with severe SUI, corroborating the findings of a network meta-analysis by Wang et al. [21] and extending its applicability to this more severe patient population. Nevertheless, no significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding intraoperative blood loss, indwelling catheterization duration, or length of hospital stay, indicating comparable perioperative safety profiles for both procedures in patients with severe SUI. In the present study, the postoperative hospital stay and indwelling catheterization duration were relatively longer than those reported in some previous studies. This may largely reflect differences in institutional perioperative management strategies and discharge criteria rather than delayed postoperative recovery itself. Importantly, both groups were managed under the same standardized perioperative protocol at our institution, thereby maintaining the validity of the intergroup comparisons.

In the present study, all procedures were performed under general anesthesia rather than local or regional anesthesia. This factor should be considered when interpreting the early postoperative recovery outcomes. Previous studies have suggested that mid-urethral sling procedures performed under local anesthesia may facilitate earlier postoperative mobilization and allow more immediate assessment of voiding function and tape tension adjustment. In contrast, general anesthesia may influence short-term recovery indicators, including postoperative pain perception, early ambulation, catheterization duration, and hospital stay. However, because both surgical groups underwent procedures under the same anesthetic protocol, the potential confounding effect of anesthesia type on intergroup comparisons was likely minimized. Future studies comparing different anesthetic strategies in patients undergoing TVT-E or TVT-O procedures may further clarify their impact on perioperative recovery optimization.

Regarding complications, the overall rates were similar between the two groups, consistent with the comparable safety profiles noted above. However, a closer examination of specific complication patterns reveals important clinical nuances that warrant discussion, particularly given that the absence of statistical significance for some comparisons may be attributable to our sample size. Notably, the incidence of postoperative groin or inner thigh pain in the TVT-O group was nearly three times that of the TVT-E group (10.60% vs. 3.70%). Although this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.113), from a clinical standpoint, this threefold difference is substantial and meaningful. It underscores a critical trade-off that must be carefully considered during preoperative counseling. The occurrence of this pain is likely related to the TVT-O needle passage through the obturator foramen and adductor muscles, potentially irritating the obturator nerve or surrounding muscular structures [9]. A cohort study focusing on patients undergoing TVT-O has similarly confirmed that groin pain is one of the most common complications associated with this procedure [22]. For patients with severe stress urinary incontinence, since their anatomical support structures may be more fragile, it remains to be further studied whether the recovery process of postoperative pain differs from that of patients with mild to moderate conditions.

Regarding bladder perforation, a few cases occurred in the TVT-E group in this study, while no such complication was observed in the TVT-O group, with no statistically significant difference detected. Bladder perforation is a complication specific to the retropubic approach, primarily resulting from needle penetration into the bladder during blind passage. The meta-analysis by Wang et al. [21] also demonstrated that the incidence of bladder perforation was slightly higher following TVT procedures compared to TVT-O, although this difference did not achieve statistical significance. Although the rate of bladder perforation in the TVT-E group in the present study remained within an acceptable range, this finding underscores the importance for surgeons of performing routine intraoperative cystoscopy to promptly identify and manage any perforation, thereby preventing serious adverse events. In patients with severe SUI, particularly those with a history of multiple deliveries or previous pelvic surgery, pelvic anatomy may be altered, theoretically increasing the risk of bladder perforation. This further highlights the necessity of intraoperative cystoscopic screening in this patient population.

Regarding voiding dysfunction, the incidence in the TVT-E group was slightly higher than that in the TVT-O group in this study, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. A similar trend was also reported in the network meta-analysis by Wang et al. [9]. The occurrence of voiding dysfunction may be associated with improper tensioning of the tape; excessive tension can lead to urethral obstruction, whereas insufficient tension may compromise continence. In patients with severe stress urinary incontinence, surgeons tend to appropriately tighten the tape to ensure continence, which may, however, increase the risk of postoperative voiding dysfunction. Therefore, achieving precise adjustment of tape tension while ensuring therapeutic efficacy is crucial to surgical success.

Regarding quality of life and efficacy, postoperative ICI-Q-SF scores decreased significantly from baseline in both groups, with no significant intergroup differences observed at any postoperative time point. The objective cure rates were also comparable between the two groups. These findings indicate that both surgical techniques are effective and reliable in improving quality of life and achieving continence in patients with severe SUI. Furthermore, concerning the risk of recurrence, the cumulative recurrence rates during follow-up were similar between the two groups, and survival curve analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in recurrence-free survival rates. This result suggests that the medium-term efficacy stability of both procedures is comparable in patients with severe SUI.

Because treatment allocation in this study was based on patient preference and surgeon recommendation rather than randomization, potential selection bias should be considered when interpreting the findings. Surgical decision-making in clinical practice is inherently individualized and may be influenced by patient anatomy, previous pelvic surgery, symptom severity, surgeon experience, and patient expectations regarding potential complications. Although the baseline characteristics between the two groups were generally balanced, residual confounding related to unmeasured factors cannot be completely excluded. Nevertheless, this pragmatic design may also better reflect real-world clinical decision-making processes.

This study has several limitations. First, despite its prospective design, this was a single-center study with a relatively limited sample size and a non-randomized controlled design, which may introduce selection bias and residual confounding related to surgeon judgment and patient treatment preference. The disparity in sample sizes between the two groups reflects the actual clinical decision-making process in practice, but it may also result in insufficient statistical power to detect differences in complications with low incidence rates. Importantly, no formal a priori sample size or statistical power calculation was performed in this study, which further limits the ability to exclude type II error, particularly for rare postoperative complications. Nevertheless, the generally balanced baseline characteristics between the groups partially support the comparability of the intergroup analyses, although unmeasured confounding factors may still have influenced the results. Second, the follow-up duration was only 12 months, which precludes accurate assessment of outcomes that may require a longer time to manifest, such as tape erosion or long-term recurrence. Third, all procedures were performed under general anesthesia, which may have influenced early postoperative recovery outcomes and may limit comparability with studies using local or regional anesthesia techniques. Additionally, this study did not perform subgroup analyses based on patients’ anatomical characteristics, factors that may influence surgical outcomes and complication rates. Future multicenter, large-sample, prospective randomized controlled trials with extended follow-up periods are warranted to further validate the long-term efficacy and safety of the two surgical techniques, and to explore factors associated with surgical outcomes and complications, thereby providing more evidence for individualized clinical decision-making.

In conclusion, both TVT-O and TVT-E demonstrate favorable safety and efficacy profiles in the treatment of severe SUI, significantly improving patients’ quality of life. TVT-O is associated with a shorter operative time but a relatively higher incidence of inner thigh pain. TVT-E carries a risk of bladder perforation, which can be promptly identified and managed through intraoperative cystoscopy. The two surgical techniques demonstrate comparable therapeutic efficacy and overall complication rates in patients with severe SUI, allowing for individualized selection in clinical practice based on patient-specific factors and surgeon expertise.
