A granularity-level information fusion strategy on hypergraph transformer for predicting synergistic effects of anticancer drugs
Authors: Wei Wang, Gaolin Yuan, Shitong Wan, Ziwei Zheng, Dong Liu, Hongjun Zhang, Juntao Li, Yun Zhou, Xianfang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCombination therapy has exhibited substantial potential compared to monotherapy. However, due to the explosive growth in the number of cancer drugs, the screening of synergistic drug combinations has become both expensive and time-consuming. Synergistic drug combinations refer to the concurrent use of two or more drugs to enhance treatment efficacy. Currently, numerous computational methods have been developed to predict the synergistic effects of anticancer drugs. However, there has been insufficient exploration of how to mine drug and cell line data at different granularity levels for predicting synergistic anticancer drug combinations. Therefore, this study proposes a granularity-level information fusion strategy based on the hypergraph transformer, named HypertranSynergy, to predict synergistic effects of anticancer drugs. HypertranSynergy introduces synergistic connections between cancer cell lines and drug combinations using hypergraph. Then, the Coarse-grained Information Extraction (CIE) module merges the hypergraph with a transformer for node embeddings. In the CIE module, Contranorm is a normalization layer that mitigates over-smoothing, while Gaussian noise addresses local information gaps. Additionally, the Fine-grained Information Extraction (FIE) module assesses fine-grained information’s impact on predictions by employing similarity-aware matrices from drug/cell line features. Both CIE and FIE modules are integrated into HypertranSynergy. INTRODUCTION

Cancer poses a significant global public health concern. Presently, the advancement of anticancer drugs stands as a principal approach to cancer treatment. However, single-drug therapy encounters numerous challenges, encompassing drug-related side effects and the emergence of drug resistance [1]. Consequently, the emergence of combination therapy has gained traction, seeking to harness synergy by amalgamating multiple drugs to address multiple targets, subpopulations, or diseases concurrently [2]. In contrast to the traditional paradigm of single-drug, single-target therapy, combination therapy demonstrably enhances effectiveness. Moreover, owing to the typically lower dosages employed in combination therapy versus individual drug regimens, potential toxicities and adverse patient reactions may be mitigated [3]. Beyond this, combination therapy exhibits the potential to curtail or even surmount instances of drug resistance. It follows that the identification of viable drug combinations holds pivotal significance in disease treatment [4–7].

Predicting drug synergies poses a challenge that can be approached as either a classification or regression task. In the context of the regression task, the objective is to quantitatively predict synergy scores attributed to drug combinations. During the training phase, a diverse range of information pertaining to drugs, target proteins and cancer cell lines is employed as feature data, while the synergy scores serve as the input label data for the model, and the classification task aims to forecast whether a given pair of drugs will yield synergy or non-synergy outcomes [8–11]. In the construction of predictive models, a plethora of data types are harnessed for prediction and screening. Renowned drug combination datasets encompass O’Neil [12], NCI-ALMANAC [13], DrugComb [14], DrugCombDB [15], GDSC Combinations [4] and others. With the surge in available synergy data, diverse prediction techniques grounded in Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) have emerged. ML methods predominantly project drug synergy predictions by establishing the correlation between input attributes of drug combinations or cell lines and the intended output (synergy/non-synergy classification or quantitative synergy score), including Support Vector Machine, Logistic Regression, Random Forest (RF), XGBoost and others [16–20]. For instance, Celebi et al. introduced a model employing DNA methylation, copy number, mutation, and gene expression as cell line features, coupled with drug chemical structures and target pathways as drug features. These features were concatenated and fed into XGBoost for prediction [19]. Li et al., on the other hand, extracted features from an array of data, including drug-target interaction data, gene interaction data and single-drug treatment data, then employed RF for synergy prediction [20]. In recent times, the release of extensive, large-scale synergy datasets has paved the way for the application of DL methodologies in drug prediction [18]. Preuer et al. harnessed chemical and cancer genomic information to formulate a model termed DeepSynergy, designed to predict anticancer drug combinations across multiple cancer types [21]. Meanwhile, Li et al. employed a Siamese network in conjunction with random matrix projection to derive drug features. Then, the features of the cancer cell line were processed through the convolutional network. Finally, the processed features were integrated and input into the multi-layer perceptron network to get the predicted score [22].

In recent years, graph-based computational methods have seen gradual development in the realm of bioinformatics, owing to the prevalence of graph-structured data. Researchers have increasingly embraced these methods to address pertinent challenges. Jiang et al. constructed a comprehensive graph by integrating drug–drug combinations, drug–protein interactions and protein–protein interactions. Subsequently, they employed the GCN model to predict synergistic combinations [23]. In a similar vein, Liu et al. introduced Hypergraphsynergy, a framework harnessing multi-relational enhanced hypergraph learning to forecast synergistic anticancer drug combinations [24]. However, a substantial of avenue for exploration persists the application of existing computational methodologies within the domain of drug combination therapy. For instance, in the context of graph structures, the propagation of information through neighborhood methods often leads to the oversight of localized information. Besides, a model’s performance is often contingent on its capacity to extract biological information from extensive datasets. However, it is difficult to develop a suitable model that can fully extract deeper information. Furthermore, the challenge of mitigating the common issue of over-smoothing within neural networks necessitates careful attention [25, 26].

Hypergraph Transformer is a DL model based on the Transformer architecture designed for processing hypergraph data. Hypergraphs are an extension of traditional graphs where relationships between nodes can be many-to-many, rather than just one-to-one. This model extends traditional graph neural networks to accommodate more complex relationships and data structures. The Hypergraph Transformer represents data using hypergraphs, where nodes can represent entities or concepts, and hyperedges can express complex relationships between them. Initially, the Hypergraph Transformer found wide application in recommendation systems, showcasing its outstanding performance. Yang et al. devised a multi-behavior hypergraph-enhanced transformer to capture short- and long-term cross-type behavioral dependencies. They encoded behavior-aware sequences at fine-grained and coarse-grained levels, then fused features from these two granularity levels into an excellent embedding representation [27].In the field of biomedical research, Shin et al. previously employed transformers to handle graph-structured data for anti-cancer drug response prediction [28]. To comprehensively understand the complexity of biological systems, researchers often need to consider both coarse- and fine-grained information for more in-depth studies. For instance, Zhu et al. innovatively integrated fine-grained (gene- and atomic-level) and coarse-grained (sample-level) information, analyzing data at different granularity levels for anti-cancer drug response prediction [29]. These works inspired us to develop an anti-cancer drug synergistic interaction prediction model based on the Hypergraph Transformer, considering different granularity levels.

Addressing existing challenges, our study proposes an end-to-end DL model, named HypertranSynergy, founded upon the hypergraph transformer. To capture intricate multi-relations between drug combinations and cancer cell lines, HypertranSynergy formulates a drug synergy hypergraph. This hypergraph is constructed using multiple synergistic drug combinations tested on cancer cell lines. Within this construct, drugs and cancer cell lines are denoted as hypergraph nodes, equipped with physiological and chemical attributes as node properties. The interplay of synergistic relationships among drug–drug–cell line triads serves as the foundation for hyperedge. In the Coarse-grained Information Extraction (CIE) module, we leverage a transformer-based technique for feature extraction, facilitating the capture of coarse-grained information, essentially sample-level details, within the hypergraph. Additionally, we introduce a Fine-grained Information Extraction (FIE) module, grounded in attention networks and similarity computation. This module effectively captures fine-grained information, encompassing gene- and atom-level details. The model culminates by employing its network output for the prediction of drug synergistic effects.

Specifically, the whole process involves initializing the features of cell lines and drugs using Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) and Graph Isomorphism Networks (GINs) for creating the node attributes of the hypergraph. The incorporation of synergistic data, encapsulating tripartite relationships between drug–drug–cell line, establishes links between hypernodes and hyperedges. In the CIE module, a transformer-based approach is adopted to extract coarse-grained information from the hypergraph. To address challenges such as limited local information extraction and over-smoothing inherent to transformers, we introduce Gaussian noise and a novel normalization layer termed Contranorm, aimed at rectifying these concerns. Recognizing the potential influence of fine-grained information on prediction outcomes, the FIE module assigns different learnable weight parameters to each vector by calculating the cosine similarity between the feature vectors in each layer, then computes the Hadamard dot product of each feature vector and the corresponding weight at each layer. The resulting informative similarity-aware matrix is employed as the output of the drug/cell line informative similarity-aware matrix. The integration of the CIE and FIE modules into HypertranSynergy facilitates the prediction of synergistic effects. To establish the model’s precision and resilience, rigorous cross-validation and comprehensive experiments are conducted across two datasets, NCI-ALMANAC and O’Neil. This comprehensive assessment demonstrates the rationality of employing hypergraphs to signify relationships between drug combinations and cell lines. Moreover, the fusion of hypergraph transformer and granularity-level information paves the way for novel concepts and strategies in predicting anticancer drug synergies. MATERIALS AND METHODS

Datasets

We employ the same synergistic data for anticancer drugs, drug molecular information, and genomic expression data of cell lines as Liu et al. The synergistic data for anticancer drugs is sourced from the O’Neil [12] and NCI-ALMANAC datasets [13]. The O’Neil dataset encompasses 23,062 samples, encompassing 38 drugs and 39 cancer cell lines. Each sample denotes the corresponding Loewe synergy score for a pair of drugs and a specific cell line. The NCI-ALMANAC dataset includes a substantial 304,549 samples. These samples feature combination scores for 104 FDA-approved drugs tested across 60 human cancer cell lines from the NCI-60 panel.

The genomic expression data for cancer cell lines were obtained from the Cell Lines Project within the COSMIC database [25]. This data focuses solely on 651 genes pertinent to cancers. Following the data extraction, it underwent log2 transformation and z-score normalization per million transcripts. The Simplified Molecular-Input Line-Entry System (SMILES) notation [30] for drugs was sourced from the PubChem database [31].

Following the exclusion of cell lines lacking gene expression data and drugs devoid of SMILES representation, the final iteration of the NCI-ALMANAC dataset comprises 74,139 samples. This dataset encompasses combination scores across 55 cancer cell lines and 87 drugs. Similarly, the O’Neil dataset contains 18,950 samples, encompassing Loewe synergy scores for 39 cancer cell lines and 38 drugs.

HypertranSynergy constitutes a drug synergy prediction model that employs a granularity-level information fusion strategy grounded in hypergraph transformer, whose architecture is visually depicted in Figure 1. The model operates by ingesting the following inputs: (i) gene expression data of cancer cell lines; (ii) chemical descriptors of drugs within a drug pair; (iii) synergistic data on drug combinations tested on cell lines. CIE extracts coarse-grained information from the drug synergy hypergraph, and FIE extracts fine-grained information from the drug/cell line initial feature matrix. The relative significance of their contributions is determined through loss computation. Ultimately, drawing from the acquired embedding representations, the model executes drug synergy predictions.

Specifically, hyperedges within this hypergraph serve the purpose of interconnecting all cell line and drug nodes, encompassing entities with known as well as unknown associations. This framework significantly enhances the potential to explore latent drug–cell line relationships. Additionally, within the realm of this hypergraph, the network acquires the capability to autonomously learn node representations. Consequently, this facilitates the establishment of high-quality links between cell lines and drugs, enabling the prediction of association scores. CIE based on hypergraph transformer

In previous research, hypergraph convolutional networks were frequently employed for hypergraph feature extraction [35]. However, hypergraph convolution primarily relies on local neighborhoods, which might result in a limited capture of global structural information. Furthermore, the features extracted via hypergraph convolution can be challenging to interpret, as it operates as a black-box technique. Consequently, understanding the meaning and individual contribution of each feature becomes complex [36–39]. Given the transformer’s remarkable capacity for modeling long-range dependencies, capturing global insights, and providing enhanced interpretability, we propose an integration of hypergraphs with transformers. This augmentation aims to bolster HypertranSynergy’s prowess in assimilating comprehensive global information embedded within the hypergraph structure [40–42]. The transformer model represents a neural network architecture founded on the principle of self-attention. This mechanism serves to encode input information and extract high-dimensional feature representations. Typically, these high-dimensional features exhibit a greater degree of expressiveness and distinctiveness. In our study, we exclusively employ the transformer encoder. This encoder comprises three core elements: the Multi-Head Attention Layer, the Feed-Forward Network Module and Residual Connections. The Multi-Head Attention module effectively captures global insights across different subspaces within the hypergraph, positioned at diverse locations. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

To comprehensively validate the efficacy of our method, we would like to address the following five pivotal questions:

Q1: Does HypertranSynergy surpass state-of-the-art baselines in terms of performance?

Q2: Do all components of HypertranSynergy demonstrate robust functionality?

Q3: How can we ensure that HypertranSynergy attains optimal performance?

Q4: Does HypertranSynergy sustain commendable performance across various scenarios?

Q5: Can HypertranSynergy discern any pertinent biological significance?

To scrutinize these inquiries, we conducted a comparative analysis involving HypertranSynergy along with various DL and ML techniques. Specifically, we compared it against SNRMPACDC, ProDeepSyn, HypergraphSynergy and DeepSynergy in the DL realm, as well as RFs, SVMs and Elastic Nets in the ML realm. The performance results for all these methods were derived from pertinent literature. In alignment with the approach taken by Preuer et al., we categorized samples with synergy scores surpassing 30 as positive samples, while categorizing the others as negative samples [21].

To avoid over-optimistic results, we divided the dataset into cross-validation set and independent test set by 9:1 in the experiment and used the model trained on the cross-validation set for the independent test set for objective evaluation. For the classification task, we dichotomized the synergy scores using predefined threshold from prior investigations, yielding positive samples denoting synergy and negative samples denoting non-synergy. Specifically, samples exhibiting synergy scores beyond 30 were classified as positive, while those below were classified as negative. To evaluate the classification outcomes, we employed four evaluation metrics: the Area Under the Curve (AUC), the Area Under the Precision-Recall curve (AUPR), the Accuracy (ACC) and the F1-score (F1). For the regression task, we gauged performance using three distinct metrics: the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC), the coefficient of determination (R2) and the root mean square error (RMSE).

Parameter sensitivity analysis

To address Q3, we investigated the impact of several key parameters on the performance of HypertranSynergy.

Effect of the size of embedding dimension processed by CIE

On the O’Neil dataset, we set the embedding dimensions to 64, 128, 256, 512 and 1024, respectively, and analyzed the performance of the model to determine the optimal embedding dimension that allows the CIE module to extract richer information from the input features. As depicted in Figure 3(A) and (B), when the embedding dimension is set to 512, both AUC and AUPR reach their maximum values. As the dimension of entity embeddings increases, AUC and AUPR steadily decrease. This outcome suggests that within a certain range, augmenting the dimension of entity embeddings can effectively encode more high-order information. However, if the dimension is too low, it might lead to insufficiently encoded information, whereas surpassing the threshold could result in overfitting. Consequently, AUC and AUPR demonstrate an inclination of initially increasing and subsequently decreasing.

Result of parameter sensitivity analysis. (A, B) Parameter analysis of embedding dimension processed by CIE. (C,D) Parameter analysis of the number of transformer multi-head attention. (E, F) Parameter analysis of transformer layers.

Effect of the number of attention heads

We explored the effect of the number of attention heads on the model’s performance. As depicted in Figure 3(C) and (D), both AUC and AUPR display a pattern of initially increasing and then decreasing. HypertranSynergy attains its peak performance when the number of attention heads is set to 2. These findings suggest that the number of attention heads plays a role in the model’s performance. This stems from the fact that multiple heads collaborate to enhance the stability of the learning process; a single attention head might not suffice to capture all the biological information of an entity, and an excessive number of attention heads could introduce redundant information.

Effect of the number of transformer layers

We assessed the influence of the number of Transformer layers on the prediction outcomes. As depicted in Figure 3E and Figure 3F, the experiments were conducted across a range of 1, 2, 3, 4 layers. The results reveal that the optimal performance is achieved when the number of layers, denoted as L, is set to 2. This phenomenon can be attributed to a delicate balance between the dependencies among positive samples and the noise inherent in negative signals. Specifically, when the model operates with only 1 layer, it struggles to effectively capture deeper semantic and contextual information, resulting in insufficient feature extraction and limited expressive capability. Consequently, the model is unable to comprehensively account for the correlations and dependencies among entities. Conversely, employing an excessively high number of layers can provide more information for each entity, yet it becomes more susceptible to the noise and interference present within the dataset. Therefore, pinpointing the optimal number of layers is essential for striking the right equilibrium between representation power and the influence of noise, thus leading to enhanced performance.

CONCLUSION

This paper introduces a novel DL approach, termed HypertranSynergy, which combines prior domain knowledge to express the multivariate relationship between cancer cell lines and drug combinations using hypergraph, and performs anti-cancer drug synergy prediction based on a granular level information fusion strategy on hypergraph transformer. Precisely, we constructed node characteristics within the hypergraph using gene expression data harnessed from cancer cell lines and the physicochemical attributes of drugs. The interaction between cell lines and drug combinations is established via synergy data, thus constructed hyperedges within the hypergraph structure. After establishing multivariate relationships between cell lines and drug combinations through hypergraph, we harness the transformer within the CIE module, thereby aggregating coarse-grained information from the hypergraph. Moreover, in order to take into account the extraction of fine-grained information, we construct informative similarity-aware matrix, serving as supplementary fine-grained information within the FIE module, thereby enriching the feature representation. Our extensive experimentation, executed across the O’Neil and NCI-ALMANAC datasets, underscores HypertranSynergy’s superior performance compared to a suite of advanced methods, across varying experimental conditions. Additionally, ablation experiments corroborate the efficacy and contributions of individual components intrinsic to HypertranSynergy. Lastly, the application of our method to biological case studies exhibits its potential to provide experts with valuable insights, facilitating their foray into the exploration of pertinent domain knowledge.

While achieving excellent performance, it’s imperative to acknowledge the existing limitations within HypertranSynergy, providing ideas for future-related research. Primarily, the information conveyed by hypergraph node features appear to be somewhat insufficient, which is related to the data we have chosen. Our selection process lacked the inclusion of toxicological attributes within the drug physicochemical information dataset. Similarly, the cell line dataset exclusively comprised gene expression data, neglecting the incorporation of supplemental aspects such as copy number variations and mutations. Existing studies accentuate the influence of these features on predicting anticancer drug synergies. Additionally, the incorporation of other graph-structured data, like drug–target interaction networks and drug–drug interaction networks, emerges as a pivotal consideration and is of great significance for the study of combined therapy.

Presently, the landscape leans towards investigating synergistic drug combinations encompassing more than two drugs, diverging from the conventional dual-drug focus. However, most prior computational methodologies are tailored for dual-drug scenarios and are less applicable to multi-drug settings. Fortunately, hypergraphs can seamlessly link any count of nodes, thus offering a viable avenue to probe synergistic associations in multi-drug compositions. Shaping strategies to harness the expressive capacity of hypergraphs for the study of multi-drug synergy, while concurrently enhancing the interpretability and generalization potential of hypergraph neural networks, will undoubtedly guide our future research trajectory. In summation, HypertranSynergy is expected to be widely used in the field of screening synergistic combinations of anticancer drugs.
