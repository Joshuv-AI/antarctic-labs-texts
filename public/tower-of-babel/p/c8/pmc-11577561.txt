Introduction

The significance of PGPR biofertilizers in plant growth

The rhizosphere, a narrow zone of soil surrounding plant roots, is typically measures from less than one to three millimeters thick and is directly influenced by the plant root system (Prashar et al., 2014). This region has higher nutrient concentrations than non-rhizosphere soils because of the accumulation of various organic compounds released from the roots (Van Loon, 2007; Moradi et al., 2021). Root exudates trigger physical and chemical changes in the rhizosphere soil, causing alterations in pH, partial oxygen pressure, and water potential compared with non-rhizospheric soil (Liu et al., 2022). Consequently, the rhizosphere experiences an increase in microbial population density, with bacteria secreting metabolites in this region (Van Loon, 2007).

PGPR encompass a diverse group of beneficial soil bacteria that stimulate plant growth when growing near a plant as its host (Khosravi et al., 2009; Khalid et al., 2009; Khosravi, and Mahmoudi, 2013; Khoshru and Sarikhani, 2018; Shirinbayan et al., 2019; Khosravi, 2019; Glick, 2020). In rhizosphere interactions, PGPR primarily attach to the surface of the plant roots. While this interaction is weaker than mutualistic relationships, such as the association between Azospirillum and cereals, it is much weaker than symbiotic relationships, such as Rhizobium-legume symbiosis. Azotobacter, Pseudomonas, and Bacillus are among the key bacteria in this group, along with others classified as diazotrophs, including Acetobacter, Azoarcus, and Azospirillum (Vocciante et al., 2022).

Having the potential of positive impact of PGPR on plant growth has led to their use as biofertilizers. PGPR biofertilizer contains an appropriate number of one or more beneficial soil bacteria that are able to provide all or part of the plant's nutritional needs for one or more elements, or stimulate plant growth, or plant tolerance to stress factors such as drought and salinity (Khosravi et al., 2009; Khosravi, 2019; Glick, 2020, 2021).

Each PGPR biofertilizer contains one or more bacterial isolates and a carrier that is packed in a special container. The carrier forms the major part of a biofertilizer in terms of weight and volume. A good carrier should be able to provide suitable respiratory, acidity and humidity conditions for the survival of bacteria in a given population from production to expire date. The necessary materials for the preparation of the carrier should be easily accessible, low in price, and have the ability to stick to the surface of the seed or root (Thompson, 1984).

Understanding the role of PGPR in enhancing plant growth

PGPR effectively impact plant growth through various mechanisms, including influencing root morphology, producing growth-promoting hormones such as auxins, gibberellins, and cytokinins, nitrogen fixation, solubilization of insoluble soil phosphates, production of siderophores, and biological sulfur oxidation (Fig. 1) (Khosravi and Mahmoudi, 2013; Yang et al., 2019; Glick, 2020; Perez et al., 2021; Herms et al., 2022; Castro-Camba et al., 2022; Fathy et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2022; Dinh et al., 2022).

Overview of current agricultural practices and the need for sustainable alternatives

The cultivated area of agricultural, horticultural and greenhouse crops in Iran is about 15.5 million hectares, and 3–3.5 million tons of chemical fertilizers are used for agricultural crops in the country every year (Anonymous a. 2023). Amounts of these fertilizers are consumed imbalanced and more than the plant needs. Excessive use of chemical fertilizers, in addition to imposing a high cost on farmers, has environmental consequences, such as soil pollution and surface and underground water sources, and the toxicity of elements in plants, which eventually enter the human food chain (Bisht and Chauhan, 2020). Based on this, today in Iran, the demand of people to buy organic products is increasing. One of the solutions based on sustainable agriculture for the healthy nutrition of plants, along with the balanced and correct use of chemical fertilizers, is to use the potential of beneficial soil microorganisms, especially PGPR to provide at least part of their nutritional needs.

Historical evolution and overview of PGPR biofertilizers

Early adoption, adaptation, policy initiatives and investments in advancing PGPR biofertilizers technologies in Iran

The first microbial fertilizer containing Rhizobium bacteria was commercialized approximately 130 years ago in the world (Vessey, 2003). The PGPR were first introduced at Auburn University in the late 1970s (Kloepper and Schroth, 1981). Extensive use of PGPR in agriculture began approximately in the former Soviet Union, with field trials investigating the effects of nitrogen-fixing bacteria on various crops (Kawaka, 2022). However, only 35 % of fields responded positively to inoculation with nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Kawaka, 2022). While biofertilizers are produced and consumed globally, their market share compared to chemical fertilizers is only approximately 1 % (Sansinenea, 2021; Joshi and Gauraha, 2022; Mordor Intelligence, 2024).

Education on soil microbiology and biofertilizers in Iran began in the 1980s then research on began in the 1990s with student theses at the University of Tehran and continued at other universities. Profound research on biofertilizers including PGPR were initiated simultaneously with the establishment of the soil biology and biotechnology research department at the Soil and Water Research Institute in the 1994. This research led to the acquisition of the technical knowledge of the production of several biofertilizers, which was transferred to the private sector for commercialization. Currently, several companies producing biofertilizers have been established in the country and they are producing different biofertilizers with various PGPR (Table 1). An accurate statistics on production and market size are unavailable. However, the widespread adoption of PGPR biofertilizers, akin to chemical fertilizers, has not made significant progress in Iran's agriculture. Additionally, no analytical report has been presented on the status of the research, production, and application of PGPR biofertilizers in the country. This review article aims to evaluate the issues and challenges facing the research, production, and development of PGPR biofertilizers and to assess the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and prospects of these biological products in Iran.

Case studies; success and failure stories: demonstrating the challenges of PGPR biofertilizers on crop productivity in Iran

Cases of success

It has been reported that inoculation with native Azotobacter chroococcum has a significant impact on wheat growth and root system enhancement in a greenhouse experiment (Khosravi et al., 1998). In a series of comprehensive and interconnected experiments, 217 strains of Azotobacter chroococcum from 362 soil samples were isolated from wheat fields across Iran. The findings revealed varying outcomes regarding the effect of inoculating superior strains on wheat growth in different regions. The results indicated a 20 % increase in wheat grain yield in East Azerbaijan, 13.5 % in Khorasan, 8 % in Fars, and 7 % in West Azerbaijan, and no significant change in Kurdistan compared to the non-inoculated treatment (Khosravi et al., 2012). In field trials, inoculation of Pseudomonas strains on wheat resulted in a significant increase in grain yield in some regions (Khavazi, 2009). Some Pseudomonas strains significantly enhance grain yield and thousand-grain weight in wheat, particularly under saline conditions (Zabihi et al., 2009).

In a pot experiment, inoculation of apple seedling roots with different native strains of Azotobacter significantly impact on K, Mg, Fe, Mn, and Zn elements absorption by leaves (Khosravi et al., 2009). In a field experiment, phosphate-solubilizing bacteria significantly influenced the growth indices of rice (Eftekhari et al., 2010). Silicate-releasing bacteria in laboratory and greenhouse experiments significantly increased the absorption of total potassium and shoot dry weight of m (Fallah Nusratabad et al., 2008). Research has also shown that laboratory PGPR biofertilizers have the potential to increase maize tolerance to drought stress (Shirinbayan et al., 2019).

In a pot experiment, Thiobacillus inoculation in sterile and non-sterile soils in the presence of 12 % lime led to a decrease of approximately 0.8 in soil pH (Khavazi, 2008). In a pot experiment without plants, inoculated sulfur-oxidizing bacteria in the presence of sulfur and organic matter resulted in a decrease in soil pH, an increase in sulfate and iron concentrations, and an increase in soil electrical conductivity (EC) (Besharti, 2016).

Cases of failure

In a greenhouse experiment, different strains of Azospirillum had no significant effect on the growth indices of wheat (Rousta et al., 1998). Inoculation of Thiobacillus and a phosphate-solubilizing bacterium on a rock phosphate source on maize growth in a greenhouse experiment revealed no significant difference between the inoculation treatments and others (Nourgholipour et al., 2006). Thiobacillus inoculation, sulfur, and rock phosphate on soybean growth in the first year, and their residual effects on maize growth in the second year, were not significant (Nourgholipour et al., 2000). It was concluded that rock phosphate, sulfur, and Thiobacillus inoculation on maize and their residual effects on barley yield in a field study had no positive effect on leaf phosphorus content, absorbability, and total phosphorus performance; however, there was a significant increase in dry yield for both crops (Iranipour et al., 2007).

The inoculation of commercial Azotobacter inoculum on wheat at eight locations in Iran did not show positive effects on wheat growth (Khosravi, 2013). In addition, using a commercially Azotobacter inoculum on maize growth across ten locations in Iran had no positive effects on crop yield (Khosravi et al., 2012).

An analytical look at and discussion on different PGPR research

Inconsistencies in laboratory, greenhouse, and field results

As mentioned above, in some cases, the plant does not respond at all to inoculation with the PGPR, which is an important challenge to be determined in future research. Research on the effects of biofertilizers containing PGPR on plant growth in Iran has yielded different results.

One of the challenges is the existence of conflicting reports on the differences between laboratory, greenhouse, and field results and the poor reproducibility of the results. These challenges may be due to an incomplete understanding of the mechanisms through which PGPR affects plant growth (Table 2)

Asadi Rahmani (2008) studied the effect of 40 native Pseudomonas strains on canola growth in sand culture medium. The results showed that R112, P21, P7, P16, P15, and P2 strains had the greatest effect on the growth indices. At this stage, the strains that produced more auxin had a greater effect on growth indices. The R187 strain was identified as the best strain in the greenhouse and soil test phases. The results of the field experiment in Isfahan showed that none of the strains had a significant effect on the growth of canola compared to non-inoculation. However, in Semnan, strain P19 significantly increased grain yield. It was concluded that the laboratory, greenhouse, and field results did not match.

Asadi Rahmani (2016) examined the effect of inoculating various strains of Microbacterium on wheat growth. Among the strains, m9, exhibited the highest auxin production, while m22 had the highest phosphate-solubilizing capacity. Seven strains were selected for greenhouse experiments. Although the greenhouse results did not specify the strains with the greatest effect on growth indices, the m20 strain significantly influenced the weight of straw and stubbles. Moreover, the m43, m34, and m40 strains showed a significant increase in other growth indices. Farm experiments in Mazandaran revealed that the m9, m11, and m40 strains exhibited a significant difference in grain yield compared with the control. In Fars, the m40 and m11 strains also demonstrated a significant difference in grain yield compared with the control. However, none of the strains had a significant effect on wheat yield in Dezful. Interestingly, a report from Kermanshah indicated that all strains had significant effects on grain yield.

Effect of Environmental Conditions on PGPR Efficiency

Another problem in the conflicting results of the effectiveness of PGPR on plants is the environmental conditions such as climate variability, plant cultivars, and soil types.

Khosravi et al. (2013) revealed that ensuring the effectiveness of Azotobacter inocula across all regions in Iran is a complex task. Notably, the type of native strain exhibited a minimal correlation with the impact of the desired strain, indicating that the geographical origin of the bacterial isolates did not consistently influence the outcomes. For instance, in the East Azerbaijan region, the strain from that region had the least effect on growth indices, whereas strains from other regions had better effects on certain growth indices. In Khorasan, the strain associated with Golestan had the most significant impact on the grain yield. In Fars, strains from various regions exhibited diverse effects on the measured growth indices. Conversely, in Kurdistan and West Azerbaijan, none of the strains exhibited a significant effect on growth indices. It is worth noting that each region employed dominant and recommended wheat varieties, resulting in naturally different wheat varieties used in the research. Therefore, the variation in results across regions with identical treatments may be attributed to differences in the wheat varieties. However, in West Azerbaijan and Kurdistan, where the same Zarin cultivar was used, inoculation did not significantly affect the growth indices.

Khavazi (2008) observed that five selected strains of Pseudomonas increased wheat grain yield in field trials conducted in Kermanshah. However, in Mashhad, Darab, and Fars, none of the strains exhibited a significant effect on the grain yield. Notably, one strain in Mazandaran and two of the five strains in Dezful significantly enhanced seed yield.

One of the most important factor in the effectiveness of PGPR is the state of soil organic matter, as most PGPR are heterotrophic and require simple carbon sources for their growth. In many agricultural soils in Iran, the level of soil organic matter is low and there is no suitable platform for the activity and effectiveness of PGPR. Studies have shown that the simultaneous inoculation of Azotobacter chroococcum and the use of manure has significant effects on the growth of wheat (Khosravi et al., 1998; Khosravi and Mahmoudi, 2013) and apple seedlings (Khosravi et al., 2009) compared to Azotobacter alone.

However, Research conducted in Iran shows that most PGPR studies were mainly conducted in laboratory and greenhouse conditions, and farm experiments were relatively less reported. This problem has made it not easy to summarize the meta-data in the field.

Nevertheless, it seems that biofertilizers can find their place in the country's fertilizer basket, provided that additional research work is done on different groups of PGPR and different plants and the different aspects presented in this review article be considered.

Standards and guidelines for PGPR biofertilizer production and quality control

Producing biofertilizers with PGPR involves a systematic approach encompassing several key stages (Ansari et al., 2020). Initially, PGPR strains are isolated from soil samples and screened for beneficial traits. Following identification and characterization, the selected strains undergo fermentation and mass production, with careful optimization of growth parameters. The resulting biomass is formulated into a stable product, subject to rigorous quality control and testing procedures. Field trials assess efficacy under diverse conditions, while regulatory compliance ensures safety and market readiness (Mishra and Barolia, 2020; Khoshru et al., 2016). Detailed documentation and continuous improvement drive process refinement, with a focus on environmental sustainability and safety considerations. By adhering to these standards and guidelines, biofertilizer production can yield effective, eco-friendly solutions for enhancing agricultural productivity (Fig. 2).

Bacterial population in inoculum

Generally, a high microbial count in biofertilizers may be desirable to ensure effective colonization and nutrient uptake by plants (BIS, 2004). Nevertheless, it has been reported that a PGPR at 105 CFU.ml−1 may induce changes in root morphology, whereas 106 CFU.ml−1 may have the opposite effect (Persello‐Cartieaux et al., 2003). Therefore, it is not known precisely which population of PGPR bacteria in the inoculum is best to influence the growth of a particular plant. However, the microbial population in biofertilizers can be different according to the specific strain of bacteria used and biofertilizer formulation. Various studies have reported different microbial populations based on factors such as the type of microorganisms, production process, and intended application (Vessey, 2003). Compilation of standards for PGPR inoculants production in Iran

The industrial production of biofertilizers faces obstacles, such as product quality and maintenance efficiency during fermentation, storage, and transportation, as well as resistance to adverse environmental conditions that must be considered by the manufacturer (Nelson, 2004). Therefore, it is essential for manufacturers and users to follow best practices for quality control, storage, and application to maintain the viability and efficacy of the microbial populations in biofertilizers. Regular testing and monitoring microbial populations can help ensure that biofertilizers meet specific quality standards and deliver the intended agricultural benefits (ISO, 2013).

As mentioned, considering the various issues and challenges that PGPR biofertilizers are facing, to date, no international standard agreed upon by most countries has been provided for the production of biofertilizers. However, national and regional guidelines have been developed in some countries. In Iran, in cooperation with the National Standards Organization and the Soil and Water Research Institute, some standards have been developed for biofertilizers. According to this, every PGPR biofertilizer must have characteristics that the producer must comply with. Quality control and assurance measures: ensuring consistency and efficacy in PGPR biofertilizer products

Quality control and assurance measures play a crucial role in ensuring the consistency and efficacy of PGPR biofertilizer products (Ghosh et al., 2001; Khoshru et al., 2016; Ansari et al., 2020). Implementing rigorous quality control protocols throughout the production process is essential to maintain product integrity and meet regulatory standards. This includes stringent monitoring of raw materials, production conditions, and final product characteristics to ensure uniformity and purity. Additionally, regular quality assurance assessments, such as microbial viability tests, microbial population, and field trials, are indispensable for verifying product performance and efficacy under real-world conditions. Embracing advanced analytical techniques, such as bacterial next-generation sequencing and metabolomics, enables comprehensive characterization of microbial consortia and formulation components, facilitating quality optimization and continual improvement. Furthermore, establishing certification schemes and industry standards enhances transparency and consumer confidence, ensuring that PGPR biofertilizer products meet the highest quality benchmarks. By prioritizing quality control and assurance measures, Iran can foster a robust biofertilizer market, underpinning sustainable agriculture practices and promoting environmental stewardship.

The responsibility for evaluating all types of fertilizers, including biofertilizers in Iran, is with the Registration and Quality Control Office of Fertilizers. This office has policy committees, technical working groups and collaborating laboratories of the private and public sectors. After going through the legal procedures, each biological fertilizer receives a registration code that is valid for two years, and after the manufactured product must be re-examined. During two years, random samples are prepared from the market and their quality control is monitored. Challenges in research and development of PGPR biofertilizers

General challenges in research management and the overall orientation of PGPR research

One of the challenges in developing PGPR biofertilizers in Iran is scattered, parallel, and repetitive of research. In universities, research is often conducted for research and not for biofertilizer development. This research mostly lacks a clear direction, which is often influenced by facilities, available equipment, and professors' expertise. Also conducting most of the research at the laboratory and greenhouse and implementation of field research in the form of treatment-repetition and a small number of research extension farms and farmers' fields. The existence of different strategies in crop management, lack of sufficient and defined communication between research, production, and promotion of PGPR and little familiarity of farmers with biofertilizers are also important challenges.

Technical and biological challenges of PGPR

The ineffectiveness of some PGPR may stem from the inability of bacteria to effectively colonize plant roots. Factors such as bacterial motility, the capacity to secrete effective proteins, the production of pili, and their response to root and seed exudates influence the efficacy of PGPR (Nelson, 2004). Uncertainty of the most effective number of bacteria to colonize a specific plant, inadequate knowledge of the status of native bacteria and their competitive status with types introduced by biofertilizers and occurrence of genetic changes in bacteria are biological challenges with PGPR. The existence of different research reports and conflicts between laboratory, greenhouse, and field results, and weak reproducibility of results is an important other challenge.

Economic and market challenges: commercialization and adoption of PGPR biofertilizers in Iranian agriculture

Problems in commercialization include the need for special expertise and equipment in the stages of production, storage, and distribution, the shelf life of microorganisms, the contamination of biofertilizer, and quality control. There are other issues such as cumbersome administrative rules and obtaining production licenses from relevant institutionsthat hinder commercialization.

Non-compliance with intellectual property rights creates concern for researchers and manufacturers who purchase technical knowledge. This is because it is not difficult to prepare the microorganisms in the fertilizer for others and abuse it.

Companies face challenges in selling and finding a market for biofertilizer products, because some commercial products may lack the necessary quality and effectiveness for the plant, which causes farmers to doubt and mistrust all biofertilizers. One of the reasons for this problem is the quality control standard of biofertilizers, because the government only considers the appropriate number of microorganisms in biofertilizers as the criterion for a production license; according to the authors, real quality control consists of checking the effectiveness of biofertilizer on plant growth and yield.

Strategies for advancing PGPR biofertilizers

Research and innovation: leveraging biotechnology and genomic tools for PGPR improvement

To propel the efficacy and applicability of PGPR biofertilizers, should leverage cutting-edge biotechnology and genomic tools. Molecular research is pivotal in deciphering the intricate genetic pathways governing PGPR-plant interactions, enabling the engineering of tailored bio-inoculants optimized for specific crops and environmental conditions. Fundamental studies on microbial competition and root colonization dynamics elucidate the factors influencing PGPR establishment and efficacy in the rhizosphere, guiding the development of strategies to maximize root colonization and nutrient mobilization. Innovative formulation research exploring synergistic combinations of biological, organic, and chemical components enhances biofertilizer stability and efficacy. Concurrent efforts to increase soil organic carbon levels complement PGPR application, fostering microbial diversity and activity. Integrated management practices such as combining biofertilizers with organic matter enrich soil fertility, enhance crop productivity, and promote environmental sustainability, positioning the country at the forefront of sustainable agriculture innovation.

Policy recommendations and industry support: promoting investment and market access for PGPR biofertilizers

To catalyze the widespread adoption of PGPR biofertilizers and foster a thriving market ecosystem, strategic policy interventions and robust industry support mechanisms are imperative. Here are several recommendations aimed at promoting investment and enhancing market access for PGPR biofertilizers:

1.Research Concentration and Certification: Directing research efforts towards biofertilizers and certifying the country's indigenous growth-promoting rhizobacteria are pivotal steps. By consolidating research endeavors, insights into native microbial strains can be harnessed, facilitating the development of tailor-made bio-inoculants optimized for local agricultural contexts. Certification ensures quality standards, instilling confidence among farmers and buyers regarding the efficacy and safety of biofertilizer products.

Conclusion

The demand for agricultural products is increasing annually. Chemical fertilizers are among the most widely used inputs for the production of agricultural products, and their excessive and unbalanced consumption causes environmental problems. Today, attention to organic agriculture and the production and consumption of healthy products is expanding in society. One solution based on sustainable agriculture is the use of PGPR biofertilizers, which can be used for a wide range of plants. In the last century, many studies have been conducted worldwide on the use of PGPR biofertilizers in the world. In Iran, during the last three decades, research has been conducted in this field, and although some of them have been commercialized, the production and consumption of these products have not progressed much compared with chemical fertilizers in the country. The most important reasons for this issue include the following: scattered, parallel research; the existence of different reports and contradictions between laboratory, greenhouse, and field results; poor reproducibility of results; and conducting a small number of studies in farms and farmers' fields. The low amount of soil organic matter; insufficient communication between the research, production, and extension departments; and the need for equipment, technology, and special considerations in the production, storage, transportation, and consumption of PGPR biofertilizers.

Future perspectives and opportunities for sustainable agriculture through PGPR biofertilizer innovation in Iran

In paving the path for the advancement of research and adoption of PGPR biofertilizers in Iran, several crucial strategies emerge to maximize efficacy and sustainability. These avenues encompass a holistic approach, ranging from enhancing soil health to leveraging cutting-edge molecular studies. First and foremost, the augmentation of soil organic carbon stands as a pivotal initiative. Elevating soil organic carbon levels not only fosters soil fertility but also enhances its water retention capacity, mitigating the impact of drought and bolstering overall resilience against environmental stressors. Furthermore, integrating biofertilizers with organic materials constitutes a synergistic approach towards sustainable agriculture. Combining the benefits of PGPR biofertilizers with organic matter not only enriches soil fertility but also promotes long-term soil health, minimizing reliance on synthetic inputs and fostering ecological balance. In parallel, the advancement of molecular studies pertaining to PGPR holds immense promise in enhancing biofertilizers efficiency. Delving into the intricate mechanisms underlying plant-microbe interactions facilitates the development of tailored bioinoculants, optimized to augment nutrient uptake, mitigate pathogen infestation, and bolster plant resilience in diverse environmental conditions. Moreover, fundamental research on microbial competition within biofertilizers and their interaction with native soil microbiota is imperative. Understanding the dynamics of microbial consortia within biofertilizers vis-a-vis indigenous soil microorganisms elucidates the factors influencing plant root colonization and nutrient cycling. Such insights empower the refinement of biofertilizer formulations tailored to specific agroecological contexts, optimizing their efficacy and ensuring seamless integration into existing agricultural practices. In essence, embracing these multifaceted strategies not only fosters the widespread adoption of PGPR biofertilizers but also heralds a paradigm shift towards sustainable agriculture in Iran. By synergizing scientific innovation with ecological stewardship, Iran can chart a course towards agricultural resilience, ensuring food security while safeguarding the delicate balance of its ecosystems.
