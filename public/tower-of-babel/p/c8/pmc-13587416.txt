Application of 3D Slicer-based three-dimensional visual surgical planning in endoscopic optic canal decompression surgery: a prospective randomised controlled study
Authors: Qi Jin, Qian Long, Jinhai Yu, Gang Hu, Yuxin Liu, Puying Gan, Zhangjun Ren, Hongfei Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the value of three-dimensional visualization preoperative planning based on the open-source software 3D Slicer in endoscopic endonasal optic nerve decompression for traumatic optic neuropathy.MethodsA prospective randomized controlled study was conducted. A total of 48 patients with traumatic optic neuropathy who underwent endoscopic endonasal optic nerve decompression at the Affiliated Eye Hospital of Nanchang University between January 2023 and June 2025 were enrolled and randomly assigned to an experimental group (3D Slicer-based three-dimensional visualization planning, n = 25) and a control group (conventional two-dimensional CT planning, n = 23). The primary outcome measures included operative time, intraoperative misjudgment rate, complication rate, surgeon’s subjective score (5-point Likert scale), and visual improvement at 3 months postoperatively. Continuous data were analyzed using independent-sample t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical data were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test.ResultsThere were no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics or preoperative visual acuity grade between the two groups (P > 0.05). The operative time was significantly shorter in the experimental group than in the control group (129.4 ± 11.6 min vs. 150.7 ± 20.4 min, P < 0.001). Postoperative visual acuity improved significantly compared with preoperative values in both groups (experimental group: P < 0.001; control group: P = 0.003), but the intergroup difference in the grade of visual improvement was not statistically significant (P = 0.439). The intraoperative misjudgment rate was 0% (0/25) in the experimental group and 13.0% (3/23) in the control group, with no statistically significant difference (P = 0.24). The incidence of cerebrospinal fluid leakage was 4.0% (1/25) in the experimental group and 21.7% (5/23) in the control group, and the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.08). The surgeon’s subjective score was significantly higher in the experimental group than in the control group [5 (5,5) vs. 4 (4,4), P < 0.001]. No severe complications such as major vessel injury or direct optic nerve injury occurred in either group.ConclusionThree-dimensional visualization preoperative planning based on 3D Slicer can significantly shorten the operative time of endoscopic endonasal optic nerve decompression, enhance the surgeon’s confidence, and a potential reduction in cerebrospinal fluid leakage that warrants further investigation. This approach is low-cost, highly generalizable, and has good clinical application value.

Background

Traumatic optic neuropathy, as a severe blinding complication following craniofacial trauma, has shown an increasing incidence trend, with an overall occurrence rate of approximately 0.7% to 2.5% [1, 2]. This condition predominantly affects young males, accounting for about 84% of cases, with road traffic accidents being the leading cause of injury [3]. Craniofacial blunt or penetrating trauma can directly or indirectly damage the optic nerve, leading to acute visual decline or even complete loss, of which approximately 46% of patients present with no light perception immediately after injury [3]. Clinical diagnosis is primarily based on a definite history of head or ocular trauma, along with signs indicative of optic nerve injury, such as afferent pupillary defect, visual decline, dyschromatopsia, and visual field defects. However, in patients with polytrauma accompanied by impaired consciousness, neurological examination is often limited, making early diagnosis difficult [4]. Although bilateral traumatic optic neuropathy is rare, its consequences are even more severe.

In the face of such blinding injury, how to effectively intervene to salvage or improve vision has long been a major challenge in the fields of neuro-ophthalmology and skull base surgery. The pathophysiological mechanism of traumatic optic neuropathy is complex, involving primary mechanical axonal shearing, secondary ischemia, edema, and subsequent apoptotic cascades [1, 2]. Based on the theory that the optic nerve within the bony optic canal may suffer secondary injury due to edema or compression by fracture fragments, various treatment strategies have been explored clinically, mainly including high-dose glucocorticoid pulse therapy, optic canal decompression surgery, or a combination of both, while some experts advocate simple observation [5, 6]. Nevertheless, which regimen is optimal remains controversial, and there is a lack of accepted clinical guidelines based on high-level evidence [7, 8]. A meta-analysis involving more than 6,000 patients showed that the rate of visual improvement with conservative observation alone was 34%, whereas the improvement rates for glucocorticoid pulse therapy and optic canal decompression surgery were 48% and 56%, respectively [3]. Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses have attempted to compare surgical and medical efficacy, but the conclusions are inconsistent. Some analyses suggest that optic canal decompression may lead to better visual outcomes compared with conservative treatment or steroid therapy alone, especially in patients with indirect injury and those undergoing surgery early (e.g., within 7 days after injury) [7, 9]. However, other reviews have indicated that surgical decompression does not demonstrate additional clear benefits over conservative observation or steroid therapy, and surgery itself carries definite risks of cerebrospinal fluid leakage and meningitis [10, 11]. This controversy largely stems from the fact that existing evidence is mostly derived from retrospective case series with small sample sizes and high heterogeneity in study design, making direct comparisons very difficult [12]. The lack of high-quality randomized controlled trials makes the principle of “first, do no harm” particularly important in clinical decision-making [6].

Regarding the choice of surgical intervention, endoscopic endonasal optic nerve decompression has gradually become the mainstream surgical approach due to its advantages of minimal invasiveness, direct access to the skull base, and preservation of olfaction [5, 13]. This technique involves grinding away the medial wall of the optic canal via the transnasal‑sphenoidal or transnasal‑ethmoidal route under endoscopic visualization to relieve bony compression of the optic nerve, and optic nerve sheath incision can be performed for further decompression [14, 15]. Clinical practice has shown that even in patients who do not respond to conventional high‑dose steroid therapy, endoscopic decompression surgery may still lead to visual improvement [16, 17]. Surgical timing and preoperative visual acuity are generally considered key prognostic factors; early surgery and patients who retain some degree of light perception or better vision preoperatively tend to achieve more favorable outcomes [18, 19]. However, endoscopic optic nerve decompression demands a high level of anatomical knowledge and surgical skills from the surgeon. The surgical area is adjacent to important structures such as the internal carotid artery, cavernous sinus, and orbital apex vascular plexus, and anatomical variations are common [20]. The optic canal itself is deeply located with a narrow diameter, and its relationship with the sphenoid sinus and posterior ethmoid air cells is complex and variable. Particularly when combined with skull base fractures and disrupted anatomical landmarks, intraoperative localization and operative safety face serious challenges [21].

To address the challenges of such complex skull base anatomy, three‑dimensional visualization and virtual surgical planning technologies have emerged. The application of computer‑assisted surgery has expanded to encompass a complete workflow including virtual surgical planning, augmented and virtual reality, three‑dimensional printing, and intraoperative image validation [22]. In endonasal skull base surgery, three‑dimensional models constructed from preoperative fusion of CT and MRI data can help surgeons precisely plan the surgical trajectory and identify critical structures preoperatively, thereby enhancing surgical safety [23–25]. For orbital fracture reconstruction, three‑dimensional technology can more accurately assess fracture defect area and orbital volume changes, with measurements significantly superior to traditional two‑dimensional methods, providing a reliable basis for the design of personalized implants [26, 27]. Multiple studies have confirmed that virtual planning can significantly reduce the cognitive load of surgeons during preoperative planning and increase their confidence when facing complex anatomy [28, 29]. Open‑source medical image computing platforms such as 3D Slicer, with their powerful three‑dimensional reconstruction and visualization capabilities, have lowered the technical and cost barriers to three‑dimensional planning, enabling a wider range of medical centers to perform accurate preoperative simulations [30, 31].Notably, this platform has been utilized in the development of advanced surgical planning and guidance systems at leading neurosurgical research centers [32].

Although three‑dimensional visualization technology is increasingly used in skull base surgery, its value in endoscopic endonasal optic nerve decompression for traumatic optic neuropathy has not yet been systematically and comprehensively evaluated. Existing studies on the efficacy of optic canal decompression have mostly focused on debates regarding whether to operate, the timing of surgery, or combination drug therapy, with less attention paid to how to optimize the surgical procedure itself and improve its predictability and safety. In the field of orbital fracture reconstruction, three‑dimensional planning has been shown to improve postoperative functional outcomes [33, 34]; however, similar prospective comparative studies are still lacking in the area of optic canal decompression. Endoscopic endonasal optic nerve decompression is performed in a deep, narrow surgical field with complex and highly variable anatomy. The optic canal, sphenoid sinus, and internal carotid artery exhibit marked individual variation, and traumatic fractures frequently disrupt normal anatomical landmarks. Conventional two‑dimensional CT relies heavily on the surgeon’s mental spatial reconstruction, which imposes a high cognitive burden and elevates the risk of misjudgment—especially in less-experienced hands. Three‑dimensional visualization enables intuitive, 360° anatomical assessment, precise identification of high‑risk structures, and virtual rehearsal of the surgical trajectory, directly addressing these technical challenges.

However, a clear research gap persists. Most existing studies on optic canal decompression have focused on debates regarding surgical timing or adjuvant drug therapy, with limited attention to optimizing the surgical workflow itself through advanced visualization. Prospective, randomized evidence evaluating 3D‑based planning in this context is lacking, and cost‑effective, open‑source solutions such as 3D Slicer have not been systematically assessed.

Based on this, the present study aims to evaluate the value of preoperative planning using three‑dimensional models constructed with the open‑source software 3D Slicer in endoscopic endonasal optic nerve decompression for traumatic optic neuropathy through a prospective randomized controlled trial.

Methods and materials

Dataset

This study was a prospective randomized controlled trial that included patients diagnosed with traumatic optic neuropathy (TON) and scheduled to undergo endoscopic endonasal optic nerve decompression at the Affiliated Eye Hospital of Nanchang University between January 2023 and June 2025.

The study adopted a prospective, open-label randomized controlled design. Random allocation was performed by a staff member independent of this research project using a lottery method. Slips labeled “experimental group” and “control group” were prepared in advance, randomly placed into sequentially numbered sealed envelopes. After the patient signed the informed consent form preoperatively, the attending surgeon opened the envelope on the spot to reveal the group assignment. Accordingly, patients in the experimental group received 3D Slicer‑based three‑dimensional visualization preoperative planning, while those in the control group received conventional two‑dimensional CT planning. Due to the nature of the intervention (three-dimensional vs. two-dimensional planning), blinding could not be implemented for the surgical team. However, to minimize potential bias, patients were not informed of their specific study group assignment (experimental or control group). Furthermore, as described later, postoperative visual acuity assessments were conducted by an independent physician who was blinded to the group allocation.

The research team systematically collected clinical baseline data, high‑resolution orbital CT imaging data, surgical records (including operative time and complications), and postoperative follow‑up visual function assessment results from all patients. This study strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Eye Hospital of Nanchang University(Approval No. YLP20221229), and all enrolled patients provided informed consent and signed written consent forms.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: This study included patients with a definite history of craniofacial trauma who presented with post‑traumatic visual decline or visual field defects. The visual function impairment of the patients was confirmed by clinical examination to have ruled out the possibility of being caused by non‑traumatic intraocular or intracranial lesions (such as optic neuritis, optic atrophy, orbital tumors, etc.).

Exclusion criteria: This study excluded the following patients: (1) those with afferent pupillary defect caused by non‑traumatic factors (such as the aforementioned optic neuritis, optic atrophy, etc.); (2) those with concomitant ocular injuries that might affect the evaluation (such as globe rupture, retinal detachment); (3) patients with bilateral traumatic optic neuropathy (because they often have more complex craniocerebral injuries); (4) patients in whom the time from trauma to planned surgery exceeded 14 days (Exceeding the generally recognised effective surgical time window).

Research methods

Data collection and model construction

After enrollment, all patients signed informed consent forms and underwent thin-slice CT scanning of the orbit-sinus region on the same CT device at our hospital, with a slice thickness of 0.6 mm. All imaging data were exported and saved in DICOM (Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine) format.

The patient’s CT imaging data were imported into the open-source medical image processing software 3D Slicer (specific version number omitted). Subsequently, preprocessing of the imaging data was performed, including image resampling and image registration, to ensure consistent data resolution and optimize subsequent segmentation effects. In the image segmentation stage, the Segment Editor module was used to segment key anatomical structures. According to the imaging characteristics of different structures, a combination of threshold segmentation, region growing, and manual painting was employed for layer-by-layer fine segmentation.

The main segmentation targets included important anatomical structures such as the orbital bone, ethmoid air cells, sphenoid sinus air cells, optic canal, and internal carotid artery. After segmentation, the three-dimensional rendering function of the software was used to generate a three-dimensional surface model of each structure. Subsequent post-processing of the models included surface smoothing, color mapping, and opacity adjustment to enhance the spatial recognition among different anatomical structures. Finally, a complete three-dimensional anatomical model of the sinus-optic nerve-internal carotid artery was generated and exported in STL (Standard Tessellation Language) format for preoperative anatomical evaluation and virtual surgical planning. Preoperative planning

After the three-dimensional model was constructed for patients in the experimental group, the attending surgeon performed preoperative anatomical assessment and surgical path planning based on the three-dimensional visualization model. The surgeon could rotate, zoom, and cut the model in the three-dimensional environment for 360-degree observation, comprehensively analyzing the spatial relationships of key anatomical structures within the sinuses and orbit from multiple angles. First, the model was used to identify the locations of the natural ostia of the maxillary, frontal, and sphenoid sinuses and their anatomical variations. Subsequently, the surgeon focused on evaluating the following anatomical parameters in the three-dimensional model:

The pneumatization type of the sphenoid sinus;The presence of an Onodi air cell;The thickness of the bony wall of the optic canal and the presence of any bony defects;The protrusion of the internal carotid artery into the sphenoid sinus;The spatial relationship between the optic canal and the internal carotid artery.

For patients in the control group, traditional radiological assessment was used for preoperative planning. The attending surgeon evaluated the anatomical structures of the sinuses and orbit by reviewing two-dimensional CT film images and the axial, coronal, and sagittal images in the PACS (Picture Archiving and Communication System) system, combined with their own clinical experience, and formulated a conventional surgical plan accordingly.

Standardized medication protocol

All enrolled patients, upon confirmed diagnosis, received high-dose glucocorticoid pulse therapy as soon as possible. The specific regimen was as follows: Methylprednisolone 1000 mg/day, administered intravenously, for a continuous 3-day pulse therapy. For pediatric patients, the dose was calculated based on body weight (15–30 mg/kg), with a maximum daily dose not exceeding 1000 mg. During medication, patients’ systemic conditions (such as blood glucose, blood pressure, gastrointestinal symptoms, etc.) were closely monitored, and gastroprotective agents were co-administered to prevent potential adverse reactions including infection, gastrointestinal ulcer bleeding, elevated blood glucose or blood pressure, osteoporosis, and delayed tissue healing. Meanwhile, both groups of patients received a unified prophylactic antibiotic regimen (second-generation cephalosporin, administered 30 min preoperatively, and continued for no more than 48 h postoperatively based on infection risk) to prevent surgical site infections, along with routine neuro-nutritive and reparative therapy: Mouse Nerve Growth Factor for Injection (30 µg, once daily, intramuscular injection) for a 6-month course. The aforementioned standardized medication regimen—including glucocorticoid pulse therapy, antibiotic prophylaxis, and neuro-nutritive therapy—was identical for all patients in both groups during the perioperative period. This standardized pharmacological protocol minimized the influence of different drug treatments as a confounding factor on the evaluation of postoperative neurological function recovery, ensuring that any observed differences in outcomes between the groups were primarily attributable to the surgical intervention itself.

Surgical implementation and data recording

Surgeries for patients in both groups were performed by the same chief surgeon with extensive experience in endoscopic nasal surgery to minimize the impact of technical differences between different surgeons on the study results. During surgery, a dedicated person recorded relevant perioperative indicators, including: optic nerve identification rate – whether the optic nerve and its anatomical location were successfully identified during surgery. The comparison between the intraoperative reconstruction model and the surgical area anatomy under the nasal endoscope is shown in Fig. 3; operative time was defined as the interval from the insertion of the nasal endoscope into the nasal cavity to the complete exposure of the optic nerve during the decompression procedure; intraoperative complications – focusing on recording possible anatomical structure injuries during surgery, including (1) dural injury, (2) medial orbital wall injury, (3) cavernous sinus injury, (4) vascular injury (including the internal carotid artery or its branches), and (5) optic nerve injury. Postoperative surgeon scoring: the attending surgeon completed a questionnaire to rate the anatomical clarity, surgical fluency, and operative confidence of the surgery on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very difficult/unclear, 5 = very easy/clear).

All perioperative data were recorded in real time by members of the surgical team and uniformly compiled after surgery for comparative analysis between the two groups.

Preoperative and postoperative follow-up and visual assessment

All patients underwent standardized visual acuity assessment at the outpatient clinic three months after surgery.

To facilitate quantification and statistical analysis of the very low vision states before and after surgery, this study adopted the common grading system for traumatic optic neuropathy used in clinical research to grade and assign values to the best-corrected visual acuity [35].

The specific grading criteria were as follows:

Grade 1: No light perception.

Grade 2: Light perception.

Grade 3: Hand motion.

Grade 4: Counting fingers.

Grade 5: Ability to recognize the largest optotype on the standard international visual acuity chart (visual acuity ≥ 0.02).

Visual acuity assessment

Visual acuity assessment was performed by the same neuro-ophthalmologist who did not participate in the surgery and was blinded to the group allocation of the patients. The visual acuity grades of the patients were recorded preoperatively (at admission) and at three months postoperatively, and were used for intra-group (preoperative vs. postoperative) and inter-group (experimental group vs. control group) comparative analyses of treatment efficacy.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 8 software. Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical data were described as frequencies and percentages or as median (interquartile range) M(Q1, Q3). The significance level was set at α = 0.05, and two-sided tests were used; a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For baseline data, continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution (e.g., age) were analyzed using the independent-samples t-test; binary categorical data such as sex were analyzed using the chi-square (χ²) test; the intergroup comparison of preoperative visual acuity grade was performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. For the primary outcome measures: operative time was analyzed using the independent-samples t-test; intraoperative misjudgment rate and complication rate were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test; the surgeon’s subjective score was analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. For the secondary outcome measures: the intra-group comparison of visual acuity grade before and after surgery was performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test; the intergroup comparison of the grade of visual improvement (postoperative visual acuity grade minus preoperative visual acuity grade) was performed using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Results

General data analysis

This study included 48 patients with traumatic optic neuropathy (TON) who underwent endoscopic endonasal optic nerve decompression at the Affiliated Eye Hospital of Nanchang University. In the experimental group, the patients’ age was 33.5 ± 12.3 years (range, 13–55 years), and in the control group, the patients’ age was 30.1 ± 11.4 years (range, 15–46 years). There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in age (independent-samples t-test) or sex ratio (chi-square test) (P > 0.05). The difference in preoperative visual acuity grade between the two groups was not statistically significant [experimental group: M(Q1, Q3) = 1.0 (1, 2); control group: M(Q1, Q3) = 1.0 (1, 2); U = 262.0, Z = − 0.586, P = 0.558 (Mann-Whitney U test)], indicating overall comparability of baseline characteristics. The impact of surgery on vision improvement

Postoperative visual acuity improved significantly compared with preoperative values in both groups (experimental group: Z = − 3.493, P < 0.001; control group: Z = − 3.002, P = 0.003). The grade of visual improvement in the experimental group [M(Q1, Q3) = 1 (0, 2)] was compared with that in the control group [0.0 (0, 1)], and the difference was not statistically significant (Z = − 0.774, P = 0.439), as determined by the Wilcoxon signed‑rank test. Comparison of surgical duration between the experimental group and the control group

The operative time in the experimental group was significantly shorter than that in the control group. The operative time was 129.4 ± 11.6 min in the experimental group (n = 25) and 150.7 ± 20.4 min in the control group (n = 23). Comparison between the two groups using the independent-samples t-test showed that the difference was statistically significant (t = 4.401, P < 0.001), indicating that the operative time in the control group was significantly longer than that in the experimental group.

Intraoperative misjudgment rate and complication incidence

In the experimental group, the number of intraoperative misjudgments was 0, accounting for 0% (0/25); in the control group, the number of intraoperative misjudgments was 3, accounting for 13% (3/23). Fisher’s exact test was used for comparison, and the difference between the two groups was not statistically significant (P = 0.24), but the misjudgment rate was numerically higher in the control group.

Regarding postoperative cerebrospinal fluid leakage, there was 1 case in the experimental group and 5 cases in the control group. The complication rate was 4.0% (1/25) in the experimental group and 21.7% (5/23) in the control group. Comparison between the two groups using Fisher’s exact test showed that the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.08), but the complication rate was numerically higher in the control group.

In this study, the number of cases of major vascular injury (specifically injury to the internal carotid artery or its branches) and direct optic nerve injury was 0 in both groups.

Comparison of chief surgeon scores

The difference in the surgeon’s subjective scores between the two groups was statistically significant (Mann-Whitney U test: U = 121.5, Z = − 3.839, P < 0.001). The score in the experimental group [M(Q1, Q3) = 5 (5, 5)] was significantly higher than that in the control group [M(Q1, Q3) = 4 (4, 4)]. Discussion and outlook

Traumatic optic neuropathy (TON) is one of the important causes of irreversible vision loss after head trauma, with an incidence rate of approximately 0.5%–5% in patients with severe head injury [1, 2, 36]. Its clinical manifestation is often acute visual decline after trauma, and in severe cases, it can rapidly progress to complete blindness [37]. According to different injury mechanisms, TON is generally classified into direct injury and indirect injury, with the latter being more common and mainly caused by blunt force transmitted through the skull to the optic canal [7]. Due to the narrow bony structure of the optic canal segment, it is prone to fracture compression, local edema, and hematoma formation, leading to nerve ischemia and secondary axonal injury. Furthermore, increased intracanal pressure can cause microcirculatory disturbances, interruption of axoplasmic transport, and nerve degeneration, which are important mechanisms leading to irreversible visual function impairment [7, 38].

Current surgical interventions for traumatic optic neuropathy include transcranial approaches and endoscopic endonasal approaches, among which optic canal decompression is one of the main surgical strategies [5]. With the development of endoscopic techniques, endoscopic optic nerve decompression (EOND) has become an increasingly important and widely used surgical approach in clinical practice due to its minimally invasive nature and excellent surgical visualization [39]. However, because of the complex anatomy and significant individual variations in the nasal-sphenoid sinus-optic canal region, this procedure still faces many technical challenges. Previous studies have shown that the degree of sphenoid sinus pneumatization varies considerably, and anatomical variations such as protrusion of the optic canal into the sphenoid sinus or even bony wall defects can significantly increase surgical risks [40]. At the same time, the optic nerve is closely related to the internal carotid artery, and the two often have a complex relationship in the lateral wall of the sphenoid sinus, further increasing the intraoperative risk [5]. Moreover, endoscopic optic nerve decompression requires a high level of anatomical recognition ability from the surgeon, and intraoperative localization relies heavily on experience. If key structures are not clearly identified or improper manipulation is performed, serious complications may occur, including cerebrospinal fluid leakage, skull base injury, vascular injury, and iatrogenic optic nerve injury.

Endoscopic endonasal optic nerve decompression requires the surgeon to have a clear understanding of the patient’s anatomical structures. Currently, most hospitals rely on traditional two-dimensional imaging, while a few hospitals have introduced expensive image-guided navigation systems to assist surgery. However, two-dimensional images are difficult to interpret. Conventional CT or MRI images are primarily two-dimensional slices, and the surgeon needs to perform spatial reconstruction mentally, which demands a high level of experience and spatial cognition from the surgeon [41]. The lack of individualized anatomical models, along with significant variations in sphenoid sinus pneumatization and optic canal course among different patients, makes it difficult to comprehensively assess anatomical variations using only conventional imaging [42]. Therefore, establishing an intuitive and interactive three-dimensional anatomical model allows the surgeon to perform virtual surgical planning preoperatively, clearly identify anatomical landmarks, anticipate surgical difficulties and risk points, and formulate the optimal surgical approach and operative plan. This has significant clinical application value in improving surgical safety, shortening operative time, and reducing complications.

In recent years, image-guided surgery has been gradually applied in endoscopic skull base surgery. Navigation systems can improve surgical positioning accuracy and reduce the risk of injury to important structures [43]. However, there are still some limitations in current clinical application: the cost of navigation systems is relatively high, and commercial navigation devices are expensive, making them difficult to widely promote in some primary hospitals. This study constructed a three-dimensional model of the sinus-optic nerve-internal carotid artery based on the open-source software 3D Slicer and applied it to EOND surgical planning. Compared with studies relying on expensive commercial software or surgical navigation systems, this approach has extremely low cost and a relatively low technical threshold. Its utility in complex surgical planning has also been demonstrated in neurosurgical applications beyond the sino-nasal region [41].The research results are expected to be rapidly promoted in hospitals at all levels, offering strong universality and economic feasibility.

Previous studies on traumatic optic neuropathy have suggested that surgical outcomes are not only related to the timing of decompression and intraoperative maneuvers but also closely associated with preoperative high-quality radiological evaluation [44]. Therefore, introducing three-dimensional visualization modeling on the basis of standard CT assessment helps to more intuitively identify individual anatomical differences and optimize preoperative strategy formulation, thereby improving patient prognosis. As an open-source medical image computing platform, 3D Slicer can be used for image segmentation, three-dimensional reconstruction, and preoperative visualization processing [41]. Through a randomized controlled design, this study systematically evaluated the clinical value of the surgical strategy based on three-dimensional visualization for preoperative planning of optic nerve decompression [45, 46].

In this study, the operative time in the experimental group was significantly shorter than that in the control group (129.4 ± 11.6 min vs. 150.7 ± 20.4 min, P < 0.001). This result suggests that preoperative three‑dimensional visualization planning helps the surgeon become familiar with the individualized anatomical structures in advance, clarify the surgical approach and operative boundaries, thereby reducing the time spent on repeatedly identifying key structures during surgery and improving surgical efficiency. Regarding intraoperative misjudgment, the number of misjudgments was 0 (0%) in the experimental group and 3 (13.0%) in the control group. Although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.24), three‑dimensional visualization planning may help reduce errors in identifying anatomical structures during surgery. The incidence of cerebrospinal fluid leakage was numerically lower in the experimental group(4.0%, 1/25) compared to the control group(21.7%, 5/23), but this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.08). From an anatomical perspective, the three‑dimensional model may help reduce the occurrence of such complications by assisting the surgeon in identifying high‑risk areas preoperatively.

In this study, no rare serious complications such as major vascular injury or direct optic nerve injury occurred in either group. This may be related to the limited sample size and the surgeon’s extensive experience, but the value of three‑dimensional visualization in identifying dangerous anatomy and avoiding catastrophic events should not be overlooked. Regarding the surgeon’s subjective score, the median score in the experimental group was significantly higher than that in the control group [experimental group: 5 (5, 5) vs. control group: 4 (4, 4), P < 0.001]. The three‑dimensional visualization model allowed the surgeon to perform virtual surgical rehearsal preoperatively, enhanced the ability to spatially integrate the surgical approach and key anatomical structures, and reduced intraoperative cognitive load, thereby improving surgical fluency and confidence.

This study also has the following limitations. First, as a single-center study, the sample size was limited (total N = 48) due to practical constraints, which may have reduced statistical power. Consequently, although some outcome measures, such as the cerebrospinal fluid leakage incidence (experimental group 4.0% vs. control group 21.7%, P = 0.08), showed a clinically meaningful decreasing trend, they did not reach statistical significance. Second, this study has inherent limitations regarding the implementation of blinding due to the nature of the intervention. The operating surgeon was aware of the patient’s group assignment while performing three-dimensional or two-dimensional planning, which may have introduced expectation or performance bias in the assessment of the “surgeon’s subjective score.” Although objective measures such as operative time are less susceptible to this influence, this potential bias should be considered when interpreting the subjective scoring results. Furthermore, to control for the impact of inter-surgeon technical variation on the outcomes, all surgeries were performed by the same experienced chief surgeon. However, this also means that all intraoperative judgments (e.g., optic nerve identification) and subjective scores originated from a single observer, potentially introducing observer bias and affecting the reliability of the results. Future studies could involve a second surgeon, blinded to group allocation, to independently evaluate surgical recordings, thereby providing more objective intraoperative indicator data.

In summary, three‑dimensional visualization preoperative planning based on 3D Slicer in endoscopic endonasal optic nerve decompression can significantly shorten operative time, show a trend toward reducing the risk of cerebrospinal fluid leakage, and enhance the surgeon’s confidence. Moreover, this approach is low in cost and highly generalizable, demonstrating good clinical application value. This study is a single‑center, exploratory study with a limited sample size. Future larger‑sample, multicenter prospective studies are still needed to further validate the actual benefits of three‑dimensional visualization planning in reducing severe complications and improving long‑term functional outcomes.
