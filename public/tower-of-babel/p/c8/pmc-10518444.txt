Data on potential land redistribution beneficiaries in South Africa
Authors: Siphe Zantsi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractLand reform is one the controversial topics confronting the democratic South Africa, as such it remains top in the country's agenda. The South African land reform is implemented through three pillars – land restitution, land tenure reform and land redistribution. Land redistribution, which is the focus of our data, is more important since large tracks of land are envisaged to be redistributed through this pillar. Despite that land redistribution has been implemented for almost twenty-five years, progress to-date is disappointing. Numerous factors have been cited to explain the unsatisfactory results. Among these explanations and reasoning it appears that not much information that can enable formulation of sound and effective policies is known about the prime land reform beneficiaries, the commercial oriented smallholders. Because the household we surveyed are the prime target beneficiaries of land redistribution, we refer to them as “potential emerging farmers”. This term is drawn from the generic definition of land reform beneficiaries known as emerging farmers. The little that is known about potential emerging farmers is from scattered case studies to which little meaningful analysis and generalisations could be made. Other smallholder datasets such as Statistics South Africa's General Household Surveys are too generic in nature, offering too little details for understanding the potential land reform beneficiaries. Here we present a modestly detailed dataset of 761 surveyed farm household heads of potential emerging farmers. These are smallholder farm household heads who at least have sold a minimum of 20 percent of their produce in previous season. The data spans across three most smallholder populous provinces in South Africa, namely the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu Natal and Limpopo. These areas, the former homelands or marginalised areas that were designated for blacks during apartheid, house smallholders with high potential of becoming the next black commercial farmers. The data consists of socio-demographic information, farmer assets, production and production orientation, risk attitudes, aspirations and willingness to partake in land redistribution.Keywords: Survey, Land reform, Beneficiary, Agrarian reform

Data Description

The household survey data was conducted in 2017 from the Eastern Cape and KwaZulu Natal and 2018 in Limpopo. The raw data is compiled in Excel spreadsheet and can be accessed in Dataverse repository using the link at the last row of Table 1. The data can be divided into four sections namely, farmer characteristics, income and expenditure, production orientation and, land reform and risk. Farmer characteristics variables starts from column B to column K in the Excel spreadsheet. Income in column CH-CN, crop and livestock production cost in column BD-BG and BW-BX, respectively. All income and expenditure are quoted in South African rand 2018 values. Crop and livestock production can be found in columns BR-BU. Head counts are used in livestock variables while for land hectares are used. Land reform and risk variables can be found in columns CB-CG and CP-CQ. The land reform and risk variables are concerned about how much land size respondent want and their willingness to relocate from where they currently live to the commercial farms, while the risk variables measure the risk associated with relocation and managing new farm enterprises which they have little or no experience in managing. The risk is measured on a scale from 1-10.

The reported data was captured with a semi-structured household questionnaire compiled in English and translated into local languages. A questionnaire was administered to a farm household head if s/he had sold at least 20 per cent of their produce in the previous season and was willing to participate in the survey. A farm household is a household that produces crops and or animals on a small-scale and not on a private land as do commercial farmers. The aim was to survey only the previously disadvantaged black farmers who farm on communal tenured land because these are the potential land reform beneficiaries according to the South African land reform policy. The survey covers smallholder farm households in the three provinces (Eastern Cape, KwaZulu- Natal, Limpopo) of South Africa. Table 1 provides a distribution of the data across provinces and district municipalities.

The summary of key selected variables is presented in Table 2. For convenience, the data is categorised into four key variable groups, namely, farmer characteristics, income and expenditure, production orientation, and land reform and risk. In the first grouping the data shows a familiar South African farmer demographics, male dominated, middle aged and secondary education [3]. What is rather not often reported though is the farmer skill set and whether a farm is full time or part times, which are important factors in selecting land reform beneficiaries.

In general, there is a great variation in the survey households and this variation is shown by higher standard deviations. In the second grouping, income and expenditure of potential land reform beneficiaries shows that a year these households earn more than R9 000 and there are optimistic in the sense that they think they can double their current income in the next five to ten years. The future commercial farmers spent significant amount (on average about R4500) in purchasing inputs for both crop and livestock. These inputs include fertiliser, seeds, labour and animal remedies contributing to the growth of agribusinesses. However, very few (only 12 %) own tractors and many hire tractor services for tilling crop lands.

In terms of livestock holding presented in the third grouping, the future commercial farmers own mostly larger sheep (mean= 32) herd and small cattle (mean=11) and goat (mean=10) herds. Pigs are owned by a few and in small numbers, however, those who only specialised in pig keeping larger flocks. In general, the survey household are inclined towards selling as per the condition for participating in the study although their degree of commercialisation differ between individual and by farm activity i.e. crop vs livestock. Degree of commercialisation is also shown by the number of hired labourers (on average 2), substantiating the optimism of the National Development Plan of job creation through agriculture in 2030 [5].

In the last category, the data captured one of the most ignored features of potential land reform beneficiaries, which are essential for success in commercial farming. These factors include risk attitudes and willingness to relocate and participate in land redistribution [6]. While there has been more emphasis on a bottom-up land reform approach, certain aspect of the beneficiaries has been assumed, for example, their willingness to relocate from their homestead farm to commercial farms [7]. The data shows that a majority (66 %) of the surveyed farms feel constrained farming in communal land and a vast majority (61 %) are willing to relocate and wants a modest land size (an average of 95 ha) where they want to plant maize, vegetables and keep small ruminants (see Fig. 1). The latter is detailed in the supplementary data. Their risk appetite is modest, implying that they are at least willing to take calculated risk, an intrinsic part of farming.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The data was collected through a multi-stage sampling in the form of a household survey with the aid of a questionnaire administered face-to-face using local languages in the Eastern Cape, KwaZulu Natal and Limpopo. The multi-stage involved selection at provincial level, district municipality level, village level and at household level. Purposive sampling was employed to select provinces and district municipalities, while villages and households were selected randomly. The provinces and districts within the provinces were chosen purposively because they have high density of smallholders [3], while at village and household level random sampling was used to select villages and households. Extension officers and industry experts were consulted for getting information on the number of villages per municipality. After getting the list of villages, each village was assigned a number and lottery random selection from a hat was employed to select ten per cent of the villages. At the village level we would skip four homesteads and knock into the fifth homestead to ask permission to interview the household head or the oldest member of the family in the absence of the household head. This is after informing the respondent about the purpose of the study and what information we would need from the respondent, also highlighting that the participation in the study is voluntary and information cannot be linked to respondents all results will be anonymised except where permission has been sought from the respondent. This was a repetition of what we did to the village authorities, either a chief or headmen in other instances a ward committee, where we introduced ourselves and explained the purpose of the study.

Adapted from previous studies [8], a condition for selecting commercially oriented households was if they had sold at least 20 per cent of their produce in the previous season. A group of enumerators who are fluent in the local languages were recruited and trained by the principal researchers and the questionnaire was translated to the local languages to ensure accuracy of responses. The questionnaire had four sections, the socio-economics information part, land and crop production, livestock production as well as risk and aspiration section. We would normally take a break after completing two sections if we sense fatigue from a respondent. This helped to get the focus of the respondent after the break where we would talk about the respondent's success stories for example, complements on beautiful homestead. The questionnaire would take between 30-45 minutes to complete. It was completed by researchers and enumerators as many respondents were not familiar with some terms, this was also done to speed-up the interview, so the respondent could only focus on answering not on writing. The responses were then coded in excel, cleaned, and validated by the principal researchers. STATA version 13 was used to do the descriptive statistics of the survey and tables and charts are used to present the data. One can perform numerous analyses with this dataset, for example computing profitability of farmers by multiplying production yields with current prices obtainable from commodity organisations like Grain South Africa and subtracting all production costs incurred. An inference causal econometric analysis could be possible, where you regress the number of agricultural trainings attended as a dependent variable with the profitability variable and other socioeconomic variables as independent variables to establish if agricultural trainings have any correlation with profitability of farmers using the Ordinary Least Square model.

Limitations

While the dataset is one of the largest datasets on smallholder farmers in South Africa, it is however, not a perfectly well representative sample of smallholders in the country. Due to the financial limitations, the author was able to interview 761 households. It is therefore, recommended that more survey be done to build and extend from the existing datasets.

Ethics Statement

Ethical approval was obtained from Stellenbosch University's ethics committee prior data collection. The ethics clearance number: REC-2017-1856. As this study involved humans (communal farmers), the major ethical issues that are most relevant and were address are: informed consent, confidentiality, fatigue, and honesty in reporting research findings. All these were considered and addressed accordingly. The farmers were all informed of the study objective and participation was strictly voluntarily and respondents had a choice to pull out of the study any time, breaks were taken whenever respondents felt tired, please see the ethics letter of approval attached.
