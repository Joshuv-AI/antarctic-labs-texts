Psoriasis-like disease prevents squamous skin tumor development by neutrophil-driven inflammation
Authors: Panagiota Tsokkou, Martin Holcmann, Katharina Rindler, Kamil Mieczkowski, Lisa Shaw, Matthias Farlik, Maria Sibilia, Erwin F. Wagner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis study challenges the prevailing view that chronic inflammation is uniformly tumor-promoting. By demonstrating that psoriasis-like inflammation prevents cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma development in different mouse models, it provides mechanistic insights into how sustained immune activation can instead limit cancer susceptibility. Clinically, these findings have implications for risk stratification in patients with chronic inflammatory skin diseases and highlight the need to consider how immunomodulatory therapies may impact cancer risk. More broadly, the study suggests a therapeutic opportunity to harness inflammation-induced senescence pathways for cancer prevention.

Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory skin disease affecting 2 to 3% of the global population (1). A dysregulated crosstalk between epidermal keratinocytes and infiltrating immune cells underlies disease pathogenesis and mediates an unresolved skin inflammation, which eventually manifests in a systemic inflammatory disease affecting distant organs (2, 3). As such, increased prevalence of several comorbid diseases has been associated with psoriasis, including psoriatic arthritis, cardio-metabolic syndromes, and certain cancers (4). Among these, cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) has gained significant attention with several epidemiological studies indicating an increased incidence and overall risk in patients with severe or long-standing psoriasis (5–7).

Chronic inflammation is a well-established hallmark of cancer with many paradigms of tumor-promoting mechanisms (8). The DMBA/TPA model of skin tumorigenesis is the most widely used experimental system for studying cSCC initiation and progression, in which chronic inflammation plays a central role. A single exposure to the polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon 7,12-dimethylbenz(a)anthracene (DMBA) induces oncogenic Ras mutations in keratinocytes, while the subsequent and repeated topical applications of the inflammation-promoting agent 12-O-tetradecanoylphorbol-13-acetate (TPA) selectively mediates the survival and clonal expansion of mutated cells. Benign tumors referred to as papillomas are then formed, and many of them eventually progress to malignant cSCC (9). Although oncogenic RAS signaling is not involved in the pathogenesis of psoriasis, downstream pathways such as the MAPK/ERK and NF-κB/AP-1, along with STAT3 and the signaling cascade PI3K/AKT/mTOR are aberrantly activated, thereby mediating the uncontrolled keratinocyte hyperproliferation and resistance to apoptosis (10).

Despite emerging evidence indicating the potential link between psoriasis and cSCC, the causality and magnitude of this relationship is still elusive. While patients with severe psoriasis may develop cSCC, the majority of tumors and precursor lesions such as actinic keratoses, do not appear within the psoriatic plaques, but rather in the nonlesional or photodamaged skin (11, 12). These clinical observations are known as the skin cancer paradox of psoriasis for which it was proposed that psoriatic keratinocytes undergo a senescence-like state induced by disease-related cytokines and as such, become irreversibly growth arrested and resistant to malignant transformation (13, 14). This paradox challenges the conventional protumorigenic role of chronic inflammation, indicating that under certain conditions, inflammatory responses may activate cell intrinsic and/or extrinsic tumor suppressive mechanisms. Hence, the dualistic role of inflammation in cancer highlights the need for a better understanding of tissue-specific contexts and studies investigating the molecular mechanisms governing cancer risk and susceptibility.

Several genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) have been generated to model psoriasis and provide mechanistic insights into disease pathogenesis (15–24). The conditional and epidermal-specific c-Jun/JunB double knock-out (DKO*) mice recapitulate many of the histopathological and immunological features of psoriasis, including psoriasis-like arthritis (16, 20–21). Due to the multifactorial and complex nature of psoriasis, the use of complementary disease models is essential. The K23 model, hereafter referred to as R23*K14, in which IL-23 is conditionally overexpressed in keratinocytes, largely mimics the skin and joint phenotypes observed in DKO* mice, while the conditional and epidermal-specific CARD14E138A gain-of-function model, hereafter referred to as CARD*K14, reflects the contribution of human mutations (23, 24).

In this study, we have experimentally challenged the skin cancer paradox of psoriasis by subjecting different inducible GEMMs of psoriasis to cell transplantation and chemical-induced models of cSCC. Our study provides functional evidence indicating inhibition of cSCC formation in psoriasis-like disease, linked to neutrophil activation and induction of senescence and/or growth arrest in keratinocytes.

Results

Psoriasis-Like Disease Suppresses Squamous Skin Tumor Development.

To investigate whether and how chronic skin and systemic inflammation in psoriasis affect squamous skin tumor development, the R23*K14 and CARD*K14 inducible GEMMs of psoriasis-like disease were subjected to a cSCC transplantation model. At week 5 post tamoxifen (TAM) administration and psoriasis-like disease induction, PDVC57B cSCC cells were intradermally injected into the back skin of R23*K14 and CARD*K14 mice and tumor volume was monitored for 8 wk (Fig. 1A). During the first 4 wk post cSCC cell injection, tumor growth was comparable between the R23*K14 and CARD*K14 mice and their littermate controls. However, at week 5 post cSCC cell injection, tumor volume started to decline in R23*K14 mice, with a significant reduction at week 6 and onward (Fig. 1 B and C). Tumor shrinkage was positively correlated with psoriasis-like disease progression, as indicated by a higher Psoriasis-like Area Severity Index (PASI) score in R23*K14 mice compared to CARD*K14 mice and controls (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B). Microscopic and molecular analyses of the back skin at endpoint, revealed a strong inflammatory response in psoriasis-like R23*K14 mice compared to CARD*K14 mice and controls. This was characterized by aberrant expression of K6 in interfollicular epidermis and K14 in suprabasal layers, enhanced inflammatory pSTAT3 signaling in keratinocytes, a robust immune cell infiltrate, as well as increased expression of psoriasis-related mediators, including TNF-α, IL-17A, IL-23, IL-22, IL-1β, IL-18, and IL-6 cytokines, IFN-γ, angiogenic VEGF, LCN2 antimicrobial peptide, S100A8/A9 alarmins, and CXCL1/2 chemokines (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 C and H). These data suggest that severe inflammatory responses in psoriasis-like disease negatively control cSCC growth. To further test this, we next injected cSCC cells in older R23*K14 mice that had already developed moderate-to-severe psoriasis-like disease (PASI score 3 and 4) at the time of injection (Fig. 1E). In line with the first set of experiments, tumor volume significantly declined from 5 wk onward in R23*K14 compared to CARD*K14 mice, which developed milder inflammation (PASI score 0 to 2) (Fig. 1 F and H). Taken together, these data indicate that in the context of severe psoriasis-like disease, skin inflammation suppresses cSCC development.

Psoriasis-Like Disease Prevents Squamous Skin Tumorigenesis.

Tumor cell transplantation models do not accurately recapitulate the early and stage-dependent events of tumor formation. Hence, to further investigate the role of psoriasis-like inflammation in a physiologically relevant model of cSCC initiation and progression, we next subjected the R23*K14 and CARD*K14 GEMMs of psoriasis-like disease to the chemical DMBA/TPA model of skin tumorigenesis (Fig. 2A). Over the course of 16 wk post DMBA administration, R23*K14 mice developed significantly fewer papillomas compared to CARD*K14 mice and littermate controls, as indicated by a lower overall incidence and reduced absolute numbers of papillomas (Fig. 2 B and C). Specifically, only approximately 40% of R23*K14 mice developed at least one papilloma at endpoint. Notably, R23*K14 mice developed more severe psoriasis-like disease compared to CARD*K14 mice as reflected by a higher PASI score and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) (Fig. 2 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), both of which were negatively correlated with papilloma growth (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C). To support these findings, we extended our studies in two additional inducible GEMMs of psoriasis-like disease. Consistent with R23*K14, both the DKO*K5 and DKO*K15 models were highly resistant to the DMBA/TPA carcinogenesis protocol in contrast to their littermate controls, with over 50% of mice remaining papilloma-free at endpoint (Fig. 2 F and G). Psoriasis-like disease severity was comparable between the DKO*K5 and DKO*K15 mice, and both the PASI and NLR scores correlated negatively with papilloma numbers (Fig. 2 H and I and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D and F). Importantly, papillomas in psoriasis-like GEMMs did not appear within the lesional skin but rather in nonaffected skin areas. These data show that severe psoriasis-like inflammation in different GEMMs prevents DMBA/TPA-induced skin tumorigenesis.

Enhanced Keratinocyte Senescence and Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype (SASP) Expression in Psoriasis-Like Skin.

To understand how severe psoriasis-like skin inflammation prevents squamous skin tumorigenesis, we first evaluated epidermal abnormalities in the lesional back skin of R23*K14 and DKO*K15 GEMMs at 16 wk post DMBA administration. These two GEMMs exhibited most consistent disease phenotypes and were therefore selected for further studies. Compared to controls, psoriasis-like epidermis of both R23*K14 and DKO*K15 mice displayed enhanced keratinocyte activation and proliferation as indicated by increased Ki67 expression, mainly in basal but also suprabasal layers, and K6 expression in interfollicular epidermis (Fig. 3 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). Aberrant suprabasal expression of K14 was also evident, indicating altered epidermal differentiation, which was further validated with an overall increased, yet uneven accumulation of K10- and K1-positive keratinocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and C–E). Additionally, severe impairment of the skin barrier was observed, characterized by increased and substantially disrupted expression of loricrin (LOR) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and F). Given that hyperproliferation and rapid differentiation can impose replicative stress in keratinocytes, we next investigated changes in cell cycle regulation and progression. In sharp contrast to controls, increased expression of p16Ink4a, p21Cip1, and p53 was detected in psoriasis-like epidermis of R23*K14 and DKO*K15 mice (Fig. 3 A and C–E). Elevated levels of these factors are commonly associated with transient cell cycle arrest and cellular senescence in response to oncogenic and/or inflammatory stress (25, 26). Interestingly, protein levels of γH2AX, a marker of DNA double-strand breaks and genomic instability, were comparable between psoriasis-like and control epidermis, indicating that cell cycle arrest is not driven by overt DNA damage, but rather oxidative stress and/or inflammatory signaling (Fig. 3 A and F). In line with this, immunostaining showed that psoriasis-like keratinocytes exhibited enhanced inflammatory pSTAT3 signaling, a hallmark of psoriasis and SASP (Fig. 3 A and G). Furthermore, protein analysis of total skin extracts revealed a significant increase in SASP-related components in R23*K14 and DKO*K15 mice compared to controls, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 cytokines, IFN-γ, angiogenic VEGF and CCL2 and CXCL1/2 chemokines, along with additional psoriasis-related mediators such as IL-18, IL-22, TGF-β, G-CSF, CXCL5, and CCL3 (Fig. 3H). Surprisingly, protein levels of IL-17A, IL-23, IL-36β, and C3 were comparable between psoriasis-like and control skin, while IL-15 and TSLP were significantly elevated only in R23*K14 and DKO*K15 mice, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S3H). Moreover, protein levels of IL-4 and IL-10 cytokines were unchanged in all groups, while S100A7, CXCL10/12, and CCL5/11 chemokines were significantly increased only in psoriasis-like skin of DKO*K15 mice, indicating a distinct immune cell infiltrate in contrast to R23*K14 and control mice. Nevertheless, no difference in the total number of immune cells was observed between psoriasis-like DKO*K15 and R23*K14 mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and G). These results suggest that, in the context of severe psoriasis-like disease, keratinocyte senescence induced by oxidative and/or inflammatory stress may contribute to tumor prevention in the DMBA/TPA model of skin tumorigenesis.

Marked Neutrophil Infiltration in Psoriasis-Like Skin.

We next defined the immune cell infiltrate in psoriasis-like and control skin by flow cytometry (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Notably, neutrophils were the most abundant cells in psoriasis-like skin compared to control, consisting of up to 40% and 20% of total immune cells in R23*K14 and DKO*K15 mice, respectively (Fig. 4B). A significant increase in the numbers of CD4 T cells was also observed, however less profound compared to neutrophils (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Interestingly, CD8 T cells were significantly reduced, possibly due to neutrophil activation, while γδHigh dendritic epidermal T cells were replaced by infiltrating γδLow T cells. Furthermore, a significant increase in the numbers of monocytes and a substantial decrease in the numbers of macrophages was observed in DKO*K15 mice but not in R23*K14 mice, which had comparable numbers with their respective controls. Similarly, pDCs were slightly, yet significantly, increased in DKO*K15 compared to R23*K14 mice, whereas cDC1s and cDC2s were decreased in both psoriasis-like models compared to controls. Finally, while there was no difference in the numbers of B cells in DKO*K15 and control mice, a small but significant reduction was observed in R23*K14 mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Importantly, marked neutrophil infiltration in psoriasis-like skin of both the R23*K14 and DKO*K15 mice was validated by immunostaining. Neutrophils were predominantly accumulated in the dermis and, to a lesser extent, in the epidermis, forming the classical psoriasis-related microabscesses (Fig. 4C). Consistent with neutrophil numbers, protein analysis of total skin extracts showed a significant increase in cytotoxic myeloperoxidase (MPO) and neutrophil elastase (NE), as well as antimicrobial LCN2 and S100A8/A9 alarmins, all of which are related to neutrophil activation (Fig. 4D). Of note, protein levels of citrullinated H3, a common marker for neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, were only increased in psoriasis-like skin of DKO*K15 mice compared to R23*K14 and control mice (Fig. 4D). These data indicate that infiltrating neutrophils may play a significant role in preventing tumor development in psoriasis-like skin, either directly by exhibiting cytotoxic and phagocytic activity or indirectly by mediating inflammatory signaling to keratinocytes.

Keratinocyte Senescence Is Associated with Activation of CD54–Expressing Neutrophils at the Tumor Initiation Stage.

In psoriasis-like mice, papilloma incidence is reduced at a very early stage, indicating that there are important alterations in the skin prior to visible papillomas. Thus, we next determined the immune cell infiltrate in psoriasis-like and control skin at 5 wk post DMBA administration, an early time point before papilloma onset. For this analysis, we used only the DKO*K15 mice that develop consistently severe skin inflammation as early as 2 wk post psoriasis-like disease induction (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). Consistent with the endpoint data, neutrophils were identified as the main immune cell population infiltrating psoriasis-like skin with up to a striking 80-fold change in the percentage of total live cells in DKO*K15 mice compared to controls (Fig. 5A). Apart from γδHigh dendritic epidermal T cells, which were reduced in psoriasis-like skin due to severe inflammation, other myeloid and lymphoid populations were unaffected at this time point, thus further indicating a significant role of neutrophils in mediating disease pathogenesis and possibly prevention of papilloma growth (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). Of note, increased numbers of neutrophils were also observed in the blood and spleen of DKO*K15 mice compared to controls, along with elevated serum levels of granulopoiesis-associated G-CSF, GM-CSF, and IL-6, as well as neutrophil chemoattractant CXCL1/2/5 and CCL3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 B and D).

To gain insight into the functional role of infiltrating neutrophils in psoriasis-like skin, we first analyzed the expression of key adhesion and activation molecules by flow cytometry. Interestingly, about 50% of psoriasis-like neutrophils were CD54–positive, a phenotype associated with enhanced phagocytosis and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production (27) (Fig. 5A). Consistent with cell numbers, mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) analysis also showed increased expression of CD54 in neutrophils infiltrating psoriasis-like skin compared to controls, along with upregulation of CD11b and CD16, whereas expression levels of CD62L, PD-L1, and CXCR4 remained unchanged (Fig. 5B). Importantly, while there was no difference in the expression of protumorigenic CD117 between neutrophils in psoriasis-like and control skin, expression levels of CD177, a marker associated with tumor-suppressive neutrophils (28) were significantly upregulated (Fig. 5B). Protein levels of MPO, NE, and LTB4 as well as antimicrobial LCN2 and S100A8/A9 were also elevated in the skin and serum of DKO*K15 mice compared to controls, while citrullinated H3 was only significantly increased in the skin, indicating a possible tissue specific signal for NET formation (Fig. 5C and SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). Neutrophil accumulation and NETs in psoriasis-like skin were then validated by immunostaining, while protein analysis of total skin extracts further revealed an increased expression of psoriasis- and SASP-related mediators consistent with the endpoint data (Fig. 5 D and E). However, at this time point, protein levels of IL-17A, IL-23, IL-36β, and C3 were elevated in psoriasis-like skin compared to controls, whereas CCL2/5 remained unchanged (SI Appendix, Fig. S5F). Importantly, while psoriasis-like epidermis of DKO*K15 mice exhibited enhanced keratinocyte proliferation, a profound increase in p16Ink4a-positive keratinocytes was also observed compared to controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 G and H and Fig. 5 F and G). Interestingly and in contrast to the endpoint data, p16Ink4a expression was not only detected in the nucleus but also in the cytoplasm of keratinocytes, many of which might have undergone differentiation as indicated by a K10 immunostaining in consecutive tissue sections (Fig. 5F). Of note, psoriasis-like skin displayed resistance to apoptosis as indicated by reduced epidermal cleaved caspase 3 activity in DKO*K15 mice compared to controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 G and I). Taken together, these data further support a tumor suppressive role of neutrophils in psoriasis-like skin, associated with enhanced cytotoxicity and/or oxidative stress-induced senescence/growth arrest in keratinocytes.

Transcriptome Profiling Identifies Keratinocyte and Neutrophil Dynamics in Psoriasis-Like Skin.

Next, we sought to investigate the crosstalk between infiltrating neutrophils and keratinocytes in psoriasis-like skin as a key mediator of tumor prevention. As such, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing on cell suspensions from the back skin of DKO*K15 and control mice at 5 wk post DMBA administration (Fig. 6A). Clustering analysis of a total of 14,746 cells (5,414 in control and 9,339 in DKO*K15 samples) revealed 13 distinct cell types with transcriptional profiles annotated according to canonical cell markers (Fig. 6B, SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B, and Dataset S1B). In line with our previous findings, quantifying the proportional change of the identified cell types revealed the biggest shift in neutrophils between DKO*K15 and control samples, followed by keratinocytes and fibroblasts (Fig. 6C). Notably, the neutrophil cluster showed expression of marker genes consistent with an inflammatory, N1-like phenotype, including Il1b, Cxcl2, Acod1, Cxcl3, and Ccrl2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B and Dataset S1B). Given that neutrophils were mainly present in the skin of DKO*K15 mice, differential gene expression analysis between the two experimental conditions was not feasible (Dataset S1H). Thus, to understand the functional role of neutrophils in psoriasis-like skin, we then performed a second level subclustering on the neutrophil population in DKO*K15 samples, which revealed two distinct subsets of N1-like neutrophils, termed hereafter as N1a and N1b (Fig. 6D). Interestingly, differential gene expression analysis between N1a and N1b cells showed 129 genes of whom 113 were up- and down-regulated in N1b compared to N1a cells, possibly due to a different maturation or activation state (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C and Dataset S1C). Nevertheless, both the N1a and N1b subsets expressed genes related to oxidative burst and ROS production, such as Ifrd1, Rac2, and Msrb1 (29–31), as well as cytotoxic granule and inflammatory mediator secretion, including Pglyrp1 and Alox5ap (32, 33), which further indicates an inflammatory, N1-like phenotype related to antitumorigenic functions (Fig. 6D). Importantly, expression of known N2-like marker genes (34) Il10 and Arg1 was not detectable, in contrast to Tgfb1 and additional N1-like marker genes Tnf and Icam1 which were comparably expressed between N1a and N1b cells (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, enrichment analysis (35) of the top differentially expressed genes revealed that the N1a subset was mainly characterized by interleukin signaling, expression of cell cycle regulatory genes, and secreted proteins contributing to the SASP (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). In contrast, the N1b subset was characterized by enrichment terms indicative of neutrophil activation and effector functions, including neutrophil degranulation, (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). Of note, assessment of segregation between the two populations revealed a continuous trajectory, consistent with a gradual transcriptional transition rather than two discrete states (SI Appendix, Fig. S6E). Moreover, differential gene expression analysis along pseudotime identified Tmsb4x and Nlrp3 as the most dynamically regulated genes, implicating cytoskeletal remodeling and inflammasome-associated pathways in this continuum. These findings indicate that N1a and N1b constitute functionally diverging activation states, rather than segregated or strictly sequential temporal stages (SI Appendix, Fig. S6E and Dataset S1D).

Following the high proportional change of N1-like neutrophils in DKO*K15 mice, we next analyzed the communication pathways from and to neutrophils using CellChat analysis (36). Considering that neutrophils were significantly low in numbers in the skin of controls, only de novo cell–cell communication pathways from neutrophils to all other cell types in psoriasis-like skin of the DKO*K15 mice were detected, with the notable exception of muscle cells (Fig. 6E and Dataset S1F). Of note, most probable communication in psoriasis-like skin was observed between the neutrophils and macrophages/dendritic cells, followed by a cell autocrine signaling, as well as communication with endothelial cells, pericytes, fibroblasts, and keratinocytes (Fig. 6E).

Given that tumor formation originates in epithelial cells, we next focused the CellChat analysis on keratinocytes. First, we defined the different keratinocyte populations, in order to validate the changes in proliferation and senescence/growth arrest that we have previously observed in DKO*K15 mice. Similar to neutrophils, we performed second level subclustering, identifying 12 subsets with distinct transcriptional profiles (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B and Dataset S1G). Quantitatively, we observed an increase of relative abundance for the KC2 and KC3 subsets (epidermal basal cells), as well as KC8 and KC10-12 subsets (hair follicle cells) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C and Dataset S1H). In line with our previous findings, cell cycle phase analysis on the different subsets revealed an overall decrease in the number of cells in the G2M or S phase and a concomitant increase of cells in the G1/G0 phase in DKO*K15 samples compared to controls (Fig. 6F). Specifically, all keratinocyte subsets in psoriasis-like skin were significantly observed in the G1/G0 phase in contrast to controls, apart from KC2, KC3, and KC4 epidermal proliferating basal cells (Fig. 6F and SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). In addition to this, gene expression analysis revealed that a substantial proportion of cells coexpressed Mki67, Trp53, and Cdkn1a (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). Of note, Krt5 expression was evenly distributed between control and DKO*K15 samples, whereas Krt14 expression was partially expressed in a subset of Krt10-positive cells. Importantly, many of Cdkn1a-positive cells lacked Krt10 expression, further supporting a distinction between growth arrested and differentiated cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). Interestingly, CellChat analysis in all keratinocytes revealed upregulation and de novo signaling to neutrophils in DKO*K15 alone and a significant reduction in cell–cell interactions to all other cell types compared to control samples, indicative of a wide-spread remodeling of the keratinocyte communication network (Fig. 6G). Specifically, keratinocyte-to-neutrophil communication in psoriasis-like skin of DKO*K15 mice involved activation of the Annexin A1 and Notch signaling pathways, both of which are associated with enhanced survival, migration, adhesion, and activation of neutrophils (Fig. 6G and Dataset S1F). In contrast, neutrophil-to-keratinocyte communication was enriched for Semaphorin 4 (SEMA4) signaling, suggesting a role in promoting inflammatory responses in keratinocytes (Fig. 6E and Dataset S1F).

To define which signaling pathways are differentially expressed in DKO*K15 compared to controls, we next assessed the information flow from and to keratinocytes (Fig. 6H). Importantly, pathways known to drive cell cycle and are involved in cSCC tumorigenesis, including the epidermal growth factor (EGF), leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) and the canonical Wnt/β-catenin (WNT) signaling pathways, were absent or significantly decreased in the DKO*K15 mice compared to controls. Absence of e-cadherin (CDH1) signaling was also observed in keratinocytes of DKO*K15 mice, along with a significant reduction in periostin and laminin signaling, all of which are known to be associated with premature senescence and SASP expression (37, 38). Of note, SASP-related and inflammatory Il1b, Cxcl2, Ccl3, and Csf1 were predominantly expressed in neutrophils compared to keratinocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S7E). These findings reveal widespread alterations in cellular composition and intercellular communication networks in psoriasis-like skin of DKO*K15 mice compared to controls, with inflammatory N1-like neutrophils orchestrating communication with multiple cell types. Importantly and consistent with our previous findings, keratinocytes in psoriasis-like skin exhibited increased signatures of growth arrest and/or senescence, alongside reduced expression of protumorigenic signaling pathways.

Neutrophil Depletion Ameliorates Psoriasis-Like Skin Inflammation and Restores Tumor Growth.

Immune cell profiling and single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that neutrophils are dramatically increased in psoriasis-like skin, reshaping cell–cell communications and potentially inducing senescence/growth arrest in keratinocytes which in turn, prevents tumor growth. To functionally assess whether neutrophil activation indeed contributes to tumor prevention in psoriasis-like skin, we efficiently depleted circulating neutrophils in DKO*K15 mice by anti-Ly6G administration for 3 wk after disease onset and prior to papilloma development (Fig. 7A and SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). Psoriasis-like disease was notably ameliorated in DKO*K15 mice treated with anti-Ly6G compared to isotype controls, as indicated by lower PASI scores and reduced ear thickness on day 14 and day 28 post first antibody treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 B and C). Importantly, during the TPA course of 15 wk, papilloma numbers were comparable between anti-Ly6G-treated DKO*K15 and untreated control mice, while isotype-treated controls developed only a few papillomas at endpoint (Fig. 7 B and C). Consistent with the amelioration of psoriasis-like disease at the time of neutrophil depletion, DKO*K15 mice treated with anti-Ly6G developed overall a milder psoriasis-like disease compared to isotype controls, as indicated by a lower PASI score and NLR at endpoint (Fig. 7 D and E). This was further confirmed by histological analysis in which, epidermal thickness of psoriasis-like skin was significantly reduced in anti-Ly6G-treated mice compared to isotype controls, along with the numbers of Ki67-positive proliferating keratinocytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 D and F). Furthermore, the levels of K10 and LOR were significantly reduced, and their expression pattern was normalized (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 D, G, and H). Histological analysis also revealed a less profound immune cell infiltrate in psoriasis-like skin of anti-Ly6G-treated mice with milder disease compared to the marked cell infiltration observed in isotype-treated mice with severe disease (SI Appendix, Fig. S8D). In line with this, flow cytometry demonstrated a substantial reduction in neutrophil infiltration in psoriasis-like skin of anti-Ly6G-treated mice, with up to a 20-fold decrease in the percentage of total live cells, in contrast to isotype-treated controls (Fig. 7F). Consistent with cell numbers, cytotoxic MPO and antimicrobial LCN2 and S100A8/A9 were diminished in psoriasis-like skin of anti-Ly6G-treated mice compared to isotype controls, along with the neutrophil chemoattractant CXCL1/2/5 and CCL3, as well as granulopoiesis-associated G-CSF and GM-CSF (Fig. 7 F and G). Additionally, protein levels of several psoriasis- and SASP-related mediators, including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6 cytokines, IFN-γ, TGF-β and angiogenic VEGF were significantly reduced in psoriasis-like skin of anti-Ly6G-treated mice compared to isotype controls (Fig. 7H). Surprisingly, IL-23 was significantly elevated in psoriasis-like skin of isotype-treated mice in contrast to anti-Ly6G-treated mice, which had comparable protein levels with untreated controls, while IL-17A remained unchanged in all groups (Fig. 7H). Interestingly, dendritic cells were increased in the back skin of anti-Ly6G-treated mice compared with isotype controls, which may be related to IL-23 expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S8I). In contrast, monocytes, macrophages, and T cells were significantly reduced in anti-Ly6G-treated mice compared with isotype controls, consistent with reduced neutrophil numbers and the amelioration of psoriasis-like disease (SI Appendix, Fig. S8I). Importantly, keratinocyte senescence indicated by p16Ink4a expression was completely abolished in psoriasis-like skin of DKO*K15 mice treated with anti-Ly6G compared to isotype controls (Fig. 7I). These findings functionally show that infiltrating neutrophils in psoriasis-like skin mediate disease pathogenesis, including oxidative and/or inflammatory stress-induced senescence in keratinocytes.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that severe chronic skin inflammation in psoriasis-like disease suppresses cSCC development and prevents DMBA/TPA-induced skin tumorigenesis. Mechanistically, we identify neutrophils as the key players, infiltrating psoriasis-like skin likely in response to increased expression of chemokines such as CXCL1/2/5 and CCL3, as well as the alarmins S100A8/A9 (21). In the skin, neutrophils exhibit N1-like effector functions, including the release of cytotoxic and inflammatory mediators and the formation of NETs. We propose that neutrophils either directly eliminate tumor cells and/or induce oxidative and inflammatory stress in keratinocytes, leading to senescence and/or growth arrest, thereby preventing tumor growth (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Functionally, early systemic depletion of neutrophils attenuated psoriasis-like inflammation, abolished the senescence-like phenotype in keratinocytes, and restored tumor growth, thereby confirming their pivotal role in disease pathogenesis and tumor prevention.

Although chronic inflammation is mainly considered to be tumor-promoting, psoriasis-like inflammation appears to inhibit tumor growth. This is consistent with findings by Cipolat et al., where chronic skin inflammation prevented DMBA/TPA-induced squamous tumor formation in a mouse model of atopic dermatitis; a phenotype associated with TSLP-mediated immune responses (39). In contrast, chronic inflammation in a discoid lupus erythematosus mouse model promoted aggressive cSCC associated with a tumor-supportive microenvironment enriched with M2-like macrophages and inflammatory mediators such as TGF-β and IL-6 (40). These studies highlight the context-dependent interplay between chronic immune activation and skin cancer susceptibility, where distinct inflammatory niches and/or milieus can either inhibit or promote tumor growth.

Paradoxically, several key inflammatory mediators associated with psoriasis pathogenesis, such as IL-17 and IL-23, have established tumor-promoting roles in the context of cSCC. For instance, Wu et al., described an IL-17R/Act1/TRAF4/MEKK3/ERK5 circuit that directly stimulates tumor formation, while Wang et al., showed that IL-17 promotes DMBA/TPA-induced skin tumorigenesis via an IL-6/STAT3 axis (41, 42). Similarly, Langowski et al., demonstrated that genetic deletion and antibody-mediated neutralization of IL-23 reduced tumor incidence, while Teng et al., revealed that IL-23 suppresses innate antitumor immunity with knock-out mice being protected against tumor initiation and metastasis (43, 44). In our GEMMs, although protein levels of IL-17A and IL-23 were elevated at the tumor initiation stage, the senescence-like phenotype in keratinocytes, possibly due to early neutrophil infiltration and activation, appears to overcome any concurrent IL-17A- and IL-23-driven proliferative and tumor-promoting signals. This is in line with our single-cell RNA sequencing data, which revealed a profound change in keratinocyte signaling at tumor initiation stage, involving reduced responsiveness in key oncogenic pathways, including EGF and WNT, which in turn might contribute to tumor prevention. Furthermore, IL-17A and IL-23 protein levels were comparable between psoriasis-like and control skin harboring tumors at the endpoint, but not at the early time point, further indicating a tumor-promoting role.

Cellular senescence represents a major tumor-suppressive mechanism in the skin, acting as a barrier to malignant transformation by halting the proliferation of damaged and/or stressed keratinocytes (31, 45). In the context of cSCC, senescence can be induced by oncogenic stress, DNA damage, or inflammatory mediators, leading to an irreversible growth arrest (46, 47). Our findings suggest that neutrophil-mediated oxidative and/or inflammatory stress in psoriasis-like skin may promote senescence and/or growth arrest in both hair follicle-derived and interfollicular keratinocytes harboring DMBA-induced mutations. This process may therefore inhibit cSCC development despite the enhanced proliferation observed in basal keratinocytes. In addition to early neutrophil activation, the pro-inflammatory cytokine TNF-α and the immunomodulatory cytokine TGF-β that were both significantly upregulated in psoriasis-like skin are well-established inducers of cellular senescence. Specifically, TNF-α has been shown to activate NF-κB and p38 MAPK signaling pathways in keratinocytes, promoting DNA damage responses and the establishment of a senescence-associated growth arrest (48, 49). Similarly, TGF-β signaling induces cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors such as p16Ink4a and p21Cip1, thereby triggering a durable senescent state in keratinocytes (31, 50). Importantly, the use of senolytic drugs to selectively eliminate senescent cells provides a powerful experimental approach for future experiments.

The phenotypic diversity and plasticity of neutrophils in the context of cancer have been extensively documented, with distinct subsets that either promote or suppress tumor growth (51). We propose that in psoriasis-like skin, activated N1-like neutrophils eliminate cancer cells and/or induce a senescence-like phenotype in premalignant keratinocytes, either directly by cell–cell interaction or indirectly by releasing neutrophil-derived enzymes and/or inflammatory and oxidative mediators. In fact, human polymorphonuclear neutrophils, exhibiting an N1-like profile, can specifically recognize and kill cancer cells via direct cytotoxicity (52), while NE has been shown to selectively kill cancer cells and attenuate tumorigenesis by degrading critical tumor survival factors (53). Interestingly, Lagnado et al., has recently shown that neutrophils can directly trigger the senescence of neighboring epithelial cells by transmitting ROS and inducing telomerase dysfunction (54). Furthermore, TRPM2 channel modulation of neutrophil chemoattraction to tumors (55) and innate immune training, which enhances granulopoiesis, can further bolster neutrophil antitumor activity (56).

Therapeutic strategies aiming to harness or activate neutrophil functions for cancer treatment have shown promising results (57). Our findings suggest that enhancing neutrophil recruitment or modulating their activation could be an avenue to induce senescence and/or growth arrest, thereby preventing cutaneous carcinogenesis. Targeted neutrophil-activating therapies might thus complement existing immunotherapies, particularly in tumors prone to developing resistance. Importantly, clinical and/or epidemiological studies defining cancer incidence and risk should be carried out on specific psoriasis subtypes, such as pustular psoriasis (58), as well as other neutrophilic diseases, where neutrophils are abundantly present and may also exert tumor-protective effects.

In summary, our study provides functional evidence linking psoriasis-like disease to reduced cSCC development. While we show that neutrophils contribute to antitumor responses in this setting, our findings also suggest that their activity occurs within the broader landscape of chronic skin inflammation and sustained immune activation. These processes may therefore promote cytotoxic and oxidative stress, inflammatory signaling, and immune crosstalk which in turn, induces senescence and/or growth arrest in keratinocytes. This context-dependent antitumor effect highlights the complexity of chronic inflammatory states and suggests that under certain conditions long-term immune activation can restrain rather than promote tumor development. Importantly, our data underscore the role of disease-specific immune microenvironments in shaping cancer risk and susceptibility with implications for cancer prevention.

Materials and Methods

Detailed methods are provided in SI Appendix. Briefly, psoriasis-like disease was induced in 8-wk-old mice by intraperitoneal injections of TAM (1 to 2 mg) or RU486 (2 mg) for 5 consecutive days. Tumorigenesis was induced by a single topical application of DMBA (50 μg) in the back skin of 6-wk-old mice, followed by weekly topical applications of TPA (4 μg) for the duration of 15 wk.
