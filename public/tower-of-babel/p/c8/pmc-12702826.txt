Vendor‐Agnostic Vision Transformer‐Based Artificial Intelligence for Peroral Cholangioscopy: Diagnostic Performance in Biliary Strictures Compared With Convolutional Neural Networks and Endoscopists
Authors: Ryosuke Sato, Kazuyuki Matsumoto, Masahiro Tomiya, Takayoshi Tanimoto, Akimitsu Ohto, Kentaro Oki, Satoshi Kajitani, Tatsuya Kikuchi, Akihiro Matsumi, Kazuya Miyamoto, Yuki Fujii, Daisuke Uchida, Koichiro Tsutsumi, Shigeru Horiguchi, Yoshiro Kawahara, Motoyuki Otsuka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesAccurate diagnosis of biliary strictures remains challenging. This study aimed to develop an artificial intelligence (AI) system for peroral cholangioscopy (POCS) using a Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture and to evaluate its performance compared to different vendor devices, conventional convolutional neural networks (CNNs), and endoscopists.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 125 patients with indeterminate biliary strictures who underwent POCS between 2012 and 2024. AI models including the ViT architecture and two established CNN architectures were developed using images from CHF‐B260 or B290 (CHF group; Olympus Medical) and SpyScope DS or DS II (Spy group; Boston Scientific) systems via a patient‐level, 3‐fold cross‐validation. For a direct comparison against endoscopists, a balanced 440‐image test set, containing an equal number of images from each vendor, was used for a blinded evaluation.ResultsThe 3‐fold cross‐validation on the entire 2062‐image dataset yielded a robust accuracy of 83.9% (95% confidence interval (CI), 80.9–86.7) for the ViT model. The model's accuracy was consistent between CHF (82.7%) and Spy (86.8%, p = 0.198) groups, and its performance was comparable to the evaluated conventional CNNs. On the 440‐image test set, the ViT's accuracy of 78.4% (95% CI, 72.5–83.8) was comparable to that of expert endoscopists (82.0%, p = 0.148) and non‐experts (73.0%, p = 0.066), with no statistically significant differences observed.ConclusionsThe novel ViT‐based AI model demonstrated high vendor‐agnostic diagnostic accuracy across multiple POCS systems, achieving performance comparable to conventional CNNs and endoscopists evaluated in this study.Keywords: artificial intelligence, bile duct neoplasms, cholangioscopy, computer‐assisted diagnosis, vision transformer

Introduction

Bile duct cancer is an increasingly prevalent disease with poor prognosis, making accurate and timely diagnosis crucial for effective management [1]. While surgery is necessary for the treatment of bile duct cancer, benign biliary strictures such as immunoglobulin G4‐related sclerosing cholangitis (IgG4‐SC) can be managed medically, highlighting the importance of differentiating between the two pathologies [2]. However, differentiating these conditions using conventional imaging techniques such as computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can be challenging. Peroral cholangioscopy (POCS) enables direct visualization and biopsy, offering diagnostic accuracy rates of 77.2% alone and 86.5% with biopsy [3]. However, the limitations of POCS‐guided biopsy, such as access challenges, small forceps size, and specimen quality issues, underscore the need for accurate image interpretation [4]. Unfortunately, the limited availability of POCS and skilled interpreters hinders its standardization and widespread use.

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have shown potential for assistance in endoscopic diagnoses, demonstrating performance comparable to expert endoscopists [5, 6]. In gastrointestinal endoscopy, AI applications have been successfully used for the real‐time qualitative diagnosis of lesions, including the assessment of gastric cancer depth [7] and detection of colonic polyps [8]. Similarly, in the field of POCS, AI models using convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have been reported to predict benign and malignant outcomes from POCS images and videos with high accuracies, exceeding 90% in some studies [9, 10].

However, significant challenges regarding the implementation of AI‐assisted POCS diagnosis remain. A key concern is the variation in image characteristics between different POCS systems [11]. The POCS market is dominated by two major vendors (Olympus Medical, Tokyo, Japan, and Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA), with new vendors having recently entered the market. These established systems employ distinct optical technologies and image‐processing algorithms, potentially affecting the generalizability of AI models trained on data from a single vendor. However, there is limited research directly comparing the diagnostic performance of AI systems using these widely used POCS platforms. Additionally, systematic comparisons of AI performance compared to expert and non‐expert endoscopists using these systems are lacking, leaving uncertainty regarding their real‐world clinical utility.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate a novel, vendor‐agnostic AI system using a Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture for evaluating biliary strictures using POCS images. To establish its performance and clinical utility, we compared its diagnostic accuracy against both conventional CNNs and endoscopists with varying levels of experience.

Methods

Patient Selection and Study Design

We retrospectively enrolled 132 patients diagnosed with indeterminate biliary strictures who underwent POCS at the Okayama University Hospital between January 2012 and October 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age > 20 years; (2) hilar or distal bile duct stenosis; and (3) a bile duct diameter > 8 mm, which is necessary for POCS insertion. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) cases with unrecorded POCS images; (2) technically unsuccessful procedures; and (3) a definitive pathological diagnosis could not be established owing to an unsuccessful biopsy or poor specimen quality.

POCS Procedure and Image Collection

All POCS procedures were performed during endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) under conscious sedation. A duodenoscope (TJF‐240, TJF‐260V, or TJF‐Q290V; Olympus Medical) was used to access the papilla, followed by either endoscopic sphincterotomy or papillary balloon dilation. For cholangioscopy, either a CHF system (CHF‐B260 or B290) or a SpyGlass system (SpyScope DS or DS II) was used via the mother‐baby technique. All procedures were performed by a team of two expert endoscopists, each with experience of over 500 ERCPs. While inserting the cholangioscope into the bile duct, strictures were visualized by bile aspiration and saline irrigation. Approximately 10–30 cholangioscopic images of bile strictures were recorded, followed by 10–120 images of the non‐strictured areas. After visual assessment, at least three biopsies were obtained from the stricture sites. To enable image classification based on anatomical origin and lesion status, POCS images were systematically captured from both strictured and predefined non‐strictured areas, such as the hilar stricture and distal bile duct in cases of hilar bile duct cancer. For non‐strictured areas, at least three biopsies were obtained from each area required for negative mapping biopsies. The final diagnosis for all biliary sites was established based on the most definitive evidence available. For strictured areas, this was POCS‐guided biopsy or surgical histopathology. For non‐strictured areas, diagnoses were based on surgical pathology if resected, direct biopsy when performed, or careful visual correlation with an adjacent, pathologically confirmed benign reference site. To further validate all benign or inconclusive biopsy results, patients underwent a minimum 1‐year clinical and imaging follow‐up to confirm the absence of malignancy. Secondary biliary strictures were specifically defined as stenosed bile ducts resulting from identifiable causes such as post‐operative changes, post‐radiation therapy, or chronic pancreatitis. This category is distinct from other specific benign inflammatory conditions, such as primary sclerosing cholangitis and IgG4‐SC. The diagnosis of any benign stricture was established through a combination of clinical history, imaging, and biopsy results that excluded malignancy. For the AI's binary classification task, images of malignant strictures were labeled “malignant.” Images of secondary biliary strictures and other benign strictures were collectively considered “benign strictures” and subsequently labeled “benign” with images from non‐lesion areas.

Algorithm of the Artificial Intelligence System

The AI system was designed to analyze white‐light POCS images rather than narrow band imaging (NBI). Images were excluded from the datasets if they met any of the following criteria: (1) poor quality; (2) difficult visual characteristics, such as obscuration by bile or bubbles; or (3) presence of obstacles, such as guidewires or forceps. Importantly, the images from both the CHF and SpyGlass systems were combined into a single dataset without vendor‐specific segregation. This approach was intentionally chosen to train the AI model on a diverse range of image characteristics inherent to different systems. By learning from this mixed‐vendor dataset, the model is encouraged to identify fundamental pathological features common across platforms, rather than features specific to a single vendor. Unlike previous POCS AI systems that have employed only CNNs, our study implemented and directly compared three distinct architectures. Our primary model was a ViT‐Base/16 model. For a head‐to‐head comparison, we also implemented two well‐established CNN architectures, Residual Network 50 (ResNet50) and Visual Geometry Group 16 (VGG16), which were trained and evaluated under identical conditions [12, 13]. ViT achieves a high image recognition accuracy by treating images as sequences of patches and applying transformer‐based self‐attention mechanisms [14], representing a fundamental difference from conventional CNN approaches like ResNet50 and VGG16. To ensure a robust and unbiased evaluation, we implemented a patient‐level, stratified 3‐fold cross‐validation. The 125‐patient cohort was partitioned into three folds stratified by final diagnosis and POCS vendor, with all images from a single patient belonging to only one fold. Within each cross‐validation iteration, the two folds designated for training were further partitioned at the patient‐level into a final training set (80%) and a validation set (20%). The model was trained on the training set, and its hyperparameters, such as learning rate and epoch selection, were tuned to achieve the best performance on the validation set. Finally, the model was retrained on the entire training pool using these optimal parameters and evaluated on the held‐out test set. All models were pretrained on ImageNet‐21k and subsequently fine‐tuned using PyTorch with a learning rate of 0.0001 over 100 epochs. Input images were standardized to 512 × 512 pixels, and we performed extensive data augmentation using random cropping and cycle‐consistent generative adversarial network (CycleGAN) [15, 16], which converted white‐light images into virtual indigo carmine‐sprayed images to improve surface visualization [17, 18]. For the final head‐to‐head comparison against endoscopists, a separate 440‐image benchmark test set was constructed by sampling images from the held‐out test sets of the patient‐level, stratified cross‐validation folds, thereby avoiding data leakage. This test set was carefully balanced by POCS vendor and lesion type (Table S1) and highly representative, containing images from 124 of our 125 patients. This study employed an internal validation framework; the primary performance estimate was derived from the 3‐fold cross‐validation, while the 440‐image set served as a benchmark for direct comparison with endoscopists.

Diagnosis by Endoscopists

Six endoscopists, blinded to clinical data, independently reviewed the same 440‐image test set used for AI evaluation. The images were presented in a random order for classification as benign or malignant. The reviewers were divided into two groups: three experts with over 10 years of experience and at least 500 ERCPs, and three non‐experts with extensive general but limited biliary endoscopy experience. To prevent bias, endoscopists did not assess cases they had originally performed. Following the independent review, a final consensus diagnosis for each group was determined through discussion to compare with the AI system's performance.

Outcome Measurements

Continuous variables are expressed as means or medians with interquartile ranges. The primary outcomes were diagnostic performance metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curves (AUCs) were calculated using Python. Statistical comparisons of diagnostic accuracy between groups, such as AI versus endoscopists or between vendor systems, were performed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) to appropriately handle clustered data from the same patient. To account for this data structure, 95% CI for all performance metrics were also generated using a patient‐level cluster bootstrap method. Inter‐observer agreement among the endoscopist groups was evaluated using Fleiss' kappa statistics. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using JMP software version 17.0 and Python version 3.11 in a Google Colaboratory environment.

Results

Patient Characteristics

The clinicopathological characteristics of the 125 patients included in this study are summarized in Table 1. The cohort had a median age of 74 years and was predominantly male (89 patients, 71.2%). The most common presenting symptom was jaundice (46 patients, 36.8%), and the most frequent stricture site was the hilar bile duct (80 patients, 64.0%). Final diagnoses revealed malignancy in 97 patients (77.6%), most commonly hilar or distal bile duct cancer. Benign conditions were diagnosed in 28 patients (22.4%), of which secondary biliary stricture was the most frequent diagnosis.

Study Population and Image Dataset

A patient flow diagram is shown in Figure 1; 125 patients and 3402 POCS images were available for the initial review. We further excluded 1340 unsuitable POCS images containing obstacles or devices such as bubbles, guidewires, and forceps, resulting in 2062 POCS images (824 malignant and 1238 benign) for analysis. Of these, 1458 images were acquired using the CHF group (CHF‐B260 or B290) and 604 images were acquired using the Spy group (SpyScope DS or DS II). All images were expanded with online data augmentation using random cropping (×4225) and CycleGAN (×2), resulting in 17,423,900 images. Representative POCS images are shown in Figure 2, depicting typical findings such as irregular surfaces for malignant strictures and the characteristic oval depression known as the dimple sign [19, 20] in non‐lesion areas.

Overall AI Performance From Cross‐Validation

The primary, most robust estimate of the AI models' generalization performance was derived from the patient‐level, 3‐fold cross‐validation on the entire 2062‐image dataset, with detailed results presented in Table 2. Our primary model, the ViT, achieved an accuracy of 83.9% (95% CI, 80.9–86.7), a sensitivity of 81.3% (95% CI, 77.0–85.9), and a specificity of 85.6% (95% CI, 80.9–89.9). Importantly, the model's accuracy was consistent between the CHF system at 82.7% (95% CI, 79.0–86.2) and the SpyGlass system at 86.8% (95% CI, 82.1–91.1), with no statistically significant difference observed (p = 0.198). On the full dataset, the ViT's performance was comparable to that of the CNN models, with accuracies of 82.6% (95% CI, 79.3–85.7; p = 0.240) for ResNet50 and 81.2% (95% CI, 77.5–84.6; p = 0.065) for VGG16. The performance of the primary ViT model was consistent across the three individual folds of the cross‐validation, as detailed in Table S2. Furthermore, in a sub‐analysis focused only on malignant and benign stricture images (n = 1053), the accuracy of the ViT model at 77.3% (95% CI, 72.0–82.1) was comparable to that of ResNet50 at 75.8% (95% CI, 70.4–81.0; p = 0.415) and VGG16 at 75.9% (95% CI, 70.2–81.6; p = 0.517).

Benchmark Comparison With Endoscopists

For a direct head‐to‐head comparison, the AI models and six endoscopists were evaluated on the 440‐image benchmark test set, which was constructed by sampling from the held‐out test sets of the cross‐validation folds. The comprehensive results of this comparison are shown in Table S3. On this benchmark set, the ViT model's accuracy was 78.4% (95% CI, 72.5–83.8). This performance was comparable to that of the expert endoscopists, who achieved an accuracy of 82.0% (95% CI, 75.7–87.7), with no statistically significant difference observed (p = 0.148). The model's performance was also comparable to the non‐expert endoscopists' accuracy of 73.0% (95% CI, 67.1–78.2) (p = 0.066), as visualized in Figure 3. The ROC curve analysis for the ViT architecture yielded an AUC of 0.86, indicating excellent discriminatory ability as plotted on the ROC curve in Figure S1. Regarding diagnostic consistency, inter‐observer agreement among the three experts was substantial (Fleiss' Kappa = 0.68), while agreement among the three non‐experts was moderate (Fleiss' Kappa = 0.53).

Comparative diagnostic accuracy of artificial intelligence (AI) system, expert endoscopists, and non‐expert endoscopists across the total dataset, CHF group, and Spy group. Our Vision Transformer (ViT) system achieved an overall accuracy of 78.4% (95% CI, 72.5–83.8), with comparable performance in both the CHF group (77.7%, 95% CI, 70.9–84.2) and Spy group (79.1%, 95% CI, 69.7–88.3). No significant difference was observed between the two vendor platforms (p = 0.534). Expert endoscopists achieved slightly higher accuracy (82.0%, 95% CI, 75.7–87.7) than the AI system, while non‐expert endoscopists performed slightly worse (73.0%, 95% CI, 67.1–78.2), though neither difference reached statistical significance (p = 0.148 and p = 0.066, respectively). Furthermore, in the sub‐analysis comparing only malignant and benign strictures, the ViT model's accuracy of 73.3% was also comparable to that of the experts (76.4%, p = 0.352) and the non‐experts (70.6%, p = 0.463).

Discussion

This study introduces a novel ViT‐based AI for diagnosing POCS biliary strictures, validated in a multi‐vendor setting. The AI demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy of 83.9% in a robust, patient‐level 3‐fold cross‐validation on our entire 2062‐image dataset. On a separate 440‐image benchmark set used for direct comparison, the AI's performance was comparable to that of expert and non‐expert endoscopists, even in a sub‐analysis of the most challenging cases. These findings suggest its value as a robust diagnostic support tool, especially where expertise is limited.

Two key innovations distinguish our approach from previous studies. First, we developed and validated our AI system on a multi‐vendor POCS dataset from both Olympus Medical and Boston Scientific. A robust, vendor‐agnostic AI has profound clinical implications, as it could help standardize diagnoses and overcome expertise barriers as POCS technology becomes more widespread. Second, we implemented a ViT architecture instead of conventional CNNs used in prior studies [12]. By analyzing global relationships between image patches via self‐attention, ViT is well suited to recognize the complex visual patterns characteristic of malignancy. While novel to cholangioscopy, this architecture has shown promise in other endoscopic applications [21], and its attention‐based mechanism may capture vendor‐independent visual features more effectively than traditional CNNs [22].

Our ViT model's performance was comparable to the evaluated CNN architectures. However, its distinct advantage was its superior vendor‐agnostic performance, showing the smallest accuracy difference between Olympus and SpyGlass images. This enhanced consistency is likely attributed to the ViT's ability to discern fundamental disease patterns over superficial, device‐specific variations. This robustness has profound clinical implications; it can provide crucial diagnostic support where POCS‐guided biopsy is technically challenging and can also help standardize care by accelerating the adoption of POCS in diverse clinical settings, ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Prior studies on POCS‐specific AI have provided a foundation for comparison, with some CNN‐based models demonstrating high performance, such as achieving over 90% sensitivity for neoplasia detection in video analysis [23]. Other research has successfully applied AI for specific tasks like automatic tumor vessel detection [24] and visualizing AI decisions with heatmaps [25]. While these studies demonstrated the potential of AI in POCS, they primarily utilized CNNs [26] on single‐vendor systems, highlighting our contribution in exploring a ViT architecture to address multi‐vendor compatibility.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged, including the retrospective single‐center design, relatively small sample size, and inclusion of only two major POCS vendors. Regarding sample size, a formal priori calculation was not performed for this retrospective study of 125 patients. While 3‐fold cross‐validation was employed for more robust performance estimation from our dataset, we acknowledge this sample size may limit statistical precision for certain subgroup analyses and the broader generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, our evaluation relies on internal validation, as the benchmark set was not derived from an external cohort; therefore, the results from the full cross‐validation should be considered the most reliable estimate of performance, as the benchmark set results may be slightly optimistic. Also, the evaluation was based solely on still images rather than real‐time videos. Future studies should address these limitations through larger prospective trials and real‐time, multi‐vendor validation.

In conclusion, our ViT‐based AI model demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy comparable to conventional CNNs and endoscopists. Furthermore, its consistent performance across different vendor platforms highlights its potential as a robust, vendor‐agnostic tool for diagnosing biliary strictures, positioning it as a valuable tool to enhance diagnostic consistency across diverse settings.
