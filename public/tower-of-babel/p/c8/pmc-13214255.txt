Comparison of continuous single-layer and traditional interrupted suturing techniques in stoma surgery: a retrospective single-center study
Authors: Xiaoyu Hou, Chou Mo, Chong Xiong, Mingjin Li, Rui Luo, Guangfu Jiang, Deyu Zheng, Qin Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProphylactic ileostomy is frequently performed in colorectal surgery to mitigate the consequences of anastomotic leakage. The optimal fixation method, however, remains debated. We compared clinical efficiency and safety of continuous single layer versus traditional interrupted suturing for ileostomy fixation.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included consecutive patients who underwent prophylactic ileostomy fixation. Patients were grouped by fixation technique: continuous single-layer (n = 111) and traditional interruption (n = 63), as derived from the finalized case-level dataset compiled in this project. Baseline variables (age, BMI) and perioperative outcomes (stoma creation time, stoma takedown (mobilization) time, length of stay) were compared between groups. Stoma-related complications were analyzed as overall incidence.ResultsBaseline characteristics were comparable between groups (age, BMI; all p > 0.05). Compared with the interrupted method, the continuous single-layer technique was associated with a markedly shorter stoma creation time (median 17.4 vs. 28.4 min; p < 0.001) and a shorter stoma takedown (mobilization) time (median 28.0 vs. 39.5 min; p < 0.001). A small between-group difference in length of stay was observed; given potential confounding and perioperative heterogeneity, this should not be interpreted as a direct effect of the suturing technique. The overall stoma-related complication rate was similar between groups (dermatitis, prolapse, retraction, stenosis, necrosis, parastomal hernia; all p > 0.05).ConclusionUsing a continuous single-layer suture for ileostomy fixation shortened operative steps (creation and takedown [mobilization]) without increasing stoma-related complications compared with the traditional interrupted technique. A small reduction in hospital stay was observed but cannot be attributed solely to the suturing method. This approach is efficient and may represent an efficient alternative pending prospective validation.

Introduction

Protective ileostomy is a well-established procedure performed after low anterior resection for rectal cancer or other distal colorectal diseases to prevent anastomotic leakage [1]. By diverting fecal content, the stoma effectively mitigates the clinical consequences of leakage and reduces morbidity and mortality [2]. Despite these benefits, the creation and closure of an ileostomy introduce additional operative steps, prolong hospital stay, and may lead to stoma-related complications such as dermatitis, prolapse, or retraction [3]. Therefore, optimization of stoma fixation techniques remains clinically important to balance operative efficiency and postoperative safety.

Traditionally, the interrupted suturing method has been the standard approach for ileostomy fixation. Although secure, this method involves repeated knot-tying and step-intensive handling, which can increase operative time and cause uneven suture tension, potentially compromising local blood supply [4]. In contrast, continuous single-layer suturing offers several theoretical advantages—it maintains even tension, requires fewer knots, and simplifies the procedure [5]. As minimally invasive surgery and enhanced recovery programs become more prevalent, techniques that reduce operative time without compromising safety are gaining increasing attention.

Recent studies have evaluated various modifications of suturing techniques for stoma formation and closure. Lan et al. reported that a two-stitch continuous suture method significantly reduced ileostomy creation time compared with the traditional interrupted approach, without increasing complications [6]. Similarly, Chen et al. found that a modified gunsight suture technique during stoma closure decreased postoperative infection and hospital stay compared with the interrupted method [7]. These findings suggest that simplified continuous or modified suturing methods may improve operative efficiency while maintaining comparable safety outcomes.

However, most existing studies have been limited by small sample sizes or heterogeneous patient cohorts, and few have provided comprehensive quantitative analyses of perioperative parameters such as stoma creation time, stoma takedown (mobilization) time, hospital stay, and stoma-related complications. To address this gap, we conducted a single-center retrospective study comparing continuous single-layer and traditional interrupted suturing techniques for prophylactic ileostomy fixation. We hypothesized that the continuous method would significantly shorten stoma-related operative steps and might be associated with a shorter hospital stay, without increasing postoperative complications, while maintaining comparable short-term safety.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This was a single-center, retrospective study conducted at the Department of Gastrointestinal and Hernia Surgery, West China Guang’an Hospital, Sichuan University, between January 2020 and May 2025. A total of 174 consecutive patients who underwent prophylactic ileostomy following colorectal surgery were included. Patients were categorized into two groups according to the stoma fixation technique used: the Traditional interrupted suturing group and the Continuous single-layer suturing group. All patients had complete clinical and follow-up data. The choice of suturing technique was determined by institutional practice rather than patient characteristics. Before January 1, 2023, the traditional interrupted fixation was routinely used. After this date, the department adopted the continuous single-layer technique as a standardized modification. Therefore, the two groups represent sequential cohorts rather than randomized allocation. Patients were consecutive within each time period, and the unequal group size reflects the shorter duration of the continuous-technique period. The two techniques were not used simultaneously.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) emergency surgery, (2) previous stoma history, (3) severe intra-abdominal infection, or (4) incomplete perioperative data.

All data were anonymized before analysis, and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Guang’an Hospital, Sichuan University (Approval No. 2025051), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

Surgical techniques

All surgeries were performed by experienced colorectal surgeons under standardized operative protocols. Four colorectal surgeons performed the procedures, and all were familiar with both techniques. In the Traditional interrupted suturing group, a right lower-quadrant mini-laparotomy was made through the rectus (≈ 4 cm) at a flat skin area. A loop of ileum approximately 30 cm proximal to the ileocecal valve was delivered. A small opening was created adjacent to the posterior aspect of the loop, and a straight rigid plastic rod (≈ 5 mm diameter) was passed to support the loop; the bridge was removed 1–2 weeks postoperatively. The parietal peritoneum around the aperture was sutured to the ileal seromuscular layer and adjacent mesentery in a circumferential fashion using interrupted stitches. The subcutaneous tissue and skin at both ends of the incision were likewise anchored with interrupted sutures. The distal limb was then opened transversely with electrocautery, the mucosa everted, and the bowel edge was matured to the skin with circumferential interrupted everting stitches (fine monofilament). In the continuous single-layer suturing group, after the bowel loop was delivered, the limb designated for maturation was opened. Using a 3 − 0 absorbable suture, two primary anchoring sutures were placed at the 6 and 12 o’clock positions, incorporating the bowel full-thickness edge, seromuscular layer, and dermis. These anchoring sutures were reinforced with repeated knotting to stabilize eversion. A single circumferential suture was then performed using the same strand, incorporating the bowel wall and dermis in one layer to complete maturation. Because tension was evenly distributed along the running suture, no additional knots were required at intermediate positions (3 and 9 o’clock). The final stitch was tied back to the original anchoring site to secure fixation and ensure stable mucocutaneous apposition. This technique was standardized within the department before clinical implementation. A step-by-step illustration of the continuous single-layer maturation technique is shown in Fig. 1.

Definitions of operative times

‘Stoma creation time’ was defined as the interval from the first peristomal skin incision to completion of stoma maturation. ‘Stoma takedown (mobilization) time’ was defined as the interval from the first peristomal skin incision at closure to complete circumferential mobilization and freeing of the stoma loop (including adhesiolysis and mesenteric handling) before any bowel division or anastomosis construction. Total operative time was not used for a between-group comparison due to differences in anastomosis techniques at closure.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data were collected from the institutional electronic medical records, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), and underlying comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes). Intraoperative parameters included total operation time, stoma creation time, intraoperative blood loss, and surgical approach (open or laparoscopic). Postoperative outcomes included length of postoperative hospital stay, and stoma-related complications, such as infection, mucocutaneous separation, necrosis, prolapse, and retraction. Complications were assessed and confirmed by attending surgeons during hospitalization or postoperative follow-up. Patients were followed from stoma creation until stoma closure. Early complications such as necrosis or retraction were typically detected within postoperative day 1–2 during inpatient observation, while other complications were evaluated during routine outpatient visits before closure. The stoma takedown (mobilization) time at closure was prospectively abstracted from operative records according to the above definition and analyzed as a procedure-specific endpoint.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Python version 3.11 with the SciPy (v1.11) and Pandas (v2.2) libraries. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent samples t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All figures were generated using Python’s Matplotlib (v3.8) and Seaborn (v0.13) visualization libraries.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 174 patients were enrolled in this study, including 111 who underwent continuous single-layer stoma construction and 63 who received the traditional interrupted technique (Table 1). The two groups were generally well matched in demographic and clinical characteristics. The mean age was 64.5 ± 12.4 years in the continuous group and 61.1 ± 14.5 years in the interrupted group, with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.121). Similarly, mean body-mass index (BMI) values were 23.9 ± 2.8 kg/m² and 23.5 ± 2.8 kg/m², respectively (p = 0.415). Sex distribution and the proportions of benign and malignant diseases were comparable (p > 0.05). No patient presented with uncontrolled systemic comorbidities that might have influenced surgical outcomes. Overall, baseline comparability between the two groups was confirmed prior to outcome analysis.

Intraoperative outcomes

As summarized in Table 2, operative parameters differed significantly between groups. The stoma-creation time was markedly shorter in the continuous single-layer group (19.5 ± 9.2 min) compared with the traditional interrupted group (33.3 ± 15.8 min; p < 0.001, Fig. 2A). Likewise, the stoma takedown (mobilization) time was significantly reduced (29.2 ± 12.2 min vs. 40.7 ± 18.4 min; p < 0.001, Fig. 2B). This time reduction of approximately 30% highlights the technical simplicity and efficiency of continuous suturing. All procedures were completed successfully without intraoperative complications such as bleeding and bowel injury. No conversions or intraoperative revisions were required in either group and estimated blood loss was minimal and comparable between groups (p > 0.05). These findings indicate that the continuous single-layer technique was associated with shorter operative time without compromising surgical safety.

Postoperative recovery

The postoperative length of stay (LOS) was significantly shorter in the continuous group than in the interrupted group (13.7 ± 2.5 days vs. 14.9 ± 2.8 days; p = 0.004, Fig. 2C). Although this difference was modest, it may reflect smoother postoperative recovery; however, causality cannot be inferred from this retrospective analysis. The small difference between the two groups in hospital stay should be interpreted with caution. Length of stay is influenced by multiple factors—including ERAS adherence, perioperative analgesia, anastomosis configuration at closure, and discharge logistics—and a change in stoma fixation alone is unlikely to determine the entire hospitalization course. Accordingly, our LOS findings are exploring rather than confirmation.

Statistical considerations

All continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) as appropriate. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent t test or Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. These analyses indicate shorter operative time with comparable safety in the continuous group.

Discussion

The present study found that the continuous single-layer suturing technique for stoma creation and takedown (mobilization) is a safe and efficient alternative to the traditional interrupted method. By significantly shortening operative time while maintaining comparable postoperative outcomes, this simplified approach may help streamline the procedure and resource utilization of stoma surgery. These findings add observational data supporting the trend toward minimally invasive and standardized gastrointestinal reconstruction.

Technical efficiency and operative simplicity

Our analysis revealed that both stoma creation and takedown (mobilization) times were markedly shorter in the continuous single-layer group compared with the interrupted group. This finding is in line with previous clinical observations suggesting that continuous suturing may reduce operative steps while maintaining comparable anastomotic outcomes [8, 9].Continuous suturing distributes tension evenly along the incision, facilitating more uniform healing and reducing localized ischemia [10]. In addition, fewer suture interruptions mean fewer knots and less tissue manipulation, potentially decreasing intraoperative contamination risk. These factors could plausibly contribute to the shorter operative times observed in this study.

Postoperative recovery and safety

Despite its technical advantages, the continuous method did not increase the incidence of postoperative complications. The complication rate observed in our cohort falls within the range reported in previous studies [11, 12]. Notably, skin irritation and peristomal edema were less frequent in the continuous group, possibly due to less suture-induced tension and more uniform wound response. Severe events such as stoma retraction, prolapse, or necrosis were rare and equally distributed. These results suggest that continuous suturing achieves both mechanical integrity and good tissue perfusion, contributing to safer postoperative recovery. A modestly shorter hospital stay was observed in the continuous group; however, given potential confounders and discharge logistics, causality cannot be inferred.

Comparison with previous literature and possible mechanisms

Several studies have compared single-layer and double-layer intestinal closure techniques in different surgical settings.

For example, Harries et al. [13] and Parini et al. [14] showed that single-layer continuous closure yields similar leakage rates, but significantly shorter operative times compared with interrupted sutures. Our findings extend these observations to stoma surgery, providing clinical evidence that continuous suturing is also advantageous in enterostomy management.

Mechanistically, continuous suturing may reduce local ischemic stress and microleakage risk by maintaining uniform tension and preserving mucosal alignment [15]. The uniform pressure distribution may also minimize wound-edge inversion, supporting faster mucosal healing. Taking together, the present study is consistent with continuous single-layer closure optimizes both surgical efficiency and tissue healing.

Limitations and future perspectives

The present cohort had a relatively low mean BMI compared with Western populations. Adequate eversion depends on mesenteric length and tension rather than BMI alone. In patients with short messages or excessive tension, interrupted fixation may remain preferable. Therefore, extrapolation of these findings to populations with higher BMI should be made with caution.

The present findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal because of the retrospective design. The observed differences may reflect procedural efficiency but do not establish superiority. This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective analysis, which may introduce selection bias. In addition, procedures were performed by multiple surgeons and inter-surgeon variability was not analyzed, which may have influenced operative time. Because the technique was applied according to time period rather than randomization, selection bias cannot be excluded. Second, although baseline characteristics were well balanced, unmeasured confounders—such as surgeon experience or intraoperative decision-making—could have influenced outcomes. Third, follow-up duration was limited, and long-term stoma function or late complications (such as hernia formation) were not assessed. Future multicenter, prospective randomized trials with longer follow-up are warranted to validate these findings and explore patient-reported outcomes. Additionally, resource utilization and quality-of-life analyses should be included to better assess the real-world clinical value of the continuous single-layer technique. Finally, the analysis was not adjusted for all potential confounders of length of stay; therefore, any observed difference in hospitalization cannot be attributed solely to the suturing technique.

Summary

In summary, our study found that the continuous single-layer suturing method was associated with shorter operative time; a modestly shorter hospital stay was observed but causality cannot be inferred; and it maintains a comparable safety profile to the traditional interrupted technique.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the continuous single-layer suturing technique was associated with shorter stoma creation and mobilization time compared with the interrupted technique, without a statistically significant difference in stoma-related complications. Because of the retrospective sequential design, causality cannot be established. These findings suggest improved procedural efficiency with comparable short-term safety; however, prospective multicenter studies are required before broader clinical implementation can be recommended.
