Knowledge of cholera, handwashing practice and its associated factors in Hayayaabe IDP camp, Borama, Somaliland: a community-based study
Authors: Mohamed Said Hassan, Mohamoud Abdulahi, Abdirahman Ibrahim Said, Farhan Abdisamad Osman, Hassan Sh Abdirahman Elmi, Ayan Husein Korse, Hana Mahdi Dahir, Muhiadin Ismail Abdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCholera, a severe diarrheal disease transmitted through contaminated water and food, poses a significant public health threat, particularly in vulnerable populations. Inadequate knowledge and poor hygiene practices are major drivers of its transmission. This study aimed to assess cholera knowledge and handwashing practices among residents of the Hayayaabe Internally Displaced Persons (IDP) camp in Borama, Somaliland.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in the Hayayaabe IDP camp (500 households) between April and June 2024. Data were collected from 251 randomly selected participants using structured questionnaires and analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and multivariable logistic regression in SPSS 26 and Stata 16 to identify factors associated with cholera knowledge and handwashing practices.ResultsOnly 25% of respondents demonstrated good knowledge of cholera, and 26.3% reported good handwashing practices. Multivariable analysis revealed that older age (AOR 0.07, 95% CI: 0.005–0.943), source of information (AOR 0.042, 95% CI: 0.003–0.556), and higher education (AOR 9.129, 95% CI: 0.999–84.29) were significantly associated with cholera knowledge. Factors considerably influencing handwashing practices included older age (AOR: 0.026, 95% CI: 0.002–0.418), sex (AOR: 0.072, 95% CI: 0.014–0.363), family income (AOR: 71.604, 95% CI: 18.787-272.945), and higher education (AOR: 34.97, 95% CI: 20.06-59.039). The high odds ratios suggest model instability due to small subgroup sizes and should be interpreted with caution.ConclusionTargeted interventions by the Ministry of Health and local government, including deploying mobile health teams and engaging community leaders, are crucial for delivering effective health education and improving handwashing practices to prevent disease in this vulnerable community.Keywords: Cholera, IDP, Hayayaabe, Handwashing, Borama, Somaliland

Introduction

Cholera is a serious health concern worldwide. It causes severe diarrhea when people drink or eat contaminated water or food infected with the Vibrio cholerae bacterium [1, 2]. Despite advances in healthcare and hygiene, cholera outbreaks remain a persistent danger, especially in areas that lack clean water and proper sanitation. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), cholera is prevalent in more than 50 countries worldwide, with an estimated 1.3–4.0 million cases and 21,000–143,000 deaths each year [2]. It is estimated that around 1.3 billion individuals in endemic countries are at risk of contracting cholera. Annually, an estimated 2.86 million cholera cases (with an uncertainty range of 1.3 million to 4.0 million) occur in these countries. Of these, approximately 95,000 deaths (with an uncertainty range of 21,000–143,000) have been reported [3].

In 2023, the entire African continent reported a total of 179,835 cholera cases and 3,220 deaths, with case fatality rates varying between 0% and 6.8% [4]. Highlighting the regional burden, Sub-Saharan Africa (excluding Djibouti and Eritrea) records an average of 141,918 cholera cases annually. Within this region, countries like Zambia, Angola, and Chad reported specific case fatality rates of 3.8%, 5.2%, and 6.8%, respectively [4]. Within this region, 4.0% of the districts had a high incidence of cholera, affecting a population of 87.2 million. Regional efforts, by prioritizing interventions in these high-incidence districts, led to the elimination of 50% of the region’s cholera cases, reaching a population of 35.3 million (credible interval: 26.3 million to 62.0 million). This represents less than 4% of the total population [5].

According to the Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA), cholera, known as Acute Watery Diarrhea (AWD), is spreading in Somalia. From January 1st to March 18, 2024, 4,380 reported cases and 54 associated deaths occurred across 32 districts. Among these deaths, 62% occurred in children under the age of 5 years. Somalia has witnessed several cholera outbreaks in recent years, with over 18,300 cases and 46 deaths reported in 2023. In that year, more than half of the victims were children under the age of five [6]. Since December 2023, there has been an increasing number of cholera outbreak cases in Somaliland, as indicated by regular updates on Acute Watery Diarrhea (AWD) investigations provided by the Ministry of Health and Development. From December 6, 2023, to January 18, 2024, the Ministry of Health and Development reported 14 cases tested using RDT and Culture [7].

The absence of proper knowledge about cholera transmission and prevention is a significant driver of outbreaks [8]. Studies have shown that communities with low awareness are less likely to adopt protective behaviors such as consuming safe water and practicing good hygiene. Hand hygiene and proper sanitation are of utmost importance to reduce the spread of the disease. Social awareness, preparedness, and the practices of inhabitants play a crucial role in controlling the spread of the disease [9, 10]. Factors such as community knowledge about cholera transmission, availability and use of toilets and sanitation facilities, and effectiveness of local health responses are pivotal in managing outbreaks. Understanding these factors can provide valuable insights into improving the region’s cholera prevention and control strategies [4, 5]. The Hayayaabe IDP camp in Borama, Somaliland, is particularly at risk of cholera outbreaks, with some confirmed cholera cases by local health officers. This study aimed to assess the knowledge of cholera and handwashing practices of IDPs in the Hayayaabe camp and to identify factors associated with these practices, which are critical for preventing a potential large-scale cholera outbreak.

Methods and materials

Study setting and population

This community-based cross-sectional study was conducted between April and June 2024 in the Hayayaabe Internally Displaced Persons (IDP) camp in Borama, Somaliland. Based on estimates provided by community leaders and local officials, the Hayayaabe IDP camp comprises approximately 500 households.

Study design

A cross-sectional survey research design was employed to assess cholera knowledge and handwashing practices among residents of the Hayayaabe IDP camp.

Target population

The study’s target population consisted of all eligible individuals (aged 15 years and older) residing in the Hayayaabe IDP camp during the study period. For this study, individuals aged 15 and older were included based on definitions used in similar humanitarian contexts, where adolescents often bear significant household responsibilities. The ethics committee approved this approach.

Sample size calculation

The required sample size was determined using Cochran’s formula for a finite population, based on an estimated total population (N) of 500 eligible adults in the camp. The calculation used a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a 5% margin of error (E = 0.05), and an estimated prevalence (p) of good cholera knowledge of 50% (0.5) to ensure the maximum sample size.

Sampling technique

A multi-stage sampling technique was employed to select participants.

A sampling interval (K) was calculated by dividing the total number of households in the cluster by the desired number of households to be sampled from that cluster (e.g., to sample 50 households from a cluster of 100, K = 100/50 = 2). Data collection instruments and procedures

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered by trained data collectors. The questionnaire was adapted from the World Health Organization’s (WHO) resources for Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP) studies on diarrheal diseases and was pilot-tested on 20 individuals in a similar setting, leading to minor revisions for clarity [11]. The 20 participants for the pilot test were selected purposively from a similar IDP setting outside the study area to provide initial feedback on the clarity and cultural appropriateness of the questionnaire. The questionnaire was translated into Somali (the local language) and back-translated to ensure accuracy. Informed oral consent was obtained from all participants after the study purpose was explained, a method chosen due to low literacy rates in the community and approved by the ethics committee.

Data analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel, cleaned, and then imported into Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26 for descriptive and bivariate analyses. Multivariable logistic regression was conducted using Stata 16. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations, were used to summarize the characteristics of the study sample and the prevalence of cholera knowledge and handwashing practices. Bivariate Analysis: Chi-square tests were used to examine the associations between categorical independent variables (e.g., sex, education level) and the dependent variables (cholera knowledge and handwashing practices). Multivariable Logistic Regression: Binary and multivariable logistic regression models were used to identify independent predictors of cholera knowledge and handwashing practices. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable models. This threshold was selected as a common practice in epidemiological studies for initial variable screening. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify the strength of association. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Factors associated with cholera knowledge and handwashing practices

Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with cholera knowledge and handwashing practices. The key findings from these analyses are presented in Tables 2 and 3. Table 2 shows the factors influencing cholera knowledge. In the multivariable analysis, older age (AOR 0.07, p = 0.045), source of information (AOR 0.042, p = 0.016), and university-level education (AOR 9.129, p = 0.03) were found to be statistically significant predictors (Table 2). Table 3 details the factors associated with handwashing practices. The multivariable model identified older age (AOR 0.026, p = 0.01), female sex (AOR 0.072, p = 0.001), higher family income, and higher education levels as significant predictors of handwashing practices (Table 3). It should be noted that the odds ratios for high income (AOR: 71.604) and university education (AOR: 34.97) are exceptionally large. This indicates potential model instability caused by small numbers of participants in some subgroups (a phenomenon known as quasi-complete separation), and therefore these specific effect sizes should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

The study revealed that only 25% of the population living in the Hayayaabe IDP had good knowledge of cholera. This finding contradicts a study conducted in Kenya, which reported that over half of the respondents had a high knowledge score on cholera, including its causes, modes of transmission, and prevention [14]. The study also disagreed with a survey conducted in Tanzania, which stated that 85% of the respondents had good knowledge of cholera [15]. This disparity may reflect differences in public health infrastructure, the intensity of recent health campaigns, cultural practices, seasonal access to clean water, and the overall socio-economic conditions of the populations studied [4]. Our finding is, however, consistent with a study in Bangladesh that found only 23% of participants could identify cholera correctly, and over half had poor knowledge, suggesting that low awareness is a common challenge in densely populated, low-resource settings [16]. This significant disparity highlights substantial gaps in education, access to information, and the dissemination of proper knowledge. Targeted interventions are urgently needed to address these deficiencies through enhanced outreach, community-based training, and culturally sensitive educational materials. The minority possessing good knowledge represents a valuable resource for peer education and community-led initiatives to improve overall awareness (Fig. 1).

Additionally, the study found that only 26.3% of the participants washed their hands before and after using the toilet. This contrasts with a survey conducted in Indonesia, which reported a higher handwashing practice rate of 71.8% [17]. Another study conducted in Ethiopia during a cholera outbreak concluded that handwashing practice was poor, with only 51.3% of participants practicing proper handwashing, which is higher than the results of this study [18, 19]. though even that figure is nearly double what we observed, highlighting the critical hygiene gap in the Hayayaabe camp. This significant disparity suggests inadequate hygiene awareness, limited access to clean water and sanitation, or both. Poor practices likely increase the community’s vulnerability to hygiene-related illnesses and disease outbreaks. While a minority display good handwashing practices, representing a foundation for positive change, urgent and multifaceted interventions are needed. These should include targeted hygiene education campaigns, improved access to handwashing facilities with soap and water, and sustained community engagement to promote healthier hygiene behaviors (Fig. 2).

Our multivariable analysis identified age, source of information, and educational level as significant predictors of cholera knowledge. The finding that higher education is associated with better knowledge is consistent with studies in Nigeria [20], Iraq [21], and Lebanon [22], reinforcing the pivotal role of formal education in public health literacy. The negative association with older age could reflect a reliance on traditional beliefs over biomedical information. In contrast, the importance of the source of information underscores the need for credible and accessible health messaging.

Similarly, this study found that age, sex, education, and family income were significant predictors of handwashing practices. The strong association between higher income and education with better practices aligns with findings from the DRC [23], Northwest Ethiopia [24], and India [25]. This suggests that both financial capacity (to afford soap and water) and educational attainment (understanding of germ theory) are crucial for good hygiene. The finding that females had poorer practices than males was unexpected and warrants further qualitative investigation, as women are often the primary targets of hygiene promotion.

Collectively, these findings have direct implications for public health policy and emergency preparedness in Somaliland. The low levels of knowledge and practice, particularly among specific subgroups such as older adults and those with no formal education, indicate that current health education strategies are insufficient for this vulnerable population. Therefore, interventions must be tailored to address these specific needs. Critical policy actions should include the deployment of mobile health teams, the launch of community-wide campaigns utilizing trusted sources such as local leaders, and ensuring the consistent availability of soap and water. From an emergency response perspective, these data serve as a crucial baseline to measure the effectiveness of future interventions and highlight the urgent need for pre-emptive hygiene promotion to mitigate the risk of a large-scale outbreak.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design means we can only report associations, not causation. Second, handwashing practice was self-reported, which is subject to social desirability bias, potentially overestimating the accurate practice rates. Third, the study was conducted in a single IDP camp, which means the findings may not be generalizable to other displaced populations in Somaliland or different humanitarian settings. Fourth, the absence of a qualitative component limits our ability to understand the complex reasons behind the observed knowledge gaps and practices; such data would be invaluable for designing culturally appropriate interventions. Fifth, a design effect (DEFF) was not applied during the sample size calculation, which may have led to an underpowered study and overly precise confidence intervals. Finally, the exceptionally high odds ratios observed for income and education suggest model instability due to small numbers in some subgroups (a phenomenon known as quasi-complete separation). Therefore, these specific effect sizes should be interpreted with considerable caution.

Conclusion

The study concluded that knowledge of cholera and handwashing practices was poor among the Hayayaabe IDP communities. The study also identified that age, sex, educational level, family income, and source of information influenced knowledge of cholera and handwashing practices. The study recommended that the Somaliland Ministry of Health and the local government enhance community knowledge and handwashing practices through targeted education and orientation. They should establish mobile teams comprising Ministry of Health offices and other trained health personnel to visit community households regularly. These teams will increase the knowledge of cholera, educate household heads on proper handwashing, and promote their awareness of the harmful effects of the outbreak. Mobile teams can also mobilize community heads, scholars, and religious leaders to spread their messages to all community members.
