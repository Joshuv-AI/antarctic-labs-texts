Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair in a Patient With Severe Degenerative Mitral Regurgitation and Mirror-Image Dextrocardia
Authors: Zhengming Jiang, Wei Yang, Liuyang Tian, Guangyuan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundManaging severe degenerative mitral regurgitation (MR) in patients with mirror-image dextrocardia is challenging given the rare, complex anatomy. Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) offers a minimally invasive alternative for patients at high surgical risk.Case SummaryA 76-year-old woman with dextrocardia presented with NYHA functional class III heart failure due to severe MR from anterior leaflet flail. Given prohibitive surgical risk, she successfully underwent TEER with 2 MitraClip XTR devices. Adaptations for the mirrored anatomy included a modified transseptal puncture site and reversed catheter maneuvering. Postprocedural MR was reduced to trivial without stenosis, with rapid symptomatic improvement.DiscussionThis case confirms the feasibility of TEER for degenerative MR in dextrocardia.Take-Home MessageTEER for degenerative MR in dextrocardia is critically dependent on preprocedural planning and specific intraoperative modifications to accommodate the reversed anatomy, expanding minimally invasive options for high-risk patients with complex cardiac conditions.

History of Presentation

A 76-year-old woman was admitted with progressive symptoms of heart failure, including worsening fatigue, poor appetite, orthopnea, and a productive cough with thick white sputum. These symptoms had advanced to NYHA functional class III status despite medical therapy. On examination, her vital signs included a temperature of 36.2 °C, blood pressure 104/60 mm Hg, pulse 62 beats/min (irregularly irregular), and respiratory rate 19 breaths/min. Heart auscultation revealed an irregular rhythm with variable intensity and a grade 3/6 holosystolic murmur best heard in the apical area. She had bilateral lower extremity pitting edema. Past Medical History

The patient's medical history was significant for permanent atrial fibrillation (managed with rate control) and chronic bronchitis. She had been known to have degenerative mitral regurgitation (MR) for several months prior to presentation. There was no history of rheumatic fever, ischemic heart disease, or prior cardiac surgery.

Differential Diagnosis

In an elderly patient with decompensated heart failure, dyspnea, and a holosystolic murmur, the differential diagnosis included acute or chronic valvular regurgitations and other causes of volume overload. Given her mirror-image dextrocardia, careful assessment was needed to localize the murmur and pathology. Severe tricuspid regurgitation or a ventricular septal defect could produce a holosystolic murmur with right-sided findings, but echocardiography quickly pinpointed the mitral valve as the source. For the mechanism of MR, possibilities included a degenerative leaflet prolapse/flail versus an acute process such as papillary muscle rupture or endocarditis causing leaflet perforation. The absence of chest pain or ischemic changes made an acute ischemic papillary muscle rupture unlikely, and there were no fevers or vegetations to suggest endocarditis. The echocardiographic morphology of the mitral valve (myxomatous degeneration with chordal rupture) was consistent with a degenerative (primary) MR etiology.

Investigations

Electrocardiogram on admission showed atrial fibrillation with a controlled ventricular rate and nonspecific interventricular conduction delay, with lead orientation findings consistent with dextrocardia (global lead reversal pattern). A chest radiograph confirmed dextrocardia with the cardiac apex in the right hemithorax. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed mirror-image dextrocardia and severe MR. The mitral valve was thickened with myxomatous changes. During systole, the anterior mitral leaflet (segments A2 and part of A3) prolapsed into the left atrium owing to chordae tendineae rupture at the A2 segment, resulting in a flail leaflet. This caused a wide coaptation gap and torrential MR. The regurgitation was quantified as severe, with a vena contracta measuring ∼7.5 mm and an effective regurgitant orifice area of approximately 0.6 to 0.8 cm2 by proximal isovelocity surface area method. The regurgitant volume was estimated around 120 mL per beat, confirming severe MR. The left atrium and left ventricle were markedly dilated, and pulmonary hypertension was evident on Doppler.

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was performed for procedural planning and provided additional detail (Figure 1). TEE confirmed a Carpentier type II lesion (leaflet prolapse) with a flail anterior segment and severe eccentric MR jet directed posteriorly (Video 1). A small 4 × 4 mm sac-like diverticulum was noted on the posterior mitral leaflet (P3 scallop), but it was not causing significant regurgitation. No thrombi were seen in the left atrium despite atrial fibrillation. The TEE helped delineate the optimal site for intervention and confirmed normal right-sided cardiac anatomy aside from the dextrocardia (no associated congenital defects). Management

The patient was initially optimized on guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure, including an angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor, a beta-blocker (metoprolol), digoxin, a sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor, an aldosterone antagonist, and diuretics. Her symptoms improved modestly with medical therapy, but she continued to experience exertional dyspnea and episodes of chest tightness. Given the persistence of severe MR and the patient's advanced age and comorbidities, the heart team deemed her a high-risk candidate for open heart surgery. After multidisciplinary discussion, the decision was made to proceed with transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) of the mitral valve. The patient provided informed consent for the procedure.

Procedural plan

A strategy was formulated to address the prolapsed segments with transcatheter repair. We anticipated the need for 2 MitraClip devices (Abbott) given the broad prolapse. The plan was to first deploy a MitraClip XTR to grasp the flail anterior leaflet segment in the A2 region, aiming to eliminate the central jet. A second clip (XTR or NT, depending on residual anatomy) would then be placed adjacent to the first, targeting the remaining prolapse toward the A3 segment if significant MR persisted (Figure 2). Procedure

The TEER procedure was performed in a hybrid operating room under general anesthesia with continuous TEE guidance. Vascular access was obtained via the right femoral vein. A steerable guide catheter was advanced to the right atrium, and transseptal puncture was performed under TEE and fluoroscopic guidance. Given the mirror-image anatomy, the interatrial septum puncture site and angle required modification. The transseptal needle was directed toward the anatomic upper-right atrial septum (ie, posterior and superior septal area in the mirror-image heart), rather than the usual posteroinferior quadrant used in normal anatomy. A more posterior puncture was achieved to ensure adequate height above the mitral valve and to reduce the risk of interfering with surrounding structures. Once across the septum, left atrial pressure was measured and was found elevated at 30 mm Hg.

Next, a MitraClip steerable guide catheter was advanced into the left atrium. In dextrocardia, the orientation of the guide catheter and device was effectively reversed. The guide system was rotated approximately 180° opposite to the usual orientation (ie, aimed toward the patient's left side, the mirror-image of the normal rightward orientation) to align with the mitral valve. All catheter manipulation had to be mentally “mirrored” by the interventional team. The MitraClip delivery system was then introduced through the guide. Initial steering of the system required counterintuitive knob adjustments (for example, certain knob turns that would deflect the catheter anteriorly in a normal heart had the opposite effect in this context given the reversed spatial orientation). Care was taken to maintain the device in the proper plane perpendicular to the mitral annular plane, which was confirmed by TEE imaging.

Under real-time 3-dimensional (3D) TEE guidance, the first MitraClip XTR was positioned over the middle of the valve (corresponding to the A2/P2 scallops region). The clip arms were opened and carefully oriented perpendicular to the line of coaptation (facilitated by the unique clockwise/counterclockwise maneuvers required in the mirrored anatomy). The clip was advanced across the mitral valve into the left ventricle and then retracted to grasp the prolapsed anterior leaflet and the opposing posterior leaflet segment. After ensuring that both leaflets were adequately captured (with sufficient leaflet insertion on both sides and no tension on the clip), the clip was closed. Immediate TEE assessment showed a substantial reduction in MR, although a residual jet lateral to the clip suggested remaining prolapse in the A3 segment. The mean mitral gradient after the first clip was 3 mm Hg, indicating no significant stenosis.

A second MitraClip was then delivered to address the residual MR. The guide catheter was reoriented, and the second clip (another XTR) was maneuvered into position adjacent to the first clip, toward the lateral (A3) portion of the valve. The second clip was deployed parallel to the first, further reducing the MR. Final intraprocedural TEE demonstrated only trivial residual MR with dual-orifice valve inflow and a mean gradient of 4 mm Hg. There were no immediate complications such as leaflet injury, mitral stenosis, or pericardial effusion. Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient's postprocedural course was uncomplicated. She was extubated the same day and monitored in the cardiac intensive care unit. Within 24 hours, she noted marked improvement in symptoms, with resolution of orthopnea and no recurrence of chest tightness. Postprocedural transthoracic echocardiography before discharge confirmed stable positioning of both MitraClips, with trivial MR. There was no residual atrial septal defect shunt on color Doppler. The patient was discharged home on postprocedural day 5 on guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure and anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation.

At the 3-month follow-up, the patient reported greatly improved exercise tolerance (now NYHA functional class I-II). Echocardiography at 3 months showed the MitraClips to be securely in place with trivial residual MR and a mean mitral valve gradient of 5 mm Hg. The left ventricular ejection fraction remained preserved, and there was evidence of positive remodeling: The left atrial size had decreased and pulmonary artery pressures had improved. This favorable outcome has been sustained to date. The successful repair dramatically alleviated the patient's heart failure symptoms and improved her quality of life.

Discussion

Dextrocardia with situs inversus is an extraordinarily rare congenital anomaly, occurring in approximately 1 in 10,000 live births.1,2 In such cases, the mirror-image orientation of the heart and great vessels poses unique challenges for diagnosis and intervention. Severe degenerative MR is a life-threatening condition; if left untreated, it leads to progressive volume overload, ventricular dysfunction, and poor outcomes.3 Surgical mitral valve repair remains the preferred treatment for degenerative (primary) MR and has been shown to improve survival compared with medical therapy.4 Early surgical intervention, even in asymptomatic patients, can restore life expectancy to near-normal levels in degenerative MR.5,6 However, a substantial subset of patients—particularly the elderly or those with significant comorbidities—are not ideal surgical candidates.7 Our patient fell into this high–surgical risk category owing to advanced age and comorbid heart failure and lung disease. For such patients, TEER has emerged as an effective alternative to surgery. The MitraClip device has demonstrated substantial clinical benefit in reducing MR and improving symptoms in patients with degenerative MR, especially in those deemed at too high risk for open surgery.

There is scant literature regarding mitral valve interventions in patients with dextrocardia. To our knowledge, only a few cases of surgical mitral valve repair or replacement in dextrocardia have been reported.8 Nie and colleagues9 described 2 cases of mitral repair in mirror-image dextrocardia, highlighting the need for tailored surgical approaches. In the transcatheter era, our case represents a rare example of a TEER procedure in this population. The technical adaptations we employed were critical for success and provide insight for future similar cases.

Performing TEER in a mirror-image heart demands adjustments at every step of the procedure. First, precise transseptal puncture is vital. In dextrocardia, standard transseptal puncture landmarks appear inverted on imaging. A higher and more posterior septal puncture was required in our case to achieve adequate alignment with the mitral valve. Use of real-time 3D TEE guidance was indispensable for localizing the optimal puncture site. In addition, intracardiac echocardiography or augmented fluoroscopic guidance (with manual inversion of imaging orientation) could be considered in such cases. Preprocedural computed tomography and even 3D-printed models of the patient's heart have been suggested as tools to help operators visualize the altered anatomy and plan the trajectory for transseptal access.

Vascular access in mitral TEER for patients with dextrocardia warrants careful planning. In the present case, the procedure was successfully performed via right femoral venous access under 3D TEE guidance, confirming the feasibility of a conventional approach. However, left femoral access may offer anatomical advantages in these patients. Given the mirrored thoracic anatomy, a left-sided approach can provide a more direct and coaxial trajectory of the transseptal sheath toward the mitral valve,10 potentially reducing catheter looping and improving maneuverability. This experience highlights the importance of individualized, anatomy-based planning in congenital heart interventions. Preprocedural simulation using multimodal imaging, such as computed tomography–based 3D reconstruction, could help compare pathways from both sides and optimize access selection for safer and more efficient device delivery.

This case underscores the significant advantage of employing a steerable transseptal sheath system (Agilis, St Jude Medical) in patients with complex anatomic variations, such as dextrocardia. Unlike conventional preshaped sheaths or manually reshaped needles, a steerable sheath enables dynamic, real-time control over the trajectory of the transseptal assembly.11 This capability proves particularly invaluable when targeting nonstandard puncture sites, as it permits precise, millimeter-by-millimeter adjustment under live echocardiographic guidance. By effectively overcoming the constraints of fixed geometry in unusual cardiac orientations, steerable sheath technology enhances both the safety and accuracy of transseptal access—a critical step upon which the success of the entire TEER procedure depends.

Second, reorientation of the MitraClip system is necessary for mirror-image anatomy. Every catheter and device manipulation must be considered in reverse. In our experience, continuously correlating fluoroscopic views with echocardiographic views helped the team maintain spatial awareness. Simple maneuvers, such as rotating the guide catheter 180° from the usual orientation, allowed the clip delivery system to align with the mitral valve despite the heart's reversed position. Operators must be prepared for counterintuitive responses when turning steering knobs or advancing catheters. Meticulous practice with a model or rehearsal in the mind's eye can be beneficial. Having team members verbalize each step (eg, “clockwise turn will move the clip medially in this patient”) can reduce confusion during the procedure.

Additionally, adjusting to reversed operation of imaging and equipment is crucial. Echocardiographic windows may need to be adjusted to obtain standard views (for example, what would normally be an apical 4-chamber view might require unconventional probe rotation in dextrocardia to visualize the chambers correctly). In our case, the echocardiographers obtained excellent imaging by treating the right chest as the location of the apex, enabling all of the standard TEE views (eg, midesophageal, 2-chamber, 4-chamber, long-axis views), albeit mirrored. A high level of communication between the echocardiography team and the interventional cardiologist was maintained throughout to ensure that catheter positions and movements were interpreted correctly on the ultrasound images.

Outcomes and Significance

The successful result in this case illustrates that TEER is a feasible and effective therapy for degenerative MR even in patients with mirror-image dextrocardia. By achieving a major reduction in MR severity (from torrential to trivial) without complications, we effectively relieved the patient's heart failure symptoms and avoided the need for a high-risk surgical intervention. This expands the horizon of TEER to anatomically complex scenarios. It also emphasizes the importance of an experienced heart team; both structural interventionists and imaging specialists must adapt their techniques in concert. As only a single case, our report is limited in scope, but it provides valuable proof of concept that can inform the management of similar patients in the future. Wider application of transcatheter therapies in congenital anatomical variants will require accumulation of more clinical experience.

Conclusions

We report a rare case of transcatheter MitraClip repair of severe degenerative MR in a patient with mirror-image dextrocardia. With careful planning and appropriate technical modifications, TEER was performed safely and resulted in excellent hemodynamic and clinical outcomes. This case demonstrates that even in the presence of unusual cardiac orientation, state-of-the-art transcatheter therapies can be used to treat structural heart disease. Wider adoption of such techniques has the potential to extend the benefits of minimally invasive cardiac intervention to patients who previously had limited options because of complex anatomy or prohibitive surgical risk.
