Real-world data landscape for glaucoma in Europe: a questionnaire-based analysis of resources among European Glaucoma Society members
Authors: Marta Pazos, Andrew Tatham, Danilo Andrade De Jesus, Luisa Sanchez Brea, Stefan Klein, David Wright, Augusto Azuara-Blanco, Fotis Topouzis, Ananth Viswanathan, on behalf of the European Glaucoma Society Real World Data Task Force
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/aimsTo investigate the landscape to support Europe-wide collaborative real-world data (RWD) collection, exploring whether required resources are available to glaucoma clinicians.MethodsThis cross-sectional study employed a two-phase method, including two consecutive electronic questionnaires. For phase I, a survey was distributed to all European Glaucoma Society members inquiring about use of electronic medical records (EMRs). For phase II, EMR users who expressed their interest in contributing to a European RWD were surveyed regarding EMR data characteristics and technical resources.ResultsPhase I garnered responses from 201 glaucoma specialists, with 68% reporting use of an EMR. Among them, 47% and 65% reported over 90% of coding rates for diagnoses and medications, respectively. Despite the EMR making available report copies for perimetry (27%) and optical coherence tomography (OCT) (37%), automatic data extraction capabilities were limited to only 10% and 8%. 47 participants responded to phase II. Their practices saw an average of 415 glaucoma patients per month. Date of intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement was the most commonly available structured data (67%), followed by visual acuity (59%) and IOP (56%). 46% had access to central computing systems, with 41% reporting past experience in federated learning initiatives and 73% willing contribution. 34% reported involvement in the development of artificial intelligence algorithms.ConclusionThis study highlights a noteworthy EMR use among European glaucoma specialists, with structured data for metrics like IOP, but identifies gaps in data extraction capabilities. Major challenges include EMR heterogeneity, lack of standardised outcomes, healthcare system differences and cost. A combined approach using structured coding, digitised EMR data and semistructured OCT/perimetry data is recommended.

Introduction

Digital innovation enables the collection and storage of extensive health data, which can be useful to monitor healthcare delivery,13 including glaucoma.4 Real-world data (RWD) spans patient demographics, diagnoses, treatments, disease progression, location, behaviours and lifestyle,5 sourced from electronic medical records (EMRs), insurance billing and claims, registries, wearable devices, social media and diagnostic or omics databases.6 7 Properly curated and analysed, these data yield real-world evidence (RWE), which offers insights into the effectiveness and safety of interventions in practical settings. RWE complements traditional research methods, such as randomised controlled trials (RCTs).5

RWD is also valuable in glaucoma management, as it reflects treatment effectiveness, adherence and disease progression in everyday settings.810 Unlike RCTs, RWD captures diverse patient experiences over time, supporting personalised care and improving risk assessment in real-life populations.11

However, the collection, curation, analysis and interpretation of RWD present challenges compared with RCTs, particularly regarding the consistency and quality control of data recording, collection and storage.12 Additionally, variances in data completeness, standards, modelling and descriptive methods can hinder effective use. Consequently, determining the suitability of RWD for generating RWE is crucial for leveraging these data to inform decision-making.13

To address these challenges, the European Glaucoma Society (EGS) has formed a task force to create standardised guidelines for RWD collection and reporting in glaucoma.14 In this context, the primary objective was to explore the potential landscape for collaborative RWD collection, assessing resource availability for glaucoma clinicians across Europe.

Methods

A two-phase method was employed, consisting of consecutive digital surveys (figure 1). The surveys were conducted by email, using the tool of the University of Edinburgh (Jisc, online survey V.3). The descriptive analysis was performed using Microsoft (Richmond, Virginia, USA) Excel (V.16.89.1). Answers to open-ended questions were subjected to thematic analysis. Feasibility was evaluated following the Structured Process to Identify Fit-for-Purpose Data (SPFID)15 and Duke Margolis16 frameworks, which provide guidance on the suitability of RWD data. Additionally, the Clinical Outcomes in Digital Environments for Electronic Health Records (CODE-EHR)17 framework was employed to assess the effectiveness of EMR usage (online supplemental table 1).

Flowchart and methods of the study. In phase I, all EGS members were surveyed. In phase II, only those with EMR access who had agreed to be contacted were included. EGS, European Glaucoma Society; EMR, electronic medical record; RWD, real-world data.

Phase I: survey to identify EMRs usage

All 665 active EGS members were invited, via e-mail, to answer a 21-question survey on demographics, EMR use and willingness to contribute data to an EGS-led project (online supplemental table 2). The survey was open from 13 April to 9 May 2021 with two reminders sent over a 4-week period.

Phase II: survey to explore feasibility of collecting RWD across Europe

During phase II, previously surveyed EGS members with EMR access who had agreed to be contacted for a potential EGS-led RWD project were invited to participate in a second survey. This consisted of 29 questions divided into six sections. For exploring the availability of key outcome variables, we used those considered ‘mandatory’ and/or ‘desirable’ in the clinical dataset for glaucoma procedures developed by the UK Royal College of Ophthalmologists in collaboration with the EGS.18 The type of questions was a combination of multiple choice and open ended (online supplemental table 3). Responses were collected between March and May 2024, with two reminders sent to non-responders.

Patient and public involvement

While no patients were directly involved in this study, their perspectives were incorporated through the Experts by Experience group—a collective of European glaucoma patients within the EGS. This group served as an advisory team across all EGS RWD project initiatives.

Results

Phase I: survey to identify EMRs usage

Demographics

In phase I, 201 clinicians from 35 European countries completed the survey (30% response rate) (online supplemental table 4). Most worked in academic centres (22%) followed by private (17%) and public centres (14.5%). Nearly 75% considered their practice representative of other practices in their country in size, surgical volume, complexity and equipment. See figure 2A for demographics.

EMR use

136 clinicians (68%) had EMR access, 58% of them for more than 5 years and 34% between 1 and 5 years. Of the 136 respondents with an EMR, 51.8% had diagnoses coded (eg, Systemised Nomenclature of Medicine Clinical Terms (SNOMED-CT)). 48 participants (36%) estimated having 100% of diagnoses entered on the EMR and 23.3% between 90% and 99%. 77% of EMRs stored information about surgery and 79.9% about medications (online supplemental figure 1). Methods of data capture varied considerably among respondents, with 26% using only clinical notes (free text boxes) and 18% a combination of structured form (drop down menus, checkboxes), clinical notes and attached PDF documents. Only 15.8% reported having a completely structured EMR; 15.8% used free text and PDF documents; 12% used structured data plus clinical notes and 3% captured data using all the different formats (figure 2C).

EMR software

There was significant heterogeneity in the different software used, although the more frequent programmes were Medisoft, OpenEyes, Epic and Softalmo (figure 2B). Bulk export of multiple patient records was reported to be possible in 52% (28% with provider assistance and possibly incurring costs). Almost half of the respondents were unsure whether their EMR had an audit function.

EMR linkage to visual field (VF) test and optical coherence tomography (OCT)

Results about EMR linkage with perimetry and OCT are shown in figure 3. 76% of clinicians reported electronic access to VF results, but only 10% had automatic EMR integration. Similarly, while 82% had electronic access to OCT results, only 8.1% could automatically extract data into their EMR, with most relying on electronic or scanned reports.

Phase II: survey to explore the feasibility of collecting RWD across Europe

A total of 116 clinicians provided contact details and were invited to participate in phase II. Of these, 47 responded for a 40.5% response rate.

Case numbers

The median number of patients seen at each practice per month was 300 (IQR 325). If taken in total, the collaborative database could potentially include 19 520 cases per month (figure 4A).

Data infrastructure

A wide range of EMR systems was used, with the most frequent custom-made software, followed by OpenEyes (figure 4B). The EMR system was hospital wide in 54% of centres (online supplemental figure 2A), with the remainder using dedicated ophthalmology EMRs. The most common method of data capture was ‘structured with some free text’ (42%), followed by ‘mostly free text with few structured variables’ (28%) and ‘all free text’ (15%) (online supplemental figure 2B). The variables with the greatest proportion of structured data were intraocular pressure (IOP) data (67%), visual acuity (59%), IOP (56%) and refraction (52%) (table 1). OCT and VF measurements were infrequently captured by EMR systems. Data export was possible patient by patient in 32% and by bulk export in 28%.

Research databases

51% of respondents had access to structured research databases, updated periodically in 63% and continuously in 33%, with a median of 3000 patients (IQR=4200).

Imaging data

Over 73% of clinicians were unaware of the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) used for imaging storage; those aware mostly used OCT manufacturer software. 51% reported their PACS system as hospital wide. Imaging devices varied: 57% had fundus photography, 55% OCT Spectralis (Heidelberg Engineering), 45% OCT HD Cirrus (Carl Zeiss Meditec) and 23% wide-field photography (online supplemental figure 3). Data export was available patient by-patient in 43% of cases and by bulk in 15%.

Computer infrastructure

22 glaucoma specialists (47%) had access to central computing infrastructure (CCI); eg, cluster or cloud environment). 12 could provide system specifications (eg, number of central processing unit and graphics processing unit cores, random access memory, etc). 11 had used the CCI, and 9 had participated in federated/distributed learning (FL) studies. 11 were open to hosting a local cluster, and 16 would be willing to provide data access for research without it leaving the institution, allowing artificial intelligence (AI) training via FL (online supplemental figure 4).

Technical support capacities

79% of respondents had contact with their information technology (IT) department, 70% with EMR representatives and 50% with PACS IT specialists. 34% had contributed to AI algorithm development and 55% had data science support access. Research-based AI models were used infrequently in clinical conditions (26%).

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the availability of data and resources for capturing RWD in glaucoma management across Europe. Frameworks such as SPIFD and the Duke-Margolis Center Data’s Fitness for Use have guided the collection of RWD, focusing on data relevance and quality.15 16 Key dimensions include population representativeness, essential data fields, accurate patient data linkage, adequate sample size and follow-up duration.16 The CODE-EHR reporting framework can also be effectively applied at the design stage for collaborative RWD collection, ensuring accessible and efficient data sharing.17

The increasing use of EMRs worldwide has made them a vital source of RWD.19 This European glaucoma specialist survey found 68% use EMRs in clinical practice, with 58% having 5-year data. However, there was significant variability in types of EMR software, data capture methods and data export capabilities across different institutions. Capture of structured data, which would facilitate data analysis, was relatively rare, accounting for only 15%. Non-structured or semistructured data such as free texts or imported PDFs was more frequent.

Recommendations about data interoperability and standards include the use of standardised terminologies such as SNOMED-CT.16 In glaucoma RWD collection, for population and outcomes, the key data elements needed would be diagnosis and procedure codes, whereas for intervention and comparator (treatments) pharmacy data and procedure codes would likely be the most valuable resources.20 Our phase I survey found that 47% of respondents coded 90–99% of patients’ diagnoses and 65% recorded 90–99% of active medications, a promising finding towards the goal of a Europe-wide RWD analysis. Tapping into existing healthcare data can uncover valuable health insights, especially when these data sources are linked.21

Although the EMR data may be incomplete, a minimum degree of completeness and quality is required, with core variables including outcome, intervention, demographics and important covariates.15 16 In the absence of a unified standard for glaucoma management, in Phase II, we explored the availability of the RCOpth-EGS recommended dataset for glaucoma procedures outcomes.18 The survey revealed a high proportion of respondents routinely collecting useful glaucoma-related information in a structured format, which would facilitate our envisaged RWD collaboration. The most commonly structured variables were IOP date, VA, IOP and refraction (>50% of respondents). Data from VF and OCT were broadly available but much less structured (<30%), often captured as free text. RWD analysis of glaucoma outcomes would be facilitated if results of VF and OCT were automatically integrated with the EMR; however, only 10% and 8% of respondents reported such a capability for perimetry and OCT, respectively. As VF loss is a key measure of glaucoma status, the lack of integration of perimetric devices and EMRs is likely to be a barrier to RWD analysis for glaucoma. Additionally, most vendors do not support straightforward data export from their devices. This fragmentation complicates glaucoma RWD audits and research, often requiring data extraction from multiple electronic sources. A key step towards resolving this issue is the adoption of the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) standard—a globally recognised protocol for formatting and sharing medical images and related information. Promoting DICOM conformance across devices, ideally through coordinated industry collaboration and regulatory support, would greatly enhance data integration and system compatibility. Until these barriers are resolved, it is apparent that glaucoma RWD audits and research wishing to incorporate perimetry or OCT will require analysis of data from more than one electronic source in the majority of centres.

An adequate sample is also mandatory for effective RWD collection.1517 Representativeness should be assessed broadly with some diversity in geography, health status and healthcare system as appropriate for glaucoma management and its regulatory context.20 Our phase I survey was distributed to all EGS members, with 201 participants from more than 35 different countries. Moreover, a sufficient sample size must be maintained to ensure reliable statistical analyses.22 Our feasibility survey involved 47 centres across Europe seeing an average of 415 patients per month, potentially contributing to a large collaborative database.

Ensuring ethical compliance, data security (eg, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, General Data Protection Regulation) and patient data anonymisation is essential.1517 Additionally, FL is recommended for decentralised, collaborative research and data sharing.17 In phase II, 22 experts had access to a CCI with 73% willing to share data for training without it leaving the institution. Technical support, particularly in data curation, is essential during platform development and analysis.17 In phase II, 79% of respondents had support and 34% contributed to AI algorithm development.

Incorporating SNOMED-CT codes and structured clinical values such as IOP and VA into a collaborative platform is feasible, as described by Sulonen et al in their prototype protocol for evaluating an RWD set using a structured EHR in glaucoma.4 However, effective inclusion of OCT and VF data may require automated extraction systems directly from electronic or PDF reports and could involve additional costs. Similarly, the Sight Outcomes Research Collaborative (SOURCE)23 is a repository of ophthalmological data extracted from EMRs (Epic Systems) across multiple US ophthalmology centres. SOURCE collects data on demographics, diagnoses identified from International Classification of Diseases, clinical findings and treatments. A recent multicentre study using SOURCE data from 36 548 individuals showed reasonable predictive accuracy in identifying glaucoma progression requiring surgery.24 Likewise, the Glaucoma Real-world Appraisal Progression Ensemble dataset includes 1115 follow-up records from 263 eyes, with VFs, fundus images, OCT measurements and clinical information, and two baseline models have shown the feasibility in the prediction of VF and its progression.25

This study has identified several challenges that need to be addressed. First, EMR data may have limited utility due to the sensitive nature of the information and the restrictive management of these systems.22 Second, variability and subjectivity in EMR entries can affect both the evaluation process and the objectivity of clinical outcomes.22 Third, data structures can be diverse, ranging from structured data to non-structured or semistructured data such as free texts, pictures or PDFs. Therefore, the combined approach of including EMR data and tests reports here suggested may lead to redundancy and duplication of data in the process of data recording, collection and storage, and in turn make data curation more difficult.26 Fourth, the format, completeness and quality of RWD from different registries, EMR vendors and healthcare providers vary significantly across countries and healthcare systems, a factor that must be considered in the platform’s design, which ideally should be freely available.27 Since imaging is central to ophthalmology, ensuring DICOM conformance among OCT devices, PAC systems, EHRs and research databases would significantly enhance data integration and workflow efficiency. Lastly, to address the lack of unified standards in glaucoma care, future work should focus on developing a core EMR dataset. We propose a two-tiered approach: a clinical tier to support decision making, and a research tier with additional standardised variables. This framework could improve interoperability and enable RWE generation across diverse clinical settings.

This study has several limitations. First, distributing the survey to all EGS members may have led to over-representation of certain countries, impacting the generalisability of findings. Second, survey design challenges, including undefined target population and potential respondent self-selection, may introduce bias. The low response rate among clinicians further limits the representativeness. Additionally, respondents may not have had sufficient knowledge of their groups’ RWD resources, and despite providing definitions for specialised terms, misunderstandings could still have affected result accuracy.

In conclusion, this study shows that EMR are commonly used among European glaucoma specialists, with key clinical metrics such as IOP available as structured data. However, limited data extraction capabilities highlight areas for improvement. Major challenges include EMR heterogeneity, lack of standardised outcomes, healthcare system differences and cost. Interest in FL and AI suggests readiness for collaborative, data-driven efforts, but further technological advances are needed. A combined approach using structured coding (eg, medications, diagnoses), digitised EMR variables (eg, IOP, VA) and semistructured data from OCT and VF reports is recommended for optimal functionality.
