Factors influencing large language model adoption among dental students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Angel M Joseph, Rahaf E Almutairi, Wejdan A Alrashidi, Reem M Almutairi, Yahya Theeban, Sami Aldhuwayhi, Ahmed Altuwalah, Mohammed Z Mustafa, Amar A Thakare, Saikarthik Jayakumar, Ashwin Joseph, Sreekanth K Mallineni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research evaluates the factors influencing the behavioural intention (BI) to adopt large language models (LLMs) among dental students in education, clinical decision support (CDS), and research, using the original unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) model, representing the first application of this model in this specific context. LLM adoption among Saudi dental students is unstructured and unregulated, making empirical evidence on adoption factors an educational and governance priority. A cross-sectional, web-based survey using convenience sampling was conducted among 700 students from six universities in central Saudi Arabia (60.71% aged 18–22 years; 58.71% female; 74% undergraduate). The UTAUT constructs: performance expectancy (PE), effort expectancy (EE), social influence (SI), and facilitating conditions (FC), were assessed using a five-point Likert scale. Measurement equivalence across English and Arabic language questionnaire versions were confirmed. All UTAUT constructs showed borderline acceptable to excellent internal consistency (α = 0.678–0.907). Structural equation modelling (SEM) with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals (CI) was used for statistical analysis. Hierarchical multiple regression was used for post-hoc sensitivity analysis to examine the moderating effects of demographic variables. The model accounted for 44.1% of the variance in BI (SEM-derived R2 = 0.441, p < 0.001). EE was the strongest significant factor influencing BI (β = 0.351, p = 0.017, 95% CI [0.056, 0.518]), followed by FC (β = 0.136, p = 0.024, 95% CI [0.019, 0.183]). The PE (β = 0.158, p = 0.162) was non-significant, due to its high latent correlation with EE, and SI (β = 0.090, p = 0.131) was also non-significant, consistent with absent institutional mandates. The UTAUT–BI relationships were not significantly moderated by age, gender, or academic level. The results indicate strong expressed BI to adopt LLMs among dental students; however, BI is a theoretical proxy and should not be equated with actual adoption behaviour, clinical competence, or ethical and effective LLM application. These findings offer preliminary evidence which may inform curriculum design and institutional governance for responsible LLM integration in dentistry. Introduction

The Fédération Dentaire Internationale has implemented strong governance frameworks to integrate artificial intelligence (AI) in dental education, clinical practice, and research1. AI-driven diagnostic, treatment planning, and clinical decision support (CDS) tools are essential components in digital dentistry. Growing evidence supports its integration into dental education can improve evidence-based practice, clinical reasoning, and communication2–4.

Using deep learning and natural language processing, large language models (LLMs) can recognize, create, and analyse human language5. The LLM landscape contains general-purpose foundational models and dental-specific multimodal vision language models6–9. LLM deployment across dental education, CDS, and research has fundamentally diverse performance profiles, risk levels, and governance requirements that has not been extensively investigated. Dental students confront LLMs in all three areas, even without domain-specific supervision. Instead of three discrete decisions, their adoption is best viewed as a common motivational disposition influenced by cross-domain exposure. Governance strategies must address LLM adoption within a single framework identifying the common motivational and contextual factors comprehensively. Although performance profiles and governance needs vary by domain, individual motivational variables of adoption, such as perceived usefulness, ease of use, social norms, and institutional support, are not domain specific. Thus, a single adoption assessment across all three domains is theoretically sound10.

In this study, the education domain encompasses the use of LLMs for information acquisition, examination preparation, adaptive learning, and personalised feedback in the formal dental curriculum. Exam performance has dominated in this research context. A systematic evaluation and meta-analysis found that newer models perform better on international dental license exams but fall short of clinical applicability standards11. Discipline-specific analyses further reveal that performance is uneven across specialities, with accuracy ranging from 72.4 to 85.5% on National Board Dental Examination questions, alongside statistically significant inter-model and inter-disciplinary variation12. In prosthodontics, LLM performance on specialisation examination questions has been shown to depend on querying method and model version13, with chatbot accuracy on prosthodontic education content ranging from 54.8% to 73% across platforms14. In endodontics, performance on patient-facing clinical questions varies markedly across models, with scores ranging from 4.81/5.00 for the highest-performing model to substantially lower ratings for others. Systematic misinformation tendencies have been documented regardless of overall performance level15. Beyond examination contexts, LLMs have shown potential as personalised feedback tools. AI-assisted feedback on radiographic diagnostic performance has been shown to significantly improve students’ diagnostic accuracy compared to conventional feedback under expert supervision16. Collectively, these findings suggest that while LLMs offer meaningful educational support, their performance remains variable, model-dependent, and insufficiently reliable for unsupervised use across dental specialities6,17,18.

This study defines the CDS domain as LLM use in in supporting diagnostic reasoning, treatment planning, and patient communication within supervised clinical training, where students apply LLM-generated information under faculty oversight rather than as independent decision-makers. LLM potential has been documented across multiple dental specialities, including implantology, orofacial pain treatment, and clinical anamnesis8,19–23. Evidence shows that LLMs may quickly synthesize and disseminate clinical information but cannot duplicate expert clinical thinking6,11,24. LLMs diagnosed oral lesions with 87.4% accuracy but declined on challenging clinical issues, suggesting a complementing role under clinician supervision25. High response consistency and a mean clinical accuracy of 57.33% with significant difficulty-dependent variation in endodontics suggest that LLMs cannot replace clinical decision-making26. Variable performance across diagnostic categorization dimensions in periodontology showed unreliable independent diagnostic support27. Expert oversight in implantology is strengthened by context-dependent dependability across complicated contexts8. LLMs supporting prosthetic treatments are also insufficient for unsupervised clinical application in prosthodontics28. Image-dependent applications further compound this performance ceiling. LLM reliability decreases significantly in visual data interpretation tasks including radiographic analysis and orthodontic aesthetic evaluation due to visual input restrictions of text-trained models29,30. Thus, text-based versus image-based activities are a clinically important LLM deployment barrier. Multi-model evaluations show that no single LLM optimises accuracy, comprehensiveness, and readability across clinical circumstances31, reinforcing the need for expert monitoring in clinical dental settings. Speciality, job complexity, and model affect the gap between LLM information retrieval and clinical decision-making skill. This variation highlights the need for research in empirical adoption factors to facilitate contextually appropriate, governance-informed LLM integration across dentistry specialities.

LLM support for literature searching, evidence synthesis, academic writing, and hypothesis development in undergraduate and postgraduate dentistry research is the research domain in this study. LLMs are useful for literature screening, data synthesis, and scientific writing32–34. They pose a unique and underestimated risk in research. LLMs may fabricate citations, misattribute findings, or produce statistically consistent but factually wrong summaries, worsening evidence quality and scientific integrity24,34. Their use in dental research is still unregulated34, and their inclusion into systematic review and literature screening workflows is beginning to be formally examined33.

Despite rapid LLM integration in dentistry, formal incorporation into dental curricula in Saudi Arabia remains limited, and institution-specific governance frameworks are lacking. In a countrywide survey of 1,370 Saudi dental students, 58.1% were aware of LLM capabilities, but only 15% reported institutional guidelines, with ethical concerns about plagiarism and academic dishonesty35. The Saudi Data and Artificial Intelligence Authority (SDAIA) has issued generative AI guidelines for government employees and the public36,37 and announced a national cross-disciplinary AI curriculum for university students38. However, there are no national LLM-specific adoption policy or governance framework for health professions or dental education at present. Therefore, Saudi dental students are already adopting LLMs in an unstructured and unregulated manner, making empirical evidence on adoption factors an important educational and governance priority. LLMs have documented “hallucination” potential24,34, which raises concerns about dependability and patient safety in high-stakes dental education and clinical practice11,24.

Overall, the evidence across these three domains indicates that LLM performance in dentistry is variable, context-dependent, and poorly controlled. Performance studies cannot address the factors that motivate or deter the dental students from adopting this technology. Understanding adoption factors is necessary to translate performance information into effective educational and governance frameworks, and this constitutes the specific research problem addressed in this study.

Saudi Vision 2030 aims for healthcare digitalization are driving institutional momentum to integrate AI and digital technology into undergraduate dental curricula. A countrywide assessment of Saudi dental schools indicated that just 64.4% had formally embraced digital dental technology39, and dedicated AI education including LLM-specific proficiency is inconsistent. Updated dental curricula for AI and LLM-specific literacy are identified as a global educational need17,18. This adoption-focused evidence is lacking in Saudi Arabia which can aid curriculum designers and institutional policymakers to understand the students’ motivational willingness to embrace these technologies, making this study’s findings relevant to Saudi dentistry education policy.

The original unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT) and its successor UTAUT2 have been validated for assessing technology adoption in medical education and healthcare40–43, but their application in dental education is underexplored. UTAUT2 includes consumer-focused constructs, but the original UTAUT model was chosen for this study because it was developed and validated for organizational and institutional technology adoption contexts10, making it the most theoretically appropriate framework for structured, professionalized educational settings like dental education. Prior UTAUT studies have examined general AI and chatbot adoption in medical education40–43, but none have investigated LLM-specific adoption in dental education. Informal student use, inter-model variation in task-specific performance, and the lack of institutional governance indicate that performance benchmarking alone does not sufficiently represent the dynamics of LLM adoption. Consequently, theoretical frameworks that examine motivational and contextual factors influencing technology acceptance, such as UTAUT, are necessary to explain adoption trends in formal educational environments.

The original UTAUT model evaluates four constructs predicting behavioural intention (BI) to adopt LLM technology. Performance expectancy (PE) is the assumption that LLMs improve academic and professional performance in education, CDS, and research. Effort expectancy (EE) measures perceived ease of learning and using LLMs. Social influence (SI) refers to the influence of faculty, peers, and institutional norms on students’ willingness to adopt LLMs. Facilitating conditions (FC) includes available institutional support, infrastructure, and training to support LLM use. Figure 1 shows that all four constructs directly influence BI44. According to UTAUT, larger PE, EE, SI, and FC indicate stronger BI. These relationships are additive rather than hierarchical, and the model does not assume that one construct subsumes another10. BI captures students expressed willingness and likelihood of using LLMs across educational, CDS, and research contexts. BI is recognized as a theoretically proven proxy for technology adoption behavior10 and should not be equated with actual adoption behaviour, clinical competence, or ethical and effective LLM application. The UTAUT model also identifies four moderating variables (age, gender, experience, and voluntariness of use) that affect construct-BI correlations10. Age and gender were explored as demographic factors in this study. Since LLM-specific competency is not formally integrated into the AI literacy component of dental curriculum in Saudi Arabia, experience and voluntariness of use were excluded from the model. The documented LLM-specific dangers are theoretically relevant to these constructs. Hallucination and fluctuating precision may lower PE because inconsistent outputs lower students’ LLM utility perceptions10,24,34. Text-based engagement may exaggerate EE regardless of clinical reliability, generating a gap between apparent ease-of-use and real performance10. Institutional oversight deficits in FC may limit adoption preparedness and increase patient safety hazards10,11,24. SI may influence risk awareness since faculty endorsement and peer norms may encourage cautious LLM usage or normalise uncritical use due to the lack of guidelines10,35,39. Collectively, examining the constructs influencing BI holds significant implications for both educational practice and institutional governance of LLM adoption.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the factors influencing the BI to adopt LLMs among dental students across educational, CDS, and research, using the UTAUT framework. This study addressed the following research questions (RQ). RQ1: Which UTAUT constructs are significant factors influencing dental students’ BI to adopt LLMs across educational, CDS, and research contexts? RQ2: Does the UTAUT model, as a whole, significantly explain the variance in BI among dental students in this context? RQ3: Do demographic variables, including age, gender, and academic level, moderate the relationships between UTAUT constructs and BI?

The UTAUT model provided the foundation for the following five hypotheses: H1: PE is positively associated with BI to adopt LLMs among dental students. H2: EE is positively associated with BI to adopt LLMs among dental students. H3: SI is positively associated with BI to adopt LLMs among dental students. H4: FC is positively associated with BI to adopt LLMs among dental students. H5: The UTAUT model significantly predicts BI to adopt LLMs among dental students.

Theoretically, this study is among the first to apply UTAUT framework to LLM-specific adoption in dental education, CDS, and research. Practically, since Saudi dental students are already adopting LLM in an unstructured and ungoverned manner, the findings provide preliminary empirical evidence to inform curriculum design and institutional governance for LLM integration within Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 healthcare digitalisation mission. The gap between expressed intention and actual behaviour is well recognised within technology adoption research10, and findings should be interpreted within this boundary.

Methods

Ethical considerations

This research was conducted after approval from the Majmaah University Institutional Review Board (IRB No. MUREC-Dec.09/COM-2025/193) and was conducted in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki and its revision in 2024. Informed consent was obtained from all students prior to participation in the study.

Sample size calculation

An a priori sample size calculation was performed in G*Power (version 3.1) using multiple linear regression parameters (fixed model, R2 deviation from zero), with a two-tailed significance level of 0.05, 80% power, and four UTAUT constructs as independent variables. Based on prior UTAUT-based research within the same regional context44 reporting a model R2 of 0.32 for the Saudi dental community, a conservative R2 of 0.15 was adopted for the present study to account for the LLM-specific context and student-specific population, where adoption may be influenced by factors beyond the classical UTAUT constructs, thereby reducing the expected explanatory power. The corresponding Cohen’s effect size was derived using the formula f2 = R2/(1 − R2) = 0.15/0.85 ≈ 0.1845, representing a small-to-medium effect, and yielding a minimum required sample size of 85 participants. The final sample of 700 participants substantially exceeded both the regression-based minimum of 85 and the structural equation modelling (SEM) minimum of 20046, yielding a participant-to-observed-variable ratio of 46.7:1, well above the recommended 10:1 threshold47. This large sample was chosen to maximise statistical power, parameter estimate stability, and institution-wide generalisability. Based on f2 = 0.18 with four constructs at a two-tailed significance level of 0.05, the achieved statistical power exceeded 99%, confirming adequacy for all primary SEM and post-hoc regression analyses.

Study instrument and survey design

The questionnaire (Supplementary file online) was developed by Author 1 and adapted from previous research44 with minor modifications to align with the study aim (Copyright: Sage Publications 2024). The first section included demographic data, namely age, gender, and academic level, identified as the most contextually relevant variables for a student-specific institutional sample42. Among the four UTAUT moderating variables, age and gender were collected and examined as direct moderators. Academic level was retained as a contextual demographic variable, though it does not directly correspond to any UTAUT moderator. The second section operationalised the UTAUT constructs, adapted with minimal modification from the original model10 by replacing the word “system” with “LLMs”, and recorded participants’ perceptions using five-point Likert-scale items ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The questionnaire comprised 17 items distributed across five constructs: PE (3 items), EE (4 items), SI (3 items), FC (4 items), and BI (3 items). This study measures BI to adopt LLMs, reflecting students’ motivational readiness, and should not be equated with actual adoption behaviour, clinical competence, or ethical and effective LLM application.

The questionnaire was administered in both English and Arabic. In Saudi Arabian health professions education, English serves as the primary medium of instruction across dental and medical programmes43, and the Arabic version was offered as a language preference option for respondents more comfortable completing the survey in Arabic. The Arabic version was developed through a forward-backward translation procedure. It was first translated into Arabic by a native Arabic-speaking team member with professional English proficiency (Author 2), then back-translated into English by a second independent team member with similar proficiency (Author 3) to verify conceptual and linguistic equivalence. Discrepancies were resolved through team consensus (Authors 4–7) before finalizing the Arabic version. Content validity of both language versions was jointly evaluated by a panel of four subject-matter experts with demonstrated proficiency in AI applications and survey methodology. Each expert independently rated every item on a four-point relevance scale (1 = not relevant, 2 = somewhat relevant, 3 = quite relevant, 4 = highly relevant), consistent with the Content Validity Index (CVI) framework recommended by Lynn48 and Polit and Beck49. The item-level CVI (I-CVI) was calculated as the proportion of experts assigning a rating of 3 or 4, with an acceptable threshold of ≥ 0.75 for a four-expert panel48,49; the scale-level CVI (S-CVI/Ave) was computed as the mean of all item-level CVIs, with a threshold of ≥ 0.90 indicative of excellent content validity48,49. All items achieved an I-CVI of 1.00 and an overall S-CVI/Ave of 1.00, which were well above the acceptable thresholds, confirming excellent content validity across both language versions, and no revisions to item wording were required prior to instrument finalisation.

The instrument was subsequently piloted among twenty dental students per language version to assess clarity and internal consistency prior to full-scale administration. All pilot participants completed the survey without requesting clarification or reporting difficulty, providing informal evidence of item clarity and comprehensibility. The pilot study demonstrated acceptable internal consistency across all UTAUT constructs in both language versions, with Cronbach’s alpha (α) values ranging from 0.606 to 0.882 for the Arabic version (n = 20) and from 0.657 to 0.873 for the English version (n = 20). The marginally lower α values for the FC construct in both versions (α = 0.606 and 0.660, respectively) were acceptable for a pilot sample, consistent with the widely accepted threshold of α ≥ 0.60 for exploratory and pilot studies50. These findings confirmed the overall suitability of the instrument for full-scale administration without modification.

Study design and sampling

This cross-sectional study is reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) recommendations51. A cross-sectional, web-based survey was conducted among dental students aged 18 or older enrolled in universities in central Saudi Arabia during December 2025 and January 2026. Convenience sampling was utilized to acquire data via WhatsApp, Snapchat, X (previously Twitter), and Telegram, which represent the most widely used social media platforms among Saudi university students52. No formal sampling frame was defined; hence the number of respondents, coverage, and response rates could not be calculated prospectively. This pragmatic sampling approach is used in web-based dental and medical education research, especially for geographically scattered student populations53.

This sampling approach may introduce selection bias, as students with greater digital literacy or prior LLM exposure may have been more likely to participate, potentially overrepresenting positive BI. The potential risk was reduced by several strategies. To maximize reach and institutional diversity, the survey was disseminated across multiple platforms, a large sample of participants was targeted to improve estimate stability, and the demographic distribution of respondents across age, gender, academic level, and university was examined post-hoc to partially characterize sample representativeness without a formal response rate54. These measures decrease but cannot eliminate sampling bias and thus findings should be interpreted with caution regarding generalisability beyond this population. Participants were informed that their responses were anonymous, that there were no right or incorrect answers, and that their participation would not affect their academic standing to reduce social desirability bias.

Several measures were implemented to ensure data quality and integrity. The survey was administered via Google Forms with account-based sign-in and one-response-per-account which prevented duplicate entries in the survey. All items were set as mandatory, ensuring only fully completed responses were accepted and eliminating incomplete submissions. The survey was structured sequentially, starting with demographic screening questions. Conditional branching logic automatically redirected ineligible respondents to a disqualification page, preventing further progression. UTAUT model access was limited to eligible respondents. As a result, all submitted responses were complete, met inclusion criteria, and were included in the study.

Statistical analysis

Multi-group confirmatory factor analysis (MGCFA) was conducted using IBM AMOS (version 29; IBM Corp., Chicago, USA), to confirm that the choice of questionnaire language version did not introduce measurement artefacts. Since language preference was recorded as an explicit variable at the outset of the survey, participants were classified into English (n = 500) and Arabic (n = 200) language groups for this analysis. Both groups independently met the minimum sample size requirements for reliable MGCFA46,47. Three sequential nested models were evaluated: a configural model confirming the same factor structure across both language groups, a metric invariance model constraining factor loadings to equality across groups, and a scalar invariance model additionally constraining item intercepts to equality. Measurement equivalence was evaluated using changes in the comparative fit index (ΔCFI ≤ 0.010) and root mean square error of approximation (ΔRMSEA ≤ 0.015) between nested models55.

To assess the potential threat of common method bias (CMB), which may arise from collecting all variables through a single self-administered questionnaire at a single time point, two complementary approaches were employed. Harman’s single-factor test was performed by entering all study variables into an exploratory factor analysis; if the first unrotated factor accounted for less than 50% of the total variance, CMB was considered unlikely to severely distort the observed associations56. A common method factor model within the CFA framework was additionally attempted as a more rigorous statistical assessment of CMB, though its estimability was contingent on model convergence and the absence of inadmissible solutions.

Descriptive statistics were used to describe demographic variables and the UTAUT constructs. Internal consistency of the Likert scale items was assessed using α. To evaluate the measurement model, a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to assess construct validity, convergent validity via average variance extracted (AVE ≥ 0.50) and composite reliability (CR ≥ 0.70), and discriminant validity using the heterotrait-monotrait (HTMT ≤ 0.90) ratio criterion57,58. SEM was selected as the primary analytical approach as it simultaneously estimates the direct effects of UTAUT constructs (PE, EE, SI, and FC) on BI while accounting for measurement error and evaluating both measurement and structural components within a single model. To obtain confidence intervals (CI) for the structural path coefficients and enhance the robustness of parameter estimates, bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples was performed in AMOS, generating 95% CI for all standardised path coefficients. Model fit was evaluated using the following indices: chi-square/df ratio (≤ 3.0), CFI ≥ 0.90, Tucker-Lewis index (TLI ≥ 0.90), RMSEA ≤ 0.08, and standardised root mean square residual (SRMR ≤ 0.08). Demographic variables were not included as covariates in the primary SEM analysis, as the study aimed to evaluate the direct effects of UTAUT constructs on BI. They were subsequently incorporated as covariates in a hierarchical multiple regression model as a post-hoc sensitivity analysis to examine their potential influence on BI beyond the UTAUT constructs.

Prior to conducting hierarchical multiple regression, all standard analytical assumptions were systematically examined using IBM SPSS (version 31; SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). Normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test in conjunction with visual inspection of normal quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots and frequency histograms of standardised residuals. Visual assessment of plots of standardised residuals against standardised predicted values and the Breusch-Pagan test assessed homoscedasticity. Linearity was examined through partial regression plots. Independence of residuals was verified using the Durbin-Watson statistic, with values between 1.5 and 2.5 considered indicative of acceptable independence54. Outliers were identified through standardised residuals exceeding ± 3.0, leverage values, and Cook’s distance, with values exceeding 1.0 indicating sensitivity analysis. Multicollinearity among UTAUT constructs was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) and tolerance values, given the theoretical intercorrelations anticipated among PE, EE, SI, and FC. Conservative thresholds of VIF < 5.0 and tolerance > 0.20 were adopted as primary criteria47,57, with VIF > 10.0 and tolerance < 0.10 considered indicative of severe multicollinearity requiring remediation47,54,57. Condition indices and variance decomposition proportions were used to detect collinearity patterns not recorded by VIF, with indices exceeding 30 considered problematic57. All assumptions were satisfactorily met prior to interpreting the regression results. The F-statistic and p-value were used to evaluate model fit at each hierarchical step. Demographic factors were quantified by measuring the change in R2 (ΔR2) and the corresponding F-change statistic (ΔF) and its significance54,57. Explanatory power was assessed using R2 and adjusted R2, with the latter being the favoured measure due to its compensation for covariates and sample size54. Effect size for each regression model was quantified using Cohen’s f2, calculated as f2 = R2/(1 − R2), and interpreted according to established benchmarks: small (f2 ≥ 0.02), medium (f2 ≥ 0.15), and large (f2 ≥ 0.35)45. The 95% CI was reported for all regression coefficients to characterise the precision of parameter estimates. All analyses were conducted at a two-tailed significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Sample characteristics

Table 1 presents the sample characteristics. Out of 700 dental students, 500 (71.43%) completed the English version and 200 (28.57%) completed the Arabic version. The majority were aged 18 to 22 years (n = 425, 60.71%), followed by those aged 23 to 27 years (n = 243, 34.71%), and 28 years or older (n = 32, 4.57%), comparable with the expected age profile of dental students. Most participants were female (n = 411, 58.71%), reflecting the dental student demographic composition in Saudi Arabia59. The majority were undergraduates in preclinical (n = 200, 28.57%) and clinical (n = 318, 45.43%) training phases, collectively representing 74% of the sample. Internship students comprised 16.86% (n = 118) and postgraduate students 9.14% (n = 64). Six Saudi universities offering dentistry programs in the central region responded to the survey, comprising Majmaah University (n = 255, 36.43%), Qassim University (n = 158, 22.57%), King Saud University (n = 102, 14.57%), Princess Nourah Bint Abdulrahman University (n = 87, 12.43%), Riyadh Elm University (n = 66, 9.43%), and Vision Colleges (n = 32, 4.57%).

Measurement equivalence across language versions

Both the English (n = 500) and Arabic (n = 200) groups independently met the minimum sample size requirements for reliable MGCFA46,47. The configural model confirmed that the same five-factor UTAUT structure was supported across both language groups (CFI = 0.948, TLI = 0.932, RMSEA = 0.086, SRMR = 0.043). The metric invariance model demonstrated that factor loadings were equivalent across groups (ΔCFI = 0.001, ΔRMSEA = − 0.004, Δχ2 (10) = 2.885, p = 0.984), supporting metric invariance. The scalar invariance model additionally confirmed that item intercepts were equivalent across groups (ΔCFI = 0.000, ΔRMSEA = − 0.003, Δχ2(10) = 7.455, p = 0.682), supporting full scalar invariance. All ΔCFI values were well within the recommended criterion of ≤ 0.01055 and all ΔRMSEA values within ≤ 0.01560. These findings confirm that the UTAUT constructs were measured equivalently across both language versions, fully justifying the pooling of data for subsequent analyses.

CMB assessment

To assess the potential threat of CMB, Harman’s single-factor test was performed by entering all 17 original study items into an unrotated principal components analysis, and the first unrotated factor accounted for 47.744% of the total variance, below the 50% threshold56, suggesting that CMB is unlikely to severely distort the observed associations among constructs. The common method factor model within the CFA framework was not estimable due to negative residual variances (Heywood case), likely reflecting the high inter-construct correlations and the resulting inability to distinguish the method factor from the substantive UTAUT constructs.

Descriptive statistics and internal consistency

Table 2 presents the descriptive statistics and internal consistency values for all constructs. Mean scores ranged from 3.47 (FC, 95% CI [3.42, 3.53]) to 4.18 (PE, 95% CI [4.11, 4.24]), with the mean BI score of 4.10 (95% CI [4.03, 4.16]) reflecting a strong expressed intention to adopt LLMs in education, CDS, and research. The full response scale was utilised across the sample (range: 1.00–5.00) for all constructs. Median values were closely aligned with means across all constructs (PE: 4.33, EE: 4.00, SI: 4.00, FC: 3.50, BI: 4.00), consistent with the generally negative skewness observed across constructs. Internal consistency was satisfactory across all constructs, with PE (α = 0.901) and SI (α = 0.907) demonstrating excellent reliability, EE (α = 0.884) and BI (α = 0.861) demonstrating good reliability, and FC (α = 0.678) demonstrating borderline acceptable reliability50. The borderline α for the original four-item FC scale is attributable to the weak item compatibility of FC3 and FC4, which demonstrated low corrected item-total correlations (r = 0.439 and r = 0.359 respectively). These items were subsequently removed based on unacceptably low standardised factor loadings in the measurement model (FC3 = 0.340; FC4 = 0.244), as reported below. The retained two-item FC construct demonstrated strong inter-item correlation (r = 0.709), α = 0.829, AVE = 0.714, and CR = 0.833, confirming satisfactory construct validity50,57. All construct scores demonstrated acceptable distributional characteristics, with skewness values ranging from − 1.46 (PE) to − 0.26 (FC) and kurtosis values ranging from − 0.55 (SI) to 2.87 (PE), all within the acceptable thresholds of |skewness|< 2 and |kurtosis|< 7 for large samples54.

Measurement model

An initial five-factor CFA was conducted on all 17 items. FC3 and FC4 demonstrated unacceptably low standardised factor loadings (0.340 and 0.244 respectively, below the recommended threshold of ≥ 0.5047,57) and were therefore removed from the measurement model. A revised five-factor CFA was subsequently conducted on the 15-item model, which demonstrated satisfactory fit: χ2 (80) = 371.443, χ2/df = 4.643, Robust CFI = 0.932, Robust TLI = 0.911, RMSEA = 0.072 [90% CI: 0.067, 0.078], SRMR = 0.046. Although the chi-square/df ratio exceeded the conventional threshold of 3.0, this is widely recognised as an expected outcome with large samples (n = 700) and was therefore not considered indicative of poor fit46,47.

All standardised factor loadings were statistically significant (p < 0.001) and ranged from 0.653 to 0.952, with BI1 (0.653) being marginally acceptable. Convergent validity was supported for all constructs, with AVE values exceeding the 0.50 threshold57 (PE = 0.762, EE = 0.664, SI = 0.769, FC = 0.714, BI = 0.699) and CR values exceeding 0.7057 (PE = 0.905, EE = 0.887, SI = 0.909, FC = 0.833, BI = 0.872). Discriminant validity was assessed using the HTMT criterion58; all construct pairs demonstrated HTMT values below the threshold of 0.90, with the exception of the PE-EE pair (HTMT = 0.886), which approached but did not exceed this threshold. This borderline value is theoretically consistent with the established conceptual proximity between PE and EE within the UTAUT framework10.

Structural model assessment and hypothesis testing

The structural model was estimated on the basis of the satisfactory measurement model fit confirmed in the CFA (Robust CFI = 0.932, RMSEA = 0.072)46,47. Bias-corrected bootstrapped 95% CI based on 5,000 resamples were generated for all structural path coefficients.

The UTAUT constructs collectively explained 44.1% of the variance in BI (R2 = 0.441, 95% CI [0.387, 0.495], p < 0.001, Cohen’s f2 = 0.789, large effect45), supporting H5. EE emerged as the strongest significant factor influencing BI (β = 0.351, z = 2.378, p = 0.017, 95% CI [0.056, 0.518], excluding zero), supporting H2; FC was also a significant factor (β = 0.136, z = 2.260, p = 0.024, 95% CI [0.019, 0.183], excluding zero), supporting H4. PE (β = 0.158, z = 1.398, p = 0.162, 95% CI [− 0.048, 0.312], crossing zero) and SI (β = 0.090, z = 1.509, p = 0.131, 95% CI [− 0.012, 0.134], crossing zero) were not significant factors influencing BI, and H1 and H3 were not supported. The non-significance of PE, despite its significant association in the post-hoc regression (β = 0.160, p = 0.002), is likely attributable to the high latent inter-construct correlation between PE and EE (r = 0.870), which reduces the unique structural contribution of PE when both constructs are modelled simultaneously. Latent inter-construct correlations ranged from 0.478 (SI-BI) to 0.870 (PE-EE)58, yet both constructs were retained as theoretically distinct UTAUT constructs. Post-hoc sensitivity analysis

Prior to hierarchical multiple regression, all standard analytical assumptions were examined. Residual normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (W = 0.970, p < 0.001); the significant result is expected with n = 700 and is not considered indicative of meaningful non-normality54. Independence of residuals was supported (Durbin–Watson = 1.713). Linearity was supported by significant positive Pearson correlations between all UTAUT constructs and BI (r = 0.416–0.572, all p < 0.001), confirmed through partial regression plots. The Breusch-Pagan test indicated statistically significant heteroscedasticity (Lagrange Multiplier, LM = 58.803, p < 0.001), likely attributable to the bounded nature of Likert-based mean scores and the negatively skewed BI distribution54. As the regression served as a post-hoc sensitivity analysis, and coefficient estimates remain unbiased under heteroscedasticity in large samples54, this violation does not affect the interpretation of findings. No multicollinearity concerns were identified, with all VIF values below 5.0 (range: 1.541–3.352) and tolerance values above 0.2057. Twelve cases (1.7%) showed standardised residuals exceeding ± 3.0; however, Cook’s distance exceeded 1.0 for no cases, indicating no influential outliers. The maximum condition index was 21.110, below the threshold of 3057. With the exception of homoscedasticity, all remaining assumptions were satisfactorily met.

In Block 1, the UTAUT constructs significantly predicted BI (R2 = 0.359, Adjusted R2 = 0.355, F(4,695) = 97.315, p < 0.001, RMSE = 0.681, Cohen’s f2 = 0.560, large effect45). EE demonstrated the strongest association (β = 0.331, t = 5.951, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.217, 0.430]), followed by PE (β = 0.160, t = 3.163, p = 0.002, 95% CI [0.061, 0.259]) and FC (β = 0.129, t = 3.432, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.063, 0.232]). SI was not significant (β = 0.075, t = 1.882, p = 0.060, 95% CI [− 0.003, 0.120]).

To examine the potential influence of demographic variables on BI beyond the UTAUT constructs, they were incorporated as covariates in Block 2. The overall Block 2 model remained significant (R2 = 0.365, Adjusted R2 = 0.356, F(10,689) = 39.570, p < 0.001, RMSE = 0.681, Cohen’s f2 = 0.574, large effect45). However, the addition of demographic variables did not significantly improve model fit over Block 1 (ΔR2 = 0.006, ΔF(6,689) = 1.044, p = 0.396). None of the demographic variables significantly predicted BI. Age comparisons of 23 to 27 vs 18 to 22 years (β = 0.046, t = 0.844, p = 0.399, 95% CI [− 0.062, 0.155]) and 28 years or older vs 18 to 22 years (β = − 0.044, t = − 0.350, p = 0.726, 95% CI [− 0.290, 0.202]), gender (β = 0.011, t = 0.210, p = 0.833, 95% CI [− 0.093, 0.115]), and academic level comparisons of clinical vs preclinical (β = − 0.018, t = − 0.279, p = 0.781, 95% CI [− 0.142, 0.107]), internship vs preclinical (β = 0.133, t = 1.641, p = 0.101, 95% CI [− 0.026, 0.293]), and postgraduate vs preclinical (β = 0.113, t = 1.106, p = 0.269, 95% CI [− 0.087, 0.312]) were all non-significant. These findings indicate that no demographic variable significantly predicted BI beyond the four UTAUT constructs, suggesting that the UTAUT-BI relationships were not substantively confounded by age, gender, or academic level in this sample.

Two R2 estimates are reported in this study. The SEM-derived R2 of 0.441 indicates the squared multiple correlation for the latent endogenous variable and is the primary indicator of explanatory power. Post-hoc hierarchical regression showed a lower adjusted R2 of 0.355, as it does not compensate for measurement error and acts on observed composite scores. These two estimates are therefore not comparable.

Discussion

The UTAUT model implies that all constructs independently and positively predict BI, with these relationships moderated by age, gender, experience, and voluntariness of use. The findings of this study partially support these assumptions. EE and FC constructs influenced BI, multicollinearity with EE weakened PE, and SI was non-significant without institutional mandates. Age and gender did not moderate any of these relationships. High self-directed adoption preparedness is indicated by the mean BI score of 4.10 (95% CI [4.03, 4.16]) and negatively skewed distribution (skewness = − 0.96). Since LLM-specific proficiency are not formally integrated into Saudi dental curricula and no institutional mandates exist39, this pattern supports the UTAUT proposition that perceived personal value drives voluntary technology adoption10 and the intrinsic motivation constructs of UTAUT240,43.

EE emerged as the strongest significant factor influencing BI (β = 0.351), consistent with findings from medical education, healthcare, and university settings where ease of use is reliably associated with stronger adoption intentions43,61–65. EE dominance in voluntary pre-adoption contexts is theoretically expected within UTAUT10, as performance beliefs may be more uniformly held due to informal prior exposure while effort perceptions vary more substantially between individuals, generating greater discriminating power for EE. This dominance reflects a specific characteristic of LLMs. LLMs use natural language online interfaces, unlike previous dental AI systems that required expert installation or domain-specific training29. This accessibility is supported by meaningful educational value, with LLM accuracy on standardized endodontic patient education questions ranging from 3.83 to 4.81 out of 5.0015, prosthodontic exam performance reaching 73–92%14, and AI-assisted feedback improving dental students’ radiographic diagnostic performance16. However, in image-dependent tasks such as bone age prediction, even the best-performing model achieved only a moderate correlation with reference standards (r = 0.546)29, suggesting that EE dominance may reflect accessibility in straightforward tasks rather than a generalized perception of low effort across all dental use contexts. Maintaining accessible LLM integration pathways may represent a priority within institutional governance frameworks, provided such access does not compromise critical appraisal and clinical accuracy13,33,66.

Unlike EE, PE did not reach statistical significance in the primary SEM analysis (p = 0.162), though its significance in the post-hoc regression (p = 0.002) indicates that perceived utility remains theoretically meaningful. Non-significance in the structural model is most parsimoniously explained by the high latent inter-construct correlation between PE and EE (r = 0.870), consistent with their established conceptual proximity within UTAUT10 and further indicated by the borderline HTMT value (0.886). This high correlation may reflect an LLM-specific characteristic whereby the natural language interface that makes LLMs easy to use is also the interface through which students assess their performance, conflating effort and performance cognitions at the interface level. LLM performance variability between models and dental specialties8,11,12,14,28 may increase between-person PE variance not reflected by construct items. These findings are broadly consistent with the research by Alqaisi et al.43 who suggested that EE and PE were the most influential factors influencing BI in Jordanian medical students using multiple regression. This SEM method utilized in this study adds methodological subtlety. The high latent inter-construct correlation suppresses the independent contribution of PE in the structural model, which regression-based designs cannot detect. Accordingly, institutional communication focused primarily on LLM performance metrics is unlikely to drive BI in this population, suggesting that motivational and contextual factors warrant greater emphasis in adoption strategies.

While EE and PE reflect individual LLM interaction evaluations, FC captures the broader institutional context. FC was a significant factor influencing BI despite having the lowest mean score (mean = 3.47), showing that adoption intentions are partly linked to insufficient institutional infrastructure. FC is more predictive in UTAUT when institutional support is limited10, and in the LLM context, FC includes faculty oversight, validated prompting protocols, and governance frameworks, which are lacking in Saudi dental education39. Scoping reviews have consistently identified the lack of defined curriculum frameworks and faculty-endorsed guidelines as barriers to appropriate LLM implementation17,18, while resource-constrained e-learning and dentistry AI adoption have shown similar FC effects67,68. Converging speciality-specific evidence supports FC. Expert oversight is essential regardless of model performance tier in endodontic patient education15, radiographic content generation16, clinical anamnesis23, orofacial pain management19, and prosthodontics28, where LLM responses were too low for clinical use without expert validation. No single LLM optimises accuracy, comprehensiveness, and readability across specialities8,31. These findings suggest that the influence of FC reflects identified governance needs rather than discretionary institutional practice, underscoring the importance of structured support frameworks for responsible LLM integration.

In contrast to EE and FC, SI was a non-significant factor influencing BI (β = 0.090, p = 0.131). The mean SI score of 3.76 indicates moderate perceived social influence, however SI did not influence BI. Within UTAUT, SI predominantly drives adoption in mandatory contexts10, and its non-significance here is logically coherent given the lack of institutional LLM mandates or formal policies in Saudi dental curricula35,39. This finding is noteworthy in Saudi Arabia’s collectivist culture, where SI would theoretically be expected to play a stronger role in BI. One plausible explanation is that LLM use is conducted privately and individually, reducing the social visibility that typically activates peer and authority influence mechanisms in collectivist environments. The lack of faculty awareness and institutional endorsement of LLMs66 suggests that the primary SI channel within the institutional context remains insufficiently operationalised. Evidence from mandatory adoption contexts documents significant SI effects among medical and university students62,65, and Liu et al.62 proposed SI to be a significant factor influencing BI among clinical medicine students in a structured institutional context, contrasting directly with the present findings and supporting a context-specific interpretation. However, SI may emerge as a significant predictor after formal LLM integration into the dental curriculum, although peer-led and authority-driven adoption strategies are unlikely to serve as effective drivers within the prevailing voluntary adoption context.

The SEM-derived R2 of 0.441 surpasses the pre-specified planning estimate (R2 = 0.15) but remains below the ideal UTAUT specification benchmark of 69%10. Prior LLM experience is a significant unmeasured covariate, given documented inter-model performance variation across dental specialities31. Students using high-performing models may have different PE and EE beliefs than those who are using lower-performing ones. Additional constructs absent from the original UTAUT model, including, hedonic motivation, habit, digital literacy, ethical concerns66,69, or openness to technology70, may further explain BI variance in this cohort. The hypothesis support for H2 and H4, in the absence of significant effects for all four constructs, partially diverges from the original UTAUT model, which predicts independent positive contributions from PE, EE, SI, and FC to BI. In this voluntary adoption context, EE and FC were effective, while PE was attenuated by multicollinearity and SI was ineffective without institutional mandates.

The findings have practical implications across three areas, namely curriculum design informed by the dominance of EE, institutional governance informed by the significance of FC despite its low mean score, and national policy situated within Saudi Arabia’s healthcare digitalisation mission.

The FC findings may have direct governance implications. FC was a significant factor influencing BI, but its low mean score (3.47) suggests that institutional circumstances for LLM adoption are lacking. This gap warrants attention at multiple levels. For institutional leaders, the FC deficit suggests that foundational infrastructure, such as institutional access to validated LLM platforms, faculty training programs for LLM oversight, and clear usage policies, may be a governance priority, though cross-sectional data cannot establish causal direction71. For faculty, the dominance of EE suggests that structured, low-effort LLM access points like faculty-endorsed prompting guides and supervised comparison exercises between LLM outputs and authoritative reference materials may be the best reference point for faculty to integrate LLMs into teaching practice13,31,66. For curriculum designers, the lack of a correlation between academic level and BI suggests that LLM proficiency activities may be introduced from early curriculum stages rather than being reserved for advanced clinical training, but confirmation in larger samples is required before informing curriculum policy. Institutional policy development may benefit from alignment with the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties and the National Commission for Academic Accreditation and Assessment, which neither specifies LLM-specific proficiency for dental programs currently. This represents a regulatory gap that institutional policymakers may consider addressing proactively. How these considerations translate into curriculum policy, institutional governance, or national policy alignment remains a question for implementation research rather than adoption surveys.

Translating these institutional recommendations into an educational structure requires a coherent competency framework. Based on these findings, we tentatively propose a conceptual LLM competency framework with three domains, namely knowledge, skills, and values. This framework needs empirical validation before implementation because it is theoretically informed and claims no originality, as it is based on broader literature. Li and Qin42 proposed curriculum recommendations for AI integration in medical education comprising of knowledge, attitude, and skill domains, while Rincón et al.72 mapped AI competency requirements in medical education through a scoping review encompassing similar dimensions. In dental education, scoping and systematic reviews have identified knowledge, application, and ethical awareness as recurring competency themes17,18, and faculty-level studies have highlighted awareness, knowledge, and attitudes as the main development areas66. However, existing frameworks have not adequately addressed LLM-specific characteristics, including hallucination risks, inter-model performance variability, and the accuracy-readability trade-off, or the institutional governance gap identified in the present study. Accordingly, the proposed LLM competency framework is grounded in the knowledge, skills, and values domain structure, adapted to address the competency requirements of responsible LLM integration within dental education.

In the knowledge domain, the FC gap identified in this sample suggests that students currently lack sufficient institutional support for LLM engagement. Addressing this gap may begin with developing students’ understanding of LLM capabilities and limitations, including hallucination risks24,34, alongside structured prompting proficiency, which has been shown to improve LLM accuracy in specialised contexts13. In the skills domain, the dominance of EE suggests that activities reducing perceived effort while maintaining clinical rigour are likely to support adoption. Case-based learning requiring critical appraisal of LLM-generated clinical inferences against evidence-based dental guidelines may enhance clinical judgement, without conflating LLM-generated inferences with clinical competence73–75. LLM performance varies across selected dental specialities, from passing-level accuracy in dental licensing examinations11,12 to limited and variable accuracy across prosthodontic examination questions, particularly in removable partial dentures14,28. Structured exposure to this performance range may help students develop realistic expectations of LLM capabilities. In the values domain, ethical guidelines emphasising the distinction between informational support and clinical decision authority, alongside reflective engagement with the governance implications identified in this sample, may cultivate responsible professional LLM utilization73,76. Unlike the knowledge and skills domains, the values domain is proposed based on professional ethics principles and governance literature rather than empirical evidence and represents the most speculative of the three domains. The LLM competency framework represent conceptual directions for future empirical validation through longitudinal and intervention-based research before translation into institutional practice.

This study may have implications within the context of Vision 2030, particularly the Health Sector Transformation Program’s commitment to leveraging AI in healthcare and the Human Capability Development Program’s priority of aligning higher education outcomes with future digital workforce needs77,78. These implications are grounded in adoption intention data and should be interpreted as conceptual directions rather than policy recommendations.

Several limitations constrain the interpretation and generalisability of these findings. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between UTAUT constructs and BI, as all associations are concurrent and bidirectional causation cannot be excluded. A further limitation concerns the construct validity of BI as an outcome measure, being a theoretical proxy for technology adoption and measures motivational readiness at a single time point10. Convenience sampling and open-access social media distribution may have introduced selection bias by over-representing digitally engaged students53, potentially limiting the generalisability of the observed BI ratings and inter-construct associations. Thus, the observed BI scores and construct connections may not fully reflect the opinions of less digitally engaged students. As the survey was openly distributed via social media over 2 months without a defined sampling frame, the total number of potential respondents could not be ascertained, precluding formal non-response bias assessment. The sample coverage does not represent all dental institutions in central Saudi Arabia, and conclusions may not apply to unsampled institutions, other regions or international dental education contexts. Prior LLM experience and voluntariness of use were not recorded, preventing a comprehensive study of all theoretically required UTAUT moderators. Voluntariness moderates the SI-BI relationship in non-mandatory adoption, making it important. Through familiarity and perceived competence effects, prior experience with specific LLM tools may also influence BI, although its relative contribution to unexplained variance in this population is unknown. Cross-sectional single-source designs still have CMB issues. Although procedural anonymity restrictions were adopted and Harman’s single-factor test showed no severe CMB, the common method factor model was non-estimable, preventing statistical ruling out of CMB. Temporal separation and marker variable techniques were design-level limitations that could not be remediated within the constraints of this study. Despite anonymity assurances and procedural measures to minimise social desirability bias, the intention-behaviour gap inherent in self-reported BI measurement may have inflated BI ratings relative to actual LLM adoption behaviour.

Future research should prioritise several methodological improvements. Longitudinal designs tracking both BI and actual LLM adoption behaviour over time would enable the first direct examination of the intention-to-behaviour gap in this population, addressing a recognised empirical void in LLM adoption research. Probability-based or stratified sampling across all dental institutions in Saudi Arabia and in international contexts would improve representativeness and permit formal assessment of non-response bias. Future studies should systematically collect prior LLM experience data, including which models were used, for which tasks, and with what perceived accuracy outcomes, to more fully operationalise the UTAUT moderators and to test whether differential experience with specific LLM tools and their perceived task accuracy accounts for unexplained variance in BI. To strengthen causal inference and permit more rigorous CMB evaluation, future studies should consider temporal separation of construct and outcome measurements across two survey administrations, alongside prospective inclusion of marker variables. Incorporation of validated social desirability scales, in future survey instruments would permit statistical control of response bias and strengthen the interpretability of self-reported BI scores. Extended UTAUT2 or hybrid technology acceptance models incorporating digital literacy, perceived risk, ethical concerns, and intrinsic motivation may achieve greater explanatory power in LLM-specific adoption contexts63,68,69. Comparative studies across dental specialities, including prosthodontics, orthodontics, and endodontics, where LLM performance profiles differ substantially14,23,28,30, would allow assessment of whether the observed construct relationships generalise across clinical training contexts. Finally, intervention studies testing structured LLM integration strategies, such as expert-supervised prompting laboratories, case-based learning modules, and faculty-endorsed model selection frameworks, would provide empirically grounded evidence for translating expressed intention into structured educational practice, moving the field beyond BI as a proxy measure toward confirmed behavioural and learning outcomes.

Conclusion

The UTAUT model was supported overall, accounting for 44.1% of BI variance in this sample. Among the four constructs, EE was the strongest significant factor influencing BI, indicating that perceived ease of use was the most influential factor affecting LLM adoption in this voluntary, unstructured and unregulated context. FC was also significant despite its low mean score, indicating that adoption is linked to perceived institutional support even when it is insufficient. The PE was non-significant, due to its high latent correlation with EE, and SI was also non-significant, consistent with absent institutional mandates. The results indicate strong expressed BI to adopt LLMs among dental students; however, BI is a theoretical proxy and should not be equated with actual adoption behaviour, clinical competence, or ethical and effective LLM application. These findings offer preliminary evidence which may inform curriculum design and institutional governance for responsible LLM integration in dentistry. The tentatively proposed LLM competency framework represents theoretical concepts which require empirical validation before implementation. The implications are grounded in adoption intention data and should be interpreted as conceptual directions rather than policy recommendations. Within the limitations of this study, dental schools in central Saudi Arabia and similar contexts may consider these findings to create responsible, critically informed, and ethically governed LLM integration guidelines for dental education, CDS, and research within Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 healthcare digitalisation mission.
