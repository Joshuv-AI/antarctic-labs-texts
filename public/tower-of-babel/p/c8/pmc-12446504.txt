Risk factors and survival impact of severe radiation-related late toxicities in head and neck cancer–a cohort study
Authors: John Mohan Mathew, Jolie Ringash, Jie Su, Wilfred Levin, Scott Bratman, BC John Cho, Ezra Hahn, Ali Hosni Abdalaty, Andrew Hope, John Kim, Andrew McPartlin, Brian O'Sullivan, C Jillian Tsai, John Waldron, Anna Spreafico, David Goldstein, Melanie Woodside, Dustin Jan Cruz, Samantha Parmelee, Jennifer Yin Yee Kwan, Shao Hui Huang, Philip Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundRadiation late toxicities (RLTs) are complications of curative-intent radiotherapy (RT) for head and neck cancer (HNC) and are increasingly relevant due to younger age at diagnosis and improved survival outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a cohort study of HNC patients who received ≥50 Gy as part of curative treatment between January 2003 and December 2020 at a Canadian quaternary cancer center. Risk factors for severe RLTs (≥RTOG Grade 3) were evaluated using time-to-event analyses. Actuarial rates of RLT and overall survival (OS) were estimated using competing risk and Kaplan–Meier methods, respectively. Cox proportional hazard models identified factors associated with RLT and OS.FindingsAmong 7622 patients, 12.6% (n = 958) developed RLTs without disease progression, with a 5-year actuarial incidence of 16% (95% CI: 15–16). A survivors subgroup (n = 4650) with ≥2 years of follow-up and no recurrence was also identified. Modifiable risk factors for RLTs included RT technique, dose, neck irradiation, neck dissection, smoking status, and chemotherapy (p ≤ 0.012). Non-modifiable factors included younger age, female sex, and oral cavity primaries (p ≤ 0.012). In multivariable analysis, RLTs were associated with increased mortality (HR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.8–2.5, p < 0.001), but RLT's impact on OS was lessened among patients referred to the Adult Radiation Late Effects Clinic (ARLEC) (HR = 1.7, 95% CI: 1.3–2.4).InterpretationRLTs are common and associated with worse survival among HNC survivors. Identification of modifiable risk factors provides opportunities for prevention. Multidisciplinary management of RLTs in specialized clinics may help improve the outcomes in this growing survivorship population.FundingNo external funding was utilized for this study.Keywords: Radiation late toxicities, Head and neck cancer, Cancer survivorship, Treatment toxicities, Late effects clinics

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We performed this study according to the CONSORT guidelines for identifying the risk factors for radiation late toxicities (RLTs) in Head and Neck cancers (HNC) and assessing the impact of RLTs on Overall Survival (OS).

We searched PUBMED from 1st January 2000 up to and including 30th June 2025, for articles published in English on the assessment of risk factors and survival impact of RLTs in HNC.

The key search criteria used to assess risk factors for late toxicities were (“head and neck cancer” OR “oral cavity cancer” OR “oropharyngeal cancer” OR “hypopharyngeal cancer” OR “nasopharyngeal cancer” OR “laryngeal cancer”) AND (“radiotherapy late effects” OR “radiation late effects” OR “late toxicities” OR “radiotherapy complications” OR “long-term toxicity”) AND (“risk factors” OR “predictors” OR “prognostic factors”). This returned 33 studies.

The search strategy for studies assessing impact of Radiation late toxicities on OS was (“head and neck cancer” OR “oral cavity cancer” OR “oropharyngeal cancer” OR “hypopharyngeal cancer” OR “nasopharyngeal cancer” OR “laryngeal cancer”) AND (“radiotherapy late effects” OR “radiation late effects” OR “late toxicities” OR “radiotherapy complications” OR “long-term toxicity”) AND (“overall survival” OR “OS” OR “mortality” OR “prognosis” OR “survival outcome” OR “survival analysis”) AND (“impact” OR “effect” OR “association” OR “influence” OR “correlation” OR “predictive value”) which returned 32 studies.

Previous evidence on RLTs in HNC largely comes from post-hoc analysis of randomized trials with selective populations, smaller retrospective cohorts, and other prospective observational studies. Findings on risk factors have been inconsistent, and the impact of RLTs on OS remains unclear. Few studies evaluated the role of specialized clinics in managing long-term RLTs.

Added value of this study

To our knowledge, this is one of the largest real-world studies to evaluate the risk factors and long-term impact of RLTs in HNCs outside the controlled setting of clinical trials. We identified key modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors for developing RLTs in both the full HNC cohort and disease-free survivors. Actuarial rates of RLTs increased over time and were associated with inferior OS. Multidisciplinary management of RLTs through a specialized radiation late effects clinic (ARLEC) trended to decrease the hazard of death, suggesting a potential aftercare pathway to improve outcomes in this growing population.

Implications of all the available evidence

Patients at high risk of developing RLTs after curative treatment for HNC may benefit from continued long-term monitoring by oncologists, community physicians, or survivorship clinics, as these toxicities can emerge or worsen over time and are associated with inferior overall survival. While referral to a multidisciplinary late effects clinic was not statistically associated with improved survival, trends suggest potential benefit, supporting the need for prospective evaluation of structured survivorship interventions.

Introduction

Radiation late toxicities (RLTs), defined as toxicities that occur or persist for >90 days after completion of treatment, significantly impact the quality of life for head and neck cancer (HNC) patients.1, 2, 3 All normal tissues can develop RLTs, including skin, muscle, salivary glands, mucosa, and bones, leading to conditions such as fibrosis, lymphedema, xerostomia, long-term dysphagia, dependence on feeding tubes, and osteoradionecrosis (ORN). With an increasing number of HNC being diagnosed in the younger patient population and progressively improving survival rates, the burden of treatment-related toxicities is also rising.4, 5, 6

Studies that examine the risks of RLT in HNC are heterogeneous in terms of study population, design, treatment techniques, and follow-up periods. This heterogeneity can lead to conflicting findings.7 Published literature has reported several factors contributing to a higher risk for RLTs after diagnosis. These include non-modifiable baseline factors such as older age, female sex, advanced T and N categories, primary tumors in the oral cavity/oropharynx/larynx/hypopharynx, and potentially modifiable factors including smoking and other treatment-related factors like use of chemotherapy, higher doses to organs at risk (OARs), increased weight loss during radiotherapy (RT), and neck dissection following RT.2,8, 9, 10, 11

Identifying patients at higher risk of RLTs can facilitate early initiation of prophylactic measures, closer monitoring, and prompt intervention, potentially improving outcomes related to toxicity.12,13 An understanding of how these risk factors evolve from diagnosis through the post-treatment phase will help us gain a clearer understanding of their dynamic nature throughout treatment and recovery. This insight will support the development of targeted interventions to improve long-term health outcomes in cancer survivors.

The impact of RLT on the survival of HNC patients has been insufficiently studied. While previous research suggests that older patients with post-treatment sequelae like aspiration pneumonia may have higher rates of mortality, comprehensive data on the broader implications of common RLTs following HNC treatment remain limited.14

Therefore, we conducted a large cohort study in a curatively treated and prospectively followed HNC population that received RT dose ≥50 Gy. Our primary objective was to identify modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors associated with severe RLT development in all HNC patients who received radiation as part of curative treatment and for survivors (patients with >2-year follow-up without disease relapse). Specifically, the full cohort was analyzed to identify risk factors at diagnosis and estimate the rate of RLTs for all patients receiving curative radiation, while the purpose of analyzing the survivors was to identify the relevance of these risk factors once they had completed radiation and did not have any cancer recurrence. The secondary objective was to compare overall survival (OS) between survivors with and without severe RLTs.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study including all patients who received ≥50 Gy RT dose as part of curative treatment (definitive, pre-operative, or postoperative) for HNC at our institution from January 1, 2003 to December 31, 2020, to identify the risk factors for severe RLTs. The factors identified are described as the risk factors for the entire cohort. Patients with documented RLTs had ≥ Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG) grade 3 toxicities arising or persisting ≥6 months following completion of RT.15

Risk factors were also analyzed for the survivors, a subgroup of the entire cohort (Fig. 1). Survivors were defined as patients without locoregional recurrence (LRR), distant metastasis (DM), and at least ≥2 years of follow-up from the date of completion of RT. Survivors were further divided into cases and controls. Patients without RLTs were defined as controls (Group A). Cases were defined as patients with RLTs without recurrent disease managed without referral to the specialized adult radiation late effects clinic (ARLEC) in the institute (Group B) or patients, also without recurrence, who were managed in ARLEC for their RLT (Group C). Patients who suffered one or more RLTs were considered as one case. A 2-year follow-up threshold was set to exclude patients who lacked sufficient follow-up for the detection of RLTs. We used 6 months as a cut-off for RLTs to ensure the exclusion of toxicities resolving within 6 months of RT and to confirm the delayed nature of the studied toxicities. Patients with LRR or DM were excluded to ensure that symptoms were attributable to RLTs rather than recurrent disease mimicking RLTs. This approach also allowed for a more accurate assessment of the impact of RLTs on patient outcomes. Re-irradiation significantly increases the risk of RLTs and was excluded to ensure a clearer evaluation of RLTs in patients treated with an initial radiation course. OS was compared between the cases and controls in the survivors.

CONSORT flow diagram of patients in the study. HNC–Head and neck cancer, Group A–Controls, Group B–Cases not managed in the Adult Radiation Late Effects Clinic (ARLEC), Group C–Cases managed in ARLEC.

Risk factors were classified into non-modifiable (age, sex, stage, and site of disease) and potentially modifiable factors (radiation technique and dose, volume of neck irradiated, neck dissection, smoking status, and use of chemotherapy).

Information was collected from a prospectively maintained institutional database of outcomes and toxicities of HNC,16 linked with the institutional cancer registry for confirmation of dates of death/last follow-up, and supplemented with chart review. The date of last follow-up for patients included was in December 2023. The prospective database documented the date of onset and the nature and severity of RLTs observed. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies and received institutional research ethics approval (20-5969) before initiation.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the patient, tumor, and treatment characteristics. Time-to-RLT development was analyzed using competing risk methods such as cumulative incidence function and the Fine–Gray model, and calculated from the date of RT completion for the entire cohort and for the survivors. The competing risks for RLTs were recurrent disease and death for the entire cohort and death for survivors. Multivariable analysis was performed to identify the risk factors for developing RLTs for the two groups.

Actuarial rates of RLT were determined for the entire cohort using the cumulative incidence function, where death or recurrence without RLT was the competing risk. OS was defined as the interval between the date of completion of RT to death or censored at the date of the last follow-up. Survival estimates were obtained using the Kaplan–Meier analysis for the survivors.

A time dependent Cox proportional hazards model was used to compare the effect of RLT management on OS in the survivors adjusted for age, stage, ECOG PS, HPV status, smoking status, disease site, and treatment. RLT was the time dependent covariate in the model. The OS in the RLT groups was estimated from this Cox model and was plotted in the adjusted survival curve.

The statistical significance threshold for all analyses was defined as p < 0.05 (two-sided). The statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.3.1) and SAS (v9.4).

Risk factors for the entire cohort (n = 7622)

Potentially modifiable risk factors associated with a higher risk of developing RLTs were the use of non-intensity modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) treatment, higher volume of neck irradiated, higher RT dose, neck dissection as part of treatment, and use of chemotherapy (p < 0.001). The non-modifiable risk factors included younger age and female sex (p < 0.001). Larynx, skin, salivary gland, and head and neck sarcoma primaries (p < 0.001) were at a lower risk of developing RLTs. The actuarial rates (95% CI) of RLTs for the entire cohort were estimated to be 12% (11–13) at 3 years and 16% (15–16) at 5 years (Fig. 3).

Risk factors for survivors (n = 4650)

Potentially modifiable factors for RLTs were non-IMRT treatment, higher volume of neck irradiated, higher RT dose, use of chemotherapy (p < 0.001), and neck dissection (p = 0.007). Smoking at diagnosis significantly increased the risk of survivors developing RLTs (p = 0.012). Non-modifiable factors included sex and primary site (p ≤ 0.003), with women being disadvantaged. Patients with oral cavity tumors had a significantly higher risk of RLTs (p = 0.002), while those with laryngeal, skin, and salivary gland tumors were at lower risk (p < 0.02). Younger age showed a trend towards increased risk but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.052).

Survival comparison

An unadjusted comparison of survival between cases (Group B and C) and controls (Group A) was not significant (p = 0.327), with a 3-year OS of 96% (95% CI: 0.95–0.98) in cases and 97% (95% CI: 0.96–0.97) in controls. The 5-year OS was 90% for both cases and controls, with a 95% CI of 88–92% for cases and 89–92% for controls.

However, using a time-dependent Cox proportional model adjusting for age, sex, performance status, tumor site, stage, use of chemotherapy, smoking status, HPV status, and primary treatment modality, patients with RLTs were at significantly higher risk of death as compared to patients without RLT (HR (95% CI) 2.1 (1.8–2.5), p = 0.001) (Table S3 in the Supplementary Appendix shows the results of the multivariable analysis for OS in survivors).

An exploratory analysis compared the adjusted survival of Groups A, B, and C using the time dependant multivariable Cox proportional hazards model. Patients who developed radiation late toxicities (RLTs) but were not referred to ARLEC (Group B) had significantly worse overall survival compared to controls without RLTs (Group A) (HR = 2.2, 95% CI: 1.9–2.7, p < 0.001). Patients with RLTs referred to ARLEC (Group C) also had worse outcomes than controls (HR = 1.7, 95% CI: 1.3–2.4, p < 0.001), but a direct comparison between Group C and Group B did not reach statistical significance (HR = 1.29, 95% CI: 0.93–1.78, p = 0.13). Discussion

In this large, real-world cohort study of HNC patients treated with curative-intent radiotherapy, we identified both baseline and potentially modifiable factors associated with the development of RLTs. The risk of RLTs increased with time, underscoring their cumulative nature. Importantly, the presence of RLTs was independently associated with inferior OS, highlighting the prognostic relevance of late effects beyond disease control. However, care provided through specialized multidisciplinary clinics like ARLEC may help mitigate the adverse impact of RLT on OS, although further refinement in patient selection and prospective evaluation is required to confirm this effect.

As patients and physicians embark on the journey of cancer care at the time of diagnosis, it is hoped that both parties can become less concerned about disease recurrence over time and shift their focus toward aftercare and survivorship issues. With advancements in treatment techniques, an increasing number of HNC patients are surviving longer, and consequently a growing population is dealing with treatment-related long-term side effects that affect quality of life and physical well-being.17,18 As the incidence and severity of RLTs accumulate over the years, it is important to identify the cumulative impact of the iatrogenic effects and the evolution of risk factors associated with their development to adapt patient care over time based on an individual's risk profile. However, investigating RLTs remains challenging due to the need for large patient cohorts with long follow-up periods, the lack of reliable documentation of toxicities, and the inherent subjectivity in grading toxicities. Despite these challenges, this study represents one of the largest investigations into RLTs, aiming to identify risk factors for all patients treated with RT (entire cohort) and among survivors and to assess the impact of these toxicities on survival in HNC patients.

A detailed evaluation of potentially modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors helped us identify a combination of baseline and treatment-related factors that puts patients at significantly higher risk for RLTs. Analysis of risk factors for the entire cohort of patients receiving treatment and for survivors made the risk factor analysis more robust, emphasizing the relevance of different factors in the varying stages of living with the disease. Most risk factors identified for RLTs in our study were consistent for the entire cohort and among survivors (Fig. 2). Identification of factors such as the use of IMRT, risk-adapted approaches to RT dose and volume selection and neck dissections, decisions to use concurrent systemic therapies, oral cavity, primaries, smoking status, and female sex may help us modify our approaches to minimize toxicities where possible, and ensure appropriate management for those at higher risk for developing RLTs.

Within the spectrum of modifiable treatment factors, some emerged as especially potent contributors to long-term toxicity. Neck irradiation was one of the strongest factors identified, with more than twice the hazard when compared to people not receiving neck RT. A significantly higher risk for RLTs was found to be associated with oral cavity tumors. Significant overlap of target volumes with critical structures like the mandible, oral cavity, and constrictor muscles could be critical in the higher risk of RLTs observed. Multimodality treatment approaches routinely employed in advanced oral cavity primaries with a combination of surgery, adjuvant radiation ± chemotherapy would also further increase the risk.

Smoking status at diagnosis strongly predicts RLTs among survivors. Current smoking at diagnosis is linked to poorer treatment outcomes,19 and continued smoking further worsens treatment outcomes and increases late toxicities.10,20,21 Evidence shows that quitting smoking at diagnosis improves toxicity rates and treatment outcomes.22 In conjunction with these observations, our findings underscore the importance of advising patients to quit smoking to enhance cancer outcomes and reduce the risk of long-term treatment-related toxicities.

Neck dissections as part of treatment was also a significant factor contributing to RLTs in the entire cohort and the survivors. This probably reflects the higher risk of RLTs associated with multimodality treatment approaches.

We identified younger age as a possible risk factor for developing RLTs among the entire cohort and the survivors. There are conflicting reports about the impact of age on RLTs.8 However, as the late effects of radiation are cumulative, it is reasonable to expect worsening late effects for younger individuals as the time they have to potentially develop RLTs also will be higher.

We observed a 16% actuarial rate of RLTs at 5 years, gradually increasing with time. This is lower than the 21% grade 3 and 4 laryngopharyngeal 2-year toxicities reported by Machtay et al. and the >30% late toxicity rates at 10 years reported by Forastiere et al.8,23 The use of conventional 2-dimensional treatment techniques in these studies could be a major factor in the increased rates of RLTs reported.8,23 However, given the cumulative nature of RT effects on normal tissues, toxicity rates are expected to rise with longer follow-up durations.24

The role of acute radiation-related toxicities in prolonging the duration of treatment and worsening quality of life has been well described in HNC.25 Similarly, RLTs are also known to impair quality of life,1,26 RTOG 9111 has reported mortalities to be intimately associated with RLTs in laryngeal cancers treated with radiation but there is limited data on how RLTs affect treatment and survival outcomes in the overall HNC population.23 In our study, patients with grade 3–4 RLTs had a higher risk for death (HR = 2.1 (1.8–2.5), p = 0.001) compared to those without RLTs. Complications such as increased aspiration risk in patients with dysphagia and challenges in maintaining oral hygiene due to osteoradionecrosis may have contributed to the reduced survival observed.14,27

Randomized clinical trials would provide the most robust evidence to evaluate the impact of specialized clinics in managing RLTs, but such studies may be constrained by ethical and practical challenges.28 In the absence of high-level trial data, our results support the potential role of dedicated late-effect clinics and survivorship programs, particularly in high-volume cancer centers. While referral to ARLEC was not associated with a statistically significant improvement in overall survival compared to non-referred toxicity cases in the adjusted time-dependent Cox model, the survival curves suggest a possible attenuation of risk in referred patients. This trend suggests the potential benefits of continued care coordinated by specialized survivorship multidisciplinary clinics where optimization of medications and collaborations between the patients' physicians, dentists, oral and maxillofacial surgeons, specialized nurses, allied health professionals, and cancer rehabilitation and survivorship services were made possible.29 However, given the lack of statistical significance, these findings should be interpreted with caution with future research needed to focus on identifying the individual and combined contributions of the multi-disciplinary survivorship care components in alleviating RLTs and their impact on outcomes.

The rising rates of RLTs with time, in conjunction with the identification of a high-risk subset of population, such as younger patients, smokers with oral cavity tumors, and tumors requiring multimodality treatment approaches, may justify the need for prolonged surveillance for this group. These patients could benefit from early prophylactic measures and targeted interventions to mitigate RLTs.13 Optimizing pre-treatment dental assessment and intervention and avoiding dental extractions with non-invasive alternatives like dental filling, encouraging oral intake, and swallowing exercises when feasible while on radiation treatment are measures that have been recommended to reduce the risks of RLTs like ORN and dysphagia.12,13 Given their elevated risk, participation in clinical trials exploring management strategies for RLTs could be particularly beneficial for this high-risk group.29,30

With the limitations in long-term oncology follow-up, sustained vigilance for RLTs must extend beyond the cancer center. Our findings of inferior survival outcomes among patients with RLTs underscore the importance of coordinated survivorship care. As patients transition from active treatment to survivorship, care pathways should include dedicated survivorship clinics to which oncologists can hand over follow-up, especially for the high-risk population. Empowering primary care physicians and nurse practitioners with the awareness and tools to recognize potential late toxicities and enabling effective communications between the health care providers and the oncologists can also facilitate the delivery of appropriate preventive and supportive management that can address the broader health needs of cancer survivors while ensuring timely co-management of emerging treatment-related complications.31, 32, 33, 34, 35

This study's limitations include its single-center retrospective design, population heterogeneity, and inherent subjectivity in grading toxicities. Additionally, a detailed risk factor analysis for each toxicity was not performed. Factors such as organ at-risk dosimetry, weight loss, comorbidities, the different settings of chemotherapy (vis-à-vis induction, adjuvant, and concurrent settings), and impact of continued smoking post-diagnosis were not evaluated. Quality of life differences between patients with and without toxicities were not assessed. The RLT rates reported for the entire cohort could have been influenced by the variable follow-up durations for the different head and neck primary sites in our center. Information about race/ethnicity was not reported. Finally, we did not consider the intent of radiation (definitive or adjuvant) as a separate risk factor for RLT but used the radiation dose as a proxy.

Nevertheless, the single-institution setting of the study provided consistency in treatment protocols, referral practices, and follow-up schedules across a large and diverse population of HNC patients. Furthermore, the extensive clinical experience at our high-volume cancer center strengthened the reliability of both treatment delivery and toxicity reporting. Importantly, the observed increase in RLTs over time and its association with inferior survival underscored the need to address late effects in survivorship care. As compared to patients treated at high-volume centers, the true burden of RLTs in broader clinical settings and community centers may be even greater, making our findings potentially conservative estimates of a widespread issue.36

While our findings suggest some signals of benefit in decreasing the impact of RLTs on survival among patients managed through ARLEC, this observation may be influenced by selection and referral biases, with incomplete consideration of potential confounders. Variables such as types of managed toxicities, patient profiles influencing clinic referrals, the role of adjunct services like dental and speech-language pathology teams, socio-economic support, the influence of coherence and resilience of patients, and the absence of cause-specific mortality data may have also contributed to the results. Despite these limitations, the study's strengths include its large sample size, robust methodology, and comprehensive approach to analyzing RLT risk factors and survival outcomes among patients at diagnosis and survivors.

Conclusion

This study offers valuable insights into RLT risk factors and their evolving impact on HNC patients and survivors. Our findings suggest that patients who develop RLTs experience worse survival, highlighting a vulnerable population. It highlights the need for proactive measures to prevent and manage toxicities, particularly in high-risk groups, and underscores the potential role of specialized late-effects clinics in improving survivorship care. Further research is needed to refine treatment strategies at diagnosis and during survivorship to mitigate the long-term burden of RLTs.
