Evaluation of the effect of omega-3 fatty acids supplementation as an adjunct to non-surgical periodontal therapy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Narges Naghsh, Faeze Karimi, Mohammad Reza Maracy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesOmega-3 fatty acids have been investigated due to their anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties identified in basic studies. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effect of omega-3 fatty acids as a supplement to SRP on improving periodontal clinical indices.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in reputable databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Embase, ProQuest, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, and Cochrane, from inception to August 11, 2025, for randomized controlled clinical trials. Eligible articles were selected to compare the effect of SRP plus omega-3 supplementation versus SRP alone or placebo in patients with periodontitis. The quality of the studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 2.0), and data on probing pocket depth (PPD) and clinical attachment level (CAL) were extracted. Meta-analysis was performed using a random-effects model in Stata software (version 17).ResultsA total of 21 studies were incorporated into this systematic review. The statistical analysis conducted on 18 of these studies demonstrated that omega-3 supplementation as an adjunct to SRP resulted in statistically significant improvements in periodontal indices. This improvement was observed in CAL, with a mean difference (MD) of 0.54 mm (95% CI: 0.36–0.72), and in PPD, with an MD of 0.57 mm (95% CI: 0.4–0.74). The beneficial effects of this treatment were sustained over time and were more pronounced in long-term assessments (6–12 months).ConclusionOmega-3 supplementation may offer a small adjunctive benefit, specifically for CAL gain. These results should be interpreted with caution given very high heterogeneity and moderate/low certainty evidence.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09314-2.Keywords: Fatty Acids, Fatty acids, Omega 3, Root planning, Periodontitis, Periodontal index

Introduction

Oral diseases significantly impact the quality of life related to oral health, affecting an individual’s overall well-being, performance, psychological state, and social interactions [1]. Periodontitis is a significant oral disease characterized as a multifactorial inflammatory condition. It arises from the host immune response to the accumulation of microbial plaque biofilm, ultimately leading to the progressive deterioration of the supporting structures of the teeth [2]. Periodontitis significantly affects individuals’ quality of life by directly impairing the vital functions of the mouth [3]. Destruction of periodontal tissues in advanced stages leads to weakness in chewing ability, restrictions in food selection, and consequent digestive disorders and malnutrition [4]. In addition, impaired pronunciation of words and decreased self-confidence due to aesthetic problems disrupt individuals’ social interactions. Chronic pain caused by this disease can lead to sleep disturbances and reduced work productivity, which, in addition to negative effects on mental health, impose a significant economic burden on both the individual and society [5].

Based on strong scientific evidence, periodontitis, as a chronic disease, has a significant relationship with a wide range of systemic diseases. This connection can be attributed to shared pathophysiological mechanisms and the potential dissemination of periodontal pathogens into systemic circulation [6]. Numerous studies have recognized periodontitis as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease, highlighting a bidirectional relationship that exacerbates disease incidence and complications. Additionally, associations have been observed between periodontitis and other health conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis, respiratory diseases, chronic kidney disease, and certain types of cancer [7, 8].

Scaling and root planing (SRP) are fundamental interventions in the management of periodontal disease. These procedures serve as a form of antimicrobial therapy by eliminating mechanical irritants and modifying local ecological factors within the periodontal environment. The primary objective of scaling and root planing is to effectively remove biofilm and calculus from compromised enamel and cementum, while also smoothing irregular surfaces to promote healing. When coupled with diligent personal and professional oral hygiene practices, these interventions significantly reduce the risk of plaque re-accumulation and subsequent disease progression. Clinically, the outcomes of these procedures include decreased probing depths and minimized gingival recession during periodontal assessments [9, 10].

However, different treatment strategies have been proposed in recent years to improve SRP outcomes and reduce the need for periodontal surgical interventions in patients with periodontitis. For example, various types of mouthwashes have been proposed as adjuncts to periodontal therapy to reduce periodontal disease indices [11]. Another widely utilized therapeutic approach in the management of periodontal diseases involves the administration of antibiotics. However, research indicates that the use of antibiotics in this context may expose patients to various adverse effects, including allergic reactions, nephritis, hematological disorders, gastrointestinal complications, neurological issues, and electrolyte imbalances [12]. Recently, host modulation therapies (HMT) have been proposed as a complement to SRP. Host modulation therapy is a therapeutic concept that reduces tissue destruction and stabilizes or even regenerates inflamed tissue by modulating host response factors. A growing body of evidence supports the use of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids as host modulation therapy in various chronic inflammatory diseases, such as cardiovascular diseases and rheumatoid arthritis [13].

The Omega-3 fatty acids involved in human physiology include α-linolenic acid (ALA), docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA). They are essential fatty acids because the body cannot synthesize them, and they must be obtained through the diet. Current guidelines recommend a daily intake of 200–300 mg of EPA and DHA for individuals without a history of cardiovascular disease [14]. In contrast, those with a history of cardiovascular disease should consume 2.12 g of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids daily [15]. The long-chain omega-3 fatty acids EPA and DHA are incorporated into cell membrane phospholipids, where they improve membrane fluidity and support cellular signaling. Intracellularly, EPA is largely metabolized to eicosanoids (including series-3 prostaglandins), which are markedly less pro-inflammatory than eicosanoids derived from arachidonic acid, an omega-6 fatty acid. In addition, both EPA and DHA act as precursors for specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs), such as resolvins and protectins, which actively terminate inflammatory responses and promote tissue repair. DHA, in particular, is vital for the structural integrity and function of neuronal and retinal membranes. By competing with omega-6 fatty acids for the same metabolic enzymes, EPA and DHA also reduce the formation of highly pro-inflammatory mediators, thereby exerting a broad regulatory effect on the inflammatory cascade [16–18].

Given the chronic inflammatory nature of periodontitis and the limitations of conventional therapies in fully modulating the host immune response, there is a growing need to develop novel adjunctive treatment strategies. Omega-3 fatty acids, recognized for their potent anti-inflammatory effects and their role in promoting inflammation resolution, represent a promising candidate for host modulation therapy in periodontitis management [19–21]. However, recent clinical studies have yielded conflicting results, underscoring the necessity and rationale for the present investigation. For example, while Martinez et al. reported no significant effect on clinical indices with 900 mg of omega-3 daily for 4 months [22], Maybodi et al. observed significant improvements in pocket depth and clinical attachment level with the same dose and duration [23]. On the other hand, Araujo et al. also found no additional clinical benefit with the combination of omega-3 and aspirin in patients with grade C periodontitis [24]. The findings of this study are expected to provide valuable evidence to establish the role of omega-3 in periodontal treatment protocols and to improve clinical outcomes, thereby informing dental practice. This study aimed to investigate the effect of omega-3 fatty acids as a supplement to scaling and root planing (SRP) treatment on improving clinical indices of periodontitis.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [25], and it was based on a protocol registered in the international PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD42024604626) before the initiation of the screening and data extraction phases. The complete protocol is available at the provided link: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42024604626.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria were meticulously formulated utilizing the PICO framework, which encompasses Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome. The research question was as follows: “In healthy patients with chronic periodontitis, does the addition of omega-3 supplementation to scaling and root planing (SRP) have a significant effect in the reduction of probing pocket depth (PPD) and gain of clinical attachment level (CAL)?” (Table 1).

Search strategy

This systematic review and meta-analysis involved a comprehensive search of relevant articles across several databases, including PubMed (ti/ab and MeSH), Embase, ProQuest, ScienceDirect, Cochrane, Web of Science, and Scopus from inception to August 11, 2025. The first one hundred articles from Google Scholar were also assessed manually. The general search line of this study is as follows: “Periodontal disease” OR “periodontal diseases” OR “periodontitis” OR “chronic periodontitis” OR “probing pocket depth” OR “clinical attachment loss” OR “clinical attachment level” OR “periodontal pocket depth” OR “pocket depth” AND “fatty acids, omega-3” OR “fatty acid” OR “fatty acids” OR “docosahexaenoic acid” OR “docosahexaenoic acids” OR “eicosapentaenoic acid” OR “eicosapentaenoic acids” OR “fish oil” OR “fish oils” OR “omega-3” OR “ω–3” OR “n-3 fatty acids” OR “PUFA” OR “poly unsaturated fatty acids” OR “unsaturated fatty acids” OR “long chain fatty acids” AND “scaling and root planing” OR “SRP” OR “nonsurgical periodontal therapy” OR “non-surgical therapy” OR “periodontal treatment” OR “periodontal therapy” OR “supplementation” OR “adjunct*”.

Selection process

After collecting the articles, we systematically eliminated duplicates and assessed the remaining studies based on established inclusion and exclusion criteria. This evaluation process was conducted independently by two reviewers (N. N. and F. K.), who analyzed the titles and abstracts to identify suitable articles for inclusion. In instances where there was a disagreement regarding the selection or rejection of an article, a third reviewer was consulted to reach a consensus (M. M.).

Data extraction and data items

To collect relevant data from each included study, we created a structured and pre-tested data extraction form. Two reviewers independently carried out the extraction process, thereby reducing errors and bias. Reviewers discussed and resolved discrepancies as they arose. From each eligible article, the following data items were extracted: Name of author, publication year, country, treatment groups and control group number, mean age, dosage of omega-3 per day, duration of omega-3 use (months), and outcome, including PPD and CAL (mean ± SD). This rigorous approach to data extraction ensured the comprehensive and consistent gathering of all necessary information for a thorough quantitative synthesis.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias in the studies included in this systematic review was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [26]. This tool aims to assess the methodological quality of randomized clinical trials, capturing five main areas: (D1) Bias arising from the randomization process, (D2) Bias due to deviations from intended intervention, (D3) Bias due to missing outcome data, (D4) Bias in measurement of the outcome, and (D5) Bias in selection of the reported result. In each area, studies were classified into one of three levels: “Low risk”, “some concern”, or “high risk” based on the answers to the key questions of the RoB 2 tool, and finally, an overall judgment (Overall Risk of Bias) was determined for each study.

Research synthesis methods

Forest plot assessed effect size (95% confidence interval [CI]) among the studies. The random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model was applied, as the I2 value was greater than 50%, and a Cochran’s Q test with a P-value of less than 0.05 indicated extreme heterogeneity. Galbraith plot assessed heterogeneity among the studies, while sensitivity analysis evaluated the robustness of the effects. Subgroup analysis was performed based on three outcomes/timepoints. Publication bias was assessed using a funnel plot and Egger’s test (to assess asymmetry in the funnel plot using a regression-based approach) and Begg’s test (to assess asymmetry in the funnel plot using a rank correlation approach). Nonparametric trim-and-fill analysis was employed to estimate the number of missing studies related to publication bias, if applicable. All data analyses were performed using Stata software version 17.

Results

Study selection

A total of 667 articles were identified through electronic databases and across the grey literature, including PubMed, Embase, ProQuest, ScienceDirect, Cochrane, Web of Science, and Scopus. The first one hundred articles from Google Scholar were also assessed. Following the removal of duplicate citations, 386 references were screened by title and abstract, resulting in 29 studies eligible for full-text evaluation. Seven articles did not meet the inclusion criteria. A total of 21 studies met our eligibility criteria and were included in the systematic review. Among these, 17 studies provided sufficient data (mean, standard deviation (SD), and sample size for both intervention and control groups for at least one outcome measure), allowing their inclusion in the meta-analysis. One study did not report an SD that was calculated from similar studies. Three studies reported outcomes as median and interquartile range without providing group-specific sample sizes and were excluded from the quantitative synthesis. Ultimately, 21 studies were selected for qualitative synthesis. Of these, 18 provided sufficient data for the meta-analysis (18 for PPD and 15 for CAL). A detailed flow chart is shown in Figure 1 in Appendix.

Risk of bias within studies

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the 21 included studies were rigorously appraised using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (ROB2) tool. The assessment results across the five domains of the tool are summarized in Figure 2 in Appendix. Overall, the body of evidence demonstrated a satisfactory level of quality. Out of the 21 studies, 8 studies achieved a ‘Low’ risk rating in their overall assessment. This indicates that these studies employed robust methodologies, with a low probability of biases significantly influencing their outcomes. The remaining studies were rated as having ‘Some Concerns’ (9 studies) or ‘High’ risk of bias (4 studies) in their overall assessment. Concerns in the ‘Some Concerns’ studies primarily stemmed from issues identified in specific domains, most notably in domains D3 (Bias due to missing outcome data) and D2 (Bias due to deviations from intended interventions). A smaller number of studies raised some concerns in domains D1 (Bias arising from the randomization process), D4 (Bias in measurement of outcome) and D5 (Bias in selection of the reported result). Notably, 4 studies were judged to have a ‘High’ risk of bias in their overall evaluation.

While the majority of studies still exhibited a low risk of bias, the presence of high-risk studies partially reduces confidence in the cumulative evidence. The potential for bias across the body of evidence is therefore more pronounced, suggesting that the synthesized results should be interpreted with some caution.

The body of evidence supporting this outcome has been assessed as having low certainty. This determination is based on a comprehensive meta-analysis encompassing 21 randomized controlled trials (RCTs). The low rating, attributable to the methodological rigor of the included RCTs, was maintained by a thorough systematic evaluation, which revealed low significant concerns in the critical domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, or other relevant factors. As a result, the evidence was unequivocally categorized as low certainty (⊕⊕◯◯), thereby instilling a moderate level of confidence that the true effect closely aligns with the estimated effect (Appendix 1, Table 2).

Three papers were excluded from the meta-analysis due to the method of reporting clinical attachment level (CAL) and probing pocket depth (PPD) as median and interquartile range (IQR) without reporting the sample size in each group. The first paper [22], , entitled “The impact of non-surgical periodontal treatment on serum levels of long chain–polyunsaturated fatty acids: A pilot randomized clinical trial”; the second [27], was “Serum level changes of long chain-polyunsaturated fatty acids in patients undergoing periodontal therapy combined with one year of omega-3 supplementation: A pilot randomized clinical trial”; and the third [28], , entitled “Clinical and microbiological outcomes of subgingival instrumentation supplemented with high-dose omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids in periodontal treatment – a randomized clinical trial.” Although one study [29] did not report the standard deviation (SD) for probing pocket depth, it was finally measured through similar studies.

Study characteristics

The number of participants in the studies ranged from 5 to 40 subjects, with an average age of 34.67 to 50.24 years. The majority of studies were conducted on systemically healthy patients, although two studies were carried out on post-menopausal women [30, 31]. PPD was recorded in all eighteen studies, but sixteen out of eighteen reported the amount of CAL. The duration of omega-3 supplementation coincided with the experimental period in most studies [19, 23, 30–40], starting at baseline. Diagnosis of periodontitis was based on the classification of Armitage et al. [41] in 2 studies, whereas 5 articles used the new classification of periodontitis [42]. Two articles used the Page RC and Eke PI classification [43], while the remaining 8 articles did not mention a specific category. Table 3 illustrates the detailed characteristics of studies (Appendix 1). To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, additional random-effects meta-regression analyses were performed for daily omega-3 dosage, EPA: DHA ratio and the type of periodontitis classification. No significant effect on clinical outcomes was observed for dosage (CAL: P = 0.98; PPD: P = 0.63), EPA: DHA ratio (CAL: P = 0.29; PPD: P = 0.26) and periodontitis classification regarding PPD (P > 0.05), but there was a significant effect when analyzing CAL (P < 0.05). Furthermore, a subgroup analysis was conducted based on periodontitis classification systems (1999, 2007, and 2017). The pooled effect size for CAL gain was largest in studies using the 2017 classification (MD = 0.88 mm; 95% CI: 0.65–1.10), followed by the 2007 (MD = 0.75 mm; 95% CI: 0.37–1.12) and 1999 systems (MD = 0.37 mm; 95% CI: − 0.03 to 0.77). However, the test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (P = 0.09), indicating that the classification system did not significantly modify the treatment effect on CAL (Appendix 1, Table 4; Appendix 2, Fig. 11).

Synthesis of results

Almost all studies reported the repeated mean ± SD of the two outcomes, CAL and PPD, at different times of follow-up. One study provided the amount of CAL and PPD based on median and interquartile range (IQR) [32]. To convert median (IQR) to mean ± SD), formulas were used as follows: Mean = Q3 + Q1+median / 3 and SD = (Q3 - Q1) / 1.35 [44]. Mean difference (MD) was calculated as the pooled effect size, and multivariate random-effects meta-analysis was performed in three steps:

Finally, a multivariate random-effects model was used to evaluate the mean difference (MD) and their standard deviation (SD) from two treatment groups vs. a common control.

Meta-analysis

Clinical attachment level (CAL)

The pooled analysis using a random-effects model revealed a statistically significant improvement in CAL following omega-3 supplementation compared with controls (MD = 0.54; 95% CI: 0.36–0.72). Subgroup analysis according to follow-up duration showed a gradual increase in treatment efficacy over time: 0.2 [-0.06–0.47] at 1–2 months, 0.58 [0.33–0.82] at 3–5 months, and 0.8 [0.47–1.14] at 6–12 months. This suggests a time-dependent and sustained beneficial effect of omega-3 fatty acids on periodontal tissue healing and regeneration. Heterogeneity among the studies was extreme (I2 = 99.78%, Q = 6176.52, P < 0.001; to address this, the model was performed with random effects), indicating variability in study characteristics, but the direction of effect consistently favored the intervention (Figure 3 in Appendix).

Probing pocket depth (PPD)

For PPD, the meta-analysis also demonstrated a significant reduction in probing depth in favor of omega-3 supplementation (MD = 0.57; 95% CI: 0. 4–0.74). Stratified by follow-up duration, the effects were 0.36 [0.11–0.61] at 1–2 months, 0.53 [0.29–0.77] at 3–5 months, and 0.86 [0.51–1.21] at 6–12 months. These findings indicate a consistent and clinically relevant improvement in periodontal pocket depth over time. The heterogeneity across studies was high (I2 = 99.78%, Q = 8099.95, P < 0.001; random effects model was used), yet all studies showed the same direction of improvement toward omega-3 supplementation (Fig. 4 in Appendix).

To ensure that the meta-analysis was not affected by the transformation of the median (IQR) to the mean ± SD, a sensitivity analysis was performed. This was done by removing the relevant study [32] and redrawing the forest plot. The results for both CAL and PPD showed that these two parameters were still significantly improved (Appendix 2, Figs. 5 and 6). To assess the robustness of our multivariate meta-analysis to the assumed within-study correlation (ρ) between time points, we repeated the analyses using a more conservative value of ρ = 0.25 (compared with the primary assumption of ρ = 0.5). For CAL, the pooled MD remained identical at 0.54 mm (95% CI: 0.36–0.72). For PPD, the pooled MD was 0.57 mm (95% CI: 0.40–0.75), compared with 0.57 mm (95% CI: 0.40–0.74) in the primary analysis. These results demonstrate that the conclusions of this meta-analysis are not sensitive to the choice of ρ and are highly robust (Appendix 2, Figs. 12 and 13).

Publication bias

The Galbraith plot assessed heterogeneity among the studies (Appendix 2, Figs. 7 and 8). Assessment of publication bias using Egger’s and Begg’s regression-based tests indicated no significant small-study effects for either outcome (Egger’s test: P = 0.36 for CAL and P = 0.05 for PPD; Begg’s test: P = 0.83 for CAL and P = 0.74 for PPD) (Appendix 1, Table 5). Funnel plots appeared symmetrical, supporting the absence of publication bias (Appendix 2, Figs. 9 and 10). A nonparametric trim-and-fill analysis was performed to adjust for potential publication bias. For CAL, the analysis imputed no missing studies (k = 0), and the adjusted pooled effect size remained unchanged from the original estimate. Similar results were observed for PPD. These findings confirm that the meta-analysis results are robust against publication bias (Appendix 1, Table 6).

Discussion

Due to the anti-inflammatory effects of omega-3 (ω-3)-derived mediators that could be beneficial in the treatment of chronic inflammation, periodontists have begun to use omega-3 supplementation as an adjunct to non-surgical periodontal treatment (NSPT). The rationale for using omega-3 as an adjunct to SRP extends beyond its anti-inflammatory effects. The biological mechanism of omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) is attributed to a decrease in the production of inflammatory cytokines and mediators, especially arachidonic acid–derived eicosanoids (prostaglandin E2), via the cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase pathways, thus reducing the adverse effects of the host’s immune system on periodontal tissues [46, 47]. PUFAs, particularly EPA and DHA, are incorporated into cell membrane phospholipids of gingival fibroblasts and immune cells. In addition, recent evidence has shown that omega-3 PUFAs could be considered as substrates for enzymatic conversion to specialized pro-resolving lipid mediators (SPMs) such as resolvins, protectins, and maresins, which are biosynthesized from EPA and DHA. Unlike conventional anti-inflammatory agents that non-specifically suppress inflammation, these lipid mediators actively facilitate the clearance of apoptotic neutrophils, attenuate chemokine signaling, and promote tissue regeneration [48, 49]. When SRP removes the bacterial biofilm, concomitant omega-3 supplementation provides the molecular substrates necessary for active resolution, thereby enhancing the host’s natural healing response and reducing the likelihood of persistent inflammation and subsequent attachment loss [50].

The present systematic review evaluated the effect of dietary supplementation of omega-3 on the amount of probing pocket depth and clinical attachment level following non-surgical periodontal treatment in systemically healthy patients. There are conflicting results regarding the efficacy of omega-3 as an adjunct to non-surgical periodontal therapy. Some of the studies revealed an improvement in clinical outcomes, whereas others did not show any positive evidence [10, 19, 28, 50]. Therefore, this meta-analysis was conducted to assess 18 RCTs evaluating the effect of the use of omega-3 after SRP. CAL and PPD are often known as the two conventional periodontal parameters used for assessment of the results of periodontal therapy [51].

The findings of the present meta-analysis showed an improvement of 0.54 mm in CAL gain when analyzing 15 studies and 0.57 mm in PPD reduction by analyzing 18 studies in comparison with controls. We performed subgroup analyses for both CAL and PPD according to different follow-up times. Three intervals were selected: Short (1–2 months), medium (4–5 months), and long term (6–12 months). In all three intervals, administration of omega-3 as an adjunct to NSPT, compared with NSPT alone, resulted in both CAL gain and PPD reduction increasing over time. This improvement was statistically significant for CAL gain and PPD reduction at all three intervals (P-value < 0.001). The sensitivity analysis using a fixed correlation of 0.25 yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of our main findings to the choice of correlation structure. No adverse effects were reported by participants in most studies, but in one article, six out of sixteen subjects reported nausea and irritating fish-scented halitosis [33].

The authors conducted a comprehensive search and applied rigorous inclusion criteria to minimize the risk of confounding factors. One of the most well-known risk factors in the progression of periodontitis is smoking. All participants in the included studies were non-smokers; however, two studies did not report on the smoking status of the patients [37, 40]. We also excluded studies in which patients had diabetes or any systemic disease that could influence inflammation. The formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) in diabetic patients and the binding of the receptor for AGE (RAGE) ultimately lead to altered inflammatory responses [52]. In addition, immune system dysfunction, including delayed apoptosis and impaired neutrophil phagocytosis, can increase periodontal tissue destruction and alter the response to periodontal therapy [53, 54].

Studies in which omega-3 was administered in combination with aspirin were also excluded from our research. Aspirin, as a member of the nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) class, inhibits the activity of cyclooxygenase (COX), which plays a role in the formation of prostaglandins (PGs) [55]. Since our goal was to examine only the effects of omega-3 supplementation, excluding studies that used aspirin as an anti-inflammatory helped determine the effects of this substance alone.

Two studies were carried out on post-menopausal women. In the first trial, twenty elderly women with chronic periodontitis were enrolled; of these, ten subjects in the test group took omega-3 fatty acid supplements twice daily for 12 months. Statistically significant improvements were observed in the test group when comparing PPD and CAL at baseline and after 12 months. There was also a significant difference between the test and control groups regarding these two clinical parameters [30]. In the second trial, twenty menopausal women aged 45 to 55 years were divided into test and control groups. Following SRP, patients in the test group received soft gelatin capsules containing polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) once daily for 12 months, whereas the controls received soft gelatin capsules containing olive oil. After 12 months, the authors concluded that, along with significant improvements in clinical parameters (CAL, PPD, Plaque Index, and Gingival Index), a meaningful decrease was also seen in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels in gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) as an inflammatory biomarker [31]. This may suggest that post-menopausal individuals also benefit from taking omega-3 supplements.

To contextualize our findings, we compared the observed effect sizes with the clinically meaningful thresholds proposed by Tan et al. [56]. Based on a systematic review of adjunctive therapies in periodontitis, Tan et al. proposed a Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID) of ≥ 1.0 mm reduction in PPD and ≥ 0.5 mm gain in CAL for considering an adjunctive treatment clinically meaningful. Our meta-analysis demonstrated a pooled PPD reduction of 0.57 mm (95% CI: 0.40–0.74), which falls below the MCID threshold of 1.0 mm. Therefore, based on the Tan framework, the observed PPD reduction cannot be considered clinically meaningful. CAL gain (0.54 mm; 95% CI: 0.36–0.72) meets the 0.5 mm clinically meaningful threshold (CMT). When the mean difference equals or exceeds the CMT threshold, the intervention is considered to provide a clinically meaningful benefit for that outcome. This differential finding is biologically plausible: CAL gain represents true connective tissue reattachment and periodontal regeneration, whereas PPD reduction may partly reflect reversible inflammation resolution without true attachment gain. Host-modulating agents like omega-3 fatty acids, which reduce tissue destruction via downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α) and promote specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs), may preferentially facilitate CAL gain. Thus, while Omega-3 does not reduce pocket depth sufficiently to meet the 1.0 mm threshold, it provides clinically meaningful attachment gain at the 0.5 mm threshold.

Based on the findings of this meta-analysis, the adjunctive use of omega-3 fatty acids with SRP can be recommended for systemically healthy patients with moderate to severe chronic periodontitis, particularly in cases where traditional SRP alone has yielded suboptimal results or in patients exhibiting a pronounced inflammatory phenotype. The treatment effect appears to be time-dependent, with more substantial benefits observed at 6–12 months of follow-up, suggesting that longer supplementation periods (at least 6 months) are preferable. However, it is important to note that these are mean differences; individual patients may experience greater or lesser benefit. Furthermore, even small mean differences can be clinically relevant when they occur across multiple tooth sites, as the cumulative effect on overall periodontal health is substantial.

The interpretation of a 0.5 mm mean gain in CAL or reduction in PPD at the patient level requires consideration of several factors.

First, these mean differences represent averages across multiple tooth sites, many of which may have had baseline probing depths of 4–6 mm. A 0.5 mm improvement in such sites, while modest, can shift a 5 mm pocket to 4.5 mm, potentially altering the prognosis and reducing the likelihood of disease progression. Second, the effects are not evenly distributed across patients or sites, with some individuals and locations exhibiting substantial improvements (> 1 mm), while others show little to no change. In the management of periodontitis, the primary objective is to halt disease progression and prevent tooth loss. Notably, even modest improvements in average clinical parameters have been linked to lower rates of tooth loss in long-term follow-up studies. Third, patient perception of treatment success is multifactorial, including symptoms (bleeding, pain, mobility), aesthetics, and functional outcomes. A 0.5 mm improvement may not be directly perceptible to patients, but the associated reduction in gingival inflammation often is. Clinicians should therefore communicate that omega-3 supplementation provides a modest but statistically robust adjunctive benefit, particularly in patients with moderate periodontitis and those who smoke or have other inflammatory comorbidities.

A major strength of this systematic review and meta-analysis is its rigorous approach to reducing publication bias, which is supported by quantitative statistical evidence. The authors implemented an extensive search strategy across major electronic databases, supplemented by ProQuest for grey literature and manual screening of the first 100 records in Google Scholar. This strategy was undertaken to minimize publication bias by identifying unpublished studies, ongoing trials, and non-English language reports that may have null or negative results, which are less likely to be published in peer-reviewed journals. The limitation of this approach is that grey literature may not have undergone the same rigorous peer-review process as published articles, potentially introducing methodological heterogeneity. Additionally, searching only the first 100 Google Scholar records may miss relevant studies indexed beyond this cutoff. However, this threshold was chosen based on evidence that relevant results typically appear within the first 50–100 records.

The effectiveness of these efforts was confirmed by both visual and statistical evaluations of publication bias. The funnel plot, along with Egger’s and Begg’s tests, showed non-significant results, indicating minimal evidence of publication bias. These findings enhance the credibility of the pooled effect estimates and suggest that the results are unlikely to reflect an inflated intervention effect due to preferential publication of positive studies. In addition, we performed a trim-and-fill analysis to adjust for potential publication bias. For both CAL and PPD, no missing studies were imputed, and the adjusted effect sizes remained identical to the original pooled estimates. This further supports the absence of publication bias and confirms the robustness of our findings. As a result, the conclusions of this review provide a more balanced and accurate picture of the current evidence, thereby increasing their usefulness for clinical decision-making and the development of future guidelines. This thorough handling of publication bias represents a methodological advance compared with previous systematic reviews on the topic. This meta-analysis extends previous reviews by incorporating 21 studies, including five recent trials (2023–2025). Using a multivariate model that accounted for multiple outcomes and timepoints simultaneously, along with rigorous publication bias assessment (trim-and-fill) and examination of periodontitis classifications, we provided a more robust estimate of the treatment effect and addressed prior inconsistencies.

Our meta-analysis had several limitations. First, the definition of periodontitis varied across studies. Approximately 0.44 of the studies did not specify any particular category, while 0.33 used the new classification [42]. In total, three different classifications were used. Although numerically larger effect sizes for CAL gain were observed in studies adopting the 2017 periodontitis classification compared with older systems using meta-regression analysis, the subgroup differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.09). This finding should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect the limited number of studies per subgroup or differences in baseline disease characteristics across classification eras. Future prospective studies are warranted to determine whether the 2017 classification identifies patients who are more responsive to omega-3 as a host-modulating therapy. It is also noteworthy that various omega-3 dosages were used. However, meta-regression did not reveal a significant dose-dependent effect on PPD or CAL. This may be due to the relatively narrow effective dose range or insufficient statistical power owing to the limited number of studies available for dose-based subgrouping. The meta-regression results should be interpreted with caution due to limited statistical power. Based on published guidelines for meta-regression [57], a minimum of 20–30 studies is generally required to achieve adequate power (≥ 80%) for detecting moderate effect sizes. Therefore, while we can conclude that no large dose-response effect exists within the 300–3000 mg/day range, we cannot rule out a modest effect that would require larger, individual patient-level data to identify. For EPA: DHA ratio, with only 16–18 studies, the power was low (estimated < 40%). The non-significant findings (P = 0.29 and 0.26) should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect. It is worth noting that baseline dietary omega-3 consumption was not systematically assessed in most included studies, which could introduce variability. However, the randomized controlled design of included studies mitigates this bias, as randomization should equally distribute dietary habits between intervention and control groups. Furthermore, studies typically required patients to maintain their usual diet throughout the trial period.

Although the number of participants in studies ranged from 5 to 40 subjects, funnel plots and regression-based Egger test and Begg’s test for small-study effects did not show evidence for small-study effects in the meta-analyses performed (Table 4). We considered three types of follow-up in our meta-analysis—short (1–2 months), medium (4–5 months), and long term (6–12 months)—and this discrepancy may introduce bias in the findings. Finally, we wish to highlight that when translating the outcomes of meta-analysis to clinical practice, results should be interpreted with caution.

Suggestions for future studies

It is suggested that future studies examine the number of sites studied in addition to the number of patients in both the test and control groups. In addition, as is well established, measuring clinical attachment level (CAL) and probing pocket depth (PPD) in areas with infrabony defects or after regenerative surgeries is very important to assess treatment success. Therefore, it is recommended that further research be conducted in this field to evaluate the therapeutic outcomes following omega-3 supplementation. It is also recommended that clinical trials be conducted to more accurately determine the omega-3 dosage regimen and duration of use, as well as to examine the effect of this supplement on inflammatory biomarkers in blood and gingival tissue. Future trials should adopt standardized reporting of baseline periodontal indices to facilitate such analyses.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis demonstrates that adjunctive omega-3 supplementation may lead to modest improvements in PPD and CAL following SRP, with time-dependent effects (greatest at 6–12 months). The CAL gain met the 0.5 mm clinically modest threshold, while the PPD reduction did not reach 1.0 mm. Within the limitations of variability in periodontitis classifications and limited power for dose-response analysis, the evidence supports that omega-3 may be a beneficial host-modulating adjunct to SRP, particularly when sustained for ≥ 6 months.
