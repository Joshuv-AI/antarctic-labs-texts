From clinics to classrooms: physicians play an essential role in transforming comprehensive sexuality education
Authors: Yasmin Jayasinghe, Frances W Grimstad, Judy Simms-Cendan, Ellen S Rome, Nichole Tyson, Marisa Labovsky, Maria Mercedes Pérez Alonso, Anna Torres, Zuzana Nižňanská, Abdul Ghani Nur Azurah, Bashir Issak MMed, Mónica Lijtenstein, Silvia Oizerovich, Gabriela Viviana Perrotta, Jane MacDougall, Michalina Drejza, Marina Gelin, International Federation of Paediatric, Adolescent Gynaecology (FIGIJ) Reproductive Justice Committee

ABSTRACT
BackgroundComprehensive sexuality education (CSE) equips youth with knowledge, life skills and attitudes to achieve healthy respectful relationships, and reach their optimal life and reproductive goals. CSE is enshrined within the right to education, health and well-being. The aspiration is for all youth to have access to CSE to reach their full potential. Great gains have been made, but there are significant economic, geographic and cultural barriers to implementation globally, leaving youth at risk for unwanted pregnancy, sexual violence, sexually transmitted infection, adversely impacting their future productivity and independence. Physicians have the potential to play a significant role in multiple domains of CSE including policy, advocacy and research. Background

Across cultures and geography, most societies believe there is an obligation to provide care and education that maximizes a young person’s potential to achieve optimal life and reproductive goals [1]. Comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) (a curriculum-based process of learning about the cognitive, emotional, physical and social aspects of sexuality), aims to support these goals [2]. However, it remains a critical unmet need globally for many adolescents. CSE has been proven to prevent sexual violence [1], reduce adolescent pregnancy [3] and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) [4]. Misinformation and politicization has contributed to CSE programs being reduced or eliminated, resulting in reduced access to education and care [5, 6]. This creates a significant public health challenge, as lack of CSE places millions of youth at risk of harm from HIV acquisition, unwanted pregnancy, and gender-based violence. These challenges disrupt life trajectories, endangering the health and welfare of young people, their offspring, and communities [7].

Positive sexual and reproductive health indicators are inextricably linked to educational and economic opportunity, productivity and human capital [7–9]. CSE delays onset of sexual debut, reduces risks of interpersonal violence, pregnancy and sexually transmitted infection including HIV [1, 10]. CSE has been shown to increase self-efficacy in sexual decision-making and healthy relationships, improves media literacy and is linked to academic success [1, 11–13]. Public policy and education are key determinants of reproductive outcomes [14]. In low-and-middle-income countries (LMICs), secondary school enrollments have exceeded 70%, linked to delayed marriage and childbirth [15]. Child marriage has dropped from one-in-four to one-in-five girls globally, and adolescent fertility rates have reduced by half since 1960, now 41 births per 1,000 aged 15–19 [16]. Female genital mutilation/cutting (FGM/C) rates in youth have decreased from 50% to 33% in high-risk countries over two decades. Since 2010, new HIV infections fell by a third (from 2.2 million to 1.5 million annually), with a projected 30% drop in 15–24 year-olds by 2030 in LMICs [17, 18]. However ongoing challenges for which CSE can help mitigate persist across the globe. For example, child marriage remains high in West and Central Africa, with 5% marrying before 15 years; and 21 million girls aged 15–19, plus two million under 15 years getting pregnant annually [16]. HIV rates are still disproportionately high in young females with a 2.6 fold increased risk associated with early sexual debut [19].

While clinicians are important, trusted providers of individual patient education and care, they are also an underutilized resource in the advocacy and implementation of school-based CSE [20, 21]. Within the patient-physician relationship, clinicians traditionally are expected to be nonjudgemental regarding the morality of patients and their culture. They utilize medical knowledge and experience to guide patients in making better health choices [22]. Pediatricians, obstetricians and gynecologists (OB/GYN), adolescent medicine specialists, pediatric and adolescent gynecologists and sexual health practitioners are expert front-line providers of sexual healthcare to young people. However, when physicians limit sexual health education solely to clinical spaces, it limits access to only those seeking clinical care, thereby reducing the potential impact and reach they could have on the wider public. The vast majority of CSE outcomes are due to structural and social determinants of health [7]. Leveraging clinicians in CSE beyond the clinical space, including research arenas, policy development, advocacy activities, and public health projects, holds the potential to deliver transformative population benefits. The benefits can be transmitted through several mechanisms: first, by contributing to a robust and real-world evidence-base for integration into policy and practice; second, by producing skilled collaborative medical educators who can partner with the education sector and as skilled end-users to inform national CSE curricula; and third by promoting trusted medical leaders in CSE who could drive policy and help to foster adoption of acceptable community-centered CSE solutions.

CSE is enshrined with the human right to education, health, and well-being, often referred to as sexual and reproductive health rights (SRHR) [2]. The aspirational vision for SRHR is one of health and gender equality, where all youth reach their full potential, ultimately contributing to the development of a compassionate and fair society. SRHR are now on the priority health, development and human rights agendas of a growing number of countries [23]. This paper provides an overview of the current state of CSE globally, explores the challenges and facilitators to CSE implementation, and discusses the untapped agency of clinicians and professional medical societies in improving CSE delivery.

Overview of CSE

Comprehensive sexuality education (CSE) equips young people with a foundation of knowledge, life skills, and values to help them optimize their own health, acquire self- esteem, develop respectful physical and emotional relationships, and critically evaluate sexual and reproductive health choices [2].

CSE should be delivered in a developmentally and age-appropriate format. Privacy, respect, and bodily autonomy, as well as safety and reporting of abuse, can be taught to elementary-aged children [1]. Adolescence is a time of critical physical, psychosocial, and cognitive development [24]. This phase includes development of peer relationships, determination of future roles in society, as well as the exploration of intimacy. Adolescents are vulnerable to the consequences of sexual and reproductive health decisions, whether made through personal choice or societal pressure. A lack of knowledge and education about puberty, sexuality, and rights concerning bodily autonomy can increase the risks of teen pregnancy, economic disempowerment, gender-based exploitation, violence, and abuse [2].

Students report the need for more in-depth age-appropriate discussion on emotional, social and psychological issues as well as sexual relationships, sexual orientation and body image [25]. Students have reported a lack of up-to-date professional interactive or online content in formal CSE training, further reinforcing their impression of a perfunctory approach by the education system to CSE [25].

Students of CSE, including those with disability, have expressed the need to be treated respectfully, and have their questions answered [26]. These needs have also been supported by parents. A study of 1592 parents of diverse backgrounds in the US demonstrated that 89% supported CSE [26]. Many parents have expressed a desire for teachers to address CSE comprehensively. Youth have expressed the desire for a joint approach between parents and teachers, so they could further explore their values and emotions around CSE at home. However, some parents report not feeling capable of delivering sexuality education to their child without further training, for fear of imparting inaccurate information [27].

Facilitators for CSE implementation

At the country level, commitment from national governments to develop partners to coordinate long-term investment in all facets of a young person’s life and health are major facilitators for CSE implementation [7]. Education and health strategies, focusing on gender equity, school enrollment, retention and progression, access to sexual and reproductive health services, parental education and national CSE policy should be integrated [28]. For low-and-middle-income countries (LMIC) requiring technical assistance, the World Health Organization (WHO) Adolescent and Youth Sexual and Reproductive Health and Rights Technical Assistance Coordination Mechanism support countries to build capacity and monitor outcomes on CSE in response to their expressed needs [23].

Integrating SRHR into national school curricula and expansion to serve the underserved is integral for achieving equity in CSE implementation [11]. Dedicated teacher training and ownership of the CSE coursework promotes reflective teaching styles which are more adolescent-friendly, can enable students to learn in ways that are relevant to their lives and empowers the teachers leading the courses [13]. The CSE curricula should follow national standards (in the US these are referred to as the National Sex Education Standards) [29]. Traditionally, self-directed online learning has been an important source of information for adolescents, particularly when discussions at home or school have not met their needs [30]. Instruction should be evidence-based, age-appropriate, culturally responsive, and inclusive. Evaluation and assessments should be made on a regular basis to determine effectiveness and refine protocols. Standardized tools to facilitate outcomes monitoring are available, including The Health Education Curriculum Analysis Tool designed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [31].

Social networks can allow adolescents to develop and express themselves sexually, and many first real-world sexual experiences for adolescents now occur online [32]. This places adolescents (who are often ill equipped to navigate or even recognize risky sexual situations) at jeopardy through access to sexually explicit material, sexting and pornography. In this current age, CSE programs that do not address cyber sexual risk are omitting a critical arena of potential sexual abuse and violence [33]. CSE must promote media deconstruction skills, skills to understand the media influence on self and peers, and improve communication with their health providers, parents and peers [1].

Encouraging community-centered efforts is a key component of an effective CSE program [34]. Embedding SRHR within the context of life skills programs can improve acceptance and adoption in many communities. For example, the Family Life and HIV Education curriculum, a school-based intervention in Nigeria helped adolescents develop increased knowledge, healthier attitudes and practice safer sexual health behaviour [35, 36]. Expansion of the program to more vulnerable youth in vocational settings demonstrated similar benefits [13]. Students also reported a greater feeling of self-worth, improved communication skills, confidence, self-control, and appropriate conduct. The SRHR-related benefits extended to siblings and friends through role modelling. Furthermore, the CSE instructors reported improved educational knowledge, teaching and parenting skills through their CSE training. These skills included greater ability to engage with students in all aspects of their teaching beyond CSE, as well as with their children at home.

CSE programs in Chicago, USA, public schools have demonstrated how school principals play a crucial role in prioritization and building school and teacher capacity in CSE [37]. Principals can act as liaison with parents and monitor progress on CSE programs. Recognition or distinction for delivering CSE should be included in school ratings to incentivize high quality programming and to provide the community with greater transparency.

Parents are an important pillar for CSE implementation and hold shared responsibility for its success. Parents provide CSE education from a different perspective. Parent–child dialogue about sexual education is associated with knowledge and positive attitudes [27]. Continual and sustained support at home reinforces lessons learned at school. Support for these conversations can include dialogue between parents and health providers including pediatricians, gynecologists and family practice clinicians. Providing online resources can further reinforce the educational programming [20].

The United States CDC recommends a whole school, whole community, whole child (WSCC) theoretical model with collaboration between schools, community, public health and health care partners to develop a comprehensive SRHR curricula [38]. An excellent example of this team effort includes a community collaboration (between parents, taxpayers, community partners and policy makers) which reversed an abstinence only approach for Pittsburgh public schools in Pennsylvania [11]. Black Girls Equity Alliance took further steps to address structural inequities experienced in their community by developing policy recommendations aligned with state and National Sex Education Standards inclusive of all cultures, genders and languages [39].

Challenges to CSE implementation

There are multiple challenges to effective global CSE implementation. Significant wealth and geographic disparities hinder the broad application of CSE [7]. Not all CSE programs are equally effective, and their content, emphasis and effectiveness vary widely [34]. CSE programs need to be carefully tailored to age, gender, ethnicity and social environments. The majority of the world’s 1.3 billion adolescents live in LMIC’s. Many of these adolescents lack access to CSE due to poverty, rural location, lack of formal education and gender inequity. [19, 27, 40]. Social disruption can be a major barrier to CSE access. An unprecedented number of adolescents (close to 50 million) are currently displaced due to conflict and crisis [41]. Young people have also faced significant social disruption due to the COVID pandemic, with less face-to-face interaction and increased use of social media and digital communication further advancing the need for CSE [7].

Social norms can hinder the implementation of CSE at the country, societal and familial level. For example, there is a false perception that CSE is solely focused on sexual relations and could have a corrupting influence on youth at an early age. However, culturally sensitive CSE programs, co-designed by youth, parents, and the community, can support a successful transition to adulthood. These programs build CSE through step-wise, age-appropriate learning, social connectedness, and resilience building. In certain environments, social beliefs may place high value on family honour, modesty, and abstinence until marriage [42]. There is a fear in societies who hold these beliefs that open discourse about sexuality or sexual diversity may initiate curiosity and promiscuity. However, of 63 studies of CSE examining this outcome, none demonstrated hastening of sexual initiation and 37% demonstrated delayed onset of first sex [9, 22]. Sexual rights may be viewed as a foreign concept, disruptive to certain societies norms and identities. Some societies may favor heteronormativity and gendered experiences leading to loss of opportunity for adolescents to actualize their identity and desires [24, 43].

Absence of strong federal SRHR policy threatens the quality of CSE implementation. A recent review of CSE in the US demonstrated that only 46% (23/50) of states mandated sex education, with only three (6%) implementing programs that conformed to national standards [11]. Of note, 16 states had abstinence only education, which has not been demonstrated to improve reproductive indicators. CSE programs in LMIC can be rudimentary as governments may not have the resources for it’s successful implementation. Other priorities of NGOs and funding organisations, who are called upon to support these programs may take precedence. As a result, implementation of national programs that don’t meet the local needs of the population can occur, resulting in no to low uptake of CSE in schools [44].

Lack of teachers who are formally trained in CSE is a critical issue resulting in scarce or superficial CSE delivery [25, 27]. Other barriers include discomfort with the educational content; perceived embarrassment of students; societal or familial bias causing misconceptions, absenteeism, and silencing by parents on CSE issues [25]. Lack of instructional materials for practical demonstration contribute to more pedagogical teaching styles and lack of student–teacher engagement. Barriers, such as limited resources, restricted access, cultural norms, and misinformation can hinder the progress of CSE. These factors often limit its reach, shape public perception, and create resistance to its evidence-based benefits.

The role of health professionals

The International Federation of Paediatric and Adolescent Gynaecology (FIGIJ), the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO), the International Association for Adolescent Health (IAAH), the International Pediatric Association (IPA) and the World Association for Sexual Health (WAS) met with UN agencies at the World Association of Sexual Health Congress in Antalya in 2023 to discuss the role of healthcare professional organizations in CSE [21]. Their members constitute over one million health professionals who take care of women, youth and children from all corners of the globe. The Antalya Consensus consolidated policy statements from these organizations, unanimously registering support for CSE in national curricula and calling for greater health professional engagement in advocacy and education efforts [21, 45–49].

Health professionals trained in adolescent health and adolescent gynaecology can serve parents and the community by delivering CSE in schools and throughout communities [34]. Health professionals are aware of the unique challenges faced by their communities and have the knowledge and skills to deliver trusted accurate non-judgemental education and training, in an adolescent-friendly manner. In fact, a study of Colombian students demonstrated that 560 out of 827 adolescents surveyed preferred CSE to be delivered by a health professional for these very reasons [27]. A recent gynecologist-led CSE intervention involving over 5000 Japanese male and female students demonstrated that expert discussion of topics often neglected in traditional CSE programs, led to increased awareness of contraception, HPV vaccination, and a greater willingness to receive the vaccine [20].

Medical training

Gaps and gender inequities in medical education perpetuate inequitable care in the community. Examination of medical student curricula in sexual health demonstrate that male topics are taught more frequently [50]. Sexual health training in medical schools has significantly declined (from 95% of schools in 1997 to 50% in 2018) with students having low rates of preparedness for addressing patient’s sexual health concerns [51]. Addressing this gap in medical education of sexual and reproductive health in medical school, residency programs and ongoing professional development (pre-service and in-service) is crucial for building the workforce of clinicians, educators and scientists who can impact the field of CSE for our youth. The World Association for Sexual Health and International Association of Sexual Medicine have published recommendations for education of young doctors' and students on CSE [52]. We must encourage the inclusion of evidence-based education about CSE in all medical curricula to build capacity of medical professionals to provide direct care in this arena, and to also expand their global footprint in CSE research, training and policy.

Medical research

Expansion of clinical trials networks can integrate new collaborators from diverse clinical areas and underrepresented communities (for example LMIC, gender diverse or disability communities). This strategy can introduce novel and culturally sensitive methodological approaches and accelerate solutions to a broader spectrum of unmet needs in CSE. However, incentives for clinicians to participate in research translation have been lacking [53]. Traditional research metrics and reward (based on publications and grant funding) have not provided a direct measure of impact in the wider community, resulting in translational researchers being overlooked for career advancement. This combined with lack of economic stability and potentially reduced clinical currency can also discourage clinicians away from CSE research [53].

Advocacy

Health professionals have an important responsibility to evaluate, understand the evidence and appreciate the critical impact of CSE. They have the potential to act as further reliable and reputable advocates for CSE access for all youth. Physicians have the power to convene in large numbers and disseminate information widely. Physicians also have a position of knowledge and trust in their communities and can be vigilant when faced with policies that could significantly adversely affect the health, wellbeing and rights of their community [53–55].

Policy

Health professionals have real-world experience and have enhanced access to policymakers, educators and community leaders and influential professional bodies. They are specially equipped to design developmentally appropriate and equitable CSE policy recommendations. But leadership in public policy development is not routinely taught in medical school, with little or no exposure to working within public policy organisations [53]. There is a lack of protected time or remuneration for the work, posing barriers for active clinicians wishing to have broad impact on CSE.

Thus medical practitioners face real-life pressures, time constraints and administrative burdens which can limit their ability to simultaneously address patient needs while offering informative, thoughtful CSE implementation. Conclusions

The transformative power of CSE to safeguard youth health, foster gender equality, and break cycles of poverty and violence is undeniable, yet it’s inconsistent global implementation remains a critical public health crisis. National education systems are the key partners for CSE implementation as they offer the opportunity to take a developmental approach to CSE at different time intervals [21]. Health, government, community partners, as well as parents and youth, all play essential roles in co-designing programs that are customized to local needs. Physicians, as trusted advocates and educators, are critical to maximizing the potential of CSE by extending their influence beyond their clinical walls into classrooms, policy arenas, and research frontiers. By championing evidence-based CSE in medical curricula, driving clinical trials, boldly advocating against regressive policies, and generating constructive policy solutions, health professionals can catalyze a global movement that empowers the next generation with the knowledge and opportunities to thrive. Systemic changes are necessary to increase the capacity of a broader pool of clinicians with expertise in CSE clinical care, research and importantly policy [53]. This urgent call to action is not just the identification of an opportunity—it is an imperative to secure a healthier, more equitable future for all youth.
