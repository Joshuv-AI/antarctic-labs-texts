A Systematic Review of the Clinical Application of Topical Tacrolimus in Vascularized Composite Allotransplantation
Authors: Hailey P. Wyatt, Maxwell N. Belisario, Alexis K. Gursky, Sachin R. Chinta, Alay R. Shah, Bruce E. Gelb, Eduardo D. Rodriguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Advancements in surgical techniques and immunosuppressive therapies have transformed care for patients through vascularized composite allotransplantation (VCA). However, lifelong systemic immunosuppression (SI) carries significant risks, specifically during acute rejection (AR), which typically requires increasing SI dosages. Mitigating such risks may be achieved through topical tacrolimus (TT). Although TT shows promise for VCA in preclinical studies, robust clinical data are lacking. This systematic review aims to evaluate the clinical use of TT in VCA.Methods:A systematic review was performed in accordance with PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 guidelines using Cochrane, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Studies included patients who underwent VCA and received TT treatment. Study details, patient demographics, VCA characteristics, Banff grade, induction and SI maintenance protocols, complications, AR episodes, and treatment plans following these episodes were collected.Results:The search yielded 271 articles; 11 met inclusion criteria, encompassing 15 VCA recipients (mean age 39.9 y). Thirty-two AR episodes were reported; 22 (68.8%) involved TT. TT was mostly used in Banff grade I–II AR episodes. Resolution occurred in 65.5% of TT-treated episodes, though this finding was not statistically significant. TT was frequently used with steroids and adjunct therapies.Conclusions:This systematic review highlights the potential role that TT may play in mitigating the need for increased doses of SI during AR episodes. As clinical data continue to grow, further research is essential to establish the potential benefits and efficacy of TT use in VCA.

Takeaways

Question: What is the clinical role of topical tacrolimus (TT) in managing acute rejection in vascularized composite allotransplantation recipients?

Findings: In a systematic review of 11 studies (15 vascularized composite allotransplantation recipients, mean age 39.9 y), TT was used in 68.8% of 32 acute rejection episodes, most often in Banff grade I–II rejection. Resolution occurred in 65.5% of TT-treated episodes, frequently alongside steroids and adjunct therapies.

Meaning: TT may help manage AR while limiting systemic immunosuppression escalation, but further research is needed to confirm its efficacy and long-term benefits in VCA.

INTRODUCTION

Vascularized composite allotransplantation (VCA) offers the unique ability to restore both form and function in patients with extensive tissue loss.1,2 Since the first successful upper extremity allotransplantation in 1998, more than 200 facial and upper extremity allografts have been transplanted worldwide. Advances in surgical techniques and immunosuppression management have contributed to improved allograft survival and functional outcomes.3–5 However, acute rejection (AR) rates in VCA continue to remain high, and lifelong systemic immunosuppression (SI) and steroid treatment regimens for AR do not come without significant risks. These risks include malignancy, opportunistic infections, metabolic complications, and end-organ toxicity.6–8 Although various immunosuppression induction and maintenance strategies have been used to minimize SI burden while reducing AR, SI remains necessary for graft survival.7,9,10

Unlike solid organ transplantation, which involves visceral tissues, VCA includes skin, a highly immunogenic component that presents both challenges and opportunities for immunomodulation. Due to its exposure and accessibility, the skin is an ideal target for localized immunosuppressive strategies aimed at reducing systemic drug burden while maintaining graft integrity.6,11,12 Furthermore, the mechanisms of rejection in VCA are understood to be largely T-cell mediated, offering a potential target for localized therapies directed at T-cell survival.13–15

One strategy is the use of topical tacrolimus (TT), which works by inhibiting calcineurin within the local skin microenvironment, reducing T-cell activation and mitigating rejection at the graft interface.14 This calcineurin inhibitor has well-established efficacy in treating other immune-mediated dermatologic conditions such as vitiligo, atopic dermatitis, and psoriasis, as well as in reducing rejection risk in high-risk corneal transplants.16–23 Many preclinical VCA animal models have explored various localized immunosuppression strategies, including TT.6,11,24–27 These studies explored various drug delivery methods of localized TT and TT in combination with other topical immunosuppressants, showing high doses of the drug in skin and surrounding tissue while maintaining low systemic levels. These studies also showed a good safety profile while providing local immunosuppression, supporting its potential implementation in VCA.

Despite encouraging findings in preclinical animal models, data on the current clinical application of TT in VCA remain limited. The extent to which TT can mitigate rejection episodes and reduce the need for SI therapy in human VCA recipients is not well understood. This systematic review aimed to evaluate cases in which TT has been used in VCA patients, assessing its effectiveness in managing AR and its potential role in reducing SI, to provide insights into the utility of TT as a targeted immunosuppressive therapy in VCA.

METHODS

A systematic review was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.28 Four electronic databases (Cochrane, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science) were queried in July 2024 using the following key terms and Medical Subject Headings: (“local” OR “topical” OR “ointment” OR “disk” OR “dermal”) AND (“application” OR “administration,” OR “delivery”) AND (“tacrolimus” OR “Protopic”) AND (“VCA” OR “vascularized composite allotransplantation”). A complete list of the search terms used in our strategy can be found in Supplemental Digital Content 1. (See Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the exact search string used to query databases, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E926.)

Eligible studies included those that reported the use of topical and/or localized administration of tacrolimus following VCA in human recipients. Our search strategy excluded non-VCA studies, studies not involving TT, animal studies, non-English studies, abstracts, commentaries, editorials, review articles, and unpublished or insufficient data. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria Used for the Systematic Review

The resulting articles were all imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), a web-based tool for systematic review processes.29 Two reviewers (M.N.B. and H.P.W.) independently performed title and abstract screening, then full-text screening, assessing each article for inclusion and exclusion criteria. A third reviewer (A.K.G.) independently resolved any resulting screening conflicts. One investigator (M.N.B.) extracted the following data: patient demographics, type of allograft, Banff classification grade, induction and maintenance immunosuppression protocols, complications, number of AR episodes, and adjustments to immunosuppression following these episodes.

All statistical analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). Fisher exact test was used to assess the association between outcomes and SI regimens with TT usage. A P value less than 0.05 was considered significant. All graphs were created using GraphPad Prism version 10.5.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA). The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools for case reports and case series were used to assess internal validity and risk of bias for all studies.30

RESULTS

In adherence to PRISMA 2020 guidelines, an initial search of the literature resulted in 271 articles. After 88 duplicates were removed, 183 unique articles remained for title and abstract screening. After title and abstract screening, 20 records remained for full-text review. Finally, 11 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in this study (Fig. 1).

After review using the JBI critical appraisal tool, all 11 studies met quality criteria and were deemed suitable for inclusion. All studies clearly described patient demographics, clinical history, interventions, and outcomes. However, 3 studies (27.3%) did not clearly report whether participants had consecutive inclusion into the study, and 2 studies (18.2%) lacked sufficient detail about the clinical setting. The 11 studies included in this systematic review were published between 1999 and 2023, including 8 case reports and 3 case series. A total of 15 unique patients who underwent VCA were captured, with a mean reported age of 39.9 ± 16.5 years. Of note, there was only 1 female and 1 pediatric patient in this cohort.9,31 Of the 15 transplants, 4 were face (26.7%), 6 were single hand (40.0%), and 5 were bilateral hand (33.3%) (Fig. 2). There was an average of 2.13 ± 1.2 AR episodes per patient, with an average follow-up period of 31.5 ± 19.3 months.1,2,9,14,31–37 When looking at the timing of AR, most episodes occurred during the first 3 months posttransplant, with a second peak at 6 months to 1 year (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

Although the field of VCA has made advancements in refining immunosuppressive regimens, lifelong SI remains a challenge. Despite preclinical applications of localized therapies seeming promising, the current use and utility of TT in human VCA remain largely unknown. This systematic review evaluated the clinical application of TT and its role in managing AR episodes in VCA. We found that TT was used in 68.8% of AR episodes, with 2 cases successfully resolving rejection without changes to maintenance SI.14 Additionally, TT was often used as part of a multimodal approach, typically in combination with SI adjustments and, in some cases, in conjunction with other topical corticosteroids or calcineurin inhibitors (pimecrolimus). Notably, mycophenolate dosage increases were more common in cases that did not use TT, whereas systemic steroid use was more prevalent in TT-treated cases, suggesting that TT may be used to complement systemic therapies in more severe scenarios.

Dose, Frequency, and Safety

Induction and maintenance SI regimens in VCA have largely been derived from and mimic solid organ transplantation.10,38 Contrarily, the application of topical immunosuppressive agents, including TT, has been more widely used in autoimmune and inflammatory dermatologic conditions.22,23,39,40 Similar to our findings, the 0.1% formulation is the preferred formulation for use, although the 0.03% has been shown to be safe and effective in the pediatric population.40 There were limited data regarding the frequency and length of treatment in our cohort; although when it was reported, it was commonly used twice a day for a duration of about 2 weeks.1,14,33 Despite initial concerns regarding systemic absorption and potential immunosuppressive overexposure, studies have shown that topically applied tacrolimus remains largely localized, with minimal systemic absorption even with long-term use.41,42 Reported side effects, such as local skin irritation, transient burning, and erythema, are typically mild and self-limiting. Furthermore, the long-term safety of topical calcineurin inhibitors has shown no increase in adverse effects such as infections or malignancies with prolonged use.41 Similarly, our systematic review found minimal reported adverse effects; only in 1 case, there was a report of burning on application, for which TT was switched to topical pimecrolimus.9 With limited follow-up data and generally short-term use, future studies are needed to systematically assess TT’s long-term safety, especially in the context of short-term and chronic use for VCA.

Years Since Initial Use

The use of TT in VCA is not new, as Dubernard et al1 reported its use in his case report of the first successful hand transplant completed in 1999. Based on our review, it continued to be sporadically reported for use in both hand and face VCA cases, with the most recent reports of its use in 2023.34 The sporadic reporting of its use may reflect the overall sparsity of published data on granular immunosuppressive management of AR in VCA, especially given that it is used only in the short term and is not a systemic treatment. This may also reflect a lack of widespread use due to limited clinical utility.

Indication and Clinical Reasoning

There is limited commentary in these studies describing the clinical reasoning behind when and why TT was used. To try to understand whether the severity of AR was an indication for use, we examined Banff grade as a proxy for severity. Our study showed that TT was most frequently used in AR episodes with Banff grades I–II. This could indicate that TT was used as a treatment for less severe episodes of AR, though there was no significant association between the use of TT and Banff grade. Of note, in the case report of the first pediatric bilateral hand transplant, for milder rejection, topical corticosteroids alone were used for AR, whereas for more severe rejection, TT was used either in conjunction with topical corticosteroids or with increasing SI and pulse-dose steroids.31 This suggests a role for TT in the pediatric population as well as a multimodal approach in more severe AR episodes. Additionally, the use of TT in the case series by Diaz-Siso et al9 was aimed at demonstrating effective early corticosteroid withdrawal and dual maintenance SI; this could suggest that to safely reduce SI burden, topicals can play an adjunctive role during AR. Finally, Chen et al14 provides the most comprehensive rationale behind TT use in AR, specifically aiming to mitigate increasing SI doses during AR by using a combination of topical clobetasol with TT. In the 2 cases reported, 2 early AR episodes were managed effectively with topicals alone without any changes in SI or additional pulse steroids.14 To further elucidate the effects of TT and clobetasol, the number of cellular infiltrates on tissue biopsy was quantified before and after treatment, finding a significant decrease in the number of cellular infiltrates. These reports, although mixed, suggest that TT has a role in complementing treatment regimens for more severe cases of AR, aiding in managing AR in the setting of dual maintenance SI, as well as treating AR without additional systemic therapy. The preclinical data show that when using TT in animals, it has the potential to extend graft survival and reduce AR episodes, with the best effects seen in conjunction with SI.27 Future research should aim to delineate whether TT directly impacts the frequency or intensity of SI adjustments. Additionally, this highlights the lack of information regarding the utility of TT to serve as a prophylactic measure to prevent AR, for which further research is needed. At our institution, TT ointment has been used for mild clinical signs of AR, although not for more severe cases of AR requiring admission for pulse-dose steroids. It has been prescribed for a course of 14 days, to be applied twice daily, at a 0.1% dose. Topical corticosteroids such as clobetasol or hydrocortisone are commonly used in conjunction with treatment for AR. In the case of the first human whole-eye transplantation recipient, TT drops have been safely used on the ocular allograft.

Preclinical Data and Future of Localized Therapies

Many animal models have moved away from topical immunosuppressant creams and have started focusing on localized delivery systems such as injections, disks, and hydrogels.6,26,27 These studies have demonstrated effective reduction of SI levels while targeting highly immunogenic tissues.26,43,44 Translating these findings into human clinical practice has proven challenging due to a small number of VCA cases limiting the feasibility of clinical trials. Furthermore, in rodent models, TT has been applied as a prophylactic measure to prevent rejection, rather than treat it, showing good allograft survival and allowing for reduction of maintenance doses of SI.11,24 This highlights the potential TT may have as a maintenance regimen as opposed to just a treatment for AR. It also highlights a significant gap in the transition from experimental models to clinical use. Developing clinically viable localized immunosuppression delivery systems remains important for advancing VCA immunosuppression regimens.

Reducing SI Burden

SI regimens in VCA largely mirror those used in solid organ transplantation but must account for the heightened immunogenicity of skin-containing grafts.38 The most commonly used regimens include induction therapy with antithymocyte globulin and maintenance triple therapy with tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and corticosteroids. Several studies have explored alternative regimens to mitigate systemic side effects while maintaining graft viability such as belatacept or rituximab therapy.45–47 Although briefly mentioned, none of these regimens include standardized approaches to localized or topical therapy.7,38,48,49 Our review shows that TT may be able to treat AR without additional pulse steroids or serve as an adjunctive therapy during AR when attempting to withdraw steroids from the maintenance regimen.9,14 Regardless, in all cases reported in our review, TT was not a component of maintenance regimens and was only used to treat episodes of rejection.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. The VCA literature is primarily composed of case reports and small case series, providing low levels of evidence to draw robust conclusions. Due to the small number of patients, stratification based on VCA type as well as AR severity or Banff grade was not possible and could potentially be achieved in further studies. Also, these reports largely lack granular details regarding the clinical reasoning, dose, frequency, and duration of use for TT. Additionally, based on 2020 PRISMA guidelines, if studies did not mention the use of TT in the title or abstract, they would have been screened out of the study, though they may have included data regarding its use in the body of the text. Further studies are warranted to collect prospective data regarding the use of TT in VCA patients.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study revealed that TT has been used since the first successful case of VCA as an adjunct treatment for AR. It has been safe and may have a role in avoiding increases in SI doses during AR or the ability to treat mild AR episodes without any additional medications, though further research is needed to confirm these claims. Recent research suggests that personalized immunosuppression protocols incorporating immune monitoring and biomarker-driven adjustments may help balance rejection risk with minimization of systemic exposure. The development of localized immunosuppression strategies, including TT, offers another promising approach to reducing systemic burden while effectively controlling rejection episodes. However, standardized protocols and long-term data are needed to refine these approaches and determine their optimal role in clinical practice. Moving forward, a shift toward targeted, patient-specific immunosuppression strategies may enhance graft survival while improving overall patient safety and quality of life in VCA recipients.
