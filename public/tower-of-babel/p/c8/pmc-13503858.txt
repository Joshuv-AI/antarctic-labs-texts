Iterating the transient light transport matrix for non-line-of-sight imaging
Authors: Talha Sultan, Eric Brandt, Khadijeh Masumnia-Bisheh, Simone Riccardo, Pavel Polynkin, Alberto Tosi, Andreas Velten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Time-of-flight non-line-of-sight imaging systems measure transient light transport at a visible relay surface to image hidden objects. Such measurements form a transient light transport matrix (TLTM). Because conventional methods capture only lower-dimensional subsets of this matrix, they are largely limited to reconstructing hidden geometry. Here, we show that a full-dimensional first-order TLTM (TLTM-1), measured between visible relay-surface patches, enables recovery of TLTM-2, the time-resolved light transport between hidden-surface patches. Using a dense laser scan and gated 16 × 16 single-photon avalanche diode array, we sample all dimensions of TLTM-1 on a planar relay wall. We then computationally focus virtual illumination and detection independently within the hidden scene, effectively turning the relay surface into a synthetic time-of-flight camera. Experiments and simulations show that TLTM-2 reveals indirect shadows, interreflections, and volumetric scattering. We further demonstrate direct-indirect transport separation, virtual relighting, and dual photography. These results establish an iterative framework for recovering higher-order transport (TLTM-n).

Introduction

Light interacting with a scene follows complex paths—reflecting, scattering, and arriving at different locations at different times. This spatiotemporal propagation of light can be encoded in the Transient Light Transport Matrix (TLTM)1, and measured using an active time-of-flight (ToF) camera (Figs. 1A and 2A). A pulsed laser sequentially illuminates a sequence of visible surface points in the scene (illumination positions). For each position, a time-resolved camera records returning photons at the surface points (detection positions)2. The TLTM provides a complete characterization of light transport within a scene. Once captured, this high-dimensional data can be processed to synthesize scene images, reconstruct visible-surface geometry, as in LiDAR, and enable advanced operations such as scene relighting, dual photography, separation of direct and indirect reflections, and specular-diffuse decomposition2–9. A notable exception is non-line-of-sight (NLOS) imaging, where the visible scene acts as an intermediary relay surface between the observer and a hidden scene10–12. In this setting, transient light transport measurements at the relay surface contain indirect paths that encode information about objects outside the direct line of sight (Fig. 2B). These include third-bounce paths in which light leaves the relay surface, reflects from a hidden surface, and returns to the relay surface. To date, NLOS research has primarily focused on using this third-bounce component to reconstruct the geometry of hidden surfaces13–27. Thus, conventional NLOS imaging extends LiDAR-style geometry recovery to hidden surfaces. Yet such reconstructions utilize only a small fraction of the information encoded in the transient light transport available at the relay surface. In this work, we go beyond geometry recovery and instead seek to reconstruct the TLTM defined over hidden surfaces. We define the TLTM of surfaces visible to the camera as the first-order TLTM (TLTM-1). Recovering richer hidden-scene information—including multi-bounce inter-reflections and material-dependent scattering effects—requires the second-order TLTM (TLTM-2): the time-resolved impulse response between the hidden surface patches. Just as a physical ToF camera measures TLTM-1 over visible surfaces, access to TLTM-2 requires a ToF camera for the hidden scene. The phasor field framework provides the basis for this synthetic camera by showing that time-resolved intensity measurements at the relay wall can be propagated and focused computationally using classical diffraction theory28–39. In this view, the scanned laser positions on the relay wall act as elements of a virtual emitter array, while the relay-wall detection positions sampled by the camera form a virtual receiver array. By applying computational beamforming operations—based on classical diffraction integrals40,41—one can synthesize a virtual source focused to illuminate a chosen 3D location in the hidden scene and a corresponding virtual camera to measure the time-resolved response from another surface patch19. In principle, this enables scanning of virtual illumination and detection points throughout the hidden scene to build the second-order TLTM. In practice, however, this capability has been limited by hardware: most ToF NLOS systems rely on single-pixel detectors42,43 or acquisition schemes that sample only reduced subsets of TLTM-1 at the relay surface—often corresponding to a diagonal16,21, a column23,24, or a row17–19,25,43,44 (Fig. 2C). While such measurements are often sufficient for hidden-scene geometry reconstruction, they do not enable experimental extraction of TLTM-2.

Recent research has begun to push these synthetic cameras toward analyzing hidden light transport beyond simple geometry. The phasor-field framework has been used to reconstruct videos of virtual light pulses propagating through hidden volumes, revealing higher-order bounces19,20 and surfaces within the NLOS “missing cone”44,45. However, because these pulses are synthesized from sparse measurements, they provide only global illumination across the hidden scene, preventing attribution of the measured response to a specific hidden source patch. Moving beyond such globally illuminated measurements requires isolating the light transport between specific pairs of hidden patches. Marco et al. took a key step in this direction by introducing the Virtual Light Transport Matrix (VLTM), the first framework to analyze light transport between hidden-patch pairs46. However, VLTM recovered steady-state intensities from simulated data rather than time-resolved transients, and required computationally intensive pairwise analysis based on sparse measurements.

We demonstrate that these limitations can be overcome by capturing the complete spatiotemporal transport across the relay surface (Fig. 2C, Column 1). By combining a dense laser scan with a 16 × 16 gated single-photon avalanche diode (SPAD) array47 focused over a sufficiently large area of the relay wall, we provide the high numerical aperture required to move beyond global virtual flashes. Unlike previous array-based systems that aggregated measurements to improve signal-to-noise at the cost of spatial resolution25,48, our system maintains full spatial diversity across the relay wall. This allows us to independently focus virtual illumination and virtual detection onto different hidden-surface patches. We can therefore iterate on TLTM-1 to experimentally extract TLTM-2 (Fig. 1). More broadly, this establishes a formalism for reconstructing scenes around multiple corners through iterative recovery of higher-order TLTMs (TLTM-n).

Together, these results show that a measured relay-surface TLTM can be iterated to recover time-resolved light transport between hidden-surface patches. By combining full TLTM-1 acquisition with computational beamforming, the proposed framework enables experimental extraction of TLTM-2 and supports analysis of hidden-scene transport beyond geometry reconstruction, including indirect shadows, volumetric scattering, direct–indirect separation, dual photography, and relighting with synthesized illumination.

Resolution limits of the synthetic ToF camera

The fidelity of the focusing operations used to construct H(2)—and thus the conditioning of the resulting matrix—is governed by the Rayleigh Criterion49 under the phasor-field framework32,36. Beamforming using phased-array principles requires sampling the relay surface at the Nyquist rate (λs = λc/2). For a grid of N positions, the aperture size is D = N ⋅ λs. Substituting this into Eq. (2) simplifies the resolution limit to: 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta x=\frac{2.44z}{N}$$\end{document}Δx=2.44zNThis relationship reveals a fundamental constraint: the resolution depends only on the number of sampled grid positions N and the depth z. In our experimental setup, the scanned laser grid contains an order of magnitude more positions than the SPAD detection grid. Consequently, the resolution of our synthetic ToF camera—and our ability to precisely probe the second-order TLTM—is ultimately limited by the spatial resolution of the detector array.

To use the higher-resolution grid for imaging, we therefore let the scanned laser positions define the virtual detection aperture, while the relay-wall positions sampled by the SPAD array define the virtual illumination grid. This choice is justified by Helmholtz reciprocity, which states that light transport is unchanged when illumination and detection are exchanged4,50. We adopt this interpretation throughout the paper.

TLTM-2 validation

In this section, we focus on validating TLTM-2 in complex hidden scenes containing both diffuse and specular elements, where higher-order light transport is difficult to extract with conventional NLOS acquisition schemes. Quantitative validation on a planar hidden surface, including comparison between simulation and experiment, is provided in Supplementary Section S5.1.

Complex hidden scene validation

We validate TLTM-2 on a complex hidden scene containing the letters n and W, a diffuse back wall, and a specular mirror (Fig. 3A). Note that the mirror does not appear in the 3D reconstruction because it lies largely within the NLOS missing cone44,45: only a limited set of relay-wall → mirror → relay-wall paths return to the relay-wall after specular reflection. Figure 3B shows representative TLTM-2 columns obtained by focusing a virtual pulsed source at selected hidden-scene locations. When focusing on a diffuse letter (Row 1), the response is temporally ordered: energy appears first at the focal region (Column 3) and subsequently on the back wall and other scene elements (Columns 4–6). Focusing on n yields a delayed response on W, strongest near the n-adjacent edge (Row 1, Column 5), and a later response in the mirror region (Row 1, Column 6) that resembles n, consistent with fourth-bounce transport via specular reflection. At the same delay (4.59 ns), n is bright under n-focused illumination (Row 1, Column 3) but remains dark under illumination focused on W (Row 2, Column 4), which stays localized on W.

TLTM-2 additionally enables a reciprocity check for specular–diffuse interactions. When focusing on the mirror (Row 3), delayed responses appear on the back wall and diffuse letters, with W preferentially illuminated along the edge closer to the mirror (Row 3, Column 4). Conversely, when focusing on n, a delayed response appears in the mirror region (Row 1, Column 6). These observations are consistent with approximately reciprocal fourth-bounce paths—relay wall→n→mirror→relay wall and relay wall→mirror→n→relay wall—which occur at similar delays (10.13 ns versus 9.84 ns). The small mismatch is consistent with the mirror’s extent and finite beamforming resolution, which can shift the effective mirror interaction point. Together, these results support that the extracted TLTM-2 preserves physically consistent reciprocal transport between diffuse and specular hidden surfaces.

Fourth-bounce shadows

Figure 4A shows a hidden scene containing a vase positioned in front of a diffuse back wall, with a specular mirror placed laterally. When the virtual illumination is focused on the mirror, TLTM-2 reveals a fourth-bounce light path in which energy reflects off the mirror and subsequently illuminates the back wall (Column 3). In the presence of the vase, a soft shadow corresponding to occlusion along this indirect path is observed on the back wall (Row 1, Column 3). Removing the vase and its stand eliminates the shadow at the same temporal frame (Row 2, Column 3), confirming that the observed effect arises from occlusion of the fourth-bounce transport path. When the virtual illumination is not focused on the mirror, the fourth-bounce signal is absent in both cases (Column 4), demonstrating that targeted synthetic focusing is essential for isolating and visualizing higher-order transport.

Light transport in clear and scattering media

Figure 4B demonstrates TLTM-2’s sensitivity to volumetric scattering. A glass aquarium placed in the hidden scene is first filled with clear water (Row 1), where transient frames show specular reflections from the front and back walls of the tank followed by indirect reflections between them (Columns 2–4). After adding milk to the tank (Row 2), the transient response changes qualitatively: light persists within the tank due to volumetric scattering, diffuse energy emerges from within the tank at later times (Columns 2–3), and reflections from the back wall are strongly attenuated. These differences directly reflect the distinct scattering properties of the two media. Full spatiotemporal videos for both cases are provided in Supplementary Movies 6 and 7.

Applications enabled by TLTM-2

While the preceding results validate that H(2) captures a physically consistent representation of complex transport—including specular interreflections and volumetric scattering—the explicit spatiotemporal structure of the matrix provides a functional framework that extends beyond mere visualization. In this section, we demonstrate how the high-dimensional control afforded by TLTM-2 enables active manipulation of the hidden light field, transforming the relay surface into a programmable interface for time-gated separation, multi-view synthesis, and high-resolution virtual relighting.

Separation of direct and indirect light transport

A fundamental challenge in NLOS imaging is that the direct third-bounce return, corresponding to light hitting a hidden surface once before returning to the relay wall, is often several orders of magnitude stronger than later-arriving multi-bounce signals. H(2) provides the temporal resolution required to disentangle these direct and indirect components. Similarly, focusing on W reveals light propagating to the back wall before returning to the edge of W.

In these measurements, the indirect signal is approximately 2–3 orders of magnitude weaker than the direct return. We observe two primary non-physical contributions in the indirect frames: a bright residual at the focal point caused by temporal sidelobes (a reconstruction artifact characterized in Supplementary Section S6.2), and leakage from the direct component. This leakage occurs because the physical spatial extent of our virtual focal spot is larger than the target letters, causing some direct light to arrive at times associated with indirect transport.

Angular relighting and multi-view reconstruction

Beyond isolating temporal paths, TLTM-2 allows us to manipulate the angular perspective of the hidden scene. Under Helmholtz reciprocity, each SPAD pixel focused onto the relay wall acts as a virtual point light source, while the scanned laser grid defines a fixed virtual detection aperture. By selecting different subsets of SPAD pixels, we effectively reposition the virtual light source across the relay wall, thereby controlling the angle of illumination.

As shown in Fig. 5B, this enables the reconstruction of multiple angular views from a single capture, revealing additional hidden-scene structure and effectively yielding a sparse 3D light field of the occluded environment. This control over illumination angles is essential for overcoming the NLOS missing cone; for example, we find that the letter 2 is only visible when illuminated by virtual sources on the bottom-left of the relay wall, while T is clearest from the top-right. Unlike traditional single-pixel SPAD systems where the detection is restricted to a single fixed location, our array-based capture preserves the angular diversity of the light transport. This allows us to resolve view-dependent shading and visibility shifts that are inherently lost in single-viewpoint reconstructions.

Synthesized illumination and pattern projection

While the previous applications focus on controlling the angle and timing of individual virtual light sources, the full TLTM-2 allows us to deliver precisely shaped, spatially structured virtual illumination into the hidden scene. By treating the columns of H(2) as a basis set, we can project complex patterns onto hidden surfaces without a physical line-of-sight. We distinguish between two modes of virtual relighting: temporally-coherent focusing and incoherent pattern projection.

Temporally-coherent focusing constructs a single column of H(2) by synchronizing virtual illumination wavefronts to a single point in time and space. While this is necessary for identifying specific multi-bounce paths (e.g., for TLTM-3 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H^{(3)}$$\end{document}H(3)) extraction), the spatial resolution for our system is fundamentally limited by the number of SPAD pixels.

To achieve the higher resolution required for complex pattern projection, we employ an incoherent approach: we jointly optimize virtual projector functions across both space and time to minimize the focal spot size. To demonstrate this, we synthesized a series of incoherent point sources to form the letter N on the hidden back wall (Fig. 5C). The fidelity of this virtual illumination is sufficient to resolve subtle topological details, such as the vertical creases in the curved back wall that modulate the shape of the reconstructed letter. Although this process sacrifices temporal coherence—limiting its use to iterate on TLTM-2 and extract TLTM-3—it provides the spatial precision necessary for spatially structured virtual relighting. Detailed formal definitions and implementation of these projector functions are provided in Supplementary Section S4.

Discussion

We present an iterative framework for the experimental recovery of the second-order Transient Light Transport Matrix (TLTM-2), extending NLOS imaging from 3D geometry reconstruction to the characterization of multi-bounce light transport. By capturing the complete 5D spatiotemporal signal on a planar relay surface with a gated SPAD array, we effectively turn the relay wall into a computational interface that can be iterated to observe interactions between hidden surfaces. This capability allows virtual illumination and detection to be independently focused at selected locations within the hidden scene, thereby revealing complex light transport effects such as volumetric scattering and indirect shadows. Although we demonstrate this framework in an NLOS setting, this iterative approach only requires an initial full TLTM-1 measurement and is therefore not restricted to NLOS scenarios; in principle, it can also be applied to line-of-sight (LOS) settings.

Physical validity and the nature of artifacts

As we move toward capturing higher-order transport—specifically photons that have undergone four or more bounces—the challenges of low signal-to-noise ratios (SNR) and mathematical inversion become paramount. To ensure the physical validity of our experimental findings, we utilized simulation-based validation to disentangle physical transport features from reconstruction artifacts in Supplementary Sections S5 and S6. This analysis confirmed that while our method accurately recovers the timing and spatial location of inter-reflections, it is subject to specific limitations. We identified temporal sidelobes—residual energy that leaks spatially and temporally around the intended focal point—which typically exhibit an extent of approximately 1.4 times the size of the central focal spot. The temporal resolution of TLTM-2 is lower-bounded by the phasor-field wavelength, limiting how well direct and indirect transport components that are closely spaced in time can be separated. These artifacts, alongside potential depth discontinuities from the max filter used in surface initialization, define the current noise floor of higher-order transport recovery. Higher-order TLTM estimates are also limited by photon statistics and calibration accuracy: low-SNR higher-order returns require longer exposures for reliable recovery, and small errors in relay-wall geometry or projected illumination and detection positions can propagate into TLTM-2 and subsequent higher-order transport estimates. However, these observations also point to future algorithmic refinements, including inverse beamforming strategies that can reduce residual sidelobes and leakage between direct and indirect components.

Resolution limits and hardware scaling

The spatial resolution of our current TLTM-2 extraction is fundamentally governed by the aperture and sampling density of the detector array. This constraint dictates the spatial resolution with which transport can be probed, which remains lower than that of the measured TLTM-1. This loss is expected: TLTM-2 relies on computational focusing and re-propagation through a finite relay-wall aperture. In our current experimental setup, for a surface 2 m from the relay wall, the spatial focusing resolution is approximately 50 cm—an order of magnitude lower than that of measured TLTM-1.

A more direct path to improvement is hardware scaling. Increasing the number of SPAD pixels simultaneously reduces the focal spot size and, consequently, the spatial extent of the temporal sidelobes. For example, a system sampling a 2 m relay surface with a 100 × 100 SPAD array supports a 4 cm phasor-field wavelength under the Nyquist criterion. At a distance of 2 m, this would enable TLTM-2 extraction with approximately 5 cm spatial resolution—representing a 10-fold improvement over our current results.

Crucially, the computational complexity of our framework remains constant with respect to SPAD pixel count, making the method scalable as detector resolution increases. As high-resolution megapixel arrays become commercially available, they will enable improved focusing resolution and reduced reconstruction artifacts without requiring a proportional increase in computational overhead.

Higher-order TLTM recovery and practical limitations

Hardware scaling is essential for recovering even higher-order transport, such as TLTM-3. Because our framework is natively iterative, the recovered TLTM-2 can serve as the input for a subsequent iteration to resolve fifth-bounce light transport. While the exponential decay of photon counts across successive bounces remains a significant barrier, the linear improvement in photon capture and spatial resolution provided by high-density SPAD arrays suggests that recovering higher-order transport, including TLTM-3, may become technically feasible.

Despite this potential, several systemic challenges remain. First, the current implementation depends on reliable hidden-surface localization prior to TLTM-2 estimation. If surfaces cannot be accurately recovered because of low SNR, occlusion, or limited angular coverage, the resulting transport estimates become unreliable. Furthermore, we face the missing cone problem—a common limitation in NLOS imaging, where surfaces oriented nearly perpendicular to the relay wall returns insufficient light to the relay wall to be reliably reconstructed. Second, although higher-resolution SPAD arrays can improve focusing fidelity, scaling detector arrays will substantially increase data volume, creating bottlenecks in acquisition, transfer, and storage. Future research must therefore explore joint surface and transport estimation, as well as compressive and adaptive acquisition strategies to manage these challenges.

Outlook

In our experiments, we show that TLTM-2 enables direct-indirect transport separation, relighting with synthesized illumination, and recovery of material-dependent effects such as volumetric scattering. These results show that iterating on TLTM-1 can recover structured hidden-scene light transport beyond 3D geometry alone. As higher-resolution SPAD arrays become available, they will improve both acquisition efficiency and the spatial resolution of extracted TLTM-2, making recovery of higher-order transport, including TLTM-3, increasingly practical. With these hardware advances, our framework will enable additional hidden-scene imaging and analysis tasks, including material characterization, BRDF estimation, and imaging around additional corners.

Applications enabled by TLTM-2: processing overview

After extracting TLTM-2, we process the recovered spatiotemporal transport to generate the applications shown in Fig. 5. For angular relighting and multi-view reconstruction, we use subsets of SPAD pixels to synthesize different virtual illumination positions on the relay wall and reconstruct the hidden scene under each illumination direction. For synthesized illumination, we form virtual point sources on hidden surfaces by optimizing projector functions over relay-wall position and temporal frequency, then combine these point sources to generate spatial illumination patterns. For direct–indirect separation, we use the recovered hidden geometry to compute the expected arrival time of the direct return at each voxel and temporally gate the TLTM-2 response into earlier direct and later indirect components. Full mathematical definitions and implementation details for these application-specific processing steps are provided in Supplementary Section S4.

Dual photography and denoising

Dual photography4 can be realized by transposing the illumination and detection coordinates of the TLTM, thereby swapping their roles and allowing the scene to be reconstructed from the perspective of the illumination positions rather than the detector. In non-confocal NLOS imaging with a single-pixel ToF detector, the measured data correspond to one row of TLTM-1, since the detector is fixed and the laser is scanned across the relay wall. Under Helmholtz reciprocity, this row can be transposed into an equivalent column, which is then processed by the reconstruction algorithm to produce a dual image of the hidden scene from the perspective of the laser grid.

A SPAD array enables the complementary primal view. For a fixed laser position, the array directly captures a column of TLTM-1 by recording multiple detector positions in parallel, allowing the hidden scene to be reconstructed from the perspective of the detector pixels rather than the illumination positions. As shown in Supplementary Section S9.1, the primal and dual reconstructions are similar because they are derived from the same relay-wall light transport and therefore do not provide fundamentally different scene information. The main advantage of the primal scheme is instead practical: it allows photon collection in parallel across detector pixels.

Although fast reconstruction algorithms can be implemented20 on chip53,54, the imaging pipeline is often limited by sequential laser scanning in Non-Confocal 1 (Fig. 2C), which typically relies on a mechanical galvanometer24. By contrast, Non-Confocal 2 (Fig. 2C) avoids this sequential detection bottleneck by measuring multiple detector positions simultaneously for a fixed laser position.

In Supplementary Section S9, we analyze this benefit qualitatively and quantitatively. We show that increasing the number of SPAD pixels with a fixed exposure time is equivalent to extending the exposure time for a single pixel, since both methods increase the total number of detected photons. Consequently, SPAD arrays can reduce acquisition time by collecting photons in parallel.

Hardware details

Figure 6 shows our NLOS imaging system and relay-wall sampling geometry. Our system is an active imaging system (Fig. 2B) that consists of an illumination source and a ToF detector. Our illumination source is a PM-1.03-25TM laser from Polar Laser Laboratories coupled with a frequency doubler that generates 515 nm pulses with a pulse width smaller than 35 ps. The laser operates in burst mode, where the burst repetition rate is 357.14 kHz, and each burst emits 14 pulses with an intra-burst repetition rate of 10 MHz. This configuration yields an effective average repetition rate of 5 MHz across bursts and provides an average output power of 375 mW. NLOS imaging system and relay-wall sampling geometry.

The left image shows the experimental non-line-of-sight (NLOS) imaging system, consisting of a pulsed laser, galvanometer, and 16 × 16 single-photon avalanche diode (SPAD) array. The right image shows the relay-wall positions sampled by the SPAD pixels in the large-field-of-view configuration. These sampled positions form the virtual illumination array used for TLTM-2 extraction, analogous to a phased array at the relay wall.

A two-mirror Thorlabs galvanometer (GVS012) is used to scan our relay surface. While the total scan time varies by experiment, the scan area is fixed at a 1.9 m × 1.9 m planar relay wall. For photon detection, we use a customized, gated 16 × 16 SPAD array47 with a deadtime of less than 100 ns and a temporal resolution characterized by a full-width at half maximum (FWHM) of approximately 60 ps. This SPAD array is positioned approximately 2.7 m from the relay surface, with different lens configurations employed to span various fields of view across the surface, allowing us to control the resolution and coverage for different experiments.

For SNR experiments outlined in Supplementary Section S9, we use a Canon EF 85 mm f/1.8 USM Lens to focus to a small 7.1 cm × 4.7 cm (Width × Height) area of the relay surface. To extract TLTM-2 from the captured TLTM-1, we need the spacing between the SPAD pixels to be roughly the same as the spacing between laser positions. The captured TLTM-1 has a resolution of (190 × 190 laser, 16 × 16 SPAD, 4096 time bins), with each laser and SPAD position focused to ~1 cm diameter spots on the relay surface and 9.375 ps temporal resolution. Our algorithm then applies Helmholtz reciprocity, swapping the illumination and detection grids, resulting in an input tensor of size (16, 16, 190, 190, 4096).

For this, we use an Edmund Optics 6X Manual Zoom Video lens with the focal length set to 30 mm, which focuses the SPAD array to a 50 cm × 32.5 cm (Width × Height) area on the relay wall. To image the hidden scene under synthesized illumination and demonstrate dual photography, we use a Fujinon 3 MP Varifocal Lens (3.8–13 mm, 3.4 × Zoom) which focuses on a 1.1 m × 0.7 m area on the relay wall (Fig. 6). This figure also shows the holes arising from the non-functional SPAD pixels, as well as the distortion in spacing arising from projecting the pixels from one side.
