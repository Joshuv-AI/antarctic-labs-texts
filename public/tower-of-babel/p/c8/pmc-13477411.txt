Pseudoinvasive Colon Cancer Mimicking Invasive Colon Cancer: A Case Report
Authors: Masaya Tsubakiyama, Shoji Oura, Katsuji Tokuhara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pseudoinvasion refers to a condition that resembles invasive cancer despite being either a non-invasive cancer or a benign disorder. We experienced a case of non-invasive colon cancer mimicking invasive colon cancer. An 82-year-old man without any surgical or endoscopic intervention to the descending colon had a descending colon polyp, which showed Japan Narrow Band Imaging Expert Team (JNET) category type 2A. However, it was large and highly elevated, had a contracted fold, and felt tense, leading to the tentative evaluation of invasive colon cancer, though lacking full endoscopic evaluation of the mass surface due to incomplete removal of mucus. Positron emission tomography (PET), however, showed no fluorodeoxyglucose uptake in the mass. We operated on the patient with a left hemicolectomy and lymph node dissection after the pathological diagnosis of well-differentiated adenocarcinoma. Postoperative pathological study showed no lymph node metastasis, non-invasive well-differentiated adenocarcinoma cells growing in a tubular fashion, and non-invasive cancer cells growing in an inverted fashion with cystic components under the non-invasive well-differentiated adenocarcinoma cells, leading to the final evaluation of pseudoinvasion. The patient recovered uneventfully, was discharged on the eighth day after surgery, and has been well for 13 months. Endoscopic specialists should take the pseudoinvasion into consideration and perform endoscopic ultrasound evaluation when noticing discrepancies among narrow band image findings, PET findings, and pathological findings of endoscopic biopsies to avoid over-treatment for non-invasive colon cancer with pseudoinvasion.

Introduction

Operable cancer treatment generally consists of resection of the primary cancer with regional node dissection regardless of cancer types. Non-invasive solid malignancies, however, do not develop lymph node metastasis and therefore need only primary tumor resection. Improved quality of endoscopic visualization has made it possible for physicians to diagnose non-invasive gastrointestinal cancers more accurately. Non-invasive gastrointestinal cancer can be cured by endoscopic resection of the primary cancerous lesion alone. Endoscopic mucosal resection and endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), therefore, have fortunately given a cure to many patients with cancer without harmful surgeries and have given markedly improved quality of life to them after treatment [1,2].

Narrow band imaging (NBI) is an endoscopic diagnostic technique that allows early detection of malignant tumor-induced angiogenesis. NBI can identify earlier forms of angiogenesis as irregular blood vessels, which easily lead to the endoscopic diagnosis of non-invasive cancer and cannot be detected by conventional endoscopy. NBI, therefore, excels at detecting early-stage cancer and has already become an important diagnostic tool for determining the indication of endoscopic treatment [3,4].

Pseudoinvasion represents a well-recognized diagnostic pitfall in colorectal pathology because epithelial misplacement into the submucosa may closely mimic invasive adenocarcinoma. Recognition of this phenomenon is essential to avoid unnecessary surgical treatment. Endoscopic specialists, therefore, must always keep the possible pseudoinvasion, though extremely rare, in mind when performing endoscopic diagnosis to provide appropriate treatment for patients [5,6].

We report a rare case of intra-mucosal adenocarcinoma with pseudoinvasion that was clinically mistaken for invasive carcinoma, and emphasize the importance of assessing the consistency among various imaging and pathological findings.

Case presentation

An 82-year-old man had undergone pancreaticoduodenectomy (PD) for a pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor. The patient had no family history of familial adenomatous polyposis, had received regular screening with upper and lower endoscopy before undergoing PD, and had undergone polypectomy for the transverse colon polyp, leading to the pathological diagnosis of tubular adenoma 20 months before the PD. The patient continued receiving colonoscopy and had been detected with multiple polyps in the ascending, transverse, descending, and sigmoid colons. Cold-snare polypectomy of all lesions, smaller than 1 cm, except the descending colon lesion, demonstrated low-grade tubular adenomas. Endoscopic examination of the descending colon mass showed a vessel pattern of regular caliber and regular distribution and a regular surface pattern, leading to the evaluation of Japan NBI Expert Team (JNET) category type 2A. Biopsy from the descending colon lesion revealed carcinoma in situ. We, however, suspected it to be invasive colon cancer because the tumor occupied one-quarter of the circumference of the colon, had a contracted fold, and appeared firm on endoscopic manipulation (Figure 1).

Lower endoscopy findings

(A) Lower endoscopy showed a protruded lesion with a contracted fold (arrow) in the descending colon. (B) Narrow band imaging showed that the tumor had a regular surface pattern and a vessel pattern with regular caliber and regular distribution, but could not show the entire mass surface due to the residual mucus (asterisk) on the mass.

Blood tests showed no abnormalities in carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) levels. Fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography (PET) demonstrated no abnormal metabolic activity within either the primary lesion or regional lymph nodes (Figure 2).

Positron emission tomography (PET) findings

PET showed no remarkable fluorodeoxyglucose uptake even in the descending colon mass (dotted circle).

Based on the clinical suspicion of invasive carcinoma, the patient underwent robot-assisted left hemicolectomy with regional lymphadenectomy. In the operation, the liver could not be adequately assessed because of dense postoperative adhesions from the previous PD, and no cancer cell dissemination was found in the observable abdominal cavity. Histopathological examination revealed well-differentiated intra-mucosal adenocarcinoma cells enveloped by the muscularis mucosae, inverted non-invasive cancer foci also enveloped by the muscularis mucosae with both cystic components and absent desmoplastic stroma suggestive of pseudoinvasion, and no invasive cancer cells, including tumor budding and lympho-vascular involvement. No metastatic foci were seen in the lymph nodes submitted for histopathological evaluation (Figure 3).

Pathological findings

(A) Macroscopic view showed a well-circumscribed mass with a cavity (arrow) within it. (B) Low-power view showed that the tumor had non-invasive adenocarcinoma cells (arrow) with an inverted non-invasive cystic component (asterisk) (hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stain ×4). (C) High-power view showed that the tumor had intramucosal adenocarcinoma cells growing in a tubular fashion (arrows) on the tumor surface (H&E ×200). (D) High-power view showed that the inverted mass components, i.e., pseudoinvasive nature, also had well-differentiated non-invasive adenocarcinoma cells (arrows) similar to the superficial cancer cells but had no invasive components (HE ×100).

The patient recovered uneventfully, was discharged on the eighth day after surgery, has been well without any adjuvant therapies for 13 months, and is scheduled for long-term follow-up on an outpatient basis.

Discussion

JNET type 2A refers to a polyp that has a regular surface pattern and a vessel pattern with regular caliber and regular distribution. This finding is commonly observed in low-grade intra-mucosal neoplasia, often indicating endoscopic treatment [7]. This patient, however, underwent surgical treatment to avoid under-treatment because the tumor seemed endoscopically invasive. Retrospectively, incomplete removal of surface mucus likely limited accurate NBI assessment. NBI evaluation correctly correlated with pathological findings in this case. It, therefore, is imperative for endoscopists that NBI evaluation be done after full removal of mucus on the tumors for accurate NBI assessment.

Adenomatous polyps sometimes had pseudoinvasive components, leading to the difficult differentiation from invasive cancer [8,9]. Tumors can develop pseudoinvasion through misplacement of epithelial components, generally accompanied by fibrous stroma in the polyp [9]. It is well known that pseudoinvasion occurs overwhelmingly more frequently in a highly mobile colon, i.e., around the sigmoid colon. In addition, misplacement can be correlated with several mechanisms, such as inflammation, infection, and iatrogenic mechanisms, including endoscopic biopsy, significantly leading to the formation of fibrous stroma. This patient had received surgical intervention around the pancreas on PD, but had not received any surgical or endoscopic procedures to the descending colon. Previous reports have shown misplaced non-invasive cancer in the fibrous stroma in cases of pseudoinvasion. However, misplaced non-invasive cancer was adjacent to but not found in the fibrous stroma in this case. It naturally remains uncertain why the correlation between fibrous stroma and misplaced non-invasive cancer cells in this case differed from those of previously reported cases.

No studies have reported pseudoinvasion in non-invasive carcinoma to date [10]. It, however, is well known that large adenomas can often have non-invasive cancer components. It, therefore, is highly expected that increased knowledge about this condition in clinicians will lead to a future rise in case reports like this one.

We cannot determine whether we could treat this colon cancer with ESD due to the lack of an actual attempt in this case. Pathological findings, however, highly suggest that we should have tried to treat this colon cancer with ESD. Endoscopic specialists should consider the possibility of pseudoinvasion when diagnosing cases that appear to be JNET type 2A but are suspected of being invasive carcinoma. Endoscopists may consider performing EUS in carefully selected cases where there are discrepancies among endoscopic findings, imaging findings, and biopsy results [8].

Conclusions

We experienced a pseudoinvasive colon cancer mimicking invasive colon cancer, leading to over-treatment, due partially to the inappropriate NBI evaluation. Clinicians need to pay careful attention, especially to colon cancer cases showing inconsistency among image findings, including NBI findings, endoscopic findings, and pathological findings of endoscopic biopsies on preoperative evaluation. Clinicians might be able to avoid over-treatment by performing the EUS evaluation for JNET classification type 2A lesions located at or near the sigmoid colon that exhibit findings suggestive of invasive carcinoma.
