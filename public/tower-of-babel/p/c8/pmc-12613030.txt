Bisphenol F-TLR4 interaction: uncovering new mechanisms in unexplained miscarriage
Authors: Yunhui Zhang
Published: 2025

Unexplained miscarriage (UM) remains a paramount unresolved challenge in reproductive medicine, with profound implications for human fertility and public health. Despite its high prevalence and significant associated emotional and social burdens, the underlying aetiology remains undefined in a substantial number of cases.1,2 Previous studies have predominantly focused on either genetic predisposition3, 4, 5 or environmental exposures,6 with limited attention to the synergy between these factors. Among environmental factors, bisphenol F (BPF)—a widely used substitute for bisphenol A (BPA)—has raised public health alarms due to its increasing detection in environmental and biological samples. Experimental evidence indicates that BPF adversely affects reproductive development, exhibiting toxicity during early embryonic stages that, in some cases, surpasses that of BPA, suggesting it may not be a safe alternative. Furthermore, significantly elevated levels of BPA metabolites in the serum of patients with recurrent miscarriage indirectly support the potential role of BPF in miscarriage processes.7 Although epidemiological studies suggest that environmental chemicals and maternal genetic background may independently contribute to pregnancy loss,3, 4, 5, 6 direct evidence elucidating their interactive mechanisms has remained elusive.

In the recent issue of eBioMedicine, Zhang and colleagues addressed this gap through an integrated approach combining epidemiological analysis with a UM case–control study, BPF-exposed mouse model, and cell-based assays.8 They demonstrated that BPF exposure and the TLR4-A transcriptotype interact to increase UM risk. Their findings reveal that BPF exposure and TLR4 genotype interactively impair trophoblast migration and invasion-essential cellular functions for embryo implantation and placental development-thereby elevating the risk of UM.

The principal strength of this work is its multi-faceted methodology. Epidemiological data first suggested an interaction between BPF exposure and TLR4 variants in determining miscarriage risk. Notably, this statistical association was subsequently validated experimentally. In functional trophoblast assays, BPF exposure inhibited migration and invasion more severely in cells harbouring the TLR4-A genotype than in those with the TLR4-G variant. This concordance between population-level data and cellular phenotypes significantly strengthens the biological plausibility of the association. Mechanistically, the authors identified the inflammatory mediator β2-glycoprotein I (β2GPI) as a key interaction partner. TLR4-A displayed stronger binding affinity to β2GPI than TLR4-G, and BPF exposure further enhanced this interaction. This enhanced binding led to the suppression of MMP9 and RAC1, two proteins critical for cell migration and invasion. By delineating a coherent pathway from environmental exposure to altered receptor binding, downstream signalling suppression, and ultimately impaired cellular function, this study provides genuine mechanistic insight that extends beyond correlation.

This research fundamentally challenges the prevailing clinical paradigm of investigating genetic and environmental factors in isolation for miscarriage. It compels the field to reconsider future risk assessments, which must now integrate individual genetic susceptibility with environmental exposures. These promising results provide significant new insights into how environment-gene interactions contribute to human disease by using BPF exposure and TLR4 genetic variation in unexplained miscarriage as a model. By experimentally demonstrating that such interactions impair cell function and revealing mechanistic pathways involving the β2GPI-TLR4 complex, MMP9, and RAC1, it moves beyond associative findings toward targetable biological processes. Looking ahead, future research in larger, multi-center cohorts could further validate its generalisation, while integrating multi-omics with exposomic profiling may uncover broader molecular networks, quantify cumulative environmental effects, and identify individuals at elevated genetic risk. Collectively, these findings not only deepen our understanding of environment-gene interplay in disease but also establish a foundation for personalised and precise prevention strategies.
