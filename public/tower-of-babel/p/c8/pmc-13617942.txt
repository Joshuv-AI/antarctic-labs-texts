Effects of energy drinks on flexural and optical properties of conventional and bulk-fill resin composites: an in vitro swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) study
Authors: Yi Hang Saw, Harith Iskandar Chai, Noor Azlin Yahya, Azwatee Abdul Aziz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEnergy drinks are widely consumed acidic beverages that may compromise the longevity of resin composite restorations. Furthermore, limited information is available regarding the application of swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) for detecting early beverage-induced alterations in resin composites.This study aimed to evaluate the effects of commercially available energy drinks on the flexural and optical properties of conventional and bulk-fill resin composites.MethodsA total of 150 specimens were prepared from a conventional nano-hybrid resin composite (Aura Easy, AE) and a bulk-fill nano-hybrid resin composite (Aura Bulk Fill, BF). One hundred rectangular specimens (12 × 2 × 2 mm; n = 10/group) were fabricated for flexural testing, while 50 disc-shaped specimens (10 × 2 mm; n = 5/group) were prepared for optical assessment. Specimens were immersed in Red Bull, Monster, Gatorade, or Extra Joss for 7 days at 37 °C. Control specimens were stored in air for flexural testing and distilled water for optical assessment. Flexural strength and flexural modulus were evaluated using a three-point bending test. Surface and subsurface alterations were qualitatively assessed using SS-OCT. Data were analysed using one-way ANOVA and independent-samples t-tests (α = 0.05).ResultsNo statistically significant differences in flexural strength or flexural modulus were observed between control and energy drink groups for either composite material (p > 0.05). Flexural strength values ranged from 69.16 to 87.82 MPa, while flexural modulus values ranged from 3.35 to 4.45 GPa. Despite the absence of significant mechanical deterioration, SS-OCT revealed localized increases in backscattered intensity and surface discontinuities in AE specimens immersed in Red Bull, Monster, and Extra Joss, and in BF specimens immersed in Monster and Extra Joss, suggesting early surface degradation and optical alterations.ConclusionsShort-term exposure to energy drinks did not significantly affect the flexural properties of the tested resin composites. However, qualitative SS-OCT assessment revealed localized optical alterations suggestive of possible surface and subsurface changes that were not reflected by conventional mechanical testing. These findings highlight the potential of SS-OCT as a non-destructive tool for evaluating early material alterations following acidic beverage exposure.

Introduction

Resin composite materials are widely used in restorative dentistry because of their favourable aesthetic properties, adhesive capability, and minimally invasive approach to restoring lost tooth structure. Conventional resin composites have demonstrated satisfactory clinical performance; however, incremental placement techniques may increase chairside time and technique sensitivity. To overcome these limitations, bulk-fill resin composites were introduced to allow placement in increments up to 4 mm while maintaining adequate polymerization, thereby simplifying restorative procedures and reducing clinical working time [1, 2]. Despite these advantages, both conventional and bulk-fill resin composites remain susceptible to degradation within the oral environment.

The longevity and clinical performance of resin composite restorations are influenced by intrinsic factors, including resin matrix composition, filler size, filler loading, and degree of conversion, as well as extrinsic factors such as moisture, thermal fluctuations, and dietary exposure to acidic beverages [3, 4]. Acidic media may promote hydrolytic degradation of the resin matrix through ester bond breakdown, water sorption, matrix plasticization, and filler–matrix debonding, ultimately compromising the structural integrity of the material [5, 6]. These processes may result in increased surface roughness, reduction in hardness and flexural properties, and deterioration of the restoration surface. In addition, prolonged exposure to coloured acidic beverages may adversely affect the aesthetic appearance of resin composites through staining and surface degradation [7]. Therefore, resistance to chemical degradation is essential for ensuring the long-term clinical success of resin composite restorations.

Flexural strength is an important indicator of the ability of resin composites to withstand masticatory and functional stresses in the oral cavity. Several recent studies have demonstrated that acidic beverages, including Coca-Cola, orange juice, coffee, and energy drinks, may significantly reduce the mechanical properties of resin composites [5, 8, 9]. Dahri et al. [8] reported significant reductions in flexural strength and hardness of bulk-fill composites following exposure to acidic environments, whereas Das et al. [5] observed increased surface roughness and reduced microhardness after immersion in acidic beverages. Similarly, Tozo-Burgos et al. [9] demonstrated progressive erosive degradation of high-translucency resin composites after exposure to energy drinks. Long-term hydrothermal aging has also been shown to reduce the mechanical stability of both conventional and bulk-fill composites, although high-viscosity bulk-fill materials may exhibit greater resistance to degradation than conventional counterparts [2, 4].

Apart from mechanical deterioration, acidic exposure may also alter the optical properties of resin composites by affecting translucency, light scattering behaviour, and refractive index characteristics. Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is a non-destructive imaging technique increasingly used in dentistry to evaluate surface and subsurface changes in restorative materials. OCT provides high-resolution cross-sectional imaging and has demonstrated considerable potential in detecting interfacial gaps, surface degradation, and early structural changes in restorative materials without damaging the specimen [10–12]. Previous studies have shown that OCT can effectively identify early degradation at the resin–dentin interface and evaluate restoration adaptation and interfacial integrity [10, 11]. Consequently, OCT represents a promising method for assessing structural and optical changes in resin composites following exposure to acidic environments.

Energy drinks are widely consumed beverages characterized by low pH and high acidic content, which may contribute to erosion of dental hard tissues and degradation of restorative materials [9]. Among these beverages, Extra Joss is commonly consumed in Southeast Asian countries and contains acidic constituents that may potentially affect the longevity and performance of resin composite restorations.

Although numerous studies have evaluated the effects of acidic beverages on the mechanical and surface properties of resin composites, limited information is available regarding the ability of non-destructive optical imaging techniques to detect early surface and subsurface alterations before measurable mechanical deterioration becomes evident. In particular, studies investigating the application of swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) for evaluating beverage-associated changes in conventional and bulk-fill resin composites remain scarce. Therefore, the present study aimed not only to evaluate the effects of energy drink exposure on the flexural properties of conventional and bulk-fill resin composites, but also to explore the potential of SS-OCT as a non-destructive method for detecting early material alterations associated with acidic challenge. Flexural and optical properties were evaluated using three-point bending testing and SS-OCT analysis. The null hypothesis was that exposure to different energy drinks would not significantly affect the flexural and optical properties of the tested resin composites.

Materials and methods

Study design and materials

This in vitro experimental study evaluated the effect of selected energy drinks on the flexural and optical properties of a conventional nano-hybrid resin composite (Aura Easy, AE) and a bulk-fill nano-hybrid resin composite (Aura Bulk Fill, BF) (SDI, Bayswater, Australia), whose behaviour is consistent with contemporary nano-hybrid and bulk-fill composites evaluated under standardized mechanical testing protocols [2, 8, 13–15]. Commercially available beverages were selected because the objective of the study was to simulate exposure to products as consumed by patients in daily life rather than to evaluate the isolated effects of specific acidic constituents.

Sample size determination

A formal a priori sample size calculation was not performed because limited preliminary data were available regarding the expected effect sizes for both flexural and SS-OCT outcomes under the specific experimental conditions investigated. Consequently, sample sizes were selected based on previously published in vitro studies evaluating resin composite degradation following exposure to acidic beverages. Although this approach is commonly adopted in exploratory laboratory investigations, it may limit the statistical power to detect small differences between groups. Therefore, nonsignificant findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Specimen preparation

A total of 150 specimens were fabricated and allocated for flexural testing and swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) assessment. For flexural testing, 100 rectangular beam-shaped specimens (12 × 2 × 2 mm; n = 10/group) were prepared according to the Mini Flexural Test (MFT) dimensions proposed as a clinically relevant alternative to ISO 4049 bar geometry for resin composites [2, 13–15]. Stainless steel moulds were used for specimen fabrication. Experimental grouping and sample allocation for flexural and SS-OCT analyses are shown in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1Grouping and number of samples for each material

Grouping and number of samples for each material

The resin composites were manipulated according to the manufacturers’ instructions and inserted into the mould cavities in a single increment, similar to protocols employed for bulk-fill restorative materials in recent studies [2, 8, 15]. Transparent Mylar strips and glass slides were applied to both specimen surfaces to minimize oxygen inhibition and reduce surface irregularities [18, 19].

Polymerization was performed using an LED light-curing unit (Bluephase® N, Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein) with an irradiance output of 1200 mW/cm² and wavelength range of 385–515 nm. Each specimen was initially light-cured for 10 s from the upper surface. Following removal of the glass slide, additional curing was performed for 10 s from both the upper and lower surfaces to ensure adequate polymerization, in accordance with contemporary multi-surface curing approaches for resin composites [2, 8, 20].

After polymerization, the specimens were removed from the moulds and finished using fine polishing discs (Sof-Lex™, 3 M ESPE, St Paul, MN, USA). Final specimen dimensions were verified using a digital calliper (ABS Digimatic, Mitutoyo, Kawasaki, Japan).

For SS-OCT analysis, 50 disc-shaped specimens (10 × 2 mm; n = 5/group) were prepared using customized stainless-steel moulds following the same fabrication and curing procedures. Similar specimen dimensions have been used in previous optical and surface characterization studies of resin composites following immersion in acidic beverages [16, 18, 19].

Immersion protocol

The specimens were randomly assigned into five immersion subgroups according to storage medium: (1) control, (2) Red Bull, (3) Monster, (4) Gatorade, and (5) Extra Joss. This experimental design was based on recent in vitro beverage-immersion studies evaluating degradation of resin-based restorative materials [18, 21].

For flexural testing, control specimens were stored in air to preserve baseline mechanical properties and avoid hydrolytic effects associated with water storage. For SS-OCT analysis, control specimens were stored in distilled water to provide a neutral hydrated environment comparable to the immersion groups and to facilitate interpretation of optical changes following beverage exposure. All specimens were immersed individually in their respective media for 7 days at 37 °C to simulate oral environmental exposure. The immersion media were refreshed daily throughout the experimental period to minimize pH fluctuations and maintain solution consistency [17, 21].

At the end of the immersion period, the specimens were removed from the storage media, rinsed with distilled water, and gently blotted dry with absorbent paper prior to testing. Flowchart of specimen preparation, immersion protocol, flexural testing, and SS-OCT assessment are shown in Fig. 2.

Flexural strength and flexural modulus evaluation

Flexural strength and flexural modulus were determined using a three-point bending test with a universal testing machine (Shimadzu Corp., Kyoto, Japan), consistent with ISO 4049 recommendations and recent investigations on bulk-fill and nano-hybrid composites [2, 13]. Testing was conducted using a 5 kN load cell at a crosshead speed of 0.5 mm/min until specimen fracture occurred.

where F/D represents the slope of the linear portion of the load–deflection curve.

Swept-source optical coherence tomography assessment

Surface and subsurface alterations of the resin composites were qualitatively evaluated using a commercially available swept-source optical coherence tomography system (OCS1300SS, Thorlabs Ltd., UK). SS-OCT has recently been applied for non-destructive evaluation of resin-based restorative materials and bonded interfaces, enabling detection of early degradation, interfacial defects, and subsurface alterations [10, 22].

The SS-OCT system utilized a tunable laser light source with a central wavelength of 1325 nm, scanning rate of 16 kHz, spectral bandwidth exceeding 100 nm, and output power of 10 mW. The system provided an axial resolution of 12 μm in air and a lateral resolution of 25 μm, with a maximum imaging depth of approximately 3 mm.

During image acquisition, the scanning beam was positioned perpendicular to the specimen surface. A standardized 4 × 4 mm imaging region was marked on each specimen to ensure reproducible image acquisition before and after immersion. Cross-sectional B-scan images were obtained at baseline and following immersion to assess changes in backscattered signal intensity, surface integrity, and subsurface morphology associated with material degradation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. Since the data demonstrated normal distribution, parametric statistical analyses were applied. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by appropriate post hoc tests (Tukey or Bonferroni, depending on homogeneity of variance), was used to compare the flexural properties among different immersion media within each material group. Independent samples t-tests were performed to compare the flexural properties between the two resin composites under identical immersion conditions. Statistical significance was established at α = 0.05. In addition, Cohen’s d effect sizes were calculated for statistically significant pairwise comparisons to estimate the magnitude of observed differences. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (d = 0.2), medium (d = 0.5), and large (d ≥ 0.8).

Results

Flexural properties

The mean flexural strength values of Aura Easy (AE) and Aura Bulk Fill (BF) following immersion in different energy drinks are presented in Fig. 3. No statistically significant differences were observed in the flexural strength of either composite after immersion in the tested beverages compared with the control group stored in air (p > 0.05).

Fig. 3Mean flexural strength (MPa) between AE and BF after immersion in different beverages for 7 days

Mean flexural strength (MPa) between AE and BF after immersion in different beverages for 7 days

For AE, the highest mean flexural strength was observed after immersion in Gatorade (87.82 MPa), whereas the lowest value was recorded after immersion in Monster (76.77 MPa). In the BF group, the highest mean flexural strength was observed after immersion in Red Bull (87.39 MPa), while the lowest value was observed after immersion in Gatorade (69.15 MPa).

Comparison between the two restorative materials within the same immersion medium demonstrated a statistically significant difference only in the Gatorade group (p = 0.009), in which AE exhibited significantly higher flexural strength than BF. The difference in flexural strength between AE and BF in the Gatorade group demonstrated a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.50), indicating a substantial magnitude of difference between the two materials.

The mean flexural modulus values of both materials are illustrated in Fig. 4. Overall, AE demonstrated higher mean flexural modulus values than BF across most immersion media and control conditions. The highest mean flexural modulus was observed in AE after immersion in Gatorade (4.45 GPa), whereas the lowest mean value was observed in BF after immersion in Extra Joss (3.35 GPa).

Fig. 4Mean flexural modulus (GPa) between AE and BF after immersion in different beverages for 7 days

No statistically significant differences were identified in the flexural modulus of AE or BF following immersion in the different energy drinks (p > 0.05). However, comparison between the two materials within the same immersion medium revealed a statistically significant difference in the Gatorade group (p = 0.014), with AE demonstrating a significantly higher flexural modulus than BF. Similarly, the difference in flexural modulus between AE and BF in the Gatorade group demonstrated a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 1.41), suggesting a substantial practical difference between the two materials.

The images presented in Figs. 5 and 6 are representative examples selected from specimens exhibiting the characteristic features observed within each experimental group. All specimens assigned to SS-OCT evaluation were examined before and after immersion. Similar qualitative patterns were observed across specimens within the same experimental groups; however, because no quantitative image analysis was carried out, the frequency and prevalence of individual OCT features could not be objectively determined. Therefore, the observations should be interpreted as qualitative indicators of possible material alteration rather than quantitative estimates of occurrence.

For AE, no observable surface alterations were detected following immersion in distilled water. However, after immersion in Red Bull, localized increases in backscattered intensity were observed together with reduced subsurface intensity. Similar alterations were identified after immersion in Monster, Gatorade, and Extra Joss, with the most pronounced changes observed following immersion in Extra Joss.

For BF, no detectable changes were observed after immersion in distilled water, Red Bull, or Gatorade. In contrast, immersion in Monster and Extra Joss resulted in localized increases in surface backscattered intensity together with reduced subsurface signal intensity.

Overall, AE demonstrated more pronounced surface and subsurface optical alterations compared with BF following exposure to selected energy drinks.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the influence of different energy drinks on the flexural and optical properties of a conventional resin composite (Aura Easy, AE) and a bulk-fill resin composite (Aura Bulk Fill, BF). Although immersion in energy drinks resulted in variations in flexural strength and flexural modulus, no statistically significant deterioration was observed for most tested groups. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that acidic beverages tend to affect surface-related properties, such as roughness and hardness, more prominently than bulk mechanical properties including flexural strength [17, 23, 24]. Therefore, the null hypothesis that immersion in energy drinks would not significantly affect the flexural properties of the tested resin composites was accepted.

The principal contribution of the present study lies in the application of SS-OCT as a complementary non-destructive technique capable of identifying localized optical alterations that were not reflected by statistically significant changes in flexural properties.

The continuous 7-day immersion protocol employed in the present study served as an accelerated aging model to facilitate standardized comparison among experimental groups. Although this approach has been widely adopted in laboratory investigations evaluating acidic beverage exposure, it may overestimate actual clinical conditions where beverage consumption is intermittent and influenced by salivary buffering, oral clearance, and temperature fluctuations. Furthermore, thermocycling and artificial saliva were not incorporated into the present experimental design. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as representing cumulative acidic challenge under controlled laboratory conditions rather than direct simulation of the oral environment.

Dry storage was selected as the control condition to preserve the baseline mechanical properties of the resin composites. Previous investigations have demonstrated that prolonged water exposure and hydrothermal aging may adversely affect resin-based restorative materials through hydrolytic degradation of the resin matrix and filler–matrix interface [25–27]. Water sorption may plasticize the polymer network, weaken intermolecular bonds, and reduce structural integrity, thereby contributing to reductions in flexural strength and modulus over time [25, 26].

Differences observed between AE and BF may be associated with variations in formulation characteristics, including resin matrix composition, filler loading, filler morphology, and filler–matrix interactions. BF contains triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA), a low-viscosity monomer incorporated to improve flowability and monomer conversion. Although TEGDMA may enhance handling characteristics and polymer network formation, previous studies have reported that TEGDMA-rich matrices may exhibit increased water sorption and hydrolytic susceptibility [27–29]. In contrast, AE contains a relatively higher filler loading, which may contribute to improved stiffness and resistance to deformation. Recent studies have shown that increased filler content is positively associated with elastic modulus and hardness, although flexural strength is also influenced by resin matrix composition and filler–matrix adhesion [27, 28]. However, as no direct chemical characterization was performed in the present study, these explanations should be regarded as potential interpretations rather than confirmed mechanisms.

Interestingly, the only statistically significant differences between the two materials were observed following immersion in Gatorade, where Aura Easy demonstrated significantly greater flexural strength and flexural modulus than Aura Bulk Fill. Although the precise mechanism cannot be determined from the present study, this observation may be associated with differences in formulation characteristics, filler loading, water sorption behaviour, and filler–matrix interactions between the materials. However, as no direct physicochemical characterization was performed, these explanations should be regarded as potential interpretations rather than confirmed mechanisms. Further investigations are required to clarify the factors underlying these material-dependent responses. The practical relevance of these findings is supported by the effect size analysis. The differences observed between AE and BF in the Gatorade group demonstrated large effect sizes for both flexural strength (Cohen’s d = 1.50) and flexural modulus (Cohen’s d = 1.41), indicating substantial differences between the two materials under the experimental conditions evaluated. Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed and effect size analyses were limited to the statistically significant comparisons identified in the present study.

Bulk-fill resin composites were introduced to simplify restorative procedures by allowing placement in thicker increments while maintaining adequate depth of cure. Contemporary bulk-fill materials have demonstrated satisfactory flexural properties and acceptable depth of polymerization while reducing clinical working time [28–30]. Despite differences in formulation and intended clinical application, both materials in the present study demonstrated flexural strength values generally within or close to the minimum requirement specified by ISO 4049 for resin-based restorative materials.

Energy drinks are characterized by low pH values and commonly contain acidic additives such as citric acid and phosphoric acid. Previous studies have shown that acidic beverages may contribute to softening of the resin matrix, degradation of the silane coupling agent, and increased surface roughness and wear of resin-based restorative materials [17, 23, 24]. Although Monster Energy demonstrated the lowest pH among the tested beverages, the present findings did not demonstrate a consistent relationship between beverage acidity and reduction in flexural properties. Although pH is commonly used as an indicator of beverage acidity, erosive potential is also influenced by titratable acidity, buffering capacity, sugar concentration, and other beverage constituents. Consequently, pH alone may not fully predict the degradative effects of acidic beverages on restorative materials. Commercially available beverages contain numerous constituents including sugars, colorants, preservatives, caffeine, vitamins, taurine, and other proprietary additives. Consequently, the observed effects cannot be attributed solely to pH or specific acidic components. The present findings therefore reflect the overall influence of commercially available beverages rather than the effects of isolated chemical variables. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies suggesting that beverage composition, buffering capacity, sugar content, and exposure duration may influence material degradation beyond pH alone [23, 24]. Future studies employing standardized acidic solutions with controlled pH, titratable acidity, and defined chemical composition would be valuable for isolating the contribution of specific beverage constituents to resin composite degradation.

SS-OCT analysis provided a non-destructive method for evaluating surface and subsurface alterations in the resin composites following immersion. The technique enabled qualitative assessment of optical changes without specimen destruction. Specimens immersed in energy drinks demonstrated localized increases in backscattered intensity and areas of reduced subsurface signal intensity, whereas minimal alterations were observed in specimens immersed in distilled water. These findings may indicate surface degradation, microstructural changes, or localized defects induced by acidic exposure. However, the SS-OCT assessment performed in the present study was qualitative and based on visual interpretation of representative B-scan images. Quantitative analyses of optical attenuation, signal intensity, or reflectivity were not performed. Consequently, the observed OCT findings should be interpreted as preliminary indicators of possible material alteration rather than definitive evidence of degradation. Although similar qualitative OCT patterns were observed among specimens within the same experimental groups, the present study did not quantify the prevalence or frequency of individual optical features. Future investigations incorporating quantitative image analysis would allow objective determination of the consistency and extent of these alterations.

Recent investigations have highlighted the usefulness of OCT in detecting early interfacial degradation, internal defects, and subsurface alterations in resin-based restorative materials [10, 22, 31]. Optical scattering observed in several specimens in the present study may be attributed to refractive index changes associated with degradation of the resin matrix or filler–matrix interface. Increased surface irregularities observed after immersion in selected energy drinks further support the occurrence of surface erosion and microstructural alteration. Interestingly, BF demonstrated fewer optical alterations than AE, suggesting possible compositional differences in resistance to acidic degradation between the two materials.

The present study demonstrated variable effects among the tested beverages, making it difficult to identify a single energy drink with the greatest detrimental effect on resin composite properties. Similar studies have also demonstrated that acidic beverages may produce material-dependent degradation patterns, particularly among bulk-fill, nano-hybrid, and giomer restorative materials [17, 23, 24]. Nevertheless, the observed optical and mechanical alterations suggest the potential influence of frequent energy drink exposure on the long-term performance of resin composite restorations.

From a clinical perspective, preventive dietary counselling remains the most effective strategy for minimizing beverage-associated deterioration of resin composite restorations. Nevertheless, when superficial discoloration or surface alterations occur, re-polishing procedures may represent a conservative management approach. Previous studies have demonstrated that re-polishing may improve surface smoothness and reduce superficial staining, although its effectiveness may be limited when changes extend beyond the superficial layer. Future investigations evaluating the effectiveness of re-polishing following energy drink exposure would be clinically valuable.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the in vitro experimental design may not accurately replicate the oral environment, where factors such as saliva, temperature fluctuations, masticatory loading, and intermittent beverage exposure may influence material degradation. Second, continuous immersion for seven days does not fully represent actual beverage consumption patterns. Another limitation is that commercially available beverages were used as immersion media. Because these products contain multiple constituents beyond acidic components, including sugars, preservatives, colorants, caffeine, vitamins, and other additives, the observed effects cannot be attributed to specific chemical factors. Furthermore, Extra Joss was prepared using tap water according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and variation in water composition may have influenced the characteristics of the immersion medium.

It should also be acknowledged that different control storage conditions were employed for flexural and SS-OCT analyses. Although these conditions were selected based on the requirements of each assessment method, the influence of storage medium itself should be considered when interpreting the results. Third, the relatively short immersion duration and limited sample size (n = 10 for flexural testing and n = 5 for SS-OCT assessment) may have reduced the statistical power to detect subtle differences between groups, thereby increasing the possibility of Type II error. In addition, no formal a priori sample size calculation, statistical power analysis, or confidence interval analysis was performed. Consequently, nonsignificant findings should be interpreted with caution, as subtle differences may not have been detected under the present experimental conditions. Furthermore, the SS-OCT analysis was qualitative in nature and did not include quantitative image analysis or formal observer agreement assessment. Consequently, the observed optical alterations should be interpreted as exploratory findings requiring further validation through quantitative OCT methodologies.

Future studies incorporating larger sample sizes, extended aging protocols, thermocycling, cyclic acidic exposure, quantitative OCT analysis, and simulated oral conditions are recommended to better evaluate the long-term effects of energy drink consumption on restorative materials.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, short-term exposure to selected energy drinks did not significantly affect the flexural strength or flexural modulus of the tested resin composites. However, qualitative SS-OCT assessment revealed localized surface and subsurface optical alterations in several immersion groups. These observations suggest that SS-OCT may be useful for detecting early material changes not identified through conventional mechanical testing. Further studies incorporating clinically relevant aging protocols, quantitative OCT analysis, and longer observation periods are required to determine the clinical significance of these findings.
