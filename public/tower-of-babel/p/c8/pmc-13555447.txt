The Contemporary Utility of the Eloesser Flap for Management of Complex Pleural Space Infections
Authors: Ioana B Florea, Johanna Lou, Keshav Kooragayala, Erin Crable, Christopher Derivaux, John Aversa, Kenji Minakata, David D Shersher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 27-year-old man with hyperimmunoglobulin E syndrome and remote left upper lobectomy for refractory hemoptysis presented with recurrent hemoptysis and a defunctionalized left lung. He underwent a reoperative thoracotomy and completion pneumonectomy complicated by a bronchopleural fistula. A 45-year-old man with a prior left upper lobectomy for adenocarcinoma presented with progressive cavitary Mycobacterium avium refractory to third-line antibiotics. These cases of chronic pleural space pathology in the immunosuppressed, reoperative setting were successfully addressed through a staged operative intervention with Eloesser thoracoplasty, demonstrating its ongoing utility in the armamentarium of the modern thoracic surgeon.

Empyema, a collection of purulent fluid within the pleural space, remains one of the most common benign consultations for the general thoracic surgeon. Among patients who present with parapneumonic effusion secondary to pneumonia, 5% to 10% progress to empyema, of which 30% require surgical intervention and 15% die.1 In the contemporary era, multimodal strategies for empyema management focus on early pleural space drainage alongside antimicrobial therapy.1,2 When nonoperative approaches fail, surgical evacuation and decortication is indicated. Chronic infections of the pleural space, however, particularly in the setting of reoperative surgery or prior radiotherapy, can present with necrotic lung parenchyma or bronchopleural fistula (BPF), mandating more creative approaches for definitive management.

The Eloesser thoracoplasty was initially described in the 1930s by Leo Eloesser and modified by Symbas and colleagues for evacuation of tuberculosis empyema. It traditionally involves creation of an epithelialized thoracostomy window with a U-shaped, inferior muscle flap secured to the pleural space and partial resection of 2 or 3 ribs that permits ongoing dependent drainage and allows access to the pleural space for ongoing débridement.3

In our modification of the technique, we perform a longitudinal minithoracotomy and create 4 diagonal incisions that allow the soft tissue edges to be sewn to the parietal pleura on the underside of the ribs (Figure 1). Our approach results in both ample working space for operative intervention and a durable site for packing. Although widespread use of the Eloesser has diminished with advances in antitubercular therapy, the technique remains effective for exceptionally challenging cases. Herein we describe 2 patients for which we used this often-forgotten procedure to help manage the most complex pleural space problems.

Case Reports

Patient 1

A 27-year-old man with a history of hyperimmunoglobulin E (hyper-IgE) syndrome on dupilumab and pulmonary emboli on apixaban presented to our tertiary care center reporting recurrent large volume hemoptysis. He had undergone emergent left upper lobe (LUL) lobectomy through a left posterolateral thoracotomy 10 years prior for Aspergillus pneumonia and hemoptysis refractory to bronchial artery embolization. Imaging on presentation demonstrated a multilobulated cystic cavity with nearly absent left lung perfusion (Figures 2A, 2B).There were no adequate endobronchial or endovascular options.

(A) Computed tomography demonstrates multilobulated left lower lobe cystic cavity with bronchiectasis, peribronchial cuffing, and pleural thickening. (B) Radionucleotide perfusion imaging confirms nearly absent left lung perfusion. (C) Chest wall wound 1 month after Eloesser thoracoplasty. (D) Well-healed former Eloesser site.

The patient subsequently underwent emergent redo left posterolateral thoracotomy, completion pneumonectomy, and pedicled intercostal muscle flap interposition. Intrapleural planes were challenging to safely dissect off the left main pulmonary artery due to scarring at the staple line from his prior nonanatomic lobectomy. The left main bronchus was left long due to ongoing coagulopathy precluding safe intrapericardial control of the pulmonary artery.

Persistent fevers, tachycardia, and leukocytosis with a worsening air leak concerning for pneumonia and BPF formation developed on postoperative days 3 to 5, and the patient was returned to the operating room. The recent thoracotomy was reopened and a large empyema was encountered in the chest with 2 pinpoint areas of dehisced bronchial defect along the LUL staple line from his prior lobectomy. This defect was closed primarily and buttressed by intercostal muscle. An Eloesser thoracoplasty was then created due to the extent of pleural contamination.

He required tracheostomy placement, yet diligent wound care and antimicrobial therapy ultimately permitted resolution of the empyema, improved nutritional and respiratory status, and subsequent reconstruction with efforts of both thoracic and plastic surgery services (Figure 2C). A high anterolateral thoracotomy was used to enter the left chest, which exhibited no residual purulence, and the BPF was reinforced primarily and buttressed with a right latissimus dorsi free flap anastomosed to thoracodorsal vessels. There was no evidence of recurrent contamination in the follow-up period.

Despite these efforts, the patient was noted to have a persistent bronchial stump leak 1 month later. After 2 additional months of nutritional support, he returned to the operating room for definitive management. Given his multiple prior operations through the left pleural space, we opted for a transsternal, transpericardial approach. A 3-cm segment of left mainstem bronchus was resected flush with the trachea and covered with pleura. The patient had an uneventful postoperative course and was discharged home after 9 days. Three months later, his wounds healed, and he remained without recurrent BPF, empyema, or hemoptysis (Figure 2D).

Patient 2

A 45-year-old man with a history of LUL adenocarcinoma after neoadjuvant chemotherapy and LUL lobectomy 5 years prior presented with a persistent cavitary left lower lobe (LLL) abscess. The patient had required a 6-month antibiotic course as a result of a cavitating LLL Acinetobacter pneumonia. Subsequent imaging revealed a persistent thick-walled LLL cavity with concern for bronchial tree communication. Prior biopsy specimens were negative for malignancy but were positive for Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC).

Despite conventional antibiotic management with azithromycin, rifampin, and ethambutol, he exhibited progression of MAC disease over several years, with serial computed tomography scans demonstrating an increased number of multiple cavitary lesions across the residual left lung and persistently positive acid-fast bacillus sputum. There was also concern for ongoing small-volume contamination of the right lung. He was thus referred to the thoracic team for evaluation of surgical options.

Interval computed tomography before surgical consideration showed complete LLL cavitation with marked parenchymal destruction (Figure 3A), and nuclear perfusion imaging demonstrated absent left lung perfusion (Figure 3B). Owing to the elevated risk of surgical site breakdown in the setting of chronic necrotizing infection, surgery was delayed while the patient could receive clofazimine for ∼3 months before surgical intervention.

(A) Computed tomography demonstrates nearly complete cavitation of the left lower hemithorax with significant cylindrical bronchiectasis, peribronchial cuffing, and basilar effusion. (B) No evidence of residual left lung perfusion and reduced perfusion in the right upper lobe. (L, left; LPO, Left Posterior Oblique; R, right.) (C) Eloesser thoracoplasty 1 week postoperatively. (D) Well-healing latissimus flap reconstruction.

The patient subsequently underwent a left posterolateral thoracotomy with completion extrapleural pneumonectomy. Preoperative bronchoscopy demonstrated significant purulence within the left mainstem bronchus, but no further evidence of right lung contamination. Upon entering the chest, significant scarring and fibrosis were encountered surrounding the lung, as well as large-volume purulence. The lung parenchyma itself was severely necrotic. To permit safe dissection of the pulmonary veins and pulmonary artery, an intrapericardial hilar dissection was performed. The pericardial defect was repaired with a latissimus dorsi pedicled muscle flap, which was concurrently used to buttress the left mainstem bronchus staple line. An Eloesser thoracoplasty was then created (Figure 3C).

His postoperative course was complicated by a recurrent laryngeal nerve injury requiring vocal cord medialization. His diet was subsequently advanced and tolerated without concern for aspiration. He was discharged home on postoperative day 12 with close wound care follow-up.

The patient returned 4.5 months later for definitive reconstruction with a contralateral, free latissimus myocutaneous flap by the plastic surgery team. His course was uneventful. His wounds were well-healed 4 months later, and he remains on maintenance MAC antibiotic therapy (Figure 3D).

Comment

Complex pleural space infections, particularly in the setting of medically refractory infection, reoperative fields, immunosuppression, and BPF, remain a formidable challenge for thoracic surgeons. These cases represent 2 such exemplars of intransigent infections of the pleural space that benefited from thoughtful use of the Eloesser thoracoplasty in the multidisciplinary, tertiary care setting.

Management of severe pulmonary complications of hyper-IgE syndrome is uniquely complex, and minimal literature exists on optimal operative approaches. Freeman and colleagues4 studied 32 patients undergoing parenchymal surgery with autosomal dominant hyper-IgE syndrome and found that complications developed in more than half, most of which were persistent BPF. Our case describes a patient who presented with life-threatening hemoptysis and exhibited numerous sequelae of hyper-IgE syndrome, including superimposed infection by Aspergillus as well as vascular anomalies and pulmonary hemorrhage in the setting of severe parenchymal damage. Similarly, pleural contamination with nontuberculous Mycobacterium remains relatively uncommon, particularly in the setting of progressive destruction of underlying lung parenchyma and persistent infection resistant to multiple rounds of advanced antibiotic therapy.

Our approach stresses expeditious source control of the underlying infection with an Eloesser and subsequent daily packing changes to permit ongoing débridement and early granulation. Considering future reconstruction of the space is critical, and the location for thoracoplasty is key, both with respect to acute infection management and in anticipation of reconstructive options. We construct the thoracoplasty at the site of the index anterolateral thoracotomy (Figures 1, 2C, 3C). This single, more superior incision assists with minimizing postoperative pain, offers an accessible site for advanced wound care, and facilitates manipulation of a well-vascularized pedicled latissimus dorsi flap to reach across the defect during eventual reconstruction.

Our typical thoracoplasty is 2 ribs in length and ∼7-cm wide with imbrication to the underlying rib space. At this size, we have found that granulation is well promoted with initial packing changes, progression to negative wound pressure therapy, and aggressive nutritional optimization. These sites then become readily accessible for flap coverage, frequently without the need for skin grating or additional intrathoracic incisions.

A nod to its tuberculosis-era utility, these complex cases stimulate the modern thoracic surgeon to consider the Eloesser thoracoplasty as an irreplaceable definitive strategy in the armamentarium for clearing the refractory pleural space of infection.
