Self‐reported barriers in self‐management of women with gestational diabetes: A systematic review of qualitative studies
Authors: Nuo Xu, Xia Han, Sijing Chen, Jingjing Zhang, Ping Gu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo provide a systematic review of the qualitative literature on self‐reported barriers to self‐management in pregnant women with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM).DesignSystematic review.MethodsThis systematic review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute meta‐aggregation approach and was evaluated using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. Qualitative studies on the barriers to self‐management among GDM pregnancy survivors conducted until 17 May 2022, were searched.ResultsA total of 30 studies were included, of which seven were in Chinese and 23 were in English, and 10 findings resulted in three themes: (a) Knowledge and belief, (b) Skills and abilities and (c) Environment and social support. By summarizing the self‐reported barriers to self‐management in patients with GDM and recommends precise interventions for these barriers, thereby saving health resources and helping to increase their willingness and ability to engage in self‐management.Keywords: barriers, gestational diabetes mellitus, self‐management, systematic review

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as hyperglycaemia that is first recognized during pregnancy (‘Diagnostic criteria and classification of hyperglycaemia first detected in pregnancy: a World Health Organization Guideline’, 2014). The International Diabetes Federation Atlas reports that the standardized global prevalence of GDM is 14.0%, and this rate has been increasing over time (Wang et al., 2022).

As a common medical complication of pregnancy, GDM can adversely affect the health of both the mother and foetus, resulting in hypertension, hydramnios, pre‐eclampsia, macrosomia, hyperbilirubinemia, hypoglycemia and shoulder dystocia (Saha, 2019). Furthermore, pre‐existing diabetes during pregnancy may result in long‐term adverse metabolic outcomes for the offspring, such as future obesity and glucose intolerance (Johns et al., 2018).

Self‐management refers to the ability of an active, responsible, informed and autonomous individual to take control of the medical, role and emotional consequences of chronic illness in collaboration with social networks and healthcare providers. Self‐management encompasses medical, emotional and role management (van de Velde et al., 2019). While several clinical trials have demonstrated the benefits of self‐management (Mohebbi et al., 2019; Rokni et al., 2022), developing more effective behavioural strategies is still necessary to achieve maximum benefit. Inadequate self‐management of GDM can increase maternal and newborn morbidity by two to three times (Lee‐Parritz, 2011). Given the potentially serious implications, it is crucial to support women with GDM to enable them to undertake the tasks of self‐management (Carolan et al., 2012).

The Integrated Theory of Health Behavior Change (ITHBC theory) (Ryan, 2009) posits that adopting healthy behaviours can be supported by developing knowledge and beliefs, improving self‐regulation skills and abilities, and enhancing social facilitation. Despite the high value placed on self‐management, the level of self‐management among patients with GDM remains poor, and interventions aimed at improving self‐management are inefficient: Patients face difficulties in adhering to self‐management (Jirojwong et al., 2017; Siad et al., 2018), which underscores the need for more effective interventions to support patients with GDM in their self‐management endeavours.

Self‐management is a vital strategy for controlling diabetes, and the effectiveness and feasibility of self‐management of gestational diabetes depend on patient motivation and practicality (Lambrinou et al., 2019). Previous studies have explored GDM self‐management and found that disease knowledge, self‐efficacy and social support levels influence the level of self‐management (Ferranti et al., 2014; Javalkar et al., 2016; Koetsenruijter et al., 2016). However, the quantitative approach used in these studies was limited in understanding of patients' lived experiences of the barriers to self‐management in GDM. With the evolution of qualitative research, a new approach has been introduced to provide a reliable and comprehensive analysis of possible barriers to self‐management in GDM. Additionally, this approach enables the generation of specific and actionable recommendations for patients, thus advancing the field.

Therefore, to identify barriers to self‐management from three perspectives and provide precise intervention variables for affordable and high‐quality solutions, this study integrated self‐reported barriers to self‐management in patients with GDM based on ITHBC theory. This will provide valuable references for professionals in different fields, promote the comprehensive development of self‐management in gestational diabetes, and improve patient outcomes.

METHODS

This qualitative systematic study followed the meta‐aggregation approach of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) (Munn et al., 2020) and was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Study population: Pregnant women with GDM or women with a history of GDM, without other related diseases; (2) Phenomenon of interest: Self‐management barriers or experiences of pregnant women with GDM, including diet management, exercise management, blood glucose management, etc.; (3) Context: The entire process of self‐management after patients were diagnosed with GDM; (4) Study design: Qualitative research or mixed research, including phenomenological research, narrative research, rooted theory research, descriptive qualitative research, ethnography, etc.

Exclusion criteria: Studies were excluded if they (1) were reviews, study protocols, etc.; (2) were not published in English or Chinese and (3) did not extract qualitative parts from studies.

Search methods

PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, CINAHL, PsycINFO, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang databases and VIP databases were comprehensively searched for relevant literature. Studies must have been published before 17 May 2022.Search terms included ‘self‐care/self‐control/self‐management’, ‘diabetes, gestational/GDM/gestational diabetes mellitus’ and ‘qualitative research/phenomenon/interview’. The databases were also searched using a combination of free words and subject word forms. Additional studies within 20 years were identified from the reference lists of the screened articles. Full details of the final search strategies for each database are available in Data S1.

Selection of studies and data extraction

Two researchers with systematic training imported the identified studies into Endnote X9 for independent screening. They eliminated duplicates and literature that did not meet the inclusion criteria. Then, we carefully read the entire text again to decide which studies should be included in this review. Data extraction and cross‐checking were conducted by the researchers, and any discrepancies were resolved by a third party. Only patient‐reported outcomes for GDM were collected from studies including healthcare professionals, such as nurses, doctors and dietitians. The author, year, country, sample size, phenomenon of interest, study design and results were the primary components of the data extraction. The reporting of the data extraction was performed in accordance with established guidelines.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed the included literature using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. The checklist included the evaluation of the research methodology and philosophical perspective, research objectives, data collection and analysis methods, interpretation of results, researcher's cultural or theoretical background and the influence of the researcher on the study. The appraisal criteria were rated as ‘yes’, ‘no’, ‘unclear’ and ‘not applicable’. Articles were graded ‘A’ if all the quality criteria were fully met, ‘B’ if they partially met the quality criteria and ‘C’ if they did not meet the quality criteria at all. The evaluation results were cross‐checked—in case of disagreement, discussions were held, and a third party assisted in the final decision. Only literature with quality grades A and B were included in this review.

Data synthesis

In this study, two researchers employed meta‐aggregation (Lockwood et al., 2015) as a synthesis approach to analyse the findings of the included studies. The process of meta‐aggregation involved three stages: (1) repeatedly reading the included studies and extracting relevant findings, (2) categorizing the findings based on similarity in meaning and (3) grouping categories into synthesized themes.

Using meta‐aggregation to synthesize the findings of qualitative studies can provide a comprehensive understanding of the research topic by combining findings of multiple studies. Overall, the use of meta‐aggregation in this study provides a rigorous approach to analysing and synthesizing the findings of qualitative studies on self‐management barriers in gestational diabetes.

RESULTS

Characteristics of included studies and methodological quality

According to the search strategy, 423 studies were initially retrieved, with an additional five articles obtained through reference tracking. After removing duplicates, 305 studies were screened based on their titles and abstracts, and 248 studies that did not meet the review's eligibility criteria were excluded. Theme 1: Knowledge and belief

Lack of knowledge for self‐management

Adequate knowledge is essential for effective self‐management in pregnant women with GDM. Some patients reported, however, that they did not receive sufficient information about the disease from their doctors (Khooshehchin et al., 2016), which hindered their self‐management efforts. In response, many women sought information from various sources, such as friends and family (de Sequeira et al., 2019), the Internet (Hewage et al., 2020) and books (Khooshehchin et al., 2016); However, the information they found was often fragmented and not helpful in supporting their self‐management (Khooshehchin et al., 2016; Mensah et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2021). Respondents expressed a desire for more comprehensive information, including information about the consequences of the disease and its treatment side effects (Figueroa Gray et al., 2017) and other important aspects that were not readily available to them. Additionally, women reported challenges understanding what to eat (Yee et al., 2015) and the importance of exercise (Ruth et al., 2018). Some pregnant women even adjusted their insulin doses without proper guidance because they lacked knowledge of the principles of insulin medication use (Wah et al., 2019). Most importantly, most respondents said that the information they received for self‐management was not sufficiently personalized (Siad et al., 2018), which made it difficult for them to trust and follow.

Cognitive deficits in diabetes

Due to the asymptomatic nature of GDM, some patients do not perceive the disease (‘I don't see any changes because I don't feel any difference’ [Dayyani et al., 2019]) and believe that GDM is not serious and will resolve after delivery. Consequently, some patients reported that they received a book on gestational diabetes from their doctor but did not read a single page. Additionally, some patients were unaware of their poor dietary habits, such as the misconception that eating only rice and vegetables is healthy (Ge et al., 2016). Some patients with religious beliefs reported relying on their faith for disease control, and they believed that ‘I cannot decide the future. it is God's decision’ (Hjelm et al., 2018). This low cognition in health further hinders motivation for self‐management among patients with GDM.

Theme 2: Skills and abilities

Limited by physical condition

Pregnant women experience more mobility issues than the general population, as they are more prone to fatigue and may suffer from pelvic and perineal pain, making it challenging for women with GDM to maintain regular exercise routines and adhere to dietary guidelines. Additionally, fear of repeated insulin injections and glucose monitoring with needles is a significant barrier to glucose self‐management, and some individuals may discontinue finger‐stick tests due to the discomfort and pain involved, resulting in inadequate self‐management outcomes (Figueroa Gray et al., 2017; Ruth et al., 2018).

Lifestyle changes with poor responses

Women with gestational diabetes often struggle to make necessary lifestyle changes to manage the disease. Sudden adjustment to these changes, such as altering one's eating patterns, can be challenging (‘It's really hard to change something you are used to suddenly, into something you don't like’) (Siad et al., 2018). For instance, some pregnant women reported that they rarely exercise because they need to care for their first child (Jirojwong et al., 2017). Furthermore, many respondents reported struggling with resisting the temptation of food, and some claimed that they were too lazy to change their exercise habits (Jirojwong et al., 2017). Moreover, they were required to reestablish a new balance between their role as a patient and their personal roles. Family disruptions can cause patients to reprioritize events and adjust their self‐management regimen accordingly. Religious beliefs and practices can also act as a potential barrier to self‐management in patients with GDM. For instance, certain religious practices may interfere with dietary requirements such as the need to consume specific foods during religious holidays, which could negatively impact glycaemic control (Carolan et al., 2012).

Plagued by negative emotions

Pregnant women with gestational diabetes may experience complex emotions during self‐management, which could cause physical and psychological stress to the patient. These patients often feel despair and frustration because self‐management was not working as they expected, and these dysphoric emotions can further hinder patients' self‐management. One patient reported that she followed self‐management but still could not control her elevated blood sugar, which led to her feeling anxious and giving up (Yee et al., 2015).

Theme 3: Environment and social support

Insufficient financial support

Limited financial resources, lack of insurance and financial instability were identified as major barriers to self‐management in patients with gestational diabetes. For instance, low‐income individuals are often more concerned with meeting their basic needs than with controlling their illness, as reflected by a patient's statement: ‘Looking at our budget and what we have now, I can either buy a stick of celery or four boxes of spaghetti so that I can have dinner for a few more days instead of a snack’ (Yee et al., 2015). Moreover, the high cost of medications, food and medical care can limit patients' choices, hindering their self‐management efforts (Mensah et al., 2019).

Poor regional development

Poor regional development can hinder self‐management for pregnant women with gestational diabetes. Some patients resided in towns or communities that lacked hospitals capable of treating the disease and in some areas where sporadic gun battles occur, making it difficult to ensure safety (Yee et al., 2015). Additionally, the lack of health insurance or limited coverage, combined with cumbersome reimbursement procedures, greatly hinder individuals' access to healthcare and create difficulties in accessing medications (Carolan et al., 2012; Siad et al., 2018). Some participants reported that they could not purchase accurate blood glucose monitors, which hindered their blood glucose monitoring and interfered with their self‐management decisions and behaviours (Ge et al., 2016; Wah et al., 2019). Furthermore, due to objective factors beyond geographical development, respondents in some areas did not have access to medication and food prescribed by their doctors: the patients reported that the variety of food available in the market was extremely limited, posing a significant challenge to self‐management (Hewage et al., 2020; Song et al., 2016). Moreover, patients had difficulty accessing stable medical resources for support due to the long distances to medical facilities (Yee et al., 2015). These factors highlight the need for more comprehensive and accessible healthcare systems to support self‐management among pregnant women with gestational diabetes.

Barriers to access to medical resources

Some respondents reported that the lack of timely access to medical care support was a significant barrier to their self‐management efforts. Constraints on appointment times (Carolan‐Olah et al., 2017), unanswered phone messages (Dayyani et al., 2019) and ineffective communication with healthcare providers (Figueroa Gray et al., 2017) negatively impacted self‐management. Furthermore, the absence of a good relationship during the consultation process hinders self‐management in patients with GDM. Personal reports indicate that there is no dedicated doctor for medical care provision, and patients reported receiving conflicting information when seeing a different doctor at each appointment (Figueroa Gray et al., 2017), leading to the lack of a trusting doctor–patient relationship.

Negative influence of family environment

Living with family members with different dietary preferences was a significant barrier to dietary self‐management in women with GDM. Patients often have to compromise with the conflicting dietary habits of their family members and may need to adjust their cooking according to their family's food choices (Tewari et al., 2020). Misconceptions about the disease and the daily behaviours of other family members can also hinder self‐management. For example, a mother's experience of pregnancy (Wu & Xu, ) or the fact that the husband can consume certain beverages while the pregnant woman cannot (Dayyani et al., 2019). Although family support can positively impact self‐management, some patients do not receive sufficient support, especially in intimate relationships (Khooshehchin et al., 2016; Neufeld, 2011). The caregiving needs of other family members for care giving can also compete for priority, leading to a neglect of self‐management in women with GDM. As one patient stated, ‘It is not easy to take time out for yourself, most of it is spent on your children’ (Tewari et al., 2020).

Lack of social environment support

The social environment is a crucial factor in self‐management for individuals with GDM. In certain countries, women with GDM may face safety issues when exercising outside, such as the risk of kidnapping in some cities, hindering their ability to engage in physical activity (Tewari et al., 2020). Eating out can also be a challenge for self‐management due to the highly processed and nutritionally uncertain nature of restaurant food, and difficulties in adhering to dietary requirements at social gatherings (Song et al., 2016; Wah et al., 2019).

Moreover, limited public awareness of GDM and the resulting social stigma hinder self‐management in affected individuals. For instance, some individuals may encounter discriminatory attitudes, such as a friend who refused to drink from a cup at her house, believing GDM to be contagious (Siad et al., 2018). The work environment can also hinder self‐management, with workplace and time constraints identified as barriers to maintaining healthy behaviours related to diet, exercise and medication (Figueroa Gray et al., 2017; Ruth et al., 2018). Conversely, peer support was reported to have a positive impact on self‐management by some respondents (Dayyani et al., 2019; de Sequeira et al., 2019).

DISCUSSION

GDM is a chronic disease that requires specific self‐management behaviours. This qualitative meta‐synthesis and systematic review explored the most significant problems encountered by pregnant women with GDM during self‐management. The review summarized and discussed the three main categories found to be central to the management of GDM: (a) knowledge and beliefs, (b) skills and abilities and (c) environment and social support.

The findings of this study highlight some overlooked aspects and themes during self‐management among pregnant women with GDM. First, regarding to knowledge and beliefs, patients generally reported inadequate knowledge about GDM risks and their self‐management regimen. Therefore, healthcare providers should ensure that patients receive reliable and sufficient information, and proper training and guidance on self‐management of the disease. The abundance of patient‐reported information available on the Internet, while beneficial, raises concerns about its quality and accuracy. Pregnant women with GDM require specialized and accurate information to better understand and manage their health. Healthcare professionals can play a vital role in empowering pregnant women with GDM by acting as mentors and providing better information resources (Lambrinou et al., 2019). Multidisciplinary collaboration could also enhance patient empowerment and improve self‐management outcomes.

Secondly, healthcare professionals must provide more personalized services to pregnant women with GDM. Attention should be paid to the specific choices and religious beliefs of these women. Some patients have limited literacy skills: It is worth exploring how to provide an easily understandable and actionable management approach for these individuals in a resource‐constrained healthcare setting. Additionally, there is a positive association between health literacy and self‐management behaviours (Billany et al., 2023; Tseng et al., 2017). Health literacy is defined as ‘the degree to which individuals have the capacity to obtain, process, and understand basic health information and services needed to make appropriate health decisions’ (Liu et al., 2015). Improving the health literacy of pregnant women with GDM may be a breakthrough in promoting self‐management and improving disease control and quality of life.

The self‐management process of pregnant women with GDM faces various barriers, including physiological limitations, poor lifestyle changes, negative emotions and lack of support from the environment and society. The results of this study indicate that patients often report insufficient knowledge and beliefs, leading to inadequate self‐management training and guidance. Healthcare professionals are crucial in empowering pregnant women with GDM by acting as mentors and providing personalized services and resources. Therefore, healthcare providers must ensure that patients receive reliable and accurate information, especially specialized and accurate information related to GDM.

Pregnant women with GDM often face physical limitations, poor adherence to managing disease, and negative emotions, such as stress, depression and frustration. Therefore, attention should be paid to the psychological and emotional well‐being of patients with GDM, and timely emotional support should be provided. Additionally, positive family intimacy and peer relationships play an important role in supporting patients; self‐management behaviours (Dayyani et al., 2019; Mensah et al., 2019). Family members can urge patients to exercise (Ge et al., 2016), and patients enjoy talking to others with experience of GDM, providing them with a ‘sense of ongoing support’ (Edwards et al., 2021).

The lack of environmental and social support is the most significant barrier to self‐management in pregnant women with GDM. Therefore, community involvement is essential in providing flexible working hours, convenient blood glucose monitoring equipment, diverse food choices and knowledge about GDM to reduce psychological stress and social stigma.

Finally, personalized care and appropriate self‐management programmes based on the physiological and personal preferences of pregnant women with GDM are necessary. For instance, the application can be established by first collecting the needs of patients and personalizing the APP page: some people might be more interested in gaining knowledge about pregnancy, while others are more inclined to what they should do. Second, it is essential to collaborate with the medical and nursing staff of the obstetrics department to customize the self‐management programme for GDM women of different ages, different levels of education and different religions to match the patients according to their personal situations. Machine learning and big data analysis can help provide personalized self‐management programmes and resources, enhancing patients' sense of self‐efficacy and improving their self‐management behaviours (Kytö et al., 2022).

Implications for future research and practice

Self‐management of gestational diabetes can effectively reduce the incidence and severity of associated complications. However, adequate education and support are crucial challenges for caregivers working with pregnant women with GDM, especially in the context of limited health resources. Tailored education and support, considering cultural differences and individual circumstances, may yield the best results. Technological advances, such as big data and machine learning, offer promising avenues for providing personalized support, but further research is needed to explore their potential.

Family support has been identified as a critical need for pregnant women with GDM, and future research could investigate ways to strengthen family education and support and determine their effectiveness. Additionally, the establishment of a social support network for pregnant women with GDM based on mobile health technology and community‐based care models, requires improvement.

Strengths and limitations

This study presents a qualitative meta‐integration of factors that hinder self‐management in pregnant women with gestational diabetes. By examining these factors, we can gain a deeper understanding of the challenges faced by pregnant women with GDM, and their needs and preferences. This information can then be used to develop targeted care and support plans and optimize health resources. By addressing the challenges and difficulties faced by pregnant women with GDM and providing the necessary support, healthcare professionals can increase their willingness and ability to engage in self‐management, thus improving their health outcomes and that of their unborn child.

However, due to demographic differences and clinical variations, this study has several limitations as follows: (1) only studies published in Chinese and English were retrieved for this systematic evaluation, leading to publication bias due to the omission of grey studies and (2) the poor quality of included studies may have affected the results.

CONCLUSION

This study presents a qualitative meta‐integration of 30 high‐quality studies highlighting the challenges faced by pregnant women with gestational diabetes in self‐management. By identifying and categorizing the key factors that impede their ability to participate in self‐management, we can develop targeted care and support plans to enhance their willingness and ability to engage in self‐management. It is essential to note that GDM self‐management education is a crucial component of any treatment plan and requires a comprehensive and multifaceted approach. Such an approach must focus on improving the health of pregnant women with GDM, empowering them and their families, and providing individualized support to meet their specific needs.
