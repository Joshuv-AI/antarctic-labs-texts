Transient frequency preference responses in cell signaling systems
Authors: Candela L Szischik, Juliana Reves Szemere, Rocío Balderrama, Constanza Sánchez de la Vega, Alejandra C Ventura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLigand–receptor systems, covalent modification cycles, and transcriptional networks are the fundamental components of cell signaling and gene expression systems. While their behavior in reaching a steady-state regime under step-like stimulation is well understood, their response under repetitive stimulation, particularly at early time stages is poorly characterized. Yet, early-stage responses to external inputs are arguably as informative as late-stage ones. In simple systems, a periodic stimulation elicits an initial transient response, followed by periodic behavior. Transient responses are relevant when the stimulation has a limited time span, or when the stimulated component’s timescale is slow as compared to the timescales of the downstream processes, in which case the latter processes may be capturing only those transients. In this study, we analyze the frequency response of simple motifs at different time stages. We use dose-conserved pulsatile input signals and consider different metrics versus frequency curves. We show that in ligand–receptor systems, there is a frequency preference response in some specific metrics during the transient stages, which is not present in the periodic regime. We suggest this is a general system-level mechanism that cells may use to filter input signals that have consequences for higher order circuits. In addition, we evaluate how the described behavior in isolated motifs is reflected in similar types of responses in cascades and pathways of which they are a part. Our studies suggest that transient frequency preferences are important dynamic features of cell signaling and gene expression systems, which have been overlooked.Subject terms: Applied mathematics, Signal processing

Introduction

Cells can sense and respond to their environment through interconnected signaling pathways that transduce extracellular signals into gene expression patterns, ultimately shaping the cell’s behavior. The regulation of these cellular processes frequently relies on signals whose activity changes over time, often in a repetitive manner. Repetitive signals can originate from both external and internal sources1–7, either take the form of periodic variations or irregular ones (e.g., quasi-periodic, stochastic), and play an important role in the precise control of cellular responses to environmental cues8. The ability of repetitive signals to provide more precise control over cellular responses can be particularly advantageous in noisy cellular environments. It is therefore crucial to determine which specific signal properties can optimize the activation of a particular target. This provides insights into the design principles of cellular signaling systems and the robustness of their responses to external perturbations such as environmental fluctuations.

The dynamic cellular environment often releases physiological ligands in pulsatile bursts with varying timescales, such as acetylcholine1, glutamate2, and GnRH (Gonadotropin-releasing hormone)3, which can span from seconds to hours. In some cases, constant external stimulation can activate a signaling pathway that works as an oscillator, generating periodic inputs for downstream signaling modules. For example, oscillations in p38 MAP kinase activity are induced by continuous IL-1b stimulation and are necessary for the efficient expression of pro-inflammatory genes such as IL-6, IL-8, and COX-24. Calcium concentration provides another example of a pulsatile signal, acting as a second messenger in diverse cellular processes and regulating targets based on its pulsatile dynamics5–7. Understanding the properties that determine the optimal activating signal for a specific target is essential to comprehend the regulatory repertoire of the cell in response to pulsatile signals.

Within repetitive inputs, we focus on periodic signals, and within this group we mainly consider pulsatile ones. Pulsatile signals are defined by several key attributes, including pulse amplitude, duration, the time interval between consecutive pulses and frequency. While constant signals primarily regulate cellular responses through amplitude modulation, where the output is determined solely by the strength of the input, pulsatile signals add additional layers of modulation as the attributes mentioned above may also impact the cellular responses9. A significant type of modulation is the frequency dependence of the cellular responses under pulsatile signals.

Biological systems can exhibit a range of frequency-dependent responses to periodic signals, which can be classified into all-, low-, band- and high-pass filters. All-pass filters describe situations where the response is not affected by the frequency of the stimulus. One example is the NFAT-mediated transcriptional regulation, which shows no significant difference in reporter expression across calcium frequencies10. Low-pass filters integrate the signal when it changes rapidly and follow it more faithfully when it changes slowly. Systems such as the high-osmolarity glycerol (HOG) signaling pathway in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and the nitrogen assimilation control network in Escherichia coli exemplify low-pass filter behavior11,12. Band-pass filters show an optimal or preferred response (resonance) for an intermediate range of input frequencies. For instance, osmo-adaptation in Saccharomyces cerevisiae exhibits frequency selectivity13. Another example is the finding by Mitchell et al.14 that an intermediate frequency induces slow proliferation in yeast subjected to oscillatory osmotic stress. Such resonant responses can be essential for biological systems to selectively respond to specific signals and stimuli. Finally, high-pass filters behave opposite to low-pass filters in the frequency domain.

The frequency-dependent behavior of the response signals to periodic inputs is attribute-dependent. To assess whether a signaling or gene expression system exhibits band-pass filtering behavior, it must first be determined which attribute of the response is the appropriate one to be evaluated. It is conceivable that some response attributes may exhibit resonance while others do not. For example, Hersen et al.11 measured the amplitude of the third level of the MAPK cascade to assess the frequency-dependent response. Hannanta-Anan and Chow10 computed the accumulated integral in NFAT transcriptional regulation. Johnson and Toettcher15 also used this operation to study cell fate in Drosophila embryos. Another possible response is the output’s integral computed over one period of the input, as used in Ryu et al.16 for the ERK activity in response to different pulse/pause regimes. Zi et al.17 quantified the signal transduction efficiency of Hog1 response to different scenarios of osmotic stress in Saccharomyces cerevisiae using the gain, defined as the integrated output divided by the integrated input. For yet another example, Pena and Rotstein18 showed that two-dimensional linear models receiving pulsatile stimulation exhibit resonance in the impedance amplitude curve, but a low-pass filter in the peak responses versus frequency curve.

Although often overlooked, the frequency-dependent response of a system to periodic inputs is also time window-dependent. Specifically, this type of response can be calculated over various temporal windows, and there is no reason to believe knowledge of the response over one (e.g., steady state, or quasi-steady state) will be predictive of the other (e.g., transient). For example, one can consider an early temporal window (transient regime), a late temporal window (periodic behavior if it exists) or a global window that encompasses both the transient and periodic regimes. Marhl et al.19 provided evidence that simple signaling motifs, which would be expected to act as low-pass filters, behave in fact as band-pass filters if the input oscillations are time-limited. Signals such as Ca2+ oscillations are time-limited and activate processes that are also time-limited, thus preventing the system from achieving stationary periodic behavior.

Even when periodic stimulation is prolonged, signaling components may still be functioning at pre-equilibrium. For instance, in Saccharomyces cerevisiae’s mating response, the signaling component that processes a stimulus operates on a slower timescale than its downstream processes. When the concentration is close to the dissociation constant, binding takes around 20 min to achieve 90% of the equilibrium level. These dynamics are not only slower than the cell division cycle (90 min), but they are also slower than the pheromone-dependent activation of the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) Fus3, which takes about 2–5 min to attain steady-state levels20. As a result, it is reasonable to conclude that the machinery downstream of the pheromone receptor must rely on transient binding information to function effectively.

An implication of the two situations described above (signals are limited in time or the timescales of the downstream signaling component are fast as compared to the receiving component) is that pathways use information coming from the transients. In most of the articles where the frequency-dependent response is studied, the initial transient behavior (first portion of the response) is discarded. The concept of information derived from transients is depicted in Fig. 1, showcasing both time-limited (A) and long-lasting (B) stimulations applied to a signaling component (C). By appropriately selecting the input duration and considering the response time of the signaling component, signal A exclusively activates the transient phase of the response in C, whereas signal B enables the response to stabilize at a steady-state periodic regime. The findings presented in this article indicate that for certain signaling components and under specific conditions, assessing the response during the transient phase may reveal the emergence of a frequency preference.

As elucidated by Fletcher et al.21, in a system with an increasing steady-state input–output relationship, pulses of increasing frequency will elicit an increasing response since increasing pulse frequency alone leads to an increase in input dose. In this situation, it is difficult to distinguish the direct effect of frequency from the effect of input dose. There are two approaches to study the effect of frequency independently of the effect of input strength. One is to use a dose conservation approach21, either by amplitude or duration compensation, while the other is to consider the gain, which represents the ratio of the response amplitude to the stimulus amplitude22,23. The dose conservation scheme employed (Fig. 2b) consists of adjusting the pulse amplitude or duration to compensate for changes in the pulse frequency, thereby avoiding a dose increase with increasing frequencies21. The gain quantifies the amplification or attenuation of the response relative to the stimulus. Most of the results presented in this paper were derived using a dose conservation method. Nonetheless, there is a complete section dedicated to re-evaluating some of the results without employing dose conservation and, instead, computing the gain.

In this article, we study the ability of simple cell signaling motifs to produce transient frequency preference responses (band-pass filters or resonance), when subjected to dose-conserved pulsatile stimulation. We focus on arguably the simplest motif where the system exhibits sustained (monotonic) activation in response to constant inputs and are steady-state low-pass filters (in response to oscillatory inputs, particularly pulsatile inputs). We exclude systems that produce sustained oscillations (limit cycles) and transient activation (adaptation, overshoot) in response to constant inputs. These two groups are expected to show preferred frequency responses in the stationary regime.

Biologically, within the motifs of interest, we first consider a ligand–receptor reaction and a synthesis-degradation signaling component24–26. Subsequently, we extend this system to a cascade by coupling simple signaling components with feedforward connections.

We measure the systems’ response to pulsatile inputs by computing the average response over a sliding window of width equal to the input period and over an expanding window starting at zero (Fig. 2a). These two observables provide complementary information about the transient vs. steady-state nature of the preferred frequency response to pulsatile inputs and the transition of these responses as time progresses. We divide the systems’ response to pulsatile inputs into two regimes (Fig. 2a): transient and periodic. While strictly speaking, periodic behavior occurs only in the stationary regime (i.e., it is reached as time approaches infinity), for simplicity in the description, in this article, we refer to the asymptotically periodic behavior approximating the stationary periodic behavior as periodic.

The major contribution of this work lies in the study of frequency responses considering a temporal window during which the output of the stimulated system is in a transient phase. We found that in several simple systems, there is a frequency preference response in some specific metrics during the transient stages, which is not present in the periodic ones. In addition, we evaluated how the described behavior in isolated motifs is reflected in similar types of responses in cascades and pathways of which they are a part. Our studies suggest that transient frequency preferences are important dynamic features of cell signaling and gene expression systems, which have been overlooked.

Gs is the output average over a period T computed between an initial time s to s + T, whereas Hs is the output average computed from 0 to a final time s. Both observables are bounded between 0 and 1, as the output y is also bounded between these values. As the parameter s is increased, the observables are computed over the different dynamical stages of the output and capture the response properties in the transient and steady-state regimes both locally and globally, as well as the transitions between them. The procedure was repeated for the two dose compensation schemes and different values of s, going from a transient stage s=0.1 to a periodic one s=10. The frequency response of the ligand–receptor system is highly dependent on parameter s (the dynamical stage the system is facing). Local fluctuations are observed over the asymmetric bell-shaped curve of the observable Hs as a function of frequency. These oscillations are due to small variations in the mean dose over the time interval [0,s] where the observable is computed, since it may not contain a complete number of periods. If one period is contained only partially, its contribution to the integral could introduce variations in the observable (also shown analytically in Supplementary Information, remark 1.1). No local fluctuations are found in the observable Gs, as the time window -from s to s+T- covers entire periods, therefore the mean dose per period is constant for all the frequencies and values of s.

Results in Fig. 3b–e show that when the observables are computed in a transient phase of the output (smaller values of s), they exhibit a band-pass filter behavior as a function of frequency. In contrast, for longer times (higher values of s) the observables display a high-pass filter behavior (there is a frequency cut-off, from which the response is insensitive to the frequency). We refer to the former situations as a transient frequency preference. This behavior has proven to be robust since it has been observed in the four combinations of observables and input dose conservation protocols. Transient frequency preference also emerges from a sinusoidal stimulation, where the dose is also conserved (see Supplementary Note 3.1) and without dose conservation (see last section in “Results”). These results suggest that a signaling component, such as the ligand–receptor system, can send different messages depending on the typical timescales of the downstream components receiving those messages.

Equation (9) presents a simplified model with potential applicability to diverse biological systems, such as synthesis and degradation of proteins. The model, linear in the variable y, includes a function F that processes the input γt. This function F can be thought of as a rapid equilibrium approximation to fast signaling dynamics that occur prior to the production of y21.

When subjected to a constant input γt=A, the variable y reaches the steady state F(A). In this context, the function F represents the input–output curve for constant stimulation. For a square-pulse input, y rises towards the steady state F(A) during the pulse and decays towards zero after the pulse ends. For an input consisting of a train of square pulses, the system exhibits an asymptotically periodic response resulting from a sequential combination of the increase and decrease phases described above (refer to Supplementary Note 1.2 for further details).

In this section, we focus on the effect of the nonlinearity of F over the frequency response curves of observable Gs. Our goal is to identify the choices of the nonlinear production rate F that lead to a band-pass filter for the sliding window Gs and to determine whether band-pass filters emerge during the transient regime, the periodic regime, or both. Based on previous research21 our study considers three types of monotonic increasing functions F: convex, concave, and sigmoidal functions. A linear function lies in the limit between convex and concave functions. While the study conducted by Fletcher and co-authors focused solely on the periodic response, we extend this study to incorporate the transient response. We examine the observable Gs as a function of the input frequency, considering an amplitude compensation scheme to maintain dose conservation.

Figure 5 shows the observable Gs vs frequency (panel b) for functions F with representative shapes (panel a) across various values of s. Analytical calculations supporting these results can be found in the Supplementary Note 2. Our numerical and analytical studies lead us to the following conclusions: if F is a linear or convex function (Fig. 5, rows 1 and 2), Gs is a low-pass filter both for the transient and periodic regimes. Conversely, if F is a concave function, then Gs acts as a high-pass filter for the periodic regime. For the transient regime, Gs can be a low or a band-pass filter depending on the choice of the function F. We present two examples in Fig. 5 (row 3): the left panel corresponds to the function F(γ)=γ10+γ, exhibiting a transient frequency preference, while the right panel corresponds to the function F(γ)=γ1000+γ, displaying a low-pass filter behavior. Finally, if F is a sigmoidal function, the behavior of Gs also depends on the specific choice of F. Two examples are presented in Fig. 5, row 4: for Fγ=γ21+γ2 (left), the response has a frequency preference both in the transient and in the periodic regime, for Fγ=γ2100+γ2 (right), the response behaves as a low-pass filter.

In this study, we demonstrate how different frequency responses to fixed-dose inputs can emerge from the interplay between the system’s kinetics, input timescales, response computation time window, and system nonlinearities. It is important to note that the presence of diverse frequency responses due to the system’s nonlinearity is intricately linked to dose conservation through amplitude compensation. The model described by Eq. (9) exhibits frequency selectivity to amplitude-compensated signals primarily due to varying input amplitudes corresponding to each frequency. By adjusting the pulse amplitudes to conserve dose, function F assigns unique amplitude values for each frequency, effectively shaping the frequency response characteristics.

For duration-compensated inputs, the production function only maps the two values in the train of square pulses since pulse amplitude A0 remains constant across all frequencies. Consequently, regardless of the function F, the frequency response solely depends on the system kinetics. Frequency selectivity does not occur in this model for duration-compensated inputs, with the transient response acting as a low-pass filter, whereas the response is the same for all frequencies in the periodic regime. The latter implies that when the frequency of the input is doubled, two pulses at the high frequency generate the same production of y as one pulse at a low frequency in the periodic regime, leading to a flat response. Similar results are obtained using a sinusoidal input (see Supplementary Note 4.2).

In summary, consistently with the findings of Fletcher et al.21 our numerical and analytical results show that only models with sigmoidal functions F under an amplitude-compensated inputs can produce band-pass filters in the periodic regime. However, models with both sigmoidal and concave functions F can produce band-pass filters in the transient regime. It is important to note that these results are not universally applicable to all sigmoidal and concave functions F, as the existence of transient band-pass filters depends on the specific choice of the parameters of F.

Coupled signaling components without feedback connections

In the preceding sections, we focused on examining the frequency preference responses in elementary signaling components, including ligand–receptor reactions and linear signaling units with additive nonlinear processing of pulsatile inputs. Our findings unveiled frequency selectivity in both systems under some conditions when the observables Gs and Hs were evaluated in the transient regime (see also examples 2.1, 2.2 and 2.3 in Supplementary Information). To delve deeper into this phenomenon, we now turn our attention to a cascade consisting of two signaling components interconnected by a feedforward connection. In this section, we aim to address whether the transient frequency preference observed in the previous systems is transmitted through the cascade. By exploring this configuration, we seek to gain insights into the behavior of the transient frequency preference within the cascade structure, including potential amplification or attenuation effects.

Initially, we analyze the effects of a concave F1 function and a linear F2 function (panel a). As described in the previous sections, y1 displays a transient frequency preference response. In isolation, y2 would act as a low-pass filter in both the transient and periodic regimes. However, different behaviors emerge in the cascade based on the relative timescale τ21. When τ21≤1, y2 exhibites a transient band-pass filter and a high-pass filter in the periodic regime. This suggests that, for relatively fast timescales, the response of the second level mirrors that of the first one, enabling the transmission of transient frequency preference downstream. Conversely, when the second level is slower than the first one (τ21>1), the transient frequency preference is not transmitted effectively due to the dilution of the influence of the first level caused by the slower timescale. In panel b, F1 is linear and F2 concave. Results are analogous to the ones obtained in the previous study: the second level follows the frequency response of the first one, instead of behaving as if it was isolated.

Together, these results provide evidence that the frequency selectivity observed in the first level can be effectively transmitted to the downstream component in the feedforward cascade, thereby altering the behavior the second level would have in isolation. However, when the second level operates at a slower pace relative to the first level, the former fails to replicate the transient frequency preference. This finding supports the notion that a single level can convey two distinct messages downstream, depending on the dynamics of the subsequent level. Consequently, a second component characterized by a rapid response can decode the transient frequency preference, while a slower component is unable to do so. As a result, different interpretations of the same signal may arise in each scenario.

Ligand receptor coupled to a covalent modification cycle

In this section, we consider a signaling network comprising a ligand–receptor system coupled to a covalent modification cycle (CMC). This cycle consists of two enzymatic reactions working in opposite directions. In this cascade, the complex formed by the ligand–receptor acts as the kinase for the CMC. Figure 7a presents a scheme of the system; the corresponding chemical reactions and ODE system are detailed in “Methods”. RLS is also the enzyme-substrate complex that catalyzes the activation of S into S*. The enzyme Ed forms a complex with S*, denoted as ES*, to deactivate it back into S, completing the covalent modification cycle27.

MAPK cascade

This section focuses on the frequency response in a more intricate cellular signaling system known as the MAPK cascade. The model employed in this study is based on the framework outlined by Huang and Ferrell28 and later adapted by Qiao et al.29. As depicted in Fig. 8a, the model comprises three interconnected levels of covalent modification cycles. In the first level, a single phosphorylation event takes place, while the second and third levels involve dual phosphorylation of the kinase. The phosphorylated substrate within each level, highlighted in color in Fig. 8a, serves as the kinase for the subsequent level. The model’s description follows mass action kinetics, leading to fifteen differential equations and over thirty parameters. This system exhibits remarkable versatility, displaying a wide range of behaviors under constant stimulation, including bistability and oscillations, as demonstrated by Qiao et al.29. In our study, we examine the frequency response of the system using a parameter set previously reported30. The complete model and corresponding parameter values can be found in “Methods”. With this parameter set, the system’s response to a step-like stimulus is monotonous.

In this section, our focus is on analyzing the frequency response of the three levels within the signaling pathway. To achieve this, we employe a periodic input, representing the total amount of Ras/MKKKK in the system. We consider a train of square pulses with dose conservation through both amplitude and duration compensation. To quantify the response, we measure the observable Gs for each of the three levels: MKKK-P, MKK-PP, and MAPK-PP.

When introducing variations in the total amount of a protein, it is important to consider mass conservation principles to ensure biological plausibility. In our study, we encountered situations where the calculated dose resulted in negative values, which are biologically nonsensical. To address this issue, we implemented an adaptation strategy for the stimulus, as in ref. 31. Specifically, at each time point, we stimulated the desired total amount of Ras/MKKKK and checked if the resulting dose was negative. If it was negative, we set the dose value to 0. This adaptation ensures that only meaningful and non-negative doses are considered in our simulations. Through extensive simulations, we observed that this adaptation did not significantly alter the overall total amount of Ras/MKKKK. The average total amount remained relatively constant across all periods examined. For further details and data, refer to “Methods”.

We explore the frequency response of the MAPK cascade under the specified conditions, and the results are presented in Fig. 8. Consistent results are observed with both dose conservation protocols. As opposed to the first level (panels b and e), the second and third levels both exhibit a clear transient frequency response. Notably, the third level displays a selectivity in frequency during the transient phase, with a comparable width to the second level but exhibiting a higher amplitude relative to the steady-state response.

For the two latter levels, as the time window is shifted toward the periodic regime there is an increase in both the preferred frequency and the maximum value of the observable. During transient stages, each level exhibits a distinct optimal frequency, with the initial level starting at a lower preferred frequency that progressively increases at each subsequent level.

We further study the impact of input’s parameters on the transient frequency preference. Specifically, we analyze how the observable Gs, for s = 0.1, in each level of the cascade is shaped by varying the duration of the input reference pulse, for both dose conservation protocols. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Note 6. As the duration of the pulse increases, the preference is more pronounced. Although the effect of input amplitude is moderate, higher values of the amplitude A0 lead to higher preferred frequencies and also their corresponding value Gmax in the three levels.

Thus, when considering the model of the MAPK pathway simulated with a parameter set derived from experimental data, we observe a transient frequency preference in the second level that is subsequently amplified in the third level. As opposed to models considered in the previous sections, this model includes hidden feedbacks32. In addition, we explore other models with similar or increased complexity, such as those incorporating explicit feedback mechanisms, related to gonadotropin-releasing hormone, finding that transient frequency preference could also emerge within these models (refer to Supplementary Note 7). Our results suggest that transient frequency preference may potentially be observed in more realistic models and that it can become more pronounced and enduring in downstream levels.

Discussion

In this study, our focus lies in investigating the frequency response of various systems that share a common characteristic: their response to a constant stimulus exhibits a monotonic behavior. The analysis of a system’s frequency response involves the consideration of several factors, including the chosen observable, the temporal window where to measure the response, and specific characteristics of the stimulus, such as whether the dose is conserved or not and the mechanisms employed for achieving it.

Given the diverse range of factors at play, characterizing a system’s ability to exhibit frequency preferences can be a complex task. Therefore, our approach in this study involves evaluating different simple systems, both in isolated and coupled configurations, across various parameter settings. Furthermore, we consider observables inspired by different experimental scenarios, thereby encompassing a broader spectrum of possibilities. This comprehensive approach allows us to explore a multitude of scenarios and gain valuable insights into the behavior of these systems.

In this paper, most of the presented results adhere to a dose conservation scheme, employing either amplitude or duration compensation, following the approach described in ref. 21 This approach ensures that we are investigating genuine frequency encoding phenomena: by maintaining a consistent mean dose of stimulation across different frequencies, we mitigate the potential influence of variations in mean dose on observed differences in frequency response. This allows us to focus specifically on the decoding mechanism of temporal information rather than confounding effects stemming from changes in the mean dose.

This approach is utilized across various experimental scenarios, indicating its significance in real cell signaling systems. The neuroendocrine control of reproduction through brain-secreted pulses of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) has long served as a model system for studying signal decoding mechanisms. Numerous studies related to GnRH employ a dose conservation approach. For instance, research has shown that the frequency preference for gonadotropin subunit primary transcript production in gonadotrophs persists even with a fixed input dose of GnRH34,35. Similarly, investigations have demonstrated that variations in GnRH pulse amplitude with pulse frequency in response to increasing levels of estradiol resemble amplitude-compensated input signals36. Furthermore, studies have explored the sensitivity of the Fshb gene to gonadotropin-releasing hormone pulse frequency with dose conservation37.

The mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway represents another prototypical system for studying signal processing and decoding, as discussed in the section titled “MAPK cascade”. This pathway has been examined under a dose conservation approach in several articles. For example, research has investigated the Hog1 MAPK response to fluctuating osmotic stress17, as well as gene expression in the mating response38, both studies done in Saccharomyces cerevisiae and employing a duration compensation protocol to achieve dose conservation.

In addition, numerous other articles could be cited, such as those examining how a transcriptional circuit filters oscillating circadian hormonal inputs to regulate fat cell differentiation39 or evaluating the response of the calcium-dependent transcription factor NFAT to pulsatile loads of calcium signal10.

From a modeling perspective, numerous studies employ a dose conservation approach21,40,41. Importantly, many articles studying frequency decoding utilize sinusoidal stimulation, which maintains a constant mean dose of stimulation across different frequencies, resulting in a mean dose per period that is independent of the period42–46.

As previously mentioned, the choice of the measured observable depends on the specific biological context. In the literature, several observables have been identified as relevant in different studies. One such observable is the response amplitude, which naturally emerges in various contexts13,26. It is defined as the difference between the maximum and minimum values of the response and provides valuable information about the magnitude of the cellular response. Another important observable is the gain, which represents the ratio of the stimulus amplitude to the response amplitude22,23. The gain quantifies the amplification or attenuation of the response relative to the stimulus and can provide insights into the system’s sensitivity. The integral of the response is also commonly considered47, capturing the accumulation of a substance that can trigger a signal, and providing the overall response over time. In certain cases, researchers may be interested in determining if the response exceeds a specific threshold. In such instances, the observable of interest becomes the integral of the response above a threshold33, allowing for a focused analysis of events surpassing the designated threshold. Other studies have explored the average response10,21, which involves normalizing the integral by the duration of the temporal window considered for the integration. The average response provides a measure of the system’s overall activity within the specified time frame. Overall, the selection of an appropriate observable depends on the specific research question and the desired aspect of the cellular response being investigated.

When investigating simple isolated systems stimulated with a train of square pulses that conserve dose, we observed the emergence of a phenomenon we called transient frequency response. This phenomenon manifests as a frequency selectivity that occurs when observables are measured before the system reaches its periodic regime. This transient frequency response holds biological significance as it may shed light on how a system can convey distinct messages downstream based on the timing of the response measurement20. Furthermore, our findings also indicate that the phenomenon is not exclusive to persistent stimuli, as it remains of interest when the stimulus duration is relatively short compared to the intrinsic time of the stimulated system.

It has become evident that the duration of signals plays a crucial role in regulating biological processes48. Notably, sustained stimulation does not always result in prolonged responses, and conversely, short-term stimulation can induce persistent responses. The former scenario is termed adaptation, wherein the system initially responds to a stimulus but eventually returns to basal or near-basal levels of activity49. Conversely, less understood is the persistence of signals after the initiating stimulus is removed, which constitutes a form of short-term memory50. In addition, the impact of signal duration has been observed in cellular responses where timing is critical, a phenomenon termed temporal filtering. Temporal filtering refers to the ability to suppress responses until stimulated for longer than a specified minimal time51. Closely related behaviors have also been described as kinetic proofreading52 or persistence detection53.

Within the subset of time-limited signals, there is a group exhibiting repetitive dynamics, commonly referred to by various names. Examples include intermittent oscillations54, transient responses with oscillatory dynamics55, transient oscillatory dynamics56, and transient oscillations57. The work by Marhl et al.19 describes several biological examples related to calcium signaling in which signals are indeed very short. In each case, the idealized situation of infinitely long oscillations gives way to transient oscillations occurring in bursts. In addition, Marhl et al. found, by mathematical modeling, that proteins are activated more specifically by shorter oscillatory signals.

Time-limited oscillations possess the capacity to serve as repetitive stimuli for downstream processes and, due to their time-limited nature, they hold the potential to selectively activate transient aspects of these downstream processes, as shown schematically in Fig. 1. This suggests that a transient frequency response may play a significant role in various biological processes, warranting further exploration and investigation.

We first used simple isolated systems (first two sections), then coupled two of those systems without feedback and finally used a well-known signaling model such as the MAPK cascade, able to produce a wide range of behaviors, from simple monotonic responses, to bistability and oscillations, adaptation and even chaotic dynamics29. This last model contains implicit feedbacks, as explained in ref. 32, and then expands the type of models considered.

The driving question behind our research was how minimalistic a system could be (in terms of number of connected nodes, the connectivity between them, the kinetics of involved mechanisms, nonlinearities, and feedback connections) to exhibit frequency selectivity. Previous studies suggested that feedback connections were necessary for frequency selectivity to arise58 and that simple systems like a ligand–receptor could not exhibit selectivity21,58. We reasoned that not only the dynamics of the input and the system’s structure play a role but also the observable and the time window in which that observable is computed. With these two additional degrees of freedom, and with the added complexity given by a dose conservation approach, we found that even simple systems with no feedback connections and described linearly could result in frequency selectivity. This implies that selectivity observed in more complex models does not necessarily stem solely from their complexity.

We characterized the transient frequency response of a ligand–receptor reaction. The selectivity can be interpreted in two ways due to the dimensionless nature of the model: the time in our system is t=t′koff, then the frequency is actually f=f′/koff. Therefore, we can think that a given ligand–receptor system has an optimal response to an input, or that, given a fixed input we find a ligand–receptor system, characterized by its dissociation rate constant, that optimally responds to that input.

It should be noted that the ligand–receptor system, besides representing the chemical reaction of ligand–receptor, serves as a general model of reversible binding between two molecules59. Moreover, it can also represent a simplified signaling model. For example, the ligand–receptor system is a limit case of covalent modification cycle, in the weakly activated regime25,31. Consequently, our findings for this model have implications that extend beyond the chemical reaction involving ligand–receptor binding.

We studied the frequency response of a signaling component admitting a linear one-dimensional mathematical description, as outlined in ref. 21. This model is also present in the literature as a feedforward system as in Fletcher et al. or as simple regulation model as in several references58,60,61. To ensure versatility in representing various biological scenarios, we examined different production rate functions denoted as F. First, we considered a linear F, which corresponds to a linear synthesis term24 and a weakly activated protein kinase cycle25. Secondly, a concave F emerged from Michaelis–Menten kinetics62. In addition, a sigmoidal F was considered, resulting from a Hill term58. Furthermore, we encountered a convex F in the same scenario, but only for low inputs.

Our numerical and analytical findings (refer to Supplementary Information), highlight the influence of the type of nonlinearity in the regulatory function on the the frequency response under amplitude-compensated inputs. These results align with the observations reported by Fletcher et al.21. Furthermore, we investigated the transient regime and discovered that concave and sigmoidal functions can induce a frequency preference response, contingent upon the specific characteristics of the production function’s shape.

We conducted a study on the frequency response of simple systems integrated within complex signaling networks. Specifically, we examined two instances of a two-node cascade without feedback loops, ensuring that the activity of the second node does not influence the first node. In both cases, we discovered that the initial node’s transient frequency preference can be transmitted and subsequently determines the frequency preference of the following node.

We analyzed a simple signaling network combining two ubiquitous mechanisms: a ligand–receptor reaction and a covalent modification cycle. The model was introduced and characterized in a previous article27. There it is shown that, as expected for a system with no feedback loops, the coupled system has a slower response time compared to each step alone. This feature is independent of the parameter set used. When evaluating the frequency response at the first level of the network, the relevant timescale is that of the LR reaction itself, given the absence of feedback connections. Conversely, when assessing the frequency response at the second level, the relevant timescale is that of the coupled LR-CMC system. The key factor in determining if the frequency response is calculated in the transient or the asymptotically periodic regime of the output is the relationship between the time to reach that asymptotically periodic regime and the time s that defines the time window where the observable Gs is calculated.

In Fig. 7, varying the parameters related to LR reaction, while those of the CMC and s values are fixed, causes the LR reaction and consequently the coupled LR-CMC system to exhibit slower dynamics in panels c compared to panel b. In this way, in panels c some of the selected values of s capture the transient regime of the responses leading to a transient frequency preference response.

In biology, the rates of ligand binding and unbinding to membrane receptors cover a wide range and several articles explore the implications of fast and slow ligand–receptor dynamics on cells’ ability to sense extracellular cues20. Similarly, the velocity of a covalent modification cycle can also exhibit variability, as highlighted in several articles where the cycle is driven by the accumulation of the activating enzyme63,64. This acknowledgment underscores the diverse kinetics observed in biological systems, emphasizing the importance of considering such variability in the context of our analysis.

We conducted an analysis to examine the emergence of transient frequency preference within a three-level MAPK-kinase cascade. The cascade was described mechanistically28 and the parameter values we used were experimentally reported30. Our findings revealed the emergence of frequency selectivity in both the second and third levels of the cascade, with a notable amplification observed in the latter stage. Considering that temporal aspects play a crucial role in signal transduction within the ERK pathway16,33,65,66, the identification of transient frequency preference in the MAPK model holds potential relevance in the context of decision-making processes. Unlike the previous systems studied, the ERK pathway exhibits sequestration-induced implicit feedback between levels, classifying it as a network with retroactivity32,67. This feedback mechanism likely contributes to the amplification of the transient frequency phenomenon. The emergence of a transient frequency preference is not exclusive to this system but also arises in simpler models as extensively described in this article. However, the intricacies of selectivity vary according to each model’s specific attributes.

Lastly, we investigated the frequency response of the ligand–receptor system under different hypotheses. We discovered the presence of frequency preference within the periodic regime, specifically when the observable considered is the integral above a threshold without dose conservation. This observation emphasizes the remarkable flexibility of a one-dimensional model, as it demonstrates the ligand–receptor system’s ability to exhibit frequency preference even in the periodic regime.

The major contribution of this work lies in the study of frequency responses considering a temporal window during which the output of the stimulated system is in a transient phase. To the best of our knowledge, there are no existing theoretical precedents employing a similar approach, except for the articles by Mahrl et al.19,68. However, it should be noted that their approach mixes transient and periodic regimes.

Our findings provide support for the hypothesis that frequency preference is not an inherent property of a system and the connectivity between nodes but instead emerges from the dynamic interplay between the stimulus and response, how the response is defined and when it is measured. This underscores the remarkable versatility of biological systems, enabling the transmission of multiple messages through a single signal while also exhibiting high specificity based on the characteristics of the signal-processing system.

Timescales and parameters

We consider systems that are not autonomous oscillators (except for the case of the MAPK cascade, that can behave as an oscillator for particular parameter values). These systems are characterized by a timescale τ, which is the time to reach steady state when stimulated with a step-like input, and are simple enough to produce, when forced with a periodic input, an output that can reach a pseudo-periodic regime. See formal proof of this last statement for simple systems in Supplementary Note 1. We define τp as the time it takes such a system to reach the pseudo-periodic solution. As proven in the Supplementary Information for simple systems, τp is a function of the period of the input, T. Since τp can be bounded for every choice of T considered in this article (see Supplementary Note 1 for more details), τ is a good estimator of the transient regime of the forced output.

The parameters involved in the first models (ligand–receptor, signaling components with a simplified linear description, and feedforward cascade) are dimensionless. For example, in the ligand–receptor system, a unit of time t′ corresponds to 1/koff. Therefore, the frequency f is also scaled by the reference time, so that a unit of frequency corresponds to koff. The concentration of the input is scaled by the dissociation constant for the binding-unbinding process and the complex is scaled by the total amount of receptor (RT).

For the rest of the explored models, some parameters have dimensions of concentrations (for example, RT, D1T, AT), some have dimensions of 1/time (koff,k1) and others have dimensions of 1/(time × concentration) such as kon or a1. The unit of time can be selected as seconds or minutes so that the responses are consistent with different biological processes, and the unit of concentration is arbitrary. This means that when a value for a parameter is listed, the corresponding unit has to be added, for example, if the value of parameter a is 10, it has to be read as 10 × 1/(min × concentration). Once the reference concentration is selected, all the parameters follow that reference unit. The interpretation of the results depends on the choice of the reference unit concentration, as the choice of the reference unit concentration remains a degree of freedom in our numerical methodology. For the MAPK cascade, parameters units are fixed, given by Shankaran et al.30, thus time has units of minute and concentration unit is μM, i. e. s is measure in minutes and frecuency unit is 1/min.

Adapted periodic input for the MAPK cascade

For the MAPK cascade system, the periodic input is the total amount of Ras/MKKKK (D1 in the model). In a closed system, the chemical variables are subject to mass conservation laws59. The total amount of a substance must consider all the species where that substance participates in all the reactions that describe the system. For example, if this substance X has two possible states, X and X′, and it could also bind to another molecule forming a complex CX, then the total amount of this substance is XT=X+X′+CX, where X, X′ and CX are variables in the ODE system. The presence of conservation laws conditions the periodic stimulation, since we introduce variations in the total amount of a protein, it is possible that the dose turns out negative, which has no biological sense. To vary the total amount XT, we adjust the value of the variable X for each time in the ODE system in a way such that according to the conservation law, the stimulus is the one desired. As X′ and CX depends on the systems dynamics, for example, if we start from a positive value of XT and the initial conditions are such that X′ takes a high value, it is impossible to fix XT=0 instantly, as in a train of square pulses, since X should be negative to compensate X′ and CX. Therefore, adapted (modified) versions of the input must be used31. The stimulus was adapted by stimulating the desired total amount of Ras/MKKKK at each time and setting its value to 0 if it resulted negative. From simulations, we concluded that this adaptation does not severely modify the total amount of Ras/MKKKK, as its average is mostly constant in all periods considered.
