Pharmacopuncture therapy versus usual care for lumbar spinal stenosis: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Su Won Lee, Yong-jun Ahn, Suna Kim, Yeonsun Lee, Hyun-Woo Cho, Dongwoo Nam, Yeoncheol Park, Eun-Jung Kim, Yoon Jae Lee, In-Hyuk Ha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe prevalence of lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is rising worldwide, particularly in aging societies, increasing the need for effective conservative treatments. Pharmacopuncture therapy (PPT), which involves injecting herbal extracts into targets such as acupoints, muscles or nerves to improve lumbar pain and function, is widely utilized in Korean clinical settings; however, high-quality evidence remains limited. We evaluated the effectiveness and safety of PPT, compared with conventional medicine-based usual care (UC).MethodsThis multicenter, pragmatic, randomized controlled trial included PPT and UC groups (1:1 ratio). Patients with LSS received treatment twice weekly for 12 weeks, with follow-ups at weeks 13, 25, and 53. Outcomes related to pain, functional disability, and quality of life were measured.ResultsNinety-six patients were randomized and included in the intention-to-treat analysis. Change in the numeric rating scale (NRS) score for dominant pain from baseline to week 13 showed a significantly greater reduction of 2.7 points in the PPT group than in the UC group. The PPT group also showed significant improvements in other secondary outcomes, and these effects were sustained until week 53. A survival analysis of patients with a ≥50 % NRS score reduction over 53 weeks indicated significantly faster recovery in the PPT group than in the UC group. No serious adverse events were observed.ConclusionCompared with UC, PPT was associated with significantly greater reductions in low back and leg pain and superior improvements in function and quality of life in patients with LSS. These findings support prioritizing PPT as an effective conservative treatment for LSS.Trial registrationThis study was registered at CRIS (cris.nih.go.kr; KCT0007145) and ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05242497).

Introduction

Lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) is a pathological narrowing of the spinal canal that compresses neurovascular structures, typically manifesting as neurogenic claudication or radicular pain.1 Affecting approximately 103 million individuals globally, LSS significantly impairs quality of life through chronic pain and functional deterioration.2 While conservative management remains the primary clinical approach, surgical intervention is often pursued for refractory cases. However, the long-term superiority of surgery remains contentious, as symptomatic benefits often diminish over time, reaching parity with non-surgical outcomes in extended follow-ups. Despite these uncertainties, surgical rates continue to rise alongside the aging population, escalating the global socioeconomic burden.3, 4 Consequently, there is a critical need to identify effective conservative treatments that can serve as viable alternatives.

In the clinical practice of the Republic of Korea, a survey of domestic Korean medicine doctors’ treatments for LSS showed that pharmacopuncture therapy (PPT), herbal medicine, and Chuna therapy are used as frequently as acupuncture.5 Among these modalities, the present study focuses on the effects of PPT, involving the injection of filtered and sterilized herbal extract into target areas such as acupoints, muscles, or nerves.6 The therapeutic effect is maximized by the synergistic influence of the needle’s mechanical and chemical stimuli of the herbal extract. Additionally, clinical practice guidelines for lumbar disc herniation also recommend combining PPT with other treatments for pain reduction and functional recovery.7

A recently published randomized controlled trial (RCT) examining the add-on effect of PPT to conventional Korean medicine treatments found that the group receiving combined PPT achieved a higher rate of clinical improvement.8 Apart from this, only retrospective comparative analyses have examined the add-on effect of bee venom PPT to acupuncture,9, 10 or RCTs in which PPT was combined with other treatments have merely confirmed its effectiveness compared with control groups.11, 12 While some studies have explored the add-on effects of PPT, no RCT has yet evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of PPT as a standalone treatment compared to other treatments for LSS.

Consequently, we conducted a pragmatic RCT to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of PPT compared to conventional usual care (UC), including physical therapy and medication. By reflecting real-world clinical settings, the study aims to identify an optimized conservative treatment strategy and provide foundational evidence for future large-scale studies.

Methods

Study ethics and design

This parallel-group, multicenter, pragmatic RCT was conducted across seven hospitals within the Republic of Korea: Jaseng Hospitals of Korean Medicine in Bucheon, Daejeon, Busan and Seoul; Kyung Hee University Korean Medicine Hospital at Gangdong and Hoegi; and Dongguk University Bundang Oriental Hospital. Ninety-eight patients with LSS who voluntarily participated and met the eligibility criteria were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either PPT or UC. Patients received treatment twice weekly for 12 weeks, with follow-up evaluations performed at weeks 13, 25, and 53.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of each site (approval numbers: JASENG 2021-12-003, JASENG 2021-12-019, JASENG 2021-12-017, JASENG 2021-12-008, KHNMCOH 2022-01-001, KOMCIRB 2021-12-002, and DOBUH 2022-001) before the trial. All study procedures were conducted in compliance with the Korean Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was registered at the Clinical Research Information Service, the South Korean Registration Service for Clinical Trials (KCT0007145) and Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05242497). Furthermore, the protocol of this study partly conformed to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials extension for pragmatic clinical trials, and the study adhered to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials guidelines and checklist. Before enrollment, participants were given a comprehensive overview of the study and subsequently provided voluntary written informed consent.

Participants

We enrolled patients aged 19-69 years with radiologically confirmed diagnosis of LSS. These participants also had to exhibit neurogenic claudication and low back pain (LBP) or radiating leg pain (LP) with a score greater than 5 in the numeric rating scale (NRS). We excluded patients with vascular claudication, other systemic diseases or soft tissue pathologies causing LBP or radiating LP. The study protocol outlines the detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria.13

Sample size

Ninety-eight patients were included in this study. This sample size was calculated to achieve 80 % power at a 0.05 significance level, assuming a between-group difference and mean standard deviation of 1.75 and 2.75, respectively.14, 15 Previous studies with this same design were not available; therefore, we referenced studies with similar designs and minimum clinically important difference (MCID) values. A sample size of 38.8 participants per group was determined based on these parameters. Considering an estimated 20 % dropout rate and the multi-center nature of the study, the total sample size was finalized at 98.

Randomization and blinding

Eligible voluntary participants were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the PPT or UC group. An independent statistician generated a random sequence before the study. For randomization, a random number table was created using R 4.1.1 software (the R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and random block sizes of 2, 4, and 6 were utilized. The resulting sequences were concealed in opaque envelopes and secured in a double-locked locker. To ensure allocation concealment, only the statistician had access to the complete randomization sequence. A delegated research coordinator at each institution was tasked with opening an envelope for each patient and assigning them to their allocated group. Due to the nature of the two interventions, it was not feasible to blind the participants or the treating physicians to the group assignments. To minimize potential performance and detection bias, the outcome assessors—who were Korean medicine doctors not involved in the treatment process—remained strictly blinded to the allocation throughout the study.

Interventions

Pharmacopuncture therapy

PPT was performed by a Korean medicine doctor, with sessions occurring twice weekly for 12 weeks. The acupoints used for PPT encompassed a series of lower back points, including BL23, BL25, BL28, and EX-B2 of L1–L5 (Hyeopcheok points). Additionally, the PPT solutions included bee venom, Hominis Placenta, Joongsungouhyul, Shinbaro, and Hwangryunhaedok-tang. The type of PPT solution and the amount delivered (ranged from 0.1 to 10 cc) were determined at the physician’s discretion, based on symptoms, radiographic findings, and the extent of improvement, and were documented in the electronic medical chart.

Usual care

Physical therapy was administered twice weekly for 12 weeks, including transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), interference current therapy (ICT), and ultrasound. The regions of physiotherapy included the lumbar area, gluteal region, and lower extremities. Specifically, the type, region, and procedure times were determined at the physician’s discretion, considering symptoms, radiographic findings, and the extent of improvement. They were documented in the electronic medical chart. Based on Western medicine doctor’s judgment, analgesics and muscle relaxants were prescribed as needed. The details of the medication—including its type, duration, and dosage—were then recorded in the case report form.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome variable was the change in the NRS score of dominant pain between LBP and radiating LP from baseline to week 13 at the end of treatment. Specifically, the NRS is a scale that allows patients to rate their pain intensity over the past week on a 0-to-10 scale, where 0 represents no pain and 10 signifies the most severe pain imaginable. The NRS was assessed at every visit throughout the treatment period and follow-up periods.

Secondary outcomes

We individually evaluated the NRS scores for LBP and LP in addition to assessing the primary outcome of dominant pain. To supplement the pain scale assessment, a visual analog scale (VAS)—which measures pain intensity on a 0–100 mm line—was used to investigate both LBP and LP. For functional assessment, claudication-free walking distance and maximum walking distance were reported based on the past three days at each assessment point. The Zurich Claudication Questionnaire (ZCQ) is a self-report tool for patients with LSS, designed to assess severity of the condition, functional ability, and treatment satisfaction. A Korean-validated version of the tool was used in this study.16 The Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) is an instrument used to evaluate the extent of disability associated with LBP. Scores on the scale range from 0 to 5, with elevated scores showing more severe disability. This study employed a Korean version of the questionnaire that has been previously validated.17 Outcome variables related to overall quality of life included the Short Form-12 Health Survey version 2 (SF-12)18 and EuroQol 5 Dimensions 5 Level (EQ-5D-5L)19 where elevated scores reflect an improved quality of life. Additionally, the Patient Global Impression of Change, a seven-point score on which patients evaluate their degree of improvement after treatment, was assessed. Regarding the assessment schedule, the VAS was measured weekly throughout the treatment period, while walking distance, ZCQ, ODI, SF-12, and EQ-5D-5L were assessed at weeks 1, 5, and 9. During the follow-up period, all outcome measures, including the PGIC, were collected at each follow-up visit.

Safety assessment

To assess safety, all unintended and undesirable symptoms, disease, or signs reported by patients or investigated by researchers at each visit throughout the trial. These included abnormal results from clinical pathology tests (clinical chemistry tests, complete blood count, and C-reactive protein levels) conducted pre- and post-trial. The causality of AEs was determined using a six-level scale from the World Health Organization Uppsala Monitoring Center Causality Assessment System, and severity was evaluated at three levels according to the Spilker classification.

Statistical analysis

The main analysis was performed using an intention-to-treat approach, with a per-protocol (PP) analysis as a supplementary measure, including only patients who completed at least 70 % of their intended treatment schedule. Baseline characteristics and treatment expectancies were summarized per group using summary statistics. Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviations, whereas categorical data was summarized using frequencies and percentiles ( %). To assess between-group differences, the chi-square or Fisher's exact test was used for categorical data, whereas Student's t-test or Wilcoxon's rank-sum test were employed for continuous data.

Outcome assessments, including the primary outcome (NRS score), were conducted using a linear mixed model. Patient random effects were incorporated into the random intercept model, and baseline outcomes and covariate factors were treated as fixed effects. The model was designed to assess the time-by-group interaction, thereby analyzing how changes over time differed between the groups. Missing data were handled utilizing a mixed models for repeated measures, and for sensitivity analysis, we used analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with multiple imputations or the last-observation-carried-forward method. Specifically, to adjust for potential confounding and baseline imbalances, the baseline outcome and treatment expectancy were included as fixed-effect covariates in the LMM and ANCOVA. The area under the curve (AUC) was also analyzed to determine cumulative effectiveness using ANCOVA with multiple imputations. In the AUC analysis, age and sex were additionally adjusted for as covariates, alongside baseline values and treatment expectancy, to further account for demographic influences. For survival analysis, an event was specified as a less than 50 % reduction in NRS score from baseline, and comparisons were then conducted with Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and the log-rank test. Calculations for hazard ratios were completed with a Cox proportional hazards model. All statistical computations were carried out using SAS© v.9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) or R© v.4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05.

Results

Participants

The study enrolled a total of 118 patients, 20 of whom dropped out after screening. Ninety-eight participants were enrolled and then randomly assigned at a 1:1 ratio, yielding 49 patients in each group. For the intention-to-treat analysis, 96 patients (PPT group, 48; UC group, 48) were analyzed after excluding two participants whose duplicate enrollment across multiple sites was found to potentially compromise data integrity (Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of the patients were presented in Table 1. Sex composition and mean age were similar between the two groups, with patients in their 60s being the most prevalent in both groups (Table 1). No significant differences were found between the two groups in sociological characteristics such as height, weight, and medical history, or in the baseline values of all outcomes. The only exception was the treatment expectancy scale, assessed at screening before group allocation, where the PPT group showed higher treatment expectancy than the UC group. Treatment

While the total number of treatment sessions was fixed and basic treatment guidelines were provided, slight variations in patient-specific treatments were possible owing to the pragmatic clinical trial design. PPT solutions, such as Shinbaro 1–3, bee venom, and Hominis placenta, were frequently used in the PPT group. The most common acupoints included EX-B2, BL25, BL28, BL23, EX-B7, BL24, and GB30, which were primarily located in the lumbar-back region of the Bladder Meridian (BL), although points on the calf (BL57), around the thoracic spine (BL20 and BL21), and the gluteal region were also used (Supplement 1). In the UC group, physical therapies, such as ICT, TENS, ultrasound, and laser treatment, were frequently used, typically for 10–20 min. Most of these were applied to the lumbar region, with the gluteal and lower extremity areas included based on the pain location. Medications used as needed typically consisted of analgesics and digestive aids to mitigate side effects and were usually prescribed for a duration of 1–2 weeks. Supplement 2 presents the detailed information on the treatment interventions and ultimately shows that the overall treatment frequency was balanced between the groups.

Regarding walking distance, the PPT group showed significantly greater improvements than the UC group at all follow-up visits. At week 13, the between-group difference for maximum walking distance was 1.61 (p < 0.001). The standard deviations were relatively large due to the presence of certain high outliers. Safety

Thirty-six AEs occurred in 19 patients. The PPT group experienced 17 events in 11 patients, and the UC group experienced 19 events in 8 patients. The occurrence rate did not differ significantly between the groups. Most of these symptoms were consistent with the common cold or upper respiratory infections unrelated to the interventions. Only four cases of skin pruritus and warmth in the PPT group were potentially associated with the injection site; however, these were expected to be local symptoms that resolved quickly. No serious AEs occurred, and no patients discontinued the study owing to an AE. One patient with abnormal blood test results had no other symptoms, and the patient who underwent regular checkups without any issues refused further follow-up. All other symptoms were confirmed to be either recovering or had resolved with appropriate measures such as medication or observation (Supplement 6).

Discussion

Summary of key findings

The findings of this study suggest that PPT provided significantly greater benefits in alleviating pain and improving function and quality of life than UC (based on physical therapy with medication as needed) in patients with LSS. This superior effect was observed not only at the primary endpoint of week 13 post-treatment, but also at weeks 25 and 53 (approximately 1 year).

Comparison with previous studies

The clinical significance of our findings is underscored when compared with established MCID thresholds from previous literature. Notably, the NRS score reduction in the PPT group exceeded the MCID of 2 points for LBP and 2-4 points for LP reported in patients undergoing surgical decompression for LSS.20 Similarly, the improvements in the ZCQ symptom and function domains met the MCID criteria (0.75-0.84 and 0.60-0.76, respectively) typically observed one year postoperatively.21 Furthermore, the ODI recovery aligned with the MCID for moderate improvement in non-surgical LSS patients.22 While quality of life trends favored PPT, only the SF-12 PCS met the intergroup MCID established in the postoperative study on recurrent LSS.23

A review of previous studies showed that while no research has established PPT as a stand-alone treatment for LSS, our results align with systematic reviews providing moderate evidence that 6–8 weeks of acupuncture for degenerative LSS was more effective than sham acupuncture24 or medication and exercise25 in the short-to-medium term. An observational study that administered integrative Korean medicine, including PPT, to patients with LSS with long-term follow-up also reported that the NRS score for LBP and LP and the ODI decreased by more than the MCID.26 The addition of PPT to integrative Korean medicine treatments for LSS resulted in greater improvement than using the conventional integrated treatment alone; however, the two groups did not differ significantly.8 Other studies9, 10, 11, 12 that examined the add-on effect of PPT to other treatments, as well as a pragmatic RCT27 for chronic LBP, further suggested the therapeutic potential of PPT.

Clinical and scientific implications

Many studies are currently being conducted to confirm the superiority of various conservative treatments to aid in clinical decision-making, and the present research on PPT is a significant contribution to this effort. Our findings demonstrate that PPT is effective in reducing both LBP and LP, while simultaneously improving functional disability and quality of life in patients with LSS compared to those receiving UC.

This superior efficacy is may be explained by PPT’s direct, targeted approach. Unlike non-invasive physical therapy, PPT induces synergistic effects by combining physical acupuncture stimulation with the pharmacological actions of herbal solutions directly at the affected site. These solutions have been shown to modulate inflammatory mediators and promote tissue recovery in LSS model,28, 29 providing a biological rationale for the more rapid and robust clinical response observed in this trial.

Building on this therapeutic potential, these results provide a clinical and scientific basis for integrating PPT into long-term management strategies for LSS. In particular, this study highlights PPT’s role as a viable non-surgical alternative for middle-aged and older adults with degenerative LSS who require sustained symptom relief. For future scientific advancement, further studies are needed to establish the definitive superiority of PPT by specifying a particular type of solution or conducting head-to-head comparisons with other injection therapies of similar invasiveness. Such research should continue to weigh the advantages and disadvantages in terms of efficacy, sustainability, and adverse effects, ultimately providing more precise evidence for evidence-based clinical practice in LSS.

Limitations and strengths

This study had some limitations. First, due to the nature of the two interventions, the study design could not be blinded to the participants. Moreover, a higher treatment expectancy was observed in the PPT group at baseline, and this difference was adjusted for as a covariate in all statistical analyses of the outcomes. To further reduce bias, the outcome assessors were Korean medicine doctors who were different from the treating physicians and were blinded to the group assignments. Second, the follow-up rate was lower in the control group than in the treatment group. While some of these were due to personal circumstances, others were voluntary withdrawals since the desired effect did not occur as the treatment progressed. These missing values did not account for a large portion of the total data and could be compensated for using statistical methods. To increase the reliability of our results, we also performed a sensitivity analysis using different missing data imputation methods and a PP analysis. Third, as a pragmatic clinical trial conducted only in one country, it may be challenging to replicate the treatment overseas, regardless of the effectiveness of the PPT. While the Republic of Korea oversees the quality control of PPT solutions via an external herbal dispensary certification system,30 other countries' Food and Drug Administrations may have different criteria. Fourth, there was considerable heterogeneity in the interventions provided. Although the overall treatment frequency was equal in both groups, and there was some focus on certain treatment modalities, the interpretation of differences in treatment effects may be limited. Fifth, as outcomes were patient-reported, subjective bias may persist. Although baseline expectancy was adjusted as a covariate, it may still have influenced other subjective measures. Finally, excluding participants over 70 may not fully reflect the clinical profile of LSS, which is highly prevalent in the elderly. To ensure the broader applicability of PPT for LSS patients, future research targeting this older demographic will be needed.

Despite these limitations, this study represents the first pragmatic RCT to compare PPT as a stand-alone treatment with UC for patients with LSS. As surgical costs and risks rises, identifying effective conservative treatment methods is particularly important, especially in older adult patients where issues such as delayed recovery and complications can occur postoperatively. It can also result in failed back surgery syndrome, in which pain persists or worsens postoperatively.31 Therefore, the risks must be carefully weighed against the potential benefits.

Conclusions and suggestions for future research

In conclusion, in this pragmatic RCT, 12 weeks of PPT for patients with LSS resulted in significant improvements in LBP, LP, function, and the quality of life compared with UC based on physical therapy and medication. These beneficial effects persisted up to week 53. Therefore, PPT could be considered a promising and safe conservative treatment option for LSS in clinical practice. Building on this therapeutic potential, future research should include larger-scale studies to compare it with other treatments and assess various aspects, such as cost-effectiveness.
