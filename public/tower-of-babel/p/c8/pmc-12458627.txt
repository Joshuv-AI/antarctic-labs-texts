Assessing a visual editor for healthcare questionnaires based on the fast healthcare interoperability resources (FHIR) standard: protocol for a cross-sectional, mixed-methods usability evaluation using eye-tracking and retrospective think-aloud
Authors: Carsten Vogel, Rüdiger Pryss, Peter Heuschmann, Viktoria Rücker, Michael Winter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundDigitalisation in healthcare has resulted in fragmented solutions and limited interoperability. The Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) standard is increasingly adopted to enable standardised data exchange, yet its complexity creates usability challenges for clinicians and developers. To address these challenges, this study evaluates the usability of an enhanced FHIR Questionnaire Resource Editor designed to improve workflow efficiency, accessibility and user satisfaction in creating and managing healthcare questionnaires.Methods and analysisThis mixed-methods usability evaluation will recruit 10 healthcare professionals and/or informatics experts via convenience sampling. The study will consist of four general phases: (1) an initial session to familiarise users with the tool; (2) a task analysis phase supported by eye-tracking to identify strengths and weaknesses; (3) retrospective think-aloud interviews to explore strategies used during tasks and (4) completion of a validated usability questionnaire, such as the System Usability Scale, to quantify user satisfaction. We will analyse quantitative data using descriptive and inferential statistics. Qualitative feedback will be examined through thematic analysis and affinity mapping. The primary outcome is to assess the editor’s usability and accessibility and to identify areas for improvement.Ethics and disseminationThis study protocol has been reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty at the University of Würzburg (ethikkommission@uni-wuerzburg.de) under approval number (24/24-sc). All participants will provide informed consent. Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, conferences and open-access platforms to inform future iterations of FHIR-based tools.Keywords: MEDICAL EDUCATION & TRAINING, Digital Technology, eHealth

Introduction

The increasing relevance of standardised medical data in modern healthcare underscores the importance of adopting robust frameworks for data exchange. The Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources (FHIR) standard has emerged as a leading protocol for facilitating interoperability between healthcare applications.1 While FHIR offers significant potential for improving the exchange and integration of health information, the complexity of its implementation presents notable usability challenges, particularly for healthcare professionals and developers.2

Usability is critical for the adoption and effective use of health Information Technology (IT) systems.3,5 Research has consistently demonstrated that systems designed with a focus on user experience are associated with higher user satisfaction and a greater likelihood of long-term adoption.6,8 In healthcare, user-friendly interfaces can alleviate the workload of medical staff, reduce the risk of errors and improve patient safety—factors that are vital for maintaining high-quality care.9,11 Conversely, poorly designed interfaces can lead to inefficiencies, increased error rates and reduced user acceptance, all of which compromise the system’s overall effectiveness.12 13

Rationale for the study

Despite the potential of FHIR-based tools, their complexity may hinder routine clinical adoption, especially for tools like questionnaire editors that require both technical and clinical input. Including these expert groups as study participants is essential, since both technical and clinical perspectives are necessary for the successful adoption and real-world utility of FHIR questionnaire tools. Structured healthcare questionnaires play a crucial role in data collection and interoperability.14 However, their adoption depends on rigorous usability evaluation.15 Our study addresses these needs by evaluating an enhanced FHIR Questionnaire Resource Editor developed with end-user input.

Methods and analysis

Study design, setting and tool description

This study will follow a prospective, observational, mixed-methods usability evaluation approach to assess the enhanced FHIR Questionnaire Resource Editor. The design is structured to capture both quantitative and qualitative data to comprehensively evaluate usability of the editor.18

The study will be conducted at the Institute for Clinical Epidemiology and Biometry, University of Würzburg, using a controlled environment equipped with eye-tracking equipment, audio recording devices and computers with the pre-installed FHIR Questionnaire Resource Editor. The controlled environment ensures minimal external distractions and consistent data collection conditions.2

Each participant will take part in a single session lasting approximately 30–45 min. All sessions will be scheduled over a 2-month data collection period (September 2025 to October 2025).

FHIR is a Health Level 7 standard enabling the electronic exchange of healthcare data. Recruitment

Participants (ie, healthcare professionals and informatics experts) will be recruited using convenience sampling methods.19 Announcements will be made via institutional mailing lists and physical notices at the University of Würzburg and affiliated institutions. Interested individuals will be provided with study details and invited to complete an eligibility screening.

Including healthcare professionals and informatics experts will provide diverse usability insights from different intended user perspectives. This approach will help identify potential usability challenges and opportunities for improvement in the FHIR Questionnaire Resource Editor.

Sample size and rationale

The study will recruit a sample size of 10 participants, following the principles of usability testing and the law of diminishing returns.20 Thereby, we aim for balanced representation with five healthcare professionals and five informatics experts participating. Research in Human-Computer Interaction indicates that the majority of usability issues can be identified with as few as five participants.21 22 Adding more participants provides diminishing additional insights, making a sample size of 10 sufficient to uncover both major and minor usability challenges.

While the sample size of 10 participants (five healthcare professionals and five informatics experts) is grounded in established usability testing principles, the study will also include exploratory and descriptive comparisons between these two user groups. Metrics such as System Usability Scale (SUS) scores and task performance will be examined to identify potential trends or meaningful differences. Although the small sample size limits statistical power, basic inferential analyses (eg, comparing group medians or distributions) may be performed to explore whether observed patterns are consistent with statistically notable effects.23

The selected sample size balances resource efficiency with sufficient depth of data collection, enabling a thorough evaluation of the FHIR Questionnaire Resource Editor. Recruiting participants with professional backgrounds relevant to the editor’s use ensures the findings are directly applicable to its target user base and yield actionable insights for iterative refinement. This approach reflects the aims of qualitative usability testing, which focuses on identifying and resolving design issues rather than producing statistically representative results.

Data recording and management

Questionnaire data: demographic forms and responses from the SUS will be securely stored in a digital format. These responses will be translated into an analysable format (eg, numerical scoring for SUS items and categorical coding for demographics) to facilitate quantitative analysis.

Eye-tracking: recoding of eye movements is employed in this study to objectively capture participants’ visual attention and navigation behaviour during usability tasks. This approach enables the identification of interface elements that attract attention, areas of confusion and workflow inefficiencies that may not be evident through observation or self-report alone. Eye-tracking data, including fixation durations, gaze paths and heatmaps, are widely recognised for their value in usability evaluation, supporting the detection of design issues and informing iterative improvements.24 25

For capturing visual metrics such as heatmaps, gaze plots and fixation durations to analyse navigation patterns and visual engagement, the Pupil Labs Core equipment will be used, a binocular mobile device operating at a sampling rate of 200 Hz with an average spatial accuracy of approximately 0.6° visual angle.26 A 5-point calibration will be performed before each session, using the Pupil Labs calibration interface. The calibration process will be repeated if tracking quality is deemed suboptimal (eg, if the average error exceeds 2°–3°). Data transformation of eye movement parameters will be performed using custom scripts to prepare the data for analysis.

Audio and video recordings: verbal feedback from the think-aloud and retrospective sessions will be recorded using a mobile device with a high-quality external microphone via a speech recording app, and system interactions will be captured with a screen recording tool. Participants will not be invited to review transcripts.

The think-aloud protocol is employed to gain real-time insights into participants’ cognitive processes as they interact with the FHIR Questionnaire Resource Editor. By encouraging participants to verbalise their thoughts, reasoning and decision-making while completing tasks, the study can identify usability barriers, misunderstandings and intuitive aspects of the interface that may not be evident from observation or performance data alone.27 This approach enriches the qualitative dataset, supports the identification of root causes behind observed behaviours and informs actionable improvements to the tool’s design. The think-aloud method is widely recognised in usability research for its effectiveness in revealing user experiences and guiding iterative system refinement.28

In general, prior successful research relied on similar setup for eye tracking and think-aloud.29 30 Data recording and management will be the responsibility of the study management team, who will collaboratively engage in a consensus-building process to ensure consistency, accuracy and correctness of all data handling procedures across the study.

Integration of quantitative and qualitative data

Quantitative metrics (eg, SUS scores, task completion times, error frequencies) will be integrated with qualitative themes from think-aloud and retrospective feedback sessions. This triangulation will enhance validity by revealing not only which usability issues occur, but also their underlying causes.39 40

The integration will follow a convergent mixed-methods design, wherein qualitative and quantitative data are collected and analysed independently but merged at the interpretation stage to develop a unified understanding of usability.41 This approach is particularly appropriate in usability research, as it allows for the correlation of performance data (eg, task times, errors) with subjective feedback (eg, confusion, frustration), providing a more nuanced perspective on user experience. For instance, lower SUS scores or observed task difficulties will be cross-referenced with verbal reports of navigation problems or interface misunderstandings to uncover underlying design issues and prioritise actionable improvements.

Qualitative data will be analysed using inductive thematic analysis, a flexible, data-driven approach that supports the discovery of patterns in user feedback without relying on pre-established categories.42 43 Emergent themes will be refined using affinity mapping, a collaborative, visual method that clusters related ideas to identify overarching categories and usability patterns.44 By integrating both data types in a structured and theory-informed manner, the study ensures that usability findings are both empirically grounded and contextually meaningful, ultimately guiding iterative tool refinement.

Supporting software and tools

Quantitative data, including descriptive statistics and SUS scoring, will be analysed using statistical software such as SPSS or R. Qualitative analysis will be conducted in Miro, which will facilitate thematic coding and affinity mapping of retrospective think-aloud transcripts and user feedback.45

Eye-tracking data will be processed using Pupil Player, the dedicated software from Pupil Labs, to extract and visualise gaze information such as heatmaps, gaze plots and fixation sequences. Additional data transformation and parameter extraction will be carried out using custom scripts. These scripts are adapted and established in-house products based on prior work within our research group, forming a standardised pipeline for efficient and detailed analysis of engagement behaviours.29 30 46

In more detail, raw eye-tracking data will be cleaned to remove artefacts, segmented by task and processed to extract key metrics such as fixation durations, saccade lengths and areas of interest. Aggregated data will be visualised using heatmaps and gaze plots, facilitating the identification of usability barriers and supporting quantitative and qualitative analysis of user interaction patterns.47

The outputs from these scripts are structured for compatibility with subsequent statistical analysis in R or SPSS, ensuring seamless integration with the overall analytical workflow.

Audio files, recorded with a Rode NT-USB,48 from think-aloud and feedback sessions will be transcribed using the Transcribe app.49 While the use of AI-assisted transcription tools is conceivable in future applications, it is not currently planned for this study. Screen interactions will be recorded with the tool OBS Studio.50

All questionnaire responses, including SUS and demographic data, will be digitally collected and translated into analysable formats (eg, numerical SUS scores, categorical variables for demographics) to facilitate integration into the analysis pipeline.

This comprehensive and structured data analysis approach ensures a robust understanding of the usability of the FHIR Questionnaire Resource Editor, supporting evidence-based recommendations for further development and refinement.

Data security

All collected data will be anonymised and stored on encrypted servers to ensure confidentiality. Personal identifiers will be kept separate from the study data and accessible only to authorised research personnel. Audio recordings will be transcribed and deleted once transcription is complete.

By combining task-based usability testing with qualitative feedback and quantitative assessments, this data collection procedure ensures a robust evaluation of the FHIR Questionnaire Resource Editor, providing actionable insights for its refinement.

Data collection procedures

The study will include the following seven phases (as shown in figure 3).

Study design.

Phase 1: study begin

Each session will be conducted with only one participant at a time to ensure a controlled environment. After expressing interest in the study, participants will receive a scheduled time slot for their session. Participants will be welcomed in a prepared study room designed to provide comfort and minimise distractions. The overall study period (ie, the full data collection window) will span 2 months starting in August 2025. Each participant will only take part in a single session lasting approximately 30–45 min. On arrival, they will be given the study description and may ask questions at any point. Inclusion and exclusion criteria will be reviewed, and if the participant is eligible and willing to proceed, written informed consent will be obtained before beginning the study.

Phase 2: basic data collection

Participants will complete a brief digital questionnaire on a mobile device to collect basic demographic information, such as age, gender and professional background. This information will be used to contextualise usability findings and ensure the inclusion of relevant user perspectives.

Phase 3: eye-tracking calibration phase

Before beginning the usability tasks, participants will be fitted with eye-tracking glasses and given time to adjust and become comfortable with the equipment. A 5-point calibration procedure will be conducted to ensure accurate tracking of gaze patterns and visual attention. Study personnel will assist with the calibration process and verify that data capture is functioning correctly before proceeding to the next phase.

Phase 4: preparatory phase

Participants will undergo an onboarding process to familiarise themselves with the study protocol and the FHIR Questionnaire Resource Editor. This phase includes a briefing on study objectives, instructions for using the editor and a demonstration of key functionalities. Each participant will then have the opportunity to explore the editor independently at their own pace (ie, time taken will be noted). Further, from this point onward, eye movements will be recorded and the duration of this phase will be determined by the participant.

Phase 5: usability testing

Once the participants feel confident in their understanding of how the editor works, they will be given five tasks to complete (eg, create a new questionnaire with a specific structure). Eye-tracking will capture gaze patterns, fixations and navigation behaviours to evaluate the editor’s intuitiveness and accessibility.24 Participants may ask for clarification at any point if needed. They will be encouraged to complete the tasks as quickly as possible, but also as thoroughly as possible. Throughout this phase, study personnel will take notes on any questions raised or if a participant encounters difficulties or becomes stuck. Further, additional parameters like time needed to complete each task will be noted.

Phase 6: retrospective feedback phase

During usability testing, participants’ interactions with the editor, including screen activity and eye movements, will be videorecorded. Following task completion, a retrospective think-aloud session will invite participants to describe the strategies they used, reflect on challenges encountered and provide feedback on their overall experience. Audio will be recorded using a microphone connected to a mobile device to capture participants’ verbalisations during this session. This session aims to identify further usability issues and gather suggestions for improvement of the FHIR Questionnaire Resource Editor.28

Phase 7: post-task usability assessment

At the end of the session, participants will complete the validated SUS questionnaire to quantify perceived effectiveness and impression of the editor.51 Participants will also have the opportunity to provide any final feedback or comments.

Bias

To reduce potential bias, several methodological safeguards are in place. First, although convenience sampling can introduce selection bias, including both healthcare professionals and informatics experts ensures diversity in experience and perspectives relevant to the tool’s target users. Second, all usability sessions will be conducted individually in a controlled environment to minimise external distractions and environmental variability. Third, observer bias is mitigated by employing objective data collection methods, including eye-tracking and system-logged task performance metrics. Finally, the retrospective think-aloud sessions are structured to elicit unfiltered participant experiences without real-time interference, helping to reduce social desirability bias.

Ethics and dissemination

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty at the University of, under approval number (24/24-sc). The study will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and all relevant local and international ethical guidelines. Prior to participation, all eligible participants will provide written informed consent. The confidentiality and anonymity of all participants will be strictly maintained throughout the study. Data will be stored securely on encrypted systems, accessible only to authorised research personnel. Personal identifiers will be removed from all data during analysis to ensure participant privacy.

Dissemination

The findings from this study will be disseminated through multiple channels to reach both academic and practitioner audiences. Results will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals and presented at relevant national and international conferences focusing on digital health, usability and health informatics. To ensure accessibility and transparency, lay summaries of the findings will be shared with participants and stakeholders. These summaries will also be made available through open-access platforms and institutional websites. Additionally, the study outcomes will be shared with software developers and healthcare organisations to guide future development of FHIR-based tools.

This comprehensive dissemination strategy ensures that the results contribute to advancing knowledge and practice in health IT usability while promoting wider adoption of effective, user-centred digital health solutions.
