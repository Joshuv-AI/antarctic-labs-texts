Attitudes, stigma and knowledge on obesity in Mexico: a national cross-sectional survey of the general population and healthcare professionals living with or without obesity
Authors: Verónica Vázquez-Velázquez, Nancy R. Mejía Domínguez, Leonardo G. Mancillas-Adame, Eduardo García-García, Gerardo Magallanes Díaz, Johann Rafael Montoya García, Stuart W. Flint
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundWeight stigma has major health impacts but remains understudied in Latin America. This study explores attitudes, stigma and knowledge of obesity in Mexican healthcare professionals (HCPs) and the general population (GenPop) living or not with overweight/obesity.MethodsA national cross-sectional, anonymous, online survey was conducted among 3859 adults (1262 GenPop and 2597 HCPs). Participants completed sociodemographic questions, three validated instruments assessing stigma, beliefs, and internalized bias, 12 items evaluating obesity knowledge and treatment perceptions, and 14 items addressing medical care experiences and weight stigma among GenPop. Four groups were created based on body mass index (BMI): GenPop and HCPs living with overweight or obesity (GenPop/OW-OB and HCPs/OW-OB, BMI ≥25 kg/m2) and without (GenPop/NOW-OB and HCPs/NOW-OB, BMI <25 kg/m2).FindingsStigma and beliefs varied significantly across groups. HCPs/NOW-OB reported the highest weight stigma scores, while GenPop/OW-OB showed the highest weight bias internalization and HCPs/OW-OB scored highest on beliefs that obesity is outside of individual control. Internalization was higher in women and decreased with age. Less than 30% define obesity as a disease, and even so, stigmatizing and outdated responses related to weight loss, weight regain, and the treatment of severe obesity were demonstrated. Personal experiences revealed that 79% of GenPop/OW-OB avoided medical visits, with over half feeling judged by doctors and 72–83% reporting weight stigma and discrimination, especially within their own families and social circles.InterpretationThis study underscores the urgent need for education addressing misconceptions about obesity's causes and controllability among HCPs and GenPop. Despite recognition of obesity as a disease, biological factors are underrepresented, and weight stigma drives healthcare avoidance. These findings highlight the importance of culturally sensitive education to reduce weight stigma and improve understanding of the complexity of obesity, and the promotion of empathy by recognizing the lived experiences faced by people living with obesity, for both Mexican HCPs and GenPop.FundingAn investigator-led grant awarded to Obesidades SC (where VVV is president) was received from Novo Nordisk to cover the cost of QualtricsTM participant pooling. The study was conceived and the methods designed by VVV and SWF before the investigator-led funding application. The sponsor had no role in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or manuscript writing. The fee for open access publication in The Lancet Regional Health - Americas was covered by the Instituto Nacional de Ciencias Médicas y Nutrición Salvador Zubirán (INCMNSZ).

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed from database inception to December 2021 using the terms “obesity stigma,” “weight bias,” “attitudes toward obesity,” “Mexico” and “Latin America”. We included peer-reviewed articles that examined stigma, attitudes, or knowledge about obesity in the general population (GenPop) or healthcare professionals (HCP). No language restrictions were applied. The search revealed extensive evidence from North America and Europe documenting pervasive weight stigma and its negative impact on healthcare experiences, patient outcomes, and social participation. However, we found very limited research in Mexico and Latin America. Research from other regions has identified an increase in weight stigma over time alongside the well-established increase in prevalence of obesity.

Added value of this study

This study extends existing knowledge by providing the first large-scale evidence on weight stigma in Mexico, a region where research has been scarce despite the high prevalence of obesity. By stratifying both the GenPop and HCP according to obesity status, it demonstrates how lived experience influences attitudes, internalization of bias, and beliefs about controllability, despite the health education. The findings reveal that obesity is predominantly viewed as within individual control, that can be managed solely by a commitment to a healthy lifestyle, which negates the evidenced complexity and multifactorial nature of obesity. Importantly, stigma persists even among those who define obesity as a disease, underscoring a disconnect between scientific knowledge and everyday perceptions. This contribution extends global literature by showing that weight stigma is not limited to high-income countries but is deeply embedded in Latin American contexts.

Implications of all the available evidence

These data provide much needed evidence about the pervasiveness of weight stigma towards people living with obesity (PLWO) in Mexico, both amongst the GenPop and HCPs. The persistence of behavior-centric views, even among those who define obesity as a disease, signals a gap in translating scientific knowledge into practice. Weight stigma reported by and recognized as an issue by all HCPs did not differ between HCPs who do and do not provide care to PLWO. Its roots vary across populations, highlighting the need for culturally sensitive education—including public education (for family, peers, and workplaces) and professional training. These efforts should focus on correcting misconceptions, identifying implicit bias early, and preparing future HCP to avoid stigma-driven inequities and discrimination in care. This study advocates for a multifaceted strategy that extends beyond education, fostering empathy and compassion by acknowledging the lived experiences of PLWO.

Introduction

The combined prevalence of overweight and obesity in Mexico increased from 62% in 2000 to 75% in 2023.1 Despite widespread weight stigma documented in North America and Europe, research on weight-related attitudes, stigma, and knowledge remains limited in Latin America. Weight stigma affects people living with obesity (PLWO) across education, workplaces, and healthcare settings.2 Healthcare professionals (HCPs) are also prone to stigmatizing attitudes, negatively impacting the healthcare experiences of PLWO, whether related or unrelated to weight concerns.3,4 Studies have demonstrated for over 40 years that HCPs hold stigmatizing views, are ambivalent about the causes of obesity, and do not refer people with obesity to services that they are eligible to attend.3, 4, 5, 6

In 2020, the Attitudes, Stigma and Knowledge (ASK) Study7 examined stigma, attitudes, and knowledge regarding obesity among the general population (GenPop) in the UK, US, Australia, and New Zealand, and HCPs from 77 countries. It highlighted significant knowledge gaps between public beliefs and scientific evidence about obesity causes, prevention, and treatment. Compared to other chronic conditions, obesity was more commonly viewed as entirely preventable or curable through lifestyle alone by both GenPop and HCPs.

It is well known that weight stigma among HCPs can negatively impact patient–provider interactions, patient outcomes, healthcare experiences, and clinical decision-making,3,8 such research in Mexico is essential. Thus, the current study replicates and extends the ASK Study7 in Mexico, a low-to-medium income country, examining whether obesity status among the GenPop and HCPs influences attitudes, stigma, and knowledge.

Methods

Design and participants

This national cross-sectional, online, anonymous questionnaire-based study examines attitudes, stigma and knowledge of obesity amongst Mexican adults from the GenPop and HCPs. A priori calculation of the sample size indicated that we might include 385 adults per group (https://www.questionpro.com/), considering the prevalence of obesity in Mexico and the number of HCP registered. Between May 10, 2022, and September 21, 2022, 4621 respondents (aged ≥18 years) completed the survey. 762 respondents were excluded for not providing consent to participate, ceased participation after providing consent, aged ≤17 years, not residing in Mexico, and errors in self-reported weight or height. Thus, the final sample was 3859 Mexican participants (1262 GenPop, 2597 HCPs); 2947 were from the metropolitan area of Mexico (Mexico City and State of Mexico), 408 from central area, 300 from northern area and 204 from southern area. In the GenPop, the most common education level was college and most were employed; see Table 1 for further breakdown. Measures

This study used the comprehensive ASK Study7 questionnaire. The lead author carried out the translation of the original survey into Spanish, and a back-translation into English, showing that the objective and tone of the questions were maintained. Once in Spanish, it was reviewed together with the rest of the authors, a group of health professionals, and a group of patients living with obesity (Obesidades Committee), to confirm that the questions were understood, depending on the population sampled (GenPop or HCPs, see Supplementary Materials). Demographic data included self-reported sex (female, male or prefer not to say), age, city of residence, occupation, specialty and primary workplace (HCPs only), weight and height were collected. Gender, race and ethnicity were not evaluated.

The survey is comprised of three validated questionnaires; 12 purposely developed questions (HCPs and GenPop) and 14 questions related to medical care and stigma (GenPop only). The three questionnaires were the Fat Phobia Short Form (F-Scale)9 assessing the extent that participants associate stereotypical characteristics, Beliefs about Obese Persons Scale (BAOP)10 measuring participants' beliefs about the perceived controllability of obesity, and the Modified Weight Bias Internalization Scale (M-WBIS)11 assessing the extent to which participants direct stereotypical statements about people living with obesity towards themselves (see Supplementary Materials). The F-Scale, BAOP and WBIS have good internal consistency in Spanish-speaking and Mexican population (Cronbach's α ·85; α ·70 and α ·94 respectively).12,13

From the twenty-six-purposively built ASK Study questions,7 ten were selected that examined beliefs, attitudes and knowledge of obesity and its treatment (e.g. definition of obesity, weight-related treatment, stigma and discrimination, perspectives on healthcare). Fourteen questions about personal experiences in medical care, with HCPs and of stigma were translated to Spanish from Puhl's et al. study.14 These questions were added to the survey aimed at the GenPop (see Supplementary Materials). For this section, we registered 3436 responses.

Procedure

The ASK Study in Mexico (ASK-M) was reviewed and approved by the Research and Ethics Committee from the Instituto Nacional de Ciencias Médicas y Nutrición Salvador Zubirán (Reference number DIA-3823-21-23-1). The GenPop were recruited using qBus (QualtricsTM a participant pooling tool) and social media reaching people all over the country, and HCPs were recruited using email invitations via Mexican professional medical societies and institutions, hospitals and universities. All participants gave informed consent.

Approach to analysis

Self-reported weight (kilograms) and height (meters) was mandatory. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated and categorized according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria.15 BMI data was cleaned for nonsensical data (e.g. self-reported weight of 1 kg or height of less than 1 m). For the GenPop, the highest level of education and occupation was recorded. HCPs were asked whether they work with obesity. For the purposes of this research, four groups were created: GenPop with or without overweight or obesity (GenPop/OW-OB & GenPop/NOW-OB respectively), and HCPs with or without overweight or obesity (HCPs/OW-OB & HCPs/NOW-OB).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are reported as median with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. To examine between group differences (four groups above) Kruskal–Wallis test was used, and for categorical variables, a G-test was performed. We examined differences in attitudes, stigma and knowledge between HCPs who do and do not provide obesity care using Mann–Whitney U test. We also performed a generalized linear model (GLM) with Gamma distribution to examine HCPs and GenPop group differences for the F-Scale, M-WBIS and BAOP scores, post-hoc comparisons with Holm correction were performed where significant differences. The models included sex and age as explanatory variables. The best-supported multivariate model using the minimum Akaike information criteria (AIC) was selected for each score. Also, differences in F-Scale, M-WBIS and BAOP scores between response items for the question “Obesity is best defined as” by four groups too were analyzed with GLM. For all models, post-hoc comparisons were performed with Holm correction where significant differences in the GLM were identified. Finally, to evaluate the association between each question (7) about participants' beliefs, attitudes, and knowledge about obesity and the explanatory variables, a multinomial logistic regression model was conducted. The categorical independent variables included in the model were the answers to "Obesity is best defined as … " and the four groups of Gen-Pop or HPCs and NOW-OB or OW-OB. For the dependent variable, each question (7) about participants' beliefs, attitudes, and knowledge about obesity, one answer was established as the reference category (for OR calculation, the dummy variable was lifestyle choice-GenPop/NOW-OB and the first answer for each question about participants' beliefs, attitudes, and knowledge about obesity). See Fig. S1 for Supplementary Material. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to quantify the strength of the associations. Missing values were not interpolated. Statistical analyses were performed with the support R software (R statistical software V4·4 R Core Team 2024).

The groups with the highest weight stigma (F-Scale) scores were HCPs/NOW-OB (3·36, 1·96–4·71) and GenPop/OW-OB (3·21, 1·86–4·86), significantly higher than HCPs/OW-OB (3·21, 1·86–4·79) and GenPop/NOW-OB (3·07, 1·57–4·74) (p < 0·05).

Significantly higher weight bias internalization (M-WBIS) was reported by the GenPop/OW-OB group compared to all other groups (4·09, 1·36–6·64, p < 0·05). The lowest was reported by the HCPs/NOW-OB group (1·82, 1·0–5·5). There were no differences between the GenPop/NOW-OB and HCPs/OW-OB (2·45, 1·0–5·48 and 2·64, 1·0–5·82, respectively; p > 0·05).

The HCPs/OW-OB reported the highest BAOP score (belief that obesity is outside of an individual control) and significantly higher than all other groups (20·0, 9–32, p < 0·05). The GenPop/NOW-OB and GenPop/OW-OB groups showed similar scores (17, 8–31 and 17, 7–30, respectively; p > 0·05), significantly lower than the HCPs/NOW-OB group (19, 9–32, p < 0·05).

The multivariate model identified that weight stigma (F-Scale) is not influenced by sex or age (p = 0·16 and p = 0·21 respectively). Weight bias internalization is significantly influenced by age (M-WBIS, p < 0·001), with a negative relationship where scores decrease when age increases. Sex has a significant effect on weight bias internalization where women reported higher weight bias internalization (M-WBIS, p < 0·001), and men reported stronger beliefs that obesity is controllable (BAOP; p < 0·001). Age had a significant effect on beliefs about the controllability of obesity (BAOP, p < 0·001) in three of the four groups where older participants reported a stronger belief that obesity is outside of an individual's control, whilst younger participants in the GenPop/NOW-OB group had stronger beliefs that obesity is controllable (data not shown).

We analyzed responses to the question “Obesity is best defined as: a) a lifestyle choice, b) a disease, c) a risk factor for other diseases and sometimes a disease in itself or d) a risk factor for other diseases”, and whether this was related to weight stigma (F-Scale), weight bias internalization (M-WBIS) and beliefs about the controllability of obesity (BAOP) between the four participant groups.

GenPop experiences in medical care

At the time of the survey, 33% of GenPop/OW-OB mentioned that in the last year they had received or were receiving professional treatment for overweight or obesity, whilst 20% had no plans for weight loss within the next 6 months. The rest were seriously considering and/or taking action to lose weight, with 11% having lost weight and maintained it in the past year.

Nearly four out of five (79%) avoid going to see their doctor even when they think they should (39% never or rarely have medical check-ups), whilst 45% agreed to avoid seeing a doctor because they feel uncomfortable when HCP examines their body. During the past 12 months, 56% have needed medical attention, 40% have delayed or avoided healthcare, and 38% have not received medical care.

Less than a half (45%) of GenPop/OW-OB felt HCPs listen to them carefully, and 35% felt that HCPs never or sometimes show respect for what they have to say. 26% of GenPop/OW-OB rate the quality of healthcare they received in the last 12 months as poor or regular, the rest rate it from good to excellent. In the last 12 months, more than half (54%) feel that a doctor judged them by their weight.

Four out of five (84%) of GenPop/OW-OB have been teased, 72% have been treated unfairly and 73% have been discriminated against because of their weight at least once in their lives. The places where they have experienced this are family (23%), friends (19%), workplace (17%), with strangers (14%), school (13%), consultation with a HCP (3%) and others (2%).

Discussion

This study provides much needed evidence about the attitudes and stigma towards PLWO, and knowledge about the causes of and treatment for obesity amongst the Mexican GenPop and HCPs living or not with overweight and obesity. Given that most research on weight stigma and its impact has originated in the United States and Europe, the recognition of weight bias among Mexican HCPs represents a crucial and important step in understanding the experiences of people living with obesity in Latin America. This finding underscores the urgent need to integrate formal education about obesity into healthcare training—particularly within medical school curricula—where such content remains notably absent, including in residency programs.16

This study confirms that HCP who actively provide obesity care exhibit similar levels of weight stigma than those who do not, suggesting that frequent clinical contact alone does not mitigate bias in the absence of structured education.17, 18, 19 These findings suggest that weight stigma is deeply rooted in broader social norms and emphasize the need for targeted educational interventions. Prior studies demonstrate that such training can effectively reduce bias, particularly when delivered before stigmatizing attitudes become entrenched.2,20, 21, 22

Consistent with previous literature,23 both GenPop and HCPs without obesity had higher explicit weight stigma than those with overweight or obesity. Also, GenPop and HCPs with obesity tended to blame themselves more with higher internalized weight bias (WBIS)—especially when they believe obesity is within personal control (a lifestyle choice). HCPs/NOW-OB were the group with the lowest weight bias internalization, especially when they think obesity is a risk factor for other diseases and sometimes a disease itself. These patterns align with existing evidence suggesting that PLWO are more vulnerable to self-directed stigma.24 Moreover, weight bias internalization was inversely associated with age, indicating greater vulnerability among younger adults,25 and significantly higher among women compared to men—consistent with broader findings on sex disparities in body image pressures.9,24,26

HCP/OW-OB demonstrated significantly stronger beliefs that obesity is less controllable by individuals (especially in men and older participants, consistent with other regions27), which could moderate their biases and suggest that personal experience may deepen understanding external influences. This suggests that the lived experience of obesity can enhance awareness of the limitations individuals face in managing weight.

In relation with knowledge of obesity, less than 30% of HCPs (both with and without obesity) recognize obesity as a disease, but this does not make any difference in weight stigmatising attitudes. HCPs believe an individual's lack of motivation and self-discipline leading to sedentary lifestyle and poor food choices is what makes difficult for people to lose weight, demonstrating how deeply rooted is the believe that weight is primarily under personal control.23

Across GenPop groups (both with and without obesity) there is also a persistent belief that obesity is largely within personal control. These findings suggest an urgent need for public education campaigns aimed at increasing awareness of the broader systemic and biological factors influencing weight status2,20,22,28 and the need for highlight how dominant ideals of thinness reinforce negative perceptions of body weight. In environments where thinness is equated with success, discipline, and desirability, PLWO may be judged harshly, regardless of their health status or behaviors. Tailored interventions should address both explicit and internalized stigma—especially among HCPs and younger women—to foster more compassionate, inclusive healthcare environments.21,29

How obesity is defined matters, but the implicit weight stigma prevails. HCPs who define obesity as a disease or risk factor primarily attribute overeating to physiological dysregulation. In contrast, HCP who view obesity as a lifestyle choice tend to emphasize emotional causes of overeating as do the GenPop. Across both groups, independently if they live or not with obesity, lifestyle interventions are consistently prioritized over medical treatments for class II obesity, with weight regain often attributed to perceived personal failures (lack of self-discipline, leading to sedentary lifestyle and poor food choices). These findings reflect entrenched stigma that persists regardless of definitional approach.

This highlights that recognizing obesity as a disease is not necessarily associated with more positive attitudes towards PLWO as suggested within previous calls for obesity to be recognized as a disease. These findings echo prior international research showing that conceptualizations of obesity shape public and professional attitudes and can reinforce stigma when framed solely as a matter of personal responsibility.24

This study highlights a critical and long-overlooked issue: 84% of PLWO (GenPop/OW-OB) in Mexico report experiencing weight stigma, and 73% have faced discrimination—most often within their own families and social circles. Healthcare encounters are especially problematic, with many people avoiding medical visits due to body-related discomfort and perceived judgment from doctors. These findings reveal an alarming fact in Mexico and underscore that weight stigma is not just socially harmful—it is a modifiable risk factor of obesity with serious implications for physical and mental health. As Tomiyama suggests,30 stigma itself may contribute to disease, yet it remains largely unaddressed across personal, professional, and policy domains. It is imperative that weight stigma be treated with the same urgency and institutional commitment as other obesity-related comorbidities, since many of them could be a consequence of it.

This study offers the first large-scale analysis of weight stigma in Mexico, revealing its widespread presence and reinforcing the urgent need for structural change. Echoing prior evidence of stigma's harmful impact on health and care engagement,3,30 our findings highlight how biased attitudes, especially among HCPs, can disrupt patient relationships and contribute to therapeutic inertia in obesity treatment, as recognized across all participant groups.

Given the priority given by HCPs to lifestyle interventions for patients with BMI ≥35 kg/m2, a lack of knowledge or the attribution of obesity to personal responsibility could be associated with a lower likelihood of initiating pharmacotherapy or delaying referrals for metabolic–bariatric surgery, despite patients meeting clinical eligibility based on Mexican guidelines.31 These findings support the need to reduce weight bias among HCPs, not only as an ethical imperative, but as a tangible pathway to improve timely access to treatment.18,30,32

The current study also explored differences in attitudes, stigma and knowledge amounts the Gen Pop and HCPs based on demographic variables known to be associated with weight stigma. These findings based on a novel sample of the Gen Pop and HCPs in Mexico is consistent with previous research.27,33,34 Weight bias internalization did decline as age increased, suggesting that younger people are more likely to internalize and thus, self-direct weight stigma attitudes towards themselves. Likewise, weight bias internalization was also higher for women compared to men, whilst men had stronger beliefs that obesity is controllable compared to women, and older age was associated with a stronger belief that obesity is controllable. There is no difference in weight stigma (F-Scale) based on sex or age, and thus the high level of weight stigma observed was consistent between men and women, and across the age range. Previous research has reported greater weight stigma in men compared to women and in younger age compared to older age.27,33,34

Our findings underscore the pressing need to reform HCPs education by incorporating up-to-date evidence on obesity, promoting empathetic care, and actively challenging stigmatizing beliefs. Given HCPs crucial role in shaping population health, public health strategies should include awareness campaigns that reframe obesity beyond personal blame, encouraging a more inclusive, systemic narrative. Recognizing weight stigma as a public health issue offers Mexico a pathway to improving care quality, health outcomes, and the overall wellbeing of PLWO.

This study is not without its limitations. Its cross-sectional design captures attitudes at a single time point. First, given that obesity is a public health priority and the continuous and consistent exposure to information about obesity including those that are stigmatizing, there is a need to use a longitudinal design to assess change in weight stigma attitudes and behaviors. Second, although internet access in Mexico is relatively high, individuals experiencing digital exclusion may have been underrepresented. Third, to calculate BMI, self-report data was used and thus, as a result of social desirability, participants may not have reported their height or weight accurately. Fourth, we did not collect data about participants ethnicity, gender or income level. Given that evidence has highlighted the intersectionality of weight stigma with, for instance, stigma related to poverty, sexuality and ethnicity,33 gathering this information may have provided further insights about potential group differences. Finally, the sample disproportionately included participants from Mexico City, which may constrain the generalizability of findings to rural or underserved communities with distinct healthcare access and cultural perspectives on obesity.

Conclusion

In summary, this study has provided the first comprehensive examination of weight-related attitudes, stigma and knowledge about obesity in Mexico in the GenPop and HCPs, as well as those living with and without obesity. The findings provide evidence of profound weight stigma and misconceptions about the management of obesity which holds significant implications from policy to practice. The findings from this study highlight the need for greater HCP education and, given the pervasiveness of weight stigma amongst the GenPop, a need for improved public awareness. However, we argue that a multipronged approach is required that goes beyond education, encouraging greater compassion and empathy towards PLWO through an understanding of the real-life experiences both of living with obesity and of experiencing stigma and discrimination in everyday life. Both HCPs and GenPop living with obesity have high weight bias internalization, which is known to be associated with mental and physical health outcomes, as well as maladaptive behavioral responses. Our findings therefore call for interventions to reduce weight bias internalization.
