Commiphora wightii and its formulations extenuate macrophage-mediated inflammatory pathology in osteoarthritis
Authors: Vanshika Srivastava, Ritik Raj, Ankit Ganeshpurkar, Priya Kulkarni, Abhay Harsulkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOsteoarthritis (OA) treatment often focuses on symptom management rather than addressing underlying inflammation and cartilage degeneration. In search of safer, long-term options, many patients turn to Ayurvedic remedies like Guggul (Commiphora wightii) and its formulations—Amritadi Guggul (AG) and Rasnadi Guggul (RG)—though biochemical validation remains limited.ObjectiveThis study evaluates the immunomodulatory effects of standard guggulsterone extract (SGE), AG, and RG on synovial inflammation, mitochondrial stress, and complement activation, using the U937 monocyte cell line.Materials and MethodsCells were stimulated with Phorbol 12-myristate 13 acetate (PMA) and treated with varying concentrations of the extracts. Anti-inflammatory effects were measured via mRNA expression of iNOS, MMP-1, MMP-13, and VEGF-1. Macrophage polarization markers (CD68, CD86, CD163), mitochondrial membrane potential (JC-1 assay), collagenase activity (gelatinase spot assay), and molecular docking with complement factor B (CFB) were also assessed.ResultsResults showed that SGE, AG, and RG significantly reduced nitric oxide and pro-inflammatory gene expression. Treatments suppressed M1 macrophage markers without promoting M2 differentiation. 5,5′,6,6′-tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′-tetraethylbenzimi- dazolylcarbocyanine iodide (JC) - 1 assays indicated improved mitochondrial stability, while all formulations inhibited collagenase activity. Docking studies revealed strong interactions between guggulsterone and CFB, suggesting complement inhibition.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the potential of Guggul and its formulations to modulate macrophage activity, reduce inflammation, and support joint preservation in OA. Though limited by the use of a monocyte-derived cell line, the study lays the groundwork for future validation in primary cells and in vivo models.Keywords: Guggul formulations, Guggulsterone, Immunomodulation, Mitochondrial membrane potential, Molecular dynamics, Osteoarthritis

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most prevalent rheumatological disorder, marked by chronic low-grade synovitis, progressive degeneration of cartilage, alterations in subchondral bone and synovial fluid composition [[1], [2], [3]]. Despite significant advances in understanding a complex interplay of mechanical damage, inflammation, and oxidative stress in OA, effective disease-modifying treatments are lacking. Current treatment that primarily focuses on providing symptomatic relief is often coupled with long-term safety concerns. On the other hand, non-pharmacological approaches primarily focus on patient education, exercise, and weight management​. In this context, there has been a surge in the use of Complementary and Alternative Medicine (CAM), with the reported usage among OA patients ranging from 68% to 91%. This is mainly attributed to the limited efficacy of conventional treatments [4,5]. Ayurveda, the traditional Indian medical system, offers promising alternatives, particularly through the use of guggul, an oleo-gum resin derived from Commiphora wightii. Guggul has been traditionally used in the formulations aimed at treating arthritis, gout, rheumatism, obesity, and lipid metabolism disorders [6].

A term ‘guggul kalpana’ in Ayurveda refers to various pharmaceutical recipes in which guggul is used as the primary ingredient. These preparations are known for their multimodal therapeutic benefits, including anti-inflammatory, hypolipidemic, analgesic, antioxidant, and detoxifying properties [[7], [8], [9]]. Potential of guggul in modulating inflammatory processes, particularly through its principal active component, guggulsterone, has been well documented [10,11]. Guggulsterone has been shown to modulate transcription factors like nuclear factor kappa beta (NF-κβ) and Signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) - 3, respectively delivering its anti-inflammatory and anti-angiogenic effects. It causes a downregulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP) −9, cyclooxygenase (COX) −2, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) −1, which can lead to halt tumor metastasis and ECM degradation [12]. However, most of these activities of guggul are validated in cancer and are extrapolated to be beneficial in other chronic inflammatory diseases like OA [11].

Synovial macrophages are pivotal in initiating and maintaining an inflammatory microenvironment through a release of a plethora of pro-inflammatory factors in OA joints [1]. Sequentially, these cells also actively contribute to the structural progression of OA including cartilage degradation and formation of osteophytes through excessive MMPs and alarmin production, respectively [13,14]. Acknowledging the role of these cells in OA, depletion of these cells or their modulation in pro-inflammatory phenotype has emerged as a promising approach to deal with the inflammation driven cascade of events in OA pathology. Therefore, this study aims to elucidate the action of guggul and its formulations on OA-specific mechanisms with special reference to their action on synovial macrophages. We here investigated the potential of guggul and its formulations to modulate pro-inflammatory phenotype of the synovial macrophages. Positive outcomes of this approach should not only define their macrophage-specific action but also sufficiently establish the foundation of their application in OA as main-stream drug. Additionally, this study elucidated the activity of guggul on the complement system by using molecular dynamics to explain a novel dimension of its potential as a therapeutic agent in OA, thereby bridging traditional Ayurvedic knowledge with modern scientific approaches, this study looks forward to providing robust evidence for the application of guggul in the management of OA, potentially expanding a scope of treatment options for this debilitating condition​.

Materials and methods

Cell line

Human-monocyte-like cell line (U937) was procured from the National Centre for Cell Science (NCCS), Pune, India, and was maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mmol/L l-glutamine, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 95% relative humidity, 5% CO2, and 37 °C.

Preparation of standardized guggul extract (SGE) and its formulations - amritadi guggul (AG), and rasnadi guggul (RG)

Standardized guggul extract powder containing 2.5% of guggulsterone (analyzed using HPTLC method) was obtained from Rishiratna Remedies Pvt. Ltd, Pune, India. Methanolic extract of SGE was prepared by dissolving 10 mg dry powder in 1 ml of methanol, which was further diluted to form 1 mg/ml working stock solution in culture media and used for the cell treatment. AG and RG are commercially available formulations and their methanolic extracts were prepared by dissolving 100 mg dry powder of both formulations separately in each 10 ml of methanol. Both the extracts were vortexed and filtered through Whatman's filter paper No. 1 and were further diluted to form a working solution of 1 mg/ml and used for further treatment during cell experiments.

U937 cell challenge test

Similar to the SF challenge test as previously described by the authors [15], U937 cell challenge test was performed using PMA (100 ng/ml) for the cell activation; this is in principle to mimic inflammation in OA. Successful cell induction was tested by estimation of NO release and gene expressions of pro-inflammatory markers like MMP-1, MMP-13, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and VEGF-1. Upon successful cell activation, U937 cells were treated with SGE and its formulations, and modulation in these markers was noted. A detailed methodology of the U937 challenge test is described as follows –

Determination of nitric oxide (NO) release in the SGE, AG, and RG treated U937 cells

NO estimation was performed using the culture media of treated U937 cells by the Griess reaction method as described previously [16]. In brief, U937 cells were seeded at the density of 1 × 106 cells/ml in a 24-well plate and induced with PMA (concentration – 100 ng/ml) for 24 h. The induced cells were further treated with the selected SGE (5 ng/ml, 10 ng/ml, 25 ng/ml, 50 ng/ml and 100 ng/ml), AG (50 ng/ml, 100 ng/ml, 500 ng/ml, 2.5 μg/ml, 5 μg/ml and 10 μg/ml), and RG (25 ng/ml, 50 ng/ml, 100 ng/ml, 500 ng/ml, 2.5 μg/ml and 5 μg/ml) concentrations determined by the cell viability assay. After 24 h of the treatment, the culture media was collected and further diluted 1:1 with the solution containing 1% sulfanilic acid (SA) and 0.1% N, N-naphthylethylenediamine- dihydrochloride (NEDD), prepared in 5% phosphoric acid. The mixture was further incubated for 10 min at room temperature. The absorbance was measured at 540 nm and calculations were performed using the standard curve prepared for serial dilutions of sodium nitrate, used as a standard.

Modulation of MMP-1, MMP-13, iNOS and VEGF-1 gene expression in treated U937 cells

For this experiment, SGE, AG, and RG treated cells (as described in the NO estimation) were further used for RNA isolation by PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen Co., Carlsbad CA, USA) and following the manufacturer's instructions. Further, cDNA synthesis was performed using the SuperScript-III Cell to cDNA kit (Invitrogen Co., Carlsbad CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. qRT-PCR analysis was performed with Applied Biosystems StepOne Real Time PCR System using the TaqMan gene expression assays (MMP-1 (Hs00900055_m1, Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), MMP-13 (Hs00942584_m1), VEGF-1 (Hs00900055_m1) and iNOS (Hs01075529_m1)) and TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). mRNA amounts of all the assays were normalized against the housekeeping gene, β-actin (ACTB) (Assay ID - Hs01060665_m1). The data analysis was carried out using Step One Software version 2.2.2. The concentrations of the formulations used during this set of experiments were – SGE (10 ng/ml, 50 ng/ml, and 100 ng/ml), AG and RG (50 ng/ml, 100 ng/ml and 500 ng/ml). PMA-induced cells were used as positive control; the experiments were performed in triplicates and repeated three times.

Collagenase inhibitory potential of guggul and its formulations

The collagenase inhibitory action of SGE, AG, and RG was evaluated using gelatinase spot assay as described [17]. This qualitative assay is based on the principle that X-ray film is coated with gelatine, which is a denatured collagen that serves as a substrate for the collagenase enzyme. Briefly, in this assay, collagenase type II, a serine protease, (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 0.125 % w/v in assay buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 6.8) was used for the estimation of anti-collagenase activity of SGE, AG and RG (concentrations SGE – 10 ng/ml, 25 ng/ml, 50 ng/ml, 100 ng/ml and 50 ng/ml; AG and RG – 50 ng/ml, 100 ng/ml, 500 ng/ml, 2.5 μg/ml and 5 μg/ml). The assay was performed after pre-incubation (5 min) of 0.125 % collagenase type II, with different concentrations of SGE, AG, and RG or assay buffer (for control) in equal volumes. Following the incubation, 3 μl of each reaction mixture was pipetted on prewashed X-ray films for 10 min. Further, the X-ray films were washed under tap water to visualize enzyme activity as indicated by spots of gelatine clearance on the films (Fig. 3).

– Collagenase inhibitory activity of SGE, AG and RG

A range of concentrations of the selected formulations was incubated with 0.125% collagenase for 10 min. Clearance of gelatine (a denatured form of collagen) coating of X-ray film indicated the collagenase-like enzyme activity for (A) SGE, (B) AG and (C) RG (AG – Amritadi guggul, RG – Rasnadi guggul, SGE – standardized guggul extract).

In vitro cell differentiation assay and functional analysis

The assay was performed on U937 cells and was based on the knowledge that PMA when used in a higher dose induces a polarization of monocytes into proinflammatory phenotype [18]. Therefore, the potential of SGE and guggul formulations to arrest the polarization is a direct confirmation of their anti-inflammatory action. In the assay, U937 cells were seeded with a density of 5 x 105 cells/ml in a 24-well plate and were induced with 100 ng/ml of PMA for 24 h. After the induction, the formulation treatment was performed using the concentrations, SGE - 10 ng/ml, 50 ng/ml, and 100 ng/ml; AG - 50 ng/ml, 100 ng/ml, and 500 ng/ml; RG - 50 ng/ml, 100 ng/ml, and 500 ng/ml. Following 24 h of the treatment, total RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis from the cells was done using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen Co., Carlsbad, CA, USA) and later with SuperScript-III Cell to cDNA kit (Invitrogen Co., Carlsbad CA, USA) as per the manufacturer's instructions. This was followed by qRT-PCR analysis (Applied Biosystems StepOne Real-Time PCR System) using TaqMan gene expression assays (CD68 (Hs02836816_g1), CD86 (Hs01567026_m1), CD163 (Hs00174705_m1) and ACTB (Hs01060665_m1)) and TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). mRNA expression amount of the selected cytokines was normalized against the amount of ACTB, using Step One Software version 2.2.2.

The functional analysis of newly differentiated cells was conducted using Human Procartaplex Mix & Match 7-plex (ThermoFisher Scientific, Vienna, Austria; Catalogue No. PPX-07-MXAADCX) for estimation of pro-inflammatory cytokine release by the newly differentiated cells in the culture media. As no significant modulation was found in the CD163 (a marker for the M2 phenotype) during in vitro cell differentiation assay, the functional analysis was restricted to estimation of pro-inflammatory cytokines representing the M1 phenotype. Median fluorescence intensity (MFI) for each selected cytokine was measured using Attune™ NxT Flow Cytometer 193 (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US Catalogue No. A24863), and the graphs were plotted using the standard curve.

Action of guggul and its formulations on mitochondrial stress

Cellular inflammation and related oxidative stress are directly linked to mitochondrial membrane potential; therefore, a positive modulation of mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) by guggul and its formulations, can be considered as an indicator of the anti-inflammatory as well as antioxidant activity of these compound. This experiment was performed on U937 cells using a mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit (with JC-1) (Elabscience, Texas, USA; Catalogue No. – E-CK-A301) as per the manufacturer's instructions. JC-1 (5,5′,6,6′-tetrachloro-1,1′,3,3′-tetraethylbenzimi- dazolylcarbocyanine iodide) is a fluorescent probe that gives a red fluorescence when it gets aggregated in a mitochondrial matrix and forms a polymer. In contrast, it fails to polymerize in case of compromised membrane potential and produce green fluorescence. Thus, a relative ratio of red and green fluorescence acts as an indicator of impaired mitochondrial membrane potential that may further lead to apoptosis. In the assay, U937 cells were seeded with 1 x 106/ml density in a 24-well plate and were induced with SGE, AG, and RG (SGE concentrations: - 10 ng/ml and 50 ng/ml; AG concentrations – 50 ng/ml, 100 ng/ml, and 500 ng/ml; RG concentrations – 50 ng/ml, 100 ng/ml, and 500 ng/ml). The cells induced with TNF-α (concentration: 200 ng/ml) were used as a positive control after determining a safe concentration of TNF-α using cell viability assay as described [19]. After 48 h of the induction, the cells were harvested by non-enzymatic digestion method using 0.5 mM ethylene diamine tetra acetate (EDTA) in phosphate buffer saline (PBS) for 15 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Later, 20 μL of JC-1 (500x) was diluted with 9 ml of ultrapure water to form JC-1 working solution. The working solution was then vortexed for enhanced mixing and then serially diluted to form a 1x JC-1 assay buffer. The positive control was prepared by incubating untreated control cells in 10 μM carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) for 20 min. The treated U937 cells were incubated with 500 μl of JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 20 min. After the incubation, the cells were washed using JC-1 assay buffer (1x) and analyzed by Attune Nxt Acoustic flow cytometer (Thermofisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For each experimental reaction, 10,000 events were acquired. The experiment was performed in triplicates and repeated for 3 times. Flow cytometry data analysis was performed using Attune Nxt software (version 2.1), and the results are expressed as the percentage of stressed cells.

Computational molecular modelling: CFB and guggulsterone

The computational tasks were performed on a 12th Gen Intel(R) Core (TM) i5-1240 P @ 1.70 GHz processor, RAM 16.00 GB system with Intel® Iris® Xe Graphics GPU running on a 64-bit Windows 11 operating system.

Homology modelling and model validation

The protein model for human CFB was obtained from the PDB (https://www.rcsb.org) with PDB id: 6T8V complexed with 4-[(2∼[1])-1-[(5,7-dimethyl-1∼[1]-indol-4-yl) methyl] piperidin-2-yl] benzoic acid (MVK) [20]. After a careful examination in Chimera (version 1.13.1rc), to identify the missing sequences and corresponding structural elements [21], the elements were appended using homology modelling by SWISS-MODEL accessible through ExPASy web server by user template mode [22]. 6T8V protein was applied as a template with the target sequence (P00751) obtained from UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org). For quality check, the homology model PROCHECK (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/PROCHECK) [23], GMQE, QMEAN and QMEANDisco [24] and further visualised by Chimera software.

Protein and ligand preparation

The protonation states of amino acid residues of the developed homology model were achieved using PDB2PQR server (https://server.poissonboltzmann.org/pdb2pqr) at pH 7.4 using Amber force field [25]. Optimisation of the hydrogen bonding network of the protein and charges to the residues were also achieved in accordance with the forcefield employed as described [26]. Amberff14SB force field was used to generate topology of protein and AM1-BCC charge scheme was employed in the Antechamber tool to assign atomic charges and force field parameters for ligands. Further, the prepared protein was converted into pdbqt format to utilize it as an input file by AutoDock 4.2. The final coordinate and topology of the complex were built by using tleap. The complex was hydrated with TIP3PBOX water using a box size of 89 × 91 × 103 under periodic boundary conditions (PBC) with 8 Å non-bonded cut-off and particle mesh eswald (PME) for long-range electrostatics. The selected ligand was subjected to energy minimisation in order to rectify the geometry, remove the steric clashes and overlapping atoms of the compounds using Open Babel software (version 2.4.0). The ligand was minimised at physiological pH using general amber force field (GAFF) with 2500 as maximum steps [27].

Grid generation and validation

The residues involved in non-covalent interactions with MVK were identified using PLIP in the selected PDB file. The identified residues with distance less than 5 Å were taken as reference points to build a grid box around the active sites. The Autogrid 4.0 was used to calculate grid maps of interaction energies with various atom types present in the ligands (A, C, HD, N, and OA) with grid size set to 78 × 60 × 66 and grid point spacing 0.375 Å as depicted in Table S1. Further grid validation was done by redocking with MVK. The redocked pose was evaluated by determining root mean square deviation (RMSD) corresponding to the heavy atoms with the co-crystallised structure of MVK. The accuracy of the docking protocol was demonstrated by the ability to efficiently reproduce the pose similar to the co-crystallised ligand conformation with least deviation.

Molecular docking

AutoDock 4.2.6 was used for molecular docking followed by precision docking. The Lamarckian genetic algorithm (LGA) was employed for conformational search and the parameters used for virtual screening and precision dockings are summarised in Table S2. A docking score less than −4.52 often suggests moderate binding affinity, while A score of −7.00 or lower usually indicates strong binding affinity [28]. The cluster size of 50 and above with a RMSD tolerance limit of 2 Å was used to ascertain the binding site of each ligand. Post-docking analysis and visualisation were performed by AutoDock tools 1.5.6 and Discovery Studio visualiser 2024.

Molecular dynamics (MD)

MD simulations were followed as per Ganeshpurkar et al., 2020 to evaluate chemistry at the molecular level [29]. MD was performed for CFB and CFB-guggulsterone complexes through Amber18. The protein-ligand complexes were immersed in a TIP3P hydrated cubic box with a 12 Å cutoff distance, and residual charges were neutralized using counter ions placed in the system via Monte Carlo method. The PME was used to handle long-range electrostatic interactions. The final production phase of 100 ns was carried out at 310.15 K as an isothermal-isobaric (NPT) ensemble. The graphical representation of the 3D models was developed using visual molecular dynamics (VMD). RMSD was used to analyze the flexibility of the receptor-ligand complex by measuring average displacement changes in an atom's position for a particular frame against a reference frame [30]. Smaller the deviation, better is the stability of the protein. Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis was also performed to indicate the fluctuation of the complex at the residue level [31]. Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) was also calculated by using a probe of van der Waals radii of 1.4 Å.

Binding free energy calculations

The molecular mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MM-PBSA) method provides a simple quantification of receptor–ligand binding free energy [32]. Analyzing binding free energy confirms intermolecular interaction strength in receptor–ligand complexes via MD simulations. The final 50 frames were considered for calculations with a salt concentration of 0.1 mM. The GB model developed by Onufriev et al., 2000 was used for Molecular mechanics with generalised Born and surface area solvation (MM-GBSA) calculations [33]. Furthermore, the total non-polar solvation free energy in MM-PBSA was modelled as linearly proportional to the SASA in a single term. The MM-GBSA and MM-PBSA were performed using python script (MMPBSA.py of Amber18).

The binding free energy was calculated using the final 50 ns stable RMSD trajectory, and the different types of energies that contributed to the binding, including van der Waals, electrostatic, polar solvation, and SASA energy, were calculated. We performed the binding free energy calculations for the CFB–MVK, and CFB–guggulsterone complexes. The free energy calculated in the gas phase corresponds to ΔGgas/ΔGMM, through the force field parameters. It consists of bonded and non-bonded energies of molecular mechanics.

The free energy change associated with solvation, when a molecule enters from gas to solvent phase, is represented by ΔGsol, which is contributed by polar and non-polar solvation terms. Further, ΔGMM-GBSA is calculated by the summation of the contribution of ΔGgas and ΔGsol. The calculation of ΔGbinding involved the contribution of the entropy term but was ignored for these calculations.

Results

Anti-inflammatory potential of SGE, AG and RG

A highly significant linear decline was observed in NO levels with the increasing concentrations of SGE, from 10 ng/ml to 100 ng/ml (p < 0.001); a dose of 5 ng/ml of SGE did not show any significant reduction in the NO levels as compared to the PMA induced cells (Fig. 1A). All the selected AG and RG concentrations significantly reduced NO levels in U937 cells as compared to PMA-induced cells (p < 0.001) after 24 h of treatment (Fig. 1B and C).

U937 cells were induced with PMA (100 ng/ml) for 24 h, used as a positive control, and further treated with the selected concentrations of (A) SGE, (B) AG and (C) RG; inflammatory response was estimated in terms of NO released in the cell culture media; the cell challenge test was performed in triplicates and repeated for three times. A comparison among the different concentrations of SGE, AG and RG with their statistical significance is indicated by the lines; for statistical significance – ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01 and ∗∗∗p < 0.001. (AG – Amritadi guggul, NO – Nitric oxide, PMA – Phorbol 12 – myristate 13 – acetate, SGE – Standardized guggul Extract, RG – Rasnadi guggul).

Estimation of pro-inflammatory markers in the U937 cells treated with SGE, AG and RG

SGE

MMP-1, MMP-13, VEGF-1 and iNOS showed a decline for all the selected concentrations, however, this decline was non-significant on statistical scale. 10 ng/ml SGE was responsible for causing the highest decline in the selected marker, making it the most effective dose among the chosen concentrations (Fig. 2 I).

– U937 cell challenge test: modulation of proinflammatory markers like MMP-1, MMP-13, VEGF-1 and iNOS after SGE, AG and RG treatment

SGE, AG and RG treated U937 cells were used for RNA isolation, followed by cDNA preparation to detect a fold change in the mRNA abundance of (A) MMP-1 (B) MMP-13 (C) VEGF-1 (D) iNOS; the experiment was performed in triplicates and repeated for three times. Inter-dose comparison for SGE, AG and RG with statistical significance was determined using analysis of variance (ANOVA) accompanied by Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, ACTB was used as the housekeeping gene. For statistical significance: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001 (ACTB – β-actin, AG – Amritadi guggul, ANOVA – Analysis of variance, iNOS – inducible nitric oxide synthase, MMP-1 – Matrix metalloproteinases-1, MMP-13 – Matrix metalloproteinases-13, RG – Rasnadi guggul, SGE – Standardized guggul extract, VEGF-1 – Vascular endothelial growth factor-1).

AG

While all the selected concentrations brought a reduction in MMP-1, 50 ng/ml of AG was the most effective causing a significant reduction in the MMP-1 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2 II A). In the case of MMP-13, 50 ng/ml (p < 0.01) and 500 ng/ml (p < 0.001) of AG was responsible for achieving a significant reduction in the marker (Fig. 2 II B). All the chosen concentrations were effective bringing a highly significant decline in VEGF-1 (Fig. 2 II C). 500 ng/ml of AG was the most effective against iNOS, causing a significant reduction (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2 II D).

RG

RG was most effective in causing downregulation of VEGF-1 with its concentrations – 50 ng/ml and 100 ng/ml (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2 III C). No chosen concentrations of RG were effective against MMP-1 and MMP-13 (Fig. 2 III A and 2 III B). 500 ng/ml of RG caused a significant downregulation of iNOS (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2 III D).

Gelatinase spot assay for SGE, AG and RG

Clear spots were observed where 0.125% collagenase type II solution was placed on the piece of X-ray films indicating gelatin digestion. In contrast, faded spots appeared on films when collagenase was mixed with higher SGE concentrations (50 ng/ml, 100 ng/ml, and 500 ng/ml), suggesting inhibition of collagenase activity by SGE (Fig. 3A). Similarly, AG and RG at 2.5 μg/ml and 5 μg/ml, and 500 ng/ml, 2.5 μg/ml and 5 μg/ml concentrations, respectively produced faded spots because of collagenase inhibition (Fig. 3B and C).

In vitro cell differentiation assay

SGE

24 h of SGE treatment caused a significant dose dependent decline in CD68 for all the selected concentrations (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4 I A). Similarly, 10 ng/ml and 50 ng/ml of SGE treatment showed a significant decline in CD86 levels, while 100 ng/ml of SGE treatment showed a minor elevation in the marker expression as compared to 50 ng/ml treatment (Fig. 4 I B). CD163 showed a declining trend after SGE treatment (Fig. 4 I C).

Statistical significance was determined ANOVA accompanied by Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, in all these experiments, ACTB was used as a housekeeping gene; for statistical significance – ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01 and ∗∗∗p < 0.001 (ACTB – β-actin, AG – Amritadi guggul, ANOVA – Analysis of variance, CD – Cluster of differentiation, RG – Rasnadi guggul, SGE – Standardized guggul extract).

AG

After 24 h of AG treatment, CD68 showed a dose-wise decline in CD68 gene expression that remained significant when compared to PMA (p < 0.001) (Fig. 4 II A). CD86 and CD163 also followed a similar trend of CD68 expression. For CD86, all the three concentrations showed a significant decline with respect to PMA (PMA vs 50 ng/ml – p < 0.01; PMA vs 100 ng/ml – p < 0.01; PMA vs 500 ng/ml – p < 0.001) (Fig. 4 II B). A non-significant increase in the expression of CD163 was observed for 500 ng/ml when compared to 100 ng/ml (Fig. 4 II C).

RG

All concentrations of RG showed a similar trend to that of AG, for CD68, CD86 and CD163 expression. After 24 h treatment, CD68 showed a decline in gene expression that remains non-significant except for 500 ng/ml (p < 0.05). A defined rise was observed in 100 ng/ml that further decreased in 500 ng/ml dose of RG (Fig. 4 III A). CD86 showed a gradual decline in gene expression levels with increasing concentrations (PMA vs 50 ng/ml – p < 0.05; PMA vs 100 ng/ml – p < 0.05; PMA vs 500 ng/ml – p < 0.001) (Fig. 4 III B). For CD163, lowest gene expression was observed in 100 ng/ml (p < 0.001), while 500 ng/ml showed marginally significant decrease when compared to control (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4 III C).

Cytokine estimation in the U937 cells treated with the SGE, AG and RG

A significantly effective reduction in interleukin (IL) - 1β, MMP-8 and MMP-13 levels were observed for all the chosen concentrations of SGE, AG and RG (Fig. 5). Higher concentrations of SGE treatment could achieve a downregulation of MMP-8 and MMP-13 matching to the levels in the control cells or even below (Fig. 5B and C). All the selected concentrations of the AG showed a dose dependent reduction in IL-1β, MMP-8 and MMP-13, reflecting 500 ng/ml as the most effective dose against these markers (Fig. 5 II A, B, C). RG followed the trend of AG revealing a dose-dependent significant decline in the selected pro-inflammatory markers (Fig. 5 III A, B, C).

Homology modelling and model validation

Residues 10 and 11 were found to be absent in 6T8V protein which was rectified by developing a homology model using SWISS-MODEL (Fig. S2A). Stability of the modelled protein was checked by obtaining the Ramachandran plot from the PROCHECK server (Fig. S2B). Almost 99.65% of residues reside in the favourable region, and MolProbity score (1.21) also indicated the acceptable quality of the model. GMQE score (0.88) and QMEAN score (0.49) indicated the similarity between model-template and favourable geometrical aspects of protein structure, respectively (Fig. S4). The standard deviation in Z-score between 0 and 1 indicates the resemblance to experimentally obtained protein structures in the real world (Fig. S3C). Further, QMEANDisco indicated that all active sites and tunnel residues had good local quality scores (Fig. S4). All these validation parameters indicated a confidence in the developed human CFB model.

Grid generation and validation

PLIP server identified hydrophobic interactions of MVK with Ala 190, Pro 191, Arg 223, Ile 244 and Trp 246 and hydrogen bonding with Asp 250, Thr 221 and Asn 254 (Fig. S5A) (Tables S3 and S4). The average RMSD deviation in both cases was very low and within the 2 Å limit. Thus, the generated grid can generate reproducible and reliable results, which could be considered for virtual screening and precision docking.

Discussion

This study highlights the immunomodulatory potential of Guggul and its formulations, AG and RG, primarily in targeting synovitis. The cell challenge test on U937 cells representing synovial macrophages showed a significant decline in key pro-inflammatory mediators of OA pathology including NO, MMP-1, MMP-13, VEGF-1 and iNOS after SGE, AG, and RG treatment for the selected concentrations (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The impact of unregulated NO and MMPs on OA pathology has been elaborately discussed in our previous publications [1,15,16]. NO is produced by cells under stress and functions as an inflammatory mediator and an indicator of oxidative stress, further triggering immune responses and promoting excessive MMP production. A marked decrease in NO levels in treated U937 cells highlights the potential of SGE, AG, and RG to alleviate cellular stress, thereby suppressing MMP production. While MMP-1 and MMP-13 expressions primarily reflect collagen-II status, they also serve as indirect markers of synovitis [34]. Hence, a positive modulation of MMP-1 and 13 in the treated cells is a confirmation of anti-inflammatory action of SGE, AG, and RG. Additionally, gelatinase spot assay showed their inhibitory potential against serine proteases. The Xray films come coated with gelatine, which is a degenerated form of collagen; taking advantage of this, gelatinase spot assay was developed by the authors, that serves as a validated, reproducible, reliable, and sensitive method for measuring collagenase type II inhibition [35]. Importantly, collagenase inhibitory activity of these formulations will have a modulatory effect on the joint cavity microenvironment in terms of halting the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that serve as a stimulant for immune responses [36,37].

In vitro cell-differentiation assay in the present work, was a methodological extension of the cell challenge test described previously by our group and represented a testimony of the potential of the guggul formulations to arrest macrophage modulation into pro-inflammatory phenotype. Although, anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory properties are well-documented for guggul, the current study uniquely focuses on its role in modulating macrophages polarization. By identifying specific factors influencing M1/M2 dynamics, this work provides mechanistic insights that extend beyond general anti-inflammatory effects and deepen our understanding of how its immunomodulatory potential is exerted. Current understanding of OA as a chronic low-grade inflammatory disease proposes synovial macrophages to play a pivotal role in the production of pro-inflammatory mediators and MMPs that maintain a vicious circle of inflammation and cartilage degradation in the disease pathology. Also, TNF-α, IL-6, chemokine (C–C motif) ligand (CCL) −2 and nerve growth factor (NGF) produced by these cells revealed a connection with OA pain [[38], [39], [40], [41], [42]]. Broadly, M1 and M2 phenotypes of the macrophages respectively represent pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory phenotype and functional status of the cells. Although recent studies pointed out the engagement of M2 macrophages too in chronic inflammatory pathology, OA pathology is defined by the dominance of M1 phenotype and increased M1/M2 ratio [43,44]. Knowing this, depletion of synovial macrophages is acknowledged as a promising approach to alleviate OA inflammation and its consequences and has shown positive outcome in pre-clinical models [[45], [46], [47], [48]]. The in vitro cell differentiation assay was a replication of the same approach wherein the authors tested the efficacy of SGE, AG and RG to limit macrophage polarization in the treated cells. Outcomes of this assay were fascinating; where a significant downregulation of CD68 was noted in the SGE and AG treated U937 cells for most of the selected concentrations (Fig. 4 A). None of these drugs were effective in promoting CD163+ phenotype modulation of the treated U937 cells (Fig. 4 C). Functional analysis of the treated cells showed a significant reduction in the pro-inflammatory markers like IL-1β, MMP-8 and MMP-13 (Fig. 5) that can be interpreted as an effective arrest of pro-inflammatory polarization of macrophages, matching with the trends of phenotype modulation. It may be noted here that although U937 cells tend to exhibit a baseline M2-like bias in unpolarized conditions, we employed validated induction methods with appropriate controls, thereby mitigating baseline bias in these cell line.

Reduced mitochondrial membrane potential in synovial macrophages is associated with multiple pathological events in OA joints. For instance, damaged mitochondria release high levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), further promoting inflammation in cartilage and synovial lining cells, which aggravates the joint inflammation. Release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (like IL-1β and IL-6) were suggestive of their inflammatory modulation [49]. Increased inflammatory state of OA joint further facilitates the pathological progression and cartilage degradation. Monocytes with dysfunctional mitochondria tend to show the Warburg effect, a metabolic shift from oxidative phosphorylation towards glycolysis even in the presence of adequate oxygen [50]. The Warburg effect supports a pro-inflammatory phenotype of macrophages, and is involved in synovial hypertrophy and osteophytic growth, extending chronic inflammation and cellular damage in the joint space. Nonetheless, damaged mitochondria release mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the synovial fluid, where it acts as DAMPs, stimulating toll-like receptor (TLRs) specific responses. Excessive mitochondrial dysfunction results in the apoptosis or necrosis, releasing more inflammatory mediators and cellular debris in the joint cavity, worsening the OA pathological cycle. This evidence sufficiently explains that effective modulation of macrophage mitochondrial dysfunction can arrest the inflammation driven cascade of events in OA pathology. On this front, the potential of SGE, AG and RG to modulate mitochondrial dysfunction was tested in the form of their action on mitochondrial membrane potential by using JC-1 assay. JC1 is a fluorescent probe, which produces a red fluorescence when aggregated in a mitochondrial matrix in the form of polymer. In case of reduced mitochondrial membrane potential JC-1 fails to accumulate and as a monomer, produces green fluorescence. Therefore, change in red/green ratio in the treated U937 cells, is an indicator of effective modulation of mitochondrial dysfunction. SGE, AG and RG showed positive action on induced U937 mitochondrial membrane potential, and the authors found a linear decline in the percentage of stressed cells (Fig. 6A) in the treated cells. A significant increase in the red/green ratio was noted for the highest selected concentration of each formulation, when compared to the ratio in tumor necrosis factor (TNF) -α induced U937 cells (Fig. 6B). SGE is a known oxidative stress reliever, which was expected to decipher positive modulation of mitochondrial membrane potential as was seen in the form of reduction in the percentage of stressed cells [51,52]. Tinospora cordifolia and Pluchea lanceolata are principal components of AG and RG, respectively and were reported to exhibit positive effects on oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in neurodegenerative disorders like Parkinson's and Alzheimer's disease [[53], [54], [55]]. Thus, the current results were in agreement of the reported studies and for the first time their positive action on the synovial macrophages is reported through this study.

Evaluation of anti-inflammatory potential of guggulsterone through complement system modulation represents a novel approach adopted by the authors in special context with macrophages. The complement system, comprising approximately 60 different proteins including activating proteins, cell surface receptors, and regulatory factors plays a crucial role in disease pathology [56], and are highly activated in OA [57,58]. Notably, complement system proteins closely interact with macrophages to facilitate complement effector functions. Monocyte and macrophage receptors such as CR1 (CD35), CR3 (CD11b/CD18), and CR4 (CD11c/CD18) engage with complement components like C1q, Mannan Binding Lectin (MBL), C3b, C4b, C3a, and C5a particularly to regulate release of ROS, cytokine production, phagocytosis and in general inflammatory responses. Additionally, tissue macrophages express complement receptorof immunoglobulin (CRIg), commonly found in activated macrophages, including synovial macrophages and liver Kupffer cells [59]. To assess the direct interactions between guggulsterone and complement proteins C3a and C5a, the authors employed molecular docking, which was further substantiated by MD simulations to analyze their binding stability. As potent complement anaphylatoxins, Complement component 3a (C3a) and C5a exhibit pro-inflammatory properties that effectively activate monocytes and macrophages via diverse signaling pathways. C3a enhances ATP release through extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2), subsequently triggering nucleotide-binding domain, leucine-rich–containing family, pyrin domain–containing-3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation [60]. Similarly, studies by Samstad et al. demonstrated that C5a, generated during complement activation by cholesterol crystals, induces NLRP3 inflammasome activation, leading to IL-1β and TNFα release, inflammation, and increased ROS production [61]. Recent research has identified elevated levels of C5a and its receptor (C5aR) in both synovium and cartilage explants of transgenic mice, while C3a and C3aR were predominantly found in synovium [62]. Furthermore, Assirelli et al. reported the expression of complement proteins C3, C4, and CFB in OA-affected tissues, including cartilage, synovial membranes, chondrocytes, and synoviocytes, highlighting their involvement in disease pathology [62,63]. Interestingly, among these proteins, CFB exhibited the highest expression in chondrocytes and cartilage explants, suggesting a dominant role for the alternative complement pathway in OA progression.

The present results showed that the CFB–guggulsterone complexes had relatively good binding activity. The CFB–guggulsterone complex had the lowest binding scores (−10.12 kcal/mol), which indicates that the complex may play an indispensable role in immune regulation. However, the lowest average binding free energy of CFB–guggulsterone complex (−8.522 kJ/mol) was less than CFB–MVK complex (−37.347 kJ/mol); this might be due to less surface area and lower molecular refractivity of guggulsterone, thus, indicating lower binding energy and better interactions. It was indicated that these complexes may exert important roles in immune regulation. Taken together, the outcomes of present work strongly indicate the potential of SGE, as a principle acting component of guggul, and other guggul formulations, AG, and RG, in limiting complement protein activation. It must be noted here that CFB is a serine protease, upon activation, the Bb domain of CFB combines with complement factor 3 b to proteolytically cleave C3 protein (hence called as C3 convertase). Linking this to outcomes of gelatinase spot assay result, wherein the authors report a significant inhibition of proteolytic activity by SGE, there is enough scope to interpret that SGE, AG and RG can inhibit the proteolytic activation of complement factors to limit the progression of inflammation.

Finally, the authors would like to acknowledge the strength and limitations of the present study. Primary limitation of this study is – majority of the cell work was performed using commercially available human macrophage cell line, which limits the extrapolation of the study outcomes in the clinical usage of the selected formulations. Therefore, these findings serve as indicative evidence towards establishing the anti-OA potential of guggul and its formulations at the cellular and molecular levels, thereby addressing a fundamental gap in Ayurveda, the ancient Indian medicinal system. Although, many studies are available that report biochemical activity of guggul in numerous diseases, current study stands out for defining macrophage specific activity of guggul and its formulations with reference to OA-specific pathological events including inflammation, oxidative-stress and mitochondrial membrane potential. Also, this is the first study to report dynamics between complement protein and Guggulsterone. To make the study outcomes clinically viable, this work needs to be repeated on primary macrophages and further in pre-clinical models for further specificity of pharmacodynamics of these formulations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings here demonstrate that SGE and the guggul formulations AG and RG exert potent immunomodulatory effects on TNF-α-challenged U937-derived synovial macrophages by significantly reducing NO, MMP-1, MMP-13, VEGF-1, and iNOS levels; inhibiting gelatinase-like proteolytic activity necessary for complement activation; limiting pro-inflammatory M1 polarization; and restoring mitochondrial membrane potential to curb DAMP-driven inflammatory cascades. These mechanistic insights into macrophage modulation, collagenase inhibition, and complement suppression underscore the therapeutic merit of guggul in synovial inflammation, although they warrant further validation in primary human macrophages and in vivo studies.
