A time-lagged psychological model of linguistic uncertainty, cognitive flexibility, and academic well-being among English major students
Authors: Xuan Guo, Xitao Fan, Abdulelah Ahmed Alghamdi, Fang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLinguistic uncertainty is an inherent feature of advanced English learning, particularly in academic contexts that require interpretation, critical analysis, and open-ended discourse. Although prior research has linked language-related uncertainty to negative academic experiences, less is known about the psychological mechanisms through which such uncertainty may be associated with students’ academic well-being over time. This study examined a three-wave time-lagged mediation model among English major students enrolled in undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral programs at multiple public universities across four regions of China. Data were collected within a single academic semester (N = 2,554 matched participants). Linguistic uncertainty was assessed at Time 1, tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility at Time 2, and academic well-being at Time 3. Structural equation modeling was used to test a parallel mediation model, controlling for age and gender. Indirect effects were evaluated using bias-corrected bootstrapping, and sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess robustness. Linguistic uncertainty at Time 1 showed a significant prospective association with academic well-being at Time 3. It was also negatively associated with tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility at Time 2, both of which were positively associated with later academic well-being. Indirect effects through tolerance of ambiguity (β = −0.084) and cognitive flexibility (β = −0.095) were significant, indicating partial mediation, with the pathway through cognitive flexibility being slightly stronger in magnitude. Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the findings to alternative model specifications, potential unmeasured confounding, and sample attrition. The findings suggest that linguistic uncertainty is linked to academic well-being over time, partly through its associations with students’ tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility. By incorporating temporal separation among constructs, this study extends prior cross-sectional research and underscores the relevance of adaptive psychological resources in linguistically demanding academic environments. Introduction

In English-major programs, students routinely encounter linguistic uncertainty as a normal feature of advanced academic English, rather than as an occasional difficulty1,2. This uncertainty emerges when learners must interpret incomplete meanings, multiple plausible readings, and competing cues in tasks such as close reading, literary/critical interpretation, academic discussion, and assessed writing—contexts in which “the next” word, meaning, or discourse move is rarely fully certain3–5. Contemporary language-processing accounts emphasize that comprehenders continuously generate expectations and update interpretations as new information arrives, which makes uncertainty management a core part of real-time comprehension and academic language use related neurocognitive perspectives likewise frame language use—especially when multiple alternatives compete—as an ongoing exercise in managing uncertainty through adaptive control processes, implying that sustained exposure to ambiguity can place recurring demands on executive resources over an academic term6. Importantly, English majors may face particularly high and persistent uncertainty because their curriculum emphasizes advanced comprehension and interpretation (e.g., academic L2 reading across courses and sustained term-level engagement with complex texts), making uncertainty a chronic feature of their academic environment rather than a one-off challenge7.

Building on the view of linguistic uncertainty as a chronic feature of advanced English study, an important but less-examined question concerns its implications for students’ academic well-being. Academic well-being is commonly conceptualized as a multidimensional construct that reflects students’ academic satisfaction, perceived academic efficacy, and positive functioning within the academic environment, rather than as isolated emotional states or performance indicators8,9. In higher education research, this construct captures how students evaluate and experience their academic lives more broadly, including their sense of competence, engagement, and fulfillment in academic roles. However, much of the existing literature in applied linguistics and language education has focused on proximal, domain-specific outcomes, particularly language anxiety and achievement/performance outcomes. A substantial body of research has documented links between uncertainty-related experiences in language learning and heightened foreign language anxiety or stress, as well as associations with test scores, grades, or self-rated proficiency10–13. While these studies provide valuable insights into affective and performance-related consequences, they offer a narrower view of student functioning and rarely address how sustained linguistic uncertainty may relate to students’ broader academic well-being across time. Consequently, there remains a notable gap in understanding whether and how linguistic uncertainty extends beyond immediate emotional reactions or performance fluctuations to shape students’ longer-term academic well-being. In particular, few studies have examined these associations using designs that introduce temporal separation among constructs, limiting insight into how early experiences of uncertainty may be linked to later academic satisfaction and efficacy. Addressing this gap naturally raises the expectation that higher linguistic uncertainty may be prospectively associated with lower academic well-being, an assumption that has received limited direct empirical attention among English-major university populations.

Extending this outcome-focused gap, a theoretical grounding for linking linguistic uncertainty to academic well-being can be drawn from uncertainty-focused frameworks and control–value theory (CVT)14. Across educational psychology, these perspectives converge on the idea that individuals’ experiences of uncertainty are closely tied to their perceptions of control and subsequent emotional and motivational responses in achievement settings. Control–value theory, in particular, posits that when learners perceive low control over academic demands—such as when outcomes or meanings are difficult to predict—negative achievement-related emotions are more likely to emerge, with implications for students’ academic satisfaction and sense of efficacy14,15. Within this framework, sustained uncertainty is not viewed as a neutral cognitive state but as a condition that can erode perceived control, thereby increasing cognitive and emotional strain. When students repeatedly encounter situations in which expectations are unclear or interpretations remain unresolved, as is common in advanced English study, this uncertainty may heighten worry, frustration, or helplessness, which can gradually spill over into broader evaluations of academic functioning. Recent theoretical extensions of CVT emphasize that such appraisal processes operate dynamically across time and contexts, linking uncertainty-related experiences to longer-term patterns of academic engagement, satisfaction, and perceived competence14.

Empirical research aligned with these perspectives further suggests that uncertainty and low perceived control are associated with poorer academic adjustment and well-being-related outcomes, even when controlling for performance indicators. For example, studies in higher education have shown that uncertainty-related stress and diminished control appraisals are associated with lower academic satisfaction and reduced academic self-efficacy—both central components of academic well-being14,16,17. Importantly, these associations are typically interpreted as relational rather than causal, reflecting the complex interplay between contextual demands, cognitive appraisals, and student functioning. Taken together, uncertainty-focused and control–value perspectives provide a coherent theoretical basis for expecting that linguistic uncertainty may be prospectively associated with academic well-being, without implying deterministic or causal effects. This theoretical grounding supports the first hypothesis of the present study, which posits a time-ordered association between linguistic uncertainty and subsequent academic well-being among English majors.

Building on this theoretical grounding, a key psychological mechanism linking linguistic uncertainty to academic well-being is tolerance of ambiguity. In learning and language contexts, tolerance of ambiguity refers to learners’ capacity to remain engaged and functional when faced with uncertain, complex, or incomplete information, such as unclear meanings, multiple interpretations, or unresolved problems18,19. In applied linguistics, this construct has been used to explain why some learners persist and adapt in challenging language-learning situations, whereas others experience heightened discomfort and disengagement when confronted with ambiguity. A growing body of research in applied linguistics and educational psychology indicates that higher tolerance of ambiguity is associated with more adaptive learning outcomes, including stronger engagement, greater openness to challenge, and lower maladaptive affect. For example, studies with university EFL learners have shown that students who report greater tolerance of ambiguity tend to experience lower levels of foreign language anxiety and are more willing to participate in communicative and interpretive tasks20–23. More recent syntheses further suggest that tolerance of ambiguity is positively related to motivational and adjustment processes in learning environments characterized by uncertainty, whereas intolerance of uncertainty is linked to stress, avoidance, and poorer psychological adjustment24–26. At the same time, tolerance of ambiguity should not be viewed as entirely static. Sustained exposure to high levels of linguistic uncertainty, particularly when students feel unable to resolve meaning or predict performance expectations, may gradually erode their willingness or capacity to tolerate ambiguity. From this perspective, persistent linguistic uncertainty may undermine students’ comfort with unresolved meaning, thereby increasing strain and reducing adaptive engagement. Over time, such erosion of ambiguity tolerance may spill over into broader academic experiences, indirectly contributing to lower academic satisfaction and perceived efficacy—central components of academic well-being. Taken together, this line of reasoning suggests that tolerance of ambiguity represents a plausible mediating mechanism linking linguistic uncertainty to academic well-being. Accordingly, the present study proposes that tolerance of ambiguity partially accounts for the time-ordered association between linguistic uncertainty and subsequent academic well-being, consistent with a mediation framework rather than a direct-only relationship.

A second psychological mechanism that may link linguistic uncertainty to later academic well-being is cognitive flexibility, typically defined as the capacity to shift perspectives and strategies, generate alternative solutions, and adaptively reappraise or adjust thinking in response to changing situational demands27. In educational contexts, cognitive flexibility is often conceptualized as a self-regulatory cognitive resource that supports adaptive responding in the face of complexity and change, and recent work continues to operationalize it as the ability to transition efficiently between thoughts and actions when faced with challenge or uncertainty28. On the other hand, neurocognitive perspectives further suggest that language use is intrinsically uncertainty-laden because comprehenders and producers routinely confront competing linguistic cues (e.g., lexical and syntactic competition) and must resolve or adapt to this uncertainty using control processes that overlap with executive functions6. From this standpoint, sustained linguistic uncertainty in academically demanding English-major contexts may repeatedly recruit executive resources for monitoring, updating interpretations, and selecting among alternatives, implying that flexible control is not merely beneficial but is continually “called upon” in advanced language learning environments. Empirically, cognitive flexibility has been linked to more adaptive affective experiences in language-related settings. For instance, in a study of university English preparatory students, cognitive flexibility showed a moderate negative association with foreign language anxiety and mediated the association between anxiety and related outcomes, consistent with the view that flexible cognition may buffer strain in language-learning contexts29,30. Evidence from adjacent applied linguistics work likewise indicates that higher cognitive flexibility is negatively related to language-teaching anxiety, reinforcing the broader link between flexible cognition and lower anxiety in language-relevant settings30. Beyond language-specific outcomes, research in higher education also suggests that cognitive flexibility is associated with lower academic stress and better emotional adjustment, supporting its relevance for well-being-related functioning27. Integrating these perspectives, a theoretically plausible pathway is that sustained linguistic uncertainty functions as a recurring cognitive demand that may tax executive resources over time, thereby reducing students’ capacity to shift strategies and reframe uncertainty adaptively (i.e., lower cognitive flexibility). Reduced flexibility may be associated with poorer subsequent academic well-being (e.g., lower academic satisfaction and efficacy), thereby positioning cognitive flexibility as a second mediator in the proposed model. Consistent with this reasoning, the present study posits that linguistic uncertainty (T1) is indirectly associated with academic well-being (T3) through cognitive flexibility (T2) (Hypothesis 3).

Because tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility capture distinct yet complementary adaptive resources, the present study conceptualized them as parallel mediators rather than assuming a causal ordering between them. Tolerance of ambiguity reflects students’ comfort with unresolved meaning and complexity, whereas cognitive flexibility reflects the capacity to shift perspectives and adapt strategies in response to demanding situations; although these processes may be correlated, they need not operate in a fixed sequence. Methodologically, a parallel mediation specification is appropriate when multiple mediators are theorized to transmit the association between an antecedent and an outcome simultaneously, allowing estimation of each mediator’s unique indirect effect while controlling for the other mediators31,32. In addition, because the two mediators were assessed at the same wave (T2), imposing a serial (sequential) ordering would require stronger assumptions about temporal precedence than the design can directly support; therefore, the parallel model provides a more defensible representation of the proposed mechanisms under the present measurement schedule32,33.

Against this conceptual backdrop, it is important to note that much of the existing evidence linking uncertainty-related experiences in language learning to student outcomes is derived from cross-sectional designs, which limit insight into temporal ordering and are vulnerable to common-method bias. While such studies have been instrumental in identifying associations among linguistic experiences, affective reactions, and performance indicators, they offer limited leverage for examining how experiences of linguistic uncertainty may unfold and relate to broader academic well-being over time. As a result, questions remain regarding whether uncertainty precedes changes in students’ academic functioning or merely co-occurs with them at a single point in time34,35. To address these limitations, the present study adopted a three-wave time-lagged design implemented within a single academic semester. By temporally separating the measurement of linguistic uncertainty (T1), psychological mechanisms (tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility; T2), and academic well-being (T3), this design helps reduce common-method variance and allows for a more defensible examination of time-ordered associations among constructs36. Although such a design does not permit causal inference, it strengthens the interpretability of mediation pathways by aligning measurement timing with theoretical assumptions about antecedents, mechanisms, and outcomes34.

Within this framework, the present study makes several contributions. First, it extends research on language-related uncertainty by shifting attention from narrowly defined affective or performance outcomes to academic well-being as a broader indicator of students’ academic functioning. Second, it integrates insights from applied linguistics and educational psychology by examining tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility as parallel psychological mechanisms, rather than assuming a single or sequential pathway. Finally, by focusing on English-major students across multiple universities and regions, the study provides a more contextualized understanding of how linguistic uncertainty may be associated with academic well-being over time in linguistically demanding higher education environments. Together, these contributions help clarify the psychological processes through which uncertainty in academic English learning may be linked to students’ academic experiences, while remaining appropriately cautious about causal claims.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a three-wave time-lagged design to examine the proposed psychological pathways among linguistic uncertainty, tolerance of ambiguity, cognitive flexibility, and academic well-being. A time-lagged design, with temporal separation among predictors, mediators, and outcomes, was employed to reduce the risk of Common Method Bias (CMB). Data were collected from English-major university students enrolled in undergraduate and graduate programs across multiple universities in China, during the fall semester of 2025. To enhance generalizability, participating institutions were selected from four broad geographic regions of China (East, West, South, and North). Approximately three universities were included from each region to ensure regional diversity while maintaining feasibility within the constraints of a multi-wave, semester-based design. Province and university names are not reported to preserve institutional anonymity.

All three waves of data collection were conducted within a single academic semester, ensuring that participants’ academic environments and instructional contexts remained stable while introducing temporal separation among key constructs. Linguistic uncertainty was assessed at Time 1 (T1) to capture students’ initial perceptions of uncertainty in their academic English learning, tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility were assessed at Time 2 (T2) as theoretically proximal psychological mechanisms, and academic well-being was assessed at Time 3 (T3) as a downstream outcome of these processes. The time interval between successive waves was approximately three weeks, a duration commonly used in educational and psychological research to strengthen temporal ordering while minimizing participant attrition.

Sample size estimation

Sample size estimation was guided by recommendations for structural equation modeling (SEM) and time-lagged mediation analysis. Given the complexity of the proposed model, a minimum ratio of 10–15 participants per estimated parameter was considered appropriate. In addition, prior methodological literature suggests that a sample size of at least 300 participants is desirable for stable SEM estimation and bootstrapped indirect effect testing. To account for potential attrition across the three waves, oversampling was implemented at Time 1. This ensured that the final matched sample across all three time points remained adequate for SEM analyses and maintained sufficient statistical power.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected using online questionnaires administered at three time points within a single academic semester. At Time 1 (T1), a total of 2,875 eligible students completed measures of linguistic uncertainty and demographic information. Approximately three weeks later, 2,668 participants from the initial sample completed the Time 2 (T2) survey, which assessed tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility. At Time 3 (T3), conducted approximately three weeks after T2, 2,554 participants completed the academic well-being measure.

Participants were matched across waves using unique anonymous identifiers to ensure accurate linkage of responses over time while maintaining confidentiality. Of the 2,875 participants who completed the Time 1 survey, 2,668 participated at Time 2 (attrition rate = 7.20%), and 2,554 completed the Time 3 survey (attrition rate from Time 2 to Time 3 = 4.27%), resulting in an overall attrition rate of 11.16% across the three waves. Only participants who completed all three waves were included in the final analytic sample. The observed attrition rates are within acceptable ranges for multi-wave, semester-based survey research and do not suggest systematic dropout attributable to the study design.

Attrition across the three waves was examined by comparing response counts at each time point. Analyses focused on participants with complete data across all waves; cases with missing responses due to wave-level non-participation were excluded from the final analytic sample. For item-level missing data in completed surveys, full-information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation was used in structural equation modeling, as this approach yields unbiased parameter estimates under missing-at-random assumptions. To examine the plausibility of the missing-at-random assumption underlying FIML estimation, attrition analyses were conducted comparing participants who completed all waves with those who did not. No significant differences were observed in baseline linguistic uncertainty, age, or gender, suggesting limited evidence of systematic attrition.

Sampling frame and recruitment

The sampling frame comprised full-time English-major students (undergraduate and graduate levels) enrolled at public universities that offer accredited English-language or literature programs in the selected provinces. Universities were identified through publicly available listings of higher education institutions and departmental program information. A multistage convenience sampling approach was used. First, universities in different provinces were contacted via departmental administrators or faculty members who teach English-related courses. Second, eligible students were invited to participate through course announcements and online survey links distributed via institutional communication platforms. This approach allowed access to a geographically diverse sample while remaining feasible within the constraints of a multi-wave semester-based design.

Eligibility criteria

Participants were eligible to take part in the study if they met the following criteria: (a) were currently enrolled as English majors at the undergraduate or graduate level; (b) were full-time students at the participating universities; (c) had sufficient proficiency in Chinese to complete the survey instruments; and (d) provided informed consent at the initial wave of data collection. To ensure cultural and linguistic comparability, international (non-Chinese) students were excluded, as differences in educational backgrounds, language socialization, and cultural adaptation processes could introduce additional sources of variance that are not central to the study’s objectives. In addition, students who self-reported a diagnosed mental disorder or current use of psychotropic medication were excluded to minimize potential confounding effects of clinical conditions on cognitive and well-being–related measures. Students enrolled in non-English majors or part-time programs were also excluded from the study.

Translation and cultural adaptation

The measures of linguistic uncertainty and tolerance of ambiguity were originally developed in English and lacked standardized, validated Mainland Chinese versions suitable for the target population. Therefore, these two instruments were translated and culturally adapted into Chinese following established forward–backward translation procedures. Specifically, the original English items were independently translated into Chinese by two bilingual researchers with expertise in psychology and applied linguistics. An independent bilingual translator, blinded to the original versions, then back-translated the Chinese versions into English. Discrepancies between the original and back-translated versions were resolved through discussion to ensure semantic equivalence, conceptual consistency, and cultural appropriateness for Chinese English major students.

Pilot testing

Prior to the main data collection, a pilot study was conducted with a small sample of English major students (n = 35) from one participating university who were not included in the final study sample. The purpose of the pilot testing was to assess item clarity, appropriateness of wording, response format, survey length, and the feasibility of a multi-wave, time-lagged data collection procedure within a single academic semester. Participants in the pilot study completed the survey instruments and provided feedback on item comprehension and overall survey flow. Minor wording adjustments were made in response to this feedback to improve clarity and reduce ambiguity. Preliminary reliability analyses indicated acceptable internal consistency for all scales, supporting their suitability for use in the main study.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Xinxiang Institute of Engineering, Xinxiang, China (0967/AR/2025). Participants provided informed consent prior to data collection. The study involved anonymous, minimal-risk educational survey procedures conducted within university settings. Consistent with IRB-approved procedures for minimal-risk university-based research involving late adolescents and young adults, additional parental consent requirements for participants below 18 years of age were waived by the approving IRB. Ethical guidelines consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki were strictly followed.

Instrumentation

Demographic characteristics and confounders

At Time 1, participants reported their age, gender, and level of study (undergraduate and graduate). Among these variables, age and gender were included as covariates in the analytical models due to their established associations with cognitive functioning, emotional adjustment, and academic well-being in university populations37–39. All demographic variables were measured at baseline and treated as exogenous covariates in subsequent analyses.

Linguistic uncertainty

Linguistic uncertainty was assessed using an adapted version of the Second Language Tolerance of Ambiguity Scale (SLTAS) originally developed by Ely (1989)18. In the present study, all 12 items from the original SLTAS framework were retained and contextually adapted for use in academic English among English-major students (e.g., uncertainty when reading academic texts or participating in English discussions). Participants responded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater perceived linguistic uncertainty (after appropriate reverse coding).

Tolerance of ambiguity

General tolerance of ambiguity was measured using the Multiple Stimulus Types Ambiguity Tolerance Scale–II (MSTAT-II) developed by McLain (2009)40. The scale comprises 13 items that assess reactions to novelty, complexity, and insoluble problems. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Several items are negatively worded and were reverse-coded according to standard scoring procedures. Higher scores reflect greater tolerance of ambiguity.

Cognitive flexibility

Cognitive flexibility was assessed using the Cognitive Flexibility Scale (CFS) originally developed by Martin and Rubin (1995)41. The original English-language version contains 12 items. In the present study, we used the Chinese revision developed by Bing et al. (2013)42, which comprises 13 items and was adapted for use among Chinese college students. Participants responded on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate greater cognitive flexibility, with designated items reverse-coded prior to analysis. The Chinese revised version has demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity among Chinese university student populations42.

Academic well-being

Academic well-being was measured using the Chinese version of the College Student Subjective Well-Being Questionnaire (CSSWQ)43. The scale consists of 16 items across four subscales: academic satisfaction, academic efficacy, school connectedness, and college gratitude, with each subscale comprising four items. Responses were recorded on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Higher scores reflect higher levels of academic well-being. In the present study, the total score was used as an indicator of overall academic well-being.

Data analysis

Data analyses were conducted using SPSS (v27) for preliminary analyses and AMOS (v26) for structural equation modeling (SEM). Descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations) and bivariate correlations were computed for all study variables. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α and composite reliability (CR), with values ≥ 0.70 indicating acceptable reliability. Convergent validity was evaluated using average variance extracted (AVE), with values ≥ 0.50 considered adequate. Discriminant validity was assessed using the Fornell–Larcker criterion.

Prior to hypothesis testing, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was performed to assess the measurement model and establish discriminant validity among linguistic uncertainty, tolerance of ambiguity, cognitive flexibility, and academic well-being. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), with CFI and TLI values ≥ 0.90 and RMSEA and SRMR values ≤ 0.08 indicating acceptable fit44.

To test the hypothesized relationships, a time-lagged parallel mediation model was estimated using SEM. Linguistic uncertainty measured at Time 1 was specified as the independent variable, tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility measured at Time 2 were specified as parallel mediators, and academic well-being measured at Time 3 was specified as the outcome variable. Age and gender were included as covariates. Indirect effects were tested using bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples, and mediation was considered significant when the 95% confidence intervals did not include zero.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of the hypothesized time-lagged parallel mediation model. First, mediation sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the extent to which the estimated indirect effects were robust to potential unmeasured confounding45. Second, alternative model specifications were tested, including models with reversed or simplified pathways, to evaluate the stability of the proposed directional structure46. Third, attrition sensitivity analyses were conducted by comparing baseline characteristics of participants who completed all three waves with those who did not, to assess the potential impact of sample attrition47. These analyses were implemented as robustness checks and were not intended to support causal inference.

Results

Participants ranged in age from 17 to 28 years (M = 21.84, SD = 2.63). The sample comprised 68.7% female students and 31.3% male students. In terms of academic level, 74.5% were undergraduate students, 18.2% were master’s students, and 7.3% were doctoral students.

As shown in Table 1, the mean values indicated moderate levels of linguistic uncertainty and tolerance of ambiguity, alongside relatively higher levels of cognitive flexibility and academic well-being. Additionally, the bivariate correlations reflect associations among constructs measured at three temporally separated time points (T1–T3), providing preliminary evidence consistent with the hypothesized temporal ordering. Linguistic uncertainty measured at Time 1 showed moderate to moderately strong negative associations with tolerance of ambiguity (r = − 0.47, p < 0.01) and cognitive flexibility (r = − 0.38, p < 0.01) measured at Time 2, and a moderate negative association with academic well-being measured at Time 3 (r = − 0.49, p < 0.01). In contrast, tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility measured at Time 2 were moderately to strongly positively associated with academic well-being at Time 3 (r = 0.51 and r = 0.55, respectively; p < 0.01). The positive association between tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility was moderate to strong (r = 0.60, p < 0.01). Overall, the magnitude and direction of these correlations were theoretically coherent and suggested that earlier linguistic uncertainty was associated with later adaptive psychological resources and subsequent academic well-being, while remaining sufficiently below unity to indicate that the constructs were empirically distinct.

Table 4 summarizes the results of the time-lagged path analysis examining the hypothesized relationships among the study variables. Linguistic uncertainty measured at Time 1 was negatively and significantly associated with academic well-being at Time 3 (β = −0.184, p < 0.05), indicating that higher perceived linguistic uncertainty was linked to poorer subsequent academic well-being. Linguistic uncertainty also showed significant negative effects on both tolerance of ambiguity (β = −0.372, p < 0.001) and cognitive flexibility (β = −0.271, p < 0.001) at Time 2, suggesting that greater uncertainty in academic English learning undermined these adaptive psychological resources. In turn, tolerance of ambiguity (β = 0.226, p < 0.01) and cognitive flexibility (β = 0.352, p < 0.001) were positively associated with academic well-being at Time 3, with the effect of cognitive flexibility being comparatively stronger. The indirect effects of linguistic uncertainty on academic well-being, mediated by tolerance of ambiguity (β = −0.084, p < 0.001) and cognitive flexibility (β = −0.095, p < 0.001), were both significant, indicating that these two variables functioned as parallel mediators. Importantly, the direct path from linguistic uncertainty to academic well-being remained significant after accounting for the mediators, indicating partial rather than full mediation (Fig. 1). In terms of explained variance, linguistic uncertainty accounted for a meaningful proportion of variance in tolerance of ambiguity (R² = 0.11) and cognitive flexibility (R² = 0.23).

Robustness checks

Sensitivity analyses indicated that the hypothesized time-lagged parallel mediation model was robust. Mediation sensitivity analysis showed that the indirect effects of linguistic uncertainty on academic well-being via tolerance of ambiguity (indirect effect = − 0.084, 95% CI [− 0.121, − 0.050]) and cognitive flexibility (indirect effect = − 0.095, 95% CI [− 0.132, − 0.061]) remained statistically significant across a range of sensitivity parameters, with confidence intervals consistently excluding zero, suggesting limited sensitivity to potential unmeasured confounding. Alternative model specifications, including models with reversed mediator–outcome paths and models excluding the direct path from linguistic uncertainty to academic well-being, demonstrated inferior fit relative to the hypothesized model (e.g., ΔCFI = − 0.03 to − 0.05; ΔRMSEA = 0.012 to 0.018), supporting the stability of the proposed directional structure. In addition, attrition sensitivity analyses comparing participants who completed all three waves with those who dropped out revealed no significant differences in baseline linguistic uncertainty, age, or gender (all p > 0.10; |d| < 0.15), indicating that attrition was unlikely to have materially biased the results.

Discussion

The present study examined a time-lagged psychological model linking linguistic uncertainty to academic well-being among English major students in China, with tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility specified as parallel mediating mechanisms. Drawing on data collected across three time points within a single academic semester, the findings provide consistent evidence that higher levels of linguistic uncertainty were associated with poorer subsequent academic well-being, both directly and indirectly through reduced tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility. Importantly, both mediators played significant roles, with cognitive flexibility emerging as the more influential pathway, while the persistence of a direct effect indicates partial mediation. Together, these results extend prior cross-sectional research by introducing temporal separation among constructs and by clarifying the distinct yet complementary roles of ambiguity-related dispositions and adaptive cognitive resources in shaping academic well-being in linguistically demanding educational contexts.

Hypothesis 1 was supported, indicating that higher linguistic uncertainty at Time 1 was associated with lower subsequent academic well-being at Time 3, even with temporal separation across the semester. This pattern is theoretically consistent with emotion–cognition accounts of academic functioning, which propose that uncertainty in evaluative learning contexts can elicit worry and reduced perceived control, thereby undermining students’ academic satisfaction and efficacy over time (e.g., control–value perspectives on anxiety in achievement settings)48. Empirically, recent work in Chinese EFL/higher education similarly shows that anxiety- and stress-related experiences in English learning contexts are associated with maladaptive academic outcomes and poorer psychological adjustment, including elevated academic burnout and weaker functioning—outcomes conceptually adjacent to reduced academic well-being49,50. In addition, studies of university-level EFL emotions report that negative language-related affect (e.g., classroom/speaking anxiety) co-occurs with indicators of impaired engagement and well-being, supporting the plausibility of a downstream link from early uncertainty to later well-being, while still warranting cautious interpretation given the observational design51–53. Overall, these findings suggest that linguistic uncertainty operates as a meaningful academic stressor for English major students in China, with effects that extend beyond immediate classroom experience to broader academic well-being across the semester.

The findings for the mediated pathway further suggested that tolerance of ambiguity at Time 2 partly accounted for the prospective association between linguistic uncertainty (T1) and academic well-being (T3). Conceptually, language learning—particularly in academically demanding English-major contexts—routinely involves incomplete information, polysemy, and uncertain interpretation, such that students who experience higher linguistic uncertainty may become less willing or able to “sit with” unresolved meaning and complexity, reflecting lower tolerance of ambiguity18,54,55. In turn, broader ambiguity tolerance has been theorized as a protective disposition that reduces perceived threat in uncertain situations and supports adaptive coping, consistent with contemporary accounts linking intolerance of uncertainty/ambiguity to heightened stress responses and poorer well-being-related outcomes56,57. This interpretation also aligns with control–value perspectives, which emphasize that uncertainty can erode perceived control and trigger negative achievement emotions that may spill over into students’ academic satisfaction and efficacy—core elements of academic well-being—although the present results should be interpreted cautiously given the observational, non-experimental design58–60. Empirically, prior work in educational and applied linguistics contexts has repeatedly linked ambiguity tolerance to more adaptive engagement and lower maladaptive affect in learning, and recent syntheses further underscore that tolerance of ambiguity and intolerance of uncertainty are meaningfully related to students’ motivational and adjustment processes in uncertain learning environments20,24. Consequently, the significant indirect effect via tolerance of ambiguity in the current time-lagged model is consistent with the view that linguistic uncertainty may undermine academic well-being partly by reducing students’ capacity to tolerate ambiguity, while acknowledging that alternative explanations (e.g., unmeasured traits or contextual stressors) cannot be fully ruled out.

The third hypothesis was supported, indicating that cognitive flexibility at Time 2 partially accounted for the prospective association between linguistic uncertainty (T1) and academic well-being (T3). Conceptually, sustained linguistic uncertainty in academically demanding English-major contexts may function as a recurring cognitive stressor, requiring students to manage incomplete meanings, competing interpretations, and performance ambiguity, which can tax executive resources and narrow students’ ability to shift perspectives and generate adaptive alternatives (i.e., lower cognitive flexibility). This interpretation aligns with uncertainty-focused neurocognitive accounts of language processing, which emphasize that language users regularly encounter uncertainty and must continuously adapt internal representations and control processes to manage competing linguistic cues24,61,62. At the same time, cognitive flexibility is widely positioned as a psychological resource that supports adaptive coping under academic stress and is positively related to well-being-relevant outcomes; for example, evidence from university student samples has shown that higher cognitive flexibility is associated with lower academic stress and better adjustment-related functioning, providing a plausible mechanism through which early uncertainty can translate into later well-being differences27,63. In language-learning contexts, research with university-preparatory students has also linked cognitive flexibility to more adaptive emotional experiences (e.g., lower foreign-language anxiety), supporting the idea that flexible cognitive responding may help students regulate uncertainty-related strain in language-learning environments29,64. In summary, the observed indirect effect through cognitive flexibility is consistent with the view that linguistic uncertainty may undermine academic well-being partly by constraining students’ capacity for flexible appraisal and coping; however, given the observational nature of the design, these findings should be interpreted as time-ordered associations that are compatible with the proposed mechanism rather than definitive evidence of causality.

Additionally, the robustness of the findings was further supported by a series of sensitivity analyses. The significant indirect effects remained stable across a range of assumptions about potential unmeasured confounding, and alternative model specifications yielded inferior fit than the hypothesized time-lagged parallel mediation model. In addition, attrition analyses suggested that participant dropout was unlikely to have materially influenced the results. Together, these checks increase confidence in the stability of the observed associations without altering the non-causal interpretation of the findings.

Moreover, the present design should be interpreted as a time-lagged mediation framework rather than a full longitudinal panel design. Because the focal constructs were not repeatedly assessed across all waves, the study was not intended to estimate autoregressive stability, reciprocal effects, or within-person longitudinal change. Instead, temporal separation among predictor, mediator, and outcome variables was implemented to strengthen temporal ordering and reduce common-method bias relative to single-wave cross-sectional designs.

Study implications

The present findings have several targeted implications for English-major programs in Chinese higher education. First, the observed association between linguistic uncertainty and later academic well-being suggests that uncertainty is not merely a transient classroom experience but may have broader implications for students’ academic adjustment across a semester. Program designers and instructors may therefore consider explicitly acknowledging uncertainty as an inherent component of advanced English learning (e.g., interpretive ambiguity in literature, open-ended academic discourse) rather than implicitly treating it as a deficit to be eliminated. Second, the mediating role of tolerance of ambiguity highlights the potential value of instructional practices that help students remain engaged when meaning is incomplete or indeterminate. For example, pedagogical approaches that encourage exploratory interpretation, delayed closure, or multiple plausible answers may help students gradually build comfort with ambiguity, which, in turn, may support more stable academic well-being. Third, the comparatively stronger mediating role of cognitive flexibility suggests that students’ ability to reappraise challenges, shift perspectives, and adapt strategies may be particularly relevant in linguistically demanding academic contexts. From a practical standpoint, this suggests the potential value of integrating activities that promote flexible thinking—such as reflective writing, perspective-taking exercises, or adaptive problem-solving tasks—into English-major curricula. Although the present study focused specifically on English-major students, the broader findings may also be relevant to other educational and professional domains characterized by uncertainty, complexity, and demands for adaptive decision-making, such as entrepreneurship education, interdisciplinary learning, and innovation-oriented training environments. In such contexts, tolerance of ambiguity and cognitive flexibility may similarly function as adaptive psychological resources that support adjustment and well-being under uncertain conditions. Importantly, these implications should be viewed as supportive rather than prescriptive, given the observational nature of the study. Nonetheless, the findings underscore the broader relevance of fostering adaptive psychological resources alongside domain-specific competencies in uncertainty-rich learning environments.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although the study employed a three-wave time-lagged design, the data remain observational and based on self-report measures; therefore, the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal relationships. Unmeasured individual or contextual factors may have influenced the reported pathways despite the temporal separation among constructs. Second, the sample was drawn from English-major students at public universities in China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to students in other disciplines, private institutions, or different cultural and educational contexts. Relatedly, international students were excluded to ensure linguistic and cultural comparability, which further constrains the scope of inference. Third, although validated instruments were used and translation procedures were carefully implemented, measurement equivalence across translated and originally validated scales cannot be fully guaranteed, and subtle cultural nuances may not have been entirely captured. In addition, academic well-being was operationalized using a global composite score; future research may benefit from examining specific well-being dimensions separately. Finally, while attrition across waves was within acceptable ranges and sensitivity analyses suggested limited evidence of systematic attrition bias, participant dropout over time cannot be entirely ruled out as a source of bias. Moreover, the use of FIML estimation assumes that missing data were missing at random (MAR), an assumption that cannot be fully verified empirically. Future studies employing experimental or fully longitudinal designs with repeated measurements of all constructs would help to further clarify the temporal dynamics identified in the present study.

Conclusion

This study examined the prospective associations among linguistic uncertainty, tolerance of ambiguity, cognitive flexibility, and academic well-being among English major students using a three-wave time-lagged design. The findings indicate that higher linguistic uncertainty was associated with lower academic well-being over time, and that this association was partially mediated by reduced tolerance for ambiguity and cognitive flexibility. While both mediators played meaningful roles, cognitive flexibility emerged as a somewhat stronger pathway in magnitude. By introducing temporal separation among constructs, the study extends prior cross-sectional research and provides a more nuanced understanding of how linguistic uncertainty may be linked to academic well-being across an academic semester. At the same time, the results should be interpreted as evidence of time-ordered associations rather than causal effects. Overall, the findings highlight the relevance of adaptive psychological resources in linguistically demanding academic contexts and point the way for future research to further clarify these relationships.
