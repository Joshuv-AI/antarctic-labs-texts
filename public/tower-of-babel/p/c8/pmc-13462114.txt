Tuning the electronic and photovoltaic properties of naphthalene diamine through molecular engineering with efficient acceptors: a quantum chemical study
Authors: Iqra Shafiq, Zain Tariq, Ayesha Asghar, Khalid Mahmood, Muhammad Imran, Ke Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-fullerene-based organic solar cells (NF-OSCs) have emerged as promising photovoltaic systems owing to their tunable optoelectronic properties and enhanced power conversion efficiencies. Currently, three series of naphthalene-1,5-diamine (NDA)-based compounds(NBT1–NBT4, NBT5–NBT8, and NBT9–NBT12) featuring an A1–π1–A2–π2–A2–π1–A1 configuration were rationally designed through terminal modifications with thiophene linkers, malononitrile (MN)-based and benzothiophene (BT) based acceptor units. Quantum chemical calculations were performed at the MPW1PW91/6-311G(d, p) level to investigate their electronic, optical, and photovoltaic characteristics. Comprehensive analyses, including frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs), global reactivity parameters (GRPs), density of states (DOS), UV–Vis absorption spectra, transition density matrix (TDM), electron–hole distribution, and open-circuit voltage (Voc), were conducted to evaluate their performance. The incorporation of different electron-withdrawing terminal acceptors effectively reduced the energy gap (2.641–2.323 eV) and broadened the absorption spectra across the visible region (534.484–638.238 nm). Comparable Voc values were obtained through HOMOPTB7–LUMOacceptor estimation, indicating favorable energy-level alignment for charge transfer. Among the designed chromophores, NBT12 exhibited the smallest energy gap (2.323 eV), the highest absorption maximum (λmax = 638.238 nm), and the most efficient intramolecular charge transfer, leading to efficient optoelectronic characteristics. These findings highlight the potential of NDA-based chromophores, particularly NBT12, as efficient non-fullerene acceptor materials for high-performance organic solar cells.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48264-1.Keywords: Organic solar cells, Naphthalene-diamine, Global reactivity parameters, DFT, Photovoltaic propertiesSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Materials science, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

Research on organic solar cells (OSCs) has made substantial progress in the past decade, driven by rapid gains in power conversion efficiency (PCE), sparking strong scientific and economic interest1. Advancements in photovoltaic materials and improved fabrication technologies lead to dramatically increased PCEs of OSCs exceeding 20%2. Moreover, OSCs offer several advantages including easy fabrication, light weight design, cost effectiveness, and suitability for large-area3. The performance of OSCs has significantly improved due to the development of advanced organic materials for the photoactive layer, which exhibit exceptional charge-donating and -accepting properties4. The OSCs operate on the photovoltaic effect, in which sunlight is directly converted into electrical energy. They typically consist of electrodes and a photoactive layer that absorbs light and generates charge carriers5,6. Organic photovoltaics are increasingly studied alongside perovskite-based systems, which continue to show promise for scalability despite stability and efficiency challenges7. Bandgap engineering is a central strategy in optimizing light-harvesting materials; similar tailoring of optical and electronic properties has been successfully demonstrated in OSCs through molecular design and end-group modification of donor–acceptor architectures7.

The OSCs incorporating fullerene acceptors (FAs) in bulk heterojunction (BHJ) architectures have achieved PCEs of approximately 11–12%. However, fullerene-based OSCs face several challenges that hinder large-scale application, including limited absorption in the visible spectrum, low open-circuit voltage (Voc), wide bandgap, high production costs, and limited tunability of electronic properties8–10. To address the limitations of fullerene-based OSCs, researchers have developed non-fullerene OSCs (NF-OSCs), where n-type organic molecules replace fullerenes as electron acceptors, offering a more efficient and tunable alternative. The NFAs enable broader light absorption, tunable energy levels, and flexible molecular design, making them efficient and scalable alternatives for next-generation organic solar cells11.

The performance of NF-OSCs strongly depends on the molecular design of chromophores, where the optical absorption, charge transfer, and energy level alignment can be precisely modulated through structural engineering. In this regard, –A–A–π–core–π–A–A type architectures, consisting of a central electron-donating or electron-withdrawing core linked via π-conjugated bridges to terminal acceptor units, have demonstrated remarkable potential for achieving strong ICT and broad-spectrum light harvesting12–14. The literature reports a wide range of organic chromophores exhibiting diverse molecular architectures, such as donor–acceptor (D–A), donor–π–acceptor (D–π–A), donor–acceptor–π–acceptor (D–A–π–A), donor–donor–π–acceptor (D–D–π–A), and acceptor–π–donor–π–acceptor (A–π–D–π–A) frameworks15,16. Each of these configurations facilitates efficient electronic coupling and charge transfer between the donor, π-spacer, and acceptor moieties. Among them, A–π–A–π–A type systems, incorporating alternating electron-accepting cores and conjugated π-bridges, have attracted significant attention due to their extended π-conjugation, ICT, and favorable film-forming properties, which collectively enhance photovoltaic performance17.

Extensive literature examples show that the chromophores with A–D–A architecture features a donor core moiety, which may include perylene diimide (PDI), benzothiophene (BT), naphthalene diamine (NDA), indacenodithiophene (IDT) etc. linked to two flanking electron-deficient end-capped acceptors exhibited significant photovoltaic characteristics. In these molecules, the extended π-conjugation responsible for the absorption of non-fullerene species has been further augmented through the strategic attachment of fluoro, chloro, and cyano groups onto the molecular skeleton. Literature review highlights naphthalene-diamine-based (NDA) chromophores as promising materials for OSCs, demonstrating strong photovoltaic performance. Their unique molecular structures contribute to excellent chemical and thermal stability, good solubility, high electron mobility, and well-aligned energy levels18. The conjugated structure of NDA-based chromophores, which is made up of alternating single and double bonds, enables effective photon absorption across a wide range of wavelengths, including visible and near-infrared light19. The attachment of electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups to the naphthalene diamine core promotes charge separation upon photon absorption, resulting in the formation of free charge carriers20,21. Furthermore, the tunable energy levels and versatile molecular design of these chromophores allow for the optimization of electronic properties, including the minimization of energy loss due to non-radiative recombination. Together, these features enhance light absorption, facilitate efficient charge transport, and ultimately lead to higher power conversion efficiencies in photovoltaic devices based on naphthalene diamine chromophores19,22. Consequently, these materials hold great promise for the development of next-generation solar cells with increased performance and stability23. In photovoltaic devices that use chromophores based on NDA, this combination of factors leads to improved charge transport and, eventually, higher power conversion efficiencies22. Owing to the favorable electronic and structural features of the naphthalene-1,5-diamine (NDA) core, the present study utilizes three of our previously reported chromophores (ND1, ND6 and MND2)19,24 originally developed as hole transport materials for perovskite solar cells—as reference compounds. Based on these structures, three new series of molecules were designed through molecular engineering, namely NBT1–NBT4 (derived from ND1), NBT5–NBT8 (from MND2), and NBT9–NBT12 (from ND6). The newly designed chromophores adopt an A1-π1-A2-π2-A2-π1-A1 configuration, featuring thiophene rings as π-linkers and malononitrile-based terminal acceptors, tailored for application in OSCs. To the best of our knowledge, naphthalene-diamine derivatives incorporating this specific multi-acceptor architecture and malononitrile as well as BT terminals have not yet been reported in the literature. The photovoltaic properties of the designed compounds (NBT1–NBT12) were evaluated using DFT and TD-DFT calculations at the MPW1PW91/6-311G(d, p) level of theory. The strong –CN groups in the malononitrile acceptors exert both inductive (–I) and resonance (–M) effects, which effectively lower the LUMO energy levels, ICT, and extend optical absorption within the visible region. These combined effects contribute to improved optoelectronic performance, suggesting that the NBT1–NBT12 series are good candidates for future synthesis and application in organic photovoltaic devices.

Computational methodology

To comprehensively evaluate the photovoltaic characteristics of the naphthalene-diamine-based chromophores across all three series (NBT1–NBT4, NBT5–NBT8, and NBT9–NBT12), computational studies were performed using the Gaussian 16 software25. The MPW1PW9126 functional in combination with 6-311G(d, p) basis set27,28 was utilized to exploit the electrical and optical characteristics of desgined chromophores. This functional was selected based on a benchmark study performed on the reference compounds19 using DFT and TD-DFT approaches, ensuring reliable accuracy in reproducing experimental trends. The molecular geometries of all designed chromophores were fully optimized, followed by frequency calculations to confirm the absence of any imaginary frequencies, thereby verifying that each optimized structure represents a true minimum on the potential energy surface. The molecular structures of all chromophores were constructed and visualized using GaussView 6.029 for input file preparation and subsequent computational analyses. The DOS analysis was accomplished through the DFT method. Whereas, other investigations including TDM, FMOs, Voc and UV-Vis were conducted using TD-DFT approach at the abovementioned level. The solvent effect was investigated through polarizable continuum model (PCM) in acetonitrile (ε = 36.64)30.The global reactivity parameters (GRPs) of the designed chromophores were determined based on the HOMO and LUMO energy values by utilizing Equations illustrated in Table S30. Additionally, the dihedral angles were analyzed to assess the molecular planarity and its influence on conjugation and charge transfer efficiency. Different software like Avogadro31, PyMOlyze32, Origin 8.033, Multiwfn version 3.834, Chemcraft35 and Gauss Sum36 were employed to interpret results from Gaussian out puts.

Results and discussion

In this study, leveraging the favorable electronic and structural features of the naphthalene-1,5-diamine (NDA) core, three previously reported chromophores (ND1, ND6, and MND2), originally developed as hole transport materials for perovskite solar cells, were selected as reference compounds. Based on these scaffolds, three new series of chromophores were rationally designed—NBT1–NBT4 (derived from ND1)19,24 main, NBT5–NBT8 (from MND2), and NBT9–NBT12 (from ND6)19,24. The selection of these reference compounds was owning to their unique electronic and photovoltaic response amidst all their derivatives and their structures are presented in Figs. S3 and S4. The newly designed chromophores adopt an extended A1-π1-A2-π2-A2-π1-A1 architecture, featuring naphthalene-1,5-diamine as the central π-spacer (π2). This core is connected to benzonitrile units (A2) at both ends (reference compounds structure). Thiophene rings (π1) act as π-bridges, promoting efficient ICT between the two segments of desgined derivatives was introduced. At the terminal sides malononitrile or benzothiophene acceptors (A1) were modulated. The strong –CN groups in the malononitrile acceptors exert both inductive (–I) and resonance (–M) effects, which effectively lower the LUMO energy levels, ICT, and extend optical absorption within the visible region. The introduction of stronger electron-withdrawing substituents on malononitrile-derived terminal units in D–π–A and A–D–A chromophores has been shown to further lower the LUMO energy, thereby narrowing the optical band gap and facilitating enhanced intramolecular charge–transfer (ICT) from donor to acceptor analogues37. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that when the acceptor strength is increased (while the donor is kept constant), the HOMO remains relatively unchanged but the LUMO is shifted downward, thus increasing the donor–acceptor driving force for electron injection in photovoltaic devices38. Therefore, by modulating the nature and conjugation extent of substituents in MN/BT-type acceptors (for instance varying –CN, –F, –Cl or additional π-conjugated nitrile fragments), one can systematically tune the absorption onset, ICT character, and photovoltaic alignment of the chromophores. The main difference between these three series is the A2 moieties and terminal acceptors. The effect of these A1 and A2 when connected with thiophene ring and malononitrile acceptors on photovoltaic characteristics of OSCs was investigated through DFT/TD-DFT approaches. The optimized molecular structures of these chromophores are illustrated in Fig. 1, while their corresponding IUPAC names, ChemDraw structures and abbreviations are provided in Table S31. The Cartesian coordinates of the optimized geometries for NBT1–NBT12 are listed in Tables S1–S12. The results demonstrate that tuning the electronic nature of the A1/A2 acceptor units by introducing thiophene ring and malononitrile/benzothiophene acceptors bearing electron withdrawing units significantly enhance the photovoltaic performance by improving intramolecular charge transfer and lowering the energy gap.

Geometrical parameters

The dihedral angles, bond lengths, and bond angles play a crucial role in modulating the ICT characteristics of π-conjugated systems. Specifically, the dihedral angle between the donor and acceptor units governs the degree of π-conjugation and electronic communication along the molecular backbone. A smaller dihedral angle (closer to planarity) facilitates extended conjugation, thereby enhancing ICT, whereas a larger dihedral angle causes twisting, which reduces orbital overlap and weakens charge delocalization39. Therefore, the calculations of dihedral angles, bond lengths, and bond angles are important for assessing the planarity of the designed chromophores (NBT1–NBT12), which plays a key role in facilitating ICT. Figure 1 shows schematic representation of dihedral angles of designed compounds (NBT1-NBT12). Table S13 lists the dihedral angles (θ1–θ6) between structural components, while bond lengths are summarized in Table S14. For NBT1–NBT4, the dihedral angles are predominantly close to 0° or 180° (e.g., θ1 = 170.97°, θ3 = 176.22°, θ4 = −176.21°), indicating near-planar conformations that favor π-conjugation and efficient ICT. Some compounds (e.g., NBT1 θ2 = −53.27° or S59-C49-C39-C35 = − 126.54°) exhibit local twists that may slightly reduce conjugation locally. Overall, the structural parameters confirm that most chromophores maintain a largely planar geometry, conducive to effective conjugation and enhanced ICT. The C–C bond lengths (e.g., L1, L2, L5, L6) generally fall within 1.406–1.470 Å, in close agreement with experimental values reported in the literature (1.364–1.475 Å)40. The C–N bond lengths (L3, L4) are consistent across compounds, typically ~ 1.273 Å for NBT1–NBT4 and slightly longer (up to ~ 1.44 Å) in NBT5–NBT12. These bond lengths support the presence of conjugated systems. Molecules exhibiting shorter C–C and C=N bond lengths and near-planar geometries show stronger ICT behavior, reflected by lower HOMO–LUMO gaps and red-shifted absorption. Overall, the structural parameters confirm that most chromophores maintain a largely planar geometry, conducive to effective conjugation and enhanced ICT.

Frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs)

The FMOs analysis is important to estimate the light absorption capacity, electronic charge distribution patterns of organic frameworks41. A comprehensive understanding of the electronic and optoelectronic properties of organic molecules is essential for evaluating and optimizing their performance in optoelectronic and photovoltaic devices. The analysis provides insights into molecular interactions, charge mobility, chemical stability, reactivity, and intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) between HOMO and LUMO orbitals42. HOMOs represent electron-donating, while LUMOs represent electron-withdrawing capabilities43. The performance of OSCs is determined by the energy gap (ΔE), which is the energy difference between HOMO and LUMO. A smaller ΔE facilitates charge transfer from the donor’s HOMO to the acceptor’s LUMO, enhancing OSC performance. Low band gap energy is associated with higher efficiency, influencing short-circuit current (Jsc), open circuit voltage (Voc), and power conversion efficiency (PCE). The energy gap significantly impacts structural stability, charge production, conductivity, and photovoltaic performance44. The computed HOMO energy values for series 1 designed compounds (NBT1-NBT4) are − 6.104, -6.108, -6.107 and − 6.115 eV respectively, while the corresponding LUMO energy values are − 3.463, -3.529, -3.530 and − 3.638 eV respectively. Consequently, the calculated energy gap values for NBT1-NBT4 are 2.641, 2.579, 2.577 and 2.477 eV respectively, representing that all the titled chromophores in series 1 exhibit comparable values of ΔE within the range of 2.5 to 2.7. Among the designed compounds, NBT4 exhibits the lowest energy gap (ΔE = 2.477 eV), primarily due to the incorporation of two trifluoromethyl (–CF3) groups on the terminal acceptor (A1). The –CF3 group is highly electron-withdrawing owing to the presence of three strongly electronegative fluorine atoms, which effectively stabilize the LUMO energy level more than the HOMO, resulting in a significantly narrowed energy gap. Additionally, the strong inductive and mesomeric effects of –CF3 enhance intramolecular charge transfer (ICT), which is beneficial for optoelectronic performance. Beyond electronegativity, the steric bulk of –CF3 groups introduces slight twisting in the molecular backbone, which can enhance ICT by modulating planarity and conjugation. In contrast, NBT1, which contains fluorine atoms on each A1 unit, shows the higher ΔE value (2.641 eV) than NBT4. Although a single fluorine atom is highly electronegative, the electron-withdrawing effect of a –CF3 group is stronger overall because it combines multiple C–F bonds and exerts a more effective inductive (-I) withdrawal of electron density from the adjacent framework45,46. NBT2, with chlorine substituents, exhibits a slightly lower energy gap (2.579 eV) than NBT1. This reduction might due to the moderately electron-withdrawing nature of chlorine, combined with its larger atomic radius compared to fluorine, which could enhance charge delocalization and slightly stabilize the electronic structure. In NBT3, the chlorine atoms are replaced with bromine, leading to a slightly reduced energy gap (2.577 eV) compared to NBT2. This reduction might be attributed to bromine’s higher polarizability, which enhances intermolecular interactions and slightly improves ICT. Additionally, their bulkiness induces minor distortions in dihedral angles, influencing conjugation and ICT47,48. Overall, the descending order of energy gap energies in this series is NBT1 > NBT2 > NBT3 > NBT4. The HOMO and LUMO energy levels of the reference compound MNNBT2 (Series 2) are calculated to be − 6.108 and − 2.304 eV resulting in an energy gap of 3.804 eV. In comparison, the designed compounds NBT5–NBT8 exhibit slightly deeper HOMO energy levels of -6.113, -6.115, -6.115, and − 6.127 eV. Similarly, the LUMO energy levels of the designed compounds NBT5–NBT8 are calculated to be − 3.555, − 3.608, − 3.638, and − 3.775 eV, respectively. Correspondingly, the ΔE are reduced to 2.558, 2.507, 2.477, and 2.352 eV. All the designed compounds in series 2 exhibit lower energy gap values compared to the reference compound MNNBT2 (ΔE = 3.804 eV), indicating improved intramolecular charge transfer, enhanced optoelectronic performance and supported their suitability for utilization as OSCs materials. The progressive reduction in energy gap across NBT5 to NBT8 is attributed to the incorporation of increasingly strong electron-withdrawing and conjugation-enhancing acceptor units. Specifically, NBT5 and NBT6 show reduced energy gaps due to the presence of –F and –Cl substituents, which possess high electronegativity and contribute to stronger LUMO stabilization. Among all, NBT8 exhibits the smallest band gap (2.352 eV), primarily due to the incorporation of –CN groups on the benzothiophene-based acceptor units, which significantly lower the LUMO energy level and facilitate easier HOMO–LUMO transitions49. The linear and relatively small –CN groups introduce minimal steric hindrance, allowing planarity and effective electron delocalization. In Series 3, the reference compound ND6 shows a energy gap of 3.780 eV with HOMO/LUMO levels at − 5.783 eV and − 2.003 eV, respectively19. All designed derivatives (NBT9–NBT12) exhibit reduced energy gaps due to the introduction of more electron-withdrawing end-capped acceptor units, which enhance intramolecular charge transfer. The corresponding energy gap values are 2.459 eV (NBT9), 2.407 eV (NBT10), 2.401 eV (NBT11), and 2.352 eV (NBT12), confirming a consistent narrowing trend. Among all the designed series, third series compounds showed least energy gap owing to the presence of conjugated A2 unit which improve the resonances and ICT resulting reduction in energy gap. Amidst third series compounds, NBT12 shows the best electronic performance, owing to the presence of –CN groups on the terminal acceptor. The cyano group enhances conjugation, extending the π-system and facilitating better electron delocalization. Overall, these results highlight that both electronic effects (electronegativity, electron-withdrawing ability) and structural features (size, bulkiness, polarizability, and planarity) critically influence the HOMO/LUMO energies, energy gaps, and ICT efficiency. Smaller, linear groups such as –CN facilitate effective conjugation and delocalization, while larger or bulkier substituents like –CF3, Cl, and Br modulate electronic properties through combined inductive effects and steric twisting, balancing stability and ICT.50.

Global reactivity parameters (GRPs)

The GRPs can be determine from the energies of the HOMO and LUMO, as they play a crucial role in determining the stability and chemical reactivity of the studied compounds. The global softness (σ)51, electron affinity (EA)52, global hardness (_η)53, global electrophilicity index (ω)54, chemical potential (µ), ionization potential (IP)55, and electronegativity (X) can be determined by utilizing Koopmans’s theorem (Table S30)56.

The stability of a chemical species was evaluated based on its global hardness (ƞ), which reflect its resistance to changes in electron density. Global hardness is closely associated with molecular stability and band gap (ΔE) while being inversely related to chemical reactivity. Higher ΔE values indicate that the chromophores are less reactive, harder, and more stable51. Reactivity and softness (σ) are directly correlated, with higher softness indicating greater reactivity and instability. IP measures the energy needed to remove an electron from the HOMO, while the energy required to add an electron to the LUMO is evaluated by EA. ΔNmax reflects the maximum electronic charge transferable in a charge transfer interaction57.

As shown in Table 2, the IP values for compounds of series 1 (NBT1-NBT4) follow the ascending order NBT1 < NBT3 < NBT2 < NBT4 indicating that NBT4 has the highest IP. This suggests that NBT4 is more stable and less reactive compared to other compounds, with NBT1 exhibiting the opposite trend. Furthermore, there is a direct correlation between the chemical potential (µ), stability and hardness (η) of a compound with its HOMO-LUMO energy gap. A compound with smaller energy gap is considered as softer, with lower stability and high reactivity and vice versa58. The descending order for the softness values of NBT1-NBT4 as: NBT4 > NBT3 > NBT2 > NBT1. Among the compounds studied, NBT4 demonstrates the lower energy gap (2.477 eV) along with lower hardness and higher softness, making it an effective OSC material with favorable photovoltaic characteristics.

Density of states (DOS)

The DOS analysis aims to examine electronic charge distribution across the molecular orbitals and providing a quantitative basis for interpreting the FMO outcomes61,62. This analysis is performed to access the individual contributions of each molecular fragment of NBT1-NBT12 to the overall charge distribution and absorption band63. The chromophores were separated into four distinct sections i.e. central core unit (π2), thiophene ring (π1), central acceptor (A2) and terminal acceptor (A1) which are displayed by pink, blue, green, and red. The electron density surrounding the HOMO and LUMO is affected by the presence of strong electron-withdrawing acceptor moieties which is seen clearly in DOS50. In DOS graphs, the valence band (HOMO) is represented on left side and the conduction band (LUMO) by right side values along the x-axis. The energy-gap is depicted by the distance between the HOMO and LUMO levels34,64. Similarly, Acceptor-2 contributes 33.9, 33.5, 33.6, and 32.8% to the HOMO, and 6.2, 6.2, 6.4, and 6.0% to the LUMO in NBT9–NBT12, respectively.

The π-spacer 1 contributes 7.1, 6.7, 6.8, and 5.9% to the HOMO, and 19.8, 19.8, 19.9, and 18.8% to the LUMO in NBT9–NBT12, respectively. These results suggest that the HOMOs of all designed molecules predominantly localize electronic populations on the π2-spacer and A2, while the LUMOs exhibit a shift of this population to the terminal acceptor regions. The DOS graphs strongly align with the FMO diagrams, further validating the electronic charge distribution patterns. The charge distribution pattern highlights significant transference of charge from the HOMO to the LUMO, making the compounds promising candidates for non-fullerene OSCs.

Transition density matrix (TDM)

Transition density matrix (TDM) analysis was conducted to evaluate the electronic transitions from the ground state (S0) to an excited state (S1) in designed compounds. Moreover, this analysis reveals donor–acceptor interactions, charge delocalization, and electron–hole coherence, which are key factors for assessing OSC performance71–73. Since H-atoms had little effect on transitions of interest, their contribution was disregarded. To conduct TDM analysis the designed compounds were separated into four parts: acceptor 1 (A1), acceptor 2 (A2), π-spacer 1, and π-spacer 2 as discussed in result and discussion. Since H-atoms had little effect on electronic transitions so, their contribution was neglected in this analysis. The x-axis and left y-axis in TDM heat maps indicate the number of atoms, whereas the electron density is shown on the right vertical axis. The segregation of electronic cloud areas of different parts in compounds is indicated by the variously colored lines. The TDM results for all the derivatives examined in this investigation are displayed in Fig. 5. The graphs clearly show that electron coherence successfully moved from π-bridge 1 in the direction of the terminal acceptors (A1), allowing electron density to flow across the end-capped acceptors. In all derivatives, charge mobility shows diagonally toward terminal acceptors. This efficient charge transfer results from the extended conjugation across the entire molecule, allowing the charge density to be effectively transferred to the other terminal acceptor groups.

Electron-hole analysis

Electron-hole analysis is a powerful tool for visualizing the spatial redistribution of electronic density during excitation74,75. In this method, excitation is described as the movement of an electron from the hole region to the electron region. This provides valuable insight into the nature of charge transfer and the extent of electron delocalization in a molecule74,76,77. In series 1 (NBT1–NBT3), hole density is primarily localized at the C35, C42, C47, and C51, while electron accumulation is observed at C45, C46, and C48 in NBT1–NBT3. For NBT4, hole density appears at C42, C37, C35, and C34, with electron density distributed over C45, C46, C48 and C53. The structures of entitled compounds with atom numbering are illustrated in Fig. 1. This trend indicates effective charge migration from the π-spacer toward the acceptor moieties across the series. In series 2 (NBT5–NBT8), the hole density on NBT5, NBT6, and NBT7 exhibited by C33, C32, and C40, with corresponding electron density at C35, C44 and C42 and C64 (terminal acceptor group). For NBT8, hole density is centered on C2 and C5 (naphthalene core), while electron density is concentrated on C39 (benzene ring bearing the cyano group) and C64, C66 (end-capped acceptor). Figure S1 shows pictorial representation of hole-electron transport analysis of NBT1-NBT12.These distributions suggest a directed charge transfer pathway toward the electron-withdrawing end groups. In series 3 (NBT9–NBT12), hole density mainly appears at C20 of azide linkage and at C29, C30 of pyrrole as well as at C38 and C39 atoms. The electron density is located on C40 and C44 of benzene ring fused with the pyrrole and C51 (thiophene ring), highlighting the role of fused ring systems in facilitating charge separation and transfer in these compounds. These observations collectively confirm the efficient intramolecular charge transfer from donor and π-bridge regions toward acceptor units in all three series.

Open circuit voltage (Voc) analysis

Open circuit voltage (Voc) analysis is a significant approach to evaluate the efficacy of OSCs78. It represents the maximum electric current that can be obtained from any optical material79. Several factors including light intensity, charge transfer efficiency, external fluorescence yield, electrodes work functions and operating temperature of photovoltaic devices significantly influence the Voc80,81. Voc is calculated as a difference between the HOMO of the donor polymer molecule and LUMO of the acceptor molecule. The Voc is strongly affected by the band gap between donor and acceptor moieties82. To achieve higher Voc values, the HOMO of the donor should be lower than the LUMO of the acceptor, resulting in smaller band gap83. In the current study for Voc calculations, the polymer PTB7 was used as donor moiety while the designed chromophores of each three series served as acceptor moieties. PTB7 is recognized as an efficient donor polymer molecule with the HOMO and LUMO energy levels of -6.467 eV and − 1.685 eV, respectively. The lowered HOMO of the PTB7 donor promotes better electron transport from the donor’s HOMO to the acceptor’s LUMO, thereby improving the photovoltaic properties of the OSCs. A direct relationship exists between the driving energy (∆E) which is the difference between the donor polymer’s HOMO and the acceptor’s LUMO and Voc. For designed molecules of series 2, the Voc values are arranged as follows: NBT8 < NBT7< NBT6 < NBT5. All of the molecules (NBT5–NBT8) have lower LUMO values, which means they will generate higher Voc and enhance the optical and electrical characteristics of OSCs. The optoelectronic properties of OSCs are improved by the improved electron transfer from the HOMO of PTB7 to the LUMO of acceptors (NBT5-NBT8), which is attributed to the reduced LUMO of the designed targets. The following decreasing order is achieved in the studied chromophores of series 3: NBT9 > NBT619> NBT11 > NBT12. The above discussion concludes that the series 3 compounds exhibit the highest (Voc) values compared to series 1 and 2 compounds. This is due to their lower HOMO energy levels, which enhance the driving force for charge separation and improve photovoltaic performance. According to observations, the PCE, FF, and ICT all rise as Voc does. According to the results above, NBT12 demonstrated the greatest energy and Voc values, giving it a reliable option for increasing solar device efficiency. In DFT, Voc is estimated from donor HOMO and acceptor LUMO levels, but is often overestimated compared to experimental values. This discrepancy arises from neglecting interfacial and environmental effects, so DFT results are mainly useful for qualitative trends.

Conclusion

To develop efficient photovoltaic materials, three series of naphthalene-1,5-diamine (NDA)-based chromophores (NBT1–NBT4, NBT5–NBT8, and NBT9–NBT12) with an A1–π1–A2–π2–A2–π1–A1 configuration were rationally designed through molecular engineering. The primary distinction among these series lies in the nature of the A1/A2 acceptor unit. By incorporating thiophene linkers and malononitrile/benzothiophene-based terminal acceptors bearing various electron-withdrawing substituents, the optoelectronic and photovoltaic behaviors of the designed molecules were effectively tuned. All the designed derivatives exhibited narrower energy gaps (2.3–2.7 eV) compared to their reference chromophores, with absorption maxima spanning the visible region (534.484–638.238 nm). The FMO, DOS, and TDM analyses collectively confirmed a pronounced ICT from the NDA donor core toward the terminal acceptors. Furthermore, the open-circuit voltage (Voc) analysis indicated comparable voltage values (2.392–2.741 V) when blended with the PTB7 donor polymer. Among all the studied systems, the third series (NBT9–NBT12) demonstrated superior optoelectronic performance owing to the presence of a more conjugated and electron-deficient A2 moiety. In particular, NBT12 exhibited the most promising photovoltaic characteristics, attributed to the strong electron-withdrawing cyano (–CN) group on the terminal acceptor, which effectively reduced the energy gap (2.323 eV), enhanced π-electron delocalization, and promoted stronger charge transfer through the thiophene bridge—resulting in improved light-harvesting efficiency in the visible region.
