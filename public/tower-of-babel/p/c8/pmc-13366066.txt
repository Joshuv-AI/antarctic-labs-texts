IFPHOR0069 The Global Opioid Access Divide: A Cross-Sectional and Longitudinal Ecological Analysis of Consumption Inequality, Health System Determinants, and Drug Composition Across 201 Countries, 2015–2023
Authors: Abdul Rhaman Kafagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobal opioid analgesic access is profoundly inequitable, with 5.5 billion people living in countries with little or no access to controlled medicines for pain and palliative care. Comprehensive multi-country analyses identifying modifiable health system drivers of this divide remain limited.MethodsA retrospective cross-sectional and longitudinal ecological analysis of country-level opioid consumption was conducted across 201 countries from 2015–2023. INCB narcotic drugs technical report data were linked to World Bank income classifications and WHO UHC Service Coverage Index scores. Inequality was quantified using population-weighted Gini coefficients and Lorenz curves. Three nested ordinary least squares regression models identified health system predictors of access, and piecewise regression characterised a structural breakpoint in the UHC-access relationship.ResultsGlobal opioid consumption inequality exceeded national income inequality in any single nation (Gini=0.892). Median consumption differed 761-fold between high-income and low-income countries (1,374.6 vs 1.81 S-DDD per million per day). The UHC index was the strongest independent predictor of access (β = 0.069, p < 0.001), outperforming GDP. A structural breakpoint was identified at UHC score 78.4, above which each additional UHC point produced 22-fold greater access gains. No convergence between income groups occurred over nine years. Low-income country formularies were disproportionately reliant on pethidine, an agent not recommended by WHO for chronic pain.ConclusionsOpioid access is determined by health system architecture, not income alone. The UHC threshold of 78.4 provides a concrete policy target, below which foundational investment in prescribing infrastructure, supply chains, and controlled substance regulation is required before marginal improvements can yield meaningful access gains.
