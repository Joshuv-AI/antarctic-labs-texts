Minimally invasive lateral tubular approach for revision of failed lumbar interbody fusion: illustrative cases
Authors: Diana Chang, Yan Zhou, Dean Chou, Daniel C. Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDInterbody cage failure is associated with loss of disc height, nerve compression, and pseudarthrosis, and often requires reoperation. Revision of failed interbody cages is challenging due to subsidence of the cage into the adjacent endplates, causing bony defects and granulation tissue, resulting in difficult exposure and a higher risk of complications.OBSERVATIONSIn this case series, the authors present 2 cases of symptomatic interbody cage failure successfully revised using a minimally invasive lateral approach. The first case utilized a transpsoas lateral approach to replace a failed L2–3 cage from prior transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion. The second case used an anterior-to-psoas oblique lateral approach to revise a subsided L3 corpectomy cage. Postoperatively, both patients had significant symptom improvement.LESSONSA lateral lumbar interbody approach—with its direct access to disc space, possibility for a relatively large-footprint cage while avoiding scar tissue from a prior approach, and favorable safety profile—is a promising option for the revision of failed interbody cages. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25750

Interbody cage subsidence and migration are associated with loss of disc height, nerve compression, and pseudarthrosis, with up to one-third of patients requiring revision surgeries.1–3 Revision of failed interbody cages is challenging due to entrapment of cage into adjacent endplates, bony defects, and scar tissue from previous surgeries.4,5

Lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF) is a surgical technique that permits access to the spinal column through retroperitoneal corridors, allowing the placement of a large-footprint interbody cage.6 Depending on the access trajectory in relation to the psoas muscle, this approach can be classified into either the transpsoas technique or the anterior-to-psoas technique, also known as oblique lumbar interbody fusion. LLIF has been utilized for various clinical indications, including neural decompression, restoration of disc height, and correction of spondylolisthesis.6–8 It has demonstrated several advantages, including direct access to disc space, a favorable safety profile, and short hospital stays.9, 10

Here, we present 2 cases of symptomatic interbody cage failure successfully revised using a lateral approach. We describe our techniques and review the current literature to provide insights into selecting revision strategies.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

Preoperative Presentation

The patient is a 62-year-old female with a history of osteoarthritis, osteopenia, lumbar stenosis, and L2–3 adjacent segment disease. She had undergone three prior surgeries. She initially had L3–S1 transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) with pelvic fixation using iliac wing screws for stenosis. Three years later, she had L5–S1 heterotrophic ossification along with L3–4 screws at the cortical surface, causing increasing radiculopathy; L3–S1 hardware was removed followed by L3–S1 allograft arthrodesis. Ten years after the second surgery, she underwent minimally invasive (MIS) L2–3 TLIF (MIS-TLIF) and posterolateral fusion for adjacent segment disease. Two years after that, the patient presented to us with progressive back pain, bilateral radiculopathy, and one episode of bowel incontinence. Preoperative imaging revealed L2–3 pseudarthrosis, spondylolisthesis, and graft subsidence (Fig. 1A–D). Because of the neurological symptoms and radiological instability, a decision was made to proceed with L2–3 LLIF to replace the subsided TLIF interbody cage and L1–4 posterolateral fusion. Operation

For stage 1, the patient was placed in the lateral decubitus position with the right side up. The bed was appropriately angled with pelvic crest overlying the break of the bed to accentuate the space between the 12th rib and the pelvis. An incision was made overlying the L2–3 disc space followed by serial dilation with tubular retractors. Expandable tubular retractor was docked through the psoas muscle, allowing direct visualization of the disc space under microscopy. The disc was entered with a disc knife, and discectomy was carried out with pituitary and Kerrison rongeurs. The expandable interbody cage was encountered loose within the disc space (Fig. 1E). Rather than reengaging the expansion mechanism of the expandable cage, which was inaccessible due to access limitations, the cage was rotated sideways for a smaller dimension to facilitate extraction. With fluoroscopic localization, a Cobb instrument was utilized to release the contralateral annulus and further retrieve the disc. A 12-mm LLIF cage was selected and inserted into the L2–3 disc space under fluoroscopy along with bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), allograft, and autograft (Fig. 1F). Closure was accomplished in a standard manner (Video 1).

For stage 2, the patient was turned to a prone position for L1–4 posterior spinal fusion through the prior midline incision. Prior L2–3 screws were loose and removed. Notably, there was an eroded pedicle screw trajectory at the L2–3 levels, requiring further drilling to expand the screw depth. Under intraoperative navigation, pedicle screws were placed at L1–4 with cement augmentation. After confirming appropriate screw placement with intraoperative CT, arthrodesis was performed with a mixture of allograft, autograft, and BMP. An appropriately sized rod was then introduced with an offset connector to accommodate the right L3 screw (Fig. 1G). Hemostasis was achieved and multilayered closure was performed. Neuromonitoring was utilized throughout the case with no evidence of nerve root irritation. Triggered EMG thresholds for all pedicle screws were above 20 mA.

Postoperative Course

At the 6-month postoperative follow-up, the patient reported significant improvement of pain, strength, and ambulatory capability compared to before surgery. Physical examination revealed full strength and normal sensation of bilateral lower extremities and normal gait. Radiographic imaging at 6 months demonstrated stable hardware and resolution of prior L2–3 anterolisthesis (Fig. 1H and I).

Case 2

Preoperative Presentation

The patient is a 62-year-old female with a history of L3 metastatic pancreatic neuroendocrine carcinoma and L3 pathological fracture. She had a surgical history of L3–4 minimally invasive decompression using METRx, followed by L3 transpedicular corpectomy with cage placement and L1–5 posterior spinal fusion. Approximately 1.5 years after the last surgery, she presented with worsened low back pain radiating to her lower extremities associated with paresthesia. Imaging revealed bilateral rod fractures at L3 and cage subsidence with posterior retropulsion of the residual L3 vertebral body resulting in stenosis (Fig. 2A–D). Operation

For stage 1, the patient was positioned right side up. Because of the patient’s history of prior abdominal surgery, vascular surgery was involved for exposure. An oblique incision was made along the flank superior to the T12 rib. The peritoneal cavity and retroperitoneum were mobilized medially and anteriorly. The inferior vena cava was slightly displaced anteriorly to expose the anterior vertebral body. The psoas muscle was then dissected at the front edge and reflected posteriorly to the transverse process of the L3 vertebral body. After exposure, L2–3 and L3–4 discectomy was performed. The expandable cage was noted to be fully expanded and subsided into the inferior L2 endplate (Fig. 2E). The cage was rotated and removed with a pituitary rongeur. Contralateral annular release was then carried out, and an appropriately sized expandable lateral lumbar cage was packed and placed into the L3 interbody. Expansion of the cage was performed under fluoroscopy (Fig. 2F). Further packing of the corpectomy space was performed with allograft and BMP. Hemostasis was achieved and the psoas muscle was replaced. Closure was completed in a standard fashion.

For stage 2, the patient was placed in a prone position for L1–5 posterior spinal fusion. The prior pedicle screws maintained good purchase in proper position as confirmed by intraoperative CT and were therefore reused. The broken rods were replaced to reduce and align the fracture. Arthrodesis was performed (Fig. 2G).

Postoperative Course

The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 4. At the 12-week postoperative follow-up, she showed good recovery with resolution of her preoperative lower extremity paresthesia, improved pain, and good ambulation. Physical examination revealed full strength and intact sensation. Postoperative radiographs revealed appropriate spinal alignment with intact hardware (Fig. 2H and I). The patient also underwent a PET CT study for cancer surveillance, which revealed no evidence of hardware-related compression of the thecal sac (Fig. 3). FIG. 3.Case 2. Discussion

Subsidence of lumbar interbody cages is a fairly common complication with an overall 13%–27% subsidence rate across different fusion techniques.3 Subsidence leads to nerve compression secondary to loss of disc and foraminal height or implant migration or malposition, failure of spinal fusion, and spinal instability and deformity, resulting in persistent or recurrent symptoms and poor clinical outcomes.

Observations

Based on the access trajectories, revision techniques for failed interbody cages can be categorized into anterior,11–15 posterior,16 and lateral5,16–19 approaches. The anterior approach is most frequently used. Safaee et al. reported on 84 patients who underwent anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) with cage removal (majority at L4–5 and L5–S1) for pseudarthrosis following a prior TLIF.14 With a left paramedian incision, they performed retroperitoneal dissection with mobilization of iliac vessels and ligation of middle sacral, iliolumbar, and lumbar segmental vessels to expose the disc. In another case report, Haider and colleagues described a modified ALIF approach performed in a lateral decubitus position through an incision above the inguinal ligament to remove an L5–S1 TLIF cage.11

An anterior approach allows placement of a wide-footprint cage to restore lumbar lordosis while preserving the paravertebral muscles, which reduces postoperative pain.20 However, an anterior approach requires significant vascular mobilization, which raises the risk of vascular complications. Indeed, Safaee et al. reported that 2 patients with prior abdominal surgeries had a tearing injury to the iliac vein during mobilization and retraction of the vessel.14 A meta-analysis of 261 patients with vascular lesions from spine surgeries showed that the highest number of injuries happened with anterior approaches (59%), followed by posterior approaches (33%), and the least from lateral approaches (8%).20 The risk of vascular injury is particularly high in the setting of prior abdominal surgeries due to tissue adhesion and chronic inflammation.14

A posterior approach can also be used for cage removal, although this is less common. One study reported on a patient with posterior migration of an L5–S1 TLIF cage.16 They utilized a high-speed burr and performed an osteotomy to retrieve the cage posteriorly, followed by replacement with a new cage via posterior lumbar interbody fusion. A posterior revision approach has the advantage of achieving direct nerve decompression and, when posterior fusion is needed, allows cage replacement and posterior instrumentation through a single incision.14 However, in the setting of prior posterior surgeries, as in our cases, altered anatomical landmarks and epidural fibrosis pose significant challenges for dural retraction and nerve mobilization.16 More importantly, failed prior posterior fusion can cause a compromised biomechanical environment, increasing the risk of pseudarthrosis in repeat posterior surgery.19

Lessons

MIS Tubular Lateral Approach for Failed Lumbar Interbody Fusion Revision

While lateral approaches for cage revision have been described in the literature,17–19 our cases highlight distinct technical refinements. Unlike earlier reports that involved aggressive instrumentation with osteotomes17 or bilateral retraction,19 which increases the risk of nerve injury, we achieved cage retrieval through precise exposure under direct microscopic visualization. In case 1, we optimized the operative space by adjusting the table angle and patient positioning. We then utilized an MIS transpsoas lateral technique facilitated by a unilateral tubular retractor system. The old expandable cage was tamped to a smaller footprint and removed in two pieces to achieve safe cage removal without the need for complex extraction maneuvers. In case 2, we used an anterior-to-psoas approach to acquire a fresh corridor away from prior abdominal and spinal surgeries. In both cases, we further reinforced the lateral fusion with posterior instrumentation, as data have shown that LLIF combined with posterior fixation has a significantly lower subsidence rate compared to LLIF alone.3 Together, we illustrated refined lateral techniques for cage revision adaptable to patients with a complex surgical history and anatomy.

In general, a lateral revision approach offers several advantages compared to anterior or posterior approaches. By creating a new surgical corridor, it avoids scar tissue and fibrosis from previous posterior procedures.19 Similar to anterior approaches, it provides direct access to the interbody cage and allows placement of a large-footprint cage that spans the apophyseal ring. This permits greater contact area to enhance fusion, help restore proper disc height, and reduce the risk of future subsidence.3,17,19,21 In contrast to anterior approaches, the lateral decubitus position takes advantage of gravitational retraction, allowing a natural exposure to the surgical field, thus reducing the risk of injury to surrounding structures.11,22,23 Furthermore, in cases in which removing the old cage is not feasible due to severe subsidence, a lateral approach offers the trajectory to place an additional cage to achieve stabilization.

Limitations of the Lateral Approach

Complications of the lateral approach include nerve and vascular injuries, although the latter is less frequent than in anterior approaches.11,24 A meta-analysis comparing the anterior-to-psoas and transpsoas approaches revealed that the transpsoas approach has a higher risk of sensorimotor complications, whereas the anterior-to-psoas approach has a higher risk of vascular complications and sympathetic nerve injuries.24

Several measures can be implemented to address the limitations. In patients with prior abdominal surgeries, vascular surgeons should be involved during an anterolateral approach, as in case 2, to prepare for anatomical changes caused by prior operations. Additionally, minimizing retraction time and using neuromonitoring with somatosensory and motor evoked potentials are crucial for reducing the risk of nerve injuries.

Factors Influencing the Selection of Surgical Approach

Tanaka et al. proposed a symptom-based classification system for posterior cage migration to guide the selection of revision strategies with mild radicular symptoms being managed with posterior approaches and those with more severe radicular symptoms managed with an anterior approach.16

While clinical symptoms are a critical metric in choosing treatments, there are more nuances to the decision-making process, including careful evaluation of radiographic findings such as the presence of disc height loss or spondylolisthesis, which may not be directly reflected in symptomology at the moment but can pose significant future risks, therefore warranting more aggressive interventions. For instance, in patients with spinal malalignment, like in these cases, revision surgery should restore lumbar lordosis with a large cage to facilitate a stable biomechanical environment for successful fusion.25

Furthermore, the size and shape of the initial cage also play an important role in the selection of revision approach. In our first case, due to subsidence, the old TLIF cage exhibited an uneven wedge shape, with the anterior side taller than the posterior (Fig. 1C and D), making it difficult to pull the cage out through a posterior route. Similarly, the tall corpectomy cage in the second case also posed challenges for posterior retrieval.

In conclusion, lateral approaches are a highly adaptable technique, offering direct access to the disc space, the ability to place a large-footprint cage for enhanced fusion and stabilization, and fresh operative corridors away from scar tissue. These advantages make it a promising option for revising failed interbody cages following prior posterior surgeries. Ultimately, the surgical planning requires a thorough consideration of the presenting symptoms, radiographic findings, size and shape of the original cage, and involved levels.
