Symptomatic nodular sarcoid myopathy: A rare extrapulmonary manifestation
Authors: Siu Fung Mak, Wing Ho Chong, On Chee Angela Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSarcoidosis is a multisystem disorder with noncaseating granulomatous inflammation. Symptomatic sarcoid myopathy is rare and its clinical manifestation is commonly nonspecific. We present a case that illustrates how magnetic resonance imaging can help with the diagnosis of sarcoid myopathy and determine its extent of involvement.Keywords: Sarcoid myopathy, Sarcoidosis, Myopathy

Introduction

Sarcoidosis is a multisystem disorder with noncaseating granulomatous inflammation. According to the American Thoracic Society [1], the diagnosis of sarcoidosis is based on 3 major criteria: compatible clinical presentation, noncaseating granulomatous inflammation in tissue samples, and exclusion of alternative causes of granulomatous disease.

Asymptomatic muscle involvement is reported to involve more than 50% of patients with sarcoidosis [2], yet symptomatic sarcoid myopathy is rare [3]. Muscle involvement results from noncaseating granulomas in the perimysial connective tissue, and muscle fiber destruction by infiltration and eventual fibrosis [4]. Differential diagnoses of myopathy in these patients include corticosteroid myopathy, polymyositis/dermatomyositis and necrotizing autoimmune myopathy. However, the clinical manifestation of symptomatic sarcoid myopathy is commonly nonspecific, including muscle weakness, myalgia, palpable nodules and fever.

We present a case that illustrates how magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can provide crucial information about the nature of myopathy and the extent of muscle involvement, both of which are helpful in the diagnosis of sarcoid myopathy.

Case report

A 60-year-old Chinese man presented to an outpatient clinic for unintentional weight loss. He was a smoker and worked as an interior construction worker. He had no significant past medical or surgical history. He had no known family history of sarcoidosis, tuberculosis or malignancy. He complained of unintentional weight loss of 5kg within 2 months.

A chest radiograph (Fig. 1) revealed thickening of the right paratracheal stripe and enlargement of bilateral hilar shadows. Erythrocyte sedimentation rate was 12mm/hour (normal: 2-19mm/hour) and C-reactive protein was 2.0mg/L (normal: ≤5.0mg/L). Serum calcium was 2.41mmol/L (normal: 2.10-2.65mmol/L). Serum angiotensin converting enzyme level test was not performed.

A posteroanterior chest radiograph of a 60-year-old average build male showing thickening of the right paratracheal stripe (dashed arrow) and enlargement of bilateral hilar shadows (arrows).

Subsequent computed tomography (CT) (Fig. 2) confirmed mediastinal and bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, with tiny perifissural lung nodules. In view of weight loss and lymphadenopathy, endobronchial ultrasound-guided mediastinal lymph node biopsy was performed and it revealed noncaseating granulomatous inflammation with no acid fast bacteria or fungal organism. Polymerase chain reactions of these biopsy samples were negative for mycobacterium tuberculosis.

A contrast enhanced CT Thorax of a 60-year-old male performed for evaluation of thickening of right paratracheal stripe and enlargement of bilateral hilar shadows in chest radiograph (A) Coronal reformat showing mediastinal (dashed arrows) and hilar lymphadenopathy (arrow) and (B) Axial lung window CT Thorax showing tiny perifissural lung nodules (arrows).

Before the diagnosis of sarcoidosis, he completed an empirical antituberculosis drug regimen due to the high prevalence of tuberculosis in Hong Kong. Post-treatment CT showed stable mediastinal and bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy and perifissural lung nodules compared to previous CT. He fulfilled all 3 major criteria for diagnosis of sarcoidosis according to American Thoracic Society [1]: compatible clinical presentation, noncaseating granulomatous inflammation in tissue samples, and exclusion of alternative causes of granulomatous disease. Pulmonary sarcoidosis was diagnosed but the patient refused to start corticosteroid treatment due to reluctance over side effects.

At a routine clinic follow-up 4 years later, he presented with a mildly painful lump over his left leg. The painful lump developed 3 years after the initial diagnosis of pulmonary sarcoidosis, with no history of trauma to his left leg. No discharge was noted from the lump and the overlying skin appeared normal. Ultrasound (Fig. 3) showed an intramuscular heterogeneously hypoechoic lesion with intralesional vascularity involving the left leg flexor digitorum longus muscle. Contrast-enhanced MRI of the left leg (Fig. 4A, B and C) revealed multiple T2-weighted (T2W) hyperintense, enhancing intramuscular lesions with perilesional muscle edema. The lesions showed nonenhancing centers which were hypointense on both T1-weighted (T1W) and T2W sequences, resembling a “dark-star” pattern. These were elongated in configuration that appeared to follow the long axis of muscle fibers, with central hypointense stripe and a hyperintense stripe on each side, resembling the “three stripes sign”

(D) Coronal maximal intensity projection of whole body diffusion-weighted imaging showing multiple intramuscular lesions (arrows) with lower extremity predilection and symmetrical configuration, resembling the “tiger man” appearance.

Whole body diffusion-weighted imaging was performed with coronal maximum intensity projection (MIP) (Fig. 4D). The MIP algorithm displayed only the voxel with highest signal intensity onto a 2-dimensional image, providing better depiction of spatial distribution of muscle involvement. It showed multiple intramuscular lesions with lower extremity predilection and symmetrical configuration, resembling “tiger man” appearance. Overall findings are in favour of a diffuse inflammatory myopathy, with sarcoid myopathy as the most likely differential diagnosis.

Sarcoid myopathy was histopathologically confirmed by ultrasound-guided biopsy of the symptomatic left leg intramuscular lesion, which showed noncaseating granulomas composed of collections of histiocytes and reactive lymphoid infiltrates.

Diagnosis and corticosteroid treatment was explained to the patient, but he refused to start corticosteroid treatment. Current management plan is to monitor the extent of sarcoidosis with interval follow-up chest radiographs and self-monitoring of the left leg mass. The left leg mass remains stable in size 1 year after the diagnosis of sarcoid myopathy and is now no longer painful. The patient will continue his follow-ups in the outpatient clinic.

Discussion

Sarcoidosis is a multisystem disorder with noncaseating granulomatous inflammation. This case illustrates a rare manifestation of sarcoidosis as symptomatic nodular sarcoid myopathy, a condition reported infrequently in literature. While asymptomatic muscular granulomas are found in more than 50% of patients with sarcoidosis on autopsy [2], symptomatic sarcoid myopathy occurs in a small minority [3], estimated at 0.5%-2.5% [5]. The nodular subtype is particularly uncommon.

Ultrasound is commonly used as the first-line imaging modality to evaluate muscle swelling. In sarcoid myopathy, it may demonstrate intramuscular hypoechoic nodules with well-defined margins [6]. However, it is not specific for sarcoid myopathy. It primarily serves to localize and guide further biopsy.

MRI is regarded as the best imaging modality to evaluate suspected sarcoid myopathy, due to its excellent soft tissue contrast and ability to accurately delineate extent and distribution of muscle involvement. Different patterns of sarcoid myopathy are described, including nodular and myopathic (acute and chronic) types. Nodular sarcoid myopathy has characteristic appearance in MRI, similar to our case, with a star-shaped hypointense center with hyperintense signal at its periphery in both T2W and postcontrast T1W images. This is described as the “dark star” pattern and histopathological correlation shows central fibrosis and surrounding granulomatous inflammation [7]. In coronal and sagittal images, an elongated nodule with inner hypointense and outer hyperintense signals on each side was described as “three stripes sign” [7]. After corticosteroid therapy, the peripheral T2W hyperintense enhancing areas typically subside, while the central hypointense fibrosis persists on follow-up MRI [8]. Acute and chronic myopathic types of sarcoid myopathy may not always show abnormality in MRI [9]. Chronic myopathic type may show nonspecific muscle atrophy and fatty infiltration.

F-18 fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET-CT is very sensitive for metabolically active granulomatous inflammation, with sensitivity ranging 94%-100% [10]. It may reveal FDG avid lymphadenopathy and multiple elongated areas of muscle uptake. The pattern of elongated skeletal muscle uptake involving upper and lower extremities is described as the “tiger man” sign [11]. Our case illustrates how whole body diffusion-weighted imaging can demonstrate similar findings in sarcoid myopathy, where there are multiple elongated areas of muscle edema in upper and lower extremities in symmetrical distribution. Whole body diffusion-weighted imaging also has advantages of superior spatial resolution and lack of ionizing radiation as compared to FDG PET-CT.

While there are no published, large scale studies reporting the sensitivity and specificity of “dark star sign”, “three stripes sign” and “tiger man sign” on MRI in diagnosing sarcoid myopathy, available case reports and review articles suggest that these may be specific, but not sensitive signs for sarcoid myopathy [12]. Further investigation into the diagnostic accuracies of these MRI signs is warranted.

Given the imaging modalities available, ultrasound should be the first imaging modality for patients presenting with muscle swelling and clinical suspicion of sarcoid myopathy. Ultrasound findings may be used to guide biopsy. MRI should be the next step in the diagnostic pathway. The presence of “dark star sign”, “three stripes sign” or “tiger man sign” on MRI supports the diagnosis of sarcoid myopathy in appropriate clinical context, and may reduce the need for further invasive investigations. FDG PET-CT can be performed for cases where assessment of systemic disease activity or occult organ involvement is necessary.

The take-home message of this case is to illustrate the characteristic “dark star sign”, “three stripes sign” and “tiger man sign” of sarcoid myopathy on MRI, as these can help differentiate sarcoid myopathy from other types of myopathy.
