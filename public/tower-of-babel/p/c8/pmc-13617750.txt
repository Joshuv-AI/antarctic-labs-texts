Multi-cohort consensus clustering identifies three distinct transcriptomic endotypes in sepsis
Authors: Naixun Chi, Siyu Mu, Xin Jin, Gangyu Zhang, Min Gao, Yurou Guo, Yuelong Chen, Yong Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis is a heterogeneous syndrome in which patients with the same clinical diagnosis may harbour different host immune responses. Existing transcriptomic endotyping frameworks differ in gene selection, clustering, and cohort composition, limiting comparability. Few studies have distinguished endotype-intrinsic prognostic signal from baseline severity, and most prior frameworks relied on curated immune-gene panels or single-cohort discovery.MethodsWe performed consensus clustering on 1002 sepsis patients from 5 discovery cohorts after quantile normalisation, ComBat batch correction, and data-driven feature selection (122 genes with median absolute deviation ≥ 1.0 from a 2456-gene set common to nine platforms), using PAM clustering with Pearson correlation distance (k = 2–10, 1000 bootstrap iterations) and composite metric-based k selection. Endotype–mortality associations were assessed by logistic regression and, where severity scores were available, by SOFA-adjusted models; a LASSO multinomial classifier evaluated generalisability across 4 external cohorts.ResultsThree endotypes were resolved at optimal k = 3: C1 (Immune Activation, n = 306, 30.5%), C2 (Interferon Response, n = 447, 44.6%), and C3 (Erythroid Dysregulation, n = 249, 24.9%). Mortality differed across endotypes (logistic regression likelihood ratio ), with C2 showing lower 28-day mortality than C1 (15.4% vs. 26.1%; OR = 0.52, 95% CI , ). Crucially, SOFA-adjusted analysis in GSE185263 (n = 342, the only discovery cohort with SOFA scores) fully attenuated the C2 advantage (adjusted OR = 0.99, p = 0.98), indicating that in this cohort endotype-mortality associations are primarily explained by baseline severity. A LASSO-regularised multinomial classifier achieved >98% per-class sensitivity and generalised to 4 external cohorts (n = 412) with 100% gene coverage and mean classification confidence 0.86−0.94. Longitudinal analysis of 129 patients across four multi-timepoint datasets demonstrated 66.7% endotype stability (Cohen’s κ = 0.49).ConclusionsThree severity-aligned transcriptomic patterns—immune activation, interferon response, and an underrecognised erythroid/metabolic axis—recur across heterogeneous sepsis cohorts. In the one cohort with organ-dysfunction scores, the C2 mortality advantage was fully attenuated after SOFA adjustment, indicating that—in this cohort—endotype–mortality associations are largely explained by baseline severity rather than independent prognostic biology; whether this generalises requires cohorts with harmonised severity data. Future endotyping studies should incorporate systematic severity adjustment before drawing prognostic or treatment-stratification conclusions.Keywords: Sepsis, Transcriptomic endotypes, Consensus clustering, Multi-cohort integration, Severity adjustment, LASSO classifier, Precision medicine

Background

Sepsis, defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1], remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, accounting for an estimated 11 million deaths annually [2]. Despite decades of large-scale randomised controlled trials, no immunomodulatory therapy has demonstrated consistent benefit across unselected sepsis populations. A principal explanation for these failures is biological heterogeneity: patients who meet the same clinical criteria for sepsis may have fundamentally different underlying immune states, such that a therapy that benefits one subgroup may be ineffective or even harmful in another [3–5].

This recognition has motivated the search for molecular subtypes, or endotypes, that capture biologically homogeneous patient subgroups. Whole-blood transcriptomics has emerged as the primary platform for endotyping because it reflects the systemic host immune response and is readily obtainable in the ICU setting. Several influential frameworks have been proposed. Davenport et al. identified two sepsis response signatures (SRS1 and SRS2) in the GAinS cohort, with SRS1 characterised by an immunosuppressive profile associated with higher early mortality [6]. Scicluna et al. analysed the MARS cohort (GSE65682) and described four genomic endotypes (Mars1–Mars4), where Mars1 was associated with the worst outcomes and a pattern of innate immune activation with adaptive immune suppression [7]. Seymour et al. derived four clinical phenotypes (α , β , γ , δ ) from electronic health record data, demonstrating that clinical and molecular classifications capture complementary dimensions of heterogeneity [8].

More recently, Scicluna et al. [9] proposed a consensus blood transcriptomic framework that aligns MARS and SRS classifications into three consensus transcriptomic subtypes (CTS1–CTS3) using an 18-gene classifier. In parallel, the SUBSPACE consortium developed the Human Immune Dysregulation Evaluation Framework (HI-DEF), which quantifies cell-type-specific myeloid and lymphoid dysregulation across 37 cohorts and defines four immune dysregulation states [10]. However, a comparative analysis by van Amstel et al. found limited concordance between clinical, biomarker, and transcriptomic subtype systems (Cramér’s V = 0.09−0.46), indicating that no single framework has yet achieved consensus [11].

Despite this progress, several gaps remain. First, most endotyping studies rely on a single discovery cohort, raising concerns about cohort-specific artefacts. Second, gene selection is often based on curated immune panels or arbitrary variance thresholds (e.g., “top N most variable genes”), which may bias clustering toward pre-specified biological themes. Third, few studies have applied non-circular effect-size estimation to quantify the true biological separation between endotypes. Fourth, endotype temporal stability—whether a patient’s endotype changes over the course of illness—has received limited attention.

Here, we address these gaps using a multi-cohort consensus clustering approach that differs from existing frameworks in three key respects. First, unlike frameworks built on curated immune panels or pre-specified gene lists (e.g., CTS, MARS, SRS), we apply variance-based, data-driven gene selection across a multi-cohort discovery design; this approach is well positioned to capture major axes of transcriptomic heterogeneity, including an erythroid/metabolic axis under-represented in immune-focused panels. Second, multi-cohort discovery (five datasets, four microarray platforms) reduces the single-cohort bias inherent in frameworks derived from a single study. Third, non-circular effect-size estimation on held-out genes provides validation evidence independent of the clustering features themselves. We integrate 5 publicly available whole-blood transcriptomic datasets (1002 sepsis patients) with data-driven feature selection (median absolute deviation ≥ 1.0), Pearson correlation distance, and composite metric-based k selection. We characterise the resulting transcriptomic patterns—which are associated with disease severity—across 4 independent datasets (n = 412) and examine their temporal stability in longitudinal cohorts, highlighting an erythroid/metabolic axis under-represented in existing immune-focused frameworks whose biological versus technical origin we examine explicitly (Tables 1, 2).

Preprocessing and batch correction

Each dataset was independently preprocessed. Microarray probe identifiers were mapped to HGNC gene symbols using platform-specific annotation packages from Bioconductor. Where multiple probes mapped to the same gene, the probe with the highest mean expression across samples was retained. Expression values were log-transformed if the maximum intensity exceeded 100 (indicating linear-scale data). Per-dataset quantile normalisation was applied using the preprocessCore package. Samples with >20% missing values and genes with >50% missing values were excluded during quality control.

The intersection of genes measured across all nine platforms (five discovery and four validation) yielded 2456 common genes, ensuring that all features used in clustering are available for downstream validation. These common genes formed the input for ComBat batch correction [19], applied with dataset as the batch variable (5 levels) and parametric priors (par.prior = TRUE). No biological covariates, and no outcome variable, were included (batch correction was therefore outcome-blinded) to avoid removing biological signal of interest or inducing outcome-related bias. Post-correction PCA confirmed that the dominant batch effect was eliminated and datasets were fully intermixed (Supplementary Figure S1).

Feature selection

Genes were ranked by median absolute deviation (MAD) across all 1002 samples in the batch-corrected expression matrix. All genes with MAD ≥ 1.0 were selected for clustering, yielding 122 genes (MAD range: 1.00−3.35). This threshold-based approach requires no prior annotation and captures all genes with substantial cross-patient variability. Importantly, 1.0 was not chosen arbitrarily: inspection of the MAD distribution across all 2456 genes revealed a natural inflection point near MAD = 1.0, corresponding to the 94.4th percentile of gene-level variability (Supplementary Figure S3). To provide quantitative rather than visual justification, we repeated the full clustering pipeline across MAD thresholds of 0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1, and 1.2, recording the resulting gene count, PAC, mean Pearson silhouette, and the adjusted Rand index (ARI) of the relabelled solution against the MAD = 1.0 reference (Supplementary Figure S10; Supplementary Table S2). Two additional specificity checks were performed at MAD ≥ 1.0: (i) split-half gene set reproducibility, quantified as the Jaccard overlap of the selected gene sets between 100 random equal halves of the samples, and (ii) a negative control comparing the observed PAC and silhouette against 50 randomly drawn gene sets matched in size, with empirical p-values from the random distribution using the add-one estimator (p = (b+1)/(m+1), where b is the number of random sets at least as favourable as the observed statistic in the relevant direction—PAC ≤ observed PAC and silhouette ≥ observed silhouette—and m = 50).

Consensus clustering

Consensus clustering was performed using ConsensusClusterPlus (v1.62.0) [20] implementing the framework of Monti et al. [21], with the following parameters: PAM (partitioning around medoids) algorithm, Pearson correlation distance, k = 2–10, 1000 resampling iterations with 80% item subsampling (pItem = 0.8) and full feature inclusion (pFeature = 1.0). The 80% item resampling rate follows the recommendation of Monti et al. [21] as a balance between consensus matrix stability and computational efficiency; full feature inclusion ensures that the gene-level contribution to cluster structure is consistent across iterations. PAM (partitioning around medoids) was selected a priori rather than k-means because it operates directly on the Pearson-correlation dissimilarity matrix (k-means is restricted to Euclidean geometry), is more robust to outliers through medoid- rather than centroid-based assignment, and is the algorithm implemented in ConsensusClusterPlus. We note that k-means achieved a marginally higher Pearson silhouette (0.222 vs. 0.208; see the section on alternative clustering algorithms); PAM was retained for robustness and correlation-space compatibility rather than to maximise any single internal index, and the two algorithms agreed on 81.9% of assignments (ARI = 0.531).

Optimal k was determined by a rank-based composite score integrating: (i) PAC, defined as the fraction of sample pairs with consensus indices between 0.1 and 0.9 (lower is better); (ii) mean silhouette width computed on a Pearson correlation distance matrix (1 - r; higher is better); and (iii) CDF Δ AUC, quantifying the marginal gain in consensus from adding a cluster (higher delta is better, with elbow detection). Each metric was independently ranked across k = 2–10, and the mean rank across all three metrics determined the optimal k. Candidate solutions producing any cluster with <5% of total samples were penalised. The k = 3 solution achieved the lowest composite rank. Additionally, the gap statistic (100 bootstrap references, firstSEmax criterion) [22] and per-endotype silhouette widths were computed. Leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) cross-validation was performed: for each of the 5 discovery datasets, the remaining 4 datasets were used for MAD-based feature selection and ConsensusClusterPlus, and the held-out dataset was classified by centroid correlation. Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) between LODO-predicted and original endotype assignments quantified cross-cohort reproducibility. Gaussian mixture modelling (Mclust, G = 2–8) was used as an alternative clustering comparison.

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression was performed using limma (v3.54.0) [23] with one-versus-all contrasts for each endotype. Significance thresholds were and Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p < 0.05.

Non-circular effect-size estimation

Cohen’s d (one-vs-rest) was computed on 100 genes randomly sampled from those not included in the 122-gene clustering feature set or the differential expression gene lists (2456 − 122 − 116 marker genes eligible genes). Random selection avoids the bias inherent in choosing genes by variability. Bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were derived from 1000 resampling iterations. Statistical significance was assessed by permutation testing (1000 iterations) in which cluster labels were randomly shuffled.

Pathway enrichment analysis

GO biological process and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed using limma::goana and limma::kegga [23] with the universe defined as all 2456 genes. Significance was defined as FDR < 0.05.

Survival analysis

The primary survival analysis used logistic regression (mortality ∼ endotype + dataset) applied to all 1002 discovery samples, as four of five datasets provide only binary mortality outcomes (survived/died) without exact time-to-event data (Supplementary Table S9). The inclusion of dataset as a covariate controls for cohort-level mortality differences. The likelihood-ratio test assessed overall endotype significance, and per-endotype odds ratios were computed with C1 as the reference.

A multivariate logistic model (mortality ∼ endotype + age + sex + dataset) was used to compute adjusted odds ratios. As a further sensitivity analysis, the two smallest cohorts (GSE95233, n = 51; GSE137340, n = 21) were excluded to test robustness to small-sample effects. Incremental predictive value of endotype over clinical covariates was assessed using ROC analysis with DeLong’s test. Kaplan–Meier estimation with log-rank testing and Cox proportional hazards regression (adjusted for age and sex) were performed on the GSE65682 subset (n = 479), which uniquely provides exact 28-day time-to-event data. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residual tests.

External validation

External validation was performed using the LASSO multinomial classifier (see below). Validation expression matrices were processed identically to discovery data: probe-to-gene mapping, log transformation (if max > 100), and quantile normalisation. Because the gene universe was defined as the intersection of all nine platforms, all 122 classifier features were available in every validation cohort (100% gene coverage). Classification confidence was defined as the maximum predicted class probability from the multinomial model.

As a complementary approach, centroid-based classification was also performed: endotype centroids were computed as the mean expression profile of each endotype’s members in the discovery cohort, and validation samples were assigned to the endotype with the highest Pearson correlation to the centroid. Centroid classification confidence (difference between best and second-best correlations) and fuzzy classification proportions were reported per validation cohort (Supplementary Table S8; Supplementary Figure S16).

Longitudinal endotype stability

Four datasets with multi-timepoint samples were analysed: GSE54514 (Day 1–5), GSE95233 (Day 0, Day 3), GSE57065 [14] (0 h, 48 h), and GSE137340 (0 h, 24 h). At each time point, endotypes were assigned using the LASSO multinomial classifier (Section 2.9), which provides substantially higher classification confidence (mean = 0.87) than centroid-based correlation (mean = 0.014). To ensure statistical independence, only the first transition (timepoint 1 → timepoint 2) per patient was used for formal analysis (N = 129 independent transitions). A 3 × 3 transition probability matrix was computed. Cohen’s κ was used to quantify agreement beyond chance. Per-endotype stability was tested against a null distribution generated by 10,000 label permutations. The full set of consecutive-timepoint pairs (215 transitions) was used for descriptive alluvial visualisation.

LASSO multinomial classifier

A LASSO-regularised multinomial logistic regression classifier was trained on the 122-gene expression matrix (1002 samples) using glmnet [24] with family = “multinomial”, 5-fold cross-validation, and α = 1 (L1 penalty). The parsimonious lambda.1se was selected. A random forest classifier (randomForest, was trained for comparison. In-sample per-class sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV were computed from confusion matrices. The trained LASSO model was applied to four validation cohorts; because all 122 features were present across all platforms (by design of the universal gene set), no gene imputation was required. Classification confidence was defined as the maximum predicted class probability.

Alternative clustering algorithms

To assess clustering robustness beyond PAM, hierarchical clustering (Ward.D2 linkage on Pearson correlation distance, k = 3) and K-means (k = 3, , 100 iterations) were applied to the same 122-gene expression matrix. Cluster labels were aligned to PAM assignments by majority-vote mapping. ARI, percentage agreement, and mean silhouette width were computed for each method; three-way concordance was defined as the percentage of samples assigned to the same endotype by all three algorithms.

Severity-adjusted analysis

In GSE185263 (n = 345), which provides SOFA scores, logistic regression was fitted as mortality ∼ endotype + SOFA + age + sex (SOFA-adjusted) and compared to the unadjusted model. In GSE54514 (n = 35, validation), which provides APACHE II scores, an exploratory APACHE-adjusted analysis was performed (noting limited statistical power). SOFA and APACHE II distributions by endotype were compared using Kruskal–Wallis tests.

Demographic, batch, and cross-framework analyses

To test whether endotypes are confounded by demographics, age was compared across endotypes by one-way ANOVA (with the Kruskal–Wallis test as a non-parametric check) and sex by Pearson’s test; effect sizes were reported as and Cramer’s V. To test whether the endotypes are a batch artifact, the joint distribution of endotype by discovery cohort was tabulated and its association quantified by Cramer’s V; a pure batch artifact would map approximately one endotype per cohort. As a technical-variance control, MAD-based feature selection and consensus clustering were also repeated on the pre-ComBat (uncorrected) matrix, and the resulting labels compared to the corrected solution by ARI (Supplementary Methods). Finally, to compare clinical utility against an existing framework without depending on shared marker genes, we used the published sample-level MARS endotype labels (Mars1–Mars4) distributed with GSE65682 [7]: our endotypes and the MARS labels were cross-tabulated (ARI, Cramer’s V), and 28-day mortality discrimination was compared between the two schemes using the area under the ROC curve (AUC) for each scheme’s categorical labels, treated as apparent, unadjusted discrimination.

Longitudinal transition–outcome associations

Using the 129 independent first transitions from the longitudinal analysis, mortality was compared between patients who maintained their endotype (“stable”) and those who transitioned, overall and per starting endotype, using Fisher’s exact test. The specific comparison of C2-stable versus C2-to-other transitioners was pre-specified given C2’s lower unadjusted mortality.

Software and reproducibility

All analyses were performed in R v4.3.3. Key packages: ConsensusClusterPlus [20], limma [23], sva (ComBat) [19], clusterProfiler [25], survival, survminer [26], and ggalluvial. Visualisations were created with ggplot2. Additional packages for reviewer analyses: pROC (ROC comparison), irr (Cohen’s κ ), mclust (Gaussian mixture modelling), glmnet [24] (LASSO multinomial), and randomForest (random forest classifier). All analysis scripts are available at https://github.com/loganylchen/sepsis_paper_2026.

Data-driven feature selection

Rather than using a curated immune gene panel or arbitrary “top N” cutoff, we selected genes with median absolute deviation (MAD) ≥ 1.0 across the batch-corrected expression matrix, yielding 122 genes (MAD range: 1.00−3.35). This threshold-based approach is data-driven and reproducible: it retains genes with substantial cross-sample variability regardless of their functional annotation, avoiding the circularity of pre-selecting genes based on expected biology.

Of the 122 selected genes, 14 overlapped with a curated panel of 39 immune-related genes present in the expression data. This natural enrichment—without explicit immune gene filtering—indicates that immune pathway genes are among the most variable in the sepsis transcriptome, consistent with their central role in sepsis pathophysiology. Sensitivity analysis confirmed that clustering was robust to MAD threshold variation: at adjacent thresholds (0.9 and 1.1), ARI versus the reference exceeded 0.71 and 0.72, respectively (Supplementary Figure S10; Supplementary Table S2). Quantitatively, PAC (lower is more stable) was minimised exactly at MAD = 1.0 (PAC = 0.324; 122 genes), with higher values at 0.8 (0.525), 0.9 (0.340), 1.1 (0.384), and 1.2 (0.360), while the mean Pearson silhouette was flat-to-rising across 0.9−1.2 (0.206−0.234) and collapsed at 0.8 (0.153); label identity was preserved within ± 0.1 of the threshold (ARI 0.71/0.72) and degraded outside it (ARI 0.39 and 0.49 at MAD 0.8 and 1.2). The selected gene set was reproducible (split-half Jaccard mean = 0.766, SD 0.026, 100 splits) and carried structure exceeding chance: the observed PAC (0.324) and silhouette (0.208) exceeded those of 50 size-matched random gene sets (random mean PAC 0.502, silhouette 0.117; empirical p ≈ 0.02), consistent with MAD ≥ 1.0 capturing reproducible cross-patient structure.

Three transcriptomic endotypes identified by consensus clustering

We applied ConsensusClusterPlus [20] with PAM clustering and Pearson correlation distance to the 122 selected genes across 1002 sepsis patients, testing k = 2–10 with 1000 bootstrap iterations (80% item resampling, 100% feature inclusion). Optimal k was determined by a rank-based composite score integrating three complementary metrics: the proportion of ambiguous clustering (PAC; lower is better), mean silhouette width (higher is better), and CDF Δ AUC elbow detection (higher delta is better). Clusters containing <5% of total samples were considered unstable and penalised.

The composite score identified k = 3 as the optimal solution (Fig. 2). At k = 3, PAC was 0.322 and mean silhouette width was 0.208 (computed on the Pearson correlation distance matrix used for clustering), representing the best balance between cluster stability and biological resolution. Note that a separate consensus-distance-based silhouette (computed on ; Supplementary Figure S2) yielded a higher value of 0.767, reflecting the stability of sample co-clustering across bootstrap resamples rather than transcriptomic similarity. The moderate Pearson-based silhouette width indicates overlapping transcriptomic programs along a continuum rather than sharply distinct categorical entities. Although k = 2 achieved a slightly higher silhouette width (0.227), it had the worst PAC (0.538), indicating highly ambiguous sample assignments. Solutions at k ≥ 4 showed progressively lower silhouette widths without compensating improvements in PAC or Δ AUC. Critically, k = 3 achieved the lowest logistic-regression AIC (1023.9) and BIC (1038.6) for 28-day mortality prediction, as well as the greatest mortality spread across endotypes (10.7 percentage points; Supplementary Table S3), demonstrating that the three-endotype solution captures the most clinically meaningful patient stratification. Systematic comparison of k = 2, 3, and 4 across cluster validity indices (Dunn, Calinski–Harabasz, connectivity), marker gene specificity, pathway enrichment, and clinical outcome prediction supported k = 3 as the most clinically interpretable partition among the tested solutions, while internal-validity metrics did not converge on a single uniquely optimal k (see below) (Supplementary Figure S11; Supplementary Table S3). Leave-one-dataset-out (LODO) cross-validation demonstrated reproducible cluster recovery across all five cohorts (mean ARI = 0.66; range 0.45–0.85). Gaussian mixture modelling (Mclust) identified G = 2 as the optimal number of components by BIC, with negligible agreement with PAM-based endotypes (ARI = -0.006). This discrepancy is expected because GMM assumes multivariate Gaussian components in Euclidean space, whereas PAM with Pearson correlation distance optimises medoid assignment in correlation space—the two methods minimise fundamentally different objective functions. The gap statistic with Pearson correlation distance favoured k = 1, a known limitation of this criterion with correlation-based dissimilarities. The composite rank of PAC, silhouette, and CDF AUC consistently supported k = 3 (Supplementary Figure S4; Supplementary Figure S12; Supplementary Table S4).

Non-circular effect-size estimation confirms genuine separation

To avoid circular inflation of effect sizes, Cohen’s d was computed on 100 randomly sampled held-out genes excluded from both the clustering feature set and differential expression gene lists [27]. All three endotypes showed mean |d| significantly exceeding permutation null expectations (p < 0.001, 1000 label permutations; null mean |d| ≈ 0.05−0.06): C1 (; 13% large effects, |d| > 0.8) showed the strongest separation, followed by C3 (; 7% large effects) and C2 (; 3% large effects). Even for C2, the observed mean |d| was ∼ 5× the null expectation, confirming genuine transcriptomic distinctiveness beyond the clustering features (Fig. 6).

Mortality differed significantly across endotypes. In a logistic regression model applied to all 1002 discovery samples (mortality ∼ endotype + dataset), the likelihood-ratio test was highly significant (, df = 2, ; Fig. 7). C2 (Interferon Response) had the lowest 28-day mortality (15.4%), significantly lower than C1 (26.1%; OR = 0.52, 95% CI , ). C3 mortality (25.3%) was intermediate and not significantly different from C1 (OR = 0.96, 95% CI , p = 0.821; Supplementary Figure S8).

After adjustment for age, sex, and dataset, C2’s lower mortality remained nominally significant (adjusted OR = 0.60, 95% CI 0.41–0.89, p = 0.012; Supplementary Table S7). However, ROC analysis showed that adding endotype to a base model of age, sex, and dataset produced only marginal improvement in discrimination (Supplementary Figure S15). Endotype did not provide substantial independent information beyond clinical covariates for mortality stratification. We note that mortality rates varied substantially across the five discovery cohorts, and although the dataset covariate partially controls for this heterogeneity, residual confounding by cohort-specific case-mix and treatment differences cannot be excluded.

In GSE185263 (n = 342 with complete SOFA data), SOFA scores differed significantly across endotypes (Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.001; C1 median = 3, C2 median = 2, C3 median = 2). The unadjusted OR for C2 versus C1 was 0.51 (95% CI 0.26−1.02, p = 0.056). After adjusting for SOFA, age, and sex, C2’s association was fully attenuated (adjusted OR = 0.99, 95% CI 0.45−2.17, p = 0.98), indicating that the lower mortality in C2 was primarily driven by lower baseline disease severity. Endotype did not provide independent prognostic information beyond SOFA, age, and sex in this subset (Supplementary Figure S20; Supplementary Table S12). In the validation cohort GSE54514 (n = 35), exploratory APACHE II-adjusted analysis showed no significant endotype effect (adjusted OR = 1.82, p = 0.52), though power was severely limited (Supplementary Table S14).

As a sensitivity analysis, Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards regression were computed on the GSE65682 subset (n = 479), which uniquely provides exact time-to-event data. C2 showed a trend toward lower mortality (HR = 0.61, 95% CI , p = 0.027; adjusted HR = 0.60, 95% CI 0.38–0.94, p = 0.027; log-rank p = 0.056). Both the unadjusted Cox p-value and the log-rank test are borderline, and consistent with the severity-associated pattern observed in the SOFA analysis in GSE185263, this association likely reflects severity differences rather than an independent endotype effect.

Endotypes are not explained by age or sex

Because age and sex are established modifiers of the sepsis transcriptome, we tested whether the endotypes simply recapitulate demographic strata (Table 1). Both associations were statistically detectable but small in magnitude. Age differed across endotypes (ANOVA ; Kruskal–Wallis ) but the effect size was negligible (), with C2 only marginally younger (mean 56.9 y) than C1 (62.1 y) and C3 (60.4 y). Sex was likewise associated () with a small effect size (Cramer’s V = 0.141), C2 being modestly more male (65.7% vs. 51.3% in C1 and 52.0% in C3). These tiny effect sizes indicate that the endotypes are not merely age- or sex-defined subgroups, although the slightly younger, more male composition of C2 is consistent with its lower-severity profile and was carried forward as a covariate in all adjusted mortality models.

LASSO multinomial classifier and external validation

To enable formal classification of new samples, we trained a LASSO-regularised multinomial logistic regression model on the 122 genes × 1002 discovery samples. Five-fold cross-validated misclassification error was 5.1% at the parsimonious lambda.1se. Per-class sensitivity ranged from 0.984 (C3) to 0.989 (C2), with PPV ≥ 0.984 for all endotypes (Supplementary Table S10). The model selected 74 non-zero genes (union across classes; Supplementary Table S15). A random forest classifier (1000 trees) achieved perfect in-sample classification (ARI = 1.0) compared to LASSO ARI = 0.962.

Critically, because the gene universe was defined as the intersection of all nine platforms (five discovery and four validation), all 122 LASSO input features were available in every validation cohort (100% gene coverage), eliminating the need for zero-imputation of missing genes—a key advantage over centroid-based approaches.

The LASSO classifier was applied to 4 external GEO datasets totalling 412 additional samples (Table 5; Fig. 8A).

Three validation cohorts (GSE54514, GSE26378, GSE26440; pooled n = 254) carried 28-day mortality, permitting an exploratory check of whether the protective direction of C2 replicates externally. Zero observed deaths does not imply zero risk—the exact (Clopper–Pearson) 95% upper confidence limit on C2 mortality is still . This replicates the direction of the unadjusted C2 protective association in independent data, but it is not severity-independent prognostic validation: these cohorts are pediatric-dominant (233/254), lack the external severity covariates needed to repeat the SOFA-style adjustment that abolished the effect in discovery, and rely on centroid rather than the primary LASSO labels. Synchronised severity data were therefore the limiting factor, not outcome data. Centroid-based classification produced qualitatively similar dominant-endotype patterns (C3-dominant in adult/ARDS cohorts, C1-dominant in pediatric cohorts) but with markedly lower classification confidence and less extreme endotype proportions (Supplementary Figure S7; Supplementary Figure S16).

Alternative clustering algorithms confirm three-subgroup structure

To verify that the three-endotype structure is not an artefact of the PAM algorithm, we applied hierarchical clustering (Ward.D2 linkage on Pearson distance) and K-means (k = 3, ) to the same expression matrix. K-means showed substantial agreement with PAM (ARI = 0.531, 81.9% concordance), while hierarchical clustering showed moderate agreement (ARI = 0.463, 78.5% concordance). Mean silhouette widths were comparable: PAM = 0.208, K-means = 0.222, hierarchical = 0.166. Across all three methods, 68.6% of samples were assigned to the same endotype, confirming that the majority of the three-subgroup structure is algorithm-independent (Supplementary Figure S19; Supplementary Table S11).

Longitudinal endotype stability

To assess whether endotype assignments are temporally stable, we analysed four datasets containing multi-timepoint samples from 129 sepsis patients: GSE54514 (Day 1–5, n = 31), GSE95233 (Day 0–3, n = 51), GSE57065 (0–48 h, n = 28), and GSE137340 (0–24 h, n = 19). At each time point, endotypes were assigned using the LASSO multinomial classifier, which provided mean classification confidence of 0.87 (compared to 0.014 for centroid-based correlation; Supplementary Figure S16C).

Using only the first two timepoints per patient to ensure statistical independence (N = 129 unique transitions), 66.7% of patients maintained their endotype (Cohen’s κ = 0.49, 95% CI 0.37–0.61; Supplementary Figure S16). Per-endotype stability was: C1 (59.6%), C2 (73.9%), and C3 (69.5%), all significantly above chance (permutation p < 0.001 for each endotype, 10,000 permutations). These values represent an improvement over centroid-based classification (60.5% overall stability, κ = 0.39), consistent with the substantially higher classification confidence of the LASSO classifier. The full transition analysis using all 215 consecutive-timepoint pairs is provided in Supplementary Figure S9; Supplementary Figure S17.

Among patients who transitioned, the most common patterns were C1→ C2 and C3→ C1, suggesting bidirectional movement between immune activation and the other endotype states. Endotype stability was not significantly different between survivors and non-survivors (Supplementary Figure S16D).

To assess whether endotype transitions carry prognostic information, we compared mortality between stable and transitioning patients among the 99 patients (of 129 transitions) with binary survival outcome data. Overall mortality did not differ between stable (26.9%, 18/67) and transitioning (21.9%, 7/32) patients (Fisher’s exact p = 0.81; Supplementary Table S13). Per-endotype analyses were similarly non-significant, likely reflecting limited statistical power.

Relationship to existing frameworks

Because the universal gene set (2456 genes common to all nine platforms) excludes many signature genes used by existing classifiers (e.g., the SRS 7-gene and CTS 18-gene signatures), direct cross-classification could not be performed in the current analysis. This is an inherent trade-off of using a universal gene set that guarantees full feature availability across all platforms: classifier-specific genes that are measured on only a subset of platforms are excluded. To nonetheless compare clinical utility directly—and without depending on shared marker genes—we exploited the fact that one discovery cohort, GSE65682, is distributed with published sample-level MARS endotype labels (Mars1–Mars4) [7]. This permits a gene-independent, head-to-head comparison on shared endpoints. Equivalent published sample-level SRS and CTS labels were not available for the shared datasets, and re-deriving them from marker genes would reintroduce exactly the gene-overlap dependency the comparison is meant to avoid; we therefore restrict the quantitative comparison to MARS. Our data-driven endotypes recovered much of the published MARS structure (Table 2): the schemes were concordant (ARI = 0.353, Cramer’s V = 0.655), with C1 mapping predominantly to Mars2, C2 to Mars3/Mars4, and the erythroid C3 to the immunosuppressed Mars1. On the shared 28-day mortality endpoint, apparent (unadjusted) discrimination was comparable and modest for both schemes (categorical-label AUC = 0.562 for our endotypes versus 0.578 for MARS). Each scheme’s expected risk contrast was recovered: our C2 was protective (OR = 0.58, 95% CI 0.37−0.91, p = 0.018) and MARS Mars1 was high-risk (OR = 2.08, 95% CI 1.33−3.26, ). We interpret these as comparable rather than superior: the two AUCs are close and both modest, and because the categorical labels were ranked by observed mortality within the same cohort, the AUCs should be read as apparent in-sample discrimination, not as validated prognostic performance.

Conceptual comparison with existing frameworks remains informative. The identification of an interferon-driven endotype (C2) with lower unadjusted mortality parallels the SRS2 phenotype [6] and the CTS3 subtype, both associated with preserved immune competence and lower disease severity. The immune activation endotype (C1) with highest unadjusted mortality shares features with Mars1 [7]. The erythroid/metabolic endotype (C3) captures a transcriptomic axis under-represented in existing immune-focused frameworks; however, because its top markers are classical erythroid genes and clustering stability decreases after removing red-blood-cell genes (ARI = 0.58), we cannot currently distinguish a genuine biological endotype from technical contributions (residual globin transcripts, reticulocytosis, or blood-composition differences), and we interpret C3 cautiously pending orthogonal (e.g., single-cell) evidence.

Discussion

Summary of findings

We applied consensus clustering to 122 data-driven genes (MAD ≥ 1.0) across 1002 sepsis patients from 5 independent cohorts and identified three transcriptomic patterns characterised by immune activation, interferon response, and erythroid dysregulation. The three-endotype solution was selected by a composite metric integrating PAC, silhouette width, and CDF delta area elbow detection. Non-circular effect-size estimation on 100 randomly sampled held-out genes, validated by permutation testing (p < 0.001), confirmed genuine transcriptomic separation for all three endotypes. Unadjusted mortality differed across endotypes (logistic regression with C2 (Interferon Response) showing lower mortality (15.4% vs. 26.1% for C1; OR = 0.52, Critically, in GSE185263 SOFA-adjusted analysis revealed that this association was fully explained by baseline disease severity (adjusted OR = 0.99, p = 0.98), indicating that—in the cohort tested—the endotype–mortality association reflects baseline severity rather than independent endotype biology. A LASSO multinomial classifier applied to 4 external cohorts (n = 412) with 100% gene coverage reproduced the endotype label distributions with high classification confidence (mean 0.86−0.94). In the validation cohorts with available outcomes, the unadjusted protective direction of C2 was reproduced (0% vs. 16.9% mortality, Fisher p = 0.011), but severity-independent prognostic validation remained out of reach because external severity scores were unavailable. A gene-independent comparison against published MARS labels showed strong structural concordance (ARI = 0.353, Cramer’s V = 0.655) and comparable, modest mortality discrimination (AUC 0.562 vs. 0.578), and the endotypes were not explained by age or sex (, Cramer’s V = 0.141). Longitudinal analysis using the LASSO classifier showed moderate temporal stability (66.7%, κ = 0.49), an improvement over centroid-based classification (60.5%, κ = 0.39) attributable to the 63-fold higher classification confidence of the LASSO model.

Biological interpretation of each endotype

C1 (Immune Activation) has the highest unadjusted mortality (26.1%) and exhibited the largest number of differentially expressed markers (59 genes). Its pathway signature—spanning immune response, defence response, HLA-associated pathways, and Th1/Th2 differentiation—indicates broad, generalised immune activation. Top markers include PFKFB2 (glycolytic regulation), DACH1 (immune cell differentiation), and BTN3A1 ( T-cell activation). This broad activation without a focused effector program may represent an uncoordinated or dysregulated immune response, consistent with the concept of “cytokine storm” in which simultaneous activation of multiple immune pathways leads to tissue damage rather than pathogen clearance [28].

C2 (Interferon Response) has the lowest unadjusted mortality (15.4%) and is characterised by a coherent type I/III interferon response signature; it is best understood as an interferon-signalling-associated transcriptional state with lower observed severity, not a biologically autonomous subtype. Top markers (UBE2H, TFDP1, SPTB, ANK1, SLC14A1) include regulators of protein homeostasis and erythrocyte structure, and pathway enrichment confirms antiviral and NOD-like receptor signalling. C2’s lower mortality was fully explained by lower baseline severity (SOFA-adjusted OR = 0.99), revealing that this endotype captures the transcriptomic signature of less severe disease rather than conferring independent survival benefit. A focused, coordinated interferon response may reflect a less severe immune state with preserved antimicrobial capacity, consistent with observations linking intact interferon signalling to lower severity in sepsis [6] and paralleling the SRS2 phenotype [29].

C3 (Erythroid Dysregulation) (24.9% of samples) has intermediate mortality (25.3%). C3 is defined by its transcriptomic marker profile; whether this corresponds to a distinct biological process or to variation in blood composition remains unresolved (see below). Its marker genes and pathway enrichment are dominated by erythrocyte differentiation, heme biosynthesis, and porphyrin metabolism—pathways not traditionally emphasised in sepsis endotyping. Top markers include BCL2L1 (erythrocyte survival), FECH (heme biosynthesis), and ANK1 (erythrocyte cytoskeleton). Heme metabolism has been implicated in sepsis pathophysiology, although the following mechanistic interpretation applies only if the C3 signal reflects genuine biology rather than blood composition: free heme released during haemolysis is a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) that activates TLR4 signalling and amplifies inflammation, while heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) induction is a key cytoprotective response. The prominence of C3 in the ARDS cohort (GSE32707, 91.0%) would be compatible with proposed roles of heme and iron dysregulation in acute lung injury [17]. We acknowledge that whole-blood transcriptomic analyses are sensitive to erythrocyte content. Sensitivity analysis removing 8 known RBC genes from the gene universe preserved the three-endotype structure (Supplementary Figure S13), and cell-type deconvolution showed that C3 assignment was not driven solely by erythrocyte proportion differences (Supplementary Figure S14). However, it is important to note that cell-type adjustment abolished the significance of C2’s mortality association (adjusted OR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.47–1.07, p = 0.104; Supplementary Table S6), indicating that endotype–mortality associations are partially confounded by cell-type composition. Furthermore, the C3 erythroid signature may reflect biological dysregulation (hemolysis, stress erythropoiesis) or technical variation in residual red blood cell RNA content in whole-blood profiles. Without hemoglobin levels, reticulocyte counts, or information on globin depletion protocols across the five discovery platforms, these contributions cannot be disentangled. Further studies with globin depletion and hematologic data are needed to confirm the biological basis of C3. This remains a critical caveat for the interpretation of this endotype.

Comparison to existing frameworks

Our three-endotype solution aligns in dimensionality with the recent consensus transcriptomic subtypes (CTS1–CTS3) proposed by Scicluna et al. [9]. Because the universal gene set (2456 genes common to all nine platforms) excludes most signature genes used by CTS (18 genes), SRS (7 genes), and MARS classifiers, direct cross-classification was not performed. This reflects the trade-off of using a platform-universal gene set: while it ensures 100% feature availability across all validation platforms, it necessarily excludes genes measured on only a subset of platforms.

Conceptually, our endotypes align with existing frameworks along two of three axes. The interferon-driven endotype (C2), associated with lower severity and unadjusted mortality, parallels the SRS2 phenotype [6] and the CTS3 subtype, both associated with preserved immune competence. The immune activation endotype (C1) with highest unadjusted mortality shares features with Mars1 [7] and HI-DEF high-dysregulation states [10].

A distinctive feature of our data-driven analysis is the prominence of the erythroid/metabolic endotype (C3), a transcriptomic axis—heme and erythrocyte biology—that has received limited attention in existing immune-focused frameworks. CTS, MARS, SRS, and HI-DEF all employ curated immune gene panels that exclude erythroid genes from consideration by design. Our data-driven approach, which selects genes solely by cross-sample variability, naturally captures this axis when it is a major source of transcriptomic heterogeneity. Whether C3 reflects a genuine pathophysiological process (e.g., sepsis-associated hemolysis or stress erythropoiesis) or is partly driven by sample-level variation in erythrocyte content remains an open question (see Limitations), but its prominence in the ARDS validation cohort (91.0%) and its association with distinct pathway enrichment suggest biological relevance beyond technical artifact. If C3 reflects genuine heme/erythroid dysregulation, therapeutic strategies targeting this axis—including iron chelation (e.g., deferoxamine), heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) modulators, or heme scavengers such as hemopexin—could represent endotype-specific interventions warranting investigation in appropriately enriched trials.

Beyond C3, the multi-cohort discovery design reduces the single-centre bias inherent in frameworks derived from a single study, and the non-circular validation on held-out genes provides evidence of genuine transcriptomic separation independent of the clustering features.

A central finding of this study is that, in the one discovery cohort with available SOFA scores (GSE185263, n = 342), the observed C2 mortality advantage appeared largely explained by baseline disease severity. After SOFA adjustment, endotype did not remain independently associated with mortality (adjusted OR = 0.99, p = 0.98) in this cohort, suggesting that, at least here, these transcriptomic signatures behave as molecular correlates of sepsis severity. This result has important implications: while previous frameworks (SRS, MARS, CTS, HI-DEF) report endotype–outcome associations, few have reported systematic severity adjustment, leaving open the question of whether their prognostic signals reflect independent biology or confounding by severity. Our analysis adds direct, severity-adjusted evidence—currently from a single cohort—that data-driven transcriptomic endotypes in sepsis can be severity-aligned, motivating severity adjustment in future endotyping studies before prognostic claims are made and replication in additional cohorts with harmonised severity scoring.

An important interpretive caveat concerns the causal role of SOFA. We have treated SOFA as a confounder of the endotype–mortality relationship, but it may also act as a mediator: if transcriptomic state contributes causally to organ dysfunction, then SOFA lies on the causal path from endotype to death, and adjusting for it would remove part of the biological effect of interest (over-adjustment). Our data cannot distinguish these possibilities. We therefore report both unadjusted and severity-adjusted estimates and do not claim that the endotypes are biologically inert with respect to outcome; rather, that their association with mortality is not independent of severity in the cohort examined. Formal mediation analysis in cohorts with longitudinal SOFA would be required to partition direct from severity-mediated effects. While the C1 and C2 endotypes recapitulate axes described by CTS, MARS, and SRS, our framework surfaces an erythroid/metabolic axis (C3) under-represented in immune-focused gene panels, and provides a severity-adjusted quantification (in the cohort with SOFA data) of the extent to which endotype–mortality associations are explained by disease severity.

A fundamental distinction between our approach and existing frameworks lies in gene selection. MARS, SRS, and CTS all rely on pre-defined immune gene panels, which inherently constrain the biological axes that can be discovered. By contrast, our data-driven MAD-based feature selection imposes no prior biological hypothesis, which may surface axes such as the erythroid/metabolic signal (C3) that immune-focused panels are less likely to capture. This is consistent with the view that unbiased, data-driven methods can complement hypothesis-driven panels for characterising sepsis heterogeneity, as hypothesis-driven gene selection may systematically overlook clinically relevant transcriptomic programs that fall outside the investigator’s a priori assumptions.

Methodological considerations

Several methodological choices distinguish this study. First, the 5-cohort discovery design reduces single-cohort bias. No dataset exceeds 48% of the total, ensuring that clustering results are not dominated by any single study. ComBat batch correction with 5 levels, validated by post-correction PCA, controls for platform effects.

Second, data-driven gene selection (MAD ≥ 1.0) avoids the subjective choice of curated gene panels or arbitrary “top N” thresholds. The MAD ≥ 1.0 criterion retains genes with genuine cross-patient variability, which is both more reproducible (a new dataset with different total gene counts would yield a comparable feature set) and less biased toward pre-specified biological hypotheses.

Third, the use of Pearson correlation distance rather than Euclidean distance for clustering and silhouette computation better captures co-expression patterns, which are more robust to platform-specific scaling differences across the five discovery datasets.

Fourth, the moderate mean silhouette width (0.208, Pearson correlation distance) warrants discussion. This value should not be confused with the consensus-distance-based silhouette (0.767; Supplementary Figure S2), which measures bootstrap co-clustering stability rather than transcriptomic proximity. Pearson-based silhouette values in the range 0.1−0.25 are consistently reported in transcriptomic clustering studies of sepsis and other critical illness states [6, 7], because high-dimensional gene expression data produce overlapping distributions even when biologically meaningful subgroups exist. Multi-index validation (Dunn, Calinski–Harabasz, connectivity) and leave-one-dataset-out cross-validation (mean ARI = 0.66) corroborate the k = 3 solution (Supplementary Figure S12; Supplementary Table S4). Notably, the gap statistic with Pearson correlation distance favoured k = 1, a known limitation of this criterion with correlation-based dissimilarities; Gaussian mixture modelling favoured G = 2 by BIC, with near-zero agreement with PAM (ARI = -0.006). The k = 3 solution achieves the lowest logistic-regression AIC and BIC for 28-day mortality prediction (Supplementary Figure S11). The discrepancy with Mclust underscores that the three-cluster structure is method-dependent: different clustering algorithms, optimising different objective functions, can identify different numbers of clusters and assign patients differently. We do not view this as invalidating the PAM-based endotypes, because their clinical and biological coherence—severity-associated mortality differences across endotypes (), distinct pathway enrichment signatures, internally consistent label re-assignment across platforms, and non-circular effect sizes exceeding null expectations—provides independent evidence beyond internal validity metrics. Nevertheless, the Mclust discrepancy underscores that these endotypes should be regarded as one valid decomposition of a continuous transcriptomic landscape, not as uniquely determined biological entities. Application of alternative clustering algorithms (hierarchical Ward.D2 and K-means) to the same expression matrix confirmed the three-subgroup structure, with 68.6% of samples concordantly assigned by all three methods and K-means achieving ARI = 0.531 versus PAM.

We emphasise that these endotypes represent poles on a transcriptomic continuum rather than discrete biological categories, as evidenced by moderate silhouette widths and semi-permeable longitudinal boundaries. The 33.3% longitudinal transition rate observed using the LASSO classifier on independent observations across four cohorts confirms semi-permeable endotype boundaries. The moderate temporal agreement (66.7%, κ = 0.49) means roughly one in three patients changes endotype between early timepoints. This degree of instability is, on its own, insufficient to support static single-timepoint endotyping for clinical decision-making, and argues for either repeated-measures endotyping or explicit modelling of trajectories before clinical application.

Fifth, the dual survival analysis approach—primary logistic regression on all 1002 samples (accommodating datasets with binary-only mortality) and sensitivity KM/Cox on the GSE65682 subset (with exact time-to-event)— maximises both sample size and analytical rigour.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged:

Severity score availability SOFA and APACHE II scores were not uniformly available across all discovery datasets. The key severity-adjustment analysis was limited to GSE185263 (n = 342) where SOFA was available; replication across additional cohorts with severity scoring would strengthen the generalisability of this finding. Nonetheless, the complete attenuation of the endotype–mortality association after SOFA adjustment (adjusted OR = 0.99, p = 0.98; Supplementary Table S12) provides strong evidence that disease severity is the primary driver of the endotype–mortality relationship. Exploratory APACHE II-adjusted analysis in GSE54514 (n = 35) was similarly non-significant, though underpowered. These findings underscore that the observed mortality differences between endotypes cannot be confidently attributed to endotype biology independent of baseline severity.

Future directions

Several priorities emerge from this work. First, prospective validation of the three-endotype classification with longitudinal sampling, standardised severity scoring (APACHE II/SOFA), and clinical outcomes (including organ dysfunction and ICU length-of-stay) is essential. Second, while the LASSO multinomial classifier presented here achieves high accuracy with 74 non-zero genes, further refinement into a minimal gene signature (10–20 genes) suitable for RT-qPCR or NanoString-based bedside endotyping would facilitate clinical translation. Third, single-cell RNA sequencing could resolve the cellular composition underlying each endotype, particularly for C3 (erythroid precursors vs. mature red cell contamination) and C1 (which immune cell subsets drive the broad activation signal). Fourth, the relationship between endotype transitions and clinical trajectories warrants investigation: whether patients transitioning from the lower-severity C2 state to C1 or C3 experience worse outcomes, and whether endotype stability predicts survival, could inform dynamic treatment strategies. Fifth, if external datasets with available clinical phenotypes (e.g., haemoglobin levels, ARDS incidence, transfusion history) can be identified, the clinical correlates of the C3 erythroid endotype should be further validated beyond the transcriptomic level. Finally, endotype-guided clinical trial stratification—for example, anti-inflammatory therapies directed at C1 patients with uncoordinated immune activation, or immunostimulatory therapies for patients transitioning out of C2—could translate these findings into improved treatment outcomes.

Conclusions

We identified three transcriptomic patterns in sepsis—immune activation (C1), interferon response (C2), and erythroid dysregulation (C3)—and showed that, in the one cohort with available severity scores (GSE185263), the C2 mortality advantage appeared largely explained by baseline disease severity (SOFA-adjusted OR = 0.99, p = 0.98). Unlike previous endotyping frameworks that report endotype–outcome associations without reported severity adjustment, our data-driven approach indicates that endotype–mortality associations can be substantially driven by disease severity, underscoring the need for severity-adjusted analyses before prognostic claims are made. A LASSO multinomial classifier reproduced the endotype label distributions across 4 external cohorts with 100% gene coverage and high classification confidence, and this framework identifies an underrecognised erythroid/metabolic axis (C3) overlooked by existing immune-focused panels. Prospective validation with standardised severity scoring is needed to determine whether severity-adjusted endotypes can guide treatment stratification.
