Neonatal exposure to high d-galactose affects germ cell development in neonatal testes organ culture
Authors: Hyuk Song, Min-Gi Han, Ran Lee, Hyun-Jung Park
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Excess exogenous supplementation of d-galactose (d-gal), a monosaccharide and reducing sugar, generates reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to cell damage and death. ROS accumulation is critical in aging. Therefore, d-gal-induced aging mouse models are used in aging studies. Herein, we evaluated d-gal’s effect on neonatal testis development using an in vitro organ culture method. Mouse testicular fragments (MTFs) derived from neonatal testes (postnatal day 5) were cultured with 500 mM d-gal for 5 days. d-gal-treated MTFs showed a significantly increased and decreased expression of undifferentiated and differentiated germ cell markers, respectively, with a substantial reduction in meiotic cells. In d-gal-exposed MTFs, expression levels of Sertoli cell markers (Sox9 and Wt1) increased, while those of StAR and 17β-HSD3, whose expressions are abundant in d-Gal treated adult Leydig cells, decreased. Additionally, the enzyme 3 β-HSD1, essential for steroidogenesis in Leydig cells, was significantly reduced in d-gal-exposed MTFs compared to that in controls.d-gal significantly increased the expression of Bad, Bax, and cleaved caspase-3 and -8. Via oxidative stress in MTF. Overall, d-gal negatively regulates germ cell and Leydig cell development in neonatal testes through pro-apoptotic mechanisms and ROS.

Introduction

d-galactose (d-gal) is a monosaccharide sugar that animals metabolize. However, excess administration of d-gal can induce cellular damage by increasing oxidative stress in the body; d-gal is a reducing sugar that reacts with amino acids to form an unstable compound, which when oxidized, produces reactive oxygen species (ROS). Excess ROS can damage cellular lipids, proteins, and DNA. In addition, d-gal increases hydrogen peroxide and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels and suppresses the production of antioxidant enzymes1,2. Moreover, d-gal can also be converted into galactitol, which accumulates in cells to release ROS3. An increase in ROS levels in the body due to excess d-gal administration is closely related to aging. Therefore, d-gal-induced aging models are widely used in aging research4–6. d-gal can induce aging in various organs, including cardiac aging in vivo and in vitro7, kidney senescence by reducing SIRT1-mediated oxidative stress8, male reproductive aging6, and lung and brain aging9,10.

Regarding the reproductive system, Malsawmhriatzuala et al. reported that the expression of the anti-apoptotic factor BCL2 decreased, whereas the ex-pression of BAX and active caspase-3 increased in d-gal-induced aged rat testes; additionally, antioxidant markers such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione (GSH), and catalase declined in the testes of these rat models11. Moreover, d-gal decreases testis weight, sperm count, and serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH) levels; however, no change in androgen levels have been observed owing to increased ROS levels and decreased antioxidant enzyme levels in mice12. In the female reproductive system, Zhang et al. reported that d-gal increases ROS, accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), and apoptotic granulosa cell death, which leads to failure of normal follicular development. FSH activation decreases and suppresses estradiol (E2) production in granulosa cells in d-gal-induced aging female models13,14.

Birth and developmental defects caused by various factors such as environmental toxicants, chromosomal defects, and genetic factors have become a worldwide concern15,16. In particular, the normal development of male germ cells in the fetus and neonate is critical for reproductive activity during adulthood. Testicular development requires multiple cascades of cellular differentiation and the activation of various genes involved in differentiation17. Meiosis is a major event in spermatogenesis, the process of sperm development, and commences approximately 7–8 days post-partum (dpp) in mouse testes. Damage to male germ cells during the first meiotic period after birth critically effects normal sperm production18. Sertoli cells, somatic cells located in the seminiferous tubules, play crucial roles during spermatogenesis. Their proliferation is more active before birth and extends up to 14 dpp in mice19,20. Fetal Leydig cells (FLCs) produce high levels of testosterone in both the fetus and neonate; moreover, this steroidogenic function of Leydig cells is essential for maintaining spermatogenesis21,22. Previously, we demonstrated that neonatal testes can develop into adult-like testes in in vitro organ culture systems23,24. In the present study, we investigated the effects of d-gal on neonatal testis development and testicular damage using an organ culture system. Furthermore, we studied the damage to each major cell type of the testis, including germ, Sertoli, and Leydig cells.

Results

In vitro culture of mouse testicular fragments with d-gal

To evaluate the effect of d-gal on neonatal testes development, mouse testicular fragments (MTFs) derived from 5.5 dpp testis were cultured in the presence or absence of d-gal (0, 50, 100, 200, or 500 mM) for 5 days. Histological analysis with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining showed the formation of seminiferous tubules with cells inside and outside, which was maintained even after 5 days of in vitro culture. MTFs exposed to 500 mM d-gal showed a partial depletion of testicular cells in the seminiferous tubules (Fig. 1A). To assess the characteristics and changes in the MTF culture exposed to d-gal, we examined the undifferentiated gene expression levels of spermatogonia owing to the ability of undifferentiated spermatogonia alone, including the SSC subpopulation, being able to repopulate seminiferous tubules depleted by d-gal. Characterization of the cultured MTF gene expression levels revealed that OCT4, GFRα-1, Sall4, and Pax7 markers of undifferentiated spermatogonia were significantly increased (Fig. 1B). Effects of d-gal on meiosis markers in MTFs

d-gal induces morphological change in the seminiferous tubules of MTFs in vitro (Fig. 1A). We examined the total germ cell markers VASA and DAZL to characterize the cells in the seminiferous tubules. There were no significant increases in the relative gene and protein levels (Fig. 2A). Previous studies reported that spermatogenesis is observed on day 2 of MTF culturing in vitro24. Therefore, we investigated whether d-gal inhibited germ cell differentiation in in vitro cultured MTFs. Exposure to d-gal for 5 days significantly decreased the expression of differentiation genes in germ cells, including Sycp3, Sohlh1, Sohlh2, Dmc1, and Stra8, compared with that in the control group (Fig. 2B). These results are consistent with the relative protein levels. The exposure of MTFs to d-gal decreased the expression of differentiated germ cell markers, suggesting that d-gal toxicity in germ cells may adversely affect spermatogenesis during development. Immunohistochemistry was used to evaluate the germ cell-specific marker VASA, undifferentiated germ cell marker SALL4, and differentiated germ cell marker SYCP3 in MTFs exposed to d-gal (Fig. 2C). The results showed that Vasa-positive cells were present in the MTFs, in both the control and those exposed to d-gal. A comparison between the number of Vasa + , SYCP3 + , and SALL4 + cells in control and d-gal-treated MTFs revealed that the number of SALL4 positive cells in d-gal-treated MTFs substantially increased; however, SYCP3 positive cells decreased (Fig. 2D). The expression of meiosis-related proteins SYCP3 and DMC1 decreased in the d-gal treated MTFs compared with that in the control (Fig. 2E). The protein levels of SOX9 were slightly higher in d-gal treated MTF compared to Controls (Fig. 3B). Effects of d-gal on Leydig cells in MTFs

Steroid hormones regulate physiological functions and are required for spermatogenesis; androgens are secreted by Leydig cells. Therefore, when the MTFs were exposed to d-gal, we assessed the characteristics of Leydig cells. Leydig cell related genes such as Cyp11a-1, StAR, 3β-HSD6, 3β-HSD1, 17β-HSD3, Cyp17a-1, and Insl3 significantly decreased in the d-gal exposed-MTFs group compared with those in the control group (Fig. 4A). Protein expression levels in the MTFs, measured using an antibody against the Leydig cell-specific marker 3β-HSD1, revealed a decrease upon exposure to d-gal, consistent with the pattern of gene expression levels (Fig. 4B). The 3β-HSD 6 positive cells identified through immunostaining were detected in both d-gal-treated and untreated MTFs, similar to the adult testis sample used as a positive control; however, the number of cells stained with green fluorescence in MTFs exposed to d-gal was significantly lower than that in the control (Fig. 4C). d-gal induced apoptosis in MTFs

We analyzed the apoptotic effects, senescence, and ROS responses of d-gal on MTFs cultured in vitro (Fig. 5). Our results showed that d-gal upregulated apoptosis-related genes such as BAX, BAD, and FAS in MTF in vitro (Fig. 5A). The caspase family is an enzyme that initiates apoptosis in cells, which is an important biological process. Cleaved caspase-3 and -8 propagate apoptotic signals to downstream targets through their enzymatic activity. Caspase activation and BAD, BAX, and BCL2 expression were evaluated using immunoblot analysis (Fig. 5B). The levels of pro-apoptotic proteins such as cleaved caspase-3 and -8 in MTF exposed to d-gal were significantly increased compared with those in the control group, and the level of Bcl-2 protein in MTF exposed to d-gal was lower than that in the control group. In MTFs exposed to d-gal, BAD and BAX proteins were significantly increased compared with those in the control group (Fig. 5B). This result is consistent with the increase in apoptosis-related genes based on previous mRNA levels (Fig. 5A,B). In addition, mRNA expression of senescence-related genes p53, p16, and p21 was upregulated in MTFs exposed to d-gal (Fig. 5C). DHE staining in response to ROS confirmed that the red fluorescence intensity in MTFs exposed to d-gal was more than threefold that of the control group (Fig. 5D). Discussion

In this study, we investigated the effects of d-gal and its metabolites on neonatal testes during testicular development. MTFs were cultured at a concentration of 500 mM D + glucose (D + glu) to determine the specific effects of d-gal on neonatal testis. There was no change in mRNA levels of Vasa, Sycp3, and 3β-HSD6 in MTFs cultured at 500 mM compared with those in the control group. Immunohistochemical staining using the above three markers also showed no change compared with the control group; however, a decrease in the mRNA levels of Sycp3 and 3β-HSD1 was observed in 500 mM d-gal treated MTFs (Supplementary Data 1). d-gal decreased cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner in mouse-derived testicular cells. In mouse spermatogonia type B GC1-spg, d-gal showed an IC50 value of 317. In Sertoli cells (TM4) and Leydig cells (TM3), IC50 values of 229 and 225 were confirmed, respectively (Supplementary Data 2). Damage to various organs, including both male and female reproductive organs, is caused by overproduction of free radicals owing to excess d-gal13,14. During postnatal development, germ cells undergo meiosis and transform into haploid spermatozoa. d-gal exposed MTFs showed that the expression levels of undifferentiated germ cell markers Gfr α-1, Oct4, Sall4, and PAX7 were significantly increased after a 5-day culture with d-gal. In contrast, the expression levels of the differentiation markers Sycp3, Sohlh1, Sohlh2, Dmc1, and Stra 8 were decreased in d-gal-exposed MTF compared with those in the control group. Furthermore, immunostaining results revealed that the percentage of SYCP3 + cells (meiosis marker) among VASA + cells (germ cell marker) was higher in the control than it was in d-gal-treated MTFs, but SALL4 + cells (undifferentiated cells) were lower in the control than they were in the d-gal-treated MTFs. These results suggest that d-gal disrupts germ cell differentiation.

Depending on the developmental conditions and levels of exposure, ROS can act positively or negatively on cells. Although considerable evidence shows that elevated ROS levels induce cell death, relatively low ROS levels promote cell growth25. In neural stem cells (NSCs), ROS generated by Nox2 activate the AKT pathway, promoting their proliferation. High endogenous ROS levels are essential for self-renewal in these cells26. In contrast, several studies have described the inhibitory effects of ROS on NSC proliferation27,28. Morimoto et al. investigated the effects of ROS on stem cell self-renewal in several tissues25. In particular, spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs) are undifferentiated germ cells in the seminiferous tubules of the testes, which typically require ROS for cell proliferation. SSCs depleted of ROS enhance self-renewal by increasing ROS levels through hydrogen peroxide treatment, which activates the mitogen-activated protein kinase/c-Jun N-terminal kinase (MAPK/JNK) signaling pathway. This is consistent with our data, which shows that SALL4 + cells were more abundant in d-gal-treated MTFs compared to the controls. In addition, spermatogonial stem cell self-renewal requires OCT4 expression in the testes29. As shown in Fig. 1b, the expression level of Oct 4 increased in the d-gal-treated samples. d-gal likely increases intracellular ROS levels, which are implicated in SSC maintenance25. In contrast, meiotic cells were markedly reduced in d-gal–treated MTFs compared with the controls. VASA, a broad germ cell marker, is expressed in spermatogonia, spermatocytes, and round spermatid, but absent in spermatozoa30. The expression levels of VASA did not change in the MTF groups with or without d-gal. In the control group (Fig. 2d), approximately 80% of VASA + germ cells were meiotic cells, which are differentiated germ cells. In contrast, only 20% of the cells in the d-gal-exposed MTFs were meiotic, and approximately 60% of the cells did not progress to the meiotic stage after d-gal treatment. Therefore, the proportion of differentiated germ cells decreased in the d-gal-treated groups, suggesting that that d-gal may affect the transition from undifferentiated to differentiated germ cells.

Various factors induce testicular oxidative stress, including testicular torsion, high testicular temperatures, varicocele, infection, hyperthyroidism, hormone imbalance, and exposure to toxins31. Elevated temperatures induce testicular damage associated with oxidative stress in the testes, eventually leading to male infertility. However, cell type-specific responses were observed in the testes, which comprised SSCs, spermatocytes, spermatozoa, Sertoli cells, and Leydig cells. Cellular responses to heat stress differ depending on the testis cell type. In particular, spermatocytes and mature sperm cells are sensitive to temperature changes in humans and rats32. Similarly, the degree of cell damage and response to d-gal exposure varied depending on the MTF cell type.

Wei et al. investigated the effects of di(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate (DEHP) on immature testes by exposing mice to DEHP from gestational day 13.5 to delivery and sacrificing the neonatal mice at 5 dpp. The results revealed that DEHP disrupted germ cell development by causing an imbalance between the self-renewal and differentiation of SSCs. Elevated oxidative stress and apoptosis, mediated by p53 activation, were observed in almost all testicular cells33.

The number of SOX9 + Sertoli cells in each tubule was not significantly different between d-gal-treated MTFs and controls. Additionally, PCNA was not detected in Sertoli cells of d-gal-treated MTFs. Similar to our results, a study reported that the number of Sertoli cells did not change in the testes of adult mice treated with d-gal at 120 mg/kg/day for 42 days; in addition, this study showed that only the ratio of spermatids and spermatozoa to Sertoli cells was significantly decreased in the testes of d-gal-administered mice, but not in neonatal testes. Reductions in sperm survival rate, normal sperm rate, and testosterone levels were also observed in the d-gal groups34.

Several studies have investigated the effects of d-gal on Sertoli and TM4 cells using in vitro systems. The expression of aging-related factors including p16INK4A, p21Waf1/Cip1, and p-γH2AX were clearly increased in 200 mM/L d-gal-exposed TM4 Sertoli cell for 48 h, and increased expression levels of inflammation-related proteins35. Another study also reported the effects of d-gal on TM4 cells related to testicular barrier function, showing that d-gal disrupts the tight and adherent junctions of these cell through the p38MAPK pathway36. Zhao et al. investigated the effects of flavonoids on d-gal-induced TM4 cell injury. After incubation with 100 mM d-gal in vitro, expression of antioxidative molecules, Nrf2, HO-1, and NQO-1, and cell survival were distinctly reduced in d-gal-treated TM4 cells35.

Steroidogenesis is a biosynthetic process for androgen production and is essential for testis development. Leydig cells are the most important source of androgen hormone production37. Postnatal Leydig cells are steroidogenically competent, and Leydig cell-specific markers such as lnsl3, LHR, and 3bHSD (steroidogenic enzyme) are expressed at this stage in Leydig cells38. However, the expression levels of steroidogenesis-related genes and 3bHSD protein were significantly decreased in MTF following d-gal treatment.

There are two types of Leydig cell: fetal Leydig cells (FLCs) and adult Leydig cells (ALCs). Generally, the population of FLCs is abundant in the testis of day-19 fetal rats. Their numbers dramatically decrease by day 4 in postnatal testes, reaching a minimum number 14 days after birth39. In addition, the expression levels of StAR, Cyp11a1, Cyp17a1, 3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 6 (3β-HSD 6), and 17β‐hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 3 (17β-HSD3) are higher in ALCs compared with those in FLCs40. Our results show that the expression of these genes are significantly downregulated by d-gal treatment. In particular, HSD17B3 was expressed only in ALCs and not in FLCs. Therefore, FLCs produce androstenedione, whereas ALCs produce testosterone41,42. In our gene expression data, the expression levels of HSD17B3 were relatively lower in d-gal–treated MTFs than those in controls. In other words, d-gal interrupts the normal development of Leydig cell in MTFs. In addition, Hsd3b1 (Hsd3b type 1) was expressed in both FLCs and ALCs, whereas Hsd3b6 (Hsd3b type 6) was expressed only in ALCs but not in FLCs. In the case of ALCs, three types of cell populations are known: HSD3B1 + /HSD3B6-, HSD3B1 + /HSD3B6 + , and HSD3B1-/HSD3B6 + . A few ALCs were also HSD3B6 negative, but no FLCs expressed HSD3B643.

In addition, our results show that the expression levels of pro-apoptotic genes, including Bad, Bax, Fas, cleaved-caspase-3, and caspase-8, were higher in d-gal-treated MTFs than those in the controls. Our results are consistent with those of most previous studies. d-gal induces necroptotic cell death in neuroblastomas44 and apoptosis in hepatocytes, glioblastomas, and cardiomyocytes7,45,46. In particular, d-gal induces cell death through the generation of ROS, particularly in aging and neurodegenerative diseases47–49. The current study demonstrated that d-gal induces cell damage through the apoptotic pathway in neonatal testicular tissue cultures at the developmental stage, especially by damaging germ cells in the tubules. These findings align with the observed effects of d-gal in vivo in mice and suggest that this model can be used to evaluate developmental damage in vitro.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethics

The mice used in the culture experiments were of the CD-1 (ICR) lineage. The 5-day-old male mice were purchased from Dae Han Bio Link (Eumseong, Republic of Korea) along with their mothers.

This animal study was conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines [PLoS Bio8 (6), e1000412, 2010; https://arriveguidelines.org/]. Approval for the experimental protocol was obtained from the Konkuk University committee and adhered to the principles outlined in the Konkuk University Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Register No. KU220000).

Testis organ culture method and treatment

Mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia with 20 mg/mL Avertin (2,2,2-tribromoehanol) (sigma-Aldrich, USA). The MTFs were cultured as previously described50,51. During each experiment, 20 testes from 10 male pups were harvested. One oval testis measuring 5–10 mm in diameter was dissected into six pieces measuring 2–3 mm in diameter. To prepare agarose gel blocks for the MTF culture, agarose powder (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in DW at 1.2% (w/v) and autoclaved. Then, 30 mL of the agarose solution was poured into a 10-cm dish, cooled, and cut into approximately 1 cm square pieces for the MTF culture. Agarose gels were soaked in the culture medium overnight before use in six-well microplates. Five MTFs were placed on a 1.2% (w/v) agarose gel and cultured for 5 days. The culture medium consisted of 10% (v/v) KnockOut serum replacement (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts) with α-minimum essential medium (WelGENE, Daegu, Korea) and was maintained below the upper surface of the agarose gel. Following cell viability tests of testicular cell lines treated with d-gal (GC1-spg: 317 mM, TM3:225.3 mM, and Tm4:229.1 mM), as reported previously36, mouse testicular fragments were treated with d-gal. d-gal was dissolved in the culture medium at concentrations of 50, 100, 200, and 500 mM, and the medium was filtered before use. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis

MTFs cultured for 5 days were placed in paraffin blocks. The MTFs were washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C, and subsequently dehydrated in DW, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100% for 6 h. The dehydrated tissues were cleared in xylene for 30 min, embedded in paraffin at 65 °C for 2 h, and hardened into blocks. The testes were sectioned at 4–5 μm thickness using a microtome (Leica, Nussloch, Germany), placed on a glass slide, and dried at 50 °C. MTFs slides were divided into three different regions, deparaffinized with xylene for 10 min, and treated with 100%, 95%, 90%, 80%, 70%, or H2O for 5 min each. The rehydrated MTF slides were stained with HE to distinguish between the nucleus and cytoplasm. After mounting the sections under a cover glass, the slides were observed under a light microscope at 100 × and 200 × magnifications (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Dihydroethidium (DHE) was used to determine ROS in the slides of the MTFs, and was detected using the cell-permeable fluorescent dye dihydroethidium. Specifically, 10 μM DHE was used to induce the reaction for 30 min in the dark at 37 °C. At the end of the incubation period, the slides were washed with PBS and the nuclei were stained with 1 µg/mL 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) in PBS for 10 min and mounted on coverslips with mounting solution (DAKO, Carpinteria, CA; S3025).

For immunohistochemical analysis, rehydrated MTF slides were boiled in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer for 20 min to induce antigen masking. The slides of the MTFs from which the antigen was recovered and permeabilized with PBS containing 0.03% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Nonspecific protein binding was blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Slides of MTFs from which antigens were recovered were incubated overnight at 4 °C with 1:300 diluted primary antibodies against VASA, SALL4, SOX9, vimentin, and 3β-HSD. Table 1 lists the sources of the antibodies used for immunostaining. After washing the MTFs slides thrice with PBS, they were treated with an Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibody diluted 1:500 in 1% BSA solution for 1 h at room temperature. The secondary antibodies used were Alexa Fluor 568 donkey anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG; Cat: A10042), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (Cat: A11001), Alexa Fluor 568 donkey anti-mouse IgG (Cat: A10037), and Alexa Fluor. Goat anti-rabbit IgG (Cat: A11008) from Life Technologies (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used. After washing thrice with PBS, tissue sections were incubated with 1 µg/mL DAPI in PBS for 10 min and mounted on coverslips with mounting solution (DAKO, Carpinteria, CA; S3025). MTFs were imaged using a Nikon E-800 fluorescence microscope (Nikon) with Motic Images Advanced 3.2 software. RNA extraction and quantitative PCR (QPCR)

cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg total RNA using the SuperScript™ III reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen, CA, USA) kit with oligo dT primer according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For gene detection, total RNA was extracted from 5-day cultured MTFs using a Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). Briefly, cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA from 4 MTFs using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, California) with Oligo(dT30)-primer, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The 30 ng of diluted cDNA was amplified using a quantitative PCR (qPCR) system (Qiagen, Rotor-Gene Q). qPCR analysis was performed based on a previously described method51, starting with denaturation and polymerase activation at 94 °C for 1 min, and followed by 40 cycles at 94 °C for 10 s, 57 °C for 10 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. Data were analyzed using the Ct method52 and glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an endogenous gene. After normalization to GAPDH cDNA levels, which were reflected in the ΔCt values, the relative quantification (RQ) of the fold change for each treatment, compared with the reference control, was determined using the following equation: RQ = 2(− ΔCt)/2(− ΔCt reference). The mean RQ and SEM were plotted on a log 2 scale. Immunoblotting

The primary antibodies used are listed in Table 1. Total protein lysates of MTF were extracted using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) and homogenized twice for 3 s at 4 °C. MTF debris was removed by centrifugation at 15,000×g for 30 min, and the super-natant was collected. Standard protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay kit (Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL, USA; #23 227). Protein samples (30 µg /lane) were loaded and separated on 10% acrylamide gels and 4%–20% mini TGX gels (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA, #456-1096). These were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes and incubated with primary antibodies (1:1000 dilution in Tween 20/Tris buffered saline containing 1% bovine serum albumin) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST three times for 10 min each, the membranes were incubated with anti-mouse, anti-rabbit, and anti-goat horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA) diluted to 1:10,000. Minimal protein signals were visualized at room temperature using an ECL substrate (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL, USA), and HyBlot CL autoradiography film (Denville Scientific, Metuchen, NJ, USA; #E3018) was used for signal testing. β-Actin was used as a control for normalization.

Statistical analysis

SPSS statistical package ver. 26.0 for Windows (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for data analysis. All data are expressed as the mean ± standard error. Differences between the two groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test, and one way ANOVA for Tukey’s Honest Significant Difference post-hoc test. Statistical significance is indicated as *P < 0.05 and **P < 0.01. Graphs were created using the SigmaPlot 8.0 software.
