Neuroprotective effects of Urolithin A and B in an intracerebroventricular streptozotocin-induced Alzheimer’s-like model in rats
Authors: Mona Taghizade Salari, Kianoush Gholami, Leila Khani, Hananeh Ahmadnia, Milad Iranshahy, Mehrdad Iranshahi, Mohammad Tohidy Majd, Morteza Behnam-Rassouli, Omid Yazarlu, Maede Hasanpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD), a prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, is characterized by amyloid beta and tau protein aggregation, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation. Urolithin A (Uro A) and Urolithin B (Uro B), known for their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective properties, are promising therapeutic candidates for AD. This study evaluated the effects of Uro A and Uro B on memory, anxiety-like behaviors, brain oxidative stress markers, and histopathological changes in male rats subjected to streptozotocin (STZ)-induced AD. Wistar rats were divided into eight groups (n = 7) and treated with Uro A or Uro B (10 or 20 mg/kg), memantine (positive control), or vehicle control for 14 days following intracerebroventricular STZ injection. Behavioral tests, biochemical analyses, and histological evaluations were conducted after six weeks. Modest improvements in memory performance were observed (p < 0.05) in the shuttle box test and reduced anxiety-like behaviors in the elevated plus-maze test. Biochemical analyses revealed decreased oxidative stress markers (malondialdehyde and hydroperoxides) and increased total antioxidant capacity (p < 0.05). Also, it seems that both compounds can reduce necrotic neurons and amyloid-like depositions in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex, and no adverse effects on the histology of the liver, kidney, pancreas, and testis were observed. These findings demonstrate the therapeutic potential of Uro A and Uro B in ameliorating cognitive deficits, oxidative stress, and neuronal damage in AD. Further studies are needed to explore the mechanisms underlying these effects and their applicability in clinical contexts.Keywords: Urolithin, Alzheimer’s disease, Neurodegeneration, Oxidative stress, Memory, Streptozotocin

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is increasingly recognized as a multifactorial disorder characterized by an interconnected pathological cascade involving amyloid-β accumulation, tau hyperphosphorylation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, synaptic loss, and neuroinflammation. These processes interact synergistically rather than occurring independently, ultimately leading to progressive neuronal degeneration and cognitive decline [1] Among these pathological mechanisms, oxidative stress plays a central role as both a contributor to and a consequence of mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and amyloid toxicity. Elevated reactive oxygen species can impair synaptic plasticity and neuronal survival, thereby accelerating disease progression [2, 3].

The FDA has approved only four drugs to treat AD, and while they provide modest symptom relief, they do not prevent cognitive decline, neuronal loss, or brain atrophy [4]. An investigation into alternative therapeutic options is imperative due to the difficulties associated with treating AD and the inadequacy of current treatments. Neuroprotective effects of Urolithins are one such promising area of research [5]. Urolithin A (Uro A), an organic compound featuring benzo-coumarin scaffolds, is a metabolite synthesized from ellagitannins and ellagic acid by the gut microbiota in the colon. Various plant-based sources, such as pomegranates, berries (such as strawberries, raspberries, and blackberries), walnuts, almonds, and other nuts, are abundant sources of Uro A. These sources provide the nutritional precursors that the gut microbiota needs to produce Uro A [6]. In the human intestine, Urolithin B (Uro B) is synthesized from Uro A through a microbial transformation process, resulting in a structure that is similar to Uro A but lacks one hydroxyl group [7]. Uro A and Uro B are gut microbiota-derived metabolites of ellagitannins that have demonstrated neuroprotective properties beyond their systemic metabolic and anti-aging effects. Experimental studies indicate their ability to modulate mitochondrial function, reduce oxidative stress, and attenuate neuroinflammatory responses, suggesting potential relevance in neurodegenerative disorders [8].

Research indicates that Uro A has prominent anti-inflammatory properties through the inhibition of key inflammatory signaling pathways, including NF-κB, and the suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines [7, 9, 10]. The anti-aging effects of Uro A stem from its capacity to stimulate mitophagy and promote mitochondrial biogenesis [11]. The antioxidant activity of Uro A has been thoroughly investigated and recorded in numerous studies [9, 12]. Recent research suggests that Uro A increases antioxidant enzyme activity, reduces lipid peroxidation, and elevates the expression of antioxidant proteins, including peroxiredoxins, catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) [13].

Preclinical studies have demonstrated the beneficial effects of UroA on age-related brain dysfunctions. In vitro studies indicated that UroA exhibits a neuroprotective effect under aging conditions characterized by oxidative stress, inflammation, and high glucose levels [14]. In vivo studies showed significant enhancements in cognition, learning, and memory among aged animals and animal models of neurodegenerative diseases [12].

Uro B has been shown to inhibit the production of nitric oxide (NO) and pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, while increasing the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 [15] reduces intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and upregulates heme oxygenase-1 expression via the Nrf2/ARE signaling pathway [16], inhibits NF-κB activity and suppresses the phosphorylation of JNK, ERK, and Akt, while enhancing AMPK phosphorylation, which are associated with anti-inflammatory processes [17]. Uro B has been shown to enhance conditions related to brain aging and cognitive impairment in a rat model induced by D-galactose [18] and on nematode Caenorhabditis elegans [19]. A study of H2O2-treated T24 cells indicated that Uro B was linked to reduced malondialdehyde levels and enhanced superoxide dismutase activity [20]. In another study involving quinolinic acid-induced neurotoxicity, Uro B exhibited potent antioxidant and neuroprotective effects [21].

These results together demonstrate the significant promise of Uro A and Uro B as compounds that target oxidative stress and age-related brain damage. Therefore, the current study intends to investigate the neuroprotective effects of Uro A and Uro B on memory, anxiety-like behaviors, and histopathological changes in male rats that were given streptozotocin-induced AD. While Urolithin A has been previously studied in transgenic and metabolic models of Alzheimer’s disease, the present study is the first to investigate the histological and behavioral impact of Urolithin B in an ICV-STZ-induced Alzheimer’s-like model. Furthermore, the combined use of behavioral observations, biochemical assays of oxidative stress, and histopathological evaluations offers a multifaceted approach. Notably, the inclusion of a comparative analysis with memantine, a clinically approved AD medication, enhances the translational relevance and novelty of the findings.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of Uro A

Initially, a round-bottom flask was charged with 2-Bromo-5-methoxybenzoic acid (5.68 g, 25 mmol), 2.0 equivalents (equiv.) of Resorcinol (5.51 g, 50 mmol), and 6.25 vol. of water (37.5 mL). Then 2.2 equiv. 50% sodium hydroxide (4.4 g, 55 mmol) was added to the mixture with agitation at 20–45° C for 20 min. In the next step, 0.001 equiv. Copper sulfate pentahydrate (6.24 mg, 0.025 mmol) was added to the reaction mixture and refluxed at 90 °C for at least three hours. After this time, the solution was cooled to 20 °C and stirred in the same way for 1 h. Finally, the methylurolithin A precipitate was filtered. The filter was washed with 4 volumes of water and 3 volumes of cold methanol. Then, the solvent was removed in a vacuum oven at 40 °C. In the next step, aluminum chloride was used for the demethylation process. First, (4.84 g, 20 mmol), 5 equiv. was mixed with Aluminum chloride (13.33 g, 100 mmol), 10 vol. of Toluene (50 mL), and stirred vigorously for at least 2 h at 90 °C. After that, the reaction was allowed to cool to 50 °C, and 10 to 15 volumes of water were slowly added. Stirring was continued for 2 h, and the precipitate was filtered. The resulting precipitate was washed with 8 volumes of water and 4 volumes of cold methanol and dried in a vacuum oven (Yield, 78%).

Synthesis of Uro B

Initially, a round-bottom flask was charged with a mixture of 2-bromobenzoic acid (3 g, 14.9 mmol), resorcinol (3.3 g, 30 mmol), and 30 mL of water. After that, a solution of NaOH (1.2 g, 30 mmol) in water (14 mL) was added dropwise to the mixture with agitation at room temperature. The addition of sodium hydroxide is slightly exothermic. The reaction mixture was heated under reflux for 30 min. After this time, aqueous CuSO4 (0.001 equivalent) was added to the mixture and refluxed again for 2 h. During the reaction, Uro B precipitated as a shiny white powder. After this time, the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature, and the precipitate was filtered off and washed several times with water. The product obtained is pure enough for further investigation (Yield, 68%).

Animals

Experiments were conducted on male Wistar rats with a weight range of 200–250 g. Only male rats were used to avoid potential confounding effects of hormonal cycles on cognitive and oxidative parameters, which may require separate analyses. Animals were obtained from the Razi Vaccine and Serum Research Institute (Mashhad, Iran), a licensed and accredited animal breeding center, and kept in the departmental animal house of Ferdowsi University of Mashhad. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and investigators conducting behavioral, biochemical, and histological assessments were blinded to treatment allocation. Rats were housed in standard polypropylene cages (5 animals per cage) with wood-chip bedding and environmental enrichment under controlled temperature (25 ± 1 °C) and a 12-h light/dark cycle. Rats were given access to a standard rat diet and tap water, ad libitum.

Surgical procedure and experimental design

Rats were anesthetized using an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (80 mg/kg BW) (Alfasan, Netherlands) combined with xylazine (10 mg/kg BW) (Interchemie, Netherlands). Adequate depth of anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of corneal and pedal withdrawal reflexes before initiation of surgical procedures, and the animal underwent stereotaxic cannulation [22] for intracerebroventricular (ICV) injections. Briefly, the rat’s head was fixed in the stereotaxic device (Stoelting, USA), a sagittal incision was made in the scalp, and one hole was drilled through the skull for placement of the cannula injection into the right lateral cerebral ventricle. The stereotaxic coordinates for ICV cannulation were anterior-posterior direction (AP) 0.8 mm, lateral (L) 1.4 mm, and dorsal-ventral direction (DV) 3.6 mm relative to bregma, right lateral ventricle, and skull surface to depths [23]. Following surgery, animals received postoperative care to ensure ethical compliance and welfare. Enrofloxacin (Baytril) was administered at a dose of 5 mg/kg subcutaneously once daily for 14 consecutive days as a prophylactic antibiotic. For analgesia, meloxicam (Meloxivet) was given at 2 mg/kg subcutaneously immediately after surgery, followed by 1 mg/kg once daily for the next two days. This regimen aligns with rodent postoperative protocols aimed at minimizing infection risk and ensuring effective pain management. Animals have received either ICV streptozotocin (STZ) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) (3 mg/kg BW using a micro syringe) or ICV ascorbic acid-saline 0.1% (STZ solvent) (10 µl) [24]. To ensure diffusion of the administered drug, the cannula was left in place for 2 min following the injection.

Animals were randomly assigned to eight groups (n = 7 per group) as outlined in Table 1. The Control group received no intervention. The Sham group received an ICV injection of ascorbic acid-saline (0.1%), the vehicle used for STZ, and an IP injection of vehicle (DMSO). Ascorbic acid was selected to ensure the stability of STZ and to control any potential effects of the solvent itself, following the methodology described by [24]. The Negative control (Neg. Con) group received ICV STZ (3 mg/kg body weight [BW]) without subsequent treatment, serving to establish the AD model. The Positive control (Pos. Con) group was administered ICV STZ and treated with memantine (2 mg/kg BW, IP) [25]. Four treatment groups received ICV STZ followed by daily IP administration of either Urolithin A or Urolithin B at doses of 10 or 20 mg/kg BW for 14 consecutive days. The selected doses were based on previous preclinical studies demonstrating neuroprotective efficacy and acceptable safety profiles in rodents, with evidence suggesting sufficient bioavailability to exert central nervous system effects [26, 27]. Treatment was initiated after disease induction to model a therapeutic rather than preventive intervention, reflecting clinical conditions in which treatment typically begins after symptom onset. Reportedly, there is a reduction in infarct volume and enhanced functional recovery in mice receiving 2.5–5 mg/kg Uro A pre- and post-MCAO. Behavioral assessments were conducted on day 15, and animals were maintained for six weeks post-surgery before being sacrificed for biochemical and histological analyses. Sample sizes were determined based on previous studies employing similar models and endpoints to ensure adequate statistical power while minimizing animal use.

Behavioral analysis

Passive avoidance testing was selected to assess learning and memory deficits associated with hippocampal dysfunction, a key feature of Alzheimer’s-like pathology. Although additional paradigms such as the Morris water maze or Y-maze provide complementary information, the passive avoidance task is a validated and widely used measure of long-term memory in rodent models.

Passive avoidance (shuttle box) test

Memory retention deficit was evaluated by a step-through passive avoidance apparatus. The apparatus consisted of equal-sized light and dark compartments (30 × 20 × 30 cm). The floor of the dark compartment comprised a metal grid attached to a shock scrambler. The two compartments were separated by a trapdoor that could be raised to 10 cm. One day before ICV STZ injection, rats were placed in the light compartment and allowed to move freely for 2 min for habituation with the gate opened (habituation phase). The acquisition phase started after 60 min; rats were placed in the light compartment again. The time that elapsed before each animal entered the dark compartment and had all four paws inside the dark compartment was measured, in seconds, and termed ‘transfer latency’. Promptly after the rat entered the dark compartment with all four paws inside, the trapdoor was closed, and an electric foot shock (50 V AC) was applied for 3 s. After 5 s, the rat was taken out of the dark compartment and returned to its cage. Rats that had a transfer latency of more than 60 s were excluded from further experiments. On the surgery day (before surgery) and 14 days after (i.e., days 0 and 15), the transfer latency and the time spent in the dark compartment were measured in the same manner as the acquisition phase. However, the foot shock was not delivered in this stage (retention phase), and the latency time was recorded to a maximum of 300 s. To improve the reliability and validity of the foot shock-avoidance test, the grid, as well as the rats’ paws, were moistened with water before foot shock because of wide variability in paw skin resistance [28].

Elevated plus maze test

The plus-maze is constructed from two opposing open arms (50 × 10 cm), crossing with two closed arms that have the same measurements and walls that are 40 cm high. The arms are connected by a central square (10 × 10 cm). On day 15, rats were placed individually in the central square. The behavior of the rat was videotaped using a high-resolution video camera mounted in the apparatus during the 5-minute test period. The following parameters were measured: The percentage of open arm times (%OAT) and the percentage of open arm entries (%OAE). Indexes of locomotor activity also included the total number of arms entered and the total number of closed arms entered [23]. To minimize the confounding effects of locomotor activity on anxiety-like behavior, total arm entries were also monitored as an index of general activity.

Behavioral tests were conducted in a fixed sequence with sufficient recovery intervals between tasks to minimize carry-over effects.

Histological analysis

Histological evaluation was performed qualitatively by experienced observers blinded to the experimental groups. Due to technical limitations and the limited number of available sections, quantitative or semi-quantitative analysis was not conducted.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining

On the 42nd post-surgery day, 3 rats were randomly selected out of each group for anesthesia, paraformaldehyde perfusion, and decapitation. According to the importance of certain tissues, the animal’s brain, liver, kidney, pancreas, and testis were removed quickly and stored in 10% paraformaldehyde solution for 24 h. Then, samples were subjected to graded ethanol and xylene transparency and finally embedded in paraffin. For brains, particularly, continuous coronal slices (5 μm thickness) were prepared according to the Paxinos and Watson atlas of the rat brain for the hippocampus (from − 3.2 to -4.2 mm bregma) [29]. The first five of every ten slides were selected for hematoxylin-eosin staining. Photos of the hippocampus and neocortex were taken by an optical microscope equipped with a camera (200×; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Congo red staining

To test whether the ICV-STZ model induces amyloid-like deposition in the rat brain, the blocks of the paraffin-embedded brain were regularly cut (5 μm), and then the sections were stained with Congo-red dye [30].

Biochemical analysis

Similarly, on the 42nd post-surgery day, the remaining 4 rats were anesthetized, saline perfused, and decapitated. Then, their brains were quickly removed, washed with ice-cold saline, and stored at − 80 °C.

For histological and biochemical analyses, animals were deeply anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg BW) and xylazine (15 mg/kg BW) to ensure complete unconsciousness. Following confirmation of deep anesthesia, transcardial perfusion with normal saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde was performed for histological analysis, after which animals were humanely sacrificed by decapitation in accordance with approved ethical guidelines.

Tissue preparation

Animals were euthanized using ketamine and xylazine, followed by confirmation of death according to institutional guidelines. Brain tissue samples were thawed and homogenized with 10 times (w/v) ice-cold 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). Aliquots of homogenates from rat brains were separated and used to determine lipid peroxidation and antioxidant capacity. The homogenates were centrifuged at 1600 x g for 10 min, 1000 x g for 15 min, and 4000 x g for 20 min at 4°c, and then the supernatant was used for malondialdehyde (MDA), total antioxidant capacity (TAC), and hydrogen peroxide (HPO) assays, respectively. Protein concentration was determined according to the method of Bradford assay [31] using purified bovine serum albumin as the standard.

Antioxidant and oxidant factors

Malondialdehyde

To investigate the concentration of MDA, we used an MDA assay kit (Kushan Zist, Tehran, Iran). MDA, a measure of lipid peroxidation, was determined using thiobarbituric acid (TBA). The MDA in the samples reacts with TBA at an acidic pH and generates a pink color MDA-TBA adduct, which can be easily quantified photometrically (530–540 nm) or fluorometrically (Ex/Em = 532/553 nm). To quantify the MDA concentration, 100 µl of the sample was prepared according to the protocol. Then the absorbance of the samples was read at a wavelength of 535 ± 5 nm.

Since MDA concentration should be normalized to homogenate protein content and reported as µM MDA/mg protein, protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay. To determine the protein concentration, 100 µl of the sample was added to 1 mL of Bradford reagent. Then the absorbance of the samples was read at a wavelength of 595 nm. Finally, to evaluate MDA concentration in the brain cortex, we plotted a standard curve from the obtained results using GraphPad Prism software.

Hydrogen peroxide

HPO was measured based on the oxidation-reduction colorimetric assay. In this research for HPO level assessment, the HPO Assay kit (ZellBio GmbH, Germany) was utilized. To evaluate the HPO concentration, 100 µl of the sample was added to 100µL of H2O2 Reagent and incubated at room temperature for 5–10 min. Then the absorbance of the samples was read at a wavelength of 546 nm, and the final concentration of HPO was measured using a standard curve.

Total antioxidant capacity

TAC was measured using the TAC assay kit (Kushan Zist, Tehran, Iran), utilizing a peroxidase chromogenic substrate (ABTS) that produces a water-soluble (blue-green) chromogen upon oxidation by ferryl myoglobin radicals. The rate of formation of colored chromogen is inhibited by the presence of antioxidants and can be measured photometrically. To measure the TAC, 10 µl of the sample was prepared according to the protocol. Then, the absorbance of the samples was evaluated at 412 ± 7 nm using a plate reader. The final concentration of TAC was measured using a standard curve.

Ethical consideration

All experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with international guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the Iran Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. IR.UM.REC.1401.213). All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and to reduce the number of animals used.

Statistical analysis

Behavioral data collected across multiple time points were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) where appropriate. All statistical analyses were performed using the R programming language version 4.3.0. ANOVA was performed, and following a significant P-value, post-hoc analysis (Tukey’s test) was used for multiple comparisons. Differences (P < 0.05) between experimental groups at each point were considered statistically significant. All graphs were created using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1. Where appropriate, effect sizes and confidence intervals were considered in interpreting the results.

Passive avoidance (shuttle box) test

Transfer latency

Statistical analysis employing one-way ANOVA in the retention test revealed a significant difference in transfer latency across all groups (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1B). Effects of Urolithin A & B on neurodegeneration in hippocampus and cortex

Histological observations were descriptive in nature and intended to provide morphological support for the biochemical and behavioral findings.

Figure 3 illustrates the histological findings from the cerebral cortex and hippocampus (CA1, CA3, and DG) among different experimental groups. The injection of STZ resulted in diminished neuronal density in the CA1, CA3, and DG subregions, coupled with an elevation in the quantity of neurons displaying dark nuclei (necrotic neurons), signifying neuronal atrophy.

Effects of Urolithin A and B on Neurodegeneration in Hippocampus and Cortex. Photomicrographs of hematoxylin and eosin-stained sections of CA1, CA3 regions of the hippocampus, dentate gyrus (DG), and cerebral cortex showed normal neurons (white arrows), necrotic and dark-colored neurons (thin black arrows), and neurofibrillary tangles (thick black arrows). (Hematoxylin and Eosin, ×400)

Histological observations (n = 2) suggest neurodegeneration in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex of STZ-treated rats compared to control and sham groups; however, it seems that the administration of Uro A and B alleviated these alterations. The groups treated with Uro A & B exhibited a major reduction in necrosis or an enhancement in healthy neurons, which were identified by their transparent cytoplasm and distinctly oval-shaped nuclei (Fig. 3). These histopathological findings qualitatively indicate a neuroprotective effect of Uro A and Uro B in relation to STZ-induced neuronal damage. This correlates with other research showing Uro A’s capacity to mitigate memory deficits, diminish neuroinflammation, and lower amyloid beta concentrations in animal models of AD [5, 33].

Effects of Urolithin A & B on Aβ aggregation in hippocampus and cortex

Our research employed Congo red staining to identify amyloid-like deposits in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex. Although amyloid-like deposition was absent in the brain tissue of both the control and sham groups, it was present in the brain tissue of the Neg. In the control and other experimental groups that received STZ, the presence of amyloid-like deposits is obvious. By histological comparison of brain tissues, the use of Uro A and B may likely decrease the formation of amyloid-like deposits (Fig. 4).

Furthermore, Uro A has demonstrated the ability to regulate the activation of microglia and astrocytes, hence reinforcing its function in mitigating neuroinflammation and neuronal injury [33].

Effects of Urolithin A & B on critical organs

As shown in Fig. 5, the qualitative results from histological analysis of critical organs revealed no significant pathological changes in the tissue structure of these organs across all experimental groups. The liver displayed a standard hepatic lobular architecture with intact hepatocytes, and no signs of inflammation, necrosis, or fibrosis were seen. Likewise, kidney sections exhibited normal glomerular and tubular morphology, devoid of interstitial inflammation, tubular damage, or vascular changes. In addition, histological evaluation of the pancreas revealed standard tissue architecture, including intact pancreatic acini and well-preserved islets of Langerhans. Similarly, microscopic examination of the testicular tissue revealed a typical architecture of the seminiferous tubules, characterized by a defined basement membrane and intact germinal layers. No histopathological lesions were observed in the testis. Although our data do not sufficiently support these statements, they suggest that the IP administration of Uro A and Uro B was likely not detrimental to the structural integrity of these critical organs. More research and more samples are needed to obtain more accurate results. Prior research further supports this concept by exhibiting the safety profile of urolithins in preclinical settings [34].

Discussion

A key limitation of this study is the limited sample size (n = 2) used for histological analysis. As a result, observations related to neurodegeneration, amyloid-like formation, and organ toxicity are strictly qualitative and exploratory. Without quantitative analysis and adequate statistical power, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn. Future studies with larger sample sizes and image-based quantification are needed to validate these preliminary histological findings.

The improvement in passive avoidance performance suggests that Urolithin A and B may preserve hippocampal-dependent memory processes impaired by STZ administration. Rather than reflecting a single behavioral effect, these findings likely represent functional recovery associated with reduced oxidative stress and preservation of neuronal integrity. Similar improvements reported in other neurodegenerative models support the notion that urolithins may enhance synaptic function and neuronal resilience through antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms.

The cognitive impairment induced by STZ is consistent with the disruption of hippocampal-dependent memory processes reported in similar models. The protective effects of urolithins observed in the present study may involve multiple mechanisms, including attenuation of tau pathology, modulation of inflammatory signaling, and enhancement of neuronal survival pathways, as suggested by previous experimental studies.

This therapeutic effect may be attributed to their ability to modulate neuroinflammatory processes and enhance neuroprotection, as evidenced by their influence on key signaling pathways involved in stress response and emotional regulation [18, 35]. Anxiety-like behavior observed in STZ-treated animals may reflect dysfunction of limbic circuits vulnerable to oxidative stress and neuroinflammation. Modulation of these pathways by urolithins could contribute to the behavioral improvements, although anxiety is not a core feature of Alzheimer’s disease, and the relevance of this finding should be interpreted cautiously. The improvement in behavioral performance observed in treated groups was consistent with the reduction in oxidative stress markers, suggesting a functional relationship between biochemical restoration and cognitive outcomes [36, 37]. While the findings suggest a protective effect of urolithins, causal relationships between treatment and amyloid reduction or neuronal preservation cannot be definitively established from the present data.

In line with previous research, ICV administration of STZ contributed to extensive neuronal death in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex, areas closely associated with memory deficits and cognitive dysfunctions characteristic of AD [38]. The CA1 region of the hippocampus revealed remarkable susceptibility to STZ-induced toxicity, aligning with previous investigations that emphasize its vulnerability to neurodegeneration in AD models [39, 40]. The current investigation corroborates prior results, since significant morphological changes were found in the hippocampus and cortex of STZ-treated rats. Treatment with Uro A and Uro B appeared to diminish these alterations, indicating their strong ability to preserve neuronal integrity and alleviate STZ-induced neurodegeneration. Uro A demonstrated a protective effect, suggesting an enhanced neuroprotective impact, likely due to its higher antioxidant activity resulting from an extra hydroxyl group in its chemical structure in comparison with Uro B.

This correlates with other research showing Uro A’s capacity to mitigate memory deficits, diminish neuroinflammation, and lower amyloid beta concentrations in animal models of AD [5, 33]. Furthermore, Uro A has demonstrated the ability to regulate the activation of microglia and astrocytes, hence reinforcing its function in mitigating neuroinflammation and neuronal injury [33]. It is noteworthy that our study is the first to examine the effects of Uro B on histological alterations in the ICV STZ-induced AD model. Although Uro B exhibited protective effects, its efficacy seemed somewhat inferior to that of Uro A. Both compounds exhibited significant promise in maintaining healthy neuronal morphology and mitigating the neurotoxic effects of STZ.

Our findings align with previous studies indicating that ICV injection of STZ compromises the structural and functional integrity of the cortical and hippocampal areas, contributing to the accumulation of beta-amyloid (Aβ) aggregates in the brain [41]. The current research indicates that the Neg. Con group had a significant increase in amyloid plaque development compared to the control group [42]. Nonetheless, treatment with Uro A and Uro B appeared to attenuate amyloid-like deposition. The exact mechanism is still to be clarified; however, this action may be associated with their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory capabilities, which have demonstrated the ability to inhibit the molecular pathways that promote Aβ aggregation. Additional research investigating these pathways might provide a greater understanding of their significance in the prevention and treatment of AD.

The ICV injection of STZ does not directly impact the other organs in rats; it is especially employed to induce AD-like pathology in the brain without eliciting systemic symptoms commonly associated with peripheral STZ administration [40]. The absence of histological abnormalities in the above-mentioned organs following treatment with Uro A and Uro B indicates that these chemicals are well-tolerated and do not cause significant toxicity in these essential organs under the specified experimental conditions. Prior research further supports this concept by exhibiting the safety profile of urolithins in preclinical settings [34]. The maintenance of hepatic, renal, pancreatic, and testicular structures further underscore the prospective translational use of Uro A and Uro B, as systemic toxicity continues to be a vital concern in drug development. Further research on long-term exposure and increased dosages is necessary to thoroughly assess their safety profile.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the neuroprotective effects of urolithins in the STZ model are likely mediated by multiple interconnected mechanisms, including antioxidant activity, modulation of neuroinflammation, and preservation of neuronal integrity.

Conclusion

The present study assessed the efficacy of Uro A and Uro B in ameliorating memory impairments, anxiety-like behaviors, and histopathological changes in an ICV-STZ-induced Alzheimer’s-like model. The results from our study show that Uro A and Uro B significantly improved memory performance by a decrease in transfer latency and shorter time spent in the dark compartment in the shuttle box test, which provides some evidence of the potential of urolithins for cognitive improvement, possibly mediated through their ability to counteract oxidative stress.

The increased levels of oxidative stress markers, such as MDA and HPO, in STZ-treated rats, along with the decreased TAC, were significantly reversed by Uro A and Uro B treatment due to their potent antioxidant activity and potential neuroprotective effects. The urolithins also attenuated anxiety-like behaviors as indicated by the elevated plus-maze test, suggesting another impact on the neuropsychiatric symptoms related to neurodegenerative diseases. Histological studies have shown the neuroprotective effect of Uro A and Uro B. Treatment with either of these two compounds appeared to diminish STZ-induced neuronal loss in both the hippocampus and cerebral cortex by reducing the number of necrotic neurons and maintaining the integrity of normal neuron morphology. Congo red staining provides morphological evidence of amyloid-like deposits but does not allow precise quantification or molecular characterization. Congo red staining showed that Uro A and Uro B appeared to reduce amyloid-like deposition in these brain regions, known to be fundamental pathological hallmarks associated with AD. It is worth mentioning that Uro A provided slightly better results, due to its stronger antioxidant properties, likely resulting from an additional hydroxyl group when compared to Uro B. In particular, there were no harmful effects of administering Uro A and Uro B on vital organs, such as the liver, kidneys, pancreas, and testis, which suggests a generally positive safety profile. Overall, our study may highlight the therapeutic potential of Uro A and Uro B in the treatment of cognitive impairment, oxidative stress, and neurodegenerative pathology in AD. These results provide a starting point for further studies that can give more details concerning specific mechanisms underlying these effects and potential clinical applications. These findings support the potential neuroprotective role of urolithins in an Alzheimer’s-like condition, although further studies are required to confirm disease-modifying effects.
