Challenges in the management of advanced rhabdomyosarcoma of the shoulder in a teenage boy: A case report
Authors: Jay Lodhia, Evance Salvatory Rwomurushaka, Felister Uisso, Mujaheed Suleman, Alex Mremi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceDelayed presentation and advanced-stage diagnosis of soft-tissue malignancies such as rhabdomyosarcoma remain major challenges in low-resource settings, often resulting in poor outcomes and complex management needs.Case presentationWe report the case of a 19-year-old Tanzanian male with advanced rhabdomyosarcoma of the right upper limb. Limited health literacy, cultural beliefs, and economic constraints delayed his initial presentation. Despite early care at a regional facility, poor referral processes and inability to afford imaging led to a one-year delay in definitive treatment. He ultimately presented with a large, ulcerated, maggot-infested tumor requiring shoulder disarticulation and forequarter amputation. Recurrent wound infections necessitated prolonged antibiotic use, raising concerns about antimicrobial resistance.Clinical discussionThis case highlights multifactorial barriers to timely cancer care, including sociocultural factors, financial limitations, and weak referral systems. The advanced presentation necessitated radical surgery, which could have been avoided with early detection. The case underscores the importance of cancer awareness programs, improved referral pathways, and antimicrobial stewardship in chronic cancer care.ConclusionThis case emphasizes the urgent need for community-based cancer awareness, streamlined referral pathways, and affordable diagnostic strategies in low-resource settings. Improving early detection, strengthening health education, and integrating antimicrobial stewardship can reduce delays, improve outcomes, and alleviate healthcare burdens for patients with advanced malignancies.

Introduction

The delay in seeking healthcare services for oncologic conditions often results in advanced disease at the time of presentation, a critical issue in low-resource settings. Factors contributing to this delay include limited health literacy, cultural beliefs that favor traditional healers or herbal treatments, and economic constraints that restrict access to prompt, specialized care [1]. These barriers are exacerbated by deficiencies in primary healthcare referral systems, which lead to delays in diagnosis and treatment initiation, contributing to poorer prognoses [2].

In Tanzania, healthcare infrastructure faces multiple challenges, particularly in rural areas, where patients often rely on primary health centers that lack specialized diagnostic capabilities. These centers may not promptly refer complex cases, delaying patients' access to tertiary-level care [3]. Consequently, patients frequently present at referral centers with advanced-stage diseases that require intensive supportive and palliative care, resulting in prolonged hospital stays and significant financial burdens on both healthcare systems and affected families [4]. Moreover, the overuse of antibiotics, common in cases of chronic wounds or infections associated with malignancy, contributes to antibiotic resistance, complicating treatment options and heightening morbidity risks [5].

This case report illustrates the challenges associated with delayed presentation and advanced-stage sarcoma in a 19-year-old Tanzanian male. His course of care highlights the impacts of health-seeking behaviors, limited health literacy, referral system inadequacies, and the cumulative burden of prolonged hospitalizations in a low-resource setting.

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [6].

Case presentation

A 19-year-old male presented with a one-year history of progressive swelling of the right upper limb, initially arising from the inner aspect of the upper arm and gradually involving the entire limb from the axilla to the fingers. The swelling was initially painless but later became painful and associated with numbness and functional limitation. Two weeks prior to admission, the patient developed an ulceration on the posterior aspect of the arm that progressed to purulent discharge and maggot infestation.

On the day of admission, the patient experienced profuse bleeding from the ulcerated mass following minor trauma, accompanied by dizziness and palpitations. He reported significant weight loss but denied respiratory or gastrointestinal symptoms.

During the course of illness, he had initially attended a regional hospital where an X-ray was performed and unspecified oral medications were prescribed without improvement. He subsequently resorted to herbal treatments for symptomatic relief. Two weeks prior to admission, he was advised to undergo an MRI at a zonal referral hospital but could not afford the investigation, returning only when the tumor ruptured and bled profusely. His past medical history was unremarkable, with no family history of malignancy.

On examination, the patient was ill-appearing, wasted, and severely pale. Vital signs showed a blood pressure of 123/74 mmHg, pulse rate of 152 beats/min, and temperature of 36.2 °C. A large exophytic ulcerated mass occupied the right axillary region, measuring approximately 21 × 18 cm, discharging serosanguinous fluid. The patient underwent right shoulder disarticulation and tumor excision under general anesthesia. Despite transfusions, preoperative hemoglobin remained low (4.4 g/dL). The axillary vessels were ligated, and the specimen—including the entire upper limb—was sent for histopathological analysis (Fig. 3). Gross examination revealed a 21 × 18 cm necrotic mass encroaching on bone. Microscopy showed a high-grade mesenchymal malignant tumor composed of pleomorphic spindle and oval cells with atypical mitoses and extensive necrosis, consistent with pleomorphic rhabdomyosarcoma (Fig. 4). Postoperatively, the patient was managed with antibiotics, analgesics, and nutritional support. One month later, a recurrent axillary mass was noted at the wound site. CT thoracoabdominal imaging revealed a soft-tissue recurrence with necrosis and scapular erosion but no visceral metastases (Fig. 5). The patient subsequently underwent a right forequarter amputation. The patient was discharged one month later following physiotherapy and rehabilitation. Six months postoperatively, the surgical site had healed completely. He was referred for oncologic evaluation, but did not attend subsequent follow-up appointments. One year later, he re-presented with a right cervical mass suggestive of tumor recurrence and was referred to the national oncology center for radiotherapy.

Discussion

This case underscores the multifaceted barriers to timely and effective cancer treatment, including poor health-seeking behavior, sociocultural factors, and healthcare system delays, which collectively contribute to late-stage presentations at tertiary centers. A lack of awareness or misconceptions about the severity of symptoms often deters patients from seeking early medical intervention. The belief in traditional medicine or local healers as primary treatment modalities is prevalent, particularly in rural regions, and can delay patients' initial engagement with formal healthcare providers [7].

Educational deficiencies among patients and communities pose a significant barrier, as evidenced by this case where the patient resorted to herbal remedies for temporary symptom relief before finally seeking hospital care. Such decisions reflect a lack of awareness about cancer's progressive nature and highlight the importance of public health education campaigns focused on early symptom recognition and the need for timely intervention. Furthermore, financial limitations prevent patients from obtaining necessary imaging or specialist consultations, as seen when the patient was advised to get an MRI but could not afford it, delaying critical diagnostics [8].

Another major contributing factor is the inefficiency of the referral process from primary to tertiary care. Primary health clinicians, especially those in remote areas, often lack the resources and knowledge to identify complex cases requiring specialist referral, leading to delays in proper diagnosis and treatment [4]. Strengthening the referral pathways and training healthcare providers in cancer symptom recognition could significantly reduce these delays and improve early diagnosis rates.

The advanced disease stage at presentation necessitates prolonged hospital stays for supportive and palliative care, placing an economic strain on both the hospital system and the patient's family. This case required complex surgical interventions, including shoulder disarticulation and forequarter amputation, followed by extended hospital stay for wound management, rehabilitation, and follow-up. The patient's inability to return for follow-up appointments further demonstrates how financial strain limits continuity of care, especially in cases requiring multi-disciplinary management and adjuvant therapies [9].

In a recent article, Wangzhong Li et al. emphasize that cancer is a significant global health issue, ranking as the third leading cause of death worldwide. The authors focus on cancers affecting adolescents and young adults (AYA), noting that these cancers exhibit distinct biological, epidemiological, and clinical characteristics compared to those in other age groups. AYA cancers are on the rise, yet they have been historically under-recognized, under-diagnosed, and under-served, with less improvement in survival rates compared to pediatric or older adult populations. The study also highlights that many AYA cancers are less influenced by traditional behavioral risk factors like tobacco, alcohol, or nutrition. However, these factors may still interact synergistically with other, less understood risk factors, potentially complicating the risk profile and contributing to the uniqueness of cancer in this age group [10].

Despite the rising cancer burden in Africa, cancer continues to receive relatively low public health priority. This is partly due to resource limitations but also because of the ongoing challenges posed by communicable diseases such as HIV/AIDS, malaria, and tuberculosis. Additionally, there is often limited awareness among policymakers, the public, and international health agencies about the current and future cancer burden and its potential economic impact. Prevention of exposure to cancer-causing agents, including infections, tobacco use, and obesity, is widely regarded as the most feasible and cost-effective strategy for cancer control in Africa. However, there is a significant gap in data on the stages at which cancers are diagnosed on the continent. For instance, at the Ocean Road Cancer Institute in Tanzania, 91 % of breast cancer patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage (stage III or IV). Earlier diagnosis and increased awareness are essential for effective cancer control, and prioritizing these strategies could significantly improve outcomes across the continent [11].

Lastly, the extended use of antibiotics in managing the patient's wound complications and recurrent infections illustrates the risk of antibiotic resistance in the context of cancer care. Without standardized antimicrobial stewardship practices, long-term antibiotic use can promote resistant infections, complicating post-surgical care and diminishing recovery outcomes [5]. Implementing infection control protocols and educating healthcare providers on appropriate antibiotic use in chronic cancer cases are vital steps toward reducing resistance risks in similar cases.

This case exemplifies the complex challenges of managing advanced-stage malignancies in low-resource settings. Limited health literacy, cultural beliefs favoring traditional medicine, and a fragmented referral system contribute to delayed presentations at tertiary centers, often resulting in advanced disease stages with limited treatment options. These delays not only increase the economic burden on patients and families but also strain the healthcare system, necessitating extended supportive and palliative care services. In addition, prolonged antibiotic use in managing chronic infections highlights the growing concern of antibiotic resistance, which can further complicate outcomes in wound care.

This case also demonstrates how sociocultural perceptions, financial barriers, and gaps in health system infrastructure intersect to influence disease trajectory. Beyond the individual level, the case underscores the urgent need for national and regional strategies aimed at improving cancer literacy, decentralizing oncology services, and integrating multidisciplinary care into existing referral systems. Strengthening local capacity through education, early screening initiatives, and international collaboration could significantly reduce diagnostic delays and improve survival outcomes for patients with rare malignancies in low- and middle-income countries. A key limitation of this case is the incomplete long-term follow-up and treatment outcome, as the patient developed tumor recurrence and was referred for radiotherapy but did not complete further management. This limits our ability to comment on the long-term prognosis, response to adjuvant therapy, or potential survival outcomes. Nevertheless, the recurrence highlights the aggressive nature of high-grade rhabdomyosarcoma and emphasizes the importance of sustained oncology follow-up and access to comprehensive cancer care.

Conclusion

Improving cancer outcomes in low-resource settings requires a multi-pronged approach that includes strengthening health education initiatives, streamlining referral pathways, and promoting timely intervention at the primary care level. Additionally, adopting antimicrobial stewardship practices is essential to mitigate the risk of antibiotic resistance in chronic cases. By addressing these barriers, healthcare providers can improve early detection and treatment access, reducing the physical and financial burden on patients and the healthcare system alike.
