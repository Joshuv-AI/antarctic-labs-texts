On the features of curved, passive reconfigurable smart electromagnetic skins
Authors: Michele Beccaria, Angelo Freni, Agnese Mazzinghi, Paola Pirinoli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper investigates the potential of incorporating a reconfigurable, curved, passive Smart Electromagnetic Skin (SES) into a streetlight or traffic light pole to enhance the coverage of a 5G Base Station operating in the millimeter-wave frequency band. The study specifically explores the possibility of achieving beam scanning performance without using active components through an innovative design of the SES that allows the change of the pointing direction of the reflected beam simply by its rotation around the pole. Moreover, a novel Unit Cell (UC) incorporating the radome is introduced to provide a more compact and practical solution from a manufacturing perspective. Numerical results confirm the effectiveness of the proposed approach and the feasibility of a smart skin that provides reconfigurability while maintaining high simplicity and reduced cost.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Applied physics

Introduction

The advent of fifth-generation (5G) communication systems has brought about a significant advance in wireless technology, offering speed, latency, and connectivity improvements compared to their fourth-generation predecessors1,2. However, as the demand for more advanced and integrated automated systems continues to rise, next-generation systems will soon face limitations in meeting the burgeoning requirements of future applications, which require unprecedented data transfer rates, wider bandwidth, improved coverage, and minimal latency to support the extensive connectivity needs of intelligent systems3,4.

To address these evolving requirements, the exploration of millimeter-wave (mm-wave) and sub-terahertz (sub-THz) frequency bands has gained interest, due to their potential to offer extremely high bandwidth and ultra-high data rates. Despite these advantages, the propagation characteristics of mm-waves and sub-THz frequencies present significant challenges, including higher free-space loss, increased penetration loss, and susceptibility to environmental factors like foliage and rain attenuation. Consequently, maintaining robust coverage remains a critical issue, particularly in non-line-of-sight (NLOS) regions. Although the most direct and effective solution to solve or at least mitigate these problems is to add further base stations, this approach is not the most convenient in many respects, as it leads to increased network complexity, higher costs, greater visual impact, and elevated electromagnetic pollution. An innovative alternative to improve wireless network performance without proliferating base stations is the creation of a Smart Electromagnetic Environment (SEE)5–11. In this paradigm, the propagation environment is engineered to enhance wireless system performance, transforming it from a passive entity into an integral component of the communication infrastructure. An example of a smart outdoor electromagnetic environment is sketched in Fig. 1, where the connection is enhanced through the introduction of suitable positions of different active or passive equipment.

Among the devices that allow the implementation of the SEE concept are Smart Electromagnetic Skins (SESs). They are thin surfaces designed to manipulate electromagnetic waves to provide non-specular or anomalous reflection, ensuring that the impinging signal is redirected to areas not in line-of-sight of the base station12–18. This is possible because smart electromagnetic skins are essentially metasurfaces discretized with an appropriate number of Unit Cells (UCs) having either resonant or sub-wavelength dimensions and characterized by one or more degrees of freedom. If cells do not include any active components, the resulting surface is passive and is referred to as SES in the strict sense, otherwise if the UCs include PIN diodes, MEMS, liquid crystals, etc., the resulting surface is capable of being reconfigured in real-time and is named Reconfigurable Intelligent Surface (RIS)19–32. An RIS can provide either beam shaping or beam scanning that, in the ideal situation, can cover a quite large scan range33, but at the cost of increased complexity and the need for more expensive hardware with respect to passive SESs.

Traditionally, SESs and RISs were envisioned as planar surfaces mounted on building walls. Although this solution seems to be the most suitable for indoor scenarios, it is not always feasible in outdoor scenarios for several reasons, such as architectural restrictions or preservation requirements in historical areas, the presence of windows, or the relative orientation of the building with respect to the base station. An alternative and innovative approach was introduced in34–36, where the possibility of integrating curved SESs into urban furniture such as street lights or traffic light poles was investigated. This strategy circumvents many installation limitations and provides the flexibility to adjust the SES orientation for optimal signal reflection. Moreover, the results on the comparison among a curved smart skin and two planar configurations confirm that the curved solution outperforms the planar SES occupying the same volume between the pole and the radome34. The configurations described in34–36 are passive and therefore no reconfigurability is provided, even if the results in34 on the analysis of the sensitivity from the direction of arrival of the incident field show that the curved SES works properly within a scanning range of approximately degrees. In several scenarios, the capability of performing beam scanning or adjusting the pointing direction is required once the smart skin is installed. This work introduces an innovative procedure enabling the design of a passive SES that can change the direction in which the reflected field is radiated simply by varying its orientation relative to the base station by a mechanically controlled rotation around the pole.

In the literature, the beam scanning capabilities of reflectarrays and transmitarrays have been extensively analyzed over the years. This property can be achieved through various methodological approaches, including the use of pseudo-stochastic optimization algorithms37–40, or deterministic methods such as the bifocal approach41,42, or by considering the phase profile as a quasi-spherical surface43. In all these cases, regardless of the surface design method, beam scanning is achieved by mechanical movement of the feed. Since the base station antenna cannot be moved, the only possibility in the case of an SES is to change the position or, better, the orientation of the surface. Although this is clearly impossible for a planar skin integrated into a wall, or either in the case in which it is mounted on a pole, the positioning of a curved surface, concentric to a pole, allows for its mechanical rotation. As shown in34, the simple rotation of an SES designed for a fixed beam pointing allows obtaining a very limited adjustment of the pointing direction (a few degrees). Thus, here we introduce a technique for the design of a metasurface that presents good beam scanning capabilities without the complexity and cost of an RIS. Moreover, the rotation mechanism can be easily integrated into the pole.

The main novelties introduced by the paper are summarized in the following.

Beam scanning, passive and curved smart skin concentric to the pole around which it is mechanically rotated to steer the beam. According to the authors, this is the first time such a configuration is introduced: reconfigurable intelligent surfaces referred to in literature are planar and use reconfigurable components in the unit cell, requiring a complex network for real-time control. Vice versa, the proposed solution only asked for a mechanism for the movement of the SES; moreover, since it is supposed to be mounted on a light pole or a traffic light pole, the power supply is guaranteed.

The paper is organized as follows: in “Design methodology for beam-scanning curved SES” the novel methodology exploited to design a passive, reconfigurable, curved SES is addressed. In “Unit cell with incorporated radome” the unit cell introduced to overcome technological and manufacturing limitations is described. Finally, in “Design and analysis of reconfigurable curved SES” the results of the numerical analysis of a configuration designed using the proposed approach and unit cell are summarized: they confirm the effectiveness of the methodology and the good performance of the designed configuration.

Design methodology for beam-scanning curved SES

The geometry considered is shown in Fig. 2. In contrast to the configuration introduced in34, here the SES consists of a metasurface conformed to a circular cross-sectional cylindrical surface, which is concentric with the pole and can rotate around the pole axis. Additionally, a metal shutter limits the metasurface view. By rotating the metasurface by an angle , it is possible to scan the reflected beam in the horizontal plane, which is perpendicular to the axis of the cylinder.

(a) 3D and (b) top view of the considered geometry, with the SES concentric to the pole where it is mounted and partially covered by the shutter.

According to the developed design procedure, the metasurface is divided into regions, numbered sequentially from left to right. The regions characterized by odd numbers are designed to point the reflected beam in different directions, while those with even indices facilitate a smooth transition between the regions close to them. In Fig. 3, the case in which the metasurface is divided into 5 regions is sketched. The central region, known as region 3, is designed to direct a beam toward the center of the desired scanning interval. In contrast, the regions at the extremes, namely regions 1 and 5, are engineered to point toward the two ends of the scanning interval. To facilitate a smooth transition between the central and the two extreme parts of the SES, regions 2 and 4 are designed by resorting to a bifocal approximation41,42. In particular, a phase delay averaged between the phase delays associated with the central and one of the extreme regions is considered. The dimension of regions 2 and 4 is chosen as a compromise between the maximization of the reflection and the width of the field of view. The shutter aperture is fixed and cannot rotate around the pole. In the following, the aperture is referred to as the visible window.

Example of the phase delay map obtained with the developed approach to scan the beam over the angular range [,]. The five different regions in which it is divided and the visible window are highlighted.

Unit cell with incorporated radome

Unlike the fixed beam configuration analyzed in34, the SES is assumed to be concentric with the pole. As a consequence, a stratified structure as that sketched in Fig. 5a can be adopted, in which both the smart skin and the shutter can be located between the pole and the radome. However, this solution requires considering the spacing between the smart skin and the shutter and between the shutter and the radome during the design. Then, it is necessary to ensure that the spacing is constant in the manufacturing and mounting processes. A more efficient and simpler alternative consists of placing the radome between the smart skin and the shutter, as shown in Fig. 5b, so that its effect could be directly introduced in the study of the unit cell.

Design and analysis of reconfigurable curved SES

Conclusions

In this work, the possibility of designing a curved passive smart skin with beam scanning capability is investigated. For this purpose, an innovative design procedure and a suitable unit cell are introduced. The results derived from the numerical analysis of an SES show that it points the main beam at directions characterized by different angles, simply rotating the skin around the pole. These confirm the feasibility of SES, which offers an efficient and low-cost solution for various urban and industrial environments, in terms of performance and manufacturing.
