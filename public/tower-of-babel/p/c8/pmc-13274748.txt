Assessing Covariate Clinical Relevance in High‐Dimensional PK Analysis: A Comparison of SCM+, FFEM, and FREM Approaches
Authors: Morgane Philipp, Sylvie Retout, Simon Buatois, France Mentré
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis work aimed to assess the correctness of covariate clinical relevance (CCR) assessment using SCM+, FFEM, and FREM within a high‐dimensional covariate framework with varying effect sizes and correlations. A clinical trial simulation inspired by the dupilumab case study was conducted (200 datasets of 300 patients each), using a 2‐compartment PK model with 12 covariates having small, medium, or high effect size. Covariate analysis was based on a 70 covariate‐parameter relationships predefined set including 12 continuous and 7 binary covariates sampled from the NHANES database, spanning high to low correlations. The simulated reference model (RM) was fitted for comparison. Parameter estimation was performed in NONMEM/PsN using FOCEi (SCM+, FFEM) or IMPMAP (FREM), with SE derived from the S matrix. CCR assessment followed a forest plot–inspired approach: 90% confidence intervals with a [0.8–1.25] reference area for clinical relevance; 5% type I error for statistical significance. Parameter estimates and SE were always obtained, allowing full CCR evaluation. For covariates with simulated effects, all methods were consistent with RM. SCM+ missed up to 9% of small‐effect covariates, whereas FFEM/FREM more often indicated insufficient information to conclude across all effect sizes. For covariates without any effect, SCM+ mostly did not select them, while FFEM/FREM was more informative by classifying them as non‐relevant or with insufficient information. As non‐selection may reflect a lack of power rather than no effect, robust CCR assessment should begin with FFEM/FREM for a comprehensive exploration, followed by SCM+ to build a parsimonious predictive model.Keywords: covariate clinical relevance, covariate selection methods, full covariate modeling methods, high‐dimensional covariate framework, pharmacokinetic

Introduction

Covariate analysis is essential in population pharmacokinetic (PK) modeling to identify and quantify sources of variability between individuals [1]. As a result, it can guide dose adjustments in subpopulations of interest, reducing the risk of under‐ or over‐exposure. It also enables predictions under new conditions, such as extrapolation to infants [2]. In the pharmacometrics field, numerous covariate modeling approaches are available and can be broadly classified into 2 families: covariate selection and full modeling methods. Despite the diversity of available methods, health authorities do not currently provide specific recommendations on the use of any particular approach. Notably, in the population PK guidance, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) stated: “covariate analysis can be performed based on several approaches or their possible combinations (e.g., stepwise covariate analysis, full covariate model approach, the LASSO)” [3].

Covariate selection methods select and include in the final model only those covariates considered to have an effect among the predefined set, according to a selection criterion. Notably, they include the widely used stepwise covariate model (SCM) approach [4, 5], and its improved version SCM+ [6]. SCM+ consists of a forward selection loop followed by a backward one, where a likelihood ratio test (LRT) evaluates whether adding or removing a covariate effect improves or deteriorates the model [6]. Before the forward loop, a pre‐selection step sets aside all non‐significant covariate–parameter relationships based on a cutoff p‐value. In the forward loop, the most significant relationship based on a forward p‐value is added at each step until none meet the inclusion criterion. Relationships previously set aside are re‐tested for inclusion before the backward loop, where the least significant relationship based on a backward p‐value is removed at each step until only statistically significant ones remain.

In contrast, full modeling methods include all covariate effects from the predefined set, without performing any selection. They include the full fixed effect model (FFEM) [7, 8, 9] and the full random effects model (FREM) [10, 11]. In FFEM, covariates are modeled as fixed effects [7, 8, 9], as is commonly done, and the covariate–parameter relationship types must be specified in advance, based on empirical knowledge, data‐driven evidence, or mathematical considerations [1]. The novelty of FREM is to model them as random variables [10, 11]. Therefore, a large variance–covariance matrix is estimated in FREM, including 4 blocks: the top‐left block corresponds to the parameter–parameter part, the top‐right and bottom‐left blocks to the covariate–parameter part, and the bottom‐right block to the covariate–covariate part. The equivalent of the fixed‐effect coefficients can be retrieved using a multivariate approach i.e., accounting for all other covariate effects simultaneously, or either a univariate approach i.e., accounting for only one covariate effect at a time [10, 11]. In the presence of correlated covariates, these two approaches correspond to conditional and unconditional (marginal) covariate effects, respectively.

Once a covariate model has been developed, the impact of covariates on PK parameters should be visualized to support dose optimization decisions [3, 12]. To that end, health authorities recommend including forest plots [13, 14]. These graphs display covariate effect ratios (CER) along with their confidence intervals (CI), relative to the PK parameter value of a typical individual. Covariate clinical relevance (CCR) can be inferred from the position of the CER and its associated uncertainty with respect to the reference interval [0.8–1.25] (a range based on bioequivalence criteria, corresponding to a ±20% change in parameters on the log scale [3, 15]) and the reference line at 1, which represents no deviation from the typical individual. A covariate is considered clinically relevant if the 90% CI (CI90) lies entirely outside the reference area. Therefore, precise and accurate estimation of CER and their associated uncertainty is critical to support dose decision‐making.

Various studies have compared covariate modeling methods in the literature [6, 10, 16, 17, 18, 19]. To our knowledge, only 2 have compared a full modeling approach with a selection method: Chasseloup et al. [17], who compared FFEM with a simplified SCM restricted to a single covariate–parameter relationship, and Amann et al. [19], who compared FREM with SCM but evaluated FREM after a selection step. These studies mainly focused on covariate selection performance and estimation accuracy, but none assessed CCR.

To address this need, we have previously performed a first study comparing SCM, SCM+ and FFEM in a clinical trial simulation framework [20, 21] using NONMEM via Perl‐speaks‐NONMEM (PsN) [22, 23, 24]. We showed that all 3 methods provided satisfactory CCR assessment. Different results were nevertheless obtained for covariates without any effect; SCM and SCM+ mainly did not select these covariates, while FFEM either identified them as non‐relevant or concluded that insufficient information was available. This first study was limited to a moderate number of covariates (7 in total, of which 1 or 4 had a simulated effect, with 14 covariate–parameter relationships investigated), with only one relevant covariate effect and, at most, moderate correlations between covariates. In addition, only one full modeling method, FFEM, was included in the comparison. There was therefore a need to evaluate these covariate modeling methods in a more complex setting, involving a high‐dimensional covariate framework with a broader range of effect sizes and correlations, ensuring that the results could be generalizable to real‐world modeling scenarios.

The main objective of this work was to assess the correctness of CCR assessment using SCM+, FFEM, and FREM algorithms implemented in PsN version 5.4.0, within a high‐dimensional covariate framework with varying covariate effect sizes and correlation levels. To this end, a clinical trial simulation was developed inspired from a real case example of patients with moderate to severe atopic dermatitis treated with dupilumab [25]. A set of 19 covariates, showing low to high correlations, was considered. A total of 70 covariate‐parameter relationships were investigated, including 12 with a simulated effect distributed across 3 effect size categories: small, medium, and high.

Clinical relevance was assessed using a 10% threshold whereas statistical significance was evaluated using a 5% nominal univariate single‐test type I error rate, corresponding to commonly applied thresholds [21]. Both clinical relevance and statistical significance were inferred from the final model. Covariates were classified as relevant (R), non‐relevant (NR), or with insufficient information (II) to conclude if the CI90 lay entirely outside, entirely within, or straddled the reference range [0.8–1.25], respectively. Covariates were significant (S) if the CI95 excluded 1, and non‐significant (NS) otherwise. Finally, CCR was evaluated using the 5‐category framework described previously [20]: R, NRS, NRNS, IIS, IINS. Simulation Study

The simulation example was designed to mimic the patient population and scale of late‐phase clinical trials, which typically provide sufficient power for covariate‐effect detection and inform dose‐adjustment decisions prior to registration. As a reference, a Phase IIb clinical trial of dupilumab in patients with moderate‐to‐severe atopic dermatitis having primarily peak and trough samples (NCT01859988, R668‐AD‐1021 [25]) was selected. Because the dupilumab PK model was developed using pooled data from several clinical trials, with multiple fixed parameters [25], covariate effects could not be reliably assessed. To improve parameter identifiability, the Phase IIb sampling scheme for each subject was enriched with a Phase I sampling design (NCT01015027, R668‐AS‐0907 [25]), with intensive sampling after the first and last doses. As a result, each patient had a rich PK design combining both Phase IIb and Phase I sampling time points.

Covariate Database

A total of 19 covariates, selected for their PK relevance to monoclonal antibodies [25, 26] and availability in the NHANES database [27] (Text S1), were included in the database, comprising 12 continuous and 7 categorical covariates (binarized, Text S2). Individuals with any missing covariate values were excluded, resulting in a database of 5765 unique individuals. Summary statistics, correlations, and distributions are shown in Table S1 and Figures S2 and S3.

Reference Population PK Model

A simplified version of the dupilumab PK model was used, consisting of a 2‐compartment (central and peripheral) distribution model with first‐order absorption and linear elimination, including 5 PK parameters: the absorption constant rate (KA), apparent clearance (CL/F), apparent intercompartmental clearance (Q/F), apparent central volume of distribution (Vc/F), and apparent peripheral volume of distribution (Vp/F). Inter‐individual variability was assumed for all PK parameters, with a diagonal variance–covariance matrix reflecting the absence of correlation between parameters. A combined error model was used.

Among the 19 available covariates, 7 were selected to have an effect on PK parameters: 3 continuous (body weight [BW], age [AGE], albumin [ALB]) and 4 categorical (sex [SEX], white race [RACE2], good health status [HLT2], poor health status [HLT3]). A total of 12 covariate–parameter relationships were simulated, with varying effect sizes based on the CER value. Effect sizes were selected to cover a range of signal strengths: small effects corresponded to CER values within the standard bioequivalence boundaries [0.8, 1.25], whereas medium and strong effects corresponded to CER values outside these boundaries, as illustrated in Figure S1. The distinction between medium and strong effects was chosen arbitrarily to span different levels of clinically relevant effects. Simulation Settings

A total of 200 datasets with 300 patients each were simulated using the RM to be consistent with our previous work [20, 21] and align with common practice in simulation studies assessing covariate modeling methods [28]. Simulations including individual PK parameters, residual errors, and concentration‐time profiles were done using the sse PsN tool. The simulated study design involved 5 repeated‐dose SC regimens; each patient had between 33 and 38 PK samples with the exact dosing and sampling scheme detailed in Table S3. Covariate profiles were sampled with replacement from the constructed covariate database, ensuring that all covariates for each individual were kept together to preserve the correlation structure within subjects. The first simulated dataset is illustrated in Figure S4.

Estimation and Implementation

The analysis was performed on a High Performance Computing infrastructure composed of 66 compute nodes, each with 96 Intel Xeon cores and 768 GB of RAM. NONMEM version 7.5.1 and PsN version 5.4.0 were used. Data visualization and results analysis, including FREM results post‐processing, were performed using R version 4.0.5.

For SCM+, forward and cutoff p‐values were set at 0.05, and backward p‐value was set to 0.01; for FFEM, 5 retries were performed keeping the best fit. For SCM+ and FFEM, covariate effect parameter estimates were bounded between −10 and 10. For FREM, covariate effects were computed in a multivariate framework to enable comparison with the other methods (Text S3). Of note, univariate coefficients were also calculated but results are only presented in Supporting Information. For all methods, initial estimates were set to simulated values for PK, RUV, and interindividual variability parameters, while covariate effects were set to 0.001.

Parameter estimation was performed using the FOCEI algorithm for RM, SCM+, and FFEM; SE were derived from the score (S) matrix [27]. For FREM, the expectation maximization algorithm IMPMAP was used to overcome estimation issues using recommended settings (AUTO = 1, RANMETHOD = 3P INTER [11]) with an increased number of iterations (NITER = 500) due to the large number of parameters to be estimated. For FREM, SE of covariate effects were obtained by sampling 1000 draws (Text S3). An issue‐handling procedure was defined in Text S4 to address problems related to convergence, parameters estimation or SE computation.

Evaluation

Covariate effect estimates accuracy and uncertainty were evaluated using relative estimation error (REE) and relative SE (RSE) for covariates with a simulated effect. For covariates without any effect, they were evaluated using estimation error (EE) and SE. CER estimation accuracy was assessed by REE, and its uncertainty by relative root mean square error (RRMSE). Formulas and rationale for the chosen metrics are provided in Text S5 [29, 30]. For CCR assessment, results for covariates with a simulated effect obtained with SCM+, FFEM, and FREM were compared with those of the RM. Of note, RM represents the estimated CCR results when the true model is always selected. Covariates without any effect are not included in RM and are expected NR. Categories not in the model (NIM) and non‐selected (NSEL) were used for RM and SCM+, respectively. OFV, BICc [31] and runtimes were compared across methods (Text S5).

Results

Issues related to convergence, parameter estimation or SE computation were encountered in 7 (3.5%), 74 (37%), 3 (1.5%), and 1 (0.5%) of the 200 fits for RM, FFEM, FREM, and SCM+, respectively (details in Text S4). After applying the issue‐specific procedures (Text S4), parameter estimates and SE were successfully recovered for all 200 datasets. Detailed SCM+ model selection results are presented in Figure S5 and Text S6.

CER Estimation Accuracy and Uncertainty

REE and EE results for the covariate effect parameter estimates are shown in Figure S6. For covariates with a simulated effect, estimates were overall unbiased, with REE medians centered on zero. Precision was generally higher with SCM+ than with full covariate modeling methods, particularly for FREM. Estimation precision increased with the magnitude of the effect. The PK parameter also influenced precision, being highest for CL/F and lowest for Q/F. For covariates without a simulated effect, estimates were likewise unbiased, with EE medians centered on zero. As SCM+ rarely selected these covariates, most EE boxplots were flat at 0. Lower precision was again observed with full modeling approaches, most notably with FREM, and for effects estimated on Q/F and ALB.

RSE and SE values are presented in Figure S7. For covariates with high or medium effects, estimates were precise across methods, with median RSE values mostly below 30% (except BW on Q/F, which remained less precise). Small‐effect covariates showed higher uncertainty, with RSE often exceeding 30%, especially with full modeling methods. For covariates without effects, SE distributions were wider with full modeling approaches, particularly for FREM. Again, the widest distributions were observed for effects estimated on Q/F and ALB.

CCR Evaluation

Results were compared to the simulated reference model (RM) and classified as relevant (R), non‐relevant significant (NRS), non‐significant (NRNS), insufficient information significant (IIS), or non‐significant (IINS).

Covariates With a Simulated Effect

The CCR evaluation of covariates with a true simulated effect revealed distinct performance patterns that varied with the magnitude of the effect (Figure 3). For high‐effect covariates, RM always concluded R as SCM+. The full modeling approaches classified them mostly as R: from 98% to 100% for FFEM and from 86% to 100% for FREM. FFEM and FREM showed a very small proportion of IIS/IINS (up to 2% and 10%, respectively). Of note, NRS/NRNS was observed in 4% of the cases by FREM for the BW effect on Vp/F.

For medium‐effect covariates, RM concluded R in most cases (71%–100%), or IIS otherwise (0%–29%). SCM+ yielded very similar results, with 72%–100% R or 0%–28% IIS otherwise. In contrast, full model approaches showed a marked increase of IIS/IINS. This was particularly evident for the effect of BW on Q/F, where FFEM classified 3% as IIS and 59% as IINS, while FREM classified 22% as IIS and 18% as IINS. Of note, among the 57 datasets where SCM+ concluded IIS, 51 were also classified as IIS by FFEM and 26 by FREM.

For small‐effect covariates, RM concluded mainly IIS (62%–92%), occasionally R (4%–38%), rarely NRS/NRNS (0%–4%) and never IINS. SCM+ also found these effects IIS in most cases (60%–89%), R in some (4%–39%) and NRS in very few (0%–2%). However, it failed to select some true effect, with AGE on KA being missed in up to 9% of cases. The full modeling approaches mostly concluded IIS/IINS, with a non‐negligible proportion of IINS ranging from 2% to 48% for FFEM and from 16% to 44% for FREM. In addition, NRNS were more frequent, reaching up to 10% for FFEM and 16% for FREM. Finally, R effects were identified in 2%–18% of cases with FFEM and in 5%–17% with FREM.

Covariates Without Any Simulated Effect

The CCR evaluation of covariates without any simulated effect yielded different results for SCM+ compared to the full modeling approaches (Figure 4). SCM+ mainly did not select these covariates (97%–100%) or found them either IIS (up to 3%) or NRS (up to 2.5%). With FFEM, null‐effect relationships were predominantly classified as NRNS or IINS, with their relative proportion depending on both the parameter and the covariate type considered. For example, more NRNS decisions were observed on CL/F (72%–100%) than on Q/F (0%–80%). Categorical covariates also showed more IINS decisions (up to 99%) compared to continuous ones (up to 86%). A small proportion of R (up to 1%), NRS (up to 3%), and IIS (up to 10%) was also observed. With FREM, null‐effect relationships were again mainly classified as NRNS or IINS, with the same dependency on both the parameter and the covariate type. Compared to FFEM, FREM showed slightly higher proportions of NRS and IIS (up to 28% for both). More importantly, FREM identified a non‐negligible proportion of R (false positives), ranging from 0% to 19%: none on CL/F, up to 8% on KA and Vc/F, up to 14% on Vp/F, and up to 19% on Q/F. Notably, the largest proportions of R were observed for covariates that already had a simulated effect on another parameter (i.e., HLT3, ALB, SEX, and HLT2).

OFV, BICc and Runtimes

FFEM outperformed SCM+ in terms of OFV, but not in terms of BICc where SCM+ yielded more parsimonious models as reported in Table 3. OFV and BICc results cannot be compared with FREM due to methodological differences including the dataset used, covariates encoded as random effects, the use of a full variance–covariance matrix, and the estimation algorithm. FFEM was 6 times faster than FREM (median runtimes of 1.16 and 6.97 h) and 13 times faster than SCM+ (median runtimes of 1.16 and 14.90 h).

Discussion

This study evaluated the ability of SCM+, FFEM and FREM algorithms implemented in PsN to correctly conclude on CCR in population PK analysis within a high‐dimensional covariate framework. Despite the higher dimensionality, all parameter estimates and SE were successfully obtained thanks to the defined issue‐handling procedure (Text S4). The applied covariate effect parameters bounds (−10 to 10), set at around 3 times the largest simulated effect (−3.45 for ALB on CL/F), likely contributed to estimation stability and represents reasonable values in PK analyses without relying too much on the simulated values. Regarding initial estimates, using simulated values does not reflect the “true” parameter values, since they already include covariate effects. Estimation instability is a known challenge for full‐modeling approaches. Therefore, 5 retries were used for FFEM, while the IMPMAP estimation algorithm was used for FREM, due to its large variance–covariance matrix and the lack of retries option in PsN for FREM. Although IMPMAP could have been used for SCM+ and FFEM, it was avoided due to runtime cost and the risk of stochastic noise with SCM+. SE computation approach was also adapted to the high‐dimensional context by using the S matrix [32]. As highlighted by Bauer et al. [32], the S matrix is “more quickly evaluated” and “sufficiently accurate if there are more subjects (preferably about two times more) than the dimension of the variance–covariance” which is the case here. Alternative methods as the Hessian (R) or the sandwich (R−1SR−1) matrices could have led to greater challenges, while bootstrap [33, 34] or sampling importance resampling [35] methods were not considered due to runtime constraints.

SCM+ demonstrated strong selection performance, recovering the exact simulated model in more than 50% of cases and a close approximation otherwise, with at most one missing small‐effect relationship and up to 5 additionally included null‐effect relationships. It therefore led to parsimonious models and reliably identified covariates with medium and high simulated effect sizes. Covariate effect parameters and CER estimates were overall well estimated and unbiased, supporting the reliability of its CCR conclusions, which closely match those of the RM for covariates with a simulated effect. However, SCM+ showed limitations for small‐effect relationships, which were more likely missed due to reduced statistical power in this complex framework. For example, the AGE effect on KA was not detected in 9% of the datasets but was instead selected on another parameter in 3.5% of cases. Correlated covariates such as COMO, ALK, and ALB (correlated with AGE at 0.49, −0.31, and −0.28, respectively) were additionally selected in 2.5%, 1%, and 1% of cases. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that correlated covariates reduce statistical power [1, 5]. Of note, in this work, SCM+ selection performance was evaluated using a single commonly used threshold set (forward p‐value = 0.05, backward p‐value = 0.01) [4, 5, 16, 18, 19, 20]. The use of a stricter backward p‐value compared to the forward one serves to control the Type I error rate; but alternative combinations could have been explored to assess its robustness. An intrinsic limitation of SCM+ is its reliance on statistical significance testing only via the likelihood ratio test. As a result, it cannot evaluate the clinical relevance of non‐selected covariates. SCM+ can therefore not distinguish a true null clinical effect (NRNS) and a relationship for which there is insufficient information to conclude on its clinical effect (IINS). It is important to note that no stage‐wise filtering approach was considered in this work, as the aim was to fully evaluate the data‐driven selection performance of SCM+. However, in Phase II/III settings, substantial prior knowledge is typically available and may justify directly incorporating mechanistic covariates without statistical testing, as suggested in stage‐wise filtering strategies. Integrating such a procedure could reduce the runtime of SCM+ (which remains one of the main drawbacks of the stepwise approach) and may also improve the power to detect true covariate–parameter relationships, as demonstrated by Svensson et al. [6].

Full modeling approaches (FFEM and FREM) evaluate all covariate‐parameter relationships of the predefined set, thereby offering a more transparent and complete assessment. Their key advantage lies in their ability to classify null‐effect relationships as either NRNS or IINS, in contrast to SCM+. For example, covariates tested on CL/F were mainly NRNS, while those on Q/F were more often classified as IINS. In addition, categorical covariates revealed more uncertainty, with a larger proportion of IINS decisions compared to continuous ones. This is likely due to several factors: correlations between covariates (Figure S2), small frequency in non‐reference groups (e.g., 21% for RACE3 and 14% for RACE4), and the implementation of HLT2 and HLT3 as binary covariates despite representing an ordinal health scale. The price to pay for this comprehensive evaluation is increased estimation uncertainty. Because full modeling approaches simultaneously estimate a large number of parameters, they produce larger SE and more frequent IIS/IINS classifications, even for covariates with medium and high simulated effects.

A specific cautionary finding of this study concerns the performance of FREM. In contrast to SCM+ and FFEM, which can assume a diagonal variance–covariance structure, FREM systematically estimates a full variance–covariance matrix of inter‐individual variability. In our high‐dimensional framework, this 24‐dimensional matrix required the estimation of a very large number of variance–covariance parameters (up to 300). One key consequence was the occurrence of false‐positive R classifications for covariates without any simulated effects, with proportions reaching up to 19% (Figure 4). This can probably be explained by covariates having a simulated effect on multiple PK parameters and being correlated to other covariates, with or without any simulated effects. FREM gives the possibility to compute univariate coefficients additionally to multivariate ones with the same run, thereby enabling the univariate effect assessment of each covariate with only one run [10, 11]. As formalized by Jonsson et al. [36], multivariate coefficients correspond to conditional effects, i.e., effects expressed after accounting for all other covariates included in the model and their correlation structure. In contrast, the univariate coefficients correspond to unconditional (or marginal) effects, which quantify the total association between a covariate and a parameter, including effects mediated through correlations with other covariates. Additional CCR results using univariate coefficients for FREM are presented in Figure S10 and discussed in Text S7. As conditional effects are known to be highly sensitive to covariate correlations and model context, their use in high‐dimensional settings may inflate apparent covariate relevance and contribute to false‐positive CCR classifications. This framework provides a mechanistic explanation for the observed differences between univariate and multivariate FREM results in our study. Of note, the use of a diagonal omega in the simulated model placed SCM+ and FFEM in an idealized scenario compared to FREM. It would be interesting to assess the results when simulating with a full omega matrix.

The novelty of this work lies in evaluating CCR under varying degrees of covariate correlation, ranging from null to high (up to 0.8). In our simulations, we considered up to highly correlated covariates among those with no effect, up to medium correlations among covariates with a true effect, and up to medium correlations between covariates with and without an effect. Under these conditions, we found that correlation did not substantially compromise CCR assessment: for SCM+, it could reduce power for small simulated effects; for FREM, as previously discussed, there was a small proportion of false positives; and FFEM appeared largely robust, even though previous literature has reported potential issues with correlated covariates [7, 8, 9]. It would be of interest in future work to identify the correlation threshold beyond which CCR evaluation becomes problematic.

The study design strongly influenced parameter and covariate effect estimates. PK parameter uncertainty depended on how well each was informed by the data. CL/F was best informed, as the dense sampling strategy provided comprehensive coverage of the concentration‐time profile (total exposure), allowing for precise estimation of elimination. In contrast, Q/F was least informed, as it is mainly captured by the transient distribution phase between absorption and elimination, represented here mostly by peak and trough concentrations. Log‐scale concentration–time profiles (Figure S4) show that the 2 slopes expected from a two‐compartment model were poorly distinguishable. Estimating Q/F was further complicated by an interindividual variability of 30% and a proportional residual error of 15%. These challenges are consistent with what Kovalenko et al. reported for analogous parameters, which could only be properly estimated using rich or mixed designs [25]. As a consequence, covariate effects on Q/F were estimated with particularly high uncertainty, leading to more IIS/IINS conclusions.

Simulations were based on late‐phase scenarios with large patient numbers and were augmented with rich, Phase I–style sampling after the first and last doses. Although such intensive sampling is uncommon in late‐phase trials, this design was chosen to minimize primary PK parameter estimation issues and to enable a first evaluation of CCR in a higher‐dimensional context. In standard practice, Phase III trials are generally sparser, and covariate analyses typically combine early‐ and late‐phase data to ensure sufficient information for parameter estimation and adequate statistical power. Extending the present evaluation to this more classical framework would therefore be of interest.

SCM+, FFEM, and FREM were overall able to conclude reasonably well on CCR in a high‐dimensional setting, albeit with method‐specific strengths and weaknesses. Importantly, CCR evaluation strongly depends on the quality and precision of parameter estimates and should therefore be interpreted in light of estimation uncertainty, as reflected by SE and RSE. Particular caution is warranted when evaluating covariate effects on poorly estimated parameters, such as primary PK parameters in this study. In such cases, it may be reasonable to restrict covariate evaluation to parameters for which the data provide adequate estimation precision (e.g., assessing an effect on CL/F but not on Q/F), provided that this decision is guided by prior knowledge and model plausibility rather than estimation uncertainty alone.

A practical recommendation is to apply both approaches in parallel. A full modeling method allows assessment of the CCR of all covariate effects, including those not retained by selection methods, thereby distinguishing cases where a definitive conclusion can be drawn (R, NRS or NRNS) from those with insufficient information (IIS or IINS). As an absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, this distinction is particularly important for documenting model evidence in regulatory submissions aligned with the ICH M15 guidelines [37]. In parallel, a selection method (e.g., SCM+) should be used to derive a parsimonious model optimized for predictive purposes, as the reduced number of parameters improves estimation precision.

Alternative strategies could also be considered to derive a parsimonious model directly from the results of the full model. For example, backward selection could be used to retain only covariates with effects significantly different from zero. Alternatively, rather than relying on a statistical criterion, a parsimonious model could be derived from the CCR evaluation by retaining only the R (with or without IIS and IINS) relationships. However, the performance of these approaches relative to standard selection methods such as SCM+ remains to be evaluated. Incorporating clinical relevance directly as a selection criterion within stepwise algorithms, rather than relying solely on statistical criteria, also represents a potential avenue for future methodological development.

It is important to note that no correction was applied when inferring statistical significance for the CCR evaluation in this project. However, performing multiple tests on the same data can increase the Type I error rate. Correction procedures such as the Bonferroni correction (which controls the family‐wise error rate) or the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (which controls the false discovery rate) could have been applied [38].

This study was performed in a “high‐dimensional” context; however, even higher dimensions are possible, for example with genetic or multi‐biomarker data. In such settings, increasing dimensionality poses challenges for full modeling approaches, including convergence issues or inflated estimation uncertainty potentially increasing the occurrences of IIS/IINS classifications. In addition, the large number of covariate–parameter relationships exacerbates the risk of Type I error in the inference of statistical significance during CCR evaluation, thereby necessitating multiple‐testing corrections, as previously discussed [38]. For stepwise methods, increasing dimensionality may result in unrealistic runtimes, and the Type I error induced by the selection process should be controlled using specific procedure‐wise corrections. Consequently, machine learning–based methods could be valuable, notably as a pre‐selection step to reduce the dimensionality of the predefined set. Approaches such as Support Vector Machines (SVM), Neural Networks (NN), and Random Forests (RF) have notably been investigated by Sibieude et al. [18]. Regarding dimensionality reduction, Variational Autoencoders (VAE) also appear promising for this purpose. Covariate selection and estimation can be performed simultaneously using penalized regression methods such as LASSO, adaptive LASSO (ALASSO), or adjusted ALASSO (AALASSO) [16, 39].

Here, only SCM+, FFEM, and FREM were compared here, though other promising approaches exist such as AALASSO cited among the best alongside SCM+ with stagewise filtering and FREM with backward selection [16]. Alternative Monolix‐based methods like COSSAC [40] or stochastic approximation–based algorithms such as SAMBA [41] could also be considered.

Finally, CCR was derived from primary PK parameters, but true exposure metrics are secondary parameters such as AUC and Cmax; CCR evaluation should therefore be extended to these endpoints.
