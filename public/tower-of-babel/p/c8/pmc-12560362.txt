Background

Clear communication between members of the surgical team is paramount to perioperative patient safety. Poor communication in the operating room (OR) has been linked with increased errors and adverse outcomes [1–3]. Conversely, improved communication has demonstrated improved patient safety outcomes as seen with implementation of the WHO surgical safety checklist [1–5]. Safety improvements have been theorized to be due to enhanced communication and cohesiveness of the surgical team [2].

The need for clear communication extends to the perioperative educational environment between the faculty surgeon and resident learner, and miscommunication about educational priorities in the OR are common [6–8]. Currently, residents often learn surgical procedures through “discovery learning” which relies on repetition, self-directed learning, and the classic “see one, do one, teach one” approach [9]. “Guided discovery learning” is a technique that may facilitate more rapid acquisition and retention of operative skills in which a senior surgeon prepares a learner prior to a case, offers guidance during the case, and gives feedback afterward [9]. This approach to perioperative teaching has been applied to a Briefing, Intraoperative teaching, Debriefing (BID) model and encapsulated in the Education Time-Out (ETO). An ETO uses goal-setting by the learner to communicate priorities for development and facilitate discussion regarding more complex parts of the case.

Prior studies using an ETO have demonstrated improved concordance between faulty and residents on educational priorities, improved rates and quality of postoperative feedback, and enhanced educational experiences following the implementation of these tools [10–15]. What remains unknown is whether a perioperative educational tool that promotes communication between faculty and resident also improves patient safety. Residents in OBGYN face a multitude of barriers to readiness for independent surgical practice including decreasing gynecologic surgical volumes [16–18], increasing minimally invasive modalities [17, 19] and possibly from work hour restrictions [20–23]. It is imperative, therefore, to assess surgical education tools that are specifically designed to optimize learning in an effort to develop competence in the OR and thereby improve patient safety.

However, it remains unknown whether surgical education tools targeted at promoting communication between faculty and residents, like the ETO, also impact patient safety outcomes. This secondary analysis of our pilot study aims to assess the impact of the perioperative ETO on gynecologic intraoperative and postoperative patient safety outcomes at the University of Utah.

Methods

Study design

This is a sub-analysis of a pilot project assessing the impact of the ETO on surgical education outcomes [14]. We developed the ETO checklist (Fig. 1) based on the BID model and created a highly realistic simulation for training faculty on ETO use. We recruited gynecologic surgical faculty to participate, and participation was voluntary. For participating faculty, the training simulation involved a didactic session on the ETO and then a simulated case where an actor portrayed the resident learner. The faculty and resident watched a video of a laparoscopic salpingectomy and used the ETO structure to set goals and provide feedback. Following this, recruited faculty were instructed to use the ETO with residents in all their future cases [14]. From April to June 2022 the parent study recruited primary and adjunct gynecologic surgical faculty from all sub-specialties associated with the University of Utah OBGYN residency program. We also recruited University of Utah OBGYN residents from post-graduate year (PGY) 1 through 3 to participate in the educational outcomes arm. Enrolled faculty underwent the highly realistic simulation training on ETO use. Enrolled residents participated in a didactic session on goal setting and ETO use. We asked faculty to complete an ETO with the resident in the perioperative period for each operative case regardless of whether the resident was enrolled in the educational outcomes arm or not. The ETO was performed independently from the general OR surgical safety timeout. We tracked educational outcomes from May 2022 through February 2023.

Education time out user checklist. This is the checklist used by faculty surgeons and trainees to complete the ETO perioperatively

We then used chart abstraction to collect data on patient safety outcomes (Table 1) for participating faculty 6 months pre-intervention (November 2021 to May 2022) and 9 months post-intervention (June 2022 to February 2023). Participating surgeons performed surgical cases within the University of Utah Health (primary faculty) or the Intermountain Healthcare (IHC) system (adjunct faculty) and data were abstracted from their respective electronic medical record (EMR) systems. This study was deemed exempt by the University of Utah IRB (00151015) and the Intermountain Health IRB (1051855).

Chart abstraction

We identified charts for review by primary surgeon name. Abstracted data were entered into the University of Utah’s Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) system [24]. Three researchers (OM, MK, TS) with existing access to both EMRs performed the data abstraction after receiving training on REDCap. We created a manual of operations specifying inclusion/exclusion criteria, variables and how to find them in the EMR, and rules for resolving uncertainties in the chart that all reviewers followed. Inclusion criteria included gynecologic surgery performed by a participating faculty for any surgical indication with any level resident. Exclusion criteria included no resident documented as present during the surgery in the operative report and emergent or unscheduled cases. Patient age was not an exclusion criterion as we recruited Pediatric and Adolescent Gynecology specialists into the study. We collected demographic data on patients including age, comorbidities, and whether the surgery was for benign or oncologic indications.

Our primary outcome was a composite score of 30-day surgical morbidity and mortality. The composite included occurrence of an intraoperative complication, in-hospital complication, 30-day emergency reoperation, 30-day readmission, or 30-day mortality. Secondary outcomes included individual intraoperative and postoperative complications such as blood transfusion and various intraoperative injury (Table 1). We arrived at these measures of perioperative safety by reviewing several previously described composite scores for surgical patient safety and accepted gynecologic intraoperative complications [25–27].

Data analysis

We used R Statistical Software for data analyses (v4.3.1; R Core Team 2021). We compared pre- and post-intervention complications using chi-square tests for overall rates. We also used paired t-tests where we found the average difference in pre- and post-intervention complication rates for each surgeon. We used logistic regression with mixed effect modeling and random intercepts for each surgeon to analyze the likelihood of experiencing a complication pre- and post- intervention while controlling for patient age, presence of comorbidities, benign or oncologic surgical indication, and major versus minor surgery. Major surgery was defined as hysterectomy of any route, myomectomy of any route (excluding hysteroscopic), any abdominal case, or any case containing a lymphadenectomy or bowel resection.

Results

Twenty-five out of 62 (40%) eligible surgeons participated, including generalists and subspecialists in gynecologic oncology, urogynecology, pediatric and adolescent gynecology, minimally invasive gynecology, and reproductive endocrinology and infertility. We identified 2,114 cases to review from November 2021 through February 2023. We reviewed 1409 cases that met inclusion criteria: 572 pre-intervention and 837 post-intervention. We excluded 705 cases for emergent/unscheduled status or absence of documented resident participation (Fig. 2). Pre- and post-intervention characteristics were similar between groups with 20% and 18% being gynecology oncology surgeries (p = 0.2) and 40% and 41% being major surgeries (p = 0.6), respectively. Medical comorbidities were present in 41% pre- and 37% (p = 0.2) post-intervention cases (Fig. 2).

Bivariate analysis of the primary outcome, composite of 30-day morbidity and mortality, did not show a statistically significant improvement in composite patient safety outcomes (pre = 9.8%, post = 8.1%, p = 0.3). Paired t-tests yielded similar results. After adjusting for age, presence of co-morbidities, cancer surgery, major surgery and the effect of individual surgeons, post-intervention surgical cases were 24% less likely to experience a complication (aOR = 0.76, 95%CI = 0.5–1.1, p = 0.2), although this was not statistically significant. Cancer and major surgery indicators strongly predicted complications both pre- and post-intervention. We found similar insignificant trends toward improvement in intraoperative (pre = 3.9%, post = 2.8%, p = 0.3) and bleeding complications, which includes cases with an estimated blood loss > 500, or that required an intraoperative or postoperative transfusion (pre = 3.3%, post = 2.3%, p = 0.3).

Discussion

Our study did not find a significant improvement in patient safety outcomes following ETO implementation; however, we were not powered for outcomes as a secondary analysis of a pilot study, but the trend toward improvement is promising. As anticipated, major surgery and oncologic cases were strong predictors of complications. Previous studies have not investigated the link between improved surgical education and improved safety outcomes, making this a potentially novel finding. Our findings have the potential to significantly impact both patient safety and how educators teach in the OR.

Communication in the OR is essential for patient safety. This has been demonstrated by multiple investigators [1–5], and implementation of the surgical safety time out has now become universally accepted and recommended for patient safety. We hypothesize that the ETO works in a similar manner by promoting clearer communication between faculty and resident during a surgical case. We did find improved adjacent markers for communication in the parent study [14], but as we did not track communication directly, we cannot state that communication is the cause of the observed trend toward improved patient safety outcomes when an ETO is used.

It is known that resident involvement in the operating room is associated with longer operative times and increased rates of complications [28–31]. Despite these liabilities, resident involvement in the OR is essential for training future gynecologists, making it critical to find ways to improve patient safety in teaching settings. Our data are promising in that they show the ETO as a potential tool to enhance patient safety in cases involving resident education. One study looking at complications following laparoscopic hysterectomy found that cases with resident involvement compared to attending alone, had increased rates of post-operative blood transfusion [28]. In comparison, we demonstrated trends towards decreasing rates of bleeding complications post-ETO implementation, illustrating the ETO as a possible tool to improve specific measures of patient safety in the operating room when there is resident involvement.

Our pilot study was novel as investigating how a surgical education tool impacts patient safety outcomes has not, to our knowledge, been published. However, as this was a pilot study, we did not adequately power it to assess safety outcomes. Our results showed a trend in improved outcomes and we are planning a powered, multi-site project with tracking of ETO use to better assess the impact of the ETO on patient safety and the role communication plays.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is its novelty of evaluating patient safety outcomes pre- and post-surgical education intervention. We also have a good representation of cases from all the gynecologic subspecialties and well as from generalists and looked at cases performed at both regional hospitals and tertiary care centers.

As mentioned previously, one of our biggest limitations is that this was not powered to assess patient safety outcomes because it was a pilot study. Additionally, due to technical issues with our survey tool in the parent study, we could not track ETO use in the OR. It is possible that if we controlled for when ETO was utilized in a case, the results may be significant. We also do not have a comparison group of surgical cases where a resident was not present, nor do we know how involved the resident was in each case. It may be that the complication rate was lower in attending-only cases or in cases where the attending performed most of the procedure. We expect residents to improve in their surgical skills over time. The post-intervention time frame did include the transition between PGY level in July and therefore we do not believe that this significantly affected the results. Additionally, we only had 40% faculty participation. There may have been bias in which faculty were willing to participate in an education intervention, such as those who were already having their residents do some type of goal setting in the OR. Furthermore, as the hospitals included are tertiary care centers with robust referral systems, it is possible that patients followed up with local medical providers for complications. We made sure to include patient-reported complications when presenting for postoperative care within the hospital systems included in the study. We do not anticipate that this significantly affected the outcome of the study, as there is no reason to believe the number of times this happened would differ among patients pre- and post-intervention.

Conclusions

This pilot study of the ETO and its impact on gynecologic surgical patient safety outcomes demonstrated a trend in improved outcomes and a novel approach of investigating the effect of a perioperative, communication-based teaching tool on patient safety outcomes.
