Finasteride-induced recurrent aphthous ulceration–like lesions: a case report with dechallenge–rechallenge confirmation
Authors: Fukun Li, Chuan Sun, Ye Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAphthous ulceration is a common disorder of the oral mucosa with a multifactorial etiology, including genetic susceptibility, infections, nutritional deficiencies, hormonal influences, and systemic conditions. Although numerous medications have been reported to induce RAU-like lesions, finasteride—a widely prescribed 5α-reductase inhibitor for male androgenetic alopecia (AGA)—has not previously been implicated.Case presentationA 45-year-old male pharmacist developed RAU-like oral ulcers within one month of initiating finasteride(1 mg/day) for AGA at the age of 39. Introduction

Oral aphthosis, a painful inflammatory condition of the oral mucosa, represents a common clinical presentation. When these episodes occur frequently, the condition is termed recurrent aphthous ulceration (RAU). These ulcerations can occur as isolated incidents or manifest secondary to a diverse array of underlying conditions [1]. A wide variety of factors are known to modify immune responses and predispose individuals to oral aphthous ulceration, including genetic susceptibility, microbial infections, food hypersensitivity, nutritional deficiencies (iron, folate, and B vitamins), systemic diseases, hormonal imbalance, mechanical trauma, and psychological stress [2, 3].

This etiological diversity makes the clinical assessment of oral aphthous ulcerations a diagnostic challenge. Consequently, a detailed patient history and thorough review of systems are paramount to determine whether the ulcers are idiopathic or a presenting sign of a systemic disease [1]. A critical component of this history is a complete medical and medication record [4]. This comprehensive approach is fundamental to an accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.

Among the diverse potential triggers, drug-induced etiologies are of particular clinical importance, as withdrawal of the offending agent may result in complete symptom resolution. Indeed, oral aphthous ulcerations have been reported with several commonly prescribed medications, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), antibiotics, β-blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and anti-anginal agents [5]. Notably, finasteride—a selective type II 5α-reductase inhibitor approved in 1992 for benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) at 5 mg/day and in 1997 for androgenetic alopecia (AGA) at 1 mg/day [6]—has not been previously implicated in this adverse effect.

Although finasteride is generally considered safe, a range of adverse effects has been reported, including sexual dysfunction, mood disturbances, endocrine effects such as gynecomastia, and dermatologic reactions [6, 7]. Oral adverse effects have rarely been described, and to date, no association with oral mucosal ulceration has been reported. Here, we present the first case of finasteride-induced RAU-like lesions confirmed by a supervised dechallenge–rechallenge test.

Case presentation

A 45-year-old Asian man, employed as a licensed pharmacist, presented with a six-year history of recurrent, painful oral ulcers. The lesions measured approximately 3–5 mm in diameter, involved the non-keratinized labial and buccal mucosa, and recurred two to three times per month, each episode lasting 7–12 days. The pain significantly interfered with eating and speaking.

The patient was diagnosed with androgenetic alopecia (Hamilton–Norwood stage III) in September 2018 and initiated finasteride 1 mg/day. Aphthous-like oral ulcers developed within one month of treatment initiation and persisted throughout the subsequent six years (September 2018–November 2024). He denied tobacco use, alcohol consumption, recreational drug use, or high-risk sexual behavior. There was no history of chronic systemic illness, autoimmune disease, or prior recurrent oral ulceration. Review of systems was negative for genital ulcers, uveitis, arthralgia, photosensitivity, gastrointestinal symptoms, or features suggestive of systemic lupus erythematosus or Behçet’s disease.

Previous symptomatic treatments included oral vitamin B complex supplementation and topical dexamethasone buccal adhesive tablets, with minimal benefit. A detailed medication history confirmed that finasteride was the only long-term medication used during the symptomatic period; notably, the patient had taken finasteride products from four different manufacturers over time.

Clinical findings

During an active episode, physical examination revealed shallow, round ulcers with a central yellow fibrinous base surrounded by an erythematous halo on the labial and buccal mucosa (Fig. 1A&B). No other mucocutaneous abnormalities were observed.

Laboratory investigations

A comprehensive laboratory evaluation (Table 1) was performed to exclude common secondary causes of recurrent oral ulceration. Nutritional markers (vitamin B12, folate, ferritin), complete blood count, autoimmune serology (antinuclear antibody, anti-double-stranded DNA), and inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein) were all within normal limits. Serologic testing for herpes simplex virus revealed negative IgM and positive IgG, consistent with past exposure without evidence of active infection.

Differential diagnosis and causality assessment

Various pathological conditions are capable of mimicking RAU [8]. Given the chronic recurrent course and characteristic morphology of the lesions, a systematic differential diagnosis was undertaken. Based on the normal levels of vitamin B12, folate, ferritin, and hemoglobin, nutritional deficiencies predisposing to RAU-like lesions were effectively ruled out. Autoimmune diseases, particularly Behçet’s disease and systemic lupus erythematosus, were considered unlikely due to the absence of systemic manifestations, negative autoimmune serology, and normal inflammatory markers. According to the International Criteria for Behçet’s Disease [9], the patient scored only 2 points (oral ulcers alone), below the diagnostic threshold. Viral infection, particularly herpes simplex virus, was also deemed unlikely based on the clinical appearance, prolonged recurrent course, and serologic profile.

The persistent temporal association between finasteride exposure and ulcer recurrence, combined with the exclusion of alternative etiologies, prompted suspicion of a drug-induced reaction.

Dechallenge and rechallenge

Finasteride was discontinued on November 1, 2024, resulting in complete healing of oral lesions within one week. The patient remained ulcer-free until January 2025. A supervised oral rechallenge was subsequently performed from January 1 to February 28, 2025, using the same therapeutic dose of finasteride (1 mg/day) without dose escalation. Oral ulcers recurred within 15 days of re-exposure and continued episodically throughout the rechallenge period.

Finasteride was permanently discontinued on March 1, 2025, after which complete resolution occurred. No recurrence has been observed during a 10-month follow-up period (March–December 2025). Discussion

This report describes, to our knowledge, the first documented case linking therapeutic-dose finasteride to RAU-like lesions [12]. The causal relationship is supported by a consistent temporal sequence, complete resolution after drug withdrawal, unequivocal recurrence upon rechallenge, and exclusion of alternative etiologies.

Although the pathophysiology of RAU is incompletely understood, hormonal influences are increasingly recognized as modulators of oral mucosal homeostasis [13, 14]. Androgen receptors are expressed in oral mucosal tissues [15], and androgens such as testosterone and dihydrotestosterone exert immunomodulatory effects, including suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokines [16, 17]. Finasteride markedly alters androgen signaling by inhibiting conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone [18]. It is therefore biologically plausible that finasteride-induced hormonal alterations could disrupt local immune regulation in the oral mucosa, predisposing susceptible individuals to ulceration.

A notable clinical lesson from this case is the six-year delay in recognizing finasteride as the causative agent, despite the patient’s medical background as a pharmacist. This underscores a common cognitive bias in clinical practice: symptoms arising during long-term therapy are often attributed to idiopathic disease or coincidence rather than considered potential adverse drug reactions (ADR). This is particularly relevant for common, multifactorial conditions such as RAU.

Limitations, and future directions

The main limitation is its nature as a single case report, which limits generalizability and precludes definitive mechanistic conclusions. Hormone levels were not measured, and HLA-B51 testing was not performed. However, the patient’s clinical presentation—characterized solely by isolated oral ulcers over six years without systemic involvement—was inconsistent with systemic autoimmune or inflammatory syndromes typically associated with such markers, rendering their absence unlikely to alter the primary etiological conclusion.

In addition, non-hormonal mechanisms, including potential formulation- or excipient-related effects, cannot be excluded.

Future studies should analyze pharmacovigilance databases and prospective cohorts to determine the incidence of this potential adverse effect. Mechanistic studies exploring the interaction between androgen signaling and oral mucosal immunity are also warranted [6].

Conclusions

This case establishes finasteride as a probable cause of RAU-like lesions, representing a previously unreported adverse drug reaction. Clinicians should systematically consider long-term medications when evaluating patients with unexplained or refractory recurrent oral ulceration. A supervised drug discontinuation trial, when clinically appropriate, may be a valuable diagnostic tool. Reporting such cases to pharmacovigilance systems is essential to refine drug safety profiles and inform clinical practice.
