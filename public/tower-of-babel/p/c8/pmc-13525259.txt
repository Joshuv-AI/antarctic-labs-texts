Background

Orthopaedic surgeons have several strategies for managing glenohumeral osteoarthritis (GHO), including both conservative and surgical approaches. Updated recommendations from the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) describe anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA) as the preferred procedure for patients with GHO and an intact rotator cuff, rather than shoulder hemiarthroplasty. 6 These guidelines are based on recent literature demonstrating that TSA provides superior pain relief, postoperative range of motion (ROM), patient satisfaction, and lower revision and complication rates. 12 While evidence shows that reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) should be considered in patients with rotator cuff pathology, severe glenoid bone loss, and in elderly populations, 5 evidence continues to support aTSA as the gold standard for shoulder replacement in younger, active populations with intact rotator cuffs. 3

Indications

The patient is a 62-year-old female with chronic progressive right shoulder pain that has failed conservative management. Examination demonstrates an intact rotator cuff. Preoperative imaging demonstrates severe joint space narrowing with goat's beard inferior humeral head osteophyte formation, subchondral sclerosis, and cyst formation. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrates reactive bony edema and confirms an intact rotator cuff. There is edema surrounding the long head of the biceps tendon. Also, three-dimensional (3D) preoperative planning is utilized. During templating, we plan for the largest glenoid component that the patient's native glenoid will accommodate. In this case, a medium-sized component was selected. We set the version, inclination, and reaming depth to maximize glenoid component seating. The targeter is patient-specific, placing the central pin in the appropriate position based on the computed tomography (CT) scan and planning for 100% backside seating of the glenoid component on the bone.

Technique Description

The patient is positioned in the beach-chair position with the aid of a pneumatic arm holder. A standard deltopectoral approach is used, with an incision starting just distal to the clavicle, passing over the lateral third of the coracoid process, and continuing down the proximal arm.

Full-thickness flaps are developed with sharp dissection. The cephalic vein is carefully mobilized and retracted medially to minimize traction. The clavipectoral fascia is identified and incised lateral to the biceps tendon.

The anterior humeral circumflex vessels are cauterized at the medial border of the biceps tendon. To improve exposure, the superior insertion of the pectoralis tendon is released. A self-retaining retractor is placed deep to the deltoid and pectoralis major. The biceps tendon sheath is incised with curved Mayo scissors, the release is extended proximally, and the rotator interval is exposed. The biceps tendon is tenodesed to the biceps sheath.

An oscillating saw and a curved osteotome are used to osteotomize the lesser tuberosity with a roughly 4-mm thick bone block. The lesser tuberosity and subscapularis are tagged with a stay suture. Capsular release from the anatomic neck and proximal humerus is then carried out using a Cobb elevator and electrocautery. The inferior capsule is released, and osteophytes are removed from the humeral head. The lateral capsule is first released from its humeral attachment. A Cobb elevator is used to tension the capsule, and an anatomic humeral head osteotomy is then performed. Circumferential retractors protect neurovascular structures and the rotator cuff. A humeral head protector is placed.

A Fukuda retractor and anterior glenoid retractors are used to expose the glenoid. The residual biceps stump is excised, and the labrum is circumferentially removed. The triceps tendon can also be released inferiorly to allow full visualization of the glenoid. The center of the glenoid is approximated with a cross-hash or a glenoid targeted during planning. Preoperative 3D CT/MRI imaging and planning software are used to assess and correct version and inclination. The central hole for the glenoid component is drilled to guide reaming. If there is excessive retroversion, the hole is angled posteriorly to permit anterior reaming. Sequential reaming is performed to remove cartilage and expose bleeding bone, with version correction as needed. The goal is to ream to the correct version and create a uniform surface for the glenoid component without violating the subchondral bone. We choose the largest glenoid component supported by the patient's native anatomy. In this system, a hybrid pegged-keel glenoid is used. A guide prepares the holes, and a keel punch completes the preparation. Holes are thoroughly cleaned and dried. Cement is injected into the holes with a syringe and a pressurizing impactor, then applied to the glenoid component's back surface. The glenoid component is impacted into place flush with the bone, and excess cement is removed. Fixation is confirmed.

Humeral preparation begins with a canal finder at the posterolateral quadrant. The humerus is reamed perpendicular to the humeral head cut. Varus malalignment is avoided, and broaching continues until the angled laser mark aligns with the resected surface. The laser marks guide the adjustment of inclination and version. For the noncemented technique, the final broach size determines the implant size. A trial head is used to confirm sizing.

Attention returns to lesser tuberosity repair: drill holes are made in the bicipital groove and medially in the proximal humerus for later cerclage repair of the subscapularis and lesser tuberosity using a stem-based repair with No. 5 nonabsorbable sutures. Vancomycin powder is applied locally before final humeral implantation to reduce the risk of periprosthetic shoulder infection. The appropriately sized implant is selected, taking into account variability in inclination, offset, and version. The stem is passed through the sutures, seated with a primary impactor, and then oriented to match the version and inclination with a secondary impactor. The stem conforms to the patient's native anatomy. The inferior inclination and superior version locking screws are sequentially tightened and secured with a torque wrench. The final humeral head is placed and rotated to match the offset, then impacted onto the Morse taper. The head is reduced, and soft-tissue tensioning is confirmed; any overhanging bone can be contoured with a rongeur as needed. A posterior shuck should result in approximately 50% posterior translation of the humeral head.

The lesser tuberosity osteotomy (LTO) is repaired using previously placed sutures. A simple suture is used in the middle, and Mason-Allen sutures are placed inferiorly and superiorly. The rotator interval is closed laterally with the arm in external rotation to avoid overtightening, approximating the subscapularis to the supraspinatus. A secure, anatomic repair is confirmed, and no motion is noted at the osteotomy site. Intraoperative ROM is assessed, and the stability of the lesser tuberosity repair is confirmed. Standard layered closure is performed. Vancomycin powder is applied once more. Postoperative anteroposterior and axillary radiographs confirm proper implant positioning and soft tissue balance.

Results

Patients are placed in a sling for 2 to 3 weeks for comfort, with immediate full passive ROM except limiting external rotation to 30° for the first 3 weeks to protect the osteotomy site. Active and active-assisted ROM begin at 4 weeks. Initial resistance strengthening begins at 5 weeks. Patients may return to unrestricted activity beginning 4 months postoperatively. Potential complications include glenoid loosening, rotator cuff failure, glenohumeral instability, and neurovascular injury. Mitigation strategies include appropriate implant size and position, meticulous soft-tissue management, and secure fixation of the lesser tuberosity. aTSA has excellent mid- and long-term outcomes, as published by the senior author. 1 In summary, we present a reproducible technique for aTSA using the deltopectoral approach and LTO. Proper exposure and neurovascular protection are critical. This implant system accommodates native anatomy with variable offset, inclination, and version. Rigid lesser tuberosity fixation allows reliable healing, predictable pain relief, and improved function in well-selected patients.

Discussion

The best approach to treating advanced GHO varies based on the patient's age, activity level, and the condition of their rotator cuff. For patients with an intact rotator cuff, aTSA is considered the standard surgical treatment. Recent AAOS guidelines support this, citing improved pain relief, greater postoperative ROM, higher satisfaction rates, and lower complication and revision rates compared with hemiarthroplasty.6,12 While rTSA is preferred in cases involving cuff deficiency, severe glenoid deformity, or in older patients, aTSA has demonstrated consistent long-term outcomes in younger, active individuals with preserved cuff function.1-3,5,7 High rates of early return to sport have been reported. 4 The rationale for utilizing LTO is bone-to-bone healing, which is biologically more reliable than tendon-to-bone or tendon-to-tendon healing. It has been shown that even though LTO increases the operating time more than subscapularis tenotomy, it heals more reliably than subscapularis tenotomy. 8 By preserving the native insertion of the subscapularis tendon on the lesser tuberosity, LTO allows for restoration of the anatomic length-tension relationship of the subscapularis and facilitates reproducible reattachment at the conclusion of the procedure. The rigid LTO fixation enables reliable subscapularis healing. Subscapularis failure has been linked to worse functional outcomes, higher pain levels, and increased glenoid loosening.9-11,13 Proper execution of the LTO is critical to achieving reliable fixation and healing, and careful attention must be paid to both the location and trajectory of the osteotomy. Bone quality may influence osteotomy technique and fixation. In patients with osteopenia or osteoporosis, the risk of suture cutout or fragment comminution is increased. In such cases, technical modifications include creating a slightly thicker osteotomy fragment and/or utilizing augmented fixation techniques, such as additional sutures. In select patients with severely compromised bone quality, alternative subscapularis management techniques may be more appropriate. Furthermore, humeral implant selection may also influence the LTO. Stemless and short-stem implants with metaphyseal fixation generally allow greater flexibility in osteotomy placement and facilitate unobstructed passage of transosseous sutures. In contrast, long-stem or diaphyseal-engaging implants may limit suture trajectories and necessitate modification of drill hole orientation or suture configuration to avoid implant interference. Additionally, wider metaphyseal short-stem designs may require careful attention to osteotomy thickness and fixation point placement to ensure adequate bone stock for secure repair. At the conclusion of the procedure, LTO fixation should be tailored to both the implant design and bone quality, using transosseous sutures, suture cerclage, or button-based constructs to achieve stable reduction and compression of the osteotomy fragment.

Potential complications of the presented procedure also include glenoid loosening, rotator cuff failure, postoperative instability, and axillary nerve injury. Glenoid loosening may be mitigated by accurately correcting glenoid version and inclination with preoperative 3D planning. When indicated, augmented or bone-preserving glenoid components should be considered, and proper cement pressurization is essential during implantation. Rotator cuff failure is often related to oversizing of the humeral component; if choosing between 2 sizes, surgeons should choose the smaller component to reduce tension on the cuff. Instability may result from component malposition or inadequate soft-tissue balancing. It can be avoided through precise implant placement, careful subscapularis repair, and minimization of posterior capsular release, especially in cases with significant retroversion. Furthermore, the axillary nerve is at risk during inferior capsular release. Gentle technique and avoiding excessive lateral retraction during glenoid exposure can help prevent nerve injury.

Conclusion

We present a reproducible technique that utilizes a deltopectoral approach and LTO. Proper anatomic exposure and protection of critical neurovascular structures are vital components of this technique. This implant system is designed to accommodate a patient's native anatomy with variable offset, inclination, and version. Rigid fixation of the lesser tuberosity promotes reliable healing. When appropriately selected, patients can achieve predictable pain relief and significant functional improvement. We illustrate this technique in a 62-year-old female who underwent right aTSA and subsequently regained excellent ROM and strength, returning to full activity.
