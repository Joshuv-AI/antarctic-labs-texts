Introduction

Growing numbers of left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) are being inserted due to severe heart failure (HF). Arrythmias often coexist in HF and the indication for implementation of intracardiac defibrillator (ICD) exists, according to guidelines.1 Patients with LVAD will often remain conscious during ventricular arrythmias (VAs). It is debated how the programming of the ICD is most sufficient. We report a case of VA in a patient with an LVAD and a suggestion for ICD programming.

Case report

A man aged in his 40s with several years of familial dilated cardiomyopathy was undergoing ambulatory diagnostic workup evaluating his candidacy for heart transplantation (HTx). Due to progressive decline in functional capacity and decompensated HF, the patient was admitted for inotropic therapy, from which he was unable to be weaned. The patient was declined INTERMACS classes 3 to 2 and it was decided to offer treatment with an LVAD (HeartMate 3, Abbott, Plymouth, MN) as a bridge to HTx. Echocardiography before LVAD implantation showed no valve pathology, severely decreased left ventricle (LV) function (ejection fraction 10%), and normal function of the right ventricle (RV). The previous implanted cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator, implanted years ago, was switched off during the operation and only the cardiac resynchronization therapy part was activated throughout the rest of the admission. The LVAD implantation was performed uneventfully. After 5 days of monitoring and stabilization in the intensive care unit, the patient was transferred to the cardiology ward. No arrhythmias were registered during that period until the patient, on day 6, developed ventricular fibrillation (VF) (Figure 1A).

During VF, the patient was sitting up comfortably. The patient moved by himself to the hospital bed next to him and the anesthesiologists were called for urgent general anesthesia, tracheal intubation, and acute direct current (DC) conversion. The patient remained conscious and awake during the course of VF until general anesthesia. The patient was clinically unaffected, with a stable Doppler-derived blood pressure (mean arterial pressure of 70 mm Hg). The LVAD, prior to DC conversion, alarmed for pulsatility index events, but resolved within minutes of onset of VF. These were interpreted as low-flow alarms. Echocardiography immediately before DC conversion showed severely dilated RV and compressed LV, which was interpreted as RV failure due to VF. DC conversion was performed under general anesthesia via the previously implanted ICD device (Figure 1B and Supplemental Video 2).

The post–DC conversion LVAD parameters improved (Table 1 and Supplemental Video 2). The duration of VF to DC conversion was approximately 28 minutes. No electrolyte disturbances were observed. The patient was discharged 23 days after LVAD implantation with fully activated cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator and amiodarone treatment without further arrhythmias observed. The patient experienced an uneventful course of LVAD treatment and had a successful HTx approximately 1 year later.

Although HTx is the gold standard of treatment for patients with severely advanced HF, donor restrictions and contraindications make it necessary to use other therapy options. LVAD has been found to increase survival, with both short-term and long-term use. However, VA is a common complication in patients with an LVAD, which has been found to increase length of hospitalization and impair quality of life.2 Patients with an LVAD are less affected hemodynamically by VA and are either asymptomatic or have mild symptoms (dyspnea or palpitation) because LV unloading occurs passively with LVAD. However, persistent VA can, in some cases, affect the RV and lead to RV failure and subsequently have a negative effect on hemodynamics. Patients with RV dysfunction and pulmonary vascular disease tolerate VA poorly.

Despite significant evidence in patients with HF and ejection fraction <35% and left bundle branch block for the use of CRT, a systematic review and meta-analysis3 found no significant difference in patients with an LVAD or in the incidence of VA, all-cause mortality, or hospitalization. There is also no consensus about the use of ICD treatment in patients with an LVAD. In a large retrospective case-control study, it was found that ICD increased mortality risk 20% in patients with LVAD and, in addition, an increase in hospitalizations and incidence of VA in patients with ICD and LVAD was reported.4 However, in contrast, other studies reported that ICD was effective, and its use was associated with a reduction in all-cause mortality.5

Most centers still implant ICDs in patients with an LVAD. Due to the hemodynamic tolerability due to VA, different algorithms dealing with the ICD programming exist. In a randomized study of patients with ICD without an LVAD, it was found that a long detection interval and antitachycardia pacing before ICD shock reduced the number of ICD shocks compared with the standard detection interval.6 Due to the negative effect of ICD shocks on psychological well-being, we have prepared a suggested set of programming to reduce ICD shock (Table 2).

Conclusion

Ventricular arrhythmia after left ventricular assist device implantation is an often observed complication. We suggested an algorithm for the intracardiac defibrillator optimization to reduce intracardiac defibrillator shock intervention in patients with left ventricular assist device.
