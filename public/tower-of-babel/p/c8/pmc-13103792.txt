Electrothermally Regenerable PEDOT:PSS/PVA Composite Films for Solid-State Dehumidification Applications
Authors: Ziwei Yang, Hikaru Kobayashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Conventional thermal regeneration of solid desiccants is often hindered by high energy consumption. To address this challenge, we propose a novel scheme that eliminates the need for preheating the return air to regenerate the desiccant. Instead, moisture removal is achieved directly via electrothermal heating. To realize this, we employed a composite film strategy based on PEDOT:PSS and PVA, leveraging PEDOT:PSS’s dual hydrophilic and conductive properties while using PVA to improve mechanical robustness. EG is commonly used as a dopant and is considered to promote a conformational change of PEDOT chains from coiled to expanded structures. This transition may contribute to a more continuous conductive network and enhance π-π stacking and charge transport. Multiscale characterization and device-relevant testing were conducted to link the morphology and surface composition to mechanical durability, electrothermal stability, and moisture-sorption behavior. The composites demonstrated significant mechanical stability under cyclic tensile loading. Incorporating PVA markedly improved the mechanical robustness of the otherwise brittle PEDOT:PSS, increasing the elongation at the break to 630%. Moreover, the film maintained structural integrity during 100 loading–unloading tensile cycles to 20% strain under 25 °C and 80% RH. Electrothermal durability tests showed no significant electrical degradation during 12 h of continuous powering, indicating prolonged electrical stability. Water-vapor sorption measurements indicated that introducing PVA reduced the saturated uptake to 82% of the pristine film; nevertheless, the selected formulation (PP6) still exhibited a high absorption capacity of 1132.80 cm3 (STP) g–1 at P/P0 = 0.94, demonstrating strong moisture uptake in the high-humidity regime. Based on comprehensive performance evaluation, PP6 was used to produce the dehumidification element. The element displayed reversible absorption–desorption during electrothermal regeneration in cycling tests powered by approximately 10 W, confirming the composite’s viability via an electro-driven pathway. Overall, this study demonstrates a self-standing PEDOT:PSS-based polymer desiccant that simultaneously achieves humid-state mechanical durability and long-term electrothermal stability, enabling stable low-power regeneration cycling without thermally preheating high-flow-rate return air.

Introduction

In recent years, global climate change has resulted in more frequent, hot, and humid weather during the summer. These environmental conditions decrease human thermal comfort and worsen indoor air quality. − Moreover, they shorten the lifespan of electronic devices, − weaken building structures, , and reduce productivity in industrial processes. Therefore, maintaining appropriate indoor temperatures and humidity is crucial to ensure health, safety, and productivity. In high-humidity environments, air conditioning systems must manage increased dehumidification loads to maintain comfortable and safe living and working conditions.

To address the increasing latent load under hot and humid conditions, desiccant air conditioning systems have attracted growing attention. , Conventional vapor-compression air-conditioning systems remove moisture by cooling and condensation. In contrast, desiccant air conditioning systems remove water vapor directly from the air using desiccant materials. Therefore, these systems are well suited for treating outdoor fresh air with high absolute humidity, which dominates latent heat loads during summer operation.

Accordingly, desiccant materials for desiccant air conditioning applications should exhibit effective moisture uptake under high-humidity conditions. Conventional desiccants, such as silica gel and zeolites, provide relatively stable moisture adsorption over a broad humidity range (10–90% RH). However, this adsorption behavior often leads to a limited increase in the uptake at high humidity. As a result, the dehumidification capacity gain can be constrained under highly humid conditions. In addition, traditional solid desiccant dehumidification systems typically rely on a hot air stream to regenerate the dehumidification module. This method necessitates extra heating units and energy input, increasing the system volume and complexity, thereby raising energy consumption and operational costs. Against the backdrop of global energy constraints and environmental concerns, improving dehumidification performance under humid conditions while maintaining high regeneration efficiency has become a key research focus. − Consequently, the development of new dehumidification materials with reversible moisture sorption-release behavior and robust structural stability is of both scientific and practical significance.

Electrothermal regeneration has recently garnered increased attention and has been explored across various disciplines. This technique leverages the inherent electrical conductivity of adsorbents by utilizing electricity as the primary heating energy source to internally generate heat through the Joule effect. This process enables regeneration without the need for external hot air. , In comparison to traditional heating approaches, this method presents benefits including streamlined processes, enhanced heating efficiency, and accelerated regeneration rates, positioning it as a promising option for future dehumidification systems. Nevertheless, many conventional solid adsorbents like silica gel and zeolites exhibit intrinsically low electrical conductivity, constraining their suitability for electrothermal-regenerative dehumidification applications.

Among candidate materials, organic conductive polymers have garnered interest due to their molecular tunability, structural flexibility, and diverse functionalities. PEDOT:PSS represents a water-dispersible conductive polymer complex. PEDOT is derived from 3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene monomers. Oxidized PEDOT demonstrates high conductivity but is water-insoluble. Its conductivity originates from delocalized π electrons in its chemical structure, while the presence of insulating polystyrenesulfonate (PSS) facilitates PEDOT dispersion in water and stabilizes PEDOT configurations through Coulombic forces. Consequently, PEDOT:PSS displays optimal electron and ion conductivities, with transmission properties exceeding 80%. Owing to its distinctive blend of high conductivity, solution processability, and mechanical flexibility, PEDOT:PSS has found widespread applications in thermoelectric devices, conductive coatings, − and flexible electronics.

Previous studies of PEDOT:PSS have primarily focused on enhancing its electrical conductivity. Common strategies have included incorporating alcohols with two hydroxyl groups (e.g., ethylene glycol, − sorbitol , ), polar solvents (e.g., dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), N,N-dimethylformamide), or employing acid treatments (e.g., sulfuric acid, phosphoric acid). Other approaches have involved incorporating conductive fillers such as multiwalled carbon nanotubes, , single-walled carbon nanotubes, , or silver nanowires to form continuous conductive networks and enhance the material’s charge transport efficiency. Post-treatment washing with solvents like ethylene glycol, among other methods, has been used to reduce or remove the insulating PSS component. Meanwhile, (fluoro)­surfactant additives (e.g., Zonyl) have also been employed to tune PEDOT–PSS phase separation and interfacial molecular orientation, thereby modifying the internal microstructure and interfacial composition distribution of the films to improve electrical conductivity. However, while some methods effectively improve the conductivity of PEDOT:PSS, they also degrade the intrinsic hygroscopic properties of PSS. For example, solvent post-treatments with EG (e.g., rinsing, drop-casting/spin-coating followed by drying) often increase film hydrophobicity by partially removing the PSS-rich surface layer, thereby decreasing the overall PSS content and ultimately reducing the water sensitivity (i.e., the humidity sensitivity of the electrical response). Preserving an appropriate amount of PSS within the system and exploiting its moisture-absorbing characteristics can extend the applicability of PEDOT:PSS to air humidity control and dehumidification. However, the inherent mechanical limitations of PEDOT:PSS hinder its stable operation in air-conditioned environments, which has restricted its exploration in the field of dehumidification materials.

Existing PEDOT:PSS studies generally follow two main routes. One approach involves utilizing it as a conductive layer or coating, or depositing it on substrate surfaces for experimental investigations. − Alternatively, researchers have developed self-standing functional materials by integrating reinforcing components to assess their integrated properties. ,, In our previous studies, we mainly adopted the substrate-supported route to develop dehumidification elements and to clarify key design factors for electrothermal regeneration. Kobayashi et al. prepared PEDOT:PSS dehumidification elements on ceramic paper by impregnation. They demonstrated electrothermal-regenerative desiccant air conditioning operation and identified electrical input and electrode contact as key factors affecting dehumidification performance. Hiura et al. mitigated drying-induced deformation in ceramic-paper-impregnated PEDOT:PSS elements and addressed their limited surface-area scalability by switching to a corrugated impregnation substrate. The corrugated design increased the air-contact area by 230% and improved the dehumidification amount by 20%. With an additional 40 °C hot-air assist, the element achieved performance comparable to a commercial high-performance desiccant rotor. Shirasu et al. further optimized this approach by employing a corrugated substrate and establishing a vacuum-assisted impregnation route, achieving a 1.6-fold higher filling fraction compared with that of conventional soaking. In addition, a PEDOT:PSS-based element with an embedded copper-plate electrode exhibited an electrical resistance of approximately 50 Ω for an element size of 100 × 30 × 10 mm. Based on water vapor sorption isotherms, the same study also reported that increasing the PSS content in the PEDOT:PSS formulation increased moisture sorption.

In related substrate-supported studies, Li and coworkers used a sulfuric acid paper support and introduced PEDOT:PSS composites by repeated soaking-drying impregnation cycles. These repeated soaking-drying cycles improved coating uniformity and reduced the sheet resistance from 13.3 to 5.6 Ω/sq. Stable cyclic operation was also reported, with dehumidification coefficient performance values of 2.00–3.80 over five consecutive cycles. Their group further demonstrated an electrothermal-regeneration dehumidification rotary-wheel device using 180 × 108 × 90 mm elements. A parametric energy coefficient of performance analysis was conducted with respect to air velocity, energization time, and inlet humidity, which supports subsequent system-level optimization. In addition, the same group showed that incorporating commercial desiccant additives could simultaneously enhance sorption and electrical properties: after screening multiple formulations, a composite containing 10% graphite powder and 10% chitosan delivered a 33% increase in moisture sorption capacity, a 37% increase in electrical conductivity, and a 3-fold increase in regeneration amount. The dehumidification coefficient performance increased from 1.7 to 4.28 as the inlet absolute humidity increased, supporting strong performance under high-humidity conditions.

To improve the gravimetric moisture sorption of PEDOT:PSS-based electrothermally regenerable dehumidification elements, we moved away from impregnation-type designs. In such designs, a large substrate mass fraction limits the effective loading of the active desiccant. The literature indicates that reinforcing-phase systems with different interaction mechanisms can exhibit markedly different structural responses under humid conditions. For example, Calvin J. Brett et al. reported that reinforcing systems governed primarily by physical interactions, such as CNF-based systems, may undergo one-time irreversible structural rearrangement and interfacial dewetting upon humidity variation. Such humidity-induced structural evolution may be detrimental to structural retention and long-term stability during repeated moisture sorption-regeneration cycles. Against this background, this study adopts a well-established PEDOT:PSS/PVA composite system as a self-standing functional membrane. Extensive prior research has validated the synergistic benefits of this architecture, where PVA contributes to mechanical integrity and enables self-standing film formation. ,, The PEDOT:PSS content can also be tuned by a formulation design. Based on this established feasibility, we employed the PEDOT:PSS/PVA composite strategy to fabricate electrothermally regenerable solid-state desiccants for dehumidification applications.

Building on this design, PEDOT:PSS was additionally doped with EG to optimize the electrical performance, and the resulting compositions were systematically evaluated in terms of microstructure, molecular interactions, mechanical integrity, electrical performance, durability, and moisture sorption-regeneration behavior. Following a comprehensive performance evaluation, an experimental dehumidification element was fabricated by using a membrane with an optimized composition. The device exhibited reversible moisture sorption-release behavior and a stable electrical response during power regeneration in cyclic tests.

In this work, the self-standing PEDOT:PSS/PVA membrane platform mitigates the dilution of effective active loading associated with the large substrate mass fraction in impregnation-type elements. It also provides a much wider formulation window for tuning the PEDOT:PSS fraction. This enables systematic optimization of the gravimetric moisture sorption and electrothermal regeneration performance. Furthermore, we established a systematic characterization framework within a consistent formulation space. The framework links composition to microstructure and molecular interactions, mechanical stability, electrical performance and durability, and moisture sorption behavior. These results provide practical material-design guidance for optimizing self-standing conductive-polymer desiccants. A prototype element based on a representative high-performing formulation showed a stable electrical response and reversible moisture sorption-release behavior during cyclic operation. These results support the use of PEDOT:PSS in dehumidification systems and highlight opportunities for conductive polymer composites in energy-efficient environmental control.

Experimental Section

Materials

A PEDOT:PSS dispersion with a conductivity of 500 S cm–1 was prepared from a starting PEDOT:PSS solution. To increase moisture uptake, PSS was added and the solids content was adjusted to 1.5 wt % (hereafter referred to as P:P150) (Create Value Co., Ltd., Japan). Following the supplier’s recommendation, 5 wt % EG (Hayashi Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Japan) was introduced as a secondary dopant to improve the electrical conductivity. According to the supplier, the conductivity of the corresponding dried film was 150 S cm–1 (supplier-reported by Create Value Co., Ltd.) measured on a film prepared by drop-casting the EG-treated dispersion onto glass and drying it using a resistivity meter (Loresta-GX) with the measured film thickness. PVA (supplied by Denka Co., Ltd., Japan), selected based on Denka’s specifications for film-forming applications, was added as a reinforcing agent to improve the mechanical strength of the films. Furthermore, 0.05 wt % nonionic surfactant (Surflon S-243, AGC Seimi Chemical Co., Ltd., Japan) was introduced to improve the structural stability of the film during drying by enhancing the interfacial affinity between the film and the carrier tray.

Sample Preparation

Five composite films with different compositions were prepared, as outlined in Table . For all cases except PP0, 80 g of P:P150 served as the base, with varying amounts of PVA added to achieve different mass ratios. The film preparation procedure is depicted in Figure and can be described as follows: A beaker was rinsed with deionized water, filled with 50–70 mL of deionized water, and heated to 90 °C. PVA was gradually added to the solution with stirring at 1000 rpm and was dissolved for 30 min. Simultaneously, another clean beaker was utilized to combine the P:P150 dispersion, EG, and the nonionic surfactant, which were then set aside. After the PVA solution had been stirred for 30 min, the mixed P:P150/EG/surfactant solution was slowly added while maintaining heat and stirring. The resulting mixture was thermally concentrated to approximately 50 g and then cast into a polypropylene tray.

To prevent cracking due to uneven stress from rapid drying, a gradual, low-pressure drying method was utilized. Initially, the cast solution was held at room temperature (approximately 25 °C) under a gauge pressure of −0.09 MPa for 12 h. Subsequently, the vacuum level was gradually reduced to a gauge pressure of −0.05 MPa over 120 h to facilitate water removal. Following this, the pressure was sustained at −0.05 MPa while the temperature was increased to 35 °C for 24 h, leading to film solidification. Finally, the pressure was returned to atmospheric levels and the film was dried at 70 °C for 1 h to produce the final composite film. For comparison, pure P:P150 and P:P150 films with 5 wt % EG were also fabricated to assess the impact of EG on the properties of PEDOT:PSS.

Characterization Techniques

The SEM images were acquired by using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (JEOL, JSM-7100F, Japan). Raman spectra were obtained with a microlaser Raman spectrometer (JASCO, NRS-5100, Japan) employing a 532 nm excitation source. This spectrometer provides a maximum spectral resolution of 1 cm–1. The exposure time for each spectrum was 20 s, based on 20 measurements. FTIR spectra were captured using an FTIR spectrometer (JASCO, FT/IR-6300, Japan) equipped with a microscope (IRT-7000) in attenuated total reflection (ATR) mode across the range 600–4000 cm–1 at a spectral resolution of 4 cm–1 with 50 scans. XPS analysis was conducted using an XPS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Theta Probe, USA) with an Al Kα source. Prior to analysis, the samples were vacuum-stored for 48 h.

Mechanical properties, including maximum strain, were assessed using an electromechanical universal testing machine (Instron 5990R, USA) with a 30 kN load cell. The samples, 40 mm in length and 25 mm in width, were tested at a crosshead speed of 0.2 mm/s under a constant 25 °C airflow generated by an air-conditioning unit (Apiste, PAU-A1400S-HC, Japan). Relative humidity (RH) was adjusted for different test conditions: 60% RH for maximum strain experiments and 40%, 60%, and 80% RH for cyclic loading tests. The maximum strain during cyclic tests was maintained at 20% of the gauge length (8 mm). For both types of mechanical tests, after the RH of the air-conditioning system had been switched from the previous experimental condition to the target set point, the specimen was mounted on the testing apparatus while the chamber humidity was adjusting toward the new condition. With reference to the macroscopic water-uptake behavior of PEDOT:PSS films reported by Lyazzat Zhanshayeva et al. as a background reference for the humidity-conditioning time scale, after the RH reading stabilized, the specimen was further kept for 30 min under the target humidity. The specimen was then removed, and the film thickness was remeasured at three positions (the corresponding data are provided in Table S1). Immediately afterward, the specimen was remounted on the testing apparatus and left for several additional minutes to release the clamping-induced stress before the formal test was started.

Sheet resistance was determined using a four-point probe resistivity tester (Napson, RT-70/RG-7, Japan) on film samples (10 × 10 mm) with a probe spacing of 0.635 mm, a tip curvature radius of 40 μm, and a maximum applied load of 200 g under recorded ambient conditions (21.7 °C, 21% RH). Five measurement points were taken on both surfaces of each film. For electrical durability testing, composite films (60 × 25 mm) were clamped between two carbon plate electrodes, exposing a central area of 25 × 25 mm inside a constant temperature and humidity chamber (Isuzu Seisakusho, Japan) at 25 °C and 60% RH. The films were subjected to a 0.5 W, 50 Hz alternating current for 12 h.

Moisture sorption isotherms were determined by utilizing a gas absorption analyzer (Anton Paar, Autosorb 6100 FKM MP, Austria). Prior to analysis, approximately 0.05 g of the sample was sliced into 2–3 mm square fragments and vacuum-dried at 70 °C for 360 min to eliminate any remaining water content. The moisture sorption-release cycles of water vapor were assessed at 25 °C under a controlled relative pressure.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of P:P150 Films and P:P150/PVA Composite Films

SEM Morphology

SEM was used to examine the morphological changes in the PEDOT:PSS films before and after EG treatment as well as the surface morphology of the films with different compositions following PVA incorporation. Figure a,b shows the microstructural SEM images of the pristine PP10 and EG-treated PP10 films (PP10 + EG). These images reveal micrometer-scale surface features, and EG addition refines the morphology, leading to finer surface aggregates and a more interconnected texture. Figure c,d illustrates the cross-sectional morphologies of the same samples. Pristine PP10 displayed a typical particulate cross-sectional texture, whereas the EG-treated film exhibited a more continuous and fibrous-like morphology. Compared with the particulate morphology of pristine PEDOT:PSS, this more interconnected structure suggests improved continuity of conductive pathways, thereby facilitating electron transport and likely contributing to the enhanced conductivity.

Characterization of films: SEM images of (a) PP10, (b) PP10 + EG, (c) PP10 (cross-sectional view), (d) PP10 + EG (cross-sectional view), (e) PP2, (f) PP4, (g) PP6, and (h) PP8 films. SEM images in parts (a–d) were captured at 500× magnification, while those in parts (e–h) were taken at 200× magnification.

Figure e–h shows SEM images of films with different PEDOT:PSS/PVA ratios (PP2-PP8). It was observed that the phase morphology strongly depends on the composition. At high PVA content (80 wt %, PP2), large white PVA-rich domains were observed (Figure e). With increasing PEDOT:PSS fraction (Figure f–h), PVA and PEDOT:PSS (including PSS) were more uniformly dispersed, the phase boundaries diminished, and no significant segregation was detected (Figure f,g). These observations confirm the formation of a homogeneous composite. Notably, PP6 exhibited a unique fibrous network (Figure g). Such fibrous structures are expected to influence both the mechanical strength and hygroscopic performance of the films, which will be discussed in detail later.

XPS Surface Composition

Owing to potential compositional variations, XPS was used to analyze the surfaces of both sides. Figure a–f shows the XPS spectra of the top and bottom surfaces of the six films, comprising PP0-PP10. These differences in composition might have originated during the drying process due to the hydrophilic/hydrophobic characteristics of the cast polypropylene tray and the physicochemical attributes of PEDOT, PSS, and PVA.

XPS profiles of (a) PP0, (b) PP2, (c) PP4, (d) PP6, (e) PP8, and (f) PP10 films.

For pure PVA, devoid of sulfur, no discernible peaks were observed in the 160–175 eV range (Figure a). In the pristine PP10 sample, both the top and bottom spectra were predominantly influenced by the PSS peak at 168 eV, with the PEDOT signal at 163.8 eV being nearly absent (Figure f). Although previous reports commonly detect both PEDOT and PSS peaks in PEDOT:PSS films, the formulation used in this work contains a substantially higher PSS fraction compared to typical literature reports. ,,, This is corroborated by the film’s relatively low conductivity (150 S cm–1). The excess PSS likely forms a PSS-enriched near-surface layer that attenuates the photoelectron signal from the PEDOT-rich domains beneath. Therefore, when the thickness of this PSS-rich surface layer exceeds the probing depth of XPS, the PEDOT-related signal becomes strongly attenuated or is nearly below the detection limit.

In contrast, the PVA-doped composite films exhibit PEDOT signals around 164 eV, indicating the presence of a detectable PEDOT-related signal on the film surfaces. However, significant differences were noted among the compositions. For PP4 and PP6 (Figure c,d), the top surface spectra displayed stronger PSS peaks with relatively weaker PEDOT signals, while the bottom spectra showed enhanced PEDOT signals and reduced PSS peaks. This surface compositional asymmetry between the two sides of the films may have been influenced by the drying process. In particular, the hydrophobic polypropylene casting tray may preferentially interact with hydrophobic PEDOT.

In addition, the presence of PVA and EG may also affect the surface-sensitive XPS features but through different mechanisms. During drying, EG, with a higher boiling point than water, accumulated preferentially on the film surface, while PSS, which has higher solubility in EG, became enriched there. As a result, for the low-PVA sample PP8 (Figure e), the overall formulation remains dominated by the solid constituents of P:P150. Under this condition, EG-assisted redistribution during drying favors PSS enrichment near the top surface; consequently, the near-surface region probed by XPS is dominated by PSS-rich species. Therefore, the PEDOT-related signal at the top surface is strongly attenuated, similar to PP10. In contrast, in PP2, with a high PVA content, SEM analysis revealed that PVA formed a continuous phase (Figure e). This continuous phase may encapsulate PEDOT:PSS-rich domains and preferentially cover the top surface. Consequently, the PEDOT signals could hardly be detected on the top surface but remained present on the bottom surface (Figure b). These observations suggest that both insufficient and excessive PVA tend to increase the compositional difference between the two surface regions, leading to more pronounced asymmetry in the XPS spectra. In contrast, at intermediate PVA contents, as discussed above for PP4 and PP6, an appropriate amount of PVA may promote more similar PEDOT/PSS surface characteristics on the two sides of the film; therefore, PEDOT remains detectable on both the top and bottom surfaces for PP4 and PP6.

In summary, these results indicate that detectable compositional differences exist near the two film surfaces and that this phenomenon may be jointly related to component redistribution during drying and the influence of other constituents. Given the surface sensitivity of XPS, further clarification of the full through-thickness compositional distribution would still require complementary techniques such as cross-sectional EDS mapping or XPS depth profiling, which will be pursued in future work.

Raman Spectroscopy

Figure a shows the Raman spectra of the PP0-PP10 films utilized for examining the structural alterations in the PEDOT:PSS following EG doping and composite creation. The Raman peak at 989 cm–1 is assigned to the symmetric stretching vibration of the benzenesulfonate group in PSS, with the peaks at 1096 cm–1 and 1126 cm–1 corresponding to asymmetric stretching vibrations.

(a) Raman spectra of the PEDOT:PSS/PVA films. (b) Schematics of benzenoid PEDOT, quinonoid PEDOT, PSS, and PVA. FTIR spectra of (c) PEDOT:PSS/PVA and (d) PP4 films. (e) PP10 hydrogel film model. (f) PEDOT:PSS/PVA hydrogel film model.

For the PEDOT:PSS dispersion (PP10), a prominent peak was observed at 1434 cm–1, corresponding to the symmetric stretching of the CαCβ bond in the PEDOT structure. In PP2-PP8, this peak shifted to 1430 cm–1, which is typically associated with Cα–Cβ bonds in the PEDOT structure. PP2-PP10 displayed peaks around 1366 cm–1, attributed to Cβ–Cβ vibrations, and peaks around 1254 cm–1, corresponding to Cα–Cα bonds in PEDOT. In contrast, PP0 exhibited absorption peaks at 1440 cm–1 and 1370 cm–1, arising from the C–H and O–H bending vibrations.

The distinctive peaks at 1440 cm–1 for PP0 and 1434 cm–1 for PP10 converged into a red-shifted peak at 1430 cm–1 in PP2-PP8. Notably, this final peak position is lower than that of either individual component, rather than an intermediate position expected from a simple spectral overlap, suggesting that intermolecular interactions induced vibrational rearrangement. This spectral evolution is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that the addition of 5% EG to PEDOT:PSS promotes the transformation of PEDOT thiophene rings from CαCβ bonds to Cα–Cβ bonds, facilitating the structural shift from a benzenoid to a quinonoid configuration, as illustrated in Figure b. − The heightened peak intensity at 1366 cm–1 in PP2-PP8 compared with PP10 further validated this transition.

Consequently, the thiophene rings of PEDOT became more coplanar with adjacent units, promoting an increased level of π electron delocalization along the chain. This increase in chain packing density expanded the conjugation domain, favoring π-π stacking. Simultaneously, the PEDOT chains transitioned from a coiled to an expanded coil or a nearly linear conformation. This rearrangement in conformation also induced a shift in the PSS chains, which interact with PEDOT through hydrogen bonding, from a disk-like (Figure e) to linear or expanded conformations. These alterations enhance the interchain interactions, creating more continuous electron pathways and thereby improving the material’s overall conductivity. In conclusion, the inclusion of EG allowed PEDOT:PSS chains within the composites to adopt expanded or linear conformations with a higher carrier mobility, resulting in enhanced electrical conductivity.

FTIR Spectroscopy

To examine the chemical compositions and structural alterations of the pristine PVA, PEDOT:PSS, and EG-doped composite films at the molecular level, FTIR spectroscopy was conducted, as shown in Figure c. The absorption peaks at 2919 cm–1 and 2849 cm–1 were attributed to the C–H stretching vibrations of the aldehyde and alkyl groups, respectively, indicating the integration of aldehyde molecules into the PVA network. The absorption peak around 3325 cm–1 corresponds to the −OH stretching vibration of PVA. In the PP2-PP8 composite films, this peak shifted to 3356 cm–1, implying a reduction in the robust hydrogen bonding interactions within and between the poly­(vinyl alcohol) chains and the emergence of weaker hydrogen bonding interactions with PSS (Figure f). ,

To further investigate the effect of moisture absorption, PP4 was chosen as a representative sample. FTIR measurements were conducted after drying under a vacuum at 70 °C for 3 h and subsequent equilibration at room temperature for 3 h to facilitate moisture uptake. The results depicted in Figure d revealed that the −OH stretching peak at 3356 cm–1 shifted to 3333 cm–1 after moisture uptake, accompanied by a significant increase in peak intensity. This red shift suggests the formation of hydrogen bonds between water molecules and hydroxyl groups during absorption, thereby strengthening the bonds and reducing the vibrational frequency, resulting in the peak shifting toward a lower wavenumber region.

Tensile and Cyclic Mechanical Properties of PEDOT:PSS/PVA Composite Films

Owing to the inherent lack of mechanical strength in pure PEDOT:PSS, it is unable to withstand the repeated expansion and contraction caused by water uptake and release during dehumidification-regeneration cycles. , Therefore, the introduction of a reinforcing phase is necessary to improve its structural stability. Previous studies have shown that the humidity response of PEDOT:PSS-based composite films is strongly system-dependent and cannot be interpreted simply as an increase in the water content. Lucas P. Kreuzer et al. investigated the water uptake behavior of PEDOT:PSS/CNFs nanocomposite films using quasi-elastic neutron scattering (QENS) and reported that water in the films can exist in two forms, namely, fast-moving bulk water and slow-moving hydration water. Under high-humidity conditions, both types of water are present, whereas under dry conditions, bulk water disappears, while part of the hydration water remains. For PEDOT:PSS/CNF systems, which are dominated by physical interactions, the first humidity cycle may also trigger an irreversible structural rearrangement. During the initial exposure to high humidity, the increased amount of hydration water enhances the mobility of polymer chains and components, allowing the film to escape from its initially kinetically trapped morphology and undergo a one-time irreversible morphological adjustment. In subsequent cycles, however, the water dynamics and structural evolution become more reproducible. These findings indicate that, in PEDOT:PSS-based composite films, moisture-induced swelling may be accompanied not only by volume expansion, but also by changes in interfacial state, component redistribution, and microstructural evolution, which can further affect the macroscopic mechanical behavior of the material. On this basis, tensile tests were performed to verify that the PVA-containing composite films exhibited adequate mechanical strength for the dehumidification-regeneration experiments.

As shown in Table and Table S1, the in-plane thickness variation of the films was typically about 0.02–0.04 mm. Considering the sample preparation and application scale of this study and the fact that mechanical stress was calculated using the average of the three thickness values measured immediately before testing under the corresponding condition, the main comparative trends discussed in this work are not considered to be dominated by thickness differences.

Tensile Test

Figure shows the stress–strain curves of PP0-PP8 samples measured at a dry bulb temperature of 25 °C and 60% RH. The results from PP0 demonstrate that PVA maintained a certain level of tensile strength, thereby confirming its potential as a mechanical reinforcement component.

Stress–strain curves of PVA and PEDOT:PSS/PVA composite films (PP2-PP8).

For the PEDOT:PSS/PVA composite films (PP2-PP8), the tensile strength decreased with increasing PEDOT:PSS content. In particular, PP8 exhibited characteristics similar to those of pure PEDOT:PSS, that is, softness and reduced toughness. PP2-PP6 exhibited lower maximum stress values compared with PP0 but showed significantly increased elongation. This suggests that within this composition range, the synergy between PEDOT:PSS and PVA enhanced the film’s ductility. FTIR analysis corroborated this finding: hydrogen bonding between PSS segments and PVA chains partially replaced strong PVA–PVA hydrogen bonds, disrupting crystalline domains and increasing the amorphous fraction. This reorganization led to a more relaxed and slidable chain network, significantly enhancing the film’s extensibility.

It is noteworthy that PP6 exhibited a higher strength than PP4 despite having a lower proportion of PVA, indicating that the fibrous morphology observed in SEM may contribute to mechanical reinforcement. Overall, the PP4-PP8 samples displayed good toughness and ductility at room temperature, supporting the structural stability required for repeated swelling–shrinking cycles during moisture absorption and electrothermal regeneration.

To elucidate the impact of PVA (PP0) on moisture absorption, a control group was created by desiccating hydrogel PVA films at 70 °C under −0.09 MPa for 6 h. Compared with the desiccated state, the hydrogel PVA films showed decreased stress but over a 5-fold rise in elongation, indicating a substantial improvement in plastic deformability due to water absorption.

To assess the toughness of the composite films for desiccant regeneration applications, their strain responses were evaluated by following 100 cycles at a 20% tensile strain. Considering the significant impact of moisture content on film strength, additional tests were conducted at 40% and 80% RH to analyze the structural integrity under varying environmental conditions. The results indicated that as relative humidity increased, the films softened due to moisture absorption, resulting in a gradual decrease in the maximum stress. Despite this decrease, all films completed 100 cycles successfully, and as shown in Figure S1a–e, the stress–strain curves stabilized during the final ten cycles, showing good cyclic stability. This property is crucial for dehumidification elements, where films undergo repeated shrinkage during electrothermal drying and swelling during moisture uptake. The observed stability confirmed that the films maintained their structural integrity even under fluctuating humidity conditions, affirming their suitability as functional units.

In both PP0 and PP4 samples at 40% RH, there was an observed increase in stress as the cycle number increased. This trend is attributed to the progressive drying that occurs during cyclic stretching. When elongated to 120% of their original length, the membranes experienced greater exposure to air, leading to enhanced water loss. Consequently, as the films dried and stiffened over time, their stress responses amplified in subsequent cycles.

Energized Performance Analysis of PEDOT:PSS/PVA Composite Films

Electrical Conductivity (Four-Point Probe)

The results indicate that with increasing PEDOT:PSS content the sheet resistance decreased exponentially. Furthermore, a clear disparity in the resistance was observed between the top and bottom sides. This finding is consistent with the XPS results, which suggest surface compositional asymmetry between the two sides of the film that may have been influenced by the drying process. As a result, the bottom surface, which showed a stronger PEDOT-related surface signature, exhibited a lower resistance.

Electrical Durability under Continuous Powering

Figure b shows the resistance changes of PP2-PP6 films under continuous AC powering (0.5 W, 50 Hz) for 12 h at 25 °C and 60% RH. The utilization of AC power was preferred to prevent bulk degradation induced by carrier migration under the DC conditions. This experiment was designed as an accelerated endurance (stress) test under sustained powering, and it was intended to verify the absence of typical failure-related electrical degradation mechanisms, such as film cracking or disruption of conductive pathways. Initially, all films displayed significant resistance fluctuations, which eventually reached a stable state. Throughout prolonged operation, the films consistently upheld their electrical characteristics, affirming their suitability for electrothermal regenerative dehumidification devices. In addition, infrared thermographs were recorded at 1 h intervals during the 12 h continuous-powering test. As shown in Figure S2, the film remained in a stable heated state throughout continuous powering, and the surface temperature stayed at approximately 55 °C during the test, suggesting that thermal decomposition of the membrane was unlikely under these conditions. ,

Moreover, the resistance variation trends differed significantly among films with different compositions, indicating the influence of material composition on the electrothermal moisture absorption and desorption behavior. Following the establishment of initial conductive pathways, the balance between drying during Joule heating and rehydration from ambient air heavily relied on the PEDOT:PSS/PVA ratio. The electrical response of the composites to moisture absorption is primarily controlled by the dissociation of sulfonic acid groups in PSS, releasing mobile protons that enhance conductivity. , Acting as a hygroscopic conductive element, PEDOT:PSS increased the moisture uptake at higher levels. Consequently, the film with the highest PEDOT:PSS content (PP6) demonstrated a continuous decrease in the resistance during electrical measurements. This decrease can be attributed to the abundant PSS domains, which facilitate a high rate of proton dissociation and a continuous conduction pathway. In practical implementations, standard closed-loop control (e.g., PID-based power regulation) can be used to compensate for resistance drift and to avoid runaway during operation. In contrast, the composite with a low PEDOT:PSS content (PP2) was primarily composed of the PVA matrix. The limited number of PSS sites resulted in a lower proton dissociation rate, which was inadequate to counterbalance the overall resistance. Therefore, a gradual increase in resistance was observed for PP2.

Assessment of Dehumidification Performance of PEDOT:PSS/PVA Composite Films

Figure a presents the water vapor sorption isotherms of the different films. The sorption curve of the pristine PP10 film displayed the typical characteristics of a type III isotherm, indicating a low uptake capacity at low relative pressures and a significant increase at high relative pressures. This behavior is distinctly different from that of common zeolite-based desiccants, which already display measurable adsorption at low relative pressures but show only a marginal increase as the relative pressure approaches unity. It should be noted that the above comparison is intended only to provide an engineering-scale reference, such as gravimetric moisture-uptake capacity, and does not imply that these materials share the same moisture-uptake mechanism or can be directly compared on a mechanistic basis. For swellable PEDOT:PSS/PVA composite films, the measured isotherms reflect not only simple surface physisorption, but also the combined effects of polymer–water interactions and bulk water uptake/swelling. Previous studies have suggested that in the low-uptake region water molecules preferentially associate with hydrophilic sulfonic acid groups and other active sites. As the uptake increases, these sites gradually approach saturation, and additional water exists in a more bulk-like/free-water state, accompanied by volume swelling of the film.

(a) Water vapor sorption isotherms of P:P150 and PEDOT:PSS/PVA composite films. (b) Water vapor adsorption isotherms of silica gel (NS10, KD Corporation, Korea) at 30 °C, silica gel at 22 °C, and UOP 3A zeolite molecular sieves at 25 °C, with values extracted from the literature.

Furthermore, the enhancement in the adsorption capacity of typical silica gel desiccants with increasing relative humidity was substantially lower than that of PP10 (Figure b). , Notably, water-vapor isotherms of silica gel measured between 25 and 45 °C largely collapse onto the same trend line, and temperature variation does not lead to a large deviation in the uptake trend within this temperature window. Therefore, the temperature differences among the literature silica-gel isotherms used here do not affect the validity of the comparison with those of our films. In contrast, the combination of low uptake at low relative pressures and strong uptake at high relative pressures enabled PP10 to perform particularly well in hot and humid environments (e.g., summer conditions), making it potentially suitable for high-humidity dehumidification applications.

In this study, the absorption capacity of PVA was only about one-third that of PP10. As a result, the saturated uptake in the PP2-PP6 composite films decreased with the inclusion of PVA. However, the general sorption patterns of PP2-PP6 aligned with those of PEDOT:PSS, suggesting that the hygroscopic characteristics of the composites were primarily influenced by the PEDOT:PSS component.

Experimental Validation of Dehumidification-Regeneration Performance

Experimental Setup and Apparatus Overview

Based on a comprehensive performance analysis, PP6 was chosen as the experimental sample for further device-level testing. A dehumidification element measuring 5 × 10 × 3.3 cm was fabricated by cutting the PP6 films into 20 rectangular sheets (6 × 3 cm each), which were evenly placed within an acrylic frame. Carbon electrodes were affixed to both sides of the frame, and the films were secured by using carbon paste to ensure consistent electrical contact. To prevent electrode oxidation during operation, carbon-based materials were utilized for all conductive connections. Because both ends of each film sheet were bonded to the carbon electrodes and were not exposed to the airflow, the air-exposed film mass participating in dehumidification was approximately 5.0 g. The final sample is shown in Figure b.

(a) Schematic of the experimental setup and instrumentation. (b) Dehumidification element model. (c) Photographs of the experimental setup.

The assembled element underwent dehumidification-regeneration cycling tests to assess the practical applicability of the PEDOT:PSS/PVA composites as dehumidification materials (Figure a). The test was intended as a feasibility demonstration rather than a dedicated moisture-uptake capacity measurement. The experimental conditions were established following the Japanese Industrial Standards (JIS B8638:2020). The dehumidification air was maintained at a dry bulb temperature of 33 °C and a wet bulb temperature of 28 °C (RH: 68.66%), while the regeneration air stream (indoor return air) was set at a dry bulb temperature of 27 °C and a wet bulb temperature of 19 °C (RH: 46.94%). The airflow velocity was fixed at 1 m/s. A servo-controlled valve switched between the two air streams every minute for dehumidification and regeneration.

During the regeneration phase, the element was heated by applying a 10 W, 50 Hz sinusoidal alternating current through a relay-controlled circuit. Based on the water vapor sorption isotherms shown in Figure a, the moisture uptake per cycle was estimated as 5% of the film mass. This estimate was used only to determine the order of magnitude of the regeneration power for the feasibility test. Thus, the water mass taken up per cycle was approximated as wwater=wfilm×0.05=5×0.05=0.25g

The latent heat of vaporization of water is approximately 2500 J/g at 0 °C. The heat required to evaporate 0.25 g of water is therefore Qwater=wwater×Lwater=0.25×2500=625J

Given a regeneration duration of 60 s per cycle, the corresponding electrical power is Ptotal=Qwatert=62560≈10.42W

Since the latent heat of vaporization decreases with temperature (e.g., approximately 2430 J/g near 30 °C), 10 W was selected as the applied electrical power for the regeneration phase. A honeycomb-flow straightener grid was positioned at the air inlet to stabilize the airflow. Three humidity–temperature sensors (HOKURIKU ELECTRIC INDUSTRY Co., Ltd., HSU-CHU-44A, Japan) were located upstream and downstream of the element to monitor variations at 1 s intervals, and an anemometer was placed at the outlet to measure the air velocity. The experimental setup is illustrated in Figure c.

Dehumidification-Regeneration Performance

To evaluate the cyclic operating performance of the dehumidification element, an electronic balance was used to compare the mass of the element before and after the cycling test so as to check whether any significant net mass loss occurred during the experiment. At the same time, temperature–humidity sensors were used to monitor the humidity changes at the inlet and outlet of the element, and the resulting outlet–inlet absolute-humidity difference was used primarily as an auxiliary quantitative reference for describing the cyclic response of the dehumidification/regeneration process.

Figure a–e presents the results of 45 min cyclic experiments under nonpowered and powered (10 W) conditions. Neglecting possible errors arising from the precision of the electronic balance and slight changes in the center of gravity of the element, the mass of the element before and after the nonpowered test remained essentially unchanged. Each 2 min cycle was divided by valve switching into a 1 min dehumidification half-cycle and a 1 min regeneration half-cycle. Under nonpowered conditions, the dehumidification and regeneration curves remained nearly symmetric over 20 cycles, indicating stable cyclic moisture uptake and release behavior of the membrane under the present test conditions. This near-symmetric behavior under nonpowered conditions also supports the stability and reliability of the sensor response in the present setup.

Under powered conditions, the mass change measured before and after the 20-cycle test was only 0.3 g, indicating that although a certain amount of water loss occurred, no significant net mass loss occurred on this cycle scale. During the regeneration stage, the peak outlet–inlet absolute humidity difference was clearly higher than that under the nonpowered condition, and still remained at approximately −0.5 g kg–1 (dry air) after 60 s. In contrast, under the nonpowered condition, the humidity difference gradually approached zero after about 40 s. These results indicate that electrothermal heating significantly enhanced the desorption process during regeneration. By contrast, during the dehumidification stage, the shape and variation trend of the outlet–inlet absolute-humidity difference curve were generally similar to those under the nonpowered condition. We speculate that this asymmetry between the dehumidification and regeneration curves under powered operation most likely arises because the rapid moisture uptake occurring immediately after electrothermal drying was not fully captured by the present sensor system. For dehumidification elements operating under continuous airflow and switching transients, a more accurate validation method, such as continuous in situ gravimetric measurement, is still necessary for rigorous performance evaluation; this issue will be addressed in future work. Nevertheless, no obvious attenuation or instability was observed in the temperature–humidity response curves over 20 cycles, indicating that the dehumidification element maintained good cyclic operational stability under the present test conditions.

Power monitoring further confirmed that the element maintained a stable power input of 10 W throughout the 45 min test without noticeable fluctuations. Together with the humidity-response profiles and mass-change analyses, these results demonstrate that the PEDOT:PSS/PVA composite film shows good stability and reversibility under cyclic dehumidification and electrothermal regeneration. In addition, Figure b and d shows infrared thermographs of the dehumidification element recorded from above the air inlet and above the air outlet, respectively. Owing to the small film thickness, front-view infrared imaging in the present setup could not clearly resolve the temperature distribution because of limited pixel coverage and related imaging constraints (Figure S3a,b). The hot region in the upper right corner of Figure b (53.7 °C) corresponds to the hot region in the upper left corner of Figure d (46.8 °C), indicating that the apparent temperature distribution was influenced by the viewing angle. Nevertheless, the thermographs confirm that the dehumidification element remained in a stable heated state, without obvious localized overheating. The measured operating temperature was well below the thermal decomposition ranges of PVA and PEDOT:PSS, indicating that thermally induced decomposition was unlikely under the present operating conditions. , These results further confirm the feasibility of the PEDOT:PSS/PVA composite film as a laboratory-scale solid-state dehumidification element.

Conclusion

In this study, PVA was introduced to reinforce the mechanical properties of pristine PEDOT:PSS films, enabling the fabrication of freestanding and structurally stable PEDOT:PSS/PVA composite films for electrothermally regenerable solid-state dehumidification systems. We systematically assessed their structural, mechanical, and electrical characteristics together with their applicability as electrothermally regenerable dehumidification components. Consistent with previous literature, the structural and chemical characterizations suggest that EG treatment is associated with a more extended and continuous conductive structure in PEDOT:PSS. Mechanical tests showed that PVA incorporation enhanced ductility, increasing the elongation at break to 630%. An appropriate PVA content was associated with a more uniform PEDOT/PSS distribution, while hydrogen bonding between PVA and PSS modified the PVA network, increasing tensile toughness and maintaining structural integrity under cyclic extension-contraction. Electrically, AC durability tests showed that no significant electrical degradation occurred during 12 h of continuous powering, supporting long-term electrical stability. Water-vapor sorption isotherms indicated that although PVA reduced the overall moisture capacity to some extent, PP6 still exhibited a high sorption capacity of 1132.80 cm3 (STP) g–1 at P/P0 = 0.94, demonstrating strong moisture uptake in the high-humidity regime. Overall, the PEDOT:PSS/PVA composite films demonstrated mechanical robustness, electrical stability, and reversible moisture uptake and release, indicating their potential as electrothermally regenerable solid-state dehumidification components.

The selected formulation (PP6) was further validated at the device level, showing stable impedance characteristics under AC bias and repeatable moisture uptake-release performance during dehumidification-regeneration cycles. The fabricated dehumidification element achieved rapid and reversible regeneration driven by a power input of 10 W. These results confirm the feasibility of the proposed electrothermal pathway.

This study establishes a self-standing PEDOT:PSS/PVA film platform for electrothermally regenerable solid-state dehumidification. Beyond demonstrating material feasibility, the results provide a materials-level basis for a regeneration route driven directly by electrical input, thereby avoiding the conventional requirement for thermally preheating high-flow-rate return air. The device-level validation of PP6 under approximately 10 W further supports the practicality of this low-power regeneration pathway and its relevance for subsequent dehumidification-element design and system-level optimization.
