Serum carboxymethyl-lysine levels inversely correlate with appendicular skeletal muscle index in female patients with non-dialysis chronic kidney disease
Authors: Yuelan Yang, Yan Li, Yunhao Fan, Yun Liu, Shuhuan Hu, Luona Wen, Ping Zhu, Chunlei Li, Rongshao Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) frequently develop sarcopenia, which worsens their prognosis. Oxidative stress contributes to the pathogenesis of sarcopenia, and advanced glycation end products (AGEs), which are associated with oxidative stress, may promote muscle loss. This study examined the association between serum carboxymethyl-lysine (CML), a major AGE, and sarcopenia in patients with non-dialysis CKD.MethodsIn this single-center cross-sectional study, 132 patients with non-dialysis CKD were enrolled based on inclusion/exclusion criteria. Clinical, anthropometric, and body composition data were obtained using bioelectrical impedance analysis. Sarcopenia was diagnosed based on the 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia criteria. Correlations between CML and sarcopenia indices were assessed using Spearman’s/Pearson’s correlation and linear regression. The predictive performance of CML for sarcopenia was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and area under the curve (AUC).ResultsAmong patients with non-dialysis CKD, the overall sarcopenia prevalence was 25.76%, and the mean serum CML concentration was 46.2 ± 7.1 µg/L. Multivariate linear regression analysis revealed a significant negative association between CML levels and appendicular skeletal muscle index (ASMI) (β = −0.27, p = 0.01), particularly among females (β = −0.40, p = 0.01). However, ROC curve analysis showed an AUC of 0.65 (p = 0.33) for serum CML in diagnosing sarcopenia in females, indicating that CML does not demonstrate statistically significant discriminative ability beyond chance for predicting sarcopenia.ConclusionsElevated serum CML levels were associated with increased sarcopenia prevalence in patients with non-dialysis CKD. CML levels were independently inversely correlated with ASMI, especially in females. The cross-sectional design and limited sample size, however, warrant cautious interpretation and validation in larger cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-04948-4.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Carboxymethyl-lysine, Advanced glycation end products, Sarcopenia

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a major global public health burden owing to its high prevalence and associated complications, posing key challenges in contemporary nephrology. Sarcopenia, characterized by progressive skeletal muscle mass loss and dysfunction, is a common comorbidity in patients with CKD, particularly in those with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1]. The coexistence of CKD and sarcopenia is associated with adverse outcomes, including reduced quality of life, depression, protein-energy wasting, fractures, cardiovascular complications, an increased risk of graft failure, postoperative complications in transplant recipients, and higher rates of hospitalization and mortality [1, 2]. These outcomes place a considerable burden on families and healthcare systems. Therefore, early identification of sarcopenia and prevention of its complications remain clinically essential.

Advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) are uremic toxins generated through non-enzymatic reactions between reducing sugars and their metabolites (proteins and amino acids), known as glycation (the Maillard reaction) [3]. AGEs have been implicated in CKD pathogenesis [4] and in CKD, AGE accumulation results from increased AGE production, decreased renal excretion, and an imbalance between oxidative and antioxidant capacities. This imbalance is a key driver of AGE synthesis. AGEs promote CKD progression and its associated complications by generating reactive oxygen species, inducing inflammation, and promoting fibrosis. These pathological processes further exacerbate AGE and uremic toxin accumulation, leading to muscle mass and function decline [5]. Loss of muscle strength is a substantial negative prognostic indicator in CKD, associated with increased mortality, reduced ESRD duration, and lower serum albumin(ALB) levels [6, 7].

Primary AGEs include carboxymethyl-lysine (CML), methylglyoxal (MG)-derived hydroimidazolone (MG-h1), and glucose ethers [4]. CML may serve as an independent risk marker for muscle mass loss in patients with CKD. Consequently, we conducted a single-center cross-sectional study to explore the relationship between serum CML levels and sarcopenia in individuals with CKD.

Materials and methods

Population and study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted between August 2021 and December 2023 at Guangzhou Red Cross Hospital. Patients with non-dialysis CKD were admitted to the Departments of Nephrology and Endocrinology. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) clinically stable CKD stages 1–5 without dialysis or renal transplantation; (3) ability to perform handgrip strength (HGS), pinch strength, and bioimpedance spectroscopy (BIS) assessments; and (4) provided informed consent. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) presence of a pacemaker; (2) malignancy, decompensated liver cirrhosis, heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular disease, or severe infection within the past 3 months; (3) cognitive or physical impairments hindering cooperation; and (4) refusal to participate. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, received approval from the Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Red Cross Hospital (No. 2023-040-01), and obtained written informed consent from all participants.

Research methodology

Demographic, clinical, and laboratory parameters

Demographic and clinical data included name, sex, smoking status, and primary diagnosis. Venous blood samples were collected early in the morning on the second day after admission by trained nurses and sent to the hospital laboratory within 2 h for analysis. The assays included routine hematology, serum biochemistry, and enzyme immunoassays. Specific parameters measured included blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, hemoglobin (Hb), C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cells (WBC), triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein, ALB, fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin, prealbumin (PAB), and calcium. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease formula [8]:

eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m2) = 186 × serum creatinine (mg/dL)−1.154 × age (years)−0.203 × (0.742 if female) × (1.21 if black).

CML measurement

Collected blood samples were processed for serum separation at the Institute of Clinical Pathology and Nutrition laboratory and stored at − 80 °C. Thereafter, the separated serum was sent to Jiangsu Lu Sen Bioengineering Co., Ltd., where serum CML levels were quantified using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Carboxymethyl-lysine ELISA Kit, AMEKO, Shanghai, China).

Anthropometry and body composition analysis

Height and weight measurements were performed using a calibrated stadiometer and scale, respectively. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as the weight in kilograms divided by the square of the height in meters. Upper arm circumference (UAC) and triceps skin-fold thickness (TSF) were assessed following the guidelines established by Frisancho [9]. All measurements were averaged, with UAC and TSF readings recorded to 0.1 cm and 0.1 mm accuracy, respectively.

Handgrip and pinch strengths were assessed using a BASELINE digital grip tester (model 12–0091, Fabrication Enterprises Inc., White Plains, NY, USA) and a BASELINE digital pinch tester (model 12–0081, Fabrication Enterprises Inc.), respectively. The participants exerted the maximum force with their dominant hand during these assessments. Each measurement was conducted three times, and the highest value was recorded. Participants’ body composition was evaluated using a body composition analyzer (Multiscan 5000; Bodystat, Douglas, Isle of Man [UK]) via BIS. These measurements were conducted while the participants were in a supine position, with electrode sheets affixed to the hands and feet on the side without a hemodialysis fistula. Relevant participant information was entered prior to the measurement. The measured parameters included lean body mass, phase angle (PhA), body cell mass, extracellular water (ECW), intracellular water (ICW), and basal metabolic rate (BMR). Sarcopenia assessment

Sarcopenia was defined according to the 2019 criteria of the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia: (1) ASMI < 7.0 kg/m2 for males and ASMI < 5.7 kg/m2 for females; (2) Handgrip strength < 28 kg for males and < 18 kg for females [13].

Statistical analyses

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism statistical software version 9.1.0 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). The normality of the distribution for continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables such as age, height, weight, BMI, HGS, and serum CML levels were normally distributed. In contrast, variables including eGFR, ASMM, ASMI, and BUN did not follow a normal distribution. Continuous data with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using the independent samples t-test. Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to assess correlations. For non-normally distributed variables, data were expressed as the median (interquartile range), with intergroup comparisons conducted using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and correlation was evaluated via Spearman’s rank analysis. The χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical data between the groups. Univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses were performed to investigate the relationship between CML and ASMI in patients with CKD. The predictive value of serum CML for sarcopenia in patients with non-dialysis CKD, stratified by sex, was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and optimal cut-off value were calculated. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Among them, 34 patients had sarcopenia (average age 66.1 years), and 98 did not (average age 67.9 years). The prevalence of sarcopenia was 25.76%; among patients with sarcopenia, 88.24% were male. The sarcopenia group had a significantly higher proportion of males (p < 0.001) and lower measurements for weight, BMI, ASMM, ASMI, UAC, HGS, lean, ICW, ECW, BMR, and Hb (p < 0.05). Relationship between CML and core sarcopenia metrics

Univariate linear regression indicated a significant inverse association between CML and ASMM (β = −0.19, p = 0.03, 95% CI: −0.62 to − 0.02). Similarly, the appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI) was significantly inversely correlated with CML (β = −0.19, p = 0.03, 95% CI: −1.86 to − 0.11). Even after adjusting for multiple variables, this correlation persisted. In contrast, no significant association was observed between CML level and handgrip strength (p > 0.05), as shown in Table 2.​

Sex-stratified correlation analysis of CML and ASMI

As indicated in Table 1, no statistically significant difference in CML levels was observed between individuals with and without sarcopenia (p > 0.05). However, a significant difference was observed between the sexes (p < 0.001). Consequently, a correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between CML and sarcopenia diagnostic indicators stratified by sex. The findings revealed that CML was independently and negatively correlated with ASMI in females (rs = − 0.256, p = 0.034). However, CML was not significantly associated with ASMI in male patients (Fig. 2).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of 132 non-dialysis CKD patients, we found a significant association between elevated serum levels of the advanced glycation end product CML and sarcopenia. The overall prevalence of sarcopenia was 25.76%. Multivariate analysis revealed an independent, inverse correlation between serum CML and ASMI in the entire cohort, with a notably stronger association observed specifically in female patients. However, the utility of serum CML as a standalone diagnostic biomarker for sarcopenia appeared limited, as indicated by its non-significant predictive performance in receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. The following discussion contextualizes these findings, exploring potential mechanisms, clinical implications, and study limitations.

CML is the most prevalent AGE in foods and organisms and is often used to study AGE toxicity, formation, and inhibition. CML is produced via various pathways involving precursors, such as glyoxal and fructosyl lysine [14–16]. One study demonstrated that elevated plasma CML levels correlate with reduced walking speeds in community-dwelling older adults [17]. Additionally, older community-dwelling females with increased CML levels had a heightened risk of severe walking impairment, with elevated CML serving as an independent predictor of this condition [18].

In this study, a 25.76% prevalence of sarcopenia was observed among patients with non-dialysis CKD, with a significantly higher percentage of male patients (88.24%). These findings align with Asian epidemiological data [19] and the biological characteristics of faster skeletal muscle loss in males [20]. The higher sarcopenia incidence in males may be closely linked to androgen secretion. Age-related androgen level decline leads to a sharp reduction in protein synthesis, triggering sarcopenia development [21]. Data show that the absolute decline in skeletal muscle mass with age is almost twice as great in males as in females, likely owing to hormonal factors (e.g., growth hormone, insulin-like growth factor-I, androgens) [22]. Notably, Yang et al. [23] found that sarcopenia prevalence was 1.49 times higher in females than in males in rural eastern China. Similarly, a Korean study of individuals aged 65–74 years showed a higher sarcopenia prevalence in females (26.4%) than in males (19.2%) [24]. These findings suggest that sex-specific differences in sarcopenia may result from complex interactions among hormonal factors, geographic variations, and lifestyle patterns, highlighting the need for further investigation.

In this study, serum CML did not increase with progression of renal function; however, it remained relatively stable, unlike previous findings [25, 26]. During CKD progression, a declining glomerular filtration rate promotes gradual AGE accumulation because of reduced renal excretion. Concurrently, an imbalance in the oxidative/antioxidant system may further facilitate AGE synthesis [5]. Previous community-based research on an older cohort identified a significant independent association between elevated CML levels and both CKD and eGFR [26]. AGEs induce skeletal muscle atrophy and impaired myogenesis via an inflammatory response and/or muscle endothelial dysfunction via interactions with receptors for advanced glycosylation end-products (RAGEs) [27]. AGE levels correlate with RAGE levels, both of which increase as renal function deteriorates [25]. The observed discrepancies may stem from several study limitations, including the analysis of a single AGE type, a relatively small sample size, and the single-center design. Comprehensive evaluations of multiple AGEs, multicenter participation, and large-sample studies are needed to further validate AGE dynamics during CKD progression.

AGEs contribute to impaired mitochondrial energy production by reducing succinate dehydrogenase activity and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator1-alpha levels [28, 29]. AGEs can also negatively impact muscle function by reducing extracellular matrix turnover and increasing the stiffness of muscular connective tissue [30]. AGEs have been shown to induce skeletal muscle atrophy by impairing myogenesis, which occurs through inflammatory responses and/or via interaction with RAGEs, leading to vascular endothelial dysfunction in muscle tissue [27]. CML has been identified as a signaling ligand for RAGEs both in vivo and in vitro, and ligand binding activates oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling cascades via nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) [31]. RAGE-mediated signaling pathways, including phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase, Ki-Ras, and mitogen-activated protein kinases, such as Erk1 and Erk2, facilitate NF-κB translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, promoting inflammation and tissue damage [32, 33]. Furthermore, the binding of AGEs to their receptors stimulates the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) through the activation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate(NADPH)oxidase and several mitochondrial pathways [34]. RAGEs are expressed at low levels under physiological conditions; however, they are upregulated during chronic inflammation and oxidative stress, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response [35, 36]. This activates a vicious circle that increases intracellular ROS synthesis and the RAGE-mediated response [5]. Elevated AGE concentrations are associated with weight loss and reduced fat mass [37]. This results from AGE accumulation, which induces inflammation, enhances proteolytic metabolism, and increases resting energy expenditure, leading to an overall catabolic state [38]. AGE accumulation can induce myosteatosis, decrease muscle mass, reduce mitochondrial efficiency, and favor the transition of fast-to-low-speed muscle fibers [5]. Increased expression of RAGEs on the cellular membrane and activation of the lipogenic pathway SCAP (sterol regulatory element binding protein cleavage-activating protein)/SREBP (sterol regulatory element binding protein) have been suggested as potential mechanisms linking intracellular [39].

Upregulation of pro-inflammatory factors contributes to the pathophysiology of several diseases, including sarcopenia [40]. Systemic inflammation may promote muscle atrophy by disrupting the balance between protein synthesis and catabolism [41, 42]. Elevated inflammatory cytokine levels have been linked to reduced muscle strength and mass [43, 44]. Inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress activate the ubiquitin-proteasome system, resulting in increased muscle proteolysis [45]. In well-functioning older adults, higher inflammatory cytokine levels correlate with decreased muscle mass [44]. In this study, CML and CRP levels were higher in the sarcopenia group among patients with non-dialysis CKD, consistent with previous research, though differences were not statistically significant, possibly owing to a small sample size. Furthermore, CRP, an acute-phase protein, is influenced by short-term factors, such as infection and stress, which were not monitored in this study. Additionally, metabolic disorders prevalent in patients with CKD may obscure the inflammatory effects of AGEs. Therefore, future studies with larger sample sizes are required to confirm these findings.

Our findings showed that higher serum CML levels were linked to increased sarcopenia prevalence and lower ASMI, especially in female patients with CKD, even after adjusting for multiple variables. It should be noted that although statistically significant, the inverse correlation between serum CML and ASMI in females was modest (rs = -0.256, p = 0.034). However, this association was not observed in males. These results support the hypothesis that AGEs contribute to sarcopenia development [27]. Muscle mass loss in humans is associated with age-related AGE accumulation [46]. While potential systematic bias may affect absolute ASMI values, the primary focus of our analysis was the relative association between serum CML and ASMI within our cohort, which remains valid for internal comparisons. Therefore, we concluded that the negative impact of CML on skeletal muscle mass in female patients with CKD and in patients with non-dialysis CKD was significant. This suggests that CML may serve as a potential biomarker associated with reduced muscle mass in female non-dialysis CKD patients. However, the ROC curve analysis revealed that serum CML alone lacked statistically significant discriminative power (AUC = 0.65, p = 0.33 in females) for diagnosing sarcopenia, with AUC values close to 0.5 indicating limited predictive utility as a standalone diagnostic tool. Several factors may explain this finding. Sarcopenia is a multifactorial condition, where the effect of CML is likely confounded by stronger determinants such as inflammation, physical inactivity, and nutritional status [47]. Furthermore, a single serum CML measurement may not accurately reflect long-term tissue accumulation of AGEs or localized intramuscular glycation, which are more directly implicated in muscle pathology. Finally, the modest sample size, particularly within sex subgroups, may have limited the statistical power to detect a significant predictive relationship. In addition, owing to the cross-sectional study design, causality between CML and skeletal muscle mass loss cannot be established and requires further investigation.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to identify CML as a potential sex-specific risk marker for muscle mass loss in the Chinese population, offering a novel perspective for early intervention in CKD-associated sarcopenia. These findings establish a theoretical foundation for investigating the inhibition of the advanced glycosylation end-product receptor (AGE-RAGE) signaling pathway as a potential early intervention target in CKD-associated sarcopenia.

This study had some limitations. First, due to its cross-sectional design, this study could not establish a causal relationship between serum CML and sarcopenia, which requires future prospective cohort or intervention studies. Second, the small sample size and single geographic location may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the findings; thus, larger multicenter studies are warranted. In addition, as AGEs comprise a large class of compounds, this study only measured serum CML levels without assessing the potential synergistic effects of other AGEs, such as pentosidine or methylglyoxal derivatives. The dietary intake of AGEs was also not determined, which may have influenced the findings. However, dietary AGE intake has been shown to correlate moderately with circulating CML concentration [48]. Future research should investigate the molecular mechanisms through which CML affects muscle mass, such as the RAGE-NF-κB signaling pathway. Elucidating these mechanisms at the cellular and molecular levels would enable a more comprehensive understanding of the combined effects of multiple AGEs.

Conclusions

Our results indicate that elevated serum CML levels were associated with an increased prevalence of sarcopenia in patients with non-dialysis CKD. Serum CML was independently and negatively correlated with ASMI, especially in female patients. These findings suggest that CML may serve as a potential indicator for assessing sarcopenia risk in CKD populations, particularly among female patients. These findings provide novel insights into early intervention strategies for CKD-related sarcopenia. However, the absence of a validation cohort and the relatively small sample size limit the strength and generalizability of our findings.​.
