Dietary index for gut microbiota, genetic risk, and incidence of breast cancer: a prospective study
Authors: Xi-Yang Chen, Ying Wang, Dong-Run Li, Bang-Quan Liu, Qian Fang, Yu-Han Chen, Jia-Xin Liu, Chao Ni, Wen-Rui Zheng, Jia-Cheng Liu, Xue Qin, Shan-Yan Gao, Ting-Ting Gong, Qi-Jun Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs a novel index designed to quantify the dietary impact on gut microbiota, the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) remains unexplored in relation to breast cancer risk. This study aimed to explore the association between DI-GM and breast cancer risk and evaluate its combined effect with genetic risk.MethodsA total of 96,195 participants from UK Biobank cohort were included in the study. DI-GM was constructed from 14 dietary components known to influence gut microbial diversity and was divided into three groups. A polygenic risk score (PRS) for breast cancer was constructed using 168 single-nucleotide polymorphisms to assess genetic risk. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsDuring a median follow-up period of 12.86 years, 3,128 individuals developed breast cancer. In the multivariable-adjusted model, each point increase in DI-GM score was associated with a 5% reduction in breast cancer risk (95% CI: 0.92–0.99). Compared with those in low DI-GM, participants with high DI-GM were associated with a decreased breast cancer risk (HR: 0.89; 95% CI: 0.81–0.97). Joint analysis revealed that participants with high DI-GM and low PRS had a 35% lower risk of breast cancer compared with those in low DI-GM and high PRS (95% CI: 0.29–0.42). Notably, additive interactions were observed between DI-GM and PRS. Compared with individuals with low DI-GM, high DI-GM was significantly associated with a reduced breast cancer risk in individuals with low PRS (HR: 0.79; 95% CI: 0.65–0.97).ConclusionHigher DI-GM was associated with a decreased risk of breast cancer, highlighting the importance of considering a gut microbiota-friendly diet in breast cancer precision prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-025-02093-8.Keywords: Breast cancer, Cohort study, Dietary index for gut microbiota, Genetic risk

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequent malignancy worldwide, and accounts for about 30% of female cancers [1, 2]. Approximately 2.3 million new cases of breast cancer and 665,000 deaths were estimated to occur in 2022 [3]. As a major contributor to the global health burden [4], identifying modifiable lifestyle risk factors for breast cancer is critical to inform effective prevention strategies and public health policies.

The gut microbiota, a complex community of microorganisms residing in the digestive tract, is emerging as a significant factor in human health and disease [5]. Emerging evidence have suggested that gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in cancer development [5, 6]. Disruptions in the composition and function of the gut microbiota, termed dysbiosis, have been associated with increased susceptibility to breast cancer, potentially affecting tumor initiation, growth, and response to therapy [7–9]. Diet is a critical determinant of gut microbiota composition, with different dietary patterns shaping the abundance and diversity of microbial species [10]. Recently, the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) was developed as a comprehensive quantitative tool to evaluate dietary patterns that foster a healthy gut microbiota [11]. It has been proven to positively correlate with urinary enterolignans, indicating a relationship with gut microbiota diversity [11]. DI-GM can be applied to identify dietary components that may influence the risk of various health outcomes, thus supporting personalized nutrition strategies. However, the potential association between DI-GM and breast cancer risk remains unexplored.

Additionally, a growing number of inherited genetic loci associated with breast cancer risk have been identified in genome-wide association studies [12, 13]. A polygenic risk score (PRS) constructed based on these loci can effectively measure the cumulative effect of multiple risk-associated variants on breast cancer risk [14, 15]. The concept of “gene-environment interactions” suggests that environmental factors, such as modifiable lifestyles (e.g., diet), may have varying impacts on breast cancer based on genetic susceptibility [16]. To our knowledge, no studies have evaluated the interaction between DI-GM and PRS on the risk of breast cancer.

Therefore, we conducted a prospective study using data from the UK Biobank to explore the associations between DI-GM and breast cancer risk, as well as whether genetic risk can modify these associations.

Method

Study design and population

Study design and methods of UK Biobank have been reported in detail previously [17]. For this analysis, we excluded participants who did not complete the 24-hour food recall questionnaire (n = 291,290), those diagnosed with any cancer at baseline (n = 17,957), those who were not of white European ancestry (n = 9,253), those with incomplete genetic data for constructing the PRS at baseline (n = 3,621), those with implausible total energy intake (men < 800 or > 5,000 kcal/day; women < 500 or > 4,000 kcal/day; n = 876) [18], and male participants (n = 82,940). The final analytic sample included 96,195 participants (Fig. 1). The present study was conducted under application number 211,772 of the UK Biobank data resource.

Flow chart of the study design

Definition of dietary index for gut microbiota

The calculation of the DI-GM was based on dietary data from the UK Biobank, which involved an initial assessment from April 2009 to September 2010 and four subsequent online follow-up surveys. For participants who completed multiple 24-hour food recall assessments, nutrient intake for each dietary component was calculated as the mean of all available measurements, following the methodology by Kase et al. [11]. This mean was used to derive a single, habitual DI-GM score at baseline, which was then treated as a fixed exposure throughout the follow-up period in our analyses, rather than a time-varying covariate.

Our study utilized the DI-GM scoring criteria proposed by Kase et al., which identified 14 food items or nutrients as components of the index [11]. These components were divided into two groups: 10 beneficial factors, including fermented dairy, chickpeas, soybeans, whole grains, fiber, cranberries, avocados, broccoli, coffee, and green tea; and 4 unfavorable factors, including refined grains, red meat, processed meat, and daily energy intake (> 40% from fat) [11]. The DI-GM score was calculated based on the sex-specific median intakes of these components, except for fat intake, which was fixed at 40% of total energy. A score of 1 was assigned to individuals who consumed above the median level of beneficial components or below the median level of unfavorable components; in other cases, a score of 0 was assigned. The total DI-GM score was derived by summing the individual component scores, ranging from 0 to 14. A higher DI-GM score indicates stricter adherence to a diet that is beneficial for gut microbiota [11]. Participants were further categorized into three groups based on their DI-GM scores: low (0–4), moderate (5–6), and high (≥ 7). The scoring criteria, coding information, and portion sizes for each component used in this study are detailed in Supplementary Tables 1–3.

Polygenic risk score for breast cancer

The genetic data from the UK Biobank were genotyped using the UK BiLEVE Array and the UK Biobank Axiom Array. A comprehensive quality control pipeline was subsequently implemented, which included variant-level and sample-level filtering, adjustment for population stratification, and imputation using the Haplotype Reference Consortium panel. To evaluate the genetic predisposition to breast cancer, we constructed a PRS based on 168 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) that achieved genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) in populations of European ancestry. These SNPs were extracted from primary studies [19]. Detailed information on the SNPs is provided in Supplementary Table 4. The PRS was calculated as the sum of risk alleles weighted by their respective effect sizes. Based on their PRS, participants were categorized into three genetic risk groups: low risk (lowest tertile), intermediate risk (middle tertile), and high risk (highest tertile) [20].

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome of interest in our study was incident breast cancer. Breast cancer cases in the UK Biobank were ascertained through record electronic linkage with the National Health Service central registers and death registries in England, Wales, and Scotland. Follow-up ended at the date of the first-ever breast cancer diagnosis, death, or the end date of follow-up (October 31, 2022, for England, May 31, 2022, for Wales, and August 31, 2022, for Scotland), whichever occurred first. Breast cancer was defined as the International Classification of Diseases edition 10: C50. Detailed codes can be found in Supplementary Table 2.

Assessment of covariates

Based on prior literature and biologically plausible associations, potential confounding covariates were identified and assessed [16, 21, 22]. These included: age at recruitment (continuous, years); annual household income (categorized as < £31,000, ≥ £31,000, or unknown/missing); education level (high, medium, or no above); the Townsend Deprivation Index (continuous); anthropometric measures, including height and weight, were collected by trained staff at baseline, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/[height (m)]2 (continuous); family history of breast cancer (yes or no) self-reported at baseline; and various lifestyle and behavioral factors, such as total energy intake (continuous, kJ/day), smoking status (never, previous, or current), alcohol consumption (heavy, moderate, or none), physical activity level [low (< 600 metabolic equivalent tasks (MET) minutes/week), medium (600-3,000 MET minutes/week), or high (> 3,000 MET minutes/week)], menopausal status (yes or no), parity (0, 1, 2, or ≥ 3), history of hormone use (yes or no), history of oral contraceptive use (yes or no), and age at menarche (< 13 years or ≥ 13 years).

Missing data for continuous covariates were imputed using mean values, while missing categorical covariates were assigned to an unknown/missing indicator category. Detailed coding information is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were described as mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables, and as counts with percentages for categorical variables, stratified by DI-GM groups. To assess differences, the Kruskal-Wallis test was applied for continuous variables, and chi-square tests for categorical variables.

The proportional hazards assumption was tested using the Schoenfeld residual test, with no violations observed (P > 0.05). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to assess the associations between DI-GM and the risk of breast cancer. Results were expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three models were developed: (1) a crude model (unadjusted for any covariates); (2) Model 1, adjusted for age, BMI, and energy intake; and (3) Model 2, further adjusted for annual household income, smoking status, education level, alcohol consumption status, physical activity level, family history of breast cancer, Townsend Deprivation Index, menopausal status, parity, history of hormone use, history of oral contraceptive use, and age at menarche. DI-GM was analyzed both as a categorical variable (with the low group as the reference) and as a continuous variable (per one-score increase). Additionally, we examined the potential nonlinear relationship between DI-GM, PRS, and breast cancer risk using restricted cubic splines (RCS), with knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles.

To evaluate the combined effects of PRS and genetic factors on breast cancer risk, participants were categorized into nine groups based on PRS categories. Additive interaction was assessed by calculating the Relative Excess Risk due to Interaction (RERI) and Attributable Proportion (AP), with significance determined using the delta method [23, 24]. Multiplicative interaction was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests.

To evaluate whether the associations between DI-GM and breast cancer risk varied across subgroups, analyses were stratified by age, BMI, education level, Townsend Deprivation Index, smoking status, alcohol consumption, annual household income, menopausal status, parity, history of hormone use, history of oral contraceptive use, and age at menarche.

We conducted a series of sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our primary findings: (1) reconstructing the DI-GM by excluding cranberries and chickpeas, because the UK Biobank not recording the specific type of dried fruit and pulses consumption [25]; (2) excluding participants diagnosed with breast cancer within the first two years of follow-up to minimize reverse causality; (3) excluding individuals who completed the 24-hour online dietary recall questionnaire only once to ensure that the average intake of all dietary components accurately reflected habitual intake; and (4) excluding individuals with unknown or missing covariates.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.1. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Baseline characteristics of participants

Among the 96,195 participants enrolled in the study, 3,128 individuals (3.25%) were diagnosed with breast cancer during a median follow-up period of 12.86 years (IQR: 12.26–13.65 years). Baseline characteristics classified according to the DI-GM are shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 5. Participants with higher DI-GM scores were older, had lower BMI and Townsend deprivation index, had a higher education level, were more likely to be never smokers, higher levels of physical activity, were more frequently postmenopausal, had a higher proportion of nulliparity (parity 0), had no history of oral contraceptive use, and had menarche onset at age 13 or older (Table 1). Furthermore, they reported higher consumption of avocado, broccoli, coffee, cranberries, fermented dairy, fiber, green tea, chickpeas, soybean, and whole grains. (Supplementary Table 5).

Associations between DI-GM and breast cancer risk

In the multivariable-adjusted model, each additional point of DI-GM score was linked to a 5% decrease in breast cancer risk (95% CI: 0.92–0.99). After adjusting for potential confounders, high DI-GM scores were associated with a reduced risk of breast cancer compared with low DI-GM (HR: 0.89, 95% CI: 0.81–0.97; Table 2). Additionally, RCS analysis showed that the DI-GM associated, in a linear dose–response manner, with breast cancer risk (P for overall = 0.027; P for non-linear = 0.434; Supplementary Fig. 1).

In subgroup analyses, higher DI-GM scores were significantly associated with reduced breast cancer risk among postmenopausal women, women with a history of oral contraceptive use, and those with menarche onset before 13 years of age (Supplementary Table 6). However, these stratification factors did not significantly modify the association between DI-GM and the risk of breast cancer (all P for interaction > 0.05). In the sensitivity analyses, the primary findings remained generally robust after excluding participants who developed cancer within the first two years, those with missing covariate data, and individuals who completed the 24-hour online dietary recall questionnaire only once. Additionally, reconstructing the DI-GM by excluding cranberries and chickpeas did not yield inconsistent results (Supplementary Tables 7–10).

Association of genetic risk with the risk of breast cancer

In the multivariable-adjusted model, per SD increment in PRS was associated with a 44% increased risk of breast cancer (95% CI: 1.39–1.49). Compared with participants with low PRS, those with moderate and high PRS had a significantly higher risk of breast cancer, with HRs (95% CIs) of 1.46 (1.32–1.61) and 2.19 (2.00-2.40), respectively (Supplementary Table 11). The RCS curves demonstrated a significant linear dose-response association between PRS and breast cancer risk (P for overall < 0.001; P for non-linear = 0.936; Supplementary Fig. 1).

The distribution of PRS was normal and the cumulative incidence was higher for participants with high PRS than those with low PRS, suggesting that the PRS could achieve efficient risk stratification for the population (Fig. 2).

Joint and interaction analysis of DI-GM and PRS with breast cancer risk

Joint analysis showed that participants with high DI-GM and low PRS had a significantly decreased risk of breast cancer compared with those in the low DI-GM and high PRS (HR: 0.35, 95% CI: 0.29–0.42; Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 12).

We observed a significant additive interaction between DI-GM and PRS in relation to breast cancer risk. The RERIs (95% CIs) for participants with a high DI-GM and low PRS were 0.163 (0.079–0.247) for risk of breast cancer, the AP (95% CIs) were 0.254 (0.118–0.389), compared with participants with low DI-GM and high PRS. However, no significant multiplicative interactions were found (P for multiplicative interaction = 0.109) (Supplementary Table 13). In further stratification analysis by PRS category, we observed that high DI-GM, compared with low DI-GM, was significantly associated with a reduced breast cancer risk in the low PRS category (HR: 0.79; 95% CI: 0.65–0.97) (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 14).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, higher DI-GM was significantly associated with a reduced risk of breast cancer, especially in individuals with a low genetic risk. In joint analyses, participants with both high DI-GM and low genetic risk had the lowest risk of breast cancer. These findings highlight that adherence to a higher DI-GM diet pattern may play a protective role in breast cancer prevention.

Emerging evidence suggests that gut microbiota may influence breast cancer development through multifaceted mechanisms. First, gut microbes modulate systemic immune responses by interacting with host immune cells, potentially altering antitumor immunity [26]. Specific bacterial taxa may promote regulatory T-cell activity or stimulate pro-inflammatory cytokines, affecting cancer surveillance [27]. Second, gut microbiota regulates estrogen metabolism via enzymatic deconjugation of estrogen metabolites, influencing circulating estrogen levels—a key risk factor for hormone receptor-positive breast cancer [28, 29]. Dysbiosis may disrupt this balance, enhancing estrogen-driven carcinogenesis [30]. Moreover, disruption of the intestinal mucosal barrier, influenced by the gut microbiota, further exacerbates inflammation and affects antigen trafficking, impacting the gut microbiome-immune system interaction [31]. This complex interplay between gut microbiota, immune function, and inflammation represents a crucial mechanism in breast cancer development. DI-GM reflects dietary patterns promoting a healthy gut microbiota, potentially mitigating breast cancer risk [32]. Fiber-rich, plant-based diets increase beneficial bacteria producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), known for their anti-inflammatory and anti-cancer properties [33, 34]. Conversely, diets high in processed foods and sugar promote detrimental bacteria, exacerbating inflammation and increasing breast cancer risk [35–37]. Understanding these mechanisms could inform dietary interventions targeting gut health for breast cancer prevention.

Expanding on these findings, our subgroup analysis indicated that the inverse association with breast cancer risk appeared more pronounced in post-menopausal women. This pattern aligns with menopause-specific alterations in estrogen metabolism and gut microbiota dynamics. In post-menopausal women, whose ovarian estrogen production has ceased, gut microbial β-glucuronidase critically influences the enterohepatic recirculation of estrogens [28, 29]. The beneficial components of DI-GM directly modulate this process, potentially rendering these women more responsive to the diet’s influence on this axis. For pre-menopausal women, the non-significant finding is likely attributable to both limited statistical power due to fewer incident cases and potentially higher endogenous ovarian estrogen production buffering microbiota-mediated influences. While the overall protective trend was consistently observed across both strata, further larger, dedicated pre-menopausal cohorts and detailed mechanistic studies are warranted to fully elucidate menopausal-stage-specific diet-microbiota-estrogen crosstalk.

Compared with previous research, our study examines the association between a dietary index specifically developed to reflect gut microbiota health and breast cancer risk. While previous studies have linked DI-GM to reduced risks of non-cancer outcomes such as depression [38] and diabetes [39], no prior work has explored its relationship with breast cancer. Prior research established that dietary patterns rich in plant-based foods reduce breast cancer incidence [16], which is consistent with our findings. Similarly, Newman et al. emphasize that adherence to the Mediterranean diet, characterized by high consumption of vegetables, fruits, and olive oil, is associated with lower breast cancer risk across all subtypes, while the Western diet, rich in animal fats and processed foods, is linked to increased risk [40]. This comparison highlights the potential of dietary patterns promoting gut microbiota health. Our findings extend current knowledge by demonstrating that higher DI-GM scores, indicative of diets promoting beneficial gut microbiota, are associated with a lower incidence of breast cancer. This aligns with broader evidence suggesting that dietary patterns rich in fiber, plant-based foods, and fermented products—key components of DI-GM—may modulate cancer risk through microbiota-mediated pathways. Our results further support the importance of considering the potential role of the gut microbiota as a mediator in the relationship between diet and breast cancer, thus suggesting fruitful avenues for targeted prevention strategies.

The DI-GM encompasses dietary factors known to foster a healthy gut microbiome, including fermented dairy, legumes (chickpeas, soybeans), whole grains, fiber, and polyphenol-rich foods (cranberries, coffee, green tea), while restricting foods linked to dysbiosis (red/processed meats, refined grains, high-fat diets). A high DI-GM pattern may contribute to maintaining gut microbiome diversity and promoting the growth of beneficial bacteria, which could potentially modulate breast cancer risk through systemic mechanisms. Soy isoflavones, for example, promote beneficial bacteria and possess phytoestrogenic properties influencing estrogen metabolism, relevant to breast cancer risk [41]. High-fiber foods, such as whole grains and vegetables, promote microbial diversity and SCFAs production, which may reduce systemic inflammation carcinogenesis [40, 42]. Furthermore, polyphenols in green tea, coffee, and cranberries may enhance beneficial bacteria while inhibiting pathogens, reducing oxidative stress, and modulating immunity [43, 44]. Fermented dairy probiotics can maintain intestinal barrier integrity and reduce systemic inflammation, crucial for breast cancer prevention [45]. These microbiome-mediated effects may explain the inverse association between higher DI-GM and breast cancer risk.

Numerous evidence indicated that gene-environment interaction contributes considerably to the etiology of human cancer [46–48]. Uniquely, our study investigated the interaction between DI-GM and genetic susceptibility to breast cancer and observed significant additive interactions between them, but no significant multiplicative interaction. This indicates that the protective effect of a gut microbiota-friendly diet, when combined with a lower PRS, yields a synergistically enhanced absolute risk reduction that surpasses the sum of their individual benefits. Consistent with prior research [16, 49], we confirmed a positive correlation between PRS elevation and increased breast cancer risk. Notably, while PRS remained the dominant risk factor as depicted, the protective effect of a high DI-GM diet was most pronounced in individuals with lower PRS, reflecting the most visible absolute risk reduction. This pattern suggests that while DI-GM offers a consistent protective effect across genetic risk strata, its absolute risk reduction is most visible and statistically detectable among individuals with lower genetic predisposition. At higher PRS levels, the observed benefits of dietary modification appear to be attenuated, suggesting that genetically-driven carcinogenesis may be less responsive to dietary changes. These observations underscore the potential impact of adopting a healthy diet in partially mitigating the heightened genetic predisposition to breast cancer, emphasizing the necessity of integrating both genetic susceptibility and dietary factors in strategies aimed at reducing cancer risk.

The strengths of the current study included its large sample size, long-term follow-up, prospective study design, and comprehensive consideration of covariates. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the relationship between DI-GM and breast cancer risk. Moreover, it examines the potential interaction and combined effects of DI-GM and genetic risk in association with breast cancer risk.

Despite these strengths, several limitations merit consideration. First, due to the observational nature, causal relationships could not be determined. Second, the DI-GM score was calculated based on self-reported dietary intake within the past 24 h—a method susceptible to recall bias. However, repeated measures were employed to reduce the impact of recall bias. Third, as in most observational studies, residual confounding from unknown or unmeasured factors cannot be fully excluded despite adjustments for major confounders. Fourth, the lack of tumor hormone receptor data precluded analyses stratified by estrogen receptor subtypes, which limits our ability to assess potential differential associations across breast cancer molecular subtypes. Finally, the study population predominantly consisted of individuals of white European ancestry, limiting the generalizability of our findings. Future research is essential to validate these findings in more diverse ethnic and demographic groups.

Conclusions

This large prospective cohort study demonstrated that a high DI-GM score was significantly associated with a decreased risk of breast cancer, especially in individuals with a low genetic risk. Individuals with a higher DI-GM score and lower genetic risk exhibited a reduced risk of breast cancer. These findings underscore the potential of personalized dietary interventions focused on DI-GM for breast cancer prevention.
