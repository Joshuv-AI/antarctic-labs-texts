Public support for regulating AI advice for mental health
Authors: Elizabeth M Stone, Rachel Topazian
Published: 2026

Introduction

Approximately 1 in 6 adults in the United States report using general-use generative artificial intelligence (AI) chatbots (eg, ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) for information related to mental health, including looking up symptoms and asking for advice on coping skills.1 There is evidence, however, that AI chatbots may respond inappropriately to prompts related to common mental health conditions—for example “colluding with delusions” and “reinforcing hallucinations.”2 AI chatbots have also been cited as direct or contributing factors in multiple suicides.3

Since 2025, more than 400 bills addressing health-related use of AI have been introduced across 49 states.4 Several states have considered and enacted policies specifically related to the use of AI in mental health care, primarily through limiting the use of AI for therapeutic purposes in clinical mental health settings (eg, Illinois House Bill 1806) or prohibiting AI systems from being represented as qualified mental health professionals (eg, Tennessee Senate Bill 1580).4 Other states have taken a broader approach. For example, in March 2026, Washington state passed House Bill 2225 regulating general-use AI chatbots (referred to as “companion chatbots”) with a specific provision that AI chatbots may not be deployed unless operators have publicly available protocols for identifying and responding to user input reflecting potential for self-harm.4

Given the evolving legislative landscape, we aimed to assess public support for a state policy regulating general-use AI chatbots’ mental health–related interactions among adults in New Jersey. To date, New Jersey has not adopted any policies on health-related use of AI, although a policy prohibiting companies from advertising AI systems as licensed mental health providers has been introduced in both 2025 and 2026.4

Methods

We fielded AI policy–related questions as part of the Rutgers-Eagleton/SSRS Garden State Panel Omnibus from March 27 to 30, 2026.5 The Garden State Panel Omnibus is a quarterly, probability-based survey; the address-based sample design covers 99% of adults aged 18 years and older living in New Jersey. Panelists are invited via email to complete the survey online in either English or Spanish and receive an electronic gift card for participation upon survey completion. The response rate was 28.8% (n = 1568).

We assessed the proportion of individuals supporting regulation of general-use AI chatbots in the context of mental health among the overall population and stratified by prior use of AI for mental health advice. Policy support was assessed using a single question measuring support for a state policy regulating how general-use generative AI chatbots (eg, ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) interact with people seeking mental health advice. We created a binary measure of policy support with responses of “strongly support” or “support” indicating support for the policy and responses of “strongly oppose” or “oppose” indicating opposition; neutral and unsure response options were not provided. The use of general-use AI chatbots for mental health advice was assessed using a binary measure asking respondents if they had ever (vs had never) sought advice or help from generative AI chatbots on topics related to mental health. Full item wording and response options are included in Appendix A. Differences in policy support by AI use were assessed using Wald tests with survey weights to represent the full New Jersey adult population (Appendix B).5 This study was deemed exempt by the Rutgers University Institutional Review Board and followed the American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) Best Practices for Survey Research.

Results

Most adults in New Jersey (59.5%) support a state policy regulating how general-use AI chatbots interact with users seeking mental health advice (Figure 1). A total of 13.5% of adults reported ever using an AI chatbot for mental health advice; 69.4% of adults who had used AI for mental health advice supported regulating general-use AI in the context of mental health compared to 57.9% of adults who had not used AI for mental health advice, an 11.5 percentage point difference (P = .0114).

Discussion

Most adults in New Jersey support a state policy regulating how general-use AI chatbots interact with people seeking mental health advice. We observed significant differences in support based on an individual's own experience using generative AI chatbots for mental health advice, with nearly three-fourths of people who had used AI for mental health advice supporting regulation.

These findings indicate high levels of public support overall for regulating commonly used general AI chatbots. The high level of support for regulation among people with experience using AI for mental health advice may, in part, reflect concerns related to their own interactions. In recent national polling, most adults who used AI chatbots for health advice were satisfied with and trusted the responses they received, but those ratings were slightly lower for mental health–related queries compared with physical health questions.1 Most people were also concerned about the privacy of personal information provided in these queries.1 As more people turn to AI chatbots for quick mental health advice, often without then following up with a medical provider, ensuring the quality and safety of these tools and their responses is essential for mitigating potential harms.1

Limitations of this study include sampling and response bias, although the Garden State Omnibus Panel follows AAPOR best practices by using a probability-based sampling approach. Support for regulation was measured generally based on a single survey item which respondents may have interpreted in different ways. While we present support stratified by any use of AI for mental health queries, other factors including age and mental health needs may influence both use of AI and support for regulation. Future research should investigate reasons for supporting regulatory actions and how support may differ for specific regulatory approaches (eg, requiring safety protocols or disclosures, enacting data privacy protections), While this study focuses on New Jersey, findings may be relevant for other states considering the best approaches for policymaking at the intersection of AI and mental health.
