Epigenetic modulation of the JAK2-STAT3 signaling pathway in osteoporosis: non-coding RNA networks as therapeutic targets
Authors: Pengjun Wang, Lu Xia, Xiaobin Shang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteoporosis, a prevalent metabolic bone disease affects over 200 million people worldwide and is associated with an elevated fracture risk, is characterized by an imbalance between bone resorption and formation. The JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway serves as a critical regulator of bone remodeling, but its chronic activation contributes to pathological bone metabolism in osteoporosis. Understanding the precise regulation of this pathway is essential for developing novel therapies.Main bodyOur review reveals that specific miRNAs directly target components of the JAK2–STAT3 pathway (e.g. JAK2, STAT3, SOCS) to fine-tune osteoblast and osteoclast activity. This regulatory network is further expanded by lncRNAs and circRNAs, which act as competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) or “molecular sponges” to sequester miRNAs, thereby indirectly modulating JAK2–STAT3 signaling. This multi-tiered ncRNA network influences key processes such as osteogenic differentiation, inflammatory response and mitochondrial redox homeostasis, ultimately determining bone metabolic balance.ConclusionThe ncRNA network represents a promising therapeutic target for bone-related and metabolic diseases, especially osteoporosis, through its precise control over the JAK2–STAT3 pathway. Targeting these ncRNAs, potentially via engineered exosomes or biomaterial-based delivery systems, offers a novel and different strategy for restoring bone homeostasis, paving the way for future precision medicine in bone metabolic diseases.

Introduction

Bone metabolism is a dynamic equilibrium critically maintained by the precise mutual regulation between osteoblasts (OBs) and osteoclasts (OCs) [1]. Disruption of this process underlies various bone diseases, particularly including osteoporosis (OP) characterized by reduced bone mineral density (BMD). These pathologies share a foundation of dysregulated intercellular metabolic crosstalk. Osteoblasts facilitate bone formation primarily through the synthesis of type I collagen (Col I) and alkaline phosphatase (ALP), both of which are essential for bone matrix formation. In contrast, osteoclasts dominate bone resorption via proton pumps (V-ATPase) and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP), which dissolve bone minerals. The RANKL/OPG and Wnt/β-catenin pathways represent central regulators of bone remodeling [2, 3]. Among these, the JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway serves as a critical node, influencing the RANKL/OPG axis through phosphorylation cascades, modulating mitochondrial redox homeostasis and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation in osteoclasts, and directing BMSC differentiation via gp130-mediated cytokines including IL-6 and IL-4 [4, 5]. Through these mechanisms, activation of JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway disrupts bone homeostasis, promoting either pathological resorption or aberrant formation.

Within this intricate regulatory network of bone metabolism, microRNAs (miRNAs)—small non-coding RNAs approximately 22 nucleotides in length—function as key epigenetic regulators. They bind through sequence-specific incomplete complementarity to the 3′ untranslated region (3′-UTR) of target mRNAs, mediating post-transcriptional gene silencing (PTGS) [6, 7]. Notably, certain miRNAs exhibit cell- and tissue-specific regulatory roles in various bone-related diseases by targeting different nodes of the JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway. For instance, miR-214 targets JAK2 and its upstream elements, while miR-21-5p and miR-155 act on STAT3 and its downstream translational machinery [8, 9]. Through translational repression of their targets, these miRNAs coordinate multiple signaling pathways, thereby orchestrating bone remodeling. Furthermore, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and circular RNAs (circRNAs) extend this multi-tiered regulatory network by functioning as molecular sponges that sequester miRNAs, thereby precisely regulating the JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway [10]. For instance, lncRNA MALAT1 can sequester miR-214, which may potentiate JAK2–STAT3 signaling and facilitate osteogenic repair in bone defects [11].

This review focuses on the role of regulatory RNAs in targeting specific nodes of the JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway and their mechanistic involvement in osteoporosis. While other less bone disorders are mentioned for comparative purposes to illustrate shared regulatory nodes within the JAK2–STAT3 pathway, the central discussion is anchored on osteoporosis with dysregulated bone metabolism. We also examine their functions in the fine-tuning of bone remodeling processes and discuss how these insights could lay a foundation for potential therapeutic interventions. This work aims to bridge molecular insights with future treatment strategies for osteoporosis, facilitating the translation of foundational research into clinically relevant applications.

The JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway

Physiological mechanism of the JAK2–STAT3 signaling in bone metabolism

The Janus kinase (JAK) family, a subset of protein tyrosine kinases (PTKs), comprises JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2. These kinases act upstream of signal transducers and activators of transcription (STATs), which include STAT1, STAT2, STAT3, STAT4, STAT5A, STAT5B, and STAT6 [12]. Upon phosphorylation, STAT proteins dimerize and translocate to the nucleus, binding specific promoter elements through their DNA-binding domains to form transcriptionally active complexes [13]. Activation of the JAK-STAT pathway is tightly controlled by negative regulators, including suppressors of cytokine signaling (SOCS), protein tyrosine phosphatases (PTPs) and protein inhibitors of activated STATs (PIAS). SOCS proteins inhibit JAK kinase activity by direct binding, while PIAS suppresses STAT-DNA binding [14]. In bone metabolism, STAT3 serves as a critical mechanotransduction regulator, directly fine-tuning osteoblast maintenance and osteoclast differentiation [15]. Phosphorylation of JAK2 (p-JAK2) facilitates STAT3 phosphorylation (p-STAT3) and nuclear translocation, establishing the JAK2–STAT3 signaling as a central cascade governing the balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts activity [16].

Activation of the JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating bone formation. Cytokines such as IL-6, IL-17A, LIF and IL-10 initiate signaling by binding to the gp130 receptor, leading to dimerization and activation of the JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway [17–19]. This results in phosphorylation of JAK2 at Tyr1007/1008 and subsequent phosphorylation of STAT3 at Tyr705. Phosphorylated STAT3 forms dimers, translocates to the nucleus, and regulates gene expression through multiple mechanisms [20]. STAT3 modulates bone metabolism through several functional axes. Within the immune inflammatory axis, STAT3 facilitates Th17 cell proliferation and differentiation, thereby enhancing osteoclastogenesis. It also establishes a positive feedback loop by increasing the secretion of IL-17 and TNF-α, which in turn further influence osteoblast activity. In contrast, regulatory T cells (Tregs) antagonize the function of Th17 cells [21]. In the osteogenic axis, P-STAT3 directly binds to the promoter of Dlx3, activating downstream DLX5 transcription and promoting osteoblast differentiation and anabolic signaling [15, 22]. Additionally, through epigenetic mechanisms, STAT3 phosphorylation inhibits CRY2 expression, thereby upregulating CLOCK/BMAL1 activity and recruiting p300 to catalyze histone H3 acetylation [23]. This open chromatin state facilitates the formation of a STAT3–RUNX2 transcriptional complex that drives the expression of osteocalcin (OCN) and osteopontin (OPN) (Fig. 1) [24]. STAT3 also enhances osteogenic function by upregulating alkaline phosphatase (ALP) synthesis [15]. Notably, STAT3 represents a double-edged sword in bone homeostasis. Under physiological or acute activation, STAT3 promotes osteogenic differentiation. In contrast, dysregulated or chronic activation may induce osteoblasts to produce and release inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, IL-17, and TNF-α, into the extracellular matrix. This process creates a pro-inflammatory microenvironment. These cytokines further stimulate osteoblasts to overexpress RANKL. Under normal conditions, osteoprotegerin (OPG) binds to RANKL, preventing its interaction with RANK on OCs and thereby suppressing osteoclast activity [26, 27]. However, when the balance is disrupted, enhanced RANKL production coupled with STAT3-mediated suppression of OPG can alter the RANKL/OPG ratio. The resulting excessive RANKL-RANK interaction may accelerate osteoclast activation and bone resorption.

Furthermore, the JAK2–STAT3 pathway further promotes bone resorption by disrupting mitochondrial redox homeostasis in osteoclasts. Redox balance involves a dynamic equilibrium between reactive oxygen species (ROS) and antioxidants such as glutathione, which permits ROS-mediated signaling while preventing cytotoxic accumulation [28]. In osteoclasts, inflammatory cytokines including IL-6 and IL-17 activate this pathway by binding to the GP130 receptor, which leads to JAK2 phosphorylation promotes STAT3 activation, and a portion of p-STAT3 dimers translocate to mitochondria, where they impair electron transport chain (ETC) function and promote mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) accumulation [29, 30]. Elevated mtROS can enhance NF-κB signaling and synergize with RANKL to activate OCs [31, 32]. Additionally, mitochondrial-localized p-STAT3 may facilitate caspase-3 activation, further amplifying ROS generation through a positive feedback loop [33]. Meanwhile, nuclear-translocated p-STAT3 transcriptionally upregulates anti-apoptotic genes including Bcl-2, which can suppress osteoclast apoptosis and enhance proton pump activity to acidify the resorption microenvironment [34]. Furthermore, STAT3 activation stimulates secretion of IL-6 and IL-4 into the bone milieu, which may indirectly inhibit the osteoblastic JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway (Fig. 2). Notably, JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway exerts biphasic regulation on OCs: its effects depend on the activation context and cellular state [35]. In summary, the JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway acts as a master upstream regulator that orchestrates the balance between bone formation and resorption. It promotes osteogenesis in osteoblasts by stimulating the secretion of OPN and OCN, while in osteoclasts, it modulates bone resorption by regulating apoptosis and redox homeostasis. Moreover, this pathway directly or indirectly modulates the core RANK/RANKL/OPG axis, which facilitates osteoblast-osteoclast crosstalk, thereby ensuring precise coordination of the balance between bone formation and resorption.

Pathological role of JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway in osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is a systemic skeletal disorder driven by disrupted bone metabolic homeostasis [36]. This condition features an imbalance between osteoblast and osteoclast activity resulting in progressive bone loss, deterioration of bone microarchitecture, reduced bone mineral density and elevated fracture risk. The mechanism primarily stems from impaired osteoblastic function and pathologically enhanced osteoclastic activity, accompanied by a decline in the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs, which further exacerbates deficits in bone formation.

Osteoporosis progression involves distinct pathological trajectories in osteoblasts and osteoclasts. Early osteoblasts demonstrate cellular atrophy, elevated nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio and Golgi apparatus degeneration. These structural abnormalities impair their capacity to synthesize bone matrix proteins including OPN and ALP, leading to compromised bone formation. Advanced disease reveals mitochondrial swelling and early apoptotic features. Conversely, osteoclasts become hypertrophic and multinucleated, with increased lysosomal content and expanded ruffled borders enriched in proton pumps. These specialized resorptive structures further enlarge during disease progression, enhancing proton pump expression and secreting hydrogen ions to exacerbate pathological resorption [37]. Physiologically, JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway helps maintain the balance of the RANKL/OPG system [38, 39]. In osteoporosis, however, diminished OPG and elevated osteoblast-derived RANKL promote osteoclast activation through RANK binding. Notably, in advanced osteoporosis, RANKL further stimulates the secretion of cytokines. These cytokines may activate the JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway within osteoclasts, establishing a positive feedback loop that sustains osteoclast activation [40]. JAK2–STAT3 signaling also contributes to other skeletal pathologies characterized by dysregulated bone metabolism beyond osteoporosis, such as osteosarcoma (OS) representing osteogenic imbalance and bone defects influencing bone resorption [41–43]. Collectively, these findings underscore the context-dependent duality of the JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway, emphasizing the necessity for cell- or stage-specific therapeutic targeting. Non-coding RNA networks represent promising epigenetic tools for treating bone metabolic diseases (osteoporosis) through extensive modulation of this pathway. The subsequent sections detail how these regulatory RNAs molecularly control JAK2–STAT3 signaling.

Regulation of miRNAs targeting JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway

miRNAs are endogenous non-coding RNAs approximately 22 nucleotides in length that post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression by guiding the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) to complementary sites in the 3′ untranslated region (3′-UTR) of target mRNAs, resulting in mRNA degradation or translational repression [44]. During their biogenesis, miRNA precursors (pri-miRNAs) are transcribed by RNA polymerase II/III and processed by the Drosha/DGCR8 complex into pre-miRNAs in the nucleus [45]. After export to the cytoplasm via Exportin-5, pre-miRNAs are cleaved by Dicer into mature miRNA duplexes. One strand is subsequently incorporated into RISC to guide target silencing [46]. The spatiotemporal specificity of miRNAs enables precise regulation of diverse physiological and pathological contexts.

For example, miR-224-5p suppresses JAK2–STAT3 phosphorylation and inhibits proliferation in lung cancer by targeting IL6ST [47], whereas miR-141-3p activates this pathway to drive aberrant growth in uterine smooth muscle cells [48]. Within bone metabolism, miRNAs critically regulate osteoblast-osteoclast communication and bone remodeling through JAK2–STAT3 targeting. Specific miRNAs modulate bone cell activity, differentiation, and apoptosis by targeting pathway components: miR-362-3p inhibits phosphorylation of JAK2 and STAT3, thereby suppressing cell growth and migration and promoting osteoclast apoptosis [49]; miR-468-3p directly targets Runx2 to impair osteogenesis; and miR-5134-5p influences osteoclast differentiation via JAK2 targeting [50, 51]. Furthermore, miR-125b-5p exerts dual effects on both osteoblasts and osteoclasts, further underscoring the pivotal role of miRNAs in the JAK2–STAT3 pathway within bone homeostasis [52]. Based on their targeting sites within the JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway (Fig. 4), these miRNAs can be categorized into two groups for discussion: those targeting upstream effectors and those targeting downstream effectors. miRNAs targeting upstream components and JAK2

Regarding miRNAs that directly target the core kinase of the JAK2–STAT3 pathway, miR-211-5p, a pro-osteogenic regulator, targets JAK2 to suppress JAK2–STAT3 signaling to reduce serum phosphorus and ALP activity while enhancing calcium deposition in OVX rats [54]. However, the osteogenic function of miR-211-5p exhibits context-dependent variability, its downregulation in osteoarthritic sclerotic bone further impairs mineralization under inflammatory conditions [55]. MiR-135b similarly binds the JAK2 3’-UTR, attenuating JAK2 and STAT3 phosphorylation to suppress ALP activity and osteogenic markers including OCN, BSP, and OSX, thereby inhibiting osteoblast proliferation and promoting apoptosis [56, 57]. Beyond classical inflammatory cytokines and epigenetic signals, osteoblast proliferation and differentiation are modulated by intercellular crosstalk through osteoclast-derived exosomes [58]. Osteoclast-derived exosomal miR-5134-5p, delivered into osteoblasts via Annexin A2–receptorrecognition, directly targets the JAK2 3′-UTRto suppress downstream Runx2, impairing osteoblastic differentiation in vitro and bone formation in vivo [51]. However, all three JAK2-directed miRNAs have been validated exclusively in murine cell lines (MC3T3–E1 or RAW 264.7) and OVX rat models. Whether their 3’-UTR engagement kinetics are recapitulated in primary human osteoblasts under osteoporotic cytokine milieus, where competing endogenous RNA networks differ substantially, remains untested.

MiR-151a-3p further illustrates the complex, context-dependent dual regulatory mode of JAK2–STAT3 signaling. It directly binds the 3′-UTR of SOCS5 mRNA and downregulates its expression, which would be predicted to activate JAK2‑STAT3 signaling. Consistently, functional studies demonstrate that miR‑151a‑3p overexpression upregulates ALP activity and the RANKL/OPG ratio in vitro, yet it concurrently suppresses Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) and bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2) expression, reduces femoral bone mineral density (BMD) and ALP levels in vivo, and promotes postmenopausal osteoporosis [59]. These effects are associated with mechanisms linked to the vitamin D receptor (VDR) pathway and the formation of a pro-inflammatory microenvironment. Conversely, miR-21b targets SOCS1 to relieve JAK2 inhibition, activating the pathway to increase alveolar bone density in periodontitis, lacking its generalisability to systemic osteoporosis [60]. In vivo, miR-125b targets the 3′-UTR of TNF receptor-associated factor 6(TRAF6) mRNA—a component of the pathway—reducing its expression and BMD in ovariectomized rat models [52]. As TRAF6 itself antagonizes JAK2–STAT3 signaling via STAT3 interaction, its suppression by miR-125b directly activates JAK2–STAT3 signaling [61]. It also interferes with vitamin D receptor-mediated calcium homeostasis. Collectively, this convergence of different signals across pathway compartments positions miRNAs as central amplifiers of osteoporotic signaling.

miRNAs targeting downstream components and STAT3

As the terminal signaling effector of the pathway, STAT3 is essential for bone anabolism, promoting osteoblast viability, ALP activity and bone formation. Its pivotal role makes it a rational therapeutic target in bone metabolic disorders [15]. Exemplifying the pathological targeting of STAT3, miR-17-5p is markedly upregulated in the bone tissue and serum of postmenopausal osteoporosis patients, as well as in osteoporosis mouse models. It directly targets STAT3 mRNA, suppressing downstream osteogenic genes such as OCN, OPN and Runx2, thereby inhibiting osteoblast precursor differentiation and leads to progressive BMD loss. Notably, the overexpression of miR-17-5p also inhibits RUNX2 expression during vascular calcification, thereby reducing calcium deposition and osteogenic differentiation in vascular smooth muscle cells, and suppresses osteoclastgenesis in rheumatoid arthritis [62, 63]. Similarly, miR-29b directly binds the 3′-UTR of STAT3 mRNA, thereby attenuating STAT3 expression. Notably, STAT3 transcriptionally activates SOCS1, a negative regulator of the NLRP3 inflammasome, miR-29b-mediated STAT3 downregulation reduces SOCS1 levels, thereby disinhibiting NLRP3-driven pyroptosis. [64, 65]. Whether miR‑29b, similarly to miR‑17‑5p, can simultaneously suppress vascular calcification and bone formation, particularly at sites of bone metabolic imbalance, remains to be determined. The resulting bone‑suppressive effects might far exceed those achieved by simply silencing a single miRNA node. Furthermore, miR-124-3p.1 binds to STAT3 mRNA, downregulates STAT3 expression, inhibits bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) osteogenic differentiation and hinders bone regeneration in rat models [66].

Beyond direct targeting, STAT3 activity is further fine-tuned by multiple regulatory factors. MiR-224 silences RAC1, a physical STAT3 interactor that promotes STAT3 activation, via its conserved 3’-UTR, thereby deactivating both JAK2–STAT3 and Wnt/β-catenin signaling to shift the balance toward bone formation, with derepressed transcription of osteogenic genes [67, 68]. Conversely, miR-148a-3p directly targets p300, the histone acetyltransferase that facilitates STAT3 acetylation and transcriptional activity, suppressing osteogenic mineralization in MC3T3–E1 cells and deteriorating bone microarchitecture in OVX mice [69, 70]. However,miR-7-5p, which directly targets and suppresses CRY2, drives P300 transcription and forms a transcriptional complex with Runx2 to promote bone formation. Notably, miR-9718, highly enriched in OCs within bone tissue, acts as a key positive regulator of osteoclast differentiation. During primary osteoclast differentiation, it targets a pseudo site within the coding sequence (CDS) of PIAS3 mRNA, suppressing PIAS3 expression, alleviating PIAS3-mediated inhibition of downstream STAT3 to enhance osteoclastogenic factor secretion, and promoting the development of RAW 264.7 cells into multinucleated OCs [71].

The interplay between inflammation and bone metabolism underscores the complexity of STAT3 regulation, with miR-21 emerging as a critical node coordinating this crosstalk through multi-tiered mechanisms. In the context of ankylosing spondylitis (AS), suboptimal concentrations of TNF-α with 0.1 ng/mL activate STAT3 signaling in fibroblasts, which possibly in turn promotes the processing of mature miR-21 from its primary transcript. Conversely, inhibition of miR‑21 by anti‑miR‑21 oligonucleotides leads to diminished STAT3 phosphorylation and attenuated expression of osteogenic markers, suggesting that mature miR‑21 feeds back to sustain or enhance STAT3 activation in this inflammatory context, as evidenced by enhanced ALP activity and upregulation of Runx2, BMP2, and OPN [72]. These cytokines activate STAT3 via the gp130 receptor, perpetuating the loop. MiR‑21 inhibition upregulates PIAS3, reduces phosphorylated STAT3 levels, and ultimately suppresses RANKL expression—thereby restoring the RANKL/OPG balance impaired by myeloma cell contact from multiple myeloma patients [73]. Proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses demonstrate that miR‑21 inhibition leads to STAT3 impairment together with upregulation of STAT‑1 and STAT‑5a/b, resulting in redirection of Th17 cells toward Th1/Th2-like phenotypes and reduced IL‑17 secretion, possibly impairing BMSC proliferation and osteogenic differentiation [74]. And miR-3074-5p directly targets the 3′-UTR of Smad4, a key transcriptional co-activator downstream of multiple osteogenic signaling pathways, concurrently attenuates ERK, AKT, and STAT3 phosphorylation, thereby promoting osteoblast apoptosis. Collectively, these multi-level mechanisms drive pathological bone resorption through inflammatory amplification and intracellular signaling hyperactivation. Beyond local bone disorders, this paradigm extends to cancer metastasis. For instance, miR-182 promotes IL-8 secretion in non-small cell lung cancer cells, which activates STAT3 signaling in osteoclast precursor cells, leading to excessive osteoclast activation and potentially facilitating osteolytic metastasis [75].

A major point of downstream convergence is Runx2, the master osteogenic transcription factor. MiRNAs, including miR-468-3p, miR-340-5p, miR-23b-3p, miR-205, miR-224-5p, miR-205-5p, miR-590-3p, miR-203-3p, miR-338-3p, and miR-122, are each individually validated by dual-luciferase reporter assay to target Runx2 and suppress OCN, ALP, type I collagen synthesis, or BMSC mineralization [50, 76–83]. Notably, miR-205, which we discuss separately because its luciferase-validated direct target is SATB2 rather than Runx2, suppresses Runx2 expression indirectly through SATB2-mediated transcriptional regulation [84]. These findings support a multidimensional regulatory model via direct JAK2–STAT3 targeting (Table 2). Some miRNAs directly target core components of the JAK2–STAT3 pathway in disorders like osteoporosis, while others may exert indirect effects by modulating inflammation-bone metabolic crosstalk, thereby influencing osteoblast-osteoclast communication, such as miR-653-5P and miR-30b-5p change the inflammatory cytokines microenvironment in bone joints which serve as the pathway’s incentives [87, 88]. However, the precise contribution of the inflammatory microenvironment to osteoporosis pathogenesis remains to be fully elucidated. The non-coding RNA regulatory network

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a class of non-coding RNAs exceeding 200 nucleotides in length. Like miRNAs, they often exhibit canonical cap and tail structures but do not encode proteins, instead regulating gene expression through various mechanisms. Similarly, circular RNAs (circRNAs) are covalently closed RNA molecules formed through back-splicing of pre-mRNA, lacking terminal cap structures [89]. The lncRNA-miRNA-JAK2–STAT3 axis

lncRNAs are a class of RNA molecules that do not encode proteins. Previous studies have demonstrated that numerous lncRNAs are associated with the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and play important roles in regulating bone metabolism [91]. As a representative example, lncRNA LINC00968 exhibits tissue-specific biphasic effects in bone metabolism: in dental pulp stem cells (DPSCs), it promotes mineralized bone matrix formation, enhances ALP activity, upregulates osteogenic markers, and demonstrates potential to facilitate osteogenic differentiation in vivo, suggesting its promise as a target for dental implant modification [86]. Conversely, in osteoporotic bone matrix, its inhibition suppresses bone formation markers and promotes BMSC apoptosis, resembling the pathological effect of miR-17-5p which negatively regulates Runx2 and downstream osteogenic factors by targeting STAT3 [63, 92]. Mechanistic investigations, including dual-luciferase reporter and RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays, confirm that miR-17-5p is directly targeted and negatively regulated by LINC00968 in a ceRNA-dependent manner. Thus, at least in part, LINC00968 enhances osteoblast differentiation and promotes a shift toward bone formation in osteoporosis by suppressing miR-17-5p. Similarly, lncRNA RP11-84C13.1, downregulated in clinical serum from 38 osteoporosis patients, acts as a ceRNA by sponging miR-23b-3p to relieve repression of RUNX2 in hBMSCs and to promote osteogenic differentiation, as evidenced by clinical correlation, cellular gain-and-loss-of-function studies, and dual-luciferase reporter assays; however, in vivo validation in preclinical osteoporosis models is lacking [77].

In contrast, the lncRNA BRE-AS1 modulates the inflammatory properties of macrophages through a JAK2–STAT3 phosphorylation-dependent, transcription-independent mechanism [88]. Based on bioinformatic prediction and correlative expression analyses, BRE-AS1/miR-30b-5p/SOCS3 axis operates in concert with other inflammatory signaling pathways, including NF-κB and the NLRP3 inflammasome, as part of a broader network involving multiple lncRNAs and miRNAs. Notably, BRE-AS1-mediated immunomodulation in macrophages may indirectly influence bone metabolic processes in the context of inflammatory bone disorders, with a potent osteoclastogenic stimulus(IL-6), although direct evidence in bone cells or osteoporosis models is currently absent. [93, 94].

The circRNA-miRNA-JAK2–STAT3 axis

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are a class of covalently closed, stable RNA molecules that do not encode proteins but regulate cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, and communication. For example, bioinformatic and in vitro HEK-293T cell studies identify that circ_0134120 could promote the expression of STAT3 mRNA. However, this effect is partially reversed by miR-590-5p, whose overexpression significantly counteracts the direct upregulation of STAT3 induced by circ_0134120, strongly supported by dual-luciferase reporter and RNA pull-down assays [95]. It is proposed that circ_0134120 modulates the STAT3–miR-590-5p signaling to inhibit osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs in postmenopausal osteoporosis, thereby suppressing bone formation. However, the functional consequence of this interaction within directly related bone cells and its direct role in disease pathogenesis require further validation.

Beyond regulating STAT3 directly, Runx2, a master transcription factor governing osteogenic differentiation, is finely regulated by circRNAs during BMSC differentiation. Clinical correlation with 37 osteoporosis patients and cellular functional studies show that circ_0027885 is significantly upregulated during osteogenesis, and its overexpression enhances osteogenic differentiation and Runx2 expression, while its knockdown attenuates these effects [81]. Supported by dual-luciferase reporter assays, direct binding of circ_0027885 to miR-203-3p indicates circ_0027885 is proposed to act as a sponge for miR-203-3p, which itself targets Runx2, thereby contributing to mitigate osteoporotic progression in osteogenic cellular models, lacking in preclinical osteoporosis models. Similarly, circ_3626, identified as a pro-osteogenic circRNA through high-throughput sequencing and validated by dual-luciferase reporter, FISH, and RNA pull-down assays, upregulates Runx2 by directly sequestering miR-338-3p. Notably, AAV-mediated circ_3626 overexpression significantly improved bone repair in an aging mouse model, positioning this axis among the most rigorously validated ceRNA circuits in the osteoporosis circRNA literature [82].

Additionally, circ_0011269, downregulated in osteoporosis patients (n = 21) relative to controls (n = 20), adsorbs miR-122 to relieve Runx2 suppression, as confirmed by dual-luciferase reporter and RIP assays; however, further validation in preclinical bone loss models is required [83]. In jawbone-derived human BMSCs, circ_0054633 promotes osteogenesis through the miR-590-3p/RUNX2 axis, with enhanced ALP activity, increased ARS staining and calcium deposition, confirming its pro-osteogenic function in osteoporosis [80]. The experimental evidence including dual-luciferase reporter, RNA pull-down and microscale thermophoresis assays represents a well-characterized ceRNA network, and further corroborated axis in vivo shows elevated bone formation in cell implantation models.

The integrated ncRNA regulatory network

At the core of this regulatory network, miRNAs function as primary effectors by directly targeting the mRNAs of key components within the JAK2–STAT3 pathway including upstream receptors like gp130 and negative regulators like SOCS. The activity of these miRNAs is further modulated by a peripheral layer of lncRNAs and circRNAs, which may act as high-capacity molecular sponges, sequestering miRNAs and fine-tuning their availability [97]. This ncRNA network produces functional outputs that are not static but are dynamically rewired under different pathological conditions, creating a context-dependent regulatory circuit [83, 98].

The ncRNA regulatory network exhibits a distinct core–periphery architecture. For instance, lncRNA LINC00968 is proposed to promote osteogenic differentiation through a mechanism that involves sequestering miR-17-5p, thereby relieving STAT3 repression [86]. Conversely, STAT3 transcriptionally activates miRNAs that target other ncRNAs, establishing reciprocal feedback loops. This hierarchical organization suggests that individual lncRNAs or circRNAs may function as molecular buffers that simultaneously modulate multiple miRNAs, which converge on key osteogenic effectors like RUNX2. Through such proposed coordinated actions, the JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway can be amplified or attenuated in a synchronized manner [81, 82]. This network plasticity highlights how identical signaling pathways may be directed toward divergent pathological outcomes through dynamic molecular reconfiguration.

Furthermore, the ncRNA regulatory framework functions as a critical integrative hub that mediates crosstalk among key signaling pathways in bone biology. For instance, some miRNAs, lncRNAs and circRNAs that target JAK2–STAT3 signaling can also directly regulate elements of the Wnt/β-catenin and RANKL pathways, thereby forming an interconnected regulatory network [97, 99]. Within this multi-tiered architecture, ncRNAs do not regulate JAK2–STAT3 in isolation but embed it within a broader molecular context, ensuring coordinated crosstalk among the various programs governing bone remodeling.

Discussion and future perspective

This review synthesizes the regulatory networks of miRNAs targeting the JAK2–STAT3 signaling pathway in bone metabolic diseases [5, 100]. While this pathway is a well-established therapeutic target in oncology and neurodegenerative disorders, with several inhibitors advancing to clinical trials, its potential for treating osteoporosis remains largely untapped [101, 102]. A defining feature in bone biology is its spatiotemporal duality: it promotes bone repair during early phases but can exacerbate inflammatory damage in chronic stages. Under physiological conditions, cytokines including IL-6, IL-17A, and TNF-α activate JAK2–STAT3 signaling to maintain bone homeostasis by balancing osteoblast and osteoclast activity [103]. Conversely, chronic inflammatory conditions, particularly the RANKL/RANK system, disrupt the expression of key bone regulators such as OPN and ALP, accelerating pathologies like osteoporosis. This paradox necessitates for therapeutic strategies that selectively modulate beneficial versus detrimental effects.

Multi-layered ncRNA networks in bone microenvironment regulation

The hierarchical RNA regulatory network (miRNA-lncRNA-circRNA) contributes to the maintenance of bone metabolic balance through cell-type-specific and layer-specific regulation: For instance, in the immune dimension, miR-124 inhibits Th17 activity and promotes Treg proliferation, partly through modulating the JAK2–STAT3 pathway in macrophages, thereby fine-tuning immune responses [21]. In inflammatory contexts, miR-30b-5p and miR-130a target SOCS3 and STAT3, both disinhibiting the JAK2–STAT3 signaling in macrophages or chondrocytes, thereby altering the inflammatory landscape [88, 104]. Epigenetically, lncRNAs and circRNAs may act as molecular sponges for miRNA, relieving its repression of mRNA and modulating osteogenic activity [86, 95]. Collectively, this multi-tiered ncRNA network orchestrates bone microenvironmental dynamics by integrating inflammatory signals with anabolic transcriptional programs.

Clinical limitations of conventional JAK/STAT inhibitors

The very complexity and dual nature of the JAK2–STAT3 pathway that makes it a compelling target also poses a fundamental challenge for conventional pharmacological inhibition. Current clinical agents, such as JAK or STAT inhibitors tofacitinib and upadacitinib, are often limited by systemic toxicity and lack of tissue specificity. Emerging clinical evidence in bone diseases underscores these limitations: while JAK2-selective inhibitors like fedratinib can improve bone mineral density, pharmacovigilance data suggest an elevated fracture risk in patients with bone disorders [105, 106]. Compared to TNF-α inhibitors, both tofacitinib and upadacitinib showed increased fracture reporting odds ratios (RORs) of 1.82 and 2.23 [107]. These findings, alongside other serious systemic side effects, including thromboembolism, malignancy, and infection, highlight the perils of non-selective pathway blockade and underscore the need for smarter, context-aware therapeutic modalities [108].

Translational challenges

Given the limitations of broad-pathway suppression, more nuanced therapeutic strategies are needed. Unlike single-target inhibitors, miRNAs may orchestrate coordinated effects on multiple nodes, potentially restoring homeostatic balance rather than merely blocking signals, supporting the investigation as theragnostic candidates for bone metabolic diseases through JAK2–STAT3 pathway [109]. Promising delivery systems for miRNA include mesenchymal stem cells engineered with miR-29b targeting senescent osteoblasts, and functionalized biomaterials such as miR‑211-5p loaded hydrogels, bioceramics and metal implants for defect regeneration [64, 110–112]. Among these, exosome/extracellular vesicle-based platforms offer inherent tropism, low immunogenicity and enhanced biocompatibility, representing a potential candidate system for translating JAK2–STAT3 pathway modulation into clinical therapy [113–115]. However, development of this platform still faces a well-defined set of translational hurdles under current scarcity of clinical evidence [116].

Manufacturing and quality control hurdles

The transition from laboratory-scale isolation to clinically viable production is a primary bottleneck. While exosomes can be isolated from various cells, scaling up production to achieve the high yields, purity, and potency required for human trials remains a major hurdle [117]. Batch-to-batch heterogeneity, stemming from variations in cellular source, culture conditions and isolation methods, directly impacts product consistency, reproducibility and dosing accuracy [118, 119]. The complex composition of exosomes also obstructs precise characterization of active ingredients and complicates the establishment of critical quality attributes, facing challenges of ensuring long-term stability of miRNA cargo. Hence, establishing standardized protocols for exosome production and characterization is a critical need for regulatory approval.

Pharmacokinetics and targeting barriers

Despite their innate tropism, natural exosomes often lack sufficient tissue specificity for therapeutic applications. After systemic administration, they are rapidly cleared and can accumulate in off-target organs like the liver and lungs, limiting targeted delivery to bone and raising concerns about potential immunogenicity and off-target toxicity [120]. Additionally, the translation of efficacy from in vitro models to in vivo environments remains a significant gap. To overcome this limitation, promising approaches for localized delivery strategies including intra-articular, intramuscular and subperiosteal injections elevate site-specific accumulation [121]. There are also other engineered modifying applications in systemically administered exosomes, like efficacy of exosomes loaded with miR-29b in osteoporotic mice after intravenous injection which exerts improved specificity [122]; for instance, TBP-CP05 peptide-modified exosomes derived from red blood cells effectively target osteoclasts and regulate bone metabolism [123]. However, those numerous studies in small animal models fail to predict long-term safety profiles and human pharmacokinetics, underscoring the need for robust validation in large and relevant animal models to de-risk clinical translation [124–126].

Therapeutic potential for miRNAs

To overcome translational hurdles in miRNA-based therapy, engineered delivery strategies have emerged as pivotal enabling technologies. Kim and colleagues employed microfluidics to generate exosome-mimetic nanoparticles with approximately 21-fold improved delivery efficiency compared with conventional production methods, while Ye et al. developed preconditioning protocols to tailor MSC-derived exosome cargo within simulated clinical microenvironments for precise tissue targeting [127, 128]. The clinical trajectory of miRNA therapeutics, however, has been shaped as much by its setbacks as by its successes. Although pharmacodynamic proof-of-concept was achieved, the first-in-class miRNA mimic(NCT01829971) between 2013 and 2016 was terminated early after four patient deaths attributable to severe immune-mediated adverse events. Outcomes collectively underscore that the principal barrier to clinical miRNA therapy is not insufficient target engagement, but rather uncontrolled immunogenicity, informing the design of next-generation delivery platforms. A more favourable safety profile was subsequently demonstrated by LNA-i-miR-221(NCT04811898), a locked nucleic acid-modified anti-miR-221 construct that completed a Phase I dose-escalation trial in patients with refractory multiple myeloma. Critically, these clinical principles are now driving the development of EV-based platforms with direct relevance to bone metabolism. The HLA-G-targeting engineered exosome platform SOB100, which delivers nucleic acid payloads via HLA-G-specific nanobodies, received U.S. FDA IND approval in March 2025 and has advanced to first-in-human studies. The AI-assisted BesKne-Exo platform has achieved GMP-compliant, high-yield production of exosomes that promote cartilage regeneration at sub-microgram doses, with efficacy validated across multiple preclinical models [129, 130]. Among the miRNA species catalogued in this review, several exhibit particularly compelling translational potential in bone models: miR-21-5p could been functionalised within DNA tetrahedron nanostructures to promote osteogenic repair of senescent bone defects, and hypoxic adipose stem cell-derived exosomes enriched in miR-21-5p embedded in GelMA hydrogel enhance type H angiogenesis and accelerate osteoporotic fracture healing in OVX mice; miR-17-5p reduces inflammation and bone erosions in mice with collagen-induced arthritis following intra-articular delivery [63, 112, 131]. For these miRNAs, the convergence of mechanistic validation with novel tractable delivery platforms positions them as clinically leading candidates for the next phase of translational development in JAK2–STAT3-targeted bone anabolic therapy.

Diagnostic potential of miRNAs

Beyond their therapeutic applications, miRNAs serve as non-invasive reporters of systemic bone metabolic status by virtue of their stability in circulation and their mechanistic proximity to the regulatory networks governing bone remodelling [132–134]. In primary osteoporosis, plasma levels of hsa-miR-21-5p decrease with progressive bone loss, while hsa-miR-133a-3p increases, demonstrating diagnostic specificity for bone mass status. Exosomal miRNA signatures further extend diagnostic resolution to complex bone disorders: in Gaucher disease, plasma exosomal hsa-miR-127-3p, hsa-miR-184, and hsa-miR-660-5p are strongly associated with bone complication severity; in osteoarthritis, elevated serum miR-151a-3p achieves an AUC of 0.921 and correlates with JAK2–STAT3 pathway activation markers [135–137]. Despite these encouraging single-centre findings, the clinical translation of circulating miRNA diagnostics for metabolic bone disease has been hampered by the absence of large-scale, prospective validation cohorts. The recently launched ROMIRNA study(NCT07616401), a prospective observational trial initiated in August 2025 with an estimated enrolment period extending to 2029, contributes to this gap. ROMIRNA is designed to profile circulating miRNA expression at baseline and at serial time points (2, 6, and 12 months) in postmenopausal women with severe osteoporosis receiving either romosozumab or abaloparatide, with the primary endpoint being the identification of early miRNA changes predictive of BMD response. This trial represents the first systematic effort to link dynamic miRNA signatures with anabolic treatment outcomes in osteoporosis and, if successful, will establish a framework for miRNA-guided therapeutic stratification. In the paediatric domain, a prospective clinical study(NCT06368154) is examining plasma exosomal miRNAs as diagnostic biomarkers for metabolic bone disease in preterm infants, with completion anticipated in 2026 [138–141]. Beyond individual diagnostic associations, the pathway architecture of the JAK2–STAT3 regulatory network suggests that certain miRNAs may serve as disproportionately informative sentinels of pathological bone metabolism. miR-125b simultaneously engages TRAF6 and the vitamin D receptor with uniform directionality favouring bone resorption, thereby functioning as a convergent amplifier of osteoporotic signalling [52, 142]. Similarly, miR-17-5p spans two hierarchical levels of the pathway by targeting both STAT3 and its downstream effector Runx2 [63]. At the systems level, ten miRNAs catalogued in this review converge on Runx2 alone, forming a redundant repressive module whose aggregate activity may determine the net suppression of bone formation. These multi-node, directionally coherent miRNAs represent rational candidates for prioritisation in future miRNA panel-based diagnostic development, as their expression changes are mechanistically predicted to reflect pathologicalbone metabolic status. Collectively, the integration of prospective biomarker discovery and mechanism-related miRNAs diagnostic programmes possibly establishes a “theranostic” paradigm: miRNA signatures derived from these cohorts may guide patient stratification for the deployment of engineered exosome-based therapies.

Conclusion

In summary, this review synthesizes the pivotal role of inflammation-activated JAK2–STAT3 signaling in osteoblast-osteoclast coupling and explores the therapeutic potential of miRNAs targeting this pathway. We systematically analyze how miRNA networks regulate key nodes in JAK2–STAT3 signaling, proposing its novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic function, primarily for osteoporosis [143]. Emerging delivery platforms, particularly engineered exosomes, represent candidate strategies to harness these miRNAs. However, translating these insights into clinical applications requires: (1) a systems-level identification of hub miRNAs within the multi-tiered RNA interactome and their synergistic combinations using high-throughput screening and AI; (2) optimization of scalable, targeted delivery systems, with a focus on engineered exosomes capable of bone-specific targeting; and (3) rigorous validation of efficacy and safety in preclinical large-animal studies. Addressing these priorities will be necessary to advance toward precision therapies for bone disorders.
