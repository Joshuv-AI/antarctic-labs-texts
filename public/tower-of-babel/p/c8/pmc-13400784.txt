A rare case of uterine carcinosarcoma presenting as complete uterine inversion: A case report
Authors: Olivia B Foy, Yingao Zhang, Julia Jahansooz, Michelle Pang, Andrew Li
Published: 2026

Introduction

Puerperal uterine inversion is an uncommon obstetric complication occurring in approximately 1:3500 deliveries. Non-puerperal uterine inversion occurs even less frequently, comprising only 16% of total uterine inversions (De et al., 2019). The most common cause of non-puerperal uterine inversions is leiomyoma (57.2%) followed by sarcoma (13.5%), the most common malignant etiology (Rosa Silva et al., 2018). Surgical intervention is the main treatment for non-puerperal uterine inversion. In the majority of cases, an attempt is made at restoring normal anatomy and performing hysterectomy once the uterine inversion has been corrected. It is rare for hysterectomy to be performed with the uterus inverted given the loss of expected anatomical planes. However, in rare cases the uterus is unable to be repositioned. We present a case of uterine inversion caused by carcinosarcoma and managed surgically with total abdominal hysterectomy performed without correcting the uterine inversion.

Case

The patient was an 80-year-old woman who presented to clinic as a referral for postmenopausal bleeding of two months duration with a necrotic vaginal mass on recent pelvic examination. Her medical and surgical history were noncontributory. She underwent menopause at age 55. She had a history of two vaginal births and three induced abortions.

On transabdominal ultrasound, there was heterogeneity of the uterus and lower uterine segment without a well-defined cervix noted. MRI pelvis revealed a 10.7 × 8.3 × 4.6 cm uterus with a heterogeneous endometrial/endocervical mass measuring 8.3 × 10.7 × 7.1 cm filling the endometrial cavity and prolapsing to the level of the vagina, with a possible stalk noted at the uterine fundus (Fig. 1). There was no definite parametrial invasion or lymphadenopathy. On exam, there was pooling of blood in the vaginal vault with friable tissue seen at the cervix. Biopsy was taken showing an entirely necrotic mass with foci of ossification and calcification, suspicious for malignancy.

She was then lost to follow up until re-presenting to the ED two months later with heavier vaginal bleeding requiring transfusion. An updated CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis showed interval uterine inversion into the vagina with similar size to prior and no evidence of extrauterine disease. She was then amenable to surgical management, with plan for exploratory laparotomy, total abdominal hysterectomy, and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy. Lymph node assessment and other staging procedures were deferred due to patient preference after counseling, and an open approach was preferred as a preoperative pelvic exam was unable to be completed due to her discomfort.

At the beginning of the case, a complete uterine inversion was identified with the fundus of the uterus seen as a concavity and adjacent pelvic structures pulled inwards (Fig. 2). An attempt was made to debulk the tumor vaginally in order to facilitate reducing the inversion manually, however this was unsuccessful. We then proceeded with the hysterectomy without reduction as the surgical planes were still able to be visualized. The round ligaments were identified, suture ligated, and incised. The retroperitoneal space was opened and the ureter was identified. This allowed the infundibulopelvic pedicles to be skeletonized, clamped, cut, and suture ligated. The utero-ovarian pedicles were taken and the bilateral ovaries and tubes were amputated and sent to pathology separately to allow better visualization of the uterus. The plane between the bladder and the uterus was developed and the remainder of the hysterectomy was performed in the standard fashion by controlling the uterine vascular supply and making a colpotomy, producing the inverted uterus, cervix, and tumor as a specimen (Fig. 3). The patient's postoperative course was unremarkable, and she was discharged on postoperative day #2. Surgical pathology revealed endometrial carcinosarcoma with rhabdomyosarcomatous and osteosarcomatous elements with cervical stromal involvement, final stage IIC. Her last point of contact was two weeks post-operative and she was recovering well from surgery. Adjuvant chemotherapy was discussed and recommended, and she made the decision to not pursue any further cancer-directed treatment. She was lost to follow up and passed away of unknown causes 9 months after surgery.

Discussion

Uterine carcinosarcoma is a rare uterine neoplasm, comprising less than 5% of all uterine neoplasms. Risk factors including smoking, previous pelvic radiation, and use of tamoxifen. Common symptoms at time of presentation are abnormal uterine bleeding and pelvic pain. Uterine carcinosarcoma is staged and treated as a gynecologic carcinoma using the FIGO staging systems for endometrial cancer, as opposed to following a sarcoma classification. The mainstay of treatment is surgical excision, followed by adjuvant therapy, usually with a combination of systemic chemotherapy and radiation therapy (Pezzicoli et al., 2021). It is rare for carcinosarcoma to present with uterine inversion, though cases of other uterine malignancies causing non-puerperal inversion have been reported in the literature (Kean and Altman, 2019).

Non-puerperal uterine inversion is managed surgically, though the surgical goals and approaches can vary from case to case. Depending on the extent of the uterine inversion patients may present with a symptomatic mass, pelvic pain, vaginal bleeding, or inability to void due to ureteric obstruction. Laparoscopic, vaginal, and abdominal approaches have been previously described as methods for managing inversion with abdominal hysterectomy being the most common approach (48.8%) due increased access for repositioning. Vaginal methods were employed 18% of the time with a combined abdominal and vaginal approach being second most common (27.1%). In a comprehensive literature review of 133 case reports of non-puerperal uterine inversion laparoscopy was utilized in only 6% of the cases, although the review included case reports from before the era in which laparoscopy became widely available (Herath et al., 2020). Few case reports exist from more recent years of laparoscopic approaches or combined laparoscopic-vaginal approaches in the setting of non-puerperal uterine inversion (Auber et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2023). In our case, an abdominal approach was utilized after a thorough discussion with the patient and her preferences to defer the preoperative pelvic exam.

Restoration of normal anatomy by reduction of the uterine inversion is often the first step of any surgical approach. The adnexal structures and round ligaments are pulled inwards during a uterine inversion leading to difficulty with the traditional steps of a hysterectomy. The plane between the uterus and bladder, as well as the delineation of the cervicovaginal junction, can be challenging to identify when an inverted uterus is present. With more advanced uterine inversions, the course of the uterine artery and relationship to the ureter can also be altered leading to surgical difficulty in taking the vascular pedicle safely. Different approaches have been described to reduce a uterine inversion. The Huntington technique involves grasping the round ligaments and uterus below the area of inversion and applying traction upward to gradually reinvert the uterus. This approach is most successful with less severe inversions and increases in difficulty with more advanced degrees of fundal prolapse (Herath et al., 2020). A second abdominal approach is the Haultain procedure where the constriction ring is incised posteriorly and the incision is carried cephalad on the uterus until there is sufficient space to manually reduce the uterus. Vaginal approaches include the Kustner and the Spinelli procedure which entail making a posterior or anterior incision on the uterus, respectively, from a vaginal approach (Rocconi et al., 2003). In our case, these procedures were not needed as the appropriate planes were able to be dissected without correcting the inversion.

A limited number of cases have been reported involving uterine inversion in the setting of a gynecologic malignancy given the rarity of these diagnoses coinciding. Many of the available case reports do not detail if reversion was attempted prior to hysterectomy, the methods used to reduce the inversion, or if hysterectomy was performed with the uterus inverted. In the literature available, we found four other cases of a uterine malignancy causing uterine inversion where hysterectomy was ultimately performed without correcting the uterine inversion. Conclusion

Non-puerperal uterine inversion is a rare occurrence with each case presenting unique challenges with respect to diagnosis and management. Surgical management is often indicated, and when malignancy is the underlying cause of the uterine inversion hysterectomy is nearly always performed. Correcting the uterine inversion allows for normal anatomical planes to be identified and entered but may not always be possible depending on the extent of the uterine inversion and accompanying pathology. It is safe and feasible to perform hysterectomy with the uterus inverted. Given the scarcity in the literature of this phenomenon this case provides valuable insight.
