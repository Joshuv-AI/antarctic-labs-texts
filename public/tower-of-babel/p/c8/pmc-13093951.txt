Social support and maternal caregiving burden in families of children with Angelman syndrome in China: the mediating role of self-stigma
Authors: Nueraili Dayimu, Lingli Leng, Maitixirepu Jilili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRearing a child with Angelman syndrome (AS) poses significant maternal burden. This study examines whether social support–including family, friends, and significant other dimensions–is associated with maternal caregiving burden among primary caregivers of children with AS in mainland China, and whether caregivers’ self-stigma mediates this relationship.MethodsIn the cross-sectional study, a total of 281 primary caregivers (263 mothers) of children with AS in China completed a questionnaire assessing sociodemographic factors, social support (MSPSS-12), self-stigma (ASS-22), and caregiving burden (ZBI-22). All participants were members of the Chinese Angelman Syndrome Organization. Statistical analyses, including multivariable regression and mediation models, were conducted in R 4.2.2. We employed nonparametric bootstrapping for sensitivity analyses.ResultsIn the composite model, lower social support was associated with higher self-stigma, which fully mediated its effect on higher caregiving burden. In the disaggregated model, lower family-support was directly and indirectly associated with higher caregiving burden, with self-stigma serving as a partial mediator. The effect of friends-support on caregiving burden was inconclusive, while significant-others-support was not significant.ConclusionThis study identifies self-stigma as a significant mediator in the relationship between social support and maternal caregiving burden, with family-support emerging as the dimension most strongly associated with these outcomes. These findings highlight the potential value of interventions aimed at enhancing family-support and reducing self-stigma to improve the well-being of mothers of children with AS in China.Keywords: Caregiving burden, Social support, Self-stigma, Angelman syndrome, Primary caregiver, Rare disease

Introduction

Imagine a world filled with laughter, yet overshadowed by profound challenges – this is the reality for families affected by Angelman Syndrome (AS), a rare neurodevelopmental genetic disorder that impacts approximately 1 in every 12,000 to 20,000 individuals worldwide [1]. AS is characterized by severe developmental delays, intellectual disability, and motor coordination impairments, requiring lifelong care and constant supervision from parents or guardians [1, 2]. Rearing a child with AS has detrimental effects on the emotional and psychological well-being of parents, often manifesting as heightened caregiving burden [2–6].

Caregiving burden has been defined as the perception of stress and burden, which is subjectively assessed by those who provide long-term care to the individuals with serious or chronic illnesses [7]. Prior literature documented the association between caregiving burden with adverse mental health outcomes, including depression, anxiety [8–10], and elevated risk of suicidal ideation [11]. However, limited medical resources, diagnostic delays, and the complexity of disease management often magnify caregiving burden in the context of rare diseases [12–16]. As a result, primary caregivers of children with rare diseases experience higher levels of caregiving burden than general children [4–6, 17, 18]. These studies highlighted the critical need to address caregiving burden and its contributing factors to improve the overall well-being of primary caregivers.

Social support plays an important role in buffering the negative effects of caregiving [19, 20]. Empirical research has demonstrated the negative association between social support and mental health [21–23]. In the context of rare disease, studies have also found this association [24–26]. Previous studies documented family [27, 28], peer groups [29–32], and online friends [33] as sources of social support. However, several studies indicate that primary caregivers of patients with rare disease often experience decreased social support, feelings of isolation, and loneliness [34, 35], which caused by disconnection with family networks, friends, and other social support systems [35–41]. Besides, parents often feel misunderstood by their family and friends due to the child’s rare condition, resulting in increased isolation [37–39, 42].

The stigma associated with rare disease may undermine the positive role of social support. It does not only affect patients but also extend to caregivers, leading to increased psychological distress [37–39, 43–45]. Primary caregivers of patients with rare disease often face stigma from the general public, employers, healthcare professionals, and even from the family members [29, 46]. Additionally, stigma from others exacerbates primary caregiver’s feelings of guilt and shame [45, 47], potentially leading to self-stigma [48]. In the Chinese context, research showed that primary caregivers of children with rare disease often experience higher levels of self-stigma, due to their child’s condition conflicts with the familism value upheld by Confucianism [49]. Therefore, we propose that inadequate social support and elevated self-stigma may negatively associate with the caregiving burden among mothers of children with AS in China.

To our knowledge, there is a lack of knowledge about caregiving burden and its contributing factors in the context of parenting a child with AS in China. Second, there is insufficient understanding of the relationship between social support, self-stigma, and caregiving burden in the context of rare disease. To address the gap, this study proposes the following hypotheses:

H1: Lower social support, particularly lower family support, is associated with a higher level of caregiving burden within AS context in China.

H1: Lower social support, particularly lower family support, is associated with a higher level of caregiving burden within AS context in China.

H2: Self-stigma mediates the relationship between social support and caregiving burden.

H2: Self-stigma mediates the relationship between social support and caregiving burden.

Data and methods

Participants

This study employed a cross-sectional design and conducted an online survey from February 20th to March 5th, 2025. Eligibility criteria of this study require participants simultaneously meet following conditions: (1) have to be parents or other legal guardians; (2) should be primary caregiver of the child with AS; (3) the child should be clinically diagnosed with AS; (4) should be resident of and placed in mainland China. Due to the absence of a definitive epidemiological survey and the difficulties in accessing this specific population, we utilized convenience sampling to recruit participants through the Chinese Angelman Syndrome Organization, which is the only national umbrella organization for patients with AS in mainland China. This organization was established in 2011 by family members of AS patients, to support individuals with AS and their families through rehabilitation guidance, psychological support, and educational advocacy. Currently, over 1,000 individuals with AS are registered with this organization. We collected 281 valid questionnaires. Each corresponds to one child with AS and their primary caregiver, the 93.6% of whom were the children’s mothers.

Measures

Perceived caregiving burden

We used the 22-item version of the Zarit Burden Interview [7] to assess the perceived caregiving burden of participants. The scale consists of 22 items and employs a 5-point Likert scale assessment. Respondents rated each item based on their personal feelings, with scores range from 0 (never) to 4 (always). The Chinese version of this scale exhibited good reliability and construct validity in previous research [50]. The total score of the scale ranges from 0 to 88, higher scores indicating severity of caregiving burden. In this study, the Cronbach’s coefficient for the scale was 0.93, demonstrating excellent internal consistency and reliability.

Social support

We utilized the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) to evaluate the social support of participants. The scale comprises 12 items, consists three distinct dimensions (family support, friend support, and support from significant others), and uses a 7-point Likert scale for evaluation. Respondents assess each item based on their subjective feelings, with scores ranging from 1 (very strongly disagree) to 7 (very strongly agree) [51]. The Chinese version of this scale was demonstrated good reliability and validity in earlier studies [52]. The total score of MSPSS scale is calculated by summing the scores of all items, which ranges from 12 to 84. Similarly, scores for three dimensions are also calculated in the same method, in which each dimension ranges from 4 to 28. Higher scores manifest higher social supports. In our study, the overall Cronbach’s α for the scale was 0.91, demonstrating high internal consistency and reliable measurement. Additionally, the Cronbach’s α for the sub dimensions of family, friend, and significant others were 0.87, 0.90, and 0.86.

Self-stigma

We adopted the Affiliated Stigma Scale (ASS) to measure participants’ self-stigma. The scale contains 22 items and utilizes a 5-point Likert scale. Respondents were asked to score each item based on their own feelings from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) [53]. Previous studies have demonstrated strong reliability and validity of Chinese version of this scale [54]. The total score of ASS scale ranges from 22 to 110, with higher scores representing severe self-stigma. In this study, the Cronbach’s α for the scale was 0.96.

Control variables

This study included a series of variables as covariates in our analysis to account for potential confounding factors, including:

Child level. Age, sex (Female = 0), only child status (Yes = 0), BMI, comorbidity (Yes = 0), and disability certificate (No = 0).

Child level. Age, sex (Female = 0), only child status (Yes = 0), BMI, comorbidity (Yes = 0), and disability certificate (No = 0).

Caregiver level. Caregiver relation to child (Others = 0), age, marital status (Divorced = 0), education (college/not = 0), employment (No = 0).

Caregiver level. Caregiver relation to child (Others = 0), age, marital status (Divorced = 0), education (college/not = 0), employment (No = 0).

Household level. Household income (Less than average = 0), residency (Rural = 0), geographical location (West China = 0).

Household level. Household income (Less than average = 0), residency (Rural = 0), geographical location (West China = 0).

Formal support. Does anyone besides the caregiver’s spouse help with caregiving (No = 0).

Formal support. Does anyone besides the caregiver’s spouse help with caregiving (No = 0).

We excluded variables from the covariates set based on two criteria: (1) categories representing less than 5% of the sample (e.g., child’s IQ impairment, caregiver’s marital status), and (2) strong multicollinearity with a primary variable, as seen with child age and time since diagnosis (VIF > 5), we removed the latter.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was proceeded in three steps. First, descriptive statistics were utilized to analyze basic characteristics of the sample. Categorical variables represented as frequencies and percentages N (%), and continuous variables as means and standard deviations (M ± SD). Bivariate relations were tested by univariate analysis between socio-demographic factors and caregiving burden, and a Pearson’s correlation matrix explored intercorrelation among social support (including its three dimensions), self-stigma, and caregiving burden.

Second, the core relationships were estimated using multivariable linear regression, adjusting for all selected covariates. To validate these estimates against potential violations of classical linear model assumptions and to provide more robust estimates, we performed a nonparametric bootstrap sensitivity analysis (with 1,000 iterations) on all linear models, reporting bootstrapped confidence intervals [55].

Finally, we performed a mediation analysis using the Mediation package to examine the mechanistic role of self-stigma. To validate the robustness of the indirect effects, we conducted additional sensitivity analyses employing both bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) and percentile bootstrap methods to drive more accurate confidence intervals. To complement the quantitative sensitivity checks, we also reported kernel density estimates of the bootstrapped indirect effects. This visualization helps assess the shape and stability of the sampling distribution.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.2. We set the seed for 123 and employed a bootstrapping procedure with 1,000 repetitions for the stability of our assessment. The statistical significance level for all analyses was set at P < 0.05 (α = 0.05).

Results

Sample characteristics

Among the 281 children with AS, 52% were boys, average age was 6.7 ± 3.6 years. The mean age at diagnosis was 4.5 ± 3 years, resulting in an average time duration since diagnosis of 4.9 ± 3 years. The mean Body Mass Index (BMI) was 17.3 ± 5.2. Notably, 16.4% of the children had comorbidities with other severe diseases, and 100% had not achieved age-appropriate IQ levels. 79.7% held disability certificate issued by the China National Disability Union. 69.8% had siblings, and 60.5% were city residents. Regarding primary caregivers (n = 281), 93.6% were mothers, with an average age of 38.2 ± 6.7 years. 97.2% of the participants were in a marital status, 54.4% were employed. 67.9% had attained a junior college or higher levels of education. 49.1% assessed their household income levels as above average in their resided areas. Geographically, 47.7% of caregivers from East China, 28.5% from the Central region, and 23.8% from the Western and Northeastern regions. 55.9% of caregivers reported receiving additional caregiving assistance from a third person.

Maternal sociopsychological characteristics

In terms of maternal socio-psychological characteristics, the average caregiving burden score was 50.1 ± 17.3. The mean social support score was 52.6 ± 15.8, with family support averaging 19.1 ± 5.6, support from friends at 16.4 ± 6.1, and from significant others was 16.0 ± 5.8. Lastly, the mean score of self-stigma was 61.9 ± 18.7 (see Fig. 1 and Table 2).

Pearson’s correlation matrix showed a significant positive correlation between caregiving burden and self-stigma (r = 0.701, P < 0.001), suggesting that as self-stigma increases, caregiving burden also tends to increase. Conversely, caregiving burden negatively correlates with social support (r = −0.309, P < 0.001), indicating that as social support decreases, caregiving burden increases. This negative correlation between caregiving burden and social support further extends to various dimensions of social support, such as family (r = −0.346, P < 0.001), friend (r = −0.293, P < 0.001), and significant others (r = −0.183, P < 0.01). Similarly, self-stigma also negatively correlated with social support (r = −0.322, P < 0.001) and its three dimensions-family (r = −0.311, P < 0.001), friend (r = −0.325, P < 0.001), and significant others (r = −0.188, P < 0.01), implicating lower support links to higher levels of self-stigma.

Multivariable regression analysis

After controlling for all covariates, the results presented in Tables 3 and 4 indicate a significant negative association between social support and caregiving burden (β = −0.271, 95% bootstrapped CI [−0.384, −0.139], P < 0.001). Additionally, Model 2 showed a significant negative association between social support and self-stigma (β = −0.307, 95% bootstrapped CI [−0.456, −0.131], P < 0.001). Self-stigma, in turn, was significantly and positively associated with caregiving burden (β = 0.616, 95% bootstrapped CI [0.537, 0.689], P < 0.001). However, after introducing mediating variable, self-stigma, the direct association between social support and caregiving burden was not significant (β = −0.082, 95% bootstrapped CI [−0.172, 0.019], P > 0.05).

To disentangle the contributions of the subdimensions of social support (family, friends, and significant others), we repeated the analysis at the dimensional level. Family-dimension was negatively associated with both caregiving burden (β = −0.706, bootstrapped 95% CI [−1.118, −0.287], P < 0.01) and self-stigma (β = −0.506, bootstrapped 95% CI [−0.963, −0.009], P < 0.05). After accounting for self-stigma, family support on caregiving burden remained significant (β = −0.401, 95% bootstrapped CI [−0.702, −0.110], P < 0.05).

For friends-dimension, the results were inconclusive. In the multidimensional model predicting caregiving burden (Model 4), friends-support showed a statistically significant association (β = −0.508, P < 0.05). However, a nonparametric bootstrap sensitivity analysis revealed this estimate to be fragile, as the 95% confidence interval was wide and crossed zero (95% CI [−0.948, 0.006]). In contrast, friends-support demonstrated a robust and significant negative association with the mediator self-stigma in Model 5 (β = −0.823, 95% bootstrapped CI [−1.316, −0.258], P < 0.01). After adjusted for self-stigma (Model 6), the direct effect of friends-support was non-significant (β = −0.010, 95% bootstrapped CI [−0.358, 0.359], P > 0.05). While friends-support is a reliable predictor of the mediator self-stigma, it does not exhibit a robust independent or total effect on caregiving burden in our models. Any observed marginal association with caregiving burden is likely contingent on model specification and is not stable across resampling. For significant-others-dimension, no statistical associations were found with either caregiving burden or self-stigma in any model (all P > 0.05). Mediation analysis

Table 5 summarized the results of mediation analysis with sensitivity checks. The findings revealed that self-stigma is a significant and robust mediator between social support and perceived caregiving burden both in composite and disaggregated model. In the composite mediation model, the indirect effect was −0.198 (P < 0.001), accounting for 69.7% of the total effect, with the 95% bootstrapped confidence interval excluding 0, confirming the significance of this indirect effect. In this model, the direct effect was not statistically significant (P > 0.05), suggesting that self-stigma fully mediates the association between social support and caregiving burden.

In the disaggregated mediation model, analysis of the specific dimensions of social support revealed a distinct pattern. For family-support, we found evidence of partial mediation. The indirect effect of family-support on caregiving burden through self-stigma was significant (β = −0.306, BCa 95% CI [−0.611, −0.01], P < 0.05), accounting for 43.3% of the total effect. Concurrently, the direct effect of family-support remained significant (β = −0.401, BCa 95% CI [−0.742, −0.10], P < 0.05), confirming that self-stigma explains a portion of the relationship. In contrast, the results for friends-support were not statistically robust. While the point estimate for the indirect effect was of similar magnitude (β = −0.498, P < 0.05), the nonparametric bootstrap sensitivity results indicate that the estimated mediation pathway for friends-support was highly unstable and sensitive to sample-specific variations.

The disaggregated analysis demonstrates that the overall mediation effect of social support on caregiving burden is primarily driven by the robust, partially mediated pathway involving the family-support dimension. The potential role of friends-support remains inconclusive and warrants verification in larger, independent samples.

Discussion

Understanding the determinants of perceived caregiving burden is crucial for supporting mothers of children with AS, yet the potential role of social support has been overlooked, especially in the Chinese context. This study aims to examine the relationship between perceived social support and caregiving burden among the primary caregivers of children with AS in China. It also tests a novel theoretical model in which self-stigma serves as a key mediator. In line with the theoretical hypothesis, the findings of this study confirm the negative association between social support and caregiving burden is significantly mediated by self-stigma within the studied maternal population. Importantly, a key nuance emerges from comparing the composite and disaggregated models: while the composite analysis suggested a full mediation, the dimension-level analysis reveals that family-support exerts a significant direct effect alongside partial mediation via self-stigma.

Perceived social support and maternal caregiving burden

The findings suggest that perceived social support is significantly associated with maternal caregiving burden. Consistent with prior research, higher levels of social support linked with lower levels of caregiving burden [24, 56–58]. Bryson and Bogart [56] conducted a study involving 1203 patients with a rare disease in the United States, revealing those reporting higher levels of emotional and companionship social support experienced better subjective life satisfaction. Similarly, Chen et al. [57] examined 2403 adults in China, finding a positive correlation between social support and psychological quality of life. Likewise, findings from a study involving 124 Chinese caregivers of children with a rare disease indicated a strong negative association between subjective social support and psychological disorders [24].

Moreover, findings of dimensional analysis offer nuanced support for the theoretical hypothesized relationships. The robust effect of family support aligns with theoretical expectation and prior research [59–61]. Previous studies have similarly highlighted the significance of family support, suggesting that caregiving tasks of looking after a patient having a rare disease, negatively affect family relationships and mutual understanding within extended families [34, 35, 62]. However, the inconsistent evidence for friends-support suggests several possibilities: first, theoretical refinement. Friends-support may operate under boundary conditions not captured in our study, or its effect might be contingent on moderating factors. Second, measurement considerations. Friends-support might be measured with greater error than family-support, attenuating its estimated effect. Third, contextual specificity. The effect of friends-support could be context dependent, varying across populations or settings. These results caution against overgeneralizing the role of friends-support while affirming the importance of family-support in understanding the maternal caregiving burden of children with AS in China.

Furthermore, this dimensional divergence may reflect cultural variation in the meaning of family ties within the disabled population. In Confucian traditions, a child’s condition is closely bound to familial reputation and face (mianzi). As a fundamental socio-cultural force, face governs everyday life by operating on two interconnected levels. Societally, it functions as a form of symbolic capital, structuring and maintaining social relationships through reciprocal exchanges and morally binding obligations. Individually, it embodies not only a symbol of one’s social standing and prestige, but also a reflection of the need for respect and the pursuit of an ideal self [63, 64]. This cultural preoccupation with face may help explain why family-support dimension appears particularly salient for the maternal experiences of children with AS in China. For parents, a child’s disability is often interpreted not merely as a medical condition, but as a threat to the family’s collective social standing – a mechanism that may underlie experiences of discrimination or rejection from extended family members [65, 66]. Where family support is present, it may be especially meaningful in this cultural context, potentially offsetting stigma-related distress by affirming the caregiver’s value. Conversely, the absence of adequate familial support may exacerbate self-stigma, and may compound caregiving burden. These findings align with previous work highlighting the significance of family support in the context of rare disease [34, 35, 62]. These observations may underscore the particular importance of familial support for maternal experiences of children with AS in the Chinese cultural context.

The mediating role of self-stigma

This study extends previous findings by illustrating how self-stigma mediates the association between social support and caregiving burden. The significant and nuanced mediating role of self-stigma in this study suggests that it may serve as a critical factor for mothers navigating caregiving burden. Our findings bridge two previously separate strands of caregiving literature. While prior work has linked social support to poorer self-stigma among schizophrenia caregivers [67], and self-stigma to higher caregiving burden in pediatric chronic care contexts [68], we integrate these relationships within a single, theoretically coherent model. This model demonstrates how self-stigma functions as a pivotal psychosocial resource that shapes caregiving experiences of mothers of children with AS in China.

Our mediation analysis reveals a critical nuance: the global effect of social support appears uniform but is primarily attributable to the specific dimension-family support, which associates with caregiving burden both directly and indirectly through self-stigma. Translated to the caregiving context within AS, this pattern suggests a potential psychosocial model: perceived inadequacy of family support may be a key vulnerability factor associated with increased self-stigma. This elevated self-stigma then serves as a proximal correlate of higher caregiving burden. Thus, the interplay between specific caregiver’s vulnerabilities (family-support) and psychosocial status (self-stigma) appears to be a significant correlate of the maternal burden [12, 45]. This mediation pathway suggests that efforts to enhance family-support and alleviate self-stigma may be a valuable intervention strategy to mitigate maternal burden within AS in China.

To the best of our knowledge, this study provides the first empirical investigation of caregiving burden among parents of children with AS in China. It advances our understanding of the complicated interplay between caregiving burden, social support, and self-stigma. By disaggregating social support, we offered novel and nuanced insights into how a specific dimension-particularly family support-is linked to caregiving burden in this understudied population. From a theoretical perspective, our findings highlight the importance of examining specific sources of social support when evaluating its protective role. Culturally, the birth of a child with a rare disease may cause inadequate family-support and elevated self-stigma among mothers, potentially stemming from the culturally rooted stigma of having a child perceived as “unexpected status,” which can impact familial face [65, 66]. Our proposed model suggests that the interplay between lower family-support and higher self-stigma is a significant correlate of severe caregiving burden. Future research should investigate the social mechanisms linking family-support to stigma, and explore how the cultural constructs–particularly familial face–shape these relationships among mothers of children with AS in China.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the use of convenience sampling through the “Chinese Angelman Syndrome Organization” may introduce selection bias. Families connected to such organizations may systematically differ from those who are not – they may have greater access to information, higher socioeconomic status, and stronger baseline peer support. These characteristics could themselves buffer against adverse outcomes, potentially underestimating both the challenges faced by and the effects observed in more isolated or resource-limited families. Additionally, our sample may have inadvertently excluded the most severely burdened caregivers with constrained time, limited access to technology or information, and those facing socioeconomic barriers – their experiences may differ substantially from those represented here.

Second, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences about the relationships between social support, self-stigma, and caregiving burden. Although we tested mediation models, these findings should be interpreted as identifying potential explanatory mechanisms rather than establishing causal pathways; the directionality of these associations remains unclear. Longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to establish temporal precedence and to assess how these relationships unfold over time.

Third, this study relied on self-report measures collected at a single time point, raising concerns about common method bias and social desirability. The exceptionally high internal consistency of the Affiliative Stigma Scale (α = 0.96), for instance, may partly reflect participants’ motivation to present themselves favorably–potentially in hope of eliciting governmental support–rather than true item redundancy. Additionally, our models did not account for several potentially important confounders, including objective indicators of family burden (e.g., expenditure ratios), familial ecology (e.g., relations with extended family, marital quality), and caregiving logistics (e.g., daily time spent on care, division of household labor). Their omission limits our ability to rule out alternative explanations for the observed associations. Future studies employing multi-method, multi-informant designs that incorporate objective measures such as administrative data or time-use diaries would help address these methodological constraints.

Fourth, the sample was predominantly composed of mothers. This homogeneity likely reflects the gendered nature of primary caregiving in China, but it precludes analysis by gender. Prior research suggests meaningful differences in how caregiving burden manifests and operates across genders [6], yet our data cannot speak to whether fathers or other family members experience similar patterns or respond to the same mediating mechanisms. Future studies with larger, more diverse samples–or dyadic designs capturing both parents–are needed to test whether the theoretical model established here extends to paternal experiences.

Finally, while this study provides quantitative evidence on maternal caregiving burden and its contributing factors, it does not capture the lived experiences underlying these associations. Qualitative insights into how social support and self-stigma embodied in mothers’ daily lives, particularly within the Chinese cultural context of caring for a child with AS remain absent. Such narratives would deepen understanding of the processes identified here and illuminate culturally nuanced dynamics, including those related to stigma and face. Future research employing in-depth qualitative or mixed-methods designs would complement these findings and enrich the evidence base for family-centered support.

Implications

Notwithstanding its limitations, this study offers clear implications for supporting families of children with AS in China. The findings underscore the necessity of implementing social support and destigmatization focused interventions, with particular emphasis on policies that bolster family-support. At the family level, practical strategies should aim to enhance family-support through improved understanding and support within the kinship network. This can be achieved by providing families with scientific information about AS and facilitating activities, such as shared meals and family gatherings, that foster constructive interactions and responsibility between family members. At the societal level, building a rare-disease-friendly society is crucial to mitigate self-stigma often experienced by patients and their caregivers. public health initiatives must work to dispel harmful stereotypes and misconceptions-such as erroneous belief that AS results from parental moral failings-through education and community outreach. Promoting accurate scientific knowledge may be essential to reduce stigma and to create a more inclusive social environment. This integrated approach may be valuable for improving maternal wellbeing and quality of life in the context of AS.

Conclusion

This study delineates a potential psychosocial pathway among mothers of children with AS in China: the family-support dimension of perceived social support may be the key correlate of maternal caregiving burden, primarily through elevated self-stigma. The partial mediation model reveals that perceived family-support is associated with caregiving burden through both direct and indirect pathways. This pattern implies the possibility that interventions reducing self-stigma could help offset challenges related to inadequate family-support. Collectively, as promising dual targets, these findings suggest that interventions designed to foster family-support while concurrently reducing self-stigma may offer an effective strategy to mitigate maternal burden in the context of AS in China.
