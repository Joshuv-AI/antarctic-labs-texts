Advanced Drug Delivery Strategies for Overcoming Biological Barriers: Tumor Microenvironment and Blood–Brain Barrier
Authors: Yicong Lei, Tingyu Xiao, Xin Hu, Huaqing Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Therapeutic efficacy for malignancies and neurological disorders is fundamentally restricted by biological barriers, particularly the complex tumor microenvironment (TME) and selective blood–brain barrier (BBB). This review analyzes advanced drug delivery technologies engineered to overcome these obstacles. For TME penetration, stimuli-responsive nanocarriers enable spatiotemporally controlled drug release, while tumor-penetrating peptide functionalized nanoparticles enhance deep tumor diffusion; metal-organic frameworks further facilitate combinatorial therapy via microenvironment-triggered payload release. Regarding BBB transcendence, receptor-mediated transcytosis strategies significantly improve brain uptake, and physical-assisted approaches achieve localized barrier modulation. Bioinspired platforms—notably cell-membrane-coated nanoparticles and exosomes—demonstrate superior immune evasion and tissue-specific accumulation. Despite promising clinical progress exemplified by ANG1005 and focused ultrasound-assisted liposomal doxorubicin, translation challenges persist, including TME heterogeneity, scalable manufacturing complexities, and long-term biosafety. Future development prioritizes multifunctional theranostic systems integrating barrier-remodeling agents, artificial intelligence (AI)-optimized nanocarrier design, and sustainable manufacturing processes. Collectively, these innovations are transforming advanced drug delivery into a core therapeutic paradigm for intractable diseases.

Key Points

This review synthesizes advanced design principles for drug delivery systems that actively exploit or transiently disrupt the tumor microenvironment and blood–brain barrier.We highlight the clinical translation landscape of these strategies, critically discussing both their promise and key limitations such as immunogenicity, penetration depth, and manufacturing scalability.The convergence of stimulus-responsive materials, bioinspired platforms, and physical modulation techniques is outlined as a pivotal pathway to achieving spatiotemporal precision in oncology and neurology.

Introduction

Malignancies and neurological disorders impose a substantial global health burden [1, 2]. A fundamental therapeutic bottleneck lies not only in target complexity but also in the systemic failure of drugs to effectively reach disease sites [3, 4]. Conventional systemic administration leads to broad distribution within the circulation, causing off-target toxicity (e.g., hematopoietic suppression by chemotherapeutics, cardiovascular risks from kinase inhibitors) [5]. This often necessitates dose reduction, compromising therapeutic efficacy at target tissues [6]. This challenge is particularly acute in solid tumors and central nervous system (CNS) diseases [7]. Solid tumors exhibit a heterogeneous tumor microenvironment (TME), where hypoxia, acidic pH, dense extracellular matrix (ECM), and immunosuppressive factors collectively form physicochemical barriers hindering drug penetration and activation [8, 9]. Meanwhile, the blood–brain barrier (BBB) excludes > 98% of potential therapeutics (including antibodies and nucleic acid drugs) via endothelial tight junctions, efflux transporters (e.g., P-gp, breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP)), and enzymatic degradation [10–12].

The advent of precision medicine has transformed therapeutic paradigms through genomics and epigenetics, enabling individualized targeted therapies [13, 14]. However, even precision agents directed against oncogenic driver mutations (e.g., EGFR, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) face dual limitations [15]: (1) insufficient effective drug concentration at the lesion site due to biological barriers and systemic clearance [16, 17] and (2) compensatory pathway activation leading to resistance following target inhibition [18, 19]. This reveals a critical scientific question: effective precision therapy requires not only "molecular precision" but also "spatiotemporal precision"—ensuring drugs reach specific cells or tissue microenvironments at the right time and effective dose [20, 21]. Overcoming this bottleneck necessitates convergence with innovations in materials science, nanotechnology, and biology [22]. Recent advances in stimuli-responsive nanocarriers (e.g., pH/enzyme/hypoxia-responsive materials) [23, 24], bioinspired delivery systems (exosomes, cell membrane-coated nanoparticles), and physical-assisted penetration techniques (focused ultrasound [FUS] with microbubbles) offer novel strategies to circumvent biological barriers [25–27]. These approaches leverage active targeting (e.g., receptor-ligand mediation) or passive accumulation (e.g., enhanced permeability and retention [EPR] effect), coupled with microenvironment-triggered drug release, significantly enhancing drug enrichment in tumors or brain tissue while reducing systemic toxicity [28, 29].

Clinical Significance of Targeted Delivery

Targeted drug delivery systems (TDDS) address two fundamental limitations of conventional pharmacotherapy [30, 31]: the constraint on therapeutic intensity imposed by systemic toxicity [32], and the failure to deliver drugs effectively to disease sites due to biological barriers [33]. Systemic drug administration often leads to widespread distribution in non-target tissues, causing severe adverse effects that necessitate dose reduction or treatment discontinuation [34, 35]. A representative example is the anthracycline doxorubicin, whose anti-cancer mechanism relies on DNA intercalation [36, 37]. However, this property concurrently induces mitochondrial damage in cardiomyocytes, frequently forcing therapeutic regimen adjustments due to cardiotoxicity [38]. Pharmacokinetic studies reveal that conventional chemotherapeutic agents exhibit minimal accumulation efficiency within solid tumors, with the majority sequestered in metabolic organs, creating a detrimental cycle of toxicity and suboptimal efficacy [39, 40]. Even molecularly targeted agents face similar challenges—trastuzumab, while precisely binding HER2-positive breast cancer cells, can induce cardiac dysfunction due to baseline HER2 expression in cardiomyocytes, leading to premature treatment cessation in some patients [41, 42]. Overcoming this toxicity challenge hinges on achieving spatial precision, confining drug activity strictly to pathological microenvironments [43, 44].

Conversely, complex biological barriers systematically impede drug delivery to target sites [45]. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), for instance, dense fibrotic stroma and elevated interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) create substantial physical barriers [46, 47]. This significantly restricts the penetration depth of drugs like gemcitabine into tumor cores, resulting in therapeutic blind spots [48]. The treatment of glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) is hampered by the formidable BBB [49]. Tight junctions between brain capillary endothelial cells, coupled with efflux pump activity, collectively prevent the majority of therapeutic agents from reaching the brain parenchyma [12]. Clinical observations confirm that conventional antibody therapies achieve subtherapeutic concentrations in the brains of patients with breast cancer metastases [50]. Notably, therapeutic antibodies for Alzheimer's disease initially faced clinical trial setbacks due to insufficient BBB penetration, succeeding only after incorporating novel delivery technologies [51].

TDDS employs multistage targeting strategies to overcome these limitations [52, 53]. Liposomal doxorubicin (Doxil®) exploits the EPR effect in tumor vasculature, significantly mitigating cardiotoxicity while enhancing local drug concentration at tumor sites [54, 55]. Antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) such as trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) utilize tumor-specific antibodies to deliver cytotoxic payloads directly to cancer cells, substantially improving intracranial treatment outcomes for breast cancer patients with brain metastases [56]. FUS coupled with microbubbles enables transient BBB opening, facilitating successful delivery of Alzheimer's therapeutics to cerebral targets [57]. These technologies collectively achieve spatial precision (reducing off-target exposure) and dose precision (enhancing effective concentration at disease sites), advancing precision medicine from target identification to effective intervention.

The clinical benefits manifest in several ways: expanded therapeutic windows allow higher tolerable doses of chemotherapeutics in colorectal cancer [58]; stroma-remodeling agents significantly improve drug penetration efficiency in PDAC, prolonging progression-free survival [59]; and BBB disruption (BBBD) technologies enable viable treatment strategies for neurodegenerative disorders [60]. These breakthroughs signify a paradigm shift in managing oncological and neurological diseases—by resolving delivery bottlenecks, the full clinical potential of targeted therapies is unlocked [61, 62]. For example, Alzheimer's antibodies, upon overcoming delivery barriers, successfully demonstrated the ability to delay cognitive decline in pivotal trials [63, 64]. Similarly, PDAC patients receiving barrier-penetrating therapeutics exhibited significantly improved tumor regression rates [65]. Collectively, these cases underscore that when delivery constraints are eliminated, the boundaries of disease treatment can be fundamentally redefined.

Key Characteristics of Biological Barriers

The heterogeneity of the TME constitutes a fundamental barrier to effective drug delivery, as its distinct features collectively undermine the efficacy of nanocarriers through multiple mechanisms [66, 67]. The abnormal vasculature serves as the primary structural barrier, where pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF, FGF) drive the formation of tortuous and leaky neovessels [68]. While endothelial gaps contribute to the EPR effect, plasma extravasation and impaired lymphatic drainage lead to significantly elevated IFP [69]. This not only impedes tumor accumulation of nanoparticles but also causes geometric obstruction of blood flow and increases metastatic risk [70]. The stromal barrier, dominated by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), further exacerbates delivery resistance: CAFs secrete factors such as TGF-β and PDGF to promote desmoplasia, forming a rigid ECM network [71]. This creates a physical barrier that increases solid stress and isolates tumor vessels, while the electrostatic properties of collagen/hyaluronic acid electrostatically trap charged nanoparticles, significantly reducing drug diffusion efficiency [72]. Metabolic dysregulation manifests as a triad of hypoxia, acidosis, and redox imbalance: abnormal vascularization induces core hypoxia, stabilizing hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) that drive epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and therapy resistance [73]; aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect) coupled with carbonic anhydrase-mediated CO₂ hydration synergistically acidifies the TME (pH ~6.5–6.9), promoting tumor invasion and impairing immune cell function [74]; reactive oxygen species (ROS) overload triggers compensatory glutathione (GSH) overexpression, maintaining redox homeostasis while conferring resistance to antioxidative therapies [75]. The immunosuppressive network is a hallmark of the TME, where myeloid cells (e.g., M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages [TAMs], myeloid-derived suppressor cells [MDSCs]) suppress T cell activity via factors like arginase-1 and IL-10, while lymphoid cells (e.g., regulatory T [Treg] cells) directly kill effector T cells and disrupt dendritic cell (DC) antigen presentation [76, 77]. Tumor cells themselves downregulate MHC-I and upregulate immune checkpoints (e.g., PD-L1, CD47), synergistically forming an immune evasion barrier that ultimately excludes cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and NK cells from the tumor core [78, 79].

These interwoven characteristics collectively compromise targeted drug delivery: vascular leakiness and high IFP limit the efficiency of passive nanoparticle targeting; the dense ECM presents a dual physical–chemical barrier hindering deep nanoparticle penetration; acidic pH and redox imbalance can be exploited for designing stimulus-responsive delivery systems, yet metabolic adaptations concurrently induce treatment resistance [28]; the immunosuppressive milieu attenuates the efficacy of immune-activating therapeutics. To counter these barriers, therapeutic strategies require multidimensional integration: anti-angiogenic agents (e.g., bevacizumab) can normalize vasculature, reduce IFP, and improve EPR effects [80]; ECM-degrading enzymes (collagenase/hyaluronidase) or CAF-targeting approaches can soften the stromal barrier; pH- or redox-responsive nanocarriers enable controlled drug release within the TME [81]; immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) (e.g., anti-PD-1) or TAM reprogramming may reverse immunosuppression [82]. In summary, the dynamic complexity of the TME necessitates that drug delivery system design concurrently addresses its structural, metabolic, and immunological features. Synergistic modulation of these multifaceted barriers is essential to enhance the precision and efficacy of tumor-targeted therapies.

CNS disorders, including Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and brain tumors, represent leading global causes of mortality [83]. Therapeutic interventions face significant challenges due to the BBB, which restricts approximately 98% of small-molecule drugs and virtually all large-molecule therapeutics from reaching brain parenchyma [84]. This complex architecture maintains cerebral homeostasis but concurrently impedes therapeutic permeation, with only ~ 1% of drugs effectively accessing CNS targets [85]. BBB permeability favors low molecular weight (< 500 Da) lipophilic compounds through passive diffusion, while other molecules require carrier-mediated transport, receptor-mediated transport, or adsorptive-mediated transport [86]. In neuro-oncology, the BBB functions as a tertiary barrier where tumor vascular leakage and elevated interstitial pressure, combined with microvascular heterogeneity, contribute to heterogeneous drug distribution and diminished efficacy [87].

Beyond the classical BBB, it is important to recognize the heterogeneity of barrier systems within the CNS and sensory organs. The blood–spinal cord barrier, for instance, regulates the microenvironment of the spinal cord and presents a unique set of challenges for drug delivery in conditions such as spinal cord injury and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [88]. Similarly, the blood–retinal barrier tightly controls the inner retinal environment, making it a critical target for nanocarriers designed to treat neovascular and degenerative retinal diseases [89]. Concurrently, alternative administration routes that circumvent these barriers are gaining significant traction. Notably, the intranasal route has emerged as a promising non-invasive strategy for direct nose-to-brain drug delivery. This pathway leverages the olfactory and trigeminal nerves, allowing therapeutics to bypass the systemic circulation and directly access the brain parenchyma. Recent advances have demonstrated the efficacy of nano-formulations delivered intranasally for the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases, brain tumors, and even psychiatric disorders [90].

Despite these advances, nanoparticle-based delivery faces critical challenges requiring optimization of particle size, surface charge, and morphology to balance penetration efficiency and biodistribution [91]. Ligand conjugation strategies must simultaneously enhance targeting specificity and facilitate immune evasion, while long-term biosafety profiles and scalable manufacturing costs require rigorous evaluation [92]. Future priorities should include developing multifunctional nanoplatforms integrating diagnostic capabilities, combination therapies, and cell-penetrating peptides to overcome BBB delivery bottlenecks [93]. Critical challenges remain in establishing standardized methodologies for evaluating nanoparticle biodistribution, optimizing ligand density for maximal receptor engagement, and elucidating potential neurotoxic effects of chronic nanoparticle exposure. Consequently, interdisciplinary collaboration between material scientists, pharmacologists, and clinicians will be essential to translate these promising approaches into clinically viable CNS therapeutics [94, 95].

Scope

Key advancements in targeted delivery systems for overcoming biological barriers like the TME and BBB center on material-driven and bioinspired strategies [96]. In the material domain, engineered nanocarriers address barrier-specific challenges. Stimuli-responsive nanoparticles, such as pH-sensitive micelles (e.g., NK105), leverage pathological TME features (e.g., acidic pH, elevated enzymes) to achieve site-specific drug release [97, 98]. Ligand-modified nanoparticles (e.g., Goldilocks® nanoparticles) designed for BBB penetration optimize receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT) efficiency. Bioinspired strategies enhance efficacy by mimicking biological mechanisms. Cell membrane-coated nanoparticles inherit source cell functions (e.g., neutrophil membrane coating enables inflammation-targeted delivery to brain tumors) [5]. Natural vesicles (e.g., exosomes) serve as platforms for delivering genetic therapeutics across the BBB. Bacterial vectors exploit inherent hypoxia tropism to penetrate deep into tumors, while "Trojan horse" systems utilize drug-loaded carrier cells (e.g., stem cells) for targeted delivery via inherent homing capabilities [100]. Technologies demonstrating high clinical translation potential include the following: ligand-mediated RMT systems (e.g., ANG1005 in phase III trials for brain metastases), FUS combined with microbubbles for reversible BBB/blood–tumor barrier opening (early-stage glioma trials), and standardized bioinspired carriers produced via microfluidics [101]. Current challenges focus on scalable manufacturing of complex systems, batch-to-batch consistency control, and long-term biosafety assessment. Accelerating the development of these innovative and translationally feasible technologies holds promise for breakthroughs in oncology and neurological disorder treatments.

Stimulus-Responsive Nanocarriers

The TME is characterized by distinct features—including hypoxia (pO2 < 10 mmHg) [102], acidity (pH 6.0–6.8) [103], ROS overexpression (H2O2 concentrations reaching 50–100 μM, significantly higher than the 20 nM in normal tissues) [104], and GSH upregulation (concentrations of 2–10 mM, representing a four- to tenfold increase over the 0.5–2 mM in normal tissues) [105]—which provide a targeting basis for designing stimulus-responsive nanocarriers. These characteristics enable prodrugs, loaded via encapsulation or covalent conjugation onto self-assembled polymeric nanoparticles, to achieve controlled release under TME-specific stimuli [106]. ROS-responsive polymers leverage oxidizable moieties such as oxalate ester bonds [107], thioether bonds [108], and selenium bonds [109], which undergo cleavage in high H2O2 environments. A representative mechanism involves the specific oxidation of oxalate ester bonds to carboxylic acids by H2O2 [110]. Nanoparticles like PEG-P(AA-DA)-CPTMA (PPDC@β-Lap) release β-lapachone (β-Lap) in ROS-rich environments; β-Lap further amplifies ROS through NAD(P)H oxidation, establishing a "release-amplification" positive feedback loop [111]. Poly(oxalate) carriers can also undergo self-immolative degradation when incorporated into the polymer backbone. Studies by Höcherl et al. demonstrated that diethylstilbestrol released from such carriers exhibited superior inhibition of drug-resistant prostate cancer cells compared to free drugs, while reducing cytotoxicity by 40% [112].

Hypoxia-responsive systems exploit the hypoxic nature of TME (resulting from impaired circulation and necrosis in solid tumors) [113]. They utilize the property of nitroimidazoles, which are reduced to hydrophilic aminoimidazoles by NADPH-coupled nitroreductases, inducing nanostructural dissociation [114]. An innovative design involves biomimetic nanovesicles (tirapazamine [TPZ]/AI-NV) integrating the photosensitizer Ce6 to achieve triple synergy: the photodynamic process consumes oxygen to generate ROS, exacerbating hypoxia; hypoxia activates TPZ to produce cytotoxic radicals; and the hydrophilic-to-hydrophobic transition of nitroimidazole triggers micelle dissociation. This synergistic strategy enhanced tumor inhibition rates by 2.3-fold compared to monotherapy [114]. Targeting the acidic TME (pH 6.5 ± 0.5 vs. 7.4 in normal tissues) [115], pH-responsive systems employ three primary release mechanisms: amine protonation (tertiary amines acquire positive charge at pH 6.5–7.0, promoting cell membrane adsorption) [116]; irreversible hydrolysis of Schiff bases in lysosomal acidic environments (pH 4.5–5.0) [117]; and cleavage of cis-aconityl bonds at pH 5.0–6.0, generating hydrophilic acrylic acid and alcohol, leading to nanoparticle disintegration [118]. Such systems can enhance drug accumulation in tumor tissue by three- to fivefold [118], and polymers containing fluorescent amines enable in situ pH imaging [119].

GSH-responsive systems capitalize on the elevated GSH concentration gradient in the TME (4–10 times higher than normal tissues) [120], utilizing reduction-sensitive bonds like disulfide bonds [121] and diselenide bonds [122] for targeted delivery. Disulfide bonds cleave into thiols (–SH) upon GSH reduction, achieving drug loading efficiencies exceeding 90% [123]. Diselenide bonds, with a lower reduction potential (− 0.366 V vs. − 0.23 V for disulfide bonds), exhibit two- to threefold faster response kinetics [122], making them suitable for theranostic carriers. Thioether bonds can achieve dual ROS-GSH responsiveness through sulfur oxidation, enhancing tumor targeting specificity [124]. Deep Penetration Strategy

The ECM constitutes a fundamental biological barrier in solid tumors, with its remodeled architecture significantly impeding nanomedicine delivery [128, 129]. This dense network, primarily secreted by CAFs, creates substantial physical resistance to nanoparticle penetration [130]. In PDAC, the stromal component containing abundant ECM may occupy up to 80% of the tumor volume, while collagen concentration in breast tumors can reach 20–40 mg/g tissue compared to 2–5 mg/g in normal mammary tissue [131]. This pathological ECM remodeling manifests through concurrent processes of increased cross-linking and selective degradation, mediated largely by MMPs [132]. Notably, MMP-9 expression shows marked elevation in glioblastoma, breast cancer, and other solid tumors, with the MMP/TIMP ratio shifting decisively toward proteolysis to facilitate invasion [133].

The translational implications of these ECM barriers are profound. Quantitative analyses demonstrate that conventional nanoparticles predominantly accumulate in perivascular regions, with penetration depths typically limited to 1–3 cell diameters from blood vessels [134]. In pancreatic cancer models, this spatial restriction results in less than 1% of administered nanoparticles reaching cancer cells, while the majority remain sequestered in stromal compartments [65]. The combination of dense ECM, aberrant vasculature, and elevated IFP creates particularly challenging delivery conditions in tumors such as pancreatic cancer and triple-negative breast cancer, where the fibrotic stroma can constitute the majority of the tumor mass [135].

To overcome these barriers, the tumor-penetrating peptide iRGD (CRGDKGPDC) employs a sophisticated multistep targeting mechanism [136]. The cascade initiates with RGD motif binding to αv integrins overexpressed on tumor vasculature, followed by MMP-mediated cleavage to expose the CendR motif, which subsequently engages neuropilin-1 receptors to activate transcytosis pathways [137]. This mechanism enables triple penetration capabilities, with documented efficacy across multiple preclinical models. Studies in orthotopic pancreatic cancer models demonstrate that iRGD-modified nanoparticles achieve significantly improved distribution throughout tumor tissue compared to conventional formulations. Similarly, in breast cancer models, iRGD conjugation enhances nanoparticle accumulation in poorly vascularized regions and improves therapeutic outcomes [138].

The clinical potential of penetration-enhancing strategies is further evidenced by combination approaches [139]. Co-administration of ECM-modulating agents with targeted nanoparticles demonstrates synergistic effects in desmoplastic tumor models. Similarly, dual-targeting systems combining iRGD with additional targeting ligands show improved distribution uniformity across heterogeneous tumor regions. Current challenges include addressing the variability in receptor expression and protease activity across different tumor types and individual patients. Future directions emphasize the development of multi-stimuli responsive carriers and personalized approaches based on individual TME characteristics to optimize penetration strategies for specific cancer subtypes.

Metal-Organic Frameworks

The increasing diversity of available cancer therapies has rendered the safe and targeted delivery of drugs and biomolecules to tumor sites while sparing healthy cells a critical challenge and primary concern in oncology research. Among various strategies, the development of stimulus-responsive drug delivery systems (SRDDSs) that exploit features of the TME and cancer cell metabolic reprogramming demonstrates significant potential for achieving highly specific and selective formulations [1].

MOFs offer a versatile platform for exploiting TME characteristics due to their tunable porosity, high specific surface area, combinatorial flexibility of metal ions and organic linkers, and surface functionalization capabilities [140]. Key TME-responsive mechanisms include the following: pH-triggered release: the acidic peritumoral pH (common in hypoxic regions) serves as a release trigger for cytotoxic (bio)molecules loaded in MOFs, enabling controlled payload discharge in tumor cells while maintaining stability in healthy tissues; ROS-responsive chemodynamic therapy (CDT): elevated ROS levels, particularly H2O2, can be therapeutically harnessed via Fenton reactions; Fe2⁺-containing MOFs catalyze H₂O₂ conversion into highly oxidative hydroxyl radicals (•OH) for CDT [140]; active targeting: TME-induced vascular remodeling promotes abnormal tumor vasculature overexpressing pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF); MOFs functionalized with monoclonal antibodies enable selective tumor tissue targeting [141]; multimodal therapy: the capacity to encapsulate diverse payloads—from small molecules to large biomolecules (enzymes, antibodies) and mixtures—facilitates multimodal strategies to overcome therapeutic resistance [142].

Zeolitic imidazolate frameworks‌ (ZIFs), an MOF subclass, feature tetrahedrally coordinated metal ions (e.g., Zn2⁺, Co2⁺, Cu2⁺, Fe2⁺) linked by imidazole-based organic ligands. ZIF-8, a zinc-based variant synthesized from Zn2⁺ ions and 2-methylimidazole (2-MIM), possesses a pore structure with 3.4-Å micropores accessing an 11.6- Å central cavity, yielding high surface area [143]. MOFs enhance biomolecule stability and bioactivity through composite formation (Biomolecule@MOFs), achieved via surface adsorption, pore encapsulation, covalent conjugation, or in situ synthesis. Biomolecules (e.g., peptides, oligonucleotides) serve as organic linkers between metal nodes. In situ encapsulation (biomineralization) occurs during crystal growth, requiring mild synthesis conditions that limit MOF selection [144–146]. For example, Chinnathambi et al. created FA@ED-ZIF-8, which, when mixed with docetaxel in a 2:1 ratio, achieved a drug loading efficiency of 91.6%—significantly higher than the 85.4% of unmodified ZIF-8 [147]. By forming a zinc–DOX coordination polymer prior to ZIF-8 synthesis, Zheng et al. successfully encapsulated the drug, with a loading capacity ranging from 14% to 20% [148]. Acidic TME conditions trigger specific payload release from ZIF nanoparticles. Following cellular internalization, MOF nanoparticles release cargo within acidic endolysosomal compartments. Encapsulated biomolecules subsequently achieve endolysosomal escape, potentially mediated by imidazole-induced proton sponge effects that disrupt membranes [149]. MOF nanoparticles enable efficient tumor-targeted payload delivery and stimulus-triggered release. These properties support multimodal therapy, where synergistic combination strategies overcome monotherapy resistance and enhance therapeutic efficacy compared to standard treatments [150, 151].

The therapeutic paradigm for CNS malignancies necessitates a sequential two-stage targeting approach: initial biodistribution to the brain followed by localized action within the tumor mass. While the TME-adaptive nanotechnologies detailed in this section—including stimulus-responsive carriers, tumor-penetrating peptides, and microenvironment-modulating MOFs—provide sophisticated solutions for intratumoral drug delivery and distribution, their therapeutic implementation is fundamentally constrained by a more primary barrier: the limited brain access of systemically administered agents. This critical pathophysiological hierarchy—where BBB penetration constitutes the mandatory first step for any therapeutic intervention, while TME manipulation represents the subsequent optimization phase—informs both the clinical logic and structural organization of this review. The following section, therefore, systematically examines advanced BBB traversing strategies, with particular emphasis on RMT systems and physical facilitation techniques, thereby establishing the essential pharmacological foundation for the TME-targeting approaches previously discussed. This integrated perspective not only reflects the natural pharmacokinetic progression from circulation to tumor core but also highlights the emerging design principles for next-generation nanocarriers capable of navigating this entire delivery cascade.

Blood–Brain Barrier Penetration Strategies

Receptor-Mediated Transcellular Transport

Approaches for non-invasive therapeutic delivery across the BBB may leverage endogenous processes including adsorptive-mediated transcytosis, carrier-mediated transcytosis (CMT), and RMT. Among these, the exploitation of RMT represents the most extensively investigated strategy for BBB delivery (Fig. 4). The reprogrammed adeno-associated virus (AAV) capsid BI-hTFR1 demonstrated CNS-specific reporter expression 40–50 times greater than AAV9 in mice, highlighting its strong potential as a vector for CNS gene therapy [152]. RMT initiates with ligand binding to cognate receptors on the luminal membrane of brain microvascular endothelial cells, progressing through a multistage process involving receptor-mediated endocytosis (via clathrin-coated or non-clathrin-coated vesicles), intracellular trafficking, vesicular sorting, and culminating in vesicle fusion with the abluminal membrane for parenchymal delivery (Fig. 5a). Transcytosis efficiency correlates with partitioning of endocytic vesicles into early endosome fractions of BBB cells [153]. Optimal RMT or CMT delivery requires target receptors or carrier proteins to exhibit high expression in cerebral vasculature endothelial cells, particularly within microvascular capillary beds, while minimizing peripheral expression to mitigate potential safety concerns—though the significance of this criterion depends on the therapeutic's propensity for peripheral on-target or off-target toxicity. To date, no proteins or pathways fully satisfying these criteria have been identified; research has instead focused on ubiquitous BBB targets including transferrin receptor (TfR), insulin receptor, low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) family members, melanotransferrin (MTf), and CD98 heavy chain (CD98hc/SLC3A2).Fig. 4:Spectrum of advanced delivery strategies. Diverse binding entities targeting these receptors have been developed, encompassing monospecific/bispecific antibodies, single-chain variable fragments (scFvs), single antigen-binding fragments (sFabs), single-domain antibodies (e.g., VNARs, camelid antibodies), dual-variable domain immunoglobulins, and peptides [154], with target selection and entity properties (affinity, pharmacokinetics) critically governing BBB transport efficiency. TfR remains the most widely validated target for RMT-based brain delivery [155, 156], with numerous technologies explored: antibody clones OX26 [156] and 8D3 [157], sFabs [158], scFvs [159], VNARs [160, 161], dual-variable domain immunoglobulins [162], TfR-targeting peptide GYR [163], and Fc-engineered antibody/enzyme transport vehicles [164, 165]. While predominantly preclinical [166], clinical translation shows promise. Optimizing TfR antibody density on nanoparticle surfaces enables significant (albeit low absolute) BBB transport [167], while antibody affinity and valency critically influence intracellular trafficking [168]: high-affinity bivalent antibodies divert antibody–TfR complexes to lysosomes for degradation and TfR downregulation, whereas optimal-affinity and/or monovalent TfR antibodies enhance transcytosis efficiency into the brain [169, 170].

Interspecies TfR variation further complicates translational research, with TfR levels in mouse brain microvessels approximately 2.5-fold higher than in human brain microvessels, necessitating human TfR-knock-in mouse models for antibody development. Ubiquitous peripheral TfR expression also risks off-target uptake in compartments such as bone marrow, liver, and spleen [167]. Consequently, TfR recycling dynamics, binding affinity, expression heterogeneity, and peripheral sinks collectively impact therapeutic utility. LDLR family members (LDLR, LRP1, LRP1B, LRP2/megalin, VLDLR, LRP4, LRP5, LRP6, LRP8, LR11) [171], several expressed on BBB cells [172], represent additional RMT candidate targets. LDLR-mediated delivery utilizes apolipoprotein E (APOE)/apolipoprotein B (APOB)-based constructs [173, 174] or engineered proteins incorporating LDLR-binding domains, shown to reverse neuropathology in a mucopolysaccharidosis type IIIA mouse model [175] and reduce amyloid-β (Aβ) in Alzheimer's disease models [176]. For successful development of RMT/CMT-based brain delivery technologies, the ideal receptor should exhibit exclusive expression on BBB cells, possess high transport capacity, and utilize selective ligands that preserve natural function without triggering lysosomal degradation or luminal recycling while mediating efficient transcytosis. Although no such "ideal" receptor has yet been identified, achieving safe and efficacious delivery of therapeutically relevant drug doses into the CNS, independent validation of BBB pathways and relevant BBB carriers, and ensuring publication of both positive and negative findings remain crucial.

Engineering Adeno-Associated Viruses for Central Nervous System

Beyond exploiting endogenous RMT pathways, engineered viral vectors, particularly AAVs, have emerged as a powerful platform for delivering genetic therapeutics across the BBB [152]. AAVs are non-pathogenic, and multiple serotypes possess inherent tropism for neural tissues.

Serotype selection and neurotropism: The choice of AAV capsid (serotype) critically determines its ability to cross the BBB and transduce specific CNS cell types upon systemic administration. AAV9 is among the most widely studied for CNS applications due to its relatively broad transduction profile, efficiently crossing the BBB to target neurons and astrocytes. However, its specificity is not absolute, and significant peripheral organ transduction occurs. Other serotypes like AAVrh.10 exhibit strong neuronal tropism, while engineered variants such as AAV-retro enable efficient retrograde transport from peripheral injection sites to connected neurons in the CNS, offering unique targeting capabilities for specific neural circuits. The ongoing engineering of novel capsids through directed evolution aims to enhance BBB penetration specificity and cell-type selectivity.

Critical challenge of immunogenicity: Despite their promise, the clinical translation of AAVs is significantly constrained by immunogenicity. This includes (1) pre-existing neutralizing antibodies in a substantial portion of the human population due to prior wild-type AAV exposure, which can completely block transduction; (2) capsid-specific T cell responses that may lead to clearance of transduced cells and limit the durability of therapeutic effect; and (3) the near impossibility of re-dosing due to the development of a robust humoral immune response after the first administration. Strategies to overcome these hurdles include the use of immunosuppressants, engineering of capsids sourced from rare serotypes or synthetic libraries to evade pre-existing immunity, and the development of transient immune modulation protocols. The clinical success of Zolgensma® (onasemnogene abeparvovec-xioi), an AAV9-based gene therapy for spinal muscular atrophy, validates the immense potential of this platform while highlighting the critical need to address immunogenicity for broader application across CNS disorders. For a comprehensive comparison of AAV serotypes and capsid engineering strategies, readers are directed to specialized reviews [60, 368].

Cell-Membrane-Coated Nanoparticles

CNPs represent an advanced targeted delivery platform characterized by a synthetic core enveloped within a naturally derived cell membrane (Fig. 5b). This unique hybrid architecture seamlessly integrates the advantageous properties of both components. The synthetic core serves as a highly customizable matrix for loading anti-cancer payloads, immunostimulants, or photo-responsive elements, often utilizing environmentally responsive nanomaterials or those promoting endosomal escape [177]. Concurrently, the natural membrane shell confers upon CNPs the distinct capability for complex bio-interface interactions with proteins, cells, and biological substrates in vivo [178]. Unlike synthetic polyethylene glycol (PEG) coatings, CNPs inherently incorporate diverse cell surface proteins, endowing them with superior immune evasion properties [179] and inheriting the homing tropism of their source cells [180]. Depending on the membrane source, CNPs can function as antigen reservoirs or present immunostimulatory signals for immunotherapy [181]. Pioneering work utilizing red blood cell (RBC) membranes to camouflage poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles [182] established the foundation for subsequent platforms employing membranes from diverse cell types to functionalize various nanomaterials [183].

Leveraging their unique bio-interface properties, CNPs have been extensively explored in preclinical oncology for targeted delivery, primarily enhancing the localization of cytotoxic drugs, phototherapeutic agents, and immunomodulatory payloads through improved tumor affinity. As a cornerstone strategy in nanomedicine, CNP-based drug delivery aims to maximize payload accumulation at the disease site [184]. For instance, RBC membrane-coated CNPs exhibit significantly prolonged circulation half-lives compared to PEGylated nanoparticles, thereby enhancing passive tumor targeting via the EPR effect [185]. The early proof-of-concept involving doxorubicin encapsulation within a PLGA core coated with an RBC membrane [186] inspired the development of diverse modular design strategies. These include passive drug loading using self-assembling polymer cores [187], porous silica for hydrophilic payloads [188], nanoscale MOFs for biomacromolecules [189], and tunable nanogels [190]. Alternative approaches encompass remote loading of drug crystals into membrane vesicles [191] and the use of liquid perfluorocarbon cores [192]. Notably, the rigidity of CNPs significantly influences their in vivo performance, with designs mimicking RBC deformability reducing immune clearance [193]. To further enhance targeting specificity, engineering efforts focus on both membrane and core components. Membrane engineering strategies involve functionalizing RBC-CNPs with ligands such as folate, AS1411 aptamer [194], RGD peptides, or angiopep-2 [195] to improve tumor homing. Streptavidin-displaying membranes enable the precise conjugation of biotinylated molecules [196] (e.g., cyclic RGD-functionalized RBC-CNPs enhancing docetaxel delivery in glioma models). Core engineering integrates stimuli-responsive release mechanisms triggered by external cues (e.g., near-infrared light using photo-responsive dyes [188] or nanomaterials [197]) or TME signals (e.g., acid-labile prodrugs [198] or pH-responsive nanogels [199]). Furthermore, carbon-based nanomaterials such as carbon dots have shown promise for BBB delivery and bioimaging, owing to their small size, facile surface functionalization, photoluminescence, and good biocompatibility. However, most research on carbon dots for CNS delivery remains at the preclinical stage, with key translational challenges including understanding their long-term in vivo fate, scalable production, and standardized characterization.

Active tumor targeting strategies exploit the inherent homing capabilities of membranes derived from cells involved in tumorigenesis, such as platelets [200] and immune cells. Platelet membrane-coated CNPs conjugated with tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) leverage P-selectin–CD44 interactions for tumor targeting [201], while modifications like tissue plasminogen activator can mitigate thrombotic risks [202]. Platelet vesicles loaded with hypoxia-activated prodrugs [203] or remotely loaded doxorubicin nanocrystals [204] demonstrate enhanced anti-tumor efficacy. Immune cell-derived membranes offer diverse functionalities: macrophage membranes enable VCAM1-mediated metastasis targeting [205] or pH-triggered release [206]; neutrophil membranes are utilized for circulating tumor cell depletion [207] or to generate cytotoxic hypochlorous acid via neutrophil-mimicking MOFs [208]. In cancer immunotherapy, CNP-based nanovaccines address challenges by enhancing antigen delivery to antigen-presenting cells (APCs) [209]. Specific approaches include using mannose-modified RBC membranes for receptor-targeted delivery [210], employing cancer cell membranes [211] to integrate tumor-associated antigens like N-glycolylneuraminic acid [212], incorporating adjuvant-loaded cores [213], fusing cancer and bacterial membranes [214], utilizing membranes from surgically resected tumors, or applying induced pluripotent stem cell membranes [215]. In situ vaccination strategies employ bacteria membrane-coated TLR agonist cores to capture radiotherapy-released antigens [216] or utilize magnesium micromotors with immunostimulatory coatings to stimulate immunity against endogenous antigens. Furthermore, CNP-based nanoscale artificial APCs (aAPCs) directly present processed antigens to T cells. Representative examples include magnetic cores coated with azide-functionalized membranes conjugated with peptide-MHC-I and anti-CD28 for T cell expansion [217], DC-cancer cell hybrid membranes coating photosensitizer-MOFs to induce immunogenic cell death [218], antigen-pulsed DC membranes coating adjuvant-PLGA cores functionalized with anti-CD3 for T cell targeting [219], and genetically engineered cancer cell membranes expressing CD80/MHC-I on nanoparticle cores, synergizing with checkpoint inhibitors [220].

Physical-Assisted Delivery

Physical-assisted delivery strategies overcome biological barriers through external energy stimulation, significantly enhancing therapeutic agent accumulation in target tissues [221]. FUS combined with microbubbles has demonstrated breakthrough efficacy in this domain [222] (Fig. 5c). The core mechanism relies on micron-sized inert gas microbubbles serving as acoustic energy transducers. When FUS beams are precisely targeted to specific regions (e.g., the BBB), microbubbles undergo periodic oscillation within the acoustic field, generating localized shear forces and cavitation effects [223, 224]. This process reversibly loosens tight junctions between cerebral capillary endothelial cells, thereby transiently opening the BBB. The procedure exhibits spatiotemporal controllability, with an opening window typically lasting 4–6 h [225]. Due to its favorable safety, low-frequency FUS (0.5–1.5 MHz) is preferred for BBB opening. Downs et al. demonstrated safe and reproducible BBB opening in primates with 0.5-MHz FUS [226]. In murine glioma models, Guo et al. showed that 1.5-MHz FUS increases BBB permeability by enhancing wall shear stress during microbubble oscillation [227]. Fan et al. further achieved precise opening in mice using 10-MHz FUS, enabling localized high-concentration drug delivery for CNS diseases with minimal systemic toxicity [228]. The degree and extent of opening can be precisely modulated by adjusting ultrasound parameters (e.g., acoustic pressure, pulse length, and microbubble dosage). Preclinical studies confirm that this technique enhances drug concentration in brain tissue by three- to five-fold while avoiding permanent damage to surrounding parenchyma [229]. Notably, the physical properties of microbubbles (e.g., size distribution, shell elasticity) directly influence cavitation efficiency. Optimized lipid- or polymer-coated microbubbles have achieved stable acoustic responsiveness, laying the foundation for clinical translation [230].

Building upon this mechanism, FUS-microbubble technology has advanced to clinical applications in neuro-oncology, particularly for glioblastoma (GBM) [231]. The infiltrative growth of GBM and the presence of the BBB limit the efficacy of systemic chemotherapy agents such as doxorubicin. To address this, researchers developed an FUS-assisted liposomal doxorubicin delivery protocol: MRI-guided FUS systems (e.g., Exablate Neuro) first precisely target tumor regions; following intravenous microbubble injection, ultrasound irradiation transiently opens the BBB [232]; subsequently, pegylated liposomal doxorubicin (e.g., Doxil®) is administered, leveraging its long-circulating properties and EPR effect to penetrate tumor tissue during the opening window [233]. Postoperative MRI confirmed the absence of severe adverse events (e.g., hemorrhage or edema). Tissue biopsies revealed a 4.2-fold increase in intratumoral doxorubicin concentration compared to controls, with drug distribution closely matching the FUS-targeted area. Although overall survival extension requires validation in larger cohorts, this study provides the first clinical evidence supporting the feasibility of FUS-microbubble-assisted nanodrug delivery for human GBM, offering critical insights for optimizing dosing regimens [234].

Despite these advantages, the clinical implementation of FUS-assisted delivery faces multifaceted challenges. Technologically, real-time closed-loop control systems require development to dynamically adjust ultrasound parameters via acoustic feedback, maintaining stable BBB opening while preventing vascular damage from excessive cavitation. Furthermore, the sequential timing of microbubble and nanodrug administration significantly impacts delivery efficiency [235, 236]. Biologically, heterogeneity in tumor hemodynamics may cause uneven opening, necessitating individualized treatment planning using perfusion imaging. Future research should prioritize designing multifunctional microbubbles—such as drug-loaded variants enabling synchronized sonodynamic drug release and BBB opening, or surface-modified versions with targeting ligands (e.g., TfR antibodies) to enhance tumor-specific uptake [237]. Concurrently, exploring FUS combined with other physical stimuli (e.g., photothermal effects) may overcome treatment limitations in deep-seated tumors.

While novel physical approaches like FUS represent the frontier of targeted barrier modulation, chemical disruption via mannitol remains the most extensively validated and clinically implemented method for achieving measurable BBB opening in human patients [238, 239]. First described by Neuwelt et al. in the 1980s, intra-arterial infusion of hyperosmolar (typically 25%) mannitol creates a profound osmotic gradient across the cerebrovascular endothelium [240]. This gradient causes rapid efflux of water from endothelial cells, resulting in cellular shrinkage and the mechanical disengagement of tight junction proteins—primarily claudins and occludins—that normally seal the paracellular pathway. This reversible physical separation creates a transient window (typically 30–45 min) of significantly enhanced vascular permeability, permitting the entry of therapeutics that would otherwise be excluded from the CNS [87]. The clinical application of this mechanism is almost exclusively centered on oncology, particularly for the treatment of primary CNS lymphoma and GBM. The protocol, known as BBBD, is routinely used in specialized centers to enhance the delivery of chemotherapeutic agents such as carboplatin, methotrexate, and cyclophosphamide [241]. Meta-analyses of clinical outcomes have demonstrated that BBBD with mannitol can increase drug concentrations in tumor tissue by 2–3 logs compared to intravenous administration alone, which has translated into improved radiographic response rates and survival in specific patient cohorts. This established efficacy profile makes mannitol-induced BBBD the current "gold standard" against which the efficacy of newer BBB opening technologies must be measured.

When contrasted with a modality like FUS, the fundamental trade-off becomes starkly apparent. Mannitol offers a proven, broad-scale opening solution that is already integrated into clinical practice for life-threatening conditions where the risk–benefit ratio justifies its invasiveness and non-specificity. In contrast, FUS aims to provide a level of sub-millimeter, image-guided precision that mannitol cannot achieve, potentially enabling its safe application in non-oncological CNS diseases like Alzheimer's or Parkinson's disease. The continued clinical use of mannitol thus serves as both a benchmark for efficacy and a reminder of the complications that future BBB modulation strategies must strive to overcome. Its enduring role in the neuro-oncological arsenal underscores that for certain clinical scenarios, a robust, if blunt, tool that is available today holds more immediate value than a precision instrument still under development.

Bionic Delivery System

Exosome Delivery Platform

Exosomes, natural nanoscale extracellular vesicles (30–150 nm) secreted by diverse cell types, have emerged as promising drug delivery vehicles due to inherent biocompatibility and targeting capabilities [245]. Their lipid bilayer membrane, enriched with tetraspanins (CD9, CD63, CD81), confers structural stability and reduces immunogenicity compared to synthetic nanoparticles [246]. Critically, exosomes exhibit intrinsic homing effects: surface adhesion molecules (e.g., integrins, ICAM-1) facilitate selective uptake by specific recipient cells through ligand–receptor interactions, while endogenous trafficking signals enable crossing of biological barriers (e.g., BBB) [247, 248]. Proteomic analyses reveal that parental cell-derived surface proteins dictate tropism—DC-derived exosomes preferentially accumulate in lymph nodes, while mesenchymal stem cell exosomes target inflamed tissues [249]. This biological targeting precision, combined with evasion of mononuclear phagocyte system clearance, results in 3.5-fold higher tumor accumulation than PEGylated liposomes in murine models, underscoring their translational potential [250].

To harness exosomes for nucleic acid delivery, engineering strategies focus on cargo loading and enhanced targeting. Small interfering RNA (siRNA) and messenger RNA (mRNA) are incorporated via electroporation (achieving ~ 15–20% loading efficiency), sonication, or freeze–thaw cycles, preserving vesicle integrity while maintaining payload bioactivity [251]. For instance, exosomes loaded with KRAS siRNA suppressed pancreatic tumor growth by 60% in vivo through efficient cytosolic delivery, outperforming commercial transfection reagents [252]. Alternatively, genetic modification of parent cells enables endogenous packaging—transfection with plasmid DNA encoding therapeutic mRNA yields exosomes with stably encapsulated transcripts. Surface functionalization further refines specificity: conjugation of neuron-targeting RVG peptides to exosomes enhanced brain delivery of BACE1 siRNA, reducing Aβ plaques in Alzheimer’s models by 50%. Similarly, folate-modified exosomes increased uptake in folate receptor-positive tumors by eightfold, demonstrating programmable targeting [253].

Despite promising preclinical outcomes, clinical translation faces challenges. Scalable production remains limited by low exosome yields from cell cultures; emerging bioreactor systems and plant-derived exosomes offer potential solutions. Cargo loading efficiency requires optimization to minimize empty vesicle ratios. Regulatory frameworks for exosome therapeutics are evolving, emphasizing rigorous characterization of composition, stability, and pharmacokinetics. Current clinical trials (e.g., NCT03608631 evaluating mesenchymal stem cell-exosome-delivered siRNA for pancreatic cancer) will validate safety and efficacy in humans [254]. Future directions include hybrid exosome-synthetic nanoparticle systems to augment drug capacity and multifunctional exosomes co-loaded with chemotherapeutics and nucleic acids for synergistic therapy. As natural intercellular communicators, exosomes represent a paradigm-shifting platform that merges biological intelligence with therapeutic engineering, poised to overcome critical barriers in precision medicine.

Cell Membrane Biomimetic Technology

Neutrophils originate from hematopoietic stem cells [255], entering circulation upon cytokine stimulation (e.g., CXC chemokines, G-CSF) [256], and comprise 50–70% of circulating leukocytes despite their short lifespan [257]. As critical first responders in acute inflammation [258], they eliminate pathogens via phagolysosomal digestion through oxidative bursts [259] and bridge innate/adaptive immunity. Their anti-tumor capacity stems from ROS/granzyme-mediated cytotoxicity [260] and downstream immune activation [261], effectively extending anti-infection mechanisms to cancer defense. Key anti-tumor mediators include neutrophil secretions: ROS activate Ca2+-dependent cancer cell death [262]; tumor-entrained neutrophils (TENs) inhibit metastasis via H2O2 production [263]; reactive nitrogen species (RNS) like nitric oxide from MET⁺ subsets [264] and TRAIL-induced apoptosis [260] enhance cytotoxicity. Enzymatic actions feature neutrophil elastase releasing CD95 death domains to eliminate cancer cells and trigger CD8⁺ T cell-mediated abscopal effects [265]. Neutrophils' tumor-targeting capability enables innovative biomimetic delivery systems: Ren et al.'s neutrophil-carried liposomes co-deliver paclitaxel/hydroxychloroquine to inhibit autophagy and stemness in breast cancer [266]; Chu et al.'s neutrophil membrane-cloaked nanoparticles exploit NI-anchored drugs for explosive release at tumor sites [267]; Cui et al.'s membrane-coated nanodevices enhance DNA damage delivery [268]. Alternative strategies include neutrophil-derived exosomes [269] and BBB-penetrating CAR-neutrophils delivering TPZ [270]. Crucially, neutrophil-derived ROS/RNS in inflammatory microenvironments induce DNA damage through 8-oxoG mutations [271] and MDA-DNA adducts that provoke cell cycle arrest and oncogenic mutations [272], highlighting their dual roles in therapy and genomic instability.

Cancer cell membrane coating exploits tumor-specific adhesion molecules (galectin-3, N-calmodulin, EpCAM) to confer homologous-targeting capabilities, enabling multicellular aggregate formation and enhanced tumor accumulation [273]. Cancer cell membranes eliminate genetic transmission risks while their tumor antigens promote differentiation and individualized immunity [274]. Ding et al. [273] demonstrated cancer cell membrane's capping efficacy using mesoporous silica nanoparticles co-loaded with DOX/ICG: uncoated nanoparticles released 81% DOX and 73% ICG within 10 h, whereas cancer cell membrane-coated counterparts showed negligible release. Similarly, Feng et al. [275] utilized HeLa membranes (HM) to encapsulate boron nitride nanospheres (BNs), achieving 862 μg/mg DOX loading versus 7 μg/mg on uncoated BNs. HM-BNs exhibited fourfold higher boron uptake in HeLa cells (0.72 pg/cell) than in HEK293 cells (0.18 pg/cell), confirming homologous adhesion-driven targeting.

Despite these advantages, cancer cell membrane's clinical translation is hampered by poor immunogenicity and immunosuppressive surface molecules that limit specific immune responses. To address this, Ling et al. [274] enhanced immunostimulation by coating bacterial ghosts—which possess intrinsic immunostimulatory properties—with cancer cell membranes, creating a synergistic platform that overcomes this critical limitation while retaining targeting functionality.

Clinical Translation Progress and Challenges

Multifunctional Integrated System

Multifunctional integrated delivery systems represent a strategic advancement in cancer immunotherapy, specifically designed to concurrently target the TME and deliver ICIs [276]. This synergistic approach addresses two critical barriers to effective anti-tumor immunity: the immunosuppressive nature of the TME, which hinders immune cell infiltration and function, and the limited bioavailability of ICIs when administered systemically. By encapsulating or co-loading ICIs within TME-responsive nanocarriers, these systems achieve spatiotemporally controlled release, ensuring precise delivery of immunomodulatory agents directly to tumor sites while minimizing off-target effects. The rational design of such platforms leverages the EPR effect or active targeting ligands to promote preferential accumulation within tumors, thereby overcoming the inefficient distribution associated with conventional ICI administration [277, 278].

The engineering of these co-delivery systems employs diverse nanoplatforms—including polymeric nanoparticles, liposomes, and inorganic carriers—functionalized with TME-responsive elements (e.g., pH-sensitive linkers, MMP-cleavable peptides) and surface-modified with targeting moieties (e.g., antibodies, peptides) specific to TME components such as TAMs, CAFs, or endothelial markers [279–281]. This multilayered functionality enables sequential or simultaneous release kinetics: initial TME remodeling (e.g., depletion of immunosuppressive cells, normalization of vasculature) followed by sustained ICI release (e.g., anti-PD-1/PD-L1, anti-CTLA-4 antibodies) to reactivate exhausted T cells [282, 283]. Recent studies demonstrate that co-delivery significantly enhances anti-tumor efficacy compared to monotherapy or free drug combinations, attributed to improved intratumoral drug exposure, prolonged immune activation, and reduced systemic toxicity [284–286].

Clinically, these integrated systems hold substantial promise for overcoming primary and acquired resistance to ICIs. Preclinical studies demonstrate that TME-targeted ICI co-delivery platforms induce more significant tumor regression and prolong survival in immunologically "cold" tumor models compared to free ICIs. Furthermore, the ability to incorporate additional therapeutic modalities (e.g., chemotherapeutics, photothermal agents) within these multifunctional carriers enables tri-combinational therapy, amplifying immunogenic cell death and anti-tumor immune priming. While scale-up complexity and long-term biocompatibility require further investigation, ongoing clinical translation efforts focus on optimizing carrier design to maximize payload capacity, control release profiles, and streamline manufacturing processes, positioning TME-targeted ICI co-delivery as a cornerstone of next-generation cancer immunotherapy [287, 288].

Intelligent Design

Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a transformative tool for de novo design and optimization of nanomedicines, particularly through its ability to predict complex nanomaterial–bio interactions at unprecedented scales [289]. Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) algorithms leverage high-dimensional datasets—encompassing nanoparticle physicochemical properties (size, shape, surface charge, hydrophobicity), protein corona profiles, and multi-omics responses—to establish quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) models. These models bypass traditional trial-and-error approaches by predicting biological outcomes such as cellular uptake efficiency, immune activation potential, and organ-specific biodistribution directly from nanomaterial descriptors. For instance, graph neural networks (GNNs) analyzing nanoparticle-protein binding affinities have successfully predicted in vivo clearance rates with >85% accuracy, while convolutional neural networks (CNNs) processing electron microscopy images enable real-time classification of nanoparticle aggregation states in biological fluids [290–293].

The predictive power of AI extends to critical safety and efficacy parameters that dictate clinical translation. Ensemble ML models integrating in vitro cytotoxicity data with nanomaterial characterization datasets can forecast hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity risks of novel nanocarriers with area under the curve (AUC) values exceeding 0.92, substantially reducing animal testing needs [294]. Similarly, deep reinforcement learning frameworks optimize targeting ligand density on nanoparticle surfaces by simulating receptor binding kinetics under physiological flow conditions, yielding designs with three- to fivefold improvements in tumor accumulation compared to heuristic approaches. Recent breakthroughs include transformer-based architectures predicting immune cell-specific responses to polymeric nanoparticles by cross-referencing epigenetic data with adsorption energy landscapes of surface-bound immunomodulators, enabling rational design of "stealth" carriers that evade macrophage clearance [295–297].

Despite these advances, challenges persist in model generalizability due to data heterogeneity and the dynamic nature of bio-nano interfaces. Federated learning approaches are being deployed to collaboratively train models across multiple institutions while preserving data privacy, enhancing predictive robustness for rare nanoparticle compositions [298]. Integration of multiscale simulations—molecular dynamics for short-term interactions coupled with AI-predicted long-term biodistribution—is yielding digital twins of nanoparticle behavior in virtual patients. As experimental techniques for high-throughput characterization advance (e.g., single-particle mass cytometry, CRISPR-based barcoding), the generated big data will further refine AI algorithms toward clinical deployment. Regulatory science is adapting through initiatives like the Food and Drug Administration’s (FDA's) AI/ML-based Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) framework, which provides pathways for validating in silico nanotoxicology models [299]. Ultimately, the convergence of AI with automated nanomaterial synthesis platforms promises closed-loop design systems capable of real-time optimization of nanomedicines for patient-specific therapeutic applications [300, 301].

Sustainable Nanocarriers

The green sustainability of nanocarriers is increasingly recognized as pivotal for translational applications, driven significantly by advances in bio-based material utilization [302]. Genetically engineered microorganisms enable efficient production of recombinant proteins that self-assemble with plant-derived lipids into biomimetic carriers, substantially lowering the environmental impact compared to conventional synthesis. Recent progress in microalgal bioreactor systems demonstrates scalable production of functionalized exosome carriers at clinically relevant yields, utilizing seawater-based media to conserve freshwater resources [303, 304]. These carriers exhibit comparable targeting efficacy to synthetic alternatives in preclinical models while eliminating organic solvent-related toxicity concerns [305, 306].

Continuous-flow manufacturing technologies are transforming production sustainability. Microfluidic platforms facilitate rapid nanoprecipitation synthesis, dramatically reducing processing time and solvent requirements relative to batch methods [307, 308]. Integrated monitoring systems ensure precise particle size control, and modular designs enable single-step functionalization through enzymatic catalysis—avoiding hazardous coupling agents. This approach has been successfully scaled for clinical-grade manufacturing, demonstrating robust production capacity to meet therapeutic demands [309–311].

End-of-life management innovations extend sustainability to the post-utilization phase [312, 313]. Smart carriers engineered with stimuli-responsive linkages undergo programmed degradation into biocompatible metabolites, enhancing systemic clearance. For metallic nanoparticles, plant-derived leaching agents enable efficient recovery of precious metals while minimizing ecological hazards. Concurrently, life cycle assessment frameworks provide standardized metrics to quantify environmental footprints across production pathways, supporting the development of certification systems aligned with carbon neutrality objectives [314, 315].

Nanotechnology-Enabled Drugs in Clinical Trials

The clinical success of nanoparticle platforms, particularly in vaccinology, was globally underscored by the lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-based mRNA vaccines from Pfizer and Moderna. While widely used in oncology, nanoparticles offer unique advantages for vaccine delivery, where polymeric and liposomal particles function as combined carriers and adjuvants. LNPs excel in protecting fragile payloads (e.g., mRNA) and can be engineered for enhanced performance: PEGylation prolongs circulation, while modulating lipid ratios or adding ligands can improve targeting. This design flexibility makes lipid-based systems a highly promising platform for achieving superior and tunable immune activation compared to traditional vaccines [316, 317]. Optimizing such formulations requires meticulous attention to critical quality attributes, including size, surface charge, and antigen loading methodology [317]. The proven translational pathway of liposomal technology is confirmed by multiple FDA-approved therapies, such as Mosquirix (malaria vaccine, 2015), Marqibo (leukemia, 2012), and Onivyde (pancreatic cancer, 2015), highlighting its robust clinical potential [318].

A 2016 analysis highlighted that the majority of FDA-approved nanoparticles were liposomal or polymeric in nature [319]. While the authors anticipated a future shift toward newer nanoparticle types due to the considerable diversity within the field [319], a 2019 review identified an emerging trend: the use of nanoparticles for mRNA delivery [320]. Contrary to earlier predictions, however, most reports since 2016 have remained centered on liposomes and their applications in oncology. This focus is frequently attributed to the landmark success of FDA-approved nanomedicines such as Abraxane and Doxil, which, at the time of publication, constituted a large proportion of nanoparticles in clinical trials and helped establish oncology as the dominant therapeutic area for nanomaterial research [321]. An updated review in 2021 [322] documented more than 35 new clinical trials, noting that 28 of these studies involved lipid-based nanoparticles—25 of which were dedicated to mRNA-based vaccines. Recent data also suggest a significant expansion in the spectrum of diseases being targeted. For instance, Crohn’s disease and multiple sclerosis are receiving growing attention, with several nanoparticle-based drugs under investigation or already approved, such as Cimzia and Copaxone [323].

The clinical efficacy of mRNA vaccines critically depends on the adjuvant activity of LNPs, with the ionizable lipid component serving as a key mediator of immune activation. This adjuvant effect has been demonstrated in clinical settings where empty LNPs co-administered with protein antigens generated potent immune responses [324]. The dual functionality of ionizable lipids—facilitating both RNA delivery and immune activation—has been strategically leveraged in cancer vaccine development. For instance, in the AMPLIFY-201 trial for pancreatic cancer, an albumin-binding amphiphile–CpG adjuvant combined with amphiphile-peptides elicited dose-dependent T cell responses [325]. Similarly, STING-activating ionizable lipids have been incorporated into mRNA cancer vaccines to enhance APC function and improve clinical immunogenicity [326]. The clinical translation of lipid-based adjuvants extends beyond mRNA platforms. Cationic liposomes have demonstrated substantial adjuvant potential by triggering DC activation and type I interferon production through non-TLR pathways [327]. This mechanism underpins the clinical advancement of PDS Biotechnology's cationic liposome-HPV peptide formulation, which has progressed to phase III trials for head and neck cancer in combination with checkpoint blockade therapy. The promising clinical results observed with these various lipid-based adjuvant systems highlight their crucial role in enhancing vaccine efficacy and support their continued development as modular components for next-generation cancer immunotherapies.

Among the therapeutic agents formulated within nanocarriers in clinical trials, paclitaxel emerged as the most prevalent, accounting for 23% of the reported cases. The dominant formulation was nanoparticle albumin-bound (NAB) paclitaxel—a protein-based nanoparticle—which constituted 57 out of the 62 paclitaxel-related trials. The second most frequently represented category was metal-based nanoparticles (11%), largely dominated by various iron formulations. Bupivacaine, a local anesthetic, represented 8% of the studies, specifically in the form of Exparel, a liposomal bupivacaine injectable suspension. In general, nearly all other agents cataloged in Figure 3a belonged to diverse classes of anti-cancer drugs, while vaccine preparations accounted for 8% of the remaining candidates [321].

Innate immune stimulators conjugated to albumin-binding lipids have emerged as an effective strategy for lymph node-targeted delivery of adjuvants such as TLR9 agonists. This approach enhances immunostimulatory efficacy while reducing systemic toxicity [328, 329]. For instance, in Elicio Therapeutics’ pancreatic cancer vaccine study, an albumin-binding amphiphile–CpG adjuvant was administered together with amphiphile–peptide conjugates, eliciting dose-dependent T cell responses in the AMPLIFY-201 trial [325]. Alternatively, covalent linkage of innate immune stimulators directly to antigens represents another promising direction. Speetjens et al. developed a construct in which a lipidated TLR2 agonist was conjugated to synthetic long peptides derived from immunodominant regions of the HPV16 E6 oncoprotein (E6 71–95 and E6 127–158) [330]. In a phase I dose-escalation trial involving patients with HPV16-positive premalignant lesions, this self-adjuvanting vaccine induced robust HPV16-specific T cell responses in all participants at a peptide antigen dose of 50 μg [330].

The collective evidence solidifies the transition of nanomedicine from a promising drug delivery concept to a clinically validated platform with demonstrated impact across multiple therapeutic areas. The landmark success of LNPs in mRNA vaccines, combined with established applications in oncology and expanding use in autoimmune diseases, underscores the platform's remarkable versatility. While lipid-based and polymeric systems currently dominate the clinical landscape, ongoing innovations in material design, targeting strategies, and immune modulation continue to broaden nanomedicine's therapeutic scope. The convergence of nanoparticle technology with immunology has been particularly transformative, enabling vaccines and immunotherapies with enhanced precision and efficacy. As characterization methods improve and manufacturing challenges are addressed, nanomedicine is poised to advance toward more personalized approaches—where nanoparticle design is tailored not only to specific diseases but to individual patient characteristics. Future progress will likely focus on developing smarter nanoparticles capable of integrating diagnostic and therapeutic functions, ultimately realizing the full potential of precision nanomedicine in clinical practice.

Outlook

Personalized therapies exhibit extreme diversity, necessitating equally varied drug delivery approaches for improvement, particularly concerning toxicity mitigation, pharmacokinetics, and biodistribution. Highly specific carriers like ADCs are optimal for delivering non-specific, toxic payloads, whereas less specific materials-based strategies (e.g., ligand-derivatized nanoparticles) better suit cargos with inherent molecular or cellular specificity. Toxicity solutions differ markedly between drugs; avoiding specific tissues (e.g., kidneys or brain) may resolve dose-limiting toxicities for some agents but not others. Hydrophilic nanoparticles, such as PEG-coated formulations, can circumvent the brain when the BBB remains intact, demonstrating how strategic material–drug pairing can enhance therapeutic indices.

Despite significant advances—including FDA-approved RNA delivery systems, marketed gene therapies, and over 50 precision small-molecule therapeutics—clinical progress remains limited for targeted delivery of kinase inhibitors, anti-androgen therapies, and newer small-molecule inhibitors. Nanomedicine’s limited application for these agents stems partly from low drug loading/delivery efficiency and synthetic complexities arising from diverse drug chemistries. Emerging strategies to enhance loading include drug nanocrystals [331, 332], solid lipid–drug particles [333], and polymeric nanoparticles [334]. Computational approaches ("nanoinformatics") are accelerating development, with quantitative structure–property relationships predicting colloidal aggregation [335, 336], lipid formulation loading [337], and in vivo performance [338, 339], while molecular dynamics simulations optimize supramolecular interactions [340, 341] and vehicle selection [342]. Nanotechnologies also aid precision medicine through tools like nanoparticle-based tumor screens predicting therapeutic potency with single-cell sensitivity [343].

Critical to clinical translation is matching nanoparticle technologies to patients, facilitated by imaging methods assessing tumor penetration potential [344] and radiolabeled nanoparticles quantifying EPR effects [345, 346] to stratify patients [347, 348]. Combining nanomedicines with radiotherapy [349], FUS, immunotherapy [350], or photodynamic therapy further enhances efficacy. Regulatory complexities persist, however, as nanoparticle-encapsulated drugs typically require Investigational New Drug applications, with vehicle components demanding careful characterization [351]—a process supported by resources like the NCI Nanotechnology Characterization Laboratory [352, 353]. Although nanomedicine enthusiasm is tempered by manufacturing complexity and limited preclinical integration with precision drugs, the clinical success of similarly complex platforms (e.g., ADCs) suggests these hurdles are surmountable. Given precision drugs’ narrow therapeutic indices, resistance issues, and combination toxicities, opportunities abound to refine their pharmacological profiles through advanced delivery.

Looking forward, the paradigm for precision nanomedicine must evolve from a singular focus on "targeted delivery" to the development of "adaptive systems" capable of navigating the dynamic heterogeneity of human tumors [354]. A critical expert perspective posits that the true challenge lies not only in matching a drug to a carrier, but in matching the drug–carrier complex to the patient's evolving disease biology [355]. This necessitates a closed-loop design philosophy, where nanocarriers are not merely passive vehicles but active participants in the therapeutic cycle. For instance, the integration of diagnostic sensors (e.g., protease-activatable fluorescent reporters) within nanoparticles could enable real-time feedback on drug release and resistance, informing subsequent treatment cycles [356]. Furthermore, the field must confront the "personalization paradox": the more tailored a therapy becomes to a specific molecular profile, the smaller the eligible patient population and the more complex the manufacturing. A promising resolution lies in modular, "plug-and-play" platforms, such as customizable LNPs or pre-functionalized polymeric backbones, that can be rapidly reconfigured with different targeting ligands or drug payloads based on diagnostic readouts, thereby balancing specificity with scalability [357, 358]. Ultimately, the next breakthrough will likely stem from viewing nanocarriers not as isolated solutions, but as integral components of a broader digital-health ecosystem, where computational predictions of patient-specific biodistribution and efficacy guide the selection of the optimal delivery system from a pre-approved arsenal.

Rather than pursuing sustained drug release—a goal that may inadvertently promote resistance in targeted cancer therapies—emerging research suggests that pulsed high-dose delivery could be more effective for certain precision agents [359]. This approach, termed "pharmacodynamic intensification," uses stimuli-responsive nanocarriers to release >90% of their cargo within a narrow therapeutic window [360]. By achieving high peak concentrations that overwhelm compensatory signaling pathways while allowing normal tissue recovery during drug-free intervals, this strategy may circumvent adaptive resistance mechanisms that plague continuous dosing regimens. Such temporal control of drug exposure represents a fundamental shift in how we define the therapeutic index—from area-under-the-curve metrics to peak-intensity-to-toxicity ratios—demanding corresponding innovations in carrier design and clinical evaluation frameworks.

A critical, yet often underappreciated, challenge lies in the industrial-scale manufacturing of these sophisticated, multifunctional drug delivery systems [361]. Reproducibly producing complex nanocarriers—featuring targeted ligands, multilayer coatings, or combination therapies—under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards presents formidable hurdles [362]. Key attributes like particle size, drug loading, and surface functionality must be rigorously controlled, yet scale-up frequently introduces variability in mixing, purification, and sterilization that can compromise efficacy and safety [363]. Bridging this translational gap necessitates adopting continuous manufacturing and "Quality by Design" (QbD) principles, supported by process analytical technologies for real-time monitoring. The successful global scale-up of LNPs for mRNA vaccines demonstrates that these barriers are surmountable, providing a blueprint for industrializing next-generation nanomedicines through early integration of engineering solutions [364, 365]. Furthermore, the biosafety evaluation of CNS-targeted nanotherapeutics must adhere to rigorous international standards to ensure translational relevance. The gold-standard framework is largely defined by regulatory guidance from the US FDA and the European Medicines Agency (EMA), as well as harmonized principles from the International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) [366, 367]. Beyond standard cytotoxicity assays, this mandates comprehensive safety pharmacology studies (ICH S7A) to screen for adverse physiological effects on the CNS [368]. Critical assessments include specialized neurotoxicity profiling in vitro (e.g., using human iPSC-derived neurons and glial cells) and in vivo, meticulous histopathological examination of brain and spinal cord tissues, and quantitative biodistribution studies to determine potential off-target accumulation [369]. Furthermore, evaluation of immunogenic potential is paramount, particularly for lipid-based and polymeric nanoparticles. Adherence to these consensus standards, alongside emerging ISO norms for nanomaterial characterization, is indispensable for de-risking the clinical translation of novel CNS therapies [370].

While numerous nanocarrier platforms demonstrate promising proof-of-concept efficacy in preclinical models, the field must increasingly adopt gold-standard quantitative metrics to robustly validate therapeutic superiority [371]. Beyond demonstrating tumor growth inhibition, rigorous pharmacokinetic analysis via mass spectrometry is essential to precisely quantify the enhanced drug accumulation in target tissues and the concomitant reduction in off-target organs, thereby directly linking delivery efficiency to potential toxicity mitigation [372]. Furthermore, the critical importance of the delivery system should be quantified through comparative dose-response studies, where a significant leftward shift in the IC50 curve of the nano-formulation, compared to the free drug, provides unequivocal evidence of enhanced potency [373]. Widespread adoption of such stringent efficacy metrics, alongside other standards like survival benefit analysis and predictive biomarkers, will be crucial for bridging the gap between promising preclinical data and successful clinical translation.

These converging advances suggest that the future of precision nanomedicine lies at the intersection of materials science, systems biology, and clinical informatics. As we develop increasingly sophisticated delivery platforms, the focus must expand beyond optimizing individual components to engineering integrated systems that can dynamically respond to disease states, adapt to patient-specific physiological barriers, and interface seamlessly with digital health technologies. Only through such multidisciplinary integration can we fully address the complex challenges of personalized therapy and realize the long-promised potential of nanomedicine in precision oncology.

Conclusion

This review has systematically examined advanced drug delivery strategies designed to overcome the TME and the BBB. By focusing on elucidating the core design principles and paradigms for traversing these biological barriers—rather than attempting to cover every technical detail of this vast field—and by anchoring the discussion in key strategies such as stimulus responsiveness, bioinspiration, and physical assistance, supplemented with an analysis of the clinical translation landscape and its challenges, we aimed to provide a “strategic blueprint” to guide future research. The persistent challenge of biological barriers—notably the TME and BBB—has fundamentally limited the efficacy of conventional drug delivery, driving the development of innovative strategies. Recent breakthroughs center on stimuli-responsive nanocarriers for targeted release, bioinspired systems leveraging natural trafficking pathways, and physical modulation techniques to transiently enhance barrier permeability. These approaches collectively address core pharmacokinetic limitations, translating into clinically validated improvements in therapeutic outcomes for previously intractable conditions. Despite promising advances, critical translational hurdles remain. Heterogeneity in biological barriers introduces significant variability in delivery efficiency, while manufacturing scalability and long-term biocompatibility concerns impede clinical adoption. Addressing these challenges necessitates integrated solutions: multifunctional platforms combining diagnostic and therapeutic capabilities, closed-loop systems enabling autonomous treatment adaptation, and co-therapies designed to remodel pathological barriers. Such synergistic strategies demonstrate tangible potential to overcome existing limitations. Looking forward, the field must prioritize three interconnected pathways: personalized profiling of barrier heterogeneity to inform precision design, accelerated translation through advanced multiscale evaluation models, and sustainable manufacturing of biodegradable delivery systems. The convergence of these efforts will catalyze a paradigm shift—transforming drug delivery from a supportive tool to a therapeutic cornerstone capable of dynamically reprogramming biological barriers. This evolution promises to unlock curative potential across oncology, neurology, and beyond, ultimately redefining standards of care for complex diseases.
