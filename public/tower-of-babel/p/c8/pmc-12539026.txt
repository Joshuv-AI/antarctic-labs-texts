Bio-integration of rhizobacteria and biogenic silver nanoparticles against Pseudomonas syringae pv. syringae causing plum bacterial canker
Authors: Misbah Kanwal, Raees Ahmed, Asma Hanif, Umair Mehmood, Mashael Daghash Alqahtani, Muhammad Aamir Iqbal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPlum is a nutritionally and medicinally valuable fruit with high economic value; however, in temperate regions, its yield continuously suffers from bacterial canker caused by Pseudomonas syringae pathovars.ResultsTo bridge the research gap, a study was conducted to optimize the use of rhizobacteria and biogenic silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) for controlling bacterial canker in plums. Rhizobacteria were isolated from plum orchards and identified (through 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, phylogenetic analysis using MEGA) and further evaluated for plant growth-promoting characteristics (siderophore production, production of hydrogen cyanide, solubilization of phosphate, and biofilm formation). The results revealed that 7 isolates out of 19 rhizobacterial isolates produced biofilm, of which Rw-1, Rw-6, Rw-7, and Hj-2 were Pseudomonas fluorescens, while Rw-12 and Hj-5 were identified as Bacillus subtilis. Besides, biogenic AgNPs were prepared in different concentrations (0.2%, 0.4%, and 0.6%) by using Cannabis sativa plant extract. X-ray diffraction (indicated the size of crystalline green silver nanoparticles of ~ 20 nm), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (confirmed the presence of alkyne molecules, polyphenols, and carbonyl compounds on the AgNPs surface), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were used for characterization of the nanoparticles. Additionally, the antibacterial activity of AgNPs was determined by the disk diffusion assay with 18-hour cultures of P. syringae. In vitro experiments showed that the highest inhibition zone (24.32 mm) was caused by the 0.6% AgNPs concentration, proving the strongest antibacterial activity. The subsequent glasshouse tests confirmed that a consortium of two strains of Bacillus with 0.6% AgNPs allowed the lowest disease incidence (18.13%), proving efficient disease suppression. Combination of Bacillus and Pseudomonas strains with 0.6% AgNPs recorded significantly higher disease incidence, with the probable reason being incompatibility among these bacterial genera.ConclusionsThe research findings emphasize the potential of applying compatible rhizobacterial consortia and biogenic (C. sativa) silver nanoparticles as a potent substitute for synthetic pesticides in controlling plum bacterial canker.Keywords: Bacterial canker, Biological control, Nanoparticles, Rhizobacteria, X-ray diffraction

Background

Plant diseases, particularly caused by a diverse range of bacteria, lead to immense economic losses due to the suboptimal productivity of modern farming systems. Pathogenic microorganisms seriously reduce the quantity and deteriorate the quality of fruits worldwide, resulting in billion-dollar losses. The current fruit production systems, especially of plums, are experiencing the challenge of lower productivity owing to several factors, including frequent incidence of pathogens and abiotic stress conditions (heat, drought, and salinity), which ultimately cause a 25–65% decline in fruit productivity [1]. Plum is considered the most taxonomically diverse stone fruit belonging to the Prunoideae subfamily of the Rosaceae family, whereas the subgenus Prunophora includes several species of Prunus, especially Prunus salicina and Prunus domestica [2]. It is believed to be the first domesticated fruit in recorded human history and holds immense economic significance among stone fruits like peaches, apricots, and cherries. Although there are over 200 varieties of plums, only a small number of them are of commercial importance because of their superior medicinal and nutritional values. Plums are a rich source of vitamins (A, B-complex including thiamine, C, and E) and several vital minerals, including calcium, magnesium, potassium, sodium, phosphorus, iron, and manganese [3–6]. In addition, the presence of bioactive compounds (citric acid, phenolic acid, and malic acid), fiber (pectin), tannins, aromatic substances, and several enzymes makes plums immensely useful in maintaining the metabolic functions of humans [7]. Besides nutritional value, it offers numerous medicinal properties such as anti-ageing and many skin-care (persistent consumption reduces pimples and blackheads) properties [8, 9]. The world’s production of plum fruit remained 12.6 million tons in the last year, which makes it the third-largest species of fruit trees in terms of production [4]. In Pakistan, annual plum production hovers around 51,732 tons, primarily from the provinces of Baluchistan and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK), including some areas of Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK) like Rawalakot, Hajira, etc [5]. Plum orchards provide a decent livelihood and can serve as an economically viable, strategically pivotal, and renewable resource to meet the United Nations’ sustainable development goals (SDGs) of poverty alleviation and zero hunger in the developing countries of South Asia [3].

However, in recent years, plums have become prone to various diseases, including plum pox virus (PPV), bacterial spot (Xanthomonas campestris), black knot fungus (Apiosporina morbosa), and particularly, bacterial canker (Pseudomonas syringae) [10, 11]. Bacterial canker of plums caused by Pseudomonas syringae has seriously reduced its yield and nutritional quality globally [12, 13]. Previously, it has been reported that this pathogen (P. syringae) exists in over 50 different pathovars, which can infect plums under varied agro-climatic conditions [14–17]. A study has reported that several factors, including soil (texture, pH, etc.), environmental factors (rainfall intensity and distribution, and low temperature), inappropriate cultural practices, and pruning-related damage, influence the severity of canker incidence on plum fruits [18]. Contrasting findings have been reported concerning the efficacy of different chemical compounds, antibiotics, and some bacterial antagonists in controlling bacterial canker of plums [19–23].

Recently, nanomaterials (NMs) and their application in biosciences have gained increasing interest, leading to the emergence of the nano-revolution. NMs hold superiority by directly affecting the target sites and are easily degraded by a wide range of microbes, indicating their dominance over the synthetic chemical compounds. NMs, particularly silver nanoparticles (AgNPs), particularly green AgNPs prepared from the extract of Cannabis sativa, have the potential to be employed as pesticides in controlling diseases like plum bacterial canker [24–26]. It has been inferred that green NMs, by virtue of their superior penetration capability, may inhibit the severity of pathogens like plum canker, whereas their small particle size, agglomeration, and high surface area increase their efficacy for fruit trees. The co-application of AgNPs with chitosan demonstrated vigorous antimicrobial properties of NMs against bacterial canker, whereas in another study, AgNPs and Cs-silver composite reduced the severity of bacterial canker in peach [27, 28].

However, research findings are scant concerning rhizobacteria co-application with green AgNPs prepared from the extract of C. sativa for controlling bacterial canker of plums in temperate regions. Therefore, we hypothesized that rhizobia strains alone in conjunction with silver nanoparticles may prove effective against bacterial canker owing to enhanced antibacterial properties. Thus, the primary aim of the present investigation was to study and identify the indigenous rhizobacteria in a temperate region of Pakistan and employ those in co-application with biogenic silver nanoparticles against P. syringae (pathogen causing bacterial canker in plums) in in vitro and glasshouse experiments.

Methods

Planting material, isolation, and purification of rhizobacteria

One and 1.5-year-old seedlings of plum cultivars were collected from the certified nurseries of Swat and Peshawar (KPK province of Pakistan) that were maintained in the greenhouse of the University of Poonch Rawalakot (UPR), Azad Jammu and Kashmir, Pakistan. Already isolated, identified, and preserved cultures of pathogenic bacteria (P. syringae pv. syringae) were collected from the Bacteriology Laboratory in the Department of Plant Pathology, UPR, Pakistan.

Rhizobacterial isolates were collected by employing the serial dilution method on nutrient agar (NA) medium with incubation at 27 ± 2 °C for 24–48 h. The stock solution was maintained by blending 50 g of rhizospheric soil into 100 ml of sterilized distilled water and making subsequent serial dilutions up to 10⁹. The diluted samples (10⁶ to 10⁹) were plated on NA and incubated for bacterial growth. Purification of both NA and King’s B media was performed using the streaking technique to obtain morphologically different single colonies for subsequent preservation.

Rhizobacterial properties and molecular identification of bacterial isolates

The rhizobacterial properties of isolated bacterial strains were evaluated using a series of tests, viz., siderophore production, HCN (hydrogen cyanide) production, biofilm formation, and P-solubilization using prescribed procedures [29]. DNA extraction of selected bacterial isolates was done from overnight-grown bacterial cultures using a shaking incubator, in NA medium. The Standard manufacturer protocol of the GeneJet DNA extraction kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was followed, and subsequently, visualization of DNA samples was performed using 1% agarose gels under a gel documentation system. PCR was done using bacterial DNA as a template, and the 16s gene fragment was amplified by optimizing the 16s rRNA primer. The reaction mixture and reaction conditions were optimized and troubleshooted as per the melting temperature of the primers. The PCR product was then segregated on agarose gel using a 1kb DNA ladder, followed by purification of the expected band of about 1500bp. The purified PCR product was used for sequencing at MacroGen Korea, and the obtained sequences were analyzed using BLAST on NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information), whereas Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (MEGA) was used for phylogenetic analysis to check genetic similarity. Finally, manipulated sequences were submitted to the NCBI database.

Pathogen inoculation protocol

A 24-hour-old culture grown on nutrient agar was used to prepare the P. syringae pv. syringae inoculum in sterile distilled water and adjusted to a final concentration of 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL (measured by a spectrophotometer at OD600 = 0.3). Thereafter, the pathogen was inoculated using a 100 mL suspension adjusted to 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL, prepared from an actively growing 48-hour culture. A sterile needle was used to gently wound the stem base and leaf axils of the plum seedlings two weeks after transplanting them into pots. Then, using a sterile brush, 2 mL of the bacterial suspension was brushed onto the wound sites. To ensure consistent infection, three inoculation sites were given to each plant. Sterile distilled water was used as a mock inoculant for the control plants.

Green silver nanoparticles’ biosynthesis and characterization

A stock solution of C. sativa leaf extract was prepared and used for the synthesis of green AgNPs. Silver nitrate solution was prepared using 0.05 M silver nitrate (AgNO3) in distilled water and further used for the preparation of AgNPs. It was continuously heated until the color changed from dark brown to black. The prepared solution of AgNO3 and plant extract was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The resultant supernatant was replaced by deionized (DI) water; thereafter, another round of washing with DI water was done. This process was repeated thrice, and 1.0 mL of DI water was used for the extract-free pellets of AgNPs homogenization to carry out further characterization. Thereafter, extract-mixed AgNPs were synthesized and subsequently dried without the use of centrifugation. The collected AgNPs were stored in covered glass vials for further studies [30], and characterization of biogenic AgNPs was done using X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning SEM, and FTIR [31].

In vitro evaluation of biogenic silver nanoparticles against P. syringae pathovars

Green silver nanoparticles were used for the evaluation of their effectiveness against P. syringae with the help of the inhibition zone technique. In total, there were varying concentrations of AgNPs (0.2%, 0.4%, and 0.6%) prepared by mixing 0.2 g, 0.4 g, and 0.6 g of green AgNPs in 100 mL of DI water. For the streaking of P. syringae pathovars, nutrient agar media plates were used. Thereafter, the inoculated plates were covered with the sterilized blotter paper after dipping in AgNPs concentrations. The control treatment had blotter paper that was dipped in DI water and stored at room temperature. The inoculated plates were then subjected to incubation for 24 h. at a constant temperature of 28 ℃. After 24 h., growth inhibition was observed, whereas a scale was used to measure the zone of inhibition for three consecutive days and compared with the control treatment [32].

Glasshouse evaluation of rhizobacteria and biogenic silver nanoparticles against P. syringae Pathovars

Seedlings of susceptible plum varieties (1 to 2 years old) were sown in pots. Each treatment was applied to 5 plants per replication, with 5 replications arranged in the CRD (completely randomized design). Hence, each treatment consisted of 25 plants in total. One week after transplantation, the plants were inoculated with the P. syringae pathogen. One week after the inoculation with 100 ml of silver nanoparticles solution, nanoparticles were applied in a consortium with rhizobacterial isolates. In the consortium treatment, the colonized particles of the biocontrol agent were thoroughly mixed with sterile soil in pots. Pots were left for one week for colonization by the biocontrol agent. For replication purposes, five repetitions of each treatment were maintained in a completely randomized design (CRD). Thereafter, pots were carefully shifted to a glasshouse having 25 ± 2 °C, and for the next 8 weeks, repeated examinations were done to observe the disease incidence-related parameters. The DI water served as a negative control in all experiments. No mineral fertilizers or pesticides were used in the glasshouse trial, whereas the time of pathogen inoculation was considered as day zero post inoculation (dpi), and a percent disease index was also calculated [33].

Application of AgNPs and rhizobacteria

As soil amendments, rhizobacterial inoculants were used. In particular, at the time of transplantation, 100 mL of bacterial suspension (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) was thoroughly mixed into the potting soil surrounding the root zone. Prior to soil application, equal volumes of bacterial strains were mixed for consortium treatments. Following a week of colonization, the same root zone was drenched with 100 mL of green AgNPs solution (0.6% concentration). In the current study, the rhizobacteria and AgNPs were applied as a single application. For eight weeks following inoculation, disease progression was monitored.

Statistical analysis

The recorded data were subjected to analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) by employing a completely randomized design (CRD) under glasshouse conditions for estimating the overall significance of the employed treatments. Thereafter, treatment means were compared using the least significant difference (LSD) test at a 5% probability level.

Results

Rhizobacterial isolates, their properties, and molecular identification

From the soil of the rhizosphere, a total of 19 morphologically different bacterial isolates were recovered (Table 1). It was observed that most of the bacterial isolates were off-white with flat colonies. A few of the bacterial isolates were observed to have irregular growth and were Gram-negative with creamy-white colonies and flat colony elevations on nutrient agar media. Of the total 19 isolates, 8 bacterial isolates were found positive to produce siderophore and produced fluorescent pigmentation on the media, and the color was diffused around the bacterial colony by producing yellow to dark greenish color (Table 2). Similarly, maximum isolates showed production of hydrogen cyanide while cultured on media supplemented with 1% NaCl. Moreover, the formation of biofilm was observed, and it was found that 7 isolates out of 19 rhizobacterial isolates produced biofilm and were visualized as purple rings. The selected rhizobacterial isolates showed positive qualitative rhizobacterial properties and were further identified based on their phylogenetic analysis using 16 s rRNA primer sequencing, in comparison with already submitted sequences on the NCBI database. The BLAST-N results showed that the rhizobacterial isolates, viz., Rw-1, Rw-6, Rw-7, and Hj-2, were Pseudomonas fluorescens, while Rw-12 and Hj-5 were identified as Bacillus subtilis (Figs. 1 and 2, and 3).

Characterization of nanoparticles based on scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The SEM technique was employed to study the morphological characteristics of the biogenic silver nanoparticles. The micrograph of C. sativa AgNPs explored their minute size, globose ball shape, and regular dissemination (Fig. 4).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis and X-ray diffraction (XRD) study

FTIR analysis revealed the surface functional groups present on the synthesized green AgNPs, indicating the biomolecules responsible for capping and stabilization. The results revealed that the prominent peaks were recorded for higher concentrations of plant extract used with the silver nitrate solution. The bands at 2152 cm-1 confirmed the presence of the C-C stretching bond of the alkyne molecule (Fig. 5). In addition, small peaks at 1000–1200 cm-1 confirmed the presence of polyphenols, phenolic, and carbonyl compounds on the silver nanoparticle surface. For structural studies of prepared green silver nanoparticles, a Model-D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer device was used for scanning with a step size from 10 to 2000, calculated with a 20–70 (2θ) width, and then analyzed. The size of crystalline green silver nanoparticles was found to be ~ 20 nm(Fig. 6).

Glasshouse evaluation of green AgNPs and rhizobacteria against P. syringae

Green silver nanoparticles alone and in consortia with rhizobacterial isolates showed positive results against P. syringae pv. syringae. Discussion

This study was conducted with a primary aim to optimize the use of rhizobacteria and biogenic silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) for controlling bacterial canker in plums. The results of the current study indicate that 7 isolates out of 19 rhizobacterial isolates produced biofilm and were visualized as purple rings, and these findings corroborate with previous findings that the production of biofilm was observed for rhizobacterial isolates on M9 media in comparison to the LB media. It was also inferred that biofilm development appeared to thrive under increased nutrient and salt stress. Over time, there was a decrease in free-floating and loosely attached cells, followed by an increase in tightly attached cells. Moreover, isolation and purification of rhizobacterial isolates associated with the plum rhizosphere were effectively made on artificial nutritional media using the serial dilution technique [34].

The results of this study regarding rhizobacterial properties showed that the maximum isolates were positive for siderophore production (a chelated iron compound), which has been reported to be an important property of microorganisms for rhizobacterial activity. Siderophore production ability with greater chelating capability that reduced the chance for pathogens to uptake the iron, thus decreasing the disease incidence, and the media having low iron may have more chelators, which was identified by producing fluorescent pigment on the media [35]. Like our findings, it was inferred that the strains of Pseudomonas produced plant growth-promoting (PGPR) properties that tended to vary significantly under diverse soil and environmental conditions [36]. Additionally, similar results were reported previously concerning the fluorescence-producing ability of Pseudomonas strains for plant growth promotion under stress conditions [37]. As noted in this study, it was found that on a specific concentration of NaCl, Pseudomonas strains produced HCN, while the production of HCN was delayed with the increase of NaCl in the media [36]. In another research, it was observed that bacterial cells defend themselves against any stress to survive in micro-colonies [38]. Furthermore, it has been reported that the environmental stresses, including nutritional or water stress, lead to enhanced competition for available nutrient uptake, and then bacteria accumulate themselves from the planktonic stage for fortification of the rhizosphere [39].

In this study, molecular identification through sequencing of 16 S rRNA genes established the identity of rhizobacterial isolates as Bacillus subtilis and Pseudomonas fluorescens, as per their plant growth-promoting characteristics. These genera have been extensively documented for their use as biocontrol agents and for increasing plant health in previous research studies [40, 41]. Moreover, our findings concerning the phylogenetic relationship based on MEGA 3.0 demonstrated a close resemblance of these genera to reference strains present in the NCBI database, whereas identification served as a foundation for their use in sustainable plant disease management.

Interestingly, AgNPs with desirable physical traits were synthesized to control the challenging plant pathogen (Pseudomonas syringae), by applying solely and in combination with rhizobia strains. The SEM analysis revealed that C. sativa AgNPs (biogenic AgNPs were prepared by using Cannabis sativa plant extract at 0.2%, 0.4%, and 0.6% concentrations) had a minute size, were globose and ball-shaped, and had regular dissemination. This morphological behavior has been reported to be a characteristic of AgNPs when synthesized under optimized and eco-friendly conditions. In accordance with our findings, it has been noticed that when utilizing zinc nitrate as a follower, the nanoparticles developed gradually and appeared in a minute globular framework and aggregated like flower-shaped bundles. The results of previous SEM studies of AgNPs have also reported the irregular shape and morphology of green-synthesized AgNPs [42, 43]. The results concerning FTIR analysis showed that surface functional groups were present on the synthesized green AgNPs, indicating the biomolecules responsible for capping and stabilization. In addition, the peaks were prominent in higher concentrations of plant extract used with the silver nitrate solution. The bands at 2152 cm-1 confirmed the presence of the C-C stretching bond of the alkyne molecule, and small peaks at 1000–1200 cm-1 confirmed the presence of polyphenols, phenolic, and carbonyl compounds on the silver nanoparticle surface. Moreover, the X-ray diffraction study revealed that the size of crystalline green silver nanoparticles was ~ 20 nm. The results of this study corroborate with the previous findings [44, 45], which reported that the bands between the functional groups were consistent. Moreover, the XRD patterns noted in the current study confirmed the face-centered cubic crystalline nature of green AgNPs that were found to have a size of 20–30 nm [30].

As per the findings of our in vitro study, the maximum inhibition zone (24.32 mm) was recorded for the concentration of 0.6%, followed by 0.4% (19.63 mm), and 13.33 mm was recorded for the concentration of 0.2%. Moreover, glasshouse trials demonstrated that the consortium of green AgNPs (0.6% concentration) with Hj-2 and Hj-5 remained superior, with a disease incidence of 18.13%, and it was followed by the consortium of single rhizobacteria Hj-5 with 0.6% concentration of green AgNPs. The in vitro and glasshouse trial results of the current research corroborate with the previous findings, whereby a significant decline was noted in the growth of Xanthomonas campestris pv. vesicatoria by green-based nanoparticles [46]. The outcomes of our trials were also in agreement with the previous findings, which reported the positive effects of biosynthesized nanoparticles from the leaf extract of moringa in suppressing disease incidence [47]. Furthermore, the glasshouse study revealed that green AgNPs alone and in consortia with rhizobacterial isolates showed positive results against P. syringae pv. syringae. Moreover, it was noted that there existed some potential antagonism between Pseudomonas and Bacillus strains. It may be inferred that combining Bacillus and Pseudomonas strains may have resulted in reduced efficacy due to microbial antagonism, possibly driven by competition for root colonization niches, nutrient uptake, or production of antagonistic metabolites. These interactions are well-documented in microbial ecology, where strain incompatibility can hinder the stability and function of bio-inoculants [48]. While our study did not include a dedicated in vitro assay to assess this antagonism, the elevated disease incidence in the combined treatments implies suboptimal synergy or even conflict between the two genera. The research findings demonstrated that the consortium of green AgNPs, having a concentration of 0.6% with Hj-2 and Hj-5, showed a minimum disease incidence (18.13%), followed by the consortium of single rhizobacteria Hj-5 with 0.6% concentration of green AgNPs (21.33%). Previous findings also supported these results by reporting that the use of green culinary techniques proved superior in suppressing the disease incidence and severity, along with being cost-effective and environmentally friendly. However, an additional in vitro compatibility assay with the selected rhizobacterial strains could have strengthened the findings. But evidence from our glasshouse trials suggests that rhizobacterial strains, particularly Bacillus subtilis isolates Hj-2 and Hj-5, exhibited enhanced disease suppression when applied in consortium with 0.6% AgNPs, reducing disease incidence to as low as 18.13%. This outcome implies that the nanoparticles did not exert antagonistic effects on these Bacillus strains and instead may have had a synergistic or at least non-inhibitory role. Moreover, the observed incompatibility between B. subtilis and P. fluorescens, evidenced by higher disease incidence in co-inoculated treatments in our study, this phenomenon likely reflects antagonism due to competition for niche or resource, or possibly due to inhibitory metabolite exchange, which needs to be investigated in further in-depth studies. The outcomes of the present research were also confirmed by previously reported results where green nanoparticles (NPs) synthesized using plant extracts remained remarkably effective against bacterial diseases by inducing the hrpE gene response in plants, which significantly reduced the severity of the disease [48].

Conclusions

This study aimed to optimize the use of rhizobacteria and biogenic silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) for controlling P. syringae pv. Syringae, as this pathogen causes bacterial canker in plums. The results concerning rhizobia revealed that the rhizobacterial isolates, viz., Rw-1, Rw-6, Rw-7, and Hj-2, were Pseudomonas fluorescens, while Rw-12 and Hj-5 were identified as Bacillus subtilis. The biogenic AgNPs were prepared by using Cannabis sativa plant extract in different concentrations (0.2%, 0.4%, and 0.6%). An in vitro study demonstrated that the highest inhibition zone (24.32 mm) was noted for 0.6% AgNPs concentration, indicating the most effective and strongest antibacterial activity in comparison to other doses. Moreover, the glasshouse trial recorded the efficacy of a consortium of two strains of Bacillus with 0.6% AgNPs, which yielded the lowest disease incidence (18.13%), proving efficient disease suppression. Contrastingly, co-application of Bacillus and Pseudomonas strains with 0.6% AgNPs caused significantly higher disease incidence, probably owing to incompatibility among these bacterial genera. The research findings emphasize the potential of applying compatible rhizobacterial consortia and biogenic silver nanoparticles as good substitutes for synthetic pesticides in controlling plum bacterial canker. Moreover, the combined strategy not only presents a sustainable plant defense measure but also fits in with environmentally conscious agricultural practices. Furthermore, the results advocate the implementation of resistant cultivars and biocontrol agents in bacterial canker disease management programs that could potentially enhance plum production in bacterial canker-prone plum-growing regions globally. However, an additional in vitro compatibility assay with the selected rhizobacterial strains might also be conducted to strengthen the research findings, instead of relying on indirect evidence exhibited by Bacillus subtilis isolates (Hj-2 and Hj-5) applied in consortium with 0.6% AgNPs. Moreover, incompatibility between B. subtilis and P. fluorescens, evidenced by higher disease incidence in our study, reflects antagonism due to competition for niche or resource, or possibly due to inhibitory metabolite exchange, which needs to be investigated in further in-depth studies. Additionally, the research findings are novel and might be useful to plum growers on a large scale to improve fruit yield and quality by controlling bacterial canker in plum through biological means; however, future investigations may test the effects of AgNPs and biocontrol bacteria on plant physiology that may be warranted to better elucidate their potential application value. Furthermore, in the long run, further in-depth research trials based on the research findings of this trial concerning environmental risk assessment may further establish this biological control technique worldwide.
