Predictive value of the novel cardiometabolic index for reduced aerobic capacity in patients with coronary heart disease: a clinical comparative study of 378 cases
Authors: He Huang, Jiangwei Ma, Gaonian Zhao, Su Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary heart disease(CHD) is an important cause of cardiovascular diseases and, under adverse conditions such as arteriosclerosis, will raise the risk of death significantly. Due to the shortcomings of traditional adiposity indices when assessing aerobic capacity (AC), this paper intended to explore whether the novel cardiometabolic index (CMI) could predict lower AC in patients with coronary heart disease.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 378 hospitalized patients with coronary heart disease who underwent treatment and completed cardiopulmonary exercise testing. Patients were grouped according to AC levels and CMI levels, and the baseline characteristics of both groups were assessed.Three multivariable binary logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the associations of the CMI, body mass index (BMI), and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) with reduced AC. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess and compare the predictive performance of the three models, including the area under the curve (AUC). Model fit was further evaluated using the − 2 log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Calibration of each model was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and DeLong’s test was used to compare differences in AUC values between models.ResultsThree multivariable logistic regression models were constructed, each incorporating different core variables (BMI, WHtR, and CMI) along with other relevant covariates. Variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis confirmed the absence of significant multicollinearity among variables. Model performance evaluation demonstrated that Model 3, which included CMI, had the best fit, with the lowest − 2 log-likelihood, AIC, and BIC values, good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow test, P = 0.441), and the highest AUC (0.671, 95% CI: 0.605–0.736). However, DeLong’s test revealed that the difference in AUC between Model 3 and Model 1, as well as between Model 3 and Model 2, did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.062 and P = 0.110, respectively).ConclusionCMI demonstrates clinical potential in evaluating the decline of AC in patients with CHD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12872-026-06191-z.

Introduction

Coronary heart disease (CHD), one of the most prevalent cardiovascular diseases worldwide, is primarily characterized by coronary atherosclerosis, the key pathological basis of this disorder. Coronary atherosclerosis leads to vascular stenosis or occlusion, resulting in insufficient myocardial perfusion, ischemia, and hypoxia. Consequently, patients may present with severe clinical manifestations, including angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, and heart failure. CHD not only markedly impairs patients’ quality of life but also imposes a substantial medical and socioeconomic burden [1] .With population aging and the shifting trends in lifestyle, the incidence of CHD has been rising annually, making it a leading cause of mortality both in China and globally [2].

Current assessments for coronary heart disease primarily encompass two aspects: organic evaluation and functional evaluation. Organic evaluation focuses on structural, morphological, and histological changes of the heart, while functional evaluation includes parameters such as cardiac pumping efficiency, reserve function, and exercise tolerance. Among these, the cardiopulmonary exercise test (CPET) is a critical tool for assessing cardiac function. Aerobic capacity (AC) measured via CPET, which reflects cardiopulmonary fitness, is now widely recognized as one of the strongest independent predictors of cardiovascular disease and all-cause mortality. In 2016, the American Heart Association officially designated cardiopulmonary fitness as a clinical vital sign, emphasizing its pivotal role in risk stratification and clinical decision-making [3]. A landmark cohort study conducted by the Cleveland Clinic further demonstrated that a 1-unit increase in AC (measured in metabolic equivalents, MET) reduces all-cause mortality by 17% among patients with confirmed cardiovascular disease [4].

In recent years, obesity has been widely recognized as a major risk factor for CHD.In particular, the synergistic effect between abdominal obesity and dyslipidemia has been documented to be closely associated with the onset and progression of CHD [5].However, traditional anthropometric indices of obesity, such as body mass index (BMI) and Waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), have certain limitations in reflecting metabolic abnormalities and cardiopulmonary function in patients.As a novel comprehensive index that combines features of abdominal obesity and dyslipidemia, the cardiometabolic index (CMI) has attracted increasing attention in recent years.Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that CMI is superior to traditional single indices in evaluating metabolic syndrome and related cardiovascular risk, and can more comprehensively reflect an individual’s metabolic load and cardiac metabolic status [6].Therefore, exploring the clinical application value of CMI in patients with CHD, especially its predictive potential for the decline in AC, is of great clinical and theoretical significance.

Current studies on AC in patients with CHD have mainly focused on CPET and the assessment of relevant physiological parameters, yet a comprehensive evaluation system incorporating metabolic indicators remains lacking [7]. Although CPET allows direct evaluation of AC, its clinical application is restricted by complex procedures and insufficient patient compliance, thus limiting its widespread use [8].In contrast, the application of metabolic indicators provides new insights into the indirect assessment of AC. Specifically, combined index models combining both overweight/obese conditions and metabolic risk factors are expected to overcome shortcomings of a single-risk factor assessment. As a new indicator combining lipid metabolism and anthropometry features, CMI can better reflect the interaction among metabolic state, cardiorespiratory performance, etc., of patients with coronary heart disease at this time to predict declines in their aerobic endurance [6].

This study aims to investigate the association between CMI and impaired AC in patients with CHD. We hypothesize that, as a composite indicator integrating abdominal obesity and dyslipidemia, CMI exhibits superior predictive efficacy compared to traditional single obesity metrics for moderate to severe impairment of AC.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Sample size estimation

This study employed a binary logistic regression model for multivariate analysis, incorporating a total of 10 independent variables. According to the Events Per Variable criterion, each independent variable required at least 10 positive outcome events; consequently, a minimum requirement of 100 positive events was determined for this study. Based on pilot studies and prior literature [9], the estimated incidence rate of outcome events was 40%, corresponding to a theoretical minimum total sample size of 334 cases.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria(N = 496): (1) Adults aged 18–60 years old with coronary heart disease confirmed by clinical assessment and coronary angiography; (2) Complete cardiopulmonary exercise test conducted in the ward and a valid result obtained; (3) Both genders recruited; (4) No serious co-morbidity affecting cardiac function, including osteoarthropathy, chronic pulmonary infection, uncontrolled hypertension, malignancy, or other related diseases; (5) Available laboratory data such as blood lipids, blood sugar, blood pressure and other relevant examination reports.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with uncontrolled hypertension, valvular heart disease, or other conditions that might affect health status(N = 32);(2) Pregnant or lactating women, and patients with malignant tumors(N = 9);(3) Patients judged unsuitable for cardiopulmonary exercise testing by rehabilitation specialists due to unstable cardiac conditions, orthopedic limitations, or other contraindications(N = 61);(4) Patients unable to cooperate with the required examinations(N = 16).

As shown in Fig. 1, A total of 378 patients were included in the final cohort.Baseline demographic data and laboratory test results were collected from the hospital’s electronic medical record system, and cardiopulmonary exercise test data were obtained from the Cardiac Rehabilitation Center.

Data collection

Data collection, conducted at the Cardiac Rehabilitation Center of Taizhou People’s Hospital, covering the period from January 2024 to December 2025, will be initiated in February 2026.Collected data include general and anthropometric indicators: Gender, Height, Weight, Waist Circumference(WC), WHtR, BMI, Hypertension history, Diabetes history, smoking History, CMI, Total cholesterol (TC), Triglycerides (TG), High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), Statin use, and CPET results. Key cardiac-pulmonary function indicators were relative maximum oxygen uptake per kilogram (VO₂peak/kg) and forced vital capacity(FVC).

All anthropometric measurements were taken in the hospital’s HIS system. The measurement personnel need to follow the prescribed procedures for actual measurements at present; Cardiopulmonary exercise testing data were obtained at the cardiac rehabilitation centre; they have been qualified rehabilitation technicians to record.

Classification of AC

This study employed the Wasserman formula to calculate estimated peak oxygen uptake (male: 60 − 0.32 × age; female: 48 − 0.27 × age) and assessed the degree of impaired AC using the percentage difference between measured values and estimated values. Aerobic exercise capacity was classified based on the ratio of actual values to estimated values: ≥80% defined as normal, 65–79% as mild impairment, 50–64% as moderate impairment, and ≤ 49% as severe impairment.

Statistical analysis

Initially all the data were entered into Excel and analysed by SPSS version 27.0, etc. The overall missing rate of this dataset is less than 3%, with a low proportion of missing samples; statistical analysis was conducted after removing missing values using the list method. The measurement data were expressed in terms of mean ± SD, and pairwise comparison among more than two groups was performed using one-way ANOVA. Categorical data were presented as percentages; Between-group comparison used the χ2-test. To identify independent correlates of decreased aerobic capacity (AC), multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed. Given strong multicollinearity among the three adiposity indicators (BMI, WHtR and CMI), three separate regression models were established to avoid collinearity bias: Model 1: BMI was included as the primary exposure variable, adjusted for the full set of predefined covariates (age, sex, smoking history, hypertension history, diabetes history, LVEF, and statin use); Model 2: WHtR was included as the primary exposure variable, adjusted for the identical covariate set listed above; Model 3: CMI was included as the primary exposure variable, adjusted for the identical covariate set listed above.Odds ratios(ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals(95%CIs) were calculated for each model. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was then used to assess the predictive value of these indices (including CMI, BMI, and WHtR) for decreased AC. The area under the curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated for each indicator. The DeLong test was applied to compare statistical differences in AUC values among the three ROC curves.

Discussion

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical data of 378 patients with CHD to systematically examine whether the CMI could predict changes in AC. CMI is a multi-faceted index combining abdominal obesity and dyslipidaemia.We found that the CMI was independently associated with reduced AC. Although Model 3 (CMI) yielded numerically superior discrimination ability and model fitting statistics for predicting AC decline compared with the models containing single anthropometric indicators (BMI and WHtR), the inter-model differences in AUC did not achieve statistical significance.

Association between CMI and AC

Our baseline analysis revealed a clear dose-response relationship between CMI and AC.With an increase in CMI, there was a significant elevation in body weight, waist circumference, BMI, WHtR and TG among patients with CHD, and more frequent occurrence of hypertension and diabetes occurred accordingly. These findings show sensitivity of CMI to obesity and metabolic disorder. Meanwhile, this finding is consistent with previous studies that have linked CMI to various cardiovascular outcomes, including coronary artery disease severity, heart failure, and all-cause mortality. The observed trend of metabolic and functional decline with increasing CMI suggests that CMI may serve as a useful marker to identify patients at high risk of impaired cardiorespiratory fitness [10]. The underlying mechanisms may involve endothelial dysfunction, chronic low-grade inflammation, and vascular calcification, all of which are key pathophysiological processes linking cardiometabolic abnormalities to reduced exercise capacity [11]. A recent comprehensive review has systematically elucidated the integrative roles of osteoprotegerin in these cardiovascular pathophysiological processes, providing a molecular basis for the association between metabolic dysregulation and cardiac dysfunction observed in our study [12]. Nevertheless, the above mechanistic interpretations are only theoretical conjectures derived from published literature rather than definitive conclusions supported by our own experimental. Therefore, CMI has broader application prospects than solely assessing weight loss or fat reduction effects on metabolic markers in clinical diagnosis of CHD metabolic complications.

Lack of significant association of traditional cardiovascular risk factors

The study incorporated age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, smoking status, LVEF, and statin use as potential confounding factors—all well-established significant variables influencing AC in patients with CHD. AC declines physiologically with age, and inherent differences between males and females in cardiopulmonary structure, muscle mass, and metabolic profiles constitute the most fundamental demographic factors affecting aerobic performance [13]. Hypertension and diabetes, as the most common comorbidities of coronary heart disease, can lead to left ventricular hypertrophy and reduced diastolic function in chronic cases; diabetes may cause microvascular complications, myocardial energy metabolism disorders, and autonomic dysfunction, both of which directly impair cardiopulmonary function [14].Smoking compromises vascular endothelial function, accelerates atherosclerosis progression, and reduces pulmonary ventilation efficiency [15]. Statins, beyond their lipid-lowering effects, exhibit multiple benefits—including anti-inflammatory properties, improved endothelial function, and plaque stabilization—which may positively impact exercise capacity in CHD patients [16]. LVEF directly determines cardiac pumping ability and is one of the most critical determinants of AC [17]. However, multivariate analysis in this study revealed no statistically significant association among these traditional risk factors with moderate-to-severe declines in AC among CHD patients.

Predictive performance of CMI, BMI and WHtR

Multivariate binary logistic regression analysis confirmed that both BMI and CMI are independent predictors of moderate to severe decline in AC among patients with CHD, with CMI demonstrating superior predictive power compared to BMI. In a model fully adjusted for potential factors, each one-unit increase in CMI was associated with a 5.193-fold higher risk of impaired AC a magnitude far exceeding the 1.100-fold effect observed with BMI. This predictive advantage of CMI likely stems from its ability to integrate comprehensive information on body fat content, fat distribution, and metabolic dysfunction rather than merely reflecting obesity severity alone [18, 19]. Previous studies have established that obesity is a significant factor affecting exercise performance, as excessive body weight increases limb workload and cardiac effort, resulting in generally lower AC in obese individuals compared to those with normal weight, these findings further support this perspective [20]. In contrast, waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) exhibited only a marginally significant association (P = 0.053), failing to reach statistical significance, which may be attributed to heterogeneity in fat distribution: individuals with shorter stature may exhibit better core stability and superior lower limb biomechanical leverage under identical waist circumference conditions [21], thereby partially offsetting the negative impact of central obesity on AC. These findings support expanding the application of the CMI for predicting the risk of AC decline in patients with coronary heart disease.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations, which are primarily reflected in the following aspects. The retrospective nature and single-center design restrict the external validity of the findings and may introduce selection bias, and the cross-sectional design prevents us from establishing a definitive causal relationship between CMI and reduced AC. Additionally, our study population was restricted to patients who were able to complete CPET, which introduced notable selection bias that was predominantly physiological : patients with severe left ventricular dysfunction, unstable angina, life-threatening arrhythmias, or other absolute contraindications to exercise testing were systematically excluded, resulting in a relatively healthier and more homogeneous cohort of CHD patients. This selection process may have compressed the distribution of traditional cardiovascular risk factors and attenuated their associations with AC observed in our study. Furthermore, although we adjusted for a comprehensive set of potential confounders, residual confounding due to unmeasured variables—such as the anatomical extent of coronary artery disease, history of revascularization, dosage and duration of beta-blocker therapy, and objective measures of habitual physical activity levels—cannot be entirely excluded. Given the retrospective design and lack of external validation, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that CMI has advantages in predicting the decline of AC in patients with CHD, when compared to BMI and WHtR.These results are applicable to similar patients with CHD at local tertiary hospitals. Further large-scale, multicenter prospective cohort studies with long-term follow-up are warranted to confirm these observations and establish the clinical applicability of CMI as a simple, non-invasive screening tool for impaired cardiorespiratory fitness in this patient population.
