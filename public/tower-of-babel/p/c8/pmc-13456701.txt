Novel minimally invasive management of gastric–diaphragmatic–pleural–bronchial fistula following sleeve gastrectomy: a case report
Authors: Zhiyuan Wang, Rongjun Nie, Di Long, Yilei Liang, Yebiao Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Gastro-pleuro-bronchial fistula is a rare but severe complication following bariatric surgery. This report highlights a novel, minimally invasive, staged interventional approach for managing such complex fistulas when conventional treatments fail.Case presentation:An 18-year-old female with metabolic syndrome developed a gastro-diaphragmatic-pleural-bronchial fistula after laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, presenting with persistent vomiting, coughing, and sputum production. Imaging and endoscopy confirmed the diagnosis.Clinical discussion:After unsuccessful surgical revisions and conservative therapy, a stepwise interventional strategy was employed. First, a gastrostomy balloon was placed for temporary fistula occlusion and drainage. Subsequently, the abdominal fistula tract was embolized using spring coils and a tissue glue-iodized oil mixture to seal the bronchial communication. Final definitive closure was achieved with endoscopic titanium clip suturing.Conclusion:This case demonstrates the successful management of a complex post-bariatric fistula through a tailored, multi-stage interventional radiology and endoscopic approach. It presents a viable and less invasive alternative to repeated major surgery in selected cases.

Introduction

Sleeve gastrectomy is widely performed in bariatric surgery, with postoperative fistulas occurring in approximately 0.3–0.5% of cases[1]. While most fistulas are localized, extension into the thoracic cavity, including gastropleural or gastrobronchial fistulas, is rare and challenging to manage. Minimally invasive approaches, including interventional radiology and endoscopic techniques, are increasingly used as alternatives to surgery[2].

However, fistulas simultaneously involving the stomach, diaphragm, pleural cavity, and bronchial tree are extremely uncommon. Their complex anatomical communication and chronic inflammatory environment make treatment difficult, particularly after failed surgical repair. We report a rare case of a gastric–diaphragmatic–pleural–bronchial fistula (GDPBF) successfully managed using a staged minimally invasive strategy.

Case presentation

An 18-year-old female with metabolic syndrome (BMI 35.49 kg/m2) underwent laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy in July 2020. She had no prior abdominal surgery, chronic diseases, or long-term medication use. There was no relevant family history of gastrointestinal or metabolic disorders. She denied smoking, alcohol consumption, or substance abuse, and no drug or food allergies were reported. Two months after surgery, she developed persistent vomiting without abdominal pain or fever. Six months later, she presented with recurrent cough, purulent sputum, and intermittent fever. Laboratory tests revealed leukocytosis (12.5 × 109/L), elevated C-reactive protein (50 mg/L), hypoalbuminemia, and severe anemia (hemoglobin 33.2 g/L), suggesting chronic infection and malnutrition. Initial bronchoscopy and upper gastrointestinal contrast studies suggested a fundic–bronchial fistula. However, subsequent fistulography through the abdominal wall tract, combined with CT and endoscopy, demonstrated that contrast passed from the gastric lumen through the subdiaphragmatic region, across the diaphragm, into the pleural cavity, and finally into the bronchial tree. These findings confirmed the diagnosis of GDPBF. Initial imaging and endoscopic findings are shown in Figure 1A–C. Contrast studies demonstrated communication between the gastric lumen and the thoracic cavity, while endoscopy revealed a fistulous orifice. Alternative diagnoses, including isolated gastrobronchial fistula, gastropleural fistula, bronchopleural fistula, and postoperative abscess, were considered. The patient underwent three surgical attempts between March 2021 and February 2022, all of which failed due to persistent infection, fibrosis, and poor tissue healing. A multidisciplinary team established a staged treatment strategy based on infection control, pressure reduction, tract isolation, and definitive closure.

In stage 1, balloon occlusion and external drainage were performed to reduce intragastric pressure and control infection. Fistulography demonstrated the complex tract involving gastric, diaphragmatic, and bronchial components (Fig. 2A). Balloon occlusion and external drainage were established to reduce pressure and control infection (Fig. 2B). Immediate closure was avoided due to active inflammation. In stage 2, interventional embolization was performed 7 days later. Fistulography confirmed the persistence of the bronchial tract, which was considered the main source of respiratory contamination. Therefore, the bronchial component was treated first. Five platinum coils were deployed, followed by the injection of a tissue adhesive–iodized oil mixture (1:3 ratio) under fluoroscopic guidance, achieving effective sealing of the tract (Fig. 2C).

In stage 3, after adequate infection control and stabilization of the fistula tract, endoscopic closure was performed. The internal opening of the gastric fistula was closed using titanium clips combined with full-thickness sutures, achieving complete closure (Fig. 3A). Follow-up imaging at 6 months demonstrated complete resolution of the fistula, with no residual communication between the gastric lumen and thoracic structures (Fig. 3B). Long-term follow-up imaging and endoscopy further confirmed sustained closure, with normal contrast passage and no evidence of recurrence (Fig. 3C and D).

Discussion

The present case illustrates an unusual and highly complex form of gastric fistula in the chest after sleeve gastrectomy. Unlike previously reported gastrobronchial or gastropleural fistulas, this patient developed a fistulous communication involving the gastric lumen, diaphragm, pleural cavity, bronchial tree, and abdominal wall. The condition was further complicated by recurrent infections, tissue scarring, and the failure of multiple surgical repairs, which made conventional surgical closure difficult.

This case illustrates several important considerations in the management of complex post-bariatric fistulas.

First, the staged strategy was based on clinical decision-making rather than a purely procedural sequence. Immediate closure was avoided because active infection, edema, and poor nutritional status are known to increase the risk of failure. Instead, the initial phase focused on drainage and decompression, followed by tract isolation and delayed definitive closure under improved conditions. This approach reflects a principle-based strategy tailored to the patient’s condition.

Second, the sequence of intervention was clinically justified. The bronchial tract was embolized first because it contributed most to respiratory symptoms and ongoing pulmonary contamination. Early isolation of the thoracic component reduced the aspiration risk and facilitated subsequent treatment.

Third, the combination of mechanical embolization (coils) and biological sealing (adhesive–iodized oil mixture) provided both structural support and effective closure. Iodized oil improved radiopacity and delayed polymerization, allowing controlled delivery under fluoroscopic guidance. Similar adhesive-based techniques have been used in gastrointestinal fistulas[3], but their application in this anatomical context remains limited.

Compared with conventional surgical approaches, such as Roux-en-Y reconstruction, total gastrectomy, or thoracoabdominal procedures[4,5], the present minimally invasive strategy may offer advantages, including reduced surgical trauma, shorter recovery time, and preservation of anatomical structures. However, it is not universally applicable. Suitable patients may include those with controlled infection, well-defined fistula tracts, and a high risk for repeat surgery.

This case also highlights why prior surgical treatments failed. Chronic infection, fibrosis, impaired vascularity, and persistent multicompartment communication limited the success of direct repair. The staged approach addressed these issues by separating stabilization, isolation, and closure phases.

Potential risks include incomplete embolization, adhesive migration, embolic complications, and recurrence. Careful imaging guidance, patient selection, and follow-up are essential to minimize these risks.

Emerging digital tools, including large language models, may assist in the management of rare conditions by supporting literature synthesis, clinical communication, and multidisciplinary decision-making[6]. However, such tools should complement rather than replace clinical expertise, particularly in complex interventional cases.

Finally, the novelty of this approach should be interpreted cautiously. Although the combination and sequencing of techniques are distinctive, similar components have been reported individually. The main contribution of this case lies in the integrated, staged strategy rather than a single novel technique.

Conclusion

This case report suggests that a carefully planned staged intervention involving spring coils, tissue adhesive, and an iodized oil mixture may be a feasible, minimally invasive option for the management of GDPBF. This strategy may provide an effective alternative for selected patients with complex fistulas who are poor candidates for repeat major surgery. However, as this report is based on a single case with limited follow-up, conclusions regarding long-term efficacy, safety, and reproducibility should be interpreted with caution. Further clinical experience and longer follow-up are needed to better define its role in complex post-bariatric fistula management.
