Biomimetic tri-layered osteochondral scaffold
Authors: Manuela Salerno, Alessandro Di Martino, Elisabetta Galassi, Laura Grillini, Alessandro Dotti, Claudio De Luca, Giuseppe Filardo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe aim of this study was to investigate a biomimetic biodegradable cell-free osteochondral scaffold in a clinically relevant large animal model to quantify the early integration and regeneration phases after implantation.MethodsBilateral critical-sized osteochondral defects were created in 14 sheep. One defect was filled with the scaffold, whereas the contralateral was left empty. The graft integration and regeneration tissue quality were evaluated at 14 and 30 days of follow-up. In particular, samples were evaluated for inflammation grade according to the ISO 10993-6 international standard biological evaluation of medical devices, macroscopic appearance graded according to a modified Fortier et al scoring system, histological quality according to a modified International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) II score, and the new trabecular bone formation with a micro-CT analysis.ResultsAt 14 days, a higher defect filling was found in the scaffold group compared to the control in the gross analysis (p < 0.001). Low inflammation was observed in both groups, with a slight increase in the scaffold group at 30 days. The scaffold group had better histological scores, with a significantly higher mean global ICRS II score at 30 days compared to controls (p = 0.031). Finally, the mean percentage of new trabecular bone (BV/TV) was significantly higher in the scaffold group versus the control group, both at 14 days (11.5% (SD 4.5) and 5.2% (SD 2.3), respectively; p = 0.005) and at 30 days (28.0% (SD 8.1) and 8.8% (SD 5.0), respectively; p = 0.002).ConclusionThe tri-layered biomaterial showed good integration with surrounding tissues and new tissue growth promotion after 14 and 30 days post-surgery, with no safety concerns. In light of the development of appropriate postoperative rehabilitation programmes to prevent the risk of early implant failure while avoiding delays and providing a proper recovery path, this study provides support for the new cartilage field trend for this osteochondral scaffold, suggesting the possibility of safely shortening the postoperative rehabilitation phase after surgery.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2025;14(11):953–968.Keywords: Cartilage lesions, Osteochondral, Scaffold integration, Regeneration, Rehabilitation, cartilage tissues, trabecular bone, inflammation, osteochondral defects, Sheep, histological scores, Animal models, Micro-CT, implant failure, biomaterials

Introduction

Lesions of the articular knee surface are common and debilitating conditions, being a major cause of patient pain, stiffness, limited range and level of movements, and dysfunction.1,2 They may also progress to more extensive joint damage with an increased risk for osteoarthritis,3-5 thereby incurring healthcare and society-related costs.6,7 Given the inadequate self-regeneration ability of the native tissues,8 the clinical management of chondral and even more osteochondral lesions is one of the most challenging issues for orthopaedic surgeons. Beyond different conservative or surgical approaches currently suggested for the treatment of these lesions,9-11 multiphasic new-generation scaffolds have been developed and proposed as effective strategies to restore the entire osteochondral unit.12,13 These biomaterials have been designed to mimic the hierarchical layered structure of the osteochondral unit while also resembling the composition of the extracellular matrix (ECM) of the native tissues, thanks to the presence of different layers with different organic and mineral compositions.13,14

Among the available osteochondral scaffold, currently the most used clinically is a biomimetic biodegradable cell-free implant, morphologically graded thanks to the presence of three layers with different collagen–hydroxyapatite ratios.15 Patients treated with this scaffold have shown considerable improvement in clinical scores, with satisfactory results stable over time.16,17 However, one major concern with the use of these devices aimed at tissue regeneration is graft detachment in its early integration phase, which can lead to implant failure. In this light, deeper investigation into the early phases after implantation in an appropriate model could be of clinical relevance by providing insights into the graft integration process, thus allowing for better management of patients postoperatively, reducing the risk of early implant detachment and failure. Animal models are helpful in this case, as they prevent patients from being subjected to invasive methods for the postoperative evaluation of the implant attachment, like biopsies – which are not appropriate for routine clinical evaluations – and also allow a quantitative analysis of the quality of the regenerated tissue.

The aim of this study was therefore to evaluate the early integration phases of a biomimetic scaffold implanted in critical-sized osteochondral defects in a sheep model.

Methods

Scaffold preparation

The osteochondral scaffold (Fin-Ceramica Faenza S.p.A., Italy) is made of three layers with different ratios of organic/mineralized compounds: a smooth upper 100% type I collagen cartilage-like layer (1.5 mm thick, according to the physiological sheep cartilage);18 an intermediate tidemark-like layer made of a combination of type I collagen and HA substitute with Mg (Mg-HA) in a nominal ratio 60%/40% (2 mm thick); and a lower bone-like layer (2 mm) consisting of a mineralized blend of type I collagen and Mg-HA in a nominal ratio of 30%/70%. The type I collagen (Opocrin S.p.A, Italy) was isolated from equine tendons and reproduced the organic matrix, whereas Mg-HA reproduced the mineralized matrix. The chemical and physical composition of the scaffold mimics the composition and structure of the osteochondral unit ECM to promote osteochondral lesion regeneration. The layers were synthesized separately using a standardized process starting from an atelocollagen aqueous solution (1%, w/w) in acetic acid. The mineralized layers were obtained by nucleating bone-like nanostructured Mg-HA into self-assembling collagen fibres, reproducing the natural ossification process.19 The self-assembled fibres were then chemically crosslinked at room temperature (RT) by BDDGE (1,4-butanediol diglycidyl ether) (Merck, Germany) to stabilize the scaffold, physically combined, freeze-dried, and gamma-sterilized at 25 kGy.

Animals and study design

A total of 14 healthy skeletally mature female sheep (aged 38.0 to 67.0 months, weight 55.4 to 88.9 kg) were used in this study conducted at the Charles River Laboratories Montreal ULC (Canada). The animals were group-housed and allowed to acclimate in the testing facilities, and had access to hay, food, and water. Animal care and all experimental procedures were conducted in Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) compliance and in accordance with ASTM F2451-0520 and ISO 10993-6 Standard.21 Animal care and surgery were reviewed and approved by the Testing Facility’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), in compliance with Canadian Council on Animal Care regulations.22 We have adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines and have supplied the ARRIVE checklist, which is included in the Supplementary Material.

Bilateral osteochondral critical-sized defects (7 mm diameter × 5.5 mm depth, approximately) were created in the trochlear groove of the operated knee joint. In one knee, the defect was filled with the scaffold (scaffold group), and the contralateral defect was left empty and used as negative control (control group). Sheep were assigned to two cohorts of seven animals each, one for the 14-day and one for the 30-day evaluation, for a total of 14 defects analyzed at each follow-up.

Surgical procedure and osteochondral defect creation

These methods have been already reported in a previous paper.23 Briefly, an antibiotic (cefazolin 25 mg/kg; Fresenius Kabi, Germany) was administered before the surgeries, and animals were then placed in dorsal recumbency and anaesthetized by an intravenous administration of midazolam 0.5 mg/kg (Sandoz), ketamine 5 mg/kg (Vetoquinol), and glycopyrrolate 0.01 mg/kg (Sandoz). During the procedures, a manual or mechanical ventilation with isoflurane in O2 was ensured. The hind limbs were flexed and medial parapatellar skin incisions of ~5 cm were made. After blunt and sharp dissection of the subcutaneous tissues, a medial arthrotomy was performed in the knees and the patella moved laterally to expose the trochlear groove. A customized instrumentation kit (Fin-Ceramica Faenza S.p.A) was used to create the osteochondral defects. In particular, a pin (Kirschner wire (K-wire)) was inserted perpendicular at about 0.7 to 0.8 cm from the cartilage surface, guiding both a circular scalpel used to sharply separate the cartilage surface of the defect from the surrounding cartilage and a reamer used to prepare the osteochondral lesion and achieve the required depth. Debris surrounding the defect area were removed by flushing the joint with saline, then four microperforations were created at the bottom of the defects using a diameter of 0.6 mm wire surrounding the central pinhole to allow bone bleeding. The implant was inserted into the defect by press-fitting with the bony layer facing down (scaffold group) or the defects were left empty (control group). After intraoperative implant stability verification with range of motion cycles was performed, routine closure of the joint capsule, subcutaneous tissues, and skin was completed.

Postoperative procedures

An intramuscular analgesic (duplocillin 1 ml/20 kg) was administered during recovery from anaesthesia, and antibiotics (carprofen 3 to 5 mg/kg; both Merck) were administered two and four days postoperatively. After surgery, the sheep were housed in smaller enclosures to prevent brisk movements or activity (i.e. running) and damage to the surgical sites. Limbs were evaluated and assessed once a week until wound healing. When required, the wound was cleaned with chlorhexidine. After 14 or 30 days, animals were anaesthetized by an intravenous injection of a cocktail of midazolam (0.5 mg/kg; Sandoz), ketamine (5 mg/kg; Vetoquinol), and glycopyrrolate (0.01 mg/kg; Sandoz), then euthanized by an injection of pentobarbital (Euthanyl). The stifle joints of both treatment groups were then harvested and analyzed in terms of macroscopic and histological appearance, presence of implant debris in the synovial space, inflammation grade, and trabecular bone formation.

Macroscopic assessment

The implant sites were evaluated grossly by an independent pathologist (from Charles River Laboratories). During the examination of the operated sites, the following parameters were evaluated: colour and quality of synovial fluid and appearance of the synovial membrane, presence or absence of fibrillation of the native cartilage, presence or absence of osteophytes, description of the colour and quality of the repair site tissue, and extent of implant integration. The quality of healing was assessed using a modified scoring system developed by Fortier et al,24 as shown in Table I.

To check the presence of calcium phosphate fragments derived from the implant, the synovial fluid from the femorotibial joint space was collected in sodium heparin tubes. Then, a few drops were placed on a slide and stained with Alizarin Red S, as indicated by the presence of orange-red stained hydroxyapatite clumps. The test was considered as strongly positive with stained clumps widely distributed and present in every high-power field or many clumps among fibrils or small clots, weakly positive with scattered clumps among fibrils, and negative with no clumps in any field.

Histological analysis

The samples were histologically evaluated to assess inflammation and tissue repair quality. In detail, the trochleae were sectioned longitudinally in two halves by sawing in the middle of the implant/defect site along its longitudinal axis. One was subjected to decalcification in formic acid for three weeks, followed by paraffin-embedding, cutting in 5 µm thin sections, and then staining with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Safranin-O Fast Green (SO), and Toluidine Blue (TB). The other one was placed in methyl methacrylate (MMA) for plastic embedding, then cut into 6 to 10 µm thin sections and subjected to H&E and TB staining.

The medial and lateral synovial membranes from both stifle joints were trimmed individually, processed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at ~5 μm, and stained with H&E. Organ samples (hearts, kidney, spleen, lungs, liver, and lymph nodes) and macroscopic observation were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 µm, stained with H&E, and examined by the independent pathologist (from Charles River Laboratories) semiquantitatively. In detail, the following sections were processed histologically and examined: heart: three sections (left ventricle with atria; right ventricle with atria; septum); kidneys: one transverse section of each kidney (mid portion); spleen: one transverse section (mid portion); lungs: one segment each from right middle lobe and left caudal lobe; total of two lung samples; liver: one section from the left lobe; popliteal lymph nodes (right and left): draining lymph nodes, submitted in individual cassettes; sublumbar lymph nodes (right and left): possible draining lymph nodes, submitted in individual cassettes; and axillary lymph nodes (right and left): distant lymph nodes, submitted in individual cassettes.

The inflammation present within the entire defect area and within the surrounding tissue was graded in accordance with ISO 10993-6 international standard biological evaluation of medical device current edition (Table II).25 The irritancy/reactivity score was calculated as follows for each defect site: Irritancy score = (Polymorphonuclear cells + Lymphocytes + Plasma Cells + Macrophages + Giant cells + Necrosis) × 2 + (Neovascularization + Fibrosis + Fatty infiltrate). The total irritancy score for each implanted or empty defect was then calculated by averaging (mean) the irritancy scores of each test article or empty defect site, respectively: irritancy/reactivity score = test Article irritancy score – empty defect irritancy score.

The tissue repair quality was assessed using a modified International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) II histological profile according to Mainil-Varlet et al26 and Kon et al27 (Table III). The stained sections were scored from 0% to 100% based on 14 parameters evaluated within the defect area and the surrounding cartilage tissue. Afterward, each score parameter was averaged, and the Global Histological Score was calculated as the mean of all 14 parameters. Both decalcified and non-decalcified slides were scored, where TB (and SO, if present) staining was used for the matrix evaluation only, while the H&E-stained slides were used for all other parameters. Also, the inflammation present within the superficial layer of the defect area was graded according to the ICRS II histological score.

Micro-CT

These methods have been reported in a previous paper.23 Briefly, a micro-CT analysis was used to quantify the amount of newly formed trabecular bone at the defect site using a Skyscan 1272 (resolution 10.8 µm; Bruker, USA). Samples were scanned at RT and then placed back in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) to obtain projection images, which were then processed with the CT Pro 3D software (Nikon Metrology Europe NV, Belgium) to obtain a floating points 3D model. For each defect, the trabecular bone volume (BV/TV, %) was calculated as follows: the ratio between the volume of trabecular bone measured in volumes of interest (VOI) and the total volume of the VOI considered, where the VOI was the area of the original defect, and all the new bone within the VOI was included in the measurement of BV/TV.

Statistical analysis

These methods have been reported in a previous paper.23 Briefly, the statistical analyses were conducted using the SigmaPlot 14.0 software (Grafiti LLC, USA). A paired t-test was used to compare continuous variables with normal distribution, whereas a Wilcoxon matched pairs signed rank test was used to compare variables with a non-normal distribution. The results were reported as mean (SD), and were considered to be statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

No major complications or adverse events occurred during the surgeries, and all animals survived until the scheduled kill. No health-related issues were observed clinically, and no histopathological findings were noted across both cohorts and in all animals.

Macroscopic evaluation

At 14 days, the surface texture of the repair tissue was graded as less than 50% normal in all animals of both groups, with abnormal findings often noted in the surrounding cartilage (e.g. slight depressions, irregular surfaces). There was a higher defect filling in the scaffold group compared to the control defect group, with 7/7 with 100% filling and 7/7 with less than 50% filling, respectively (p < 0.001). In the scaffold group, there were no gaps between the repair tissue within the defect and the surrounding cartilage, but a clear demarcation in colour and texture was present. More variability in the colour of the scaffold group defects (varied from pink to red to tan, often in combination) with a cobblestone texture was seen, whereas the control group defects were more consistently mottled red (or dark red)/tan (or pale tan) with a smooth texture (Figure 1). At 30 days, the surface texture of the repair tissue was graded as less than 50% normal in all animals of both groups, with abnormal findings often noted in the surrounding cartilage (e.g. slight depressions, irregular surfaces) in both groups. Most of the defects had less than 50% of the area of the surface defect filled in both groups. In the scaffold group, a clear demarcation in colour and texture of the repair tissue was seen, but no gaps between the repair tissue and the surrounding cartilage were noted. The colour of the repair tissue in both groups varied between tan, red, and brown, single or in combination. The presence of osteophytes was noted in 3/7 scaffold group defects and 2/7 control group defects. The presence of fibrillation of surrounding native cartilage was not noted in any of the scaffold-treated defects, and in only 2/7 empty defects (Figure 1). No differences between the groups related to the synovial fluid and synovial membrane were found both at 14 and 30 days. Most fluids were low viscosity and pale, straw in colour, and the membranes were pale pink, smooth, and with a normal thickness. All evaluated synovial fluid across all cohorts in both groups was graded as negative in the Alizarin S Red analysis, indicating no evidence of HA crystal/implant debris present in the synovial fluid (data not shown).

Inflammation evaluation

According to the analysis of the histological sections following the ISO 10993-6 current edition, overall low levels of inflammation were observed at 14 days in both groups within and in close association with the entire defect area. Slightly more giant cells were found in the scaffold group compared to the control in close association with the implant. Both groups presented signs of necrosis, with necrotic adipocytes surrounded by living marrow cells in the bone marrow surrounding the defect, while in the control group defect regions, necrotic adipocytes were also present within the defect area (Figure 2). In the control group, there was also an increased fibrosis and fatty infiltrate compared to the scaffold group. At 30 days, an overall increased number of inflammatory cells was observed in the implanted defects, in particular of lymphocytes. More giant cells were also present in the scaffold group in close association with the implant. The scores for necrosis, neovascularization, and fatty infiltrate were similar in the two groups. In both groups, necrotic adipocytes surrounded by living marrow cells were observed within the bone marrow surrounding the defect, whereas the control group presented increased fibrosis compared to the scaffold group. As shown by the histological analysis of 14-day trochlea samples (Figure 2), there was increased vascularization as well as a trend towards decreased structural integrity with surrounding tissue and towards increased inflammation in the scaffold group compared to the control. Although an overall small amount of new bone formation was observed in both groups with similar scores, there was a slight trend toward more new bone formation in the scaffold group, also together with a better score for subchondral bone abnormalities. The repair tissue was higher at the centre of the defect and higher filling of the defect in the scaffold group compared to the control, whereas the amount of new bone formation and the filling of the defect with new bone was similar between the groups. The amount of defect filling with new bone tissue was also similar between the groups. In the scaffold group, the majority of the implant in the superficial and intermediate layers was present, whereas signs of bioresorption of the lower layer were found.

Compared to the control group at 30 days (Figure 3), the scaffold group samples had a better score for basal integration with subjacent bone, as well as for subchondral bone abnormalities. The scaffold group also had a higher vascularization and a higher inflammation than controls. Only the control group presented occasional granulomas associated with necrotic adipocytes within the defect area. Both groups presented low levels of fibrin and a small amount of haemorrhage. The amount of new bone formation and the filling of the defect with bone, as well as the osseointegration at the bottom of the defect, were higher in the scaffold group compared to the control. The repair tissue at the centre of the defects tended to be higher in the scaffold group, whereas the filling of the defect area was relatively similar between the two groups. At 30 days, a better score for basal integration with subjacent tissue and a better score for subchondral bone abnormalities were observed in the scaffold group compared to the control group. Moreover, a higher vascularization and a higher inflammation with an overall increased number of inflammatory cells were also seen in the implanted group. Moderate amounts of residual implant were found in the superficial layer and less in the intermediate and lower layers. More giant cells were present in the treatment group than in the control, and in close association with the implant. The scores for necrosis, neovascularization, and fatty infiltrate were similar between the groups. In both groups, the bone marrow surrounding the defect area presented necrotic adipocytes (surrounded by living marrow cells), and a small level of haemorrhage was found. Only the control group had an increased fibrosis and occasional granulomas associated with necrotic adipocytes within the defect area. The amount of new bone formation and the filling of the defect with bone was higher in the scaffold group compared to the control, and the scaffold group also had higher osseointegration at the bottom of the defect.

The histological findings within all other non-target tissues and organs were barely perceptible microscopically and were considered as clinically not relevant, incidental, a peri-mortem change, and/or of the nature commonly observed in this strain and age of sheep, and therefore were considered not specific to the implantation of the scaffold.

Micro-CT

At both follow-ups, the percentage of trabecular bone (BV/TV) was significantly higher in the implanted defects compared to the control empty defects. In detail, the mean BV/TV was 11.5% (SD 4.5) and 5.2% (SD 2.3) at 14 days (p = 0.005), and 28.0% (SD 8.1) and 8.8% (SD 5.0) at 30 days (p = 0.002), respectively. The growth of new trabecular bone in the scaffold group was observed across the entire bony defect area, with good filling, unlike empty defects in which small amounts of trabecular bone were visible only at the defect edges (Figure 5).

Discussion

The main finding of this study is that this biomimetic tri-layered scaffold showed good integration with surrounding tissues already at very short follow-up, with no safety concerns. Importantly, compared to empty controls, the scaffold induced a significantly higher trabecular bone formation at 14 and 30 days, as well as a significantly better quality of the newly formed cartilage at 30 days.

This biomimetic scaffold is one of the most widely used in clinical practice, with good clinical results over time.16,17 However, although several studies reported an overall low number of failures17,28 unlike other techniques,29-31 the possibility of early implant detachment is a possible risk.32 Moreover, most of the analyzed studies described a slow scaffold maturation process.16 The surgical technique has been improved over time to guarantee a more stable implant,33 although some concerns remain in relying only on the press-fit fixation.34 This could impact the stability of the scaffold/host tissues interface and the quality of the regenerated tissues, especially in the first phases after implantation,35,36 concerns which influenced patient management during the first months post-surgery. An appropriate rehabilitation programme is essential to optimize the results and accelerate the return to sport with reduced risk of re-injury.37 However, it is still unclear which timepoint is safest to allow full weightbearing on the treated joint with a full range of motion, and protocols are cautionary as for other regenerative surgical approaches.38,39 The scaffold used in this study foresees no weightbearing and a joint mobilization only with a continuous passive motion machine for the first month, then only a weight touchdown with crutches is allowed until progressive moving to full weightbearing. The full joint impact activities can be resumed one year post-surgery, thus impacting patients’ lives for a long time.40

Investigating the early phases after implantation can help to understand the graft integration and regeneration processes, guiding the definition of an optimal postoperative rehabilitation protocol.41 A few data are available for autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI), indicating that the first weeks of the healing process are key for primitive cell response and tissue filling of the defect.39,42 However, no studies to date have defined a timeline for the earlier phases of osteochondral scaffold integration. MRI represents the method of choice for non-invasive follow-up of patients,43-45 but the most detailed morphological information useful to evaluate the success and outcome of the regeneration requires a deeper analysis. The evaluation of the quality of the newly formed tissue can be assessed by histological analyses, which allows the quantification of the overall tissue quality, the cell morphology, the presence of abnormal calcification, the bone-cartilage interface, and the presence and location of proteoglycans and glycosaminoglycans within the new repair tissue.46-48 Moreover, micro-CT evaluations can help to document and quantify integration and bone tissue formation of the implanted scaffolds.

In this light, animal models represent an important tool to study the early implant maturation.49,50 The trochlear groove showed to be an appropriate defect site in this animal model for osteochondral defect repair.51 In fact, sheep are quadrupeds and possess different gait patterns and knee resting positions than bipeds,52 and cannot be subjected to restricted loading or non-weightbearing as it is required by rehabilitation in humans. Therefore, implantation in the trochlea helps in the simulation of the device’s postoperative restricted load conditions.53 In this study, bilateral defects were created in the trochlear groove, and the early scaffold maturation was evaluated compared to empty defect controls at 14- and 30-day follow-ups to study the first delicate phase of the scaffold integration process.

This early stability study documented several important findings that could not be identified by previous studies focusing on six- to 12-month evaluations, including the first host response to an implanted device: the initial inflammatory response.54 Overall, low levels of inflammation were seen after 14 days in both groups, with a moderately increased number of inflammatory cells seen in the scaffold group at the 30-day follow-up, which can be considered as a sign of the healing process. In fact, the complex interactions between the immune cells and the cells involved in tissue repair, like osteoblasts and osteoclasts, are now known to be essential for tissue formation, remodelling, and repair.55 More giant cells were also noted at 30 days in close association with the implant. Accordingly, the residual intermediate and lower scaffold layers at this endpoint were lower compared to the first follow-up, indicating that the implant resorption was in progress. The macroscopic grading of the defect areas showed a higher level of defect filling in the implanted groups at 14 days due to the scaffold, which remained stable in the implantation sites and already showed signs of integration. Moreover, at the 30-day follow-up, the scaffold led to a better basal integration with subjacent bone, a lower level of subchondral bone abnormalities, improved vascularization, and higher inflammation, with the global ICRS II score being significantly higher in the scaffold group compared to the control group. These findings indicate that the scaffold maturation process at 30 days results in a partial degradation of the scaffold, which is replaced by a new tissue with good quality and no sign of implant dislocation. These findings were confirmed by the quantification of the newly formed bone tissue through a 3D micro-CT analysis. Subchondral bone maturation is of key importance in osteochondral defects, also in light of some clinical findings showing subchondral bone signal abnormalities.40 This study was able to document a significantly higher percentage of trabecular bone after 14 days, which was confirmed at 30 days, whereas only small amounts of trabecular bone were visible at the empty defect edges.

The results of this study indicate that the healing process induced by this biomimetic tri-layered scaffold starts in the first month post-surgery, both in terms of new cartilage and new subchondral bone tissues, with the implant showing early signs of integration. This offers precious insights to be considered when deciding the rehabilitation protocols regarding physical and activity restrictions, adding important information to controversial literature. Previous studies suggested that the early resumption of a full range of motion can improve cartilage healing and that physical exercise increases bone and muscle mass, while reduced loading and long inactivity, like in cases of prolonged postoperative immobilization, are associated with cartilage atrophy.56-59 Interestingly, the post-cartilage surgery rehabilitation protocols are evolving, with new trends encouraging the acceleration of weightbearing and activity after knee cartilage surgery.37,60,61 The goal of postoperative rehabilitation is the restoration of optimal joint function, which can be achieved via a gradual and progressive programme that emphasizes full motion, progressive loading, and controlled exercises but with a timeline that depends on the patient’s individuality, the defect site and size, the kind of surgical procedure, the joint biomechanics and, of course, the maturation phases of the implant. This study provides for the first time specific findings on this osteochondral scaffold, demonstrating early regeneration processes and implant integration from the first phase, helping clinicians to tailor proper rehabilitation protocols towards patient recovery.

The results of this study are subject to some limitations. First, the choice of the animal model has intrinsic limits, like different loading conditions due to anatomical and biomechanical joint conditions of quadrupeds. Then, the control group foresaw empty controls, whereas the comparison of two groups with different weightbearing protocol conditions would have given information about the different effects of loading on scaffold maturation. Moreover, this study did not focus on innovative changes in scaffold technology and composition but rather investigated a specific critical phase of an osteochondral scaffold implantation. In this regard, this study shows for the first time data on the early regeneration processes, documenting good scaffold stability and integration starting from the first weeks post-implantation. This is of particular clinical relevance as it supports the field trends on the possibility of reducing the postoperative rehabilitation phase after cartilage surgery, which is still a debated issue. To this aim, further studies could also focus on the biomechanical property changes in the different maturation phases, to characterize the evolution of the scaffold over time also to this regard. Finally, a molecular analysis of the repair tissue by means of molecular assays could have been useful to give more information about the repair tissue quality. Despite these limitations, we found a statistically significant superiority of the healing tissue in the presence of the scaffold, together with the documentation of the early integration and regeneration phases of this osteochondral scaffold.

In conclusion, based on this ovine trochlea osteochondral defect model, the biomimetic tri-layered scaffold showed a good integration with surrounding tissues and promoted new tissue growth after 14 and 30 days post-surgery, with no safety concerns. In particular, compared to the empty controls, the scaffold induced a significantly higher trabecular bone formation at both 14 and 30 days and a significantly better quality of the newly formed cartilage at 30 days. The study supports the new field trends suggesting the possibility of optimizing postoperative rehabilitation in cartilage surgery, as well as in the case of lesions treated with this osteochondral scaffold. Of course, patient and lesion-specific conditions remain key and should be taken into account to develop an appropriate rehabilitation programme, preventing the risk of early implant failure and ensuring a successful outcome.
