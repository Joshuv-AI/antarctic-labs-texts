Associations of cumulative exposure and dynamic trajectories of the cholesterol–HDL–glucose index and its obesity-related derivatives with cardiovascular disease: a nationwide prospective cohort study
Authors: Han Wang, Guirong Song, Guorong Li, Dongmei Hu, Ying Zhang, Zhuowei Sun, Chenxi Zhai, Dongyong Zhang, Xiao Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntegrating metabolic biomarkers with anthropometric indicators has been shown to enhance the discriminative capacity for cardiovascular disease (CVD). However, the impact of the cholesterol–HDL–glucose index (CHG) and its derivatives on CVD risk remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the associations of CHG and its derivatives with CVD and to compare their discriminative performance.MethodsThis prospective study included 3,359 CVD-free participants from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Repeated measurements obtained in 2011 and 2015 were used to derive cumulative exposure and longitudinal trajectory patterns of CHG and its derivatives. Associations of baseline values, cumulative exposure, and longitudinal trajectories of these indicators with incident CVD and stroke were examined using Cox proportional hazards regression models.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 5 years, 466 participants (13.9%) developed CVD. Both CHG and its derivatives were significantly associated with CVD across baseline measurements, cumulative exposure, and longitudinal trajectories. Cumulative CHG derivatives showed significantly better discriminative power than isolated cumulative CHG (Delong’s test P < 0.05), with the highest associations observed for cumulative CHG–waist circumference (HR = 1.29) and cumulative CHG–Chinese visceral adiposity index (HR = 1.28). Regarding trajectory patterns, participants with stably high CHG-CVAI exhibited the greatest risk compared with those maintaining stable low levels (HR = 1.79), followed by CHG-WC (HR = 1.65). Additionally, ascending and declining trajectories of both CHG-CVAI and CHG-WC were associated with increased CVD risk (ascending CHG-CVAI: HR = 1.79; declining CHG-CVAI: HR = 1.54; ascending CHG-WC: HR = 1.45; declining CHG-WC: HR = 1.54).ConclusionsCHG and its derivatives were strongly associated with incident CVD, with CHG-CVAI and CHG-WC exhibiting the most pronounced associations. Moreover, ascending and declining trajectories of CHG-CVAI and CHG-WC were linked to higher risk, highlighting the importance of tracking dynamic increases while also accounting for baseline metabolic status. These indicators may facilitate the identification of individuals at elevated risk for CVD.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03295-w.Keywords: The cholesterol–HDL–glucose index, CHG and its obesity-related derivatives, Dynamic trajectories, CVD, CHARLS

Research insights

What is currently known about this topic?

The cholesterol–HDL–glucose (CHG) index has been associated with an elevated incidence of cardiovascular disease (CVD).

Integration of biochemical markers with anthropometric measures is commonly applied to enhance predictive performance.

What is the key research question?

Do elevated CHG-derived obesity indices confer increased risk of CVD?

Which trajectory patterns of CHG-derived obesity indices are most strongly associated with CVD risk?

What is new?

CHG-derived obesity indices demonstrate stronger associations with CVD risk than CHG alone.

All CHG-derived obesity indices and their cumulative values were significantly associated with incident CVD, with CHG–waist circumference (CHG-WC) and CHG–Chinese visceral adiposity index (CHG-CVAI) exhibiting the strongest associations.

Ascending trajectories of CHG-CVAI and CHG-WC were linked to higher CVD risk. Counterintuitively, declining trajectories remained associated with elevated risk.

How might this study influence clinical practice?

Long-term monitoring of CHG and its derivatives, particularly CHG-WC and CHG-CVAI, may enhance personalized prevention strategies and risk stratification for CVD.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of mortality worldwide, imposing a substantial burden on global health. Recent estimates indicate that CVD accounts for nearly one-third of all deaths globally, with mortality rising from 12.1 million in 1990 to approximately 20 million in 2022 [1–3]. In China, the prevalence of CVD is particularly concerning, affecting over 300 million individuals, with CVD-related deaths accounting for 44% of total mortality [4]. Extensive epidemiological evidence has identified major risk factors for CVD, with a significant proportion of cases attributable to common modifiable exposures. Among these, metabolic abnormalities represent the largest contributor to cardiovascular burden [5], highlighting the critical role of metabolic disturbances in CVD development and the need for refined assessment strategies to improve risk stratification.

Elevated circulating lipids and glucose levels are well-established risk factors for CVD [6], with insulin resistance (IR) serving as a recognized underlying biological mechanism [7]. In addition, systemic inflammatory responses, which are closely linked to lipid and glucose dysregulation, constitute another important contributor to CVD [8]. The cholesterol–HDL–glucose (CHG) index, calculated from fasting blood glucose (FBG), total cholesterol (TC), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), may serve as a surrogate marker of IR and inflammation. Originally developed for diabetes risk assessment [9], CHG has subsequently been validated across a range of cardiometabolic outcomes, including diabetic retinopathy, stroke, and chronic kidney disease [10–12]. Compared with indices primarily reflecting triglyceride-related metabolic abnormalities, such as the triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index and the plasma atherogenic index (AIP), CHG incorporates total cholesterol burden along with glucose metabolism, thereby offering a more comprehensive perspective on cardiometabolic risk. Obesity is recognized as a key determinant of cardiometabolic risk and contributes to the development and progression of atherosclerosis through multiple interconnected metabolic pathways [13–15]. Accordingly, composite indices that combine metabolic biomarkers (e.g., TyG, the cholesterol–triglyceride index [CTI], and AIP) with obesity-related parameters have been proposed to improve risk characterization in cardiometabolic disorders [16–18]. Despite these advances, prospective cohort evidence evaluating the association between CHG and incident CVD remains limited, and systematic assessment of CHG-based obesity-related indicators is still lacking, particularly with respect to their longitudinal trajectory patterns [19, 20].

To address these gaps, the present study combined CHG with multiple obesity-related indices, including CHG–body mass index (CHG-BMI), CHG–waist circumference (CHG-WC), CHG–waist-to-height ratio (CHG-WHtR), CHG–body roundness index (CHG-BRI), CHG–Chinese visceral adiposity index (CHG-CVAI), CHG–weight-adjusted waist index (CHG-WWI), and CHG–a body shape index (CHG-ABSI). Leveraging data from a large sample cohort, this study systematically evaluated the associations of baseline levels, cumulative exposure, and longitudinal trajectories of these indicators with the risks of CVD and stroke.

Methods

Study design and population

Data for this study were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative prospective survey of middle-aged and older adults in China [21]. Employing a stratified random sampling design, CHARLS recruited participants from 450 communities across 28 provinces. The initial survey, conducted in 2011, enrolled 17,708 individuals, with subsequent follow-ups in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. This longitudinal framework provides a continuous and reliable source of data for ageing-related research. The study protocol was approved by the Peking University Ethics Review Committee (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Among participants enrolled at baseline (Wave 1), individuals aged < 45 years (n = 777) and those with pre-existing CVD (n = 2,650) were excluded. Participants lacking sufficient data to calculate CHG or its derivatives at baseline (n = 7,046), as well as those without any follow-up visits (n = 178), were further excluded. Ultimately, Cohort 1 comprised 7,057 participants with baseline CHG and its derivatives. For analyses of longitudinal trajectories in CHG and its derivatives, additional exclusions were applied: participants missing CHG or obesity-related derivative measurements at Wave 3 (n = 2,916), those who developed CVD during Waves 2 or 3 (n = 604), and individuals lost to follow-up (n = 156). This resulted in a final sample of 3,359 participants with measurements of both CHG and its derivatives at wave 1 and wave 3 (cohort 2; Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics of the included (N = 3,359) and excluded (N = 14,349) participants are presented in Table S1. Compared with included individuals, those excluded showed a slightly higher prevalence of chronic diseases and were more likely to reside in urban areas. However, most standardized mean differences were below 0.20, indicating core baseline variables, including age and sex, remained broadly comparable.

Exposure assessment

In the first and third waves, fasting venous blood samples were collected following standardized procedures, and all anthropometric indices were measured during the same scheduled visit. Serum concentrations of FBG, HDL-C, TC, and triglycerides (TG) were measured using enzymatic colorimetric methods [22]. During anthropometric measurements, participants wore light, loose-fitting clothing and were barefoot. Height, weight, and WC were recorded accordingly.

CHG and its derivatives were calculated as follows: CHG-derived indices were defined as the product of CHG and each obesity index (CHG-BMI, CHG-WC, CHG-WHtR, CHG-BRI, CHG-CVAI, CHG-WWI, and CHG-ABSI). Cumulative CHG and its obesity-related derivatives were computed using the formula: (indicator2012 + indicator2015)/2 × time (2015 − 2012).

Dynamic trajectories

To classify longitudinal trajectories for CHG and its derivatives, fixed cutoffs for each indicator were established based on baseline medians (CHG: 5.280; CHG-BMI: 121.976; CHG-WC: 444.671; CHG-WHtR: 2.822; CHG-BRI: 21.208; CHG-CVAI: 483.963; CHG-WWI: 58.717; CHG-ABSI: 0.437). For each indicator, measurements at Wave 1 and Wave 3 were dichotomized as “high” (≥ baseline median) or “low” (< baseline median).

Participants in Cohort 2 were subsequently categorized into four distinct longitudinal trajectories for each indicator (Fig. 2): (1) stable high (maintaining high at both waves), (2) stable low (maintaining low at both waves), (3) high-to-low (declining from high to low), and (4) low-to-high (ascending from low to high).

Assessment of outcomes

In this study, CVD was defined as the primary outcome, with stroke as a secondary outcome. Heart disease was identified based on self-reported physician-diagnosed conditions, including heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart-related problems. Stroke was also self-reported. Participants were considered to have CVD if they reported either heart disease or stroke. This definition is consistent with previous studies utilizing CHARLS data [16].

Covariates

Baseline covariates were selected based on prior research and included age, sex, education level (below high school, high school, or college and above), marital status (married or others), residence (urban or rural), drinking and smoking status (ever or never), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), kidney disease, diabetes, and hypertension. Hypertension was defined as DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, physician diagnosis, or use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes was defined as FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL, use of antidiabetic medication, or physician diagnosis. Kidney disease was based on self-reported physician diagnosis.

Assessment of potential confounders

To mitigate the risk of overadjustment bias, covariates were selected based on a causal framework using a directed acyclic graph (DAG) constructed in DAGitty (dagitty.net) and R, to identify the minimally sufficient confounder set for estimating the total effect of CHG and its obesity-related derivatives on incident CVD [23, 24]. Among all variables, kidney disease, diabetes, hypertension, and hs-CRP were exclude as mediators. The final minimal adjustment set included age, sex, education level, residential, marital status, smoking status, and drinking status (Fig. 3).

Handling missing variables

Missing patterns of covariates among the study participants are presented in Tables S2–S3. Missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations implemented with the R package “mice”. The imputation model included all missing covariates except for the exposure and outcome variables. Five imputed datasets were generated under the missing-at-random assumption using the Classification and Regression Trees algorithm. Hazard ratios and 95% CIs were pooled based on Rubin’s rules.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Differences between groups were assessed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to examine the associations of baseline CHG and its derivatives with incident CVD in cohort 1 and of their cumulative values with incident CVD in cohort 2. To facilitate comparability of effect sizes, all continuous exposure variables were standardized using Z-score transformation, with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. Complementary analyses were conducted by categorizing baseline measurements into quartiles, with the lowest quartile serving as the reference group. Three models were specified: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age and sex; Model 3 further adjusted for education level, residence, marital status, drinking and smoking status. The Schoenfeld residual test was used to test the proportional hazard assumption. Longitudinal trajectories of CHG and its derivatives were analyzed in cohort 2 using the same modelling framework, with the persistent low group as the reference. Potential nonlinear associations between exposure indicators and CVD risk were evaluated using restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with four knots. Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed to estimate the cumulative incidence of CVD stratified by quartiles and trajectory patterns of cumulative CHG and its derivatives. Discriminative performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC), with comparisons of AUCs performed using the DeLong test.

Stratified analyses were conducted to examine associations across subpopulations defined by age, sex, drinking and smoking status, diabetes, kidney disease, and hypertension. Sensitivity analyses included: (1) multivariable logistic regression to assess associations of all exposure indicators with incident CVD; (2) k-means clustering to derive trajectory-based exposure subgroups, which were then examined for their associations with incident CVD; (3) restriction of analyses to participants without hypertension, kidney disease, or diabetes at baseline to test robustness of associations; (4) weighted Cox proportional hazards models incorporating CHARLS survey weights and accounting for clustering and stratification of the survey design; (5) Cox proportional hazards models using the individual components of CHG, including FBG, HDL-C, and TC, as exposure variables to evaluate the robustness of the observed associations; and (6) complete-case analyses restricted to participants with complete information on all covariates.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2. Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of study participants

During a median follow-up of 5 years, 3,359 participants were categorized into four CHG trajectories: stable low (n = 1,498), high-to-low (n = 730), low-to-high (n = 182), and stable high (n = 949). Distribution of sex and residence differed across groups, with higher proportions of females and urban residents in the stable high group. Prevalence of hypertension and diabetes was also highest in the stable high group. The stable low group consistently demonstrated the most favorable cardiometabolic profile, characterized by lower levels of SBP, DBP, FBG, TC, and hs-CRP, as well as higher HDL-C. Moreover, the stable high group exhibited the highest incidence of CVD (17.2%) and stroke (7.4%), compared with 11.5% and 4.2%, respectively, in the stable low group (Table 1).

Association of baseline CHG and its derivatives with incident CVD

As summarized in Table 2, baseline CHG and its derivatives were positively associated with CVD across all models. Specifically, each 1-unit increment in CHG was associated with a 13% higher risk of incident CVD (HR: 1.13, 95% CI: 1.07–1.19). CHG-derived indices demonstrated stronger associations than CHG alone, with CHG-CVAI and CHG-WC showing the most pronounced effects (HR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.21–1.35; HR: 1.26, 95% CI: 1.20–1.33, respectively), followed by CHG-BMI (HR: 1.25, 95% CI: 1.19–1.31), CHG-BRI (HR: 1.24, 95% CI: 1.18–1.30), and CHG-WHtR (HR: 1.23, 95% CI: 1.17–1.30). Quartile-based analyses corroborated these findings, showing significant associations of CHG and its derivatives with CVD risk. Among these indicators, CHG-CVAI exhibited the strongest association, with participants in the highest quartile having the greatest risk of CVD (HR: 1.89, 95% CI: 1.61–2.23).

Association of trajectories of CHG and its derivatives with incident CVD

Regarding the 5-year trajectories of CHG and its derivatives, as presented in Table 3, participants in the stable-high groups demonstrated a greater risk of CVD compared with their corresponding stable-low groups, particularly for CHG-CVAI (HR: 1.79, 95% CI: 1.43–2.24) and CHG-WC (HR: 1.65, 95% CI: 1.33–2.03). Similarly, ascending trajectories of CHG-CVAI and CHG-WC were significantly associated with elevated CVD risk (HR: 1.79, 95% CI: 1.33–2.41; HR: 1.45, 95% CI: 1.04–2.04, respectively). In addition, declining trajectories of CHG, CHG-CVAI, CHG-WC, and CHG-BMI also showed significantly higher risks of CVD, with corresponding HRs of 1.37 (95% CI: 1.08–1.74), 1.54 (95% CI: 1.02–2.33), 1.54 (95% CI: 1.14–2.08), and 1.42 (95% CI: 1.02–1.97), respectively.

Comparable patterns were observed for stroke, as shown in Table S7. The stable-high groups across all indicators exhibited an increased risk of stroke, particularly for CHG-CVAI and CHG-WC. Similarly, both increasing CHG-CVAI and decreasing CHG-CVAI and CHG-WC trajectories were associated with a higher risk of stroke.

The discriminative ability of cumulative CHG and its derivatives for CVD, as measured by AUC, is presented in Table S8. The AUCs for cumulative CHG-WC (0.606) and cumulative CHG-CVAI (0.604) were comparable to that of cumulative CHG alone (0.593). DeLong’s test showed that all CHG-based obesity-related indicators except CHG-WWI had significantly higher AUCs than CHG alone (all P < 0.05), while CHG had no higher AUCs than AIP, TyG and CTI. Except for CHG-WC and CHG-BMI, CHG-CVAI exhibited a significantly higher AUC than all other obesity-related indicators, AIP, TyG, and CTI (all P < 0.05), though the absolute differences in AUC values were modest (Tables S9-S12).

A significant interaction was observed for drinking status in the association between cumulative CHG and CVD risk (P for interaction = 0.01), indicating stronger effects among individuals who consumed alcohol. Across most subgroups, “stable high” and “high-to-low” trajectories were generally associated with an increased risk of CVD. Sensitivity analyses further corroborated the robustness of the primary findings. First, results from multivariable logistic regression were consistent with those from Cox proportional hazards models (Tables S20 and S21). Persistently high trajectories conferred the highest risk, while both increasing and decreasing trajectories of CHG-WC and CHG-CVAI, also remained linked to elevated risk. Second, cluster-based analyses revealed higher CVD risk among participants in higher trajectory classes, especially for CHG-CVAI (Table S22). Third, exclusion of participants with kidney disease, hypertension, or diabetes at baseline yielded results that were largely consistent with the primary analyses, except that the elevated CVD risk associated with declining trajectories was no longer observed (Tables S23 and S24). Fourth, weighted Cox proportional hazards models incorporating the CHARLS survey weights yielded results that were materially consistent with the primary analyses, with CHG-CVAI remaining significantly associated with a higher risk of CVD (Tables S25 and S26). Fifth, in sensitivity analyses using the components of CHG as separate exposure variables, only HDL showed a significant association with CVD. This finding further supports the superiority of the combined CHG index, as it demonstrated stronger and more robust associations with CVD than any single component (Table S27). Finally, complete-case analyses restricted to participants with complete covariate information yielded findings comparable to those from the primary analyses based on multiple imputation (Tables S28 and S29).

Discussion

With an 8-year follow-up in this large prospective cohort study, the associations of baseline levels, cumulative exposure, and longitudinal trajectories of CHG and its derivatives with the risks of CVD and stroke were comprehensively evaluated. Elevated CHG levels were consistently associated with higher incidence of both outcomes, and these associations were further reinforced when CHG was integrated with obesity-related indices. Among the derivatives examined, CHG-WC and CHG-CVAI exhibited the strongest associations in both baseline and cumulative analyses. Trajectory analyses further demonstrated that persistently high patterns were associated with the greatest CVD risk, whereas both increasing and decreasing trajectories of CHG and CHG-CVAI were also linked to elevated risk. These findings were supported by subgroup and sensitivity analyses.

CHG, as an emerging biomarker integrating TC, HDL-C, and FBG, was originally developed for diabetes risk assessment [9]. Subsequently, growing evidence has extended its application to a broader spectrum of metabolic disorders, including metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, carotid atherosclerotic plaques, and diabetic microvascular complications such as retinopathy and nephropathy [10, 12, 25, 26]. More recently, its utility has further extended beyond myocardial infarction to the broader evaluation of cardiometabolic diseases [27]. However, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated but are likely related to dysregulation of lipids and glucose, as well as IR [28]. Mechanistically, IR may impair insulin signaling and disrupt glucose homeostasis, thereby inducing endothelial dysfunction, while concomitant dyslipidemia, characterized by atherogenic lipoproteins and reduced HDL-C, further exacerbates vascular inflammation and promotes plaque formation. These interrelated metabolic disturbances act synergistically to accelerate atherosclerotic progression [29, 30], ultimately contributing to an increased risk of CVD.

Some evidence suggests that CHG may offer advantages over established cardiometabolic markers. The incorporation of FBG may enhance its discriminative ability for hypertension compared with the AIP [31], whereas the inclusion of TC and HDL-C provides complementary atherogenic information beyond the TyG index [20]. These features may partly explain the superior performance of CHG over the TyG index in identifying MASLD, incident stroke, and carotid atherosclerotic plaques [10–12]. Moreover, TyG and AIP are predominantly anchored in triglyceride-driven metabolic pathways and are primarily considered surrogate markers of hepatic insulin resistance. Consequently, they may incompletely capture disturbances in reverse cholesterol transport and the overall atherogenic cholesterol burden in particular [32]. Additionally, CHG may provide a more comprehensive representation of systemic glucolipotoxicity in individuals with coexisting dyslipidemia and dysglycemia. Therefore, in diabetic microvascular complications, CHG has been associated with both diabetic retinopathy and nephropathy, whereas the TyG index has primarily been linked to nephropathy alone [25, 26]. Despite these advances, only few cohort studies have specifically investigated the association between CHG and CVD, with most have relied on baseline measurements [19, 20]. To date, only one Chinese cohort has evaluated the association of cumulative CHG with incident CVD, demonstrating increased risk with higher cumulative exposure and persistently high-risk trajectories [33]. However, the largely parallel trajectory patterns observed in that study suggest that these associations may have been primarily driven by baseline differences, providing limited insight into the impact of dynamic trajectories over time. These gaps highlight the necessity of investigating longitudinal trajectories of CHG to better elucidate its prognostic utility. In this context, our study extends the current evidence by demonstrating that CHG is significantly associated with incident CVD from baseline levels, cumulative exposure, and longitudinal trajectory patterns.

Emerging evidence indicates that integrating metabolic indices with obesity-related measures enhances CVD risk stratification, as exemplified by the TyG index [16], the AIP index [17], and the CTI index [18], suggesting that adiposity contributes additional pathophysiological information beyond conventional metabolic markers. Excess adiposity promotes atherosclerosis through mechanisms including systemic inflammation, ectopic lipid deposition, and IR [34–37]. However, evidence regarding CHG-derived obesity indices remains limited. To date, only one nationwide Chinese cohort has examined associations of CHG and its derivatives—namely CHG–BMI, CHG–WC, CHG–WHtR, CHG–BRI, CHG–CVAI, CHG–WWI, and CHG–ABSI—with stroke [19]. Nevertheless, its reliance on baseline measurements may limit the robustness of the findings [38]. To our knowledge, the present study is the first to investigate the effects of cumulative exposure and dynamic trajectories in CHG-derived obesity indices. Integration of CHG with obesity-related indicators yielded stronger associations with CVD outcomes than CHG alone, for both baseline and cumulative exposures. Moreover, in our head-to-head comparisons using DeLong’s test, the original CHG index showed a comparable discriminative ability to AIP, TyG, and CTI. However, among its obesity-related derivatives, CHG-CVAI demonstrated the highest AUC, with statistically significant differences compared with the other indices, although the absolute differences were modest. These results suggest that combining CHG with adiposity measures provides a more comprehensive assessment of cardiometabolic risk, with potential implications for targeted prevention strategies among middle-aged and older adults.

Notably, among the CHG-derived obesity indices, CHG-WC and CHG-CVAI demonstrated the strongest associations in our study. A similar pattern has been observed in a large Chinese cohort that evaluated associations of TyG and its derivatives—including TyG-BMI, TyG-WHtR, TyG-WC, TyG-CVAI, TyG-WWI, TyG-BRI, and TyG-ABSI—with CVD risk [16]. This may be partly attributable to ethnic-specific differences in fat distribution: Asian populations generally accumulate greater visceral fat at a given BMI compared with Western populations [39], and traditional anthropometric indices such as BMI, WC, and WHtR may be less sensitive for identifying high-risk individuals, as these indices cannot distinguish between subcutaneous and visceral fat compartments. The CVAI, developed as a surrogate marker of visceral adiposity in Asian populations [40], has been validated as a practical alternative to CT- or MRI-based assessments in large-scale studies [41, 42], demonstrating superior association for CVD [43–45] and hypertension [46, 47]. By incorporating race-specific parameters in its derivation, CVAI may more accurately capture visceral adiposity-related risk in Chinese cohorts than indices derived primarily from Caucasian populations, whose cutoffs and associations with metabolic risk may be less applicable to Asian populations [40, 48].When combined with CHG, CHG-CVAI may therefore capture the concurrent deterioration of metabolic status and visceral adiposity [41, 49, 50], thereby amplifying the contribution of combined lipid and glucose abnormalities to risk stratification. Similarly, CHG-WC, as a readily available measure of central obesity, reflects both metabolic dysregulation and abdominal adiposity [51], consistent with findings from another nationwide Chinese cohort in which TyG-WC exhibited superior discriminative ability relative to other TyG-derived obesity indices [52].

Trajectory analyses revealed that persistently elevated levels of CHG and its derivatives were associated with the highest risk of CVD, highlighting the deleterious effects of long-term metabolic burden. The ascending trajectories of CHG-CVAI and CHG-WC were associated with a significantly higher risk of CVD, suggesting that tracking dynamic rises beyond baseline measurements may help identify individuals at increased risk. Notably, declining trajectories of CHG, CHG-WC and CHG-CVAI remained associated with elevated cardiovascular risk, whereas other indices—including CHG-WHtR, CHG-BRI, CHG-WWI, and CHG-ABSI—demonstrated no significant correlations. These observations are consistent with prior evidence from a Chinese cohort in which participants with high baseline CHG but rapidly declining trajectories remained at greatest risk for incident hypertension [31]. This seemingly counterintuitive finding warrants cautious interpretation. One possible explanation is that these individuals may have a higher prevalence of unhealthy lifestyle behaviors and a less favorable metabolic profile. This interpretation is substantiated by two lines of evidence from our study. First, in the sensitivity analysis excluding participants with baseline hypertension, diabetes, or kidney disease, the elevated CVD risk associated with declining trajectories was no longer observed. Second, we found that individuals in the descending CHG trajectory group had the highest rates of smoking and alcohol consumption. Alternative explanations, including cumulative metabolic exposure and pharmacological treatment, may also have contributed to the observed associations. Therefore, the declining trajectories may reflect a heterogeneous subgroup rather than true metabolic recovery, and should not be interpreted as indicating metabolic improvement [53]. Overall, these findings underscore the importance of early and sustained management of metabolic risk factors, and suggest that longitudinal trajectories should be interpreted in the context of baseline metabolic status and potential clinical interventions.

This study, based on the large sample CHARLS cohort, comprehensively evaluated baseline, cumulative, and dynamic trajectories in CHG and its derivatives in relation to CVD and stroke. To our knowledge, this study provides the first systematic assessment of the combined effect of CHG-centered metabolic and obesity indices. A range of rigorous statistical approaches—including multivariable Cox regression, subgroup analyses, RCS regression, ROC modelling, and k-means clustering—was employed to ensure the reliability and robustness of the findings. The prospective design and large sample size enhanced the statistical power and supported the robustness of the findings. Furthermore, the consistent effect directions across subgroups suggest that our findings are reasonably generalizable to community-dwelling Chinese adults aged ≥ 45 years. Nonetheless, several limitations merit consideration. First, CVD outcomes were ascertained through self-reported physician diagnoses without independent verification through medical records or clinical adjudication, which may have introduced outcome misclassification. However, this approach has been widely adopted in CHARLS and other large cohort studies, and previous evidence supports acceptable agreement between self-reported and clinically verified CVD events [54]. Second, our analytic sample was substantially reduced due to missing data and loss to follow-up, which may introduce selection bias. We addressed this by comparing included versus excluded participants and applying survey weights, although generalization to the broader population warrants caution. Third, the study was limited to middle-aged and older Chinese adults (≥ 45 years), which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to younger individuals and other ethnic or geographic populations. Therefore, further studies in more diverse populations are warranted to validate our findings. Fourth, only the association of CHG and its derivatives with CVD was explored, and no prediction model was established. Further studies are needed to evaluate the efficacy of CHG and its derivatives in predicting the risk of CVD. Fifth, only two repeated measurements were available in the CHARLS study, precluding the application of formal trajectory modelling approaches. Future studies with more repeated measurements are needed to validate and extend our findings. Sixth, in the absence of established clinical thresholds for CHG and its derivatives, baseline median-based cutoffs were used to define exposure categories and longitudinal trajectories, consistent with methodologies adopted in previous studies [55]. Future studies with clinically validated cutoffs are warranted. Seventh, although CHG may provide a broader assessment of cardiometabolic dysfunction by integrating glucose- and cholesterol-related information, no statistically significant differences in discriminative ability between CHG and other indices (TyG, AIP, CTI) were observed. Consequently, whether CHG provides incremental value beyond existing cardiometabolic markers remains uncertain and should be further investigated in future studies.

Conclusion

Elevated levels of CHG and its derivatives were significantly associated with increased risk of CVD, with CHG-CVAI and CHG-WC demonstrating the strongest associations. Beyond persistently high trajectories, ascending trajectories of CHG-CVAI and CHG-WC were also linked to elevated CVD risk, underscoring the importance of tracking dynamic increases. Counterintuitively, declining trajectories also showed elevated risk, highlighting the need to consider baseline metabolic status. These findings suggest that combined prevention targeting both metabolic dysfunction and adiposity may offer enhanced cardiovascular benefits.
