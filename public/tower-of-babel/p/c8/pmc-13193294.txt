Beyond disease treatment and prevention: From geroscience and molecular hallmarks to gerotherapeutics and precision geromedicine
Authors: Li-Ning Peng, Fei-Yuan Hsiao, Liang-Kung Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGeroscience—the interdisciplinary field investigating the causal relationship between the biology of aging and age-related chronic disease—has undergone a remarkable evolution since its formalization within the United States National Institutes of Health in the early 2010s. Grounded in the recognition that aging is the paramount modifiable risk factor for most noncommunicable diseases, geroscience has produced a coherent molecular taxonomy of aging processes (12 hallmarks), delineated proaging and antiaging molecular pathways (gerogenes, gerosuppressors, and gerozymes), and catalyzed a new clinical vocabulary—geroprotection, gerodiagnostics, and gerotherapeutics. Two institutional milestones anchor the field’s translational ambitions: the World Health Organization’s (WHO) codification of Ageing-Associated Decline in Intrinsic Capacity as International Classification of Disease (ICD)-11 code MG2A; and the United States Food and Drug Administration’s (FDA) acceptance of the Targeting Aging with Metformin (TAME) trial—the first prospective clinical trial designed to delay aging as a composite multidisease outcome, currently underway. The biomarker science of aging has advanced in parallel, from first-generation DNA methylation clocks to organ-specific plasma proteomic signatures capable of predicting various age-related diseases with clinical-grade precision. The gerotherapeutic landscape has expanded substantially. Metformin, which engages more aging hallmarks than any other candidate gerotherapeutic, provided the regulatory impetus for TAME; in a rigorous 40-month multiomics study in cynomolgus monkeys, it decelerated plasma proteomic biological age by 6.41 years—the strongest pharmacological evidence to date for systemic biological age modification in a primate model. Senolytics and senomorphics target senescent cell burden; SGLT-2 inhibitors are notable for their indirect senolytic mechanism, enhancing immunosurveillance of senescent cells glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists attenuate inflammaging; NAD+ precursors restore mitochondrial and sirtuin function; and the gerozyme (15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase [15-PGDH]) inhibitor offers a mechanistically distinct proregenerative approach with emerging relevance as an adjunct to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy. Multidomain lifestyle programs address multiple aging hallmarks simultaneously and have demonstrated measurable intrinsic capacity improvement in randomized trials. Building on these foundations, this review proposes the Geroscience-Responsive Aging Care Ecosystem (GRACE)—a three-element service model operationalizing geroscience and gerotherapeutic evidence within the WHO Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) framework.Keywords: Biological age, Geroscience, Gerotherapeutics, Intrinsic capacity, Precision geromedicine

1. INTRODUCTION

By 2050, more than two billion individuals worldwide will be aged 60 years or older, with super-aged societies—defined as nations where more than 20% of the population exceeds 65 years—proliferating across East Asia, Europe, and North America.1 Taiwan reached super-aged status in 2025, compressing a demographic transition that took Western nations several decades into a single generation. Yet lifespan extension has not been matched by healthspan extension—the period of life spent free from significant chronic disease or disability. Traditionally, Medicine has approached aging as a backdrop to disease: people accumulate diagnoses over decades, each managed by a different specialist, with little coordinated attention to the shared biology underlying multimorbidity. The emergence of geroscience—and its medical corollary, geromedicine—offers a fundamentally different paradigm: if aging biology is the key driver of most chronic diseases, then targeting aging processes directly should delay or prevent multiple conditions simultaneously, compressing morbidity and extending functional independence.2 This review traces the history and core concepts of geroscience from its United States National Institutes of Health (NIH) origins; defines the essential vocabulary of the field; discusses the landmark World Health Organization (WHO) International Classification of Disease (ICD)-11 MG2A classification and the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA)–accepted Targeting Aging with Metformin (TAME) trial; examines the molecular architecture of aging including hallmarks, gerogenes, and gerosuppressors (Fig. 1A, B); surveys biomarker achievements from epigenetic clocks through organ-specific plasma proteomics; reviews the gerotherapeutic landscape; and culminates in the proposal of the Geroscience-Responsive Aging Care Ecosystem (GRACE)—an integrated three-element service model (Fig. 1, Table 1) that aligns geroscience discovery with the WHO Integrated Care for Older People (ICOPE) framework.

2. THE DEVELOPMENT OF GEROSCIENCE

Precision geromedicine has given rise to a specialized lexicon essential for academic clarity, policy dialogue, regulatory communication, and clinical implementation. Table 2 defines the conceptual bedrock of the field with the primary source references underpinning each term. The formal institutionalization of geroscience as a distinct scientific program emerged in the early 2010s within the United States National Institute on Aging (NIA). Dr. Felipe Sierra articulated the proposition because aging biology underlies all chronic diseases of interest to every NIH Institute. Every Institute should engage with aging research, which led to the formation of the Trans-NIH Geroscience Interest Group (GSIG) in 2011, consolidated from researchers across NIH Institutes and Centers.33,40 In 2013, NIA hosted the First NIH Summit on Advances in Geroscience and the position paper formally defined the geroscience mission: linking aging to chronic disease and arguing that targeting shared aging biology represents a more efficient approach to reducing chronic disease burden than addressing each disease individually.32 The proposal of “Hallmarks of Aging” in 2013 established the molecular taxonomy that geroscience required. The convergence of these two frameworks—molecular hallmarks and disease linkage—formed the conceptual foundation upon which the subsequent development of geroscience as a discipline has been built.3,32

The geroscience hypothesis holds that because aging is the primary risk factor for most chronic diseases, biological aging processes are the common upstream cause of disparate downstream diseases. An intervention that meaningfully slows biological aging would therefore simultaneously delay or prevent multiple age-related conditions. The original NIH framework described seven pillars of aging: adaptation to stress, epigenetics, inflammation, macromolecular damage, metabolism, proteostasis, and stem cells/regeneration.32 NIA’s Interventions Testing Program (ITP)—a rigorous, multisite preclinical program testing geroprotective compounds in genetically diverse mouse cohorts—provided empirical grounding for these ideas, demonstrating healthspan and lifespan extension with rapamycin, acarbose, 17-alpha-estradiol, and nordihydroguaiaretic acid (the latter two with predominant effects in males), thereby establishing that aging pace is pharmacologically modifiable in mammals. By 2016, NIA established the National Translational Geroscience Network to bridge preclinical discoveries and human clinical trials. Subsequent Geroscience Summits progressively addressed health equity, mathematical modeling and artificial intelligence (AI) applications, and new clinical trial design features for aging-targeted endpoints.40 Internationally, geroscience expanded rapidly across the world.34

3. INSTITUTIONAL MILESTONES: ICD-11 MG2A CODIFICATION AND THE TAME TRIAL

ICD-10 carried code R54 Senility—a term increasingly abandoned in clinical practice due to its pejorative connotations. During the development of ICD-11 (2016–2019), a proposal emerged to introduce Old Age as a new diagnostic category under code MG2A. An international coalition engaged the WHO formally, arguing that the term pathologized normal aging without therapeutic implications. Following multistakeholder deliberation, the WHO accepted Ageing-Associated Decline in Intrinsic Capacity as the MG2A designation in October 2021; ICD-11 came into official effect on January 1, 2022.29,30

ICD-11 code MG2A carries three clinical implications. First, it enables systematic population health surveillance of intrinsic capacity (IC) trajectories in standardized epidemiological frameworks. Second, it creates a formally recognized indication—establishing aging-related functional decline as a legitimate target for medical intervention. Third, it provides a multidimensional composite outcome framework that mirrors the multi-hallmark nature of aging biology, making it directly applicable to gerotherapeutic clinical trial design.30,31 A complementary extension code XT9T—Ageing-Related in the Causality/Aetiology section—allows any condition to be flagged as causally attributable to biological aging processes, including specific hallmarks such as inflammaging and mitochondrial dysfunction. Together, MG2A and XT9T provide the ICD-11 architecture needed to operationalize aging as a medical target.29 The International Conference for Frailty and Sarcopenia Research (ICFSR) Geroscience Task Force explicitly identified MG2A as a bridge between geroscience and clinical trial regulatory frameworks. The Task Force recommended adopting a composite IC score as a primary endpoint in gerotherapeutic clinical trials, arguing that its ICD-11 endorsement may facilitate regulatory acceptance by drug agencies.31

Metformin, FDA-approved in 1994 for type 2 diabetes, emerged as a candidate geroprotective drug following a retrospective analysis at Cardiff University, which demonstrated that approximately 78,000 adults with type 2 diabetes in their early 60s who initiated metformin or sulfonylurea monotherapy were compared with matched non-diabetic controls; metformin-initiators showed survival comparable to or exceeding the non-diabetic comparator group—a finding that electrified the geroscience community.41 Subsequent methodological scrutiny tempered this interpretation; however, the survival advantage of metformin-treated patients over the internal diabetic comparator arm was at least partly attributable to the excess mortality conferred by sulfonylurea-associated hypoglycemia in the comparator group, rather than to an intrinsic geroprotective effect of metformin per se—a distinction that underscores the necessity of further trials to establish causal geroprotection in nondiabetic populations. Mechanistically, metformin activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), inhibits mitochondrial complex I, suppresses mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling, attenuates nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB)–mediated inflammaging, reduces DNA damage accumulation, and exhibits senomorphic properties by inhibiting senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) components. Metformin targets more aging hallmarks than any other candidate gerotherapeutic, making it simultaneously the broadest-acting and most extensively safety-profiled geroprotective compound available.42,43

In 2015, Barzilai et al42 initiated discussions with the FDA regarding a clinical trial designed to demonstrate that metformin could delay aging as a composite biological phenomenon. The resulting TAME trial, granted FDA Investigational New Drug (IND) approval, will randomize adults aged 65 to 79 years without diabetes to metformin extended-release 1500 mg/d or placebo over a 6-year follow-up period.42 The primary composite endpoint encompasses the incidence of any of the following: myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, most cancers, mild cognitive impairment/dementia, or death. The trial’s most transformative feature is its regulatory architecture: if successful, TAME will establish a precedent that the FDA will accept composite aging-related endpoints as trial outcomes. If successful, TAME’s endpoint framework will create a template that any pharmaceutical company can adopt to seek an aging-related drug indication. As of 2025, a TAME-like trial framework using the same composite endpoint approach is under discussion for the evaluation of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) therapies in the context of aging.42,43

4. MOLECULAR ARCHITECTURE OF AGING

The molecular understanding of aging was transformed by López-Otín et al3 hallmarks framework in 2013, cataloguing nine cardinal features of biological aging: genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis, dysregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem cell exhaustion, and altered intercellular communication. An expanded framework published in 2023 added three further hallmarks—disabled macroautophagy, chronic inflammation (inflammaging), and dysbiosis—bringing the total to 12 and establishing the most comprehensive current taxonomy of aging biology (Fig. 1A).4 These hallmarks are organized hierarchically: primary hallmarks represent root molecular causes; antagonistic hallmarks are initially adaptive responses that become pathogenic when chronically overactivated; and integrative hallmarks represent the systemic effectors of accumulated damage that translate molecular injury into clinical disease. Each hallmark satisfies three empirical criteria: it manifests progressively with chronological age, its experimental exacerbation accelerates aging, and its attenuation extends healthspan in appropriate models.

A conceptual breakthrough arrived with the formal articulation of the gerogene/gerosuppressor framework in 2024.5 Drawing an explicit analogy with oncology’s oncogene/tumor suppressor paradigm, the framework proposes that biological aging is governed by the balance between gerogenes—genes and pathways that accelerate aging when overactivated—and gerosuppressors—genes and pathways that decelerate aging when functionally intact (Fig. 1B). This duality provides the conceptual engine for precision geromedicine: individuals whose multiomics profiles reveal overactivated gerogenes or depleted gerosuppressor activity are mechanistically identifiable candidates for targeted interventions that restore molecular balance.

The gerogene/gerosuppressor framework with the 12 hallmarks articulates the full mechanistic architecture of precision geromedicine.6 This synthesis demonstrated how gerogenes and gerosuppressors interact dynamically with environmental exposures and behavioral factors to produce the profound heterogeneity in biological aging trajectories observed across individuals of identical chronological age—explaining why the transition from population-average geriatric care to individualized precision geromedicine is scientifically necessary.

5. BIOMARKERS OF AGING

The quantification of biological age underwent a revolution in 2013 when Horvath introduced the first pan-tissue DNA methylation (DNAm) clock, demonstrating that CpG methylation patterns at 353 genomic sites predict chronological age across multiple tissue types.9 This established the proof of concept that the methylome records the biological passage of time as a molecular odometer—opening an entirely new domain of aging biomarker science (Fig. 1C). First-generation clocks, including the Horvath pan-tissue clock and the Hannum blood clock, were calibrated to chronological age—predicting how old someone was, not how quickly they were aging. Second-generation clocks addressed this limitation: PhenoAge was trained on clinical biomarkers associated with mortality rather than chronological age, yielding superior prediction of chronic disease onset and survival.10 GrimAge incorporated plasma protein measures (DNAm surrogates of aging-associated plasma proteins) to generate a mortality-predicting biological age estimator with strong associations with disease-free survival.11 The third generation—exemplified by DunedinPACE—shifted from estimating biological age at a point in time to measuring the pace of aging as a dynamic, longitudinal quantity.12,17 DunedinPACE was trained on 19 biomarkers tracked from age 26 to age 45 in the Dunedin birth cohort, capturing the rate at which physiology was changing—analogous to a speedometer rather than an odometer—making it particularly sensitive to lifestyle and pharmacological interventions that alter aging pace. Fourth-generation clocks integrate multiple molecular data streams—DNAm combined with transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data. The emerging consensus is that no single omic layer captures the full complexity of biological aging, and maximally accurate aging phenotyping requires multimodal integration.

While epigenetic clocks dominated aging biomarker research for a decade, plasma proteomics has emerged as an equally powerful platform. A study in 2024 developed a proteomic aging clock (ProtAge) using 2897 plasma proteins measured in 45 441 UK Biobank participants. Using 204 proteins, ProtAge strongly predicted chronological age, and proteomic aging acceleration independently predicted the incidence of 18 major chronic diseases, multimorbidity, and all-cause mortality across diverse validation cohorts.14 A subsequent study estimated the biological age of 11 distinct organs using plasma proteomic data (2916 proteins) from 44 498 UK Biobank individuals. This organ-specific aging atlas revealed that organs age at substantially different rates within the same individual—a concept termed ageotyping—and that accelerated brain aging conferred an Alzheimer’s disease risk comparable to carrying one copy of APOE4, independent of actual APOE genotype.15 Furthermore, another study successfully validated organ-specific proteomic aging clocks across cohorts (combined n >48 000), demonstrating high cross-population accuracy and confirming that accelerated organ-specific aging predicted disease onset and mortality beyond established clinical and genetic risk factors.16

A critical equity challenge is that most existing aging clocks were developed in predominantly European-ancestry cohorts, with documented performance disparities across ancestrally diverse populations driven by methylation quantitative trait loci (meQTLs) that differ by ancestry.13,44 Addressing this requires intentional inclusion of diverse populations in training datasets, ancestry-stratified calibration of clock performance, and development of equity-calibrated multiomics clocks—a scientific and ethical imperative for precision geromedicine to achieve population-level impact.

6. GEROTHERAPEUTICS: CURRENT LANDSCAPE

Gerotherapeutics—interventions targeting the biological mechanisms of aging rather than individual diseases—now encompasses an expanding armamentarium ranging from precision pharmacology to structured lifestyle programs. The evidence base supporting several of these classes has matured sufficiently to warrant clinical consideration, even as pivotal long-term trials remain ongoing.

6.1. Metformin

Metformin holds a unique position in the gerotherapeutic armamentarium as the compound whose regulatory history catalyzed the modern era of precision geromedicine. From a pharmacological standpoint, metformin activates AMPK and suppresses mTORC1 signaling, attenuates NF-κB-driven inflammaging, reduces accumulation of DNA damage, and exhibits senomorphic properties through inhibition of SASP components. In doing so, it engages more aging hallmarks simultaneously—nutrient-sensing dysregulation, inflammaging, genomic instability, and mitochondrial dysfunction—than any other single candidate gerotherapeutic currently available.

The most compelling biological validation of metformin’s geroprotective potential derives from a 40-month randomized study in adult cynomolgus monkeys, in which metformin decelerated plasma proteomic biological age by 6.41 years, with parallel deceleration of DNA methylation, transcriptomic, and metabolomic aging clocks across multiple organs—the strongest pharmacological evidence to date for systemic biological age modification in a primate model.45 Against this biological foundation, two well-powered randomized controlled trials in older adults—MASTERS and MET-PREVENT—found that metformin failed to improve muscle mass, physical performance, or functional status, and demonstrated increased adverse events without functional benefit in frail populations.46,47 This apparent contradiction is mechanistically explicable: the AMPK–mTORC1 axis through which metformin decelerates senescence, inflammaging, and aging clock advancement is the same pathway whose suppression attenuates the anabolic response to resistance exercise and protein intake. Anabolic functional endpoints are therefore structurally misaligned with metformin’s mode of action—a misspecification of trial endpoints rather than evidence of absent geroprotective activity. The appropriate biological outcomes for metformin trials—senescence burden, systemic inflammaging trajectory, epigenetic clock advancement, and proteome-wide aging signatures—were not measured in either trial.

6.2. Senolytics

Senolytics—drugs that selectively eliminate senescent cells—represent the most rapidly advancing pharmacological gerotherapeutic class. The prototypical combination dasatinib plus quercetin (D + Q) has demonstrated pathological senescent cell clearance in multiple human tissues in early-phase clinical trials.48 The NIA Translational Geroscience Network currently oversees clinical trials exploring senolytics across organ systems targeting frailty, pulmonary fibrosis, neurodegenerative disease, and musculoskeletal aging.36 Cardiovascular geroscience perspectives on senolytic and other gerotherapeutic strategies have been reviewed systematically in the context of clinical cardiology practice.49

6.3. Senomorphics

Senomorphics, including rapamycin (mTOR inhibitor), JAK1/2 inhibitors, suppress the SASP without eliminating senescent cells, attenuating the inflammatory and tissue-remodeling consequences of senescence. Rapamycin has the most extensive preclinical longevity evidence of any compound; however, a recent systematic review concluded that existing human evidence remains insufficient to recommend rapamycin for gerotherapeutic indications in healthy adults outside of clinical trial frameworks.50

6.4. SGLT-2 inhibitors

Sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors as senotherapeutics represent an emerging and clinically immediately actionable gerotherapeutic class. Originally developed as antidiabetic agents, SGLT-2 inhibitors have demonstrated remarkable pleiotropic geroprotective properties: a study demonstrated that canagliflozin enhanced immune-mediated clearance of senescent cells through AMPK-mediated metabolic reprogramming, reduced senescent cell burden across visceral adipose tissue and vascular tissue, and extended lifespan in premature aging mouse models independently of glucose lowering.51 A comprehensive review in 2025 positioned SGLT-2 inhibitors as the first widely approved pharmacological class with direct antisenescence properties and multiorgan geroprotective evidence—encompassing cardiovascular protection (reduced heart failure hospitalization and cardiovascular mortality), renal preservation, anti-inflammatory SASP suppression, and putative telomere preservation effects. The clinical availability, established safety profile, and breadth of geroprotective mechanisms make SGLT-2 inhibitors among the most immediately deployable gerotherapeutics in the current armamentarium.51,52

6.5. GLP-1 receptor agonists

GLP-1 RAs (semaglutide, tirzepatide, and related agents) have emerged as plausible candidate gerotherapeutics engaging multiple aging hallmarks simultaneously. Beyond their established cardiovascular mortality reduction and cardiometabolic benefits, GLP-1 receptor agonists attenuate chronic low-grade inflammation (inflammaging), reduce visceral adiposity—a key driver of senescent cell accumulation and SASP—improve mitochondrial function, and in preclinical models reduce amyloid burden and neuroinflammation. Emerging human evidence from large real-world cohorts found GLP-1 RA use associated with reduced dementia incidence, while exploratory epigenetic analyses reported that semaglutide treatment reduced multiple validated biological age clocks including PCGrimAge and DunedinPACE. GLP-1 RAs have been reported to be plausible candidates for the first approved longevity drug class, contingent on clinical trial evidence in nondiabetic aging populations.53

6.6. NAD+ precursors

NAD+ precursors (nicotinamide riboside [NR] and nicotinamide mononucleotide [NMN]) replenish cellular NAD+ pools that decline markedly with aging, restoring sirtuin deacetylase activity, poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP)-mediated DNA repair, and mitochondrial biogenesis.35 A recent review reported that NMN and NR supplementation increased blood NAD+ levels reliably across completed clinical trials, with promising signals for improved skeletal muscle function—including maintained walking speed in older adults in randomized controlled trials—and cardiometabolic parameters. Sex-specific response heterogeneity and optimal dosing regimens for aging endpoints remain under investigation; long-term trials powered for meaningful aging outcomes are underway.

6.7. 15-Hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase inhibition and the PGE2 regenerative axis

15-Hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase (15-PGDH) was the first formally described gerozyme—an enzyme whose age-associated overaccumulation enzymatically degrades endogenous regenerative signaling molecules, thereby suppressing tissue homeostasis in a manner conceptually analogous to oncogenic driver mutations in malignant transformation (Fig. 2). 15-PGDH degrades prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), an eicosanoid lipid mediator essential to the regeneration of skeletal muscle, articular cartilage, bone marrow, peripheral nerve, and intestinal tissue; its expression and activity increase approximately two-fold across these tissues with advancing age, resulting in systemic PGE2 depletion and progressive multitissue functional decline.7

Small-molecule inhibition of 15-PGDH (SW033291) restores physiological PGE2 concentrations acting via the EP4 receptor on myofibers, reversing atrophy mechanisms and increasing aged muscle mass, strength, and endurance. The regenerative scope of gerozyme inhibition has since been extended to neuromuscular junction restoration following acute and chronic denervation, and most recently to hyaline articular cartilage regeneration in osteoarthritic joints, via a chondrocyte-autonomous gene expression reprogramming mechanism distinct from the stem cell expansion operative in muscle and hematopoietic tissues.7,38,8 GLP-1 RA–induced weight loss, while conferring substantial cardiometabolic benefit, impairs muscle stem cell function and post-injury regenerative capacity. In preclinical models, coadministration of a 15-PGDH inhibitor with semaglutide preserved muscle mass, restored stem cell–driven regeneration, and rescued post-injury strength recovery, suggesting that 15-PGDH inhibition may serve as a rational protective adjunct to GLP-1 RA therapy to mitigate the sarcopenia risk accompanying obesity treatment in older adults.54

6.8. Lifestyle gerotherapeutics

Lifestyle gerotherapeutics constitute the most evidence-supported, immediately actionable tier. Progressive resistance exercise simultaneously targets multiple aging hallmarks: activating AMPK and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor coactivator γ-1α (PGC-1α), promoting mitophagy, attenuating inflammaging, and stimulating systemic myokine secretion with cross-organ geroprotective effects. Mediterranean-type diets and time-restricted eating modulate the mTOR-AMPK-sirtuin axis with demonstrated measurable biological age reduction in randomized trials. Integrated multimodal exercise and nutritional programs as endorsed by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2025 represent the current evidence-based standard for muscle health gerotherapeutics.18,55,56

The translational potential of multidomain lifestyle gerotherapeutics for improving IC, specifically—rather than isolated functional endpoints—was demonstrated by the Taiwan Integrated Geriatric Care (TIGER) trial, a pragmatic multicenter randomized controlled trial enrolling 398 community-dwelling older adults aged ≥65 years with three or more chronic conditions. The 12-month multidomain intervention, comprising structured communal physical exercise, cognitive training, nutritional counseling, disease education, and individualized specialist-delivered integrated care, significantly improved quality of life compared with usual care across a 12-month follow-up.57 A subsequent ancillary analysis of the same trial demonstrated that the intervention arm achieved significantly greater improvement in the composite IC score relative to the usual care arm, with the most pronounced gains in the locomotion and cognition subdomains, alongside favorable effects on related physiological biomarkers.58 These findings establish that a structured, pragmatic multidomain lifestyle program delivered within primary care infrastructure can produce measurable, multidomain IC improvement in multimorbid older adults and provide direct empirical support for embedding IC improvement as a primary outcome of lifestyle gerotherapeutic trials.

6.9. AI as a gerotherapeutic personalization engine

A critical but historically under-recognized dimension of precision geromedicine is the role of digital technology and AI in enabling, scaling, and personalizing geroprotective interventions. The absence of a robust digital infrastructure represents a significant gap in current geroscience translation—one that the field must address to realize its clinical potential at the population scale.59 AI platforms capable of integrating cardiometabolic variability signatures, genetic predispositions, and longitudinal biomarker trajectories to identify which individuals will respond optimally to specific gerotherapeutic candidates—SGLT-2 inhibitors, GLP-1 RAs, or NAD+ precursors—before prescribing. This precision gerotherapeutic matching represents the next evolution from population-based to individual-level geroscience-guided prescribing.60

Digital biomarkers and wearable monitoring are transforming the gerodiagnostic landscape. Consumer and clinical-grade wearable devices now enable continuous, passive capture of physiological parameters—gait speed, heart rate variability, sleep architecture, physical activity, and circadian rhythm patterns—that serve as validated biological aging indicators. A comprehensive review catalogued digital biomarkers across multiple physiological systems with demonstrated relationships to biological aging trajectories, noting that wearable-based accelerometry enables circadian rhythm analysis as a validated digital biomarker of aging and healthspan.61 These tools extend gerodiagnostic capability into daily life and community settings far beyond the reach of clinical laboratory measurements.

AI-powered clinical decision support addresses the computational challenge inherent in multiomics aging biology: integrating proteomic, metabolomic, epigenetic, and functional data streams to generate actionable clinical insights exceeds human analytical capacity. Machine learning models have demonstrated clinical-grade accuracy in frailty prediction using electronic health records and wearable sensor data, in identifying biological age acceleration from standard clinical measurements, and in optimizing multidomain intervention regimens for individual aging trajectories.60 Telehealth and remote patient monitoring platforms operationally reach older adults in home and community settings. WHO ICOPE’s MONITOR mobile application represents the paradigm of scalable digital gerodiagnostics: enabling IC domain screening and longitudinal tracking on basic mobile devices by community health workers without specialist involvement, as demonstrated across 18 international implementation programs. Digital dance and technology-assisted rehabilitation programs have demonstrated efficacy in cognitive and physical function outcomes in randomized controlled trials.19,62

7. THE GRACE PROPOSED SERVICE MODEL

Despite extraordinary scientific momentum, the clinical translation of geroscience insights into healthcare delivery remains incomplete.63,64 Existing service models for older people predominantly adopt a reactive, disease-centered orientation—organizing care around established diagnoses without engaging the upstream aging biology that generates them.20 The GRACE resolves this tension by constructing a three-element service architecture that simultaneously addresses all stages of the aging health trajectory within a single coherent framework. The model is built on two intersecting conceptual structures: the classical preventive medicine tripartite of primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention; and the WHO ICOPE framework (Fig. 1).19,28,65

The WHO ICOPE framework operationalizes the aging health continuum through five IC domains—locomotion, cognition, vitality, psychology, and sensory function—and a four-step care pathway: basic assessment for loss of IC and key health factors (Step 1), in-depth assessment encompassing loss of IC, diseases and risk factors, and social and physical environments (Step 2), development of a personalized integrated care plan co-produced with the older person (Step 3), and coordinated implementation and monitoring by a multidisciplinary team with reassessment triggered by care plan schedules, acute events, or changes in social role or living situation (Step 4). Moreover, the updated ICOPE Handbook extended this framework into primary care and formalized its digital implementation architecture.19,28

The GRACE proposes that the geroscience lens and ICOPE infrastructure are most powerfully realized in combination: geroscience provides the biological mechanistic architecture; ICOPE provides the clinical assessment and care pathway infrastructure; and the preventive medicine framework provides the population targeting logic that determines which service element each individual requires at any given point in their aging trajectory.

Element I targets adults aged ≥50 years who are functionally intact and free of frailty but demonstrate early signals of accelerated biological aging detectable through gerodiagnostic biomarker profiling or ICOPE Step 1 screening. This represents the optimal window for geroscience-informed intervention: aging hallmarks are measurably active but have not yet produced irreversible organ damage, functional decline, or clinical multimorbidity.20 The AWGS 2025 consensus endorsed screening commencement at age 50, recognizing that midlife represents the early actionable stage of muscle health where reversal or deceleration is feasible.18,66

Entry into Element I is triggered by one of three pathways. The ICOPE Pathway uses the WHO ICOPE Step 1 screening to detect early, subclinical IC impairment before clinical disease is manifest.19,21 The Biomarker Pathway uses multiomics gerodiagnostic profiling: epigenetic aging clocks (PhenoAge, GrimAge, DunedinPACE) and plasma proteomic aging assessment across up to 11 organ systems.12,14,15 Individuals whose biomarker-derived biological age significantly exceeds their chronological age (acceleration >1 SD above population mean) are enrolled. The Organ Health Pathway uses validated assessment algorithms organized by organ system, encompassing standardized screening instruments and objective functional and structural evaluations conducted in accordance with the evidence-based guidelines relevant to each organ domain—as exemplified by the AWGS 2025 for muscle health.18

Element II targets older adults with two or more concurrent age-related chronic conditions who remain free of frailty and functional limitation. This group constitutes the majority of individuals aged ≥65 seen in primary care, yet receives the least tailored care: disease-specific guidelines are designed for single-condition populations, and the cumulative pharmacological burden of guideline-concordant disease management generates polypharmacy-associated iatrogenic harm.25 The geroscience perspective reframes multimorbidity not as disease accumulation but as a biological aging phenomenon: the simultaneous appearance of multiple diseases reflects shared upstream hallmark activation rather than independent coincidental disease onset.20,39 This reframing implies that the most effective strategy for managing multimorbidity is not merely optimizing each disease in isolation but targeting the shared aging biology that produces them collectively. The critical clinical imperative of Element II is to prevent the trajectory from multimorbidity to frailty.

Element III targets older adults with established frailty and/or functional limitation.26,22 From a geroscience perspective, frailty represents the clinical phenotype of advanced, multisystem hallmark convergence: the simultaneous expression of sarcopenia, inflammaging, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, and altered intercellular communication. Element III is not pessimistic: frailty is a dynamic state, and evidence from multiple randomized controlled trials demonstrates that frailty is reversible in a meaningful proportion of prefrail and moderately frail individuals with timely, appropriate, multidisciplinary intervention.26

A defining architectural feature of the GRACE is its dynamic, bidirectional transition design (Fig. 1): individuals move between elements based on evolving biological aging status and functional trajectory, not on fixed diagnostic criteria. Downward transitions—from Element I to II, or from II to III—are triggered by ICOPE monitoring signals (new domain impairment, rising frailty score), biomarker acceleration (biological aging pace ≥1 SD above individual baseline), or clinical events (hospitalization, new chronic disease, functional decline). Upward transitions—the goal of active intervention—are triggered by demonstrated IC improvement, frailty reversal, or biomarker deceleration.26 Systematic biannual ICOPE rescreening—as implemented in the INSPIRE ICOPE-CARE program at Toulouse University Hospital since 2020—provides the operational monitoring infrastructure for transition detection at population scale.21,67 ICD-11 code MG2A serves as the formal clinical coding entry point across all three elements—providing a shared nosological identity that unifies the GRACE under a single regulatory and reimbursement framework.30

The GRACE is designed to function within existing healthcare architectures. Element III maps directly onto established geriatric medicine services. Element II maps onto primary care and general medicine practice, enriched with geroscience-guided prescribing and ICOPE monitoring. Element I maps onto health promotion, preventive services, and community aging services, enhanced by aging biomarker capability where available. Scalability in low-resource settings is enabled by ICOPE’s digital architecture: the WHO ICOPE MONITOR mobile application enables IC screening and longitudinal tracking on basic mobile devices, extending elements I and II assessment capability to community health workers without specialist involvement.21,67

Equity imperatives require that aging biomarker reference databases be calibrated across diverse ancestral, socioeconomic, and cultural populations, and that social IC determinants—loneliness, food insecurity, housing instability—receive attention equal to that given to pharmacological interventions in determining aging trajectories.68

8. CHALLENGES, FUTURE DIRECTIONS, AND CONCLUSIONS

Geroscience has fundamentally transformed the intellectual architecture of aging medicine—from viewing aging as an inevitable backdrop to disease, to recognizing it as a modifiable, multimechanistic biological process that constitutes the master driver of most age-related morbidity.69,70 Yet the translation of geroscience into precision geromedicine continues to face interconnected scientific, regulatory, ethical, and economic challenges that must be honestly acknowledged alongside the field’s remarkable advances.

Scientifically, causal relationships between aging hallmarks and clinical outcomes in humans remain far less established than in model organisms; many promising preclinical interventions have demonstrated modest or null effects in early human trials. Regulatory frameworks for gerotherapeutics are absent in virtually all jurisdictions outside the FDA’s evolving TAME-influenced approach—a 2025 scoping review of 3780 publications identified no geroscience-specific regulatory frameworks in any major jurisdiction, and delineated four key barriers: the absence of regulatory recognition of aging as a therapeutic target, lack of clear approval pathways, economic uncertainties, and insufficient public and policy engagement. Ethically, the commercialization of biological aging assessment risks creating a two-tier system in which precision geromedicine remains accessible only to the affluent, deepening existing health inequities. The proliferation of direct-to-consumer epigenetic clock products and off-label gerotherapeutic prescribing—including unsupervised rapamycin self-administration—currently proceeds faster than the supporting evidence base and without adequate clinical oversight.

The future of geroscience is nonetheless compelling. Expert consensus anticipates that the coming decade will witness the first FDA approvals for geroscience-specific indications informed by the TAME regulatory template; integration of biological age monitoring into health insurance risk stratification and preventive care reimbursement frameworks; AI-guided personalized gerotherapeutic platforms capable of dynamically adapting intervention regimens to evolving aging trajectories; and the emergence of organ-specific therapeutic targeting—delivering gerotherapeutics preferentially to the organ systems identified by proteomics as aging most rapidly within each individual.

The institutional scaffolding upon which precision geromedicine is being built is already substantial. The NIH GSIG’s formalization of the geroscience paradigm in 2011–2014, the WHO’s codification of aging-associated decline in IC under ICD-11 MG2A in 2022, and the FDA’s acceptance of the TAME trial’s composite aging endpoint collectively represent landmark regulatory and scientific milestones. The extraordinary advances in aging biomarker science—from first-generation epigenetic clocks through fourth-generation organ-specific proteomic aging signatures—have delivered clinically deployable tools capable of characterizing individual biological aging trajectories with unprecedented molecular precision. The AWGS 2025 consensus demonstrates that geroscience principles can be translated into scalable clinical practice frameworks endorsed by major international guideline bodies.

The GRACE proposed in this review offers the field’s most operationally concrete translation of these advances: a three-element service architecture that simultaneously satisfies the logic of preventive medicine, the clinical infrastructure of WHO ICOPE, and the mechanistic imperatives of aging biology. Element I proactively targets accelerated biological aging in midlife adults before disease onset; Element II applies geroscience-guided management to decouple multimorbidity from the frailty cascade; and Element III delivers evidence-based, rehabilitation-focused care to frail older adults within a geroscience framework that pursues active frailty reversal rather than passive management. Together, the three elements form a longitudinally coherent service continuum unified by shared biomarker tools, a shared ICD-11 regulatory identity, and a shared clinical ambition: to ensure that the extended years of a long human life are lived with vitality, function, cognitive clarity, and independence.
