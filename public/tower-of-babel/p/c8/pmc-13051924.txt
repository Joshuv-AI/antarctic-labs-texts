Fatal Spontaneous Retroperitoneal Hematoma Following Intense Physical Exertion in a Young Healthy Male: A Case Report
Authors: Golnaz Moradi, Pezhman Farshidmehr, Reyhane Yahya, Hoda Asefi, Samira Mirzaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn young adults who experience sudden flank or abdominal pain and hemodynamic instability following intense physical activity, spontaneous retroperitoneal hemorrhage due to an iliac arterial lesion must be considered, even in the absence of evident contrast extravasation on CT angiography, requiring immediate endovascular assessment and damage‐control resuscitation.Keywords: CT angiography, endovascular stent‐graft, hemorrhagic shock, iliac artery rupture, spontaneous retroperitoneal hematoma, strenuous exertion

Introduction

Spontaneous retroperitoneal hematoma (SRH) is a rare but potentially life‐threatening condition characterized by hemorrhage into the retroperitoneal space without an obvious traumatic, iatrogenic, or aneurysmal cause. Although often linked to anticoagulation therapy, hemodialysis, or vascular abnormalities, SRH may also occur in individuals without predisposing factors, posing a significant diagnostic challenge [1, 2].

The retroperitoneum contains numerous vessels, including lumbar arteries, iliac vessels, and venous plexuses, which can bleed. Typical features include sudden abdominal or flank pain, hypovolemia, and occasionally Lenk's triad (pain, palpable mass, shock) [3]. Contrast‐enhanced CT is preferred for diagnosis and planning, with angiography used for both diagnosis and intervention [1, 4].

Although exceedingly rare, physical exertion, particularly involving intense Valsalva maneuvers or heavy lifting, has been identified as a trigger for vascular rupture [5]. We report the case of a young, previously healthy male who developed a fatal SRH following strenuous exercise, a presentation highlighting the importance of recognizing exertion‐related vascular emergencies.

Case Presentation

History and Examination

A 39‐year‐old male with no significant past medical history or medication use presented to the emergency department with acute‐onset, severe, progressive abdominal pain, predominantly localized to the left side. The pain began after intensive physical exertion involving heavy lifting early in the morning and progressively worsened, culminating in a syncopal episode prior to hospital arrival.

On initial presentation to the hospital at approximately 9:00 AM, the patient was hemodynamically unstable, exhibiting profound hypotension (BP: 70/65 mmHg) and tachycardia (HR: 110 bpm).

Investigations and Management

An urgent contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis revealed an extensive retroperitoneal hematoma with concomitant partial thrombosis of the left common iliac artery (CIA), without evidence of contrast extravasation (Figure 1a,b). He remained at the first hospital for about 8 h. After fluid resuscitation and stabilization, he was transferred to a tertiary center for further management.

(a,b) Contrast‐enhanced CT of the abdomen and pelvis illustrating a considerable retroperitoneal hematoma with partial thrombotic occlusion of the left common iliac artery (solid arrows).

Upon arrival at the second hospital at 5:30 PM, the patient's blood pressure was 100/70 mmHg, and the heart rate was 86 bpm. Initial laboratory investigations revealed a hemoglobin level of 10.3 g/dL, white blood cell count of 18 × 103/μL, and a platelet count of 265 × 103/μL. Renal function tests showed a serum creatinine of 1.22 mg/dL and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) of 47 mg/dL. Electrolyte levels were as follows: Sodium 140.9 mEq/L, potassium 4.61 mEq/L, and calcium 8.3 mg/dL. Liver enzymes were mildly elevated, with aspartate transaminase (AST) at 34 U/L and alanine transaminase (ALT) at 49 U/L. Coagulation parameters, including prothrombin time (PT) and international normalized ratio (INR), were within normal limits.

Emergency digital subtraction angiography (DSA) demonstrated the absence of definitive aneurysmal dilation; however, a small focal outpouching and an intraluminal floating thrombus were identified within the left common iliac artery (CIA) (Figure 2a). Importantly, there was no angiographic evidence of contrast extravasation to indicate active hemorrhage. Due to suspicion of a contained arterial rupture, endovascular repair of the left common iliac artery (CIA) was performed. This involved deployment of a Bentley BeGraft stent graft (10 × 57 mm) followed by a Fluency stent graft (12 × 66 mm). Post‐dilatation was then carried out using a 12 × 60 mm Armada balloon catheter to ensure optimal stent apposition and luminal patency (Figure 2b). The angiography and endovascular intervention were completed by approximately midnight.

(a) Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) demonstrating a focal outpouching (open arrow) and an intraluminal mobile thrombus (solid arrow) within the left common iliac artery. (b) DSA showing successful deployment of two stent grafts in the left common iliac artery to exclude the lesion and restore vessel patency.

Outcome

Following the intervention, the patient was transferred directly to the ICU for close monitoring. Despite aggressive supportive efforts, his clinical status deteriorated significantly overnight. Hemoglobin declined (from 9.4 g/dL to 8.1 g/dL), renal function worsened (creatinine levels increased from 2 mg/dL to 3.19 mg/dL), and marked electrolyte/acid–base derangements developed, including hypernatremia (sodium 157.6 mEq/L) and severe metabolic acidosis. Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated profound acidosis (pH 6.63, bicarbonate 5.1 mEq/L, and PCO2 48.1 mmHg). Coagulation parameters later became significantly abnormal (INR up to 2.71 with considerably prolonged PTT). Lactate level was not available. Aggressive supportive management, including fluid/electrolyte correction and ongoing packed red blood cell transfusion, was continued in the ICU (total transfused: 5 units of RBCs). No repeat angiography or surgical exploration was pursued based on the post‐procedural angiographic findings and clinical judgment.

Despite intensive care, the patient continued to deteriorate and necessitated endotracheal intubation around 11:00 AM the following day. He subsequently suffered cardiac arrest at about 2:00 PM and died despite cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Overall, the clinical course was consistent with refractory shock complicated by severe metabolic acidosis, acute kidney injury, and coagulopathy following spontaneous retroperitoneal hemorrhage.

Discussion

Spontaneous retroperitoneal hematoma (SRH) is a life‐threatening condition, most often arising from renal, lumbar, or iliopsoas sources and frequently related to anticoagulation [4]. Rupture of the common iliac artery (CIA) is rare and easily missed due to nonspecific findings and concealed retroperitoneal bleeding. Rapid imaging and endovascular control are therefore essential. The American College of Radiology recommends contrast‐enhanced CT with CT angiography (CTA) for rapid localization and detection of active bleeding at rates as low as 0.3 mL/min; catheter angiography is reserved for unstable patients or when simultaneous therapy is planned [6].

Reported causes of CIA rupture include penetrating atherosclerotic ulcer (PAU) [7, 8], aneurysm or pseudoaneurysm with infection [9], vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome (vEDS) [10], and segmental arterial mediolysis (SAM) [11]. Non‐aneurysmal PAU can precipitate rupture even in vessels of normal caliber [7]. In younger patients, vEDS and SAM should be considered due to their association with multifocal arterial fragility [10, 11]. A definitive etiologic classification cannot be established without histopathologic or genetic evaluation.

While most SRH is anticoagulation‐related, strenuous exertion has also been implicated. In a previous CrossFit‐associated case, imaging showed massive left CIA extravasation with a large retroperitoneal hematoma and CIA ectasia (10 mm), and the patient underwent endovascular stent placement [5]. To our knowledge, this is only the second reported case linked to strenuous exercise. A summary of previously reported cases in young adults is provided in Table 1.

For suspected CIA rupture, multiphase CTA is recommended to localize bleeding, detect arterial wall defects, and plan intervention. Venous‐phase imaging helps exclude iliac vein rupture, often associated with May–Thurner anatomy, which can mimic arterial bleeding [15, 16].

Endovascular therapy is preferred. A 2024 meta‐analysis of 141 patients reported 100% technical success for trans‐arterial embolization, supporting intervention even without visible blush [4]. Covered stent‐grafts rapidly seal focal defects while preserving pelvic flow, whereas embolization or surgery is reserved for unsuitable anatomy, failed repair, or infection [9].

Despite technically successful stent‐graft repair, our patient died from progressive shock and metabolic collapse, likely due to massive concealed blood loss, prolonged hypotension, and ischemia–reperfusion injury causing severe metabolic acidosis, renal failure, and multiorgan dysfunction. Evaluation for a connective tissue disorder panel was not possible due to rapid deterioration and death.

This case of exertion‐associated SRH with a focal CIA abnormality, but no active extravasation, supports a contained rupture from a discrete arterial wall lesion. Given the patient's young age and the absence of radiographic features of atherosclerosis, PAU remains a possible but unconfirmed mechanism, and the underlying arteriopathy cannot be determined from imaging alone. Key lessons include: (1) obtaining rapid multiphase CTA, including a venous phase if iliac venous rupture is suspected [6, 15, 16]; (2) initiating early endovascular management even in the absence of a contrast blush [4]; and (3) considering an underlying arteriopathy in young patients (e.g., connective tissue disorders such as vEDS, SAM, fibromuscular dysplasia, or focal dissection); as PAU is less typical in this age group unless atherosclerotic changes are evident [7, 11].

Conclusion

This case highlights a rare exertion‐related CIA rupture presenting as SRH. Even without contrast blush on CTA, subtle arterial changes warrant urgent endovascular intervention. Early recognition and timely management are crucial.
