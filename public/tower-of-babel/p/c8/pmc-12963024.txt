Concurrent Sporadic Late‐Onset Nemaline Myopathy and an Excessive Glycogen Accumulation Associated With Monoclonal Gammopathy
Authors: Felipe J S Jones, Ikreet Cheema, Ruple S Laughlin, Eli Muchtar, Teerin Liewluck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMonoclonal gammopathy‐associated myopathies (MGAMs) include light chain (AL) amyloid myopathy, sporadic late‐onset nemaline myopathy (SLONM), and vacuolar myopathy with monoclonal gammopathy and stiffness (VAMMGAS). These subtypes usually occur separately, although rare overlap has been described. We report a patient with monoclonal gammopathy and concurrent SLONM and excessive glycogen accumulation, resembling VAMMGAS but with distinct features.MethodsCase report with clinical, electrophysiological, pathological, and therapeutic characterization of a patient with IgG‐kappa monoclonal gammopathy and coexisting SLONM and glycogen accumulation within muscle fibers.ResultsA 69‐year‐old man developed subacute progressive axial and limb weakness, head drop, dysphagia, and weight loss. Examination showed proximal/distal weakness, neck extensor weakness, and lumbar hyperlordosis. EMG revealed myopathic motor unit potentials without electrical myotonia or complex repetitive discharges. Serum studies identified IgG‐kappa monoclonal protein with elevated kappa light chain and ratio. Muscle biopsy demonstrated nemaline rods (1% of fibers) and scattered fibers with non‐rimmed vacuoles (0.3% of fibers) containing PAS‐positive, diastase‐labile material consistent with concurrent SLONM and glycogen storage myopathy‐like pathology. Genetic testing for congenital nemaline and glycogen storage myopathies was negative. Plasma‐cell directed therapy with daratumumab, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone led to rapid functional improvement and M‐protein reduction. Further gains followed autologous stem cell transplantation.ConclusionThis case expands the MGAM spectrum, highlighting co‐occurrence of SLONM and an excessive glycogen accumulation responsive to immunotherapy and transplantation. These findings suggest a spectrum of related disease processes. Recognition of this combined pathology is clinically important, as affected patients may benefit from targeted immunotherapy or stem cell transplantation.

Monoclonal gammopathy has been associated with light chain (AL) amyloid myopathy and sporadic late‐onset nemaline myopathy (SLONM) [1, 2]. A third type of monoclonal gammopathy‐associated myopathy (MGAM), vacuolar myopathy with monoclonal gammopathy and stiffness (VAMMGAS), was recently described [3]. The three reported VAMMGAS patients had subacutely progressive proximal and axial weakness, muscle stiffness, weight loss, hyperCKemia, complex repetitive discharges, and an excessive glycogen accumulation in myofibers in the context of IgG‐kappa monoclonal gammopathy. Genetic testing for glycogen storage myopathy related genes was negative. These patients responded to immunotherapy, suggesting an immune‐mediated etiology [3]. We also previously reported a patient with glycogen storage myopathy and coexisting polyneuropathy, organomegaly, endocrinopathy, M protein, and skin changes (POEMS) syndrome, who had IgG‐kappa and IgA‐lambda biclonal gammopathies, normal creatinine kinase (CK), and no muscle stiffness or complex repetitive discharges [4]. These three MGAM subtypes typically occur in distinct patients, although rare cases of coexisting SLONM and AL amyloid myopathy have been reported [5, 6]. Herein, we describe a patient with monoclonal gammopathy and concurrent SLONM and glycogen storage myopathy‐like pathology.

Case

A 69‐year‐old previously healthy man with no family history of neuromuscular disorders presented with a 2‐year history of progressive weakness. Over months to 1 year, he gradually developed exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, neck stiffness, head drop, dysphagia, and limb weakness. He required to use his arms to pull himself up from sitting and lost 9 kg. He denied ptosis, diplopia, autonomic or sensory symptoms, or prior rhabdomyolysis. Examination revealed mild bilateral facial weakness, moderate neck extension weakness (Medical research council, MRC grade 4‐), lumbar hyperlodosis, and weakness affecting left proximal and distal upper extremity (MRC 4), right hip flexor (MRC 4), and right distal lower extremity (MRC 4+). The baseline MRC sum score was 55/60. Nerve conduction studies showed right median neuropathy at the wrist. Phrenic motor conduction studies and diaphragmatic ultrasound were normal bilaterally. EMG revealed small motor unit potentials with rapid recruitment in all muscles examined, most severely affecting proximal and axial musculature. Sparse fibrillation potentials were present in axial muscles. Myotonic and complex repetitive discharges were absent. Laboratory investigation revealed IgG‐kappa monoclonal protein (0.6 g/dL) with elevated serum kappa light chain and kappa: lambda ratio (2.62 and 2.88 mg/dL, respectively). Complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, CK (271 U/L; normal < 308 U/L), acid alpha‐glucosidase activity by blood spot, myositis specific and immune‐mediated necrotizing myopathy antibody panel, thyroid stimulating hormone, and HIV serology were normal/negative. Fat aspirate was negative for amyloid. Pulmonary function testing showed reduced maximal respiratory pressures. Echocardiogram was normal. Left trapezius muscle biopsy (Figure 1) showed rare necrotic, few regenerating, and several atrophic fibers. Aproximately 1% of myofibers contained nemaline rods on modified Gomori trichrome staining (all of which were atrophic). Scattered fibers (~0.3%) harbored non‐rimmed vacuoles filled with PAS‐positive, diastase‐labile material consistent with glycogen accumulation. Myophosphorylase reactivity was preserved. Congo‐red staining viewed under rhodamine optics showed no amyloid. Electron microscopy and immunohistochemistry for immunoglobulin light chains were not available. Next‐generation sequencing revealed no pathogenic variants in genes associated with inherited nemaline or glycogen storage myopathies. Bone marrow biopsy was negative for plasma‐cell dyscrasia, lymphoproliferative disorder, and amyloid. The patient was diagnosed with SLONM and a concomitant glycogen storage myopathy‐like pathology associated with IgG kappa monoclonal gammopathy. He was treated with daratumumab, lenalidomide, and dexamethasone, 6 weeks after treatment initiation, his weakness showed marked functional improvement and reduction of the serum M‐spike to 0.3 g/dL. At 6 months, he was able to rise from sitting without using his arms, and his head drop had improved significantly. Following autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) with melphalan conditioning, he experienced further improvements in strength and overall functionality. At last follow‐up, the MRC sum score was 58/60, with partial improvement of head drop (MRC grade 4+), andserum M‐spike was 0.1 g/dL.

Discussion

This case expands the phenotypic spectrum of MGAM by demonstrating the co‐occurrence of SLONM and a potentially novel entity characterized by glycogen storage myopathy‐like pathology, resembling but not fully conforming to prior VAMMGAS reports [3]. Although the frequency of vacuolated fibers was relatively low, the consistent identification of PAS‐positive, diastase‐labile material in conjunction with negative genetic testing supports an acquired rather than inherited process.

In contrast to previously reported VAMMGAS patients, our patient had normal CK and no complex repetitive discharges, resembling our previously reported POEMS patient with biclonal gammopathies and glycogen storage myopathy‐like pathology [4]. These differences suggest that the pathological findings do not associate with the full VAMMGAS phenotype and might represent a broader, heterogeneous spectrum that requires further characterization. The pathophysiology of this potentially novel glycogen storage myopathy remains uncertain but may involve direct monoclonal protein‐mediated myotoxicity or immune‐mediated muscle injury, similar to mechanisms potentially implicated in SLONM [7]. Light chain deposition within the rod‐containing fibers has been identified in a few patients with monclonal gammopathy‐associated SLONM [8]. This feature was evaluated in only one of the previously reported VAMMGAS cases and was negative. The subacute disease course and marked responsiveness to plasma cell–directed therapy and autologous stem cell transplantation in our case parallel prior VAMMGAS reports and our previously reported patients with ASCT‐responsive myopathy featuring glycogen storage pathology, [3, 4].

While the limited number of reported cases may preclude definition of a distinct nosological entity, the term monoclonal gammopathy–associated glycogen storage myopathy (MG‐GSM) may be used to describe this emerging spectrum, as not all patients exhibit the complete clinical or electrophysiological features originally reported in VAMMGAS despite shared pathological findings.

Clinicians should screen for monoclonal gammopathy in adults with subacute‐onset glycogen accumulation on muscle biopsy when genetic testing is unrevealing. Recognition of this potentially treatable entity is critical, as affected patients may experience substantial clinical improvement with immunotherapy or ASCT.
