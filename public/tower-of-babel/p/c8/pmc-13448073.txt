Dual-attention residual U-Net with Huber loss for robust and efficient porosity prediction from well logs
Authors: Amirreza Mehrabi, Matin Mahzad, Majid Bagheri, Majid Nabi Bidhendi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate porosity prediction from well logs is critical for reservoir characterization, yet existing deep learning approaches face notable limitations. Sequential LSTM-based architectures suffer from computational bottlenecks due to their recurrent processing of depth sequences, which limits training efficiency and scalability on large well-log datasets. Single-pass CNN-Transformer models, while more parallelizable, often fail to preserve multi-scale features, resulting in inadequate capture of geological heterogeneity across varying stratigraphic resolutions. To address these challenges, we propose a novel 1D U-Net architecture that integrates ResNet blocks with dual attention mechanisms and is trained using Huber loss for robust handling of well-log noise. The encoder-decoder structure employs residual blocks for efficient, parallelized processing of depth-windowed sequences, overcoming LSTM’s sequential bottlenecks while preserving multi-resolution features through skip connections enhanced by attention gates. Self-attention at the bottleneck further captures global stratigraphic patterns across depth intervals, compensating for the limited multi-scale representation in standard CNN-Transformer designs. Huber loss (with δ = 0.005 calibrated to the empirical distribution of training residuals) combines quadratic penalties for high-precision fitting in tight formations and linear penalties for outlier robustness, with approximately 24.7% of residuals engaging the linear regime to mitigate measurement artifacts common in well-log data. We evaluate the model on a carbonate reservoir dataset using blind well testing. Compared to baselines including CNN-Transformer, PPTransformer, U-Net-LSTM, and PSO-GBDT, our architecture achieves superior performance with R² = 0.9912 ± 0.0008 and RMSE = 0.0042 ± 0.0003, while training approximately 7 times faster than U-Net-LSTM. Autocorrelation-corrected paired t-tests with empirically justified effective sample size (, ) and large effect sizes (Cohen’s d > 0.8) confirm statistically significant improvements at across all baseline comparisons. Attention visualizations demonstrate focus on lithologically sensitive depth intervals, enhancing geological interpretability. This work establishes ResNet-based encoder-decoder architectures with dual attention and robust loss functions as an efficient and accurate framework for well log-based porosity prediction.Keywords: Porosity prediction, Well logs, Residual U-Net, Attention mechanism, Huber loss, Carbonate reservoirsSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Porosity prediction from well logs is fundamental to reservoir characterization, guiding hydrocarbon exploration, production optimization, and subsurface storage site assessment. While direct core measurements provide ground truth, their spatial coverage is limited and expensive, necessitating predictive models that leverage readily available well log measurements including gamma ray, bulk density, compressional sonic, and resistivity for continuous depth profiling. However, well log data present significant modeling challenges: geological heterogeneity introduces complex nonlinear relationships, measurement artifacts create outliers, and stratigraphic boundaries demand multi-scale feature extraction. These characteristics require robust architectures capable of capturing both local correlations and global dependencies while maintaining resilience to measurement noise.

Traditional machine learning approaches established baseline performance benchmarks. Feng et al.1 applied Random Forest for missing well log imputation, demonstrating ensemble methods’ utility for nonlinear feature interactions but remaining limited by inability to model sequential depth dependencies inherent in stratigraphic data. Zhang et al.2 integrated particle swarm optimization with support vector machines for dolomite reservoir characterization, optimizing hyperparameters including regularization coefficients and kernel parameters. Moosavi et al.3 developed Fuzzy support vector regression with fuzzy c-means clustering for porosity prediction, incorporating uncertainty quantification in well log interpretation. He4, achieved state-of-the-art performance among traditional methods with PSO-optimized gradient boosting decision trees through iterative ensemble learning. Despite strong performance, tree-based methods fundamentally lack capacity to exploit depth-ordered sequential structure or learn hierarchical feature representations across spatial scales.

Neural network architectures introduced parametric nonlinearity and gradient-based learning. Multi-layer perceptrons provided frameworks for complex input-output mappings, with Alatefi et al.5 employing Bayesian-optimized MLPs for carbonate reservoir porosity, Bennani et al.6 comparing MLPs with support vector regression and Random Forest for shale formations, and Talebkeikhah et al.,7 benchmarking multiple methods with SVM achieving superior results. While MLPs model nonlinear relationships through hidden layer activations, their fully connected architecture treats features independently, discarding sequential depth structure encoding geological stratification and failing to leverage spatial autocorrelation in well log measurements. The growing adoption of deep learning for subsurface characterization has demonstrated neural networks’ capacity to capture complex geological patterns, with U-Net architectures showing particular promise for multi-scale feature extraction in seismic interpretation8, three-dimensional petrophysical property characterization9, and attention-based seismic denoising10.

Convolutional neural networks introduced spatial feature extraction suited to depth-sequential data. Wang et al.11 developed 1D CNN with sliding window processing to capture local spatial correlations in depth-sequential measurements, while Zhong et al.12 pioneered 2D CNN application transforming well logs into geological feature representations. These approaches successfully exploit local receptive fields for stratigraphic pattern identification but remain constrained by fixed window sizes, precluding long-range dependency modeling across extended depth intervals characterizing large-scale geological structures such as unconformities and diagenetic alteration zones.

Recurrent architectures addressed temporal dependency limitations through memory mechanisms. Chen et al.13 implemented multilayer LSTM for depth-sequential porosity prediction, demonstrating superior performance over GRU at depth inflection points. While LSTMs effectively model temporal dependencies through cell state propagation, they suffer from critical computational bottlenecks. Their sequential processing prevents parallelization during training, as each time step must wait for the previous one to complete. This results in substantially longer training times that scale linearly with sequence length, creating a severe limitation when processing thousands of depth measurements across multiple wells.

Recognition that spatial and temporal patterns contribute complementary information motivated hybrid architectures. Shan et al.14 proposed dual-branch CNN-BiLSTM with attention where CNN captures local spatial properties while bidirectional LSTM processes temporal context. Wang and Cao15, developed integrated networks combining 1D CNN for hierarchical feature extraction with BiGRU for bidirectional variation trend capture. Shi et al.16 employed 1D CNN-GRU for tight sandstone reservoirs with Pearson correlation-based feature selection. Mehrabi et al.17 developed hybrid convolutional recurrent neural networks for complex carbonate reservoirs, integrating spatial feature extraction with sequential modeling. These hybrids successfully integrate spatial and temporal modeling but inherit sequential processing bottlenecks from recurrent components, limiting training efficiency at scale. Furthermore, single-pass encoder structures lack explicit multi-resolution feature preservation mechanisms essential for simultaneously capturing fine-scale lithological variations and large-scale stratigraphic trends—a deficiency particularly consequential in heterogeneous carbonate reservoirs exhibiting porosity variations across multiple spatial scales.

Transformer architectures emerged leveraging self-attention for global dependencies without sequential constraints. Sun et al.18 introduced CNN-Transformer hybrids where CNN layers extract spatial features while Transformer encoders capture global dependencies through parallel computation, overcoming RNN sequential processing limitations. Huang19, developed PPTransformer adapting Vision Transformer framework with convolutional feature extraction and multi-head self-attention incorporating lithological constraints. Oppong et al.20 combined U-Net encoder-decoder with LSTM where U-Net extracts multiscale spatial features through symmetric contracting and expanding paths while LSTM models sequential depth-wise trends. While Transformer approaches successfully parallelize global dependency modeling and recent U-Net hybrids preserve multi-resolution features through skip connections, critical limitations persist: pure Transformers process sequences in single-pass encoders without explicit multi-scale feature preservation across network depth, while U-Net-LSTM hybrids reintroduce sequential constraints negating training efficiency gains. Moreover, existing frameworks predominantly employ mean squared error or mean absolute error loss functions which either disproportionately penalize outliers through quadratic amplification or uniformly weight residuals, failing to provide robust optimization suited to measurement artifacts, borehole conditions, and instrument calibration errors ubiquitous in field-acquired well log data.

This study addresses these architectural and optimization limitations through three innovations. First, we propose a 1D U-Net encoder-decoder framework integrating ResNet blocks in place of sequential LSTM components, enabling parallelized processing of depth-windowed sequences while preserving gradient flow through skip connections that mitigate vanishing gradients via identity mappings. U-Net skip connections preserve multi-resolution spatial information by transmitting high-resolution feature maps from encoder to decoder stages, enabling reconstruction of fine-grained details while maintaining computational efficiency through parallel processing. Second, we implement dual attention combining attention gates on skip connections for selective multi-resolution feature weighting with self-attention at the bottleneck for global stratigraphic pattern capture across entire depth sequences. Third, we employ Huber loss with δ = 0.005, calibrated through sensitivity analysis to position the quadratic-to-linear transition at the 75th percentile of the empirical training residual distribution in normalized porosity space, ensuring the linear outlier-resistance regime is actively engaged for approximately 25% of training samples corresponding to artifact-contaminated measurements—combining variance minimization of least squares with outlier resistance of absolute loss for robust well log optimization containing sporadic artifacts from tool malfunction and formation invasion effects.

This architectural synthesis achieves computational efficiency through parallel ResNet processing, multi-scale feature preservation via U-Net skip connections, adaptive feature weighting through dual attention, and robust optimization via Huber loss. We evaluate our approach on carbonate reservoir datasets, benchmarking against four state-of-the-art methods: CNN-Transformer18, PPTransformer19, U-Net-LSTM20, and PSO-GBDT4. Performance is assessed using coefficient of determination, root mean squared error, and mean absolute error metrics, with statistical validation via paired t-tests and effect size analysis. Attention visualization examines model interpretability for geological applications, investigating whether the architecture focuses on lithologically sensitive depth intervals.

Methodology

Figure 1 presents the end-to-end experimental workflow adopted in this study. The pipeline begins with data acquisition from seven hydrocarbon wells and preparation of continuous NMR-derived ground truth labels, followed by standardized preprocessing and well-level partitioning to ensure clean separation between training, validation, and blind test sets. Final evaluation is performed on the completely blind test well HD-04, with performance benchmarked against four state-of-the-art baselines using autocorrelation-corrected statistical tests. Geological setting and data acquisition

The study utilizes well log data from seven hydrocarbon wells (HD-01 through HD-07) penetrating a carbonate reservoir located in an offshore Middle Eastern basin (Fig. 2). The reservoir comprises a stratigraphic succession of six lithological units — Ghar Cap Rock, Ghar-1, Ghar-2, Ghar-3, Asmari-A, and Asmari-B — spanning a depth interval of approximately 350 m with cumulative thickness variations controlled by synsedimentary tectonics and differential compaction across the anticline structure. Porosity distribution within this succession is controlled by superimposed diagenetic processes operating at multiple spatial scales: regional dolomitization creates high-porosity dolomite intervals exhibiting characteristic RHOB decrease and GR suppression; localized dissolution along stylolites and fracture networks generates vuggy secondary porosity that decouples sonic transit time from the time-average relationship; and pervasive cementation by calcite and anhydrite creates tight baffles that compartmentalize the reservoir vertically. These lithological controls manifest as highly nonlinear, spatially variable relationships between well log responses and effective porosity — a heterogeneity regime that fundamentally requires multivariate nonlinear modeling and motivates the architectural design choices presented in this study. Well log suites comprise four standard petrophysical measurements acquired at 0.15-meter depth intervals: gamma ray (GR) in gAPI units quantifying natural radioactivity related to clay content, bulk density (RHOB) in g/cm³ from compensated density logging, compressional sonic transit time (DT) in µs/ft from acoustic wave propagation, and deep resistivity (RD) in Ω·m from deep induction measurements. Each well comprises approximately 13,000 depth samples, yielding a total dataset of ~ 91,000 measurements across the seven wells. Figure 4 presents representative well log display showing the characteristic responses of these measurements in the carbonate reservoir. Effective porosity (PHIE) serving as ground truth was derived from nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) logging acquired continuously at 0.15-meter depth resolution across all seven wells, providing direct formation porosity measurements at every sampled depth interval without interpolation or gap-filling. NMR processing followed a standard workflow: raw T2 distributions were acquired using a 0.6-ms echo spacing and 128 echoes per stack, with effective porosity computed by integrating the T2 distribution above a 33-ms cutoff to exclude clay-bound water contributions, consistent with established carbonate NMR interpretation practice. NMR-derived effective porosity was calibrated against helium porosimetry measurements on approximately 2,100 core plug samples (average spacing 0.30 m) across the seven wells following standard API RP40 procedures. The calibration yields R²=0.94 and RMSE = 0.004 v/v between NMR-derived PHIE and core helium porosity, confirming measurement accuracy within the NMR tool’s intrinsic precision floor. Figure 5 presents the NMR-versus-core calibration plot with regression statistics. This calibration step corrects for residual clay-bound water contributions and confirms that NMR-derived PHIE provides reliable continuous ground truth at the native 0.15-meter sampling resolution of the input well logs, eliminating label sparsity and interpolation artifacts entirely.

NMR logs were acquired on the same physical logging tool string as the density, sonic, gamma ray, and resistivity measurements in a single continuous logging pass, ensuring co-registration at identical depth stations without post-acquisition depth shifting or depth-matching correction. Depth references for all measurements were tied to a common cable depth reference at surface, eliminating any possibility of systematic depth offset between input logs and the NMR-derived target variable. No core-to-log depth adjustment was applied to the NMR-derived PHIE target variable, eliminating any possibility of information leakage through log-derived porosity substitution. The NMR ground truth therefore reflects direct formation measurements entirely independent of the input log processing workflow.

The dataset undergoes partitioning into training, validation, and blind test subsets with distinct roles in model development. Well HD-04 is designated as the completely blind test well, containing approximately 13,000 depth samples that remain entirely unseen throughout model development and optimization, serving exclusively for final performance assessment on a truly independent geological realization. To prevent window-level overlap between training and validation partitions inherent to the sliding window construction, we adopt well-level partitioning for the validation set. Wells HD-01 and HD-02 are designated as validation wells, with their complete depth profiles withheld from training. The remaining four wells (HD-03, HD-05, HD-06, HD-07) constitute the training set, providing approximately 52,000 depth samples for gradient-based optimization, while the two validation wells contribute approximately 26,000 samples for early stopping and model selection. This well-level partitioning ensures complete separation between training and validation windows, providing a clean generalization signal for hyperparameter tuning and early stopping criteria. The blind well HD-04 provides the critical test of whether learned patterns transfer to completely unseen geological settings, as this well contributed zero samples to training or validation, representing a scenario analogous to applying the trained model to a newly drilled well in field operations. The partitioning strategy maintains statistical representativeness across splits, verified through Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests confirming distributional equivalence (p > 0.05) for all input features and target porosity across the three well subsets, with equivalence confirmed consistently across all five independent training runs.

Sliding window strategy for sequential depth modeling

The architecture processes well log data through a sliding window approach that converts continuous depth sequences into fixed-length input segments suitable for hierarchical feature extraction. Rather than treating individual depth measurements independently, the model requires local stratigraphic context to enable multi-scale downsampling and capture both fine-grained lithological variations and large-scale stratigraphic trends. For each target depth dd d where porosity prediction is required, we extract a centered window of \:W consecutive depth measurements spanning the interval . The resulting input tensor contains four well log measurements (GR, RHOB, DT, RD) across \:W spatially ordered depth positions.

The window length parameter is set to depth samples, corresponding to 9.6 m of vertical formation given the 0.15-meter sampling interval. This window size was determined through empirical validation to satisfy two competing requirements: sufficient spatial extent to capture local stratigraphic continuity and enable four levels of binary downsampling through the encoder hierarchy , yet computationally tractable for batch processing during training. Smaller windows would limit the receptive field and prevent adequate downsampling depth, while larger windows would increase computational cost without proportional performance gains given the predominantly local nature of well log correlations21.

The sliding window advances through each well’s depth profile with unit stride (), generating overlapping training samples that ensure comprehensive coverage of depth-wise variations. For a well containing \:N depth measurements, this produces windowed samples, each labeled with the core-measured porosity value at the window’s center depth. Depths at well boundaries where complete windows cannot be constructed—specifically the first and last samples—are handled through symmetric zero-padding, extending the well log sequences with zero-valued measurements to maintain consistent input dimensionality. This padding strategy enables predictions across the full vertical extent of each well while acknowledging reduced prediction confidence at extreme boundaries due to asymmetric context availability.

During training, windowed samples from the four training wells (HD-03, HD-05, HD-06, HD-07) are pooled and shuffled, enabling the model to learn stratigraphic patterns generalizing across different geological intervals represented in the dataset. The validation wells (HD-01 and HD-02) monitor generalization during training through early stopping and learning rate scheduling, with their complete depth profiles entirely withheld from the training pool to ensure clean window-level separation. The blind test well HD-04 undergoes identical windowing transformation for final evaluation, though this well contributed zero samples to training or validation phases. This windowing framework transforms the porosity prediction task into a supervised learning problem where the model learns the mapping from depth-windowed well log inputs to center-depth porosity outputs, with network parameters optimized through backpropagation.

Huber loss function

Training employs Huber loss to balance sensitivity to small prediction errors with robustness against large outliers arising from measurement artifacts in field-acquired well log data. The loss function implements a piecewise definition that transitions between quadratic and linear penalties based on residual magnitude:

where \:y represents the ground truth porosity at the target depth,denotes the center-extracted model prediction, and defines the threshold separating the quadratic and linear regimes. For residuals satisfying , the quadratic penalty applies, generating gradients proportional to residual magnitude that drive tight convergence in well-characterized formations. This quadratic regime enforces variance minimization analogous to mean squared error optimization, penalizing even small deviations to achieve precise predictions in the majority of samples exhibiting low measurement noise.

For residuals exceeding the threshold , the penalty transitions to the linear term , which grows proportionally rather than quadratically with increasing error magnitude. This linear regime reduces the influence of outliers stemming from borehole washouts, tool calibration drift, formation invasion effects, or other measurement artifacts that create sporadic large residuals in the training data. Unlike quadratic penalties that amplify outlier contributions through squared terms—potentially destabilizing optimization by allowing individual extreme samples to dominate gradient updates—the linear penalty bounds outlier influence to constant-magnitude gradients regardless of error size. Learning rate scheduling employs ReduceLROnPlateau with patience 10 epochs, multiplicative reduction factor 0.5, and validation loss as the monitoring metric. When validation performance fails to improve for 10 consecutive epochs, the learning rate reduces by half to enable finer-grained parameter updates as optimization approaches local minima. Early stopping monitors validation loss with patience threshold 30 epochs, terminating training when validation performance stagnates for 30 consecutive epochs to prevent overfitting to the training distribution. Model checkpointing saves network parameters achieving minimum validation loss throughout training, with the best-performing checkpoint restored for final test set evaluation. Training continues for maximum 200 epochs or until early stopping criteria trigger, whichever occurs first.

During inference on the blind test well HD-04, predictions are generated through depth-wise sliding window evaluation. For each target depth dd d in the test well, the centered 64-depth window is extracted and processed through the trained network via forward pass . The center position prediction is extracted as the porosity estimate for depth \:d. The window advances by unit stride to depth and the process repeats, generating continuous predictions across the well’s vertical extent. Edge effects at well boundaries—specifically the first and last 32 depths where complete centered windows cannot be constructed—are handled via symmetric zero-padding to maintain consistent input dimensions, though predictions in these boundary regions exhibit reduced confidence due to asymmetric context availability.

All experiments were executed on NVIDIA GPU hardware with CUDA acceleration, utilizing PyTorch 2.0 framework. Mixed-precision training through automatic mixed precision (AMP) reduces memory consumption by storing activations in float16 precision during forward passes while maintaining float32 precision for parameter updates, accelerating computation on tensor cores while preserving numerical stability. This enables larger effective batch sizes and faster training throughput without sacrificing convergence quality.

The U-Net-LSTM architecture required 29.6 h for convergence, representing a 7.0× training time increase relative to our proposed method. This efficiency gap stems from fundamental differences in computational structure. LSTM layers process depth sequences through state dependencies where computation at each position requires completion of the previous position, creating a sequential bottleneck that prevents parallelization across the depth dimension. GPU compute units remain underutilized as they await sequential operations to complete. ResNet-based encoder processes eliminate this constraint through position-independent convolutions, where operations at all depth samples execute concurrently. This architectural difference enables full utilization of GPU tensor cores for simultaneous matrix operations across spatial dimensions.

The training time advantage extends beyond computational efficiency to practical research workflows. Hyperparameter optimization, architecture search, and cross-validation require multiple training runs. The 7 times speedup enables approximately seven complete training cycles in the time required for a single U-Net-LSTM run, substantially accelerating model development. The reduced memory footprint (8.4 GB versus 11.8 GB) permits larger batch sizes on memory-constrained hardware, improving gradient estimate quality and training stability.

Huber loss with δ = 0.005 demonstrates convergence properties distinct from mean squared error employed by baseline methods. The validation loss trajectory exhibits smooth monotonic decrease without oscillatory instabilities. With 24.7% of training residuals actively engaging the linear penalty regime, the loss function provides genuine outlier resistance throughout training: quadratic penalties drive tight convergence on the majority of well-characterized formation samples, while linear penalties bound gradient magnitudes for the artifact-contaminated tail, preventing individual outliers from destabilizing optimization through excessively large parameter updates. This active engagement of both regimes distinguishes the calibrated Huber loss from MSE and validates its contribution as a distinct architectural component.

The theoretical minimum achievable prediction error — the Bayes error — for NMR-derived porosity prediction in heterogeneous carbonate reservoirs reflects two irreducible uncertainty sources. First, NMR measurement repeatability introduces an intrinsic precision floor of approximately ± 0.005–0.008 v/v under field acquisition conditions, arising from tool noise, borehole rugosity effects, and incomplete polarization recovery in microporosity-dominated intervals. Second, sub-tool-resolution heterogeneity — diagenetic features operating at centimeter scale below the 0.15-meter log sampling interval — creates genuine formation variability that no prediction model can resolve from log measurements alone. These represent fundamentally different error budgets: core-to-formation errors reflect sub-plug-scale heterogeneity and sampling representativeness, while log-to-log prediction errors reflect the model’s ability to learn the multivariate mapping between co-acquired measurements. This distinction is consistent with established practice in the well log porosity prediction literature, where sub-0.01 RMSE is routinely reported and accepted as a meaningful performance benchmark for log-derived porosity prediction tasks15,22. The proposed model’s MAE = 0.0031 falls within the NMR log precision floor, confirming that further accuracy improvement would be constrained by measurement physics rather than model architecture.

Comparative analysis

Direct pairwise comparisons quantify the magnitude of performance improvements achieved by our architectural design. Relative to CNN-Transformer18, our method achieves 41.7% RMSE reduction (from 0.0072 ± 0.0005 to 0.0042 ± 0.0003) and 42.6% MAE reduction (from 0.0054 ± 0.0004 to 0.0031 ± 0.0002). These improvements stem from U-Net encoder-decoder topology’s multi-scale feature preservation through skip connections, contrasting with the single-pass Transformer encoder that processes features unidirectionally without mechanisms to retain high-resolution spatial information from early layers. The attention gate mechanism amplifies this advantage by selectively weighting skip connection features based on decoder context, enabling adaptive emphasis of lithologically informative depth intervals while suppressing homogeneous zones that contribute minimal information for reconstruction.

Compared to PPTransformer19; R²=0.9778 ± 0.0012), our architecture demonstrates 37.3% RMSE reduction and 36.7% MAE reduction. Both methods incorporate attention mechanisms, but PPTransformer applies self-attention within a single-pass Vision Transformer framework that processes the entire depth sequence in parallel without hierarchical feature abstraction. Our dual attention strategy provides complementary benefits: attention gates on skip connections enable spatial feature selection at multiple resolution scales corresponding to different geological hierarchy levels (bed-scale, formation-scale, sequence-scale), while bottleneck self-attention captures global stratigraphic patterns across the full depth interval. This architectural decomposition aligns with the multi-scale nature of carbonate diagenesis, where porosity distributions reflect superimposed processes operating at different spatial scales—dissolution at pore scale, dolomitization at bed scale, and burial diagenesis at formation scale.

The 28.8% RMSE reduction relative to U-Net-LSTM20; R²=0.9823 ± 0.0010, RMSE = 0.0059 ± 0.0004) requires examination, as both architectures employ U-Net topology with skip connections for multi-resolution feature preservation. The performance gap arises from our replacement of LSTM components with ResNet blocks. LSTM hidden states propagate information through sequential multiplicative gating operations, where errors compound across depth steps through the recurrent dependency chain. Residual blocks instead process features through parallel convolutions with identity skip connections that provide direct gradient pathways, maintaining numerical precision throughout the network depth without sequential error propagation. ResNet blocks enable deeper architectures without vanishing gradient pathology—our 4-level encoder-decoder achieves effective depth of 20 + convolutional layers through residual connections, extracting more abstract feature hierarchies than possible with LSTM-based architectures constrained by gradient decay through sequential unrolling.

The computational efficiency warrants emphasis: our 4.2-hour training time versus U-Net-LSTM’s 29.6 h represents a qualitative shift in research workflow feasibility. This efficiency gain enables ensemble methods, extensive hyperparameter search, and uncertainty quantification through multiple training runs—approaches impractical within U-Net-LSTM’s computational budget.

The empirical results demonstrate that our Dual-Attention Residual U-Net achieves state-of-the-art porosity prediction accuracy through integration of four architectural principles: multi-scale feature preservation, parallel processing, adaptive attention mechanisms, and robust loss optimization. Each design decision addresses specific limitations in existing approaches.

The encoder-decoder architecture with skip connections addresses the information loss problem inherent to single-pass encoder networks. During encoder downsampling, spatial resolution decreases through progressive pooling operations, concentrating semantic information while discarding fine-grained spatial details. For geological applications, this creates tension: thin-bedded carbonate formations exhibiting meter-scale porosity variations require high spatial resolution for accurate characterization, yet large-scale stratigraphic patterns spanning hundreds of meters demand substantial receptive fields achievable only through aggressive downsampling. Single-pass architectures cannot simultaneously preserve spatial resolution and expand receptive fields.

U-Net skip connections resolve this limitation by transmitting high-resolution encoder features directly to corresponding decoder stages through concatenation, enabling the decoder to access both coarse semantic features from the bottleneck (capturing formation-scale trends) and fine spatial details from early encoder layers (resolving bed-scale variations). This mechanism explains our performance at stratigraphic boundaries visible in Fig. 9, where sharp porosity transitions occur over sub-meter intervals: the decoder combines bottleneck features encoding the regional porosity trend with skip-connected fine-resolution features preserving the exact depth location of lithological contacts.

The 41.7% RMSE improvement over CNN-Transformer directly reflects this multi-scale preservation advantage. Transformer encoders process sequences through self-attention layers that mix information across all positions, but operate unidirectionally from input to output without dedicated pathways for high-resolution feature preservation. When encoder depth increases to expand semantic capacity, early-layer fine-grained features become progressively diluted through repeated attention pooling operations, degrading the network’s capacity to reconstruct sharp spatial transitions. Skip connections bypass this information bottleneck by providing direct feature transmission from each encoder resolution level to its decoder counterpart, preventing fine-scale information loss regardless of network depth.

The replacement of LSTM components with residual convolutions yields both computational efficiency gains (7 times training speedup) and accuracy improvements (28.8% RMSE reduction versus U-Net-LSTM) through fundamentally different information propagation mechanisms. LSTM architectures process depth sequences through sequential state updates where each position depends on the previous position’s completion. This sequential dependency chain creates two limitations: a computational bottleneck where each depth step requires completion of the previous step’s forward pass before proceeding, prohibiting parallelization across the depth dimension; and error accumulation where numerical errors propagate through the recurrent state chain, with precision degrading as sequence length increases.

ResNet blocks eliminate sequential dependencies through parallel convolutions operating independently at each spatial position. All spatial locations process simultaneously in a single forward pass, fully exploiting GPU parallelism for tensor operations. The identity skip connection provides additional gradient pathways during backpropagation, where the gradient flow includes both the standard chain rule through convolutional layers and a direct additive path through the identity mapping. This additive structure ensures gradients flow directly from output to input without multiplicative attenuation, preventing the vanishing gradient pathology that constrains LSTM network depth. Our 4-level encoder-decoder with residual blocks achieves effective network depth exceeding 20 convolutional layers while maintaining stable gradients, enabling extraction of hierarchical feature abstractions from raw well log measurements (low-level edge detection) to mid-level lithological patterns to high-level stratigraphic trends—a feature hierarchy depth unattainable in LSTM architectures limited by gradient decay through sequential unrolling.

The accuracy improvement over U-Net-LSTM, despite both employing U-Net topology, confirms that parallel residual processing captures depth dependencies more effectively than sequential recurrent processing for well log data. This reflects the spatial rather than temporal nature of geological stratification: while depth sequences exhibit correlation structure (adjacent measurements share similar values due to lithological continuity), this correlation arises from spatial proximity rather than causal temporal dependencies. Convolutional receptive fields naturally model such spatial autocorrelation through local feature aggregation, whereas LSTM’s temporal modeling paradigm—designed for genuinely sequential data like text or time series—imposes unnecessary sequential constraints while introducing numerical error accumulation that degrades prediction accuracy.

The attention mechanism combination—attention gates on skip connections and self-attention at the bottleneck—implements a hierarchical feature selection strategy aligned with geological scale separation. Attention gates compute spatial attention maps that modulate skip connection features channel-wise, where the gating signal from the decoder provides semantic context indicating which spatial locations require fine-scale detail for accurate reconstruction, while encoder features supply the high-resolution information. This mechanism adaptively emphasizes lithologically heterogeneous depth intervals (e.g., dolomite-limestone contacts, vuggy porosity zones) while suppressing homogeneous formations where fine-scale features contribute minimal reconstruction value.

Bottleneck self-attention captures global dependencies through pairwise similarity computation between all depth positions, enabling the network to associate stratigraphically distant but lithologically similar intervals—for example, recognizing that porosity characteristics of a shallow dolomitized zone should inform predictions in a deeper dolomitized zone separated by hundreds of meters of intervening limestone. The multi-head decomposition with four heads enables parallel attention to different geological aspects: one head may focus on gamma ray patterns indicating clay content, another on density-neutron separation signaling gas effects, and others on resistivity variations reflecting fluid saturation—with the combined representation integrating these complementary signals into a unified stratigraphic context.

The dual attention strategy explains our performance across the entire porosity range: attention gates improve prediction accuracy in heterogeneous zones by selectively weighting fine-scale features, while self-attention enhances performance in data-sparse regions by leveraging global stratigraphic context from lithologically analogous intervals. This architectural decomposition aligns with geological principles of scale hierarchy, where carbonate porosity reflects both local diagenetic processes (bed-scale dolomitization, fracture networks) and regional burial history (formation-scale compaction, hydrocarbon emplacement).

The piecewise loss function combines mean squared error’s tight convergence properties for small residuals with mean absolute error’s outlier resistance for large residuals. This hybrid approach addresses the bimodal error distribution characteristic of well log data: the majority of measurements exhibit small errors arising from minor measurement noise and lithological micro-heterogeneity, while a minority contain large errors stemming from borehole washouts, tool malfunction, or formation invasion effects.

For the dominant population of near-accurate measurements, the quadratic penalty applies when residuals fall below the threshold, generating gradients proportional to residual magnitude that drive rapid convergence toward zero error in well-characterized formations, explaining our performance in the low-error regime (90th percentile error of 0.0068 versus 0.0095 for U-Net-LSTM). For outliers exceeding the threshold, the linear penalty yields constant-magnitude gradients that prevent individual outliers from dominating the optimization trajectory through excessively large parameter updates.

This gradient clipping prevents individual outliers with large residuals from exerting disproportionate influence during training. In mean squared error optimization, a single outlier with large residual contributes quadratically amplified loss, while many typical predictions with small residuals contribute minimal combined loss—the outlier exerts outsized influence despite representing a small fraction of samples. Huber loss equalizes this imbalance: the outlier contributes linearly scaled loss while typical predictions contribute quadratically penalized loss, reducing outlier influence relative to MSE.

The empirical validation appears in our maximum error metric: 0.0287 versus U-Net-LSTM’s 0.0356 (19.4% reduction). This worst-case improvement confirms Huber loss’s effectiveness in constraining prediction errors on challenging samples where measurement artifacts or geological complexities create large training residuals. The robust optimization prevents the network from overfitting to outliers at the expense of typical predictions, instead maintaining balanced performance across the full error distribution.

The 7 times training speedup relative to U-Net-LSTM (4.2 h versus 29.6 h) alters research workflow economics and enables deployment scenarios infeasible with sequential architectures. This efficiency gain arises from ResNet’s elimination of temporal dependencies that force serialized processing in recurrent networks.

Modern GPU architectures achieve peak performance through massive parallelism: NVIDIA A100 contains 6,912 CUDA cores executing identical operations across thousands of data elements simultaneously. LSTM forward passes exhibit inherent serialization—computing hidden state at position t requires completed computation of state at position t-1—creating data dependencies that prevent parallel execution across the depth dimension. Consequently, GPU utilization degrades: while batch dimension permits parallelism across samples, the depth dimension remains sequential, leaving GPU cores idle awaiting completion of predecessor time steps. For sequences of length 1000 depth samples, this serialization bottleneck dominates runtime, as each of the 1000 sequential steps underutilizes the GPU’s parallel capacity.

ResNet convolutions eliminate this bottleneck through spatial parallelism: the operation at position i proceeds independently of computations at other positions, enabling simultaneous processing across all positions. GPU tensor cores execute the full depth sequence in parallel within a single kernel launch, achieving near-peak hardware utilization. The efficiency extends to multi-sample batches: with batch size 128 and depth sequence length 1000, the GPU processes 128,000 positions concurrently, fully exploiting its parallel architecture.

This computational efficiency enables practical applications previously infeasible under LSTM’s computational budget. Uncertainty quantification through Monte Carlo dropout requires 100 + forward passes per prediction to estimate posterior distributions—tractable at 4.2 h per model but prohibitive at 29.6 h. Ensemble methods training 10 models with different initializations achieve diversity for robust predictions, feasible within 42 h (overnight execution on multiple GPUs) but requiring 296 h (12 + days) with LSTM. Hyperparameter search exploring 50 configurations becomes practical (210 h distributed across a GPU cluster) versus infeasible (1,480 h). These methodological enhancements—uncertainty quantification, ensembling, systematic hyperparameter optimization—transition from theoretical possibilities to operational realities through the computational efficiency gains enabled by parallel ResNet processing.

The memory efficiency advantage (8.4 GB versus 11.8 GB) compounds these benefits by permitting larger batch sizes within GPU memory constraints. Increasing batch size from 128 to 256 would improve gradient estimate quality by reducing stochastic noise, accelerating convergence and potentially enabling further training time reductions. The memory savings arise from ResNet’s stateless processing: convolutional operations maintain no persistent state between depth positions, requiring memory only for weight matrices and intermediate activations. LSTM architectures must store hidden states for all positions simultaneously during backpropagation through time, increasing memory footprint proportional to sequence length and hidden dimension.

Geological interpretation of model predictions

The spatial selectivity of the attention mechanism is architecturally designed to emphasize lithologically sensitive depth intervals. By construction, attention gates compute decoder-guided relevance scores that suppress homogeneous formation intervals while amplifying zones of rapid porosity change — specifically diagenetic contacts, vuggy dissolution zones, and tight cemented intervals characteristic of the Ghar-Asmari carbonate succession. At diagenetic contacts where dolomitization fronts create abrupt mineralogical transitions, simultaneous RHOB decrease and GR suppression produce sharp log deflections that the attention gates selectively weight during skip connection feature transmission. At vuggy dissolution zones, DT exhibits anomalous behavior and RHOB decouples from the expected density-porosity trend due to secondary pore space development — precisely the multivariate nonlinear signal that the decoder context signal identifies as requiring fine-scale reconstruction detail. At tight cemented intervals, calcite and anhydrite cementation drives all four logs toward matrix values, and attention weights are correspondingly suppressed, consistent with the minimal reconstruction information these homogeneous zones contribute.

The stratigraphic architecture of the reservoir — comprising Ghar Cap Rock, Ghar-1, Ghar-2, Ghar-3, Asmari-A, and Asmari-B units — imposes a hierarchical porosity structure that the dual attention mechanism captures at complementary scales. Bottleneck self-attention captures formation-scale porosity trends reflecting burial compaction history and regional diagenetic overprinting shared across stratigraphic units, while attention gates on skip connections resolve bed-scale porosity variations arising from localized dolomitization and dissolution operating within individual units. This scale decomposition is directly aligned with the known diagenetic architecture of Middle Eastern carbonate reservoirs, where regional burial diagenesis operates at formation scale while hydrothermal dolomitization and meteoric dissolution operate at bed scale (Ahmadi et al., 2025).

The model’s superior performance at stratigraphic boundaries — demonstrated by sub-meter porosity transitions captured in Fig. 9 — reflects the encoder-decoder architecture’s simultaneous access to compressed bottleneck features encoding regional porosity trends and high-resolution skip-connected features preserving exact boundary locations in depth. In geological terms, this corresponds to a model that understands both which stratigraphic unit it is predicting within and precisely where the lithological contact occurs — a dual-scale geological awareness that distinguishes the proposed architecture from single-pass Transformer approaches that encode regional trends at the expense of boundary sharpness. This architectural alignment with the multi-scale nature of carbonate diagenesis demonstrates that the reported performance gains reflect genuine improvement in the model’s capacity to learn geologically meaningful relationships between well log responses and effective porosity, rather than numerical optimization artifacts.

Limitations and future directions

While our architecture achieves state-of-the-art performance on carbonate reservoir datasets, several limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, evaluation focuses on a single geological setting—offshore Middle Eastern carbonate reservoirs—with lithological characteristics dominated by dolomitization and dissolution diagenesis. Performance in clastic reservoirs exhibiting different porosity-controlling mechanisms (compaction, cementation, clay content) requires validation. Transfer learning experiments could assess whether features learned from carbonate data generalize to sandstone formations, or whether formation-specific fine-tuning is necessary.

Second, the training dataset size (~ 91,000 samples from 7 wells) remains modest by deep learning standards. Data augmentation techniques—synthetic minority oversampling for rare porosity ranges, mixup regularization combining feature-label pairs from adjacent depth intervals—could enhance generalization. Active learning frameworks might optimize core sampling strategies, identifying depth intervals where new core measurements would maximally reduce prediction uncertainty.

Third, our current framework produces point predictions without uncertainty quantification. Bayesian extensions incorporating Monte Carlo dropout or ensemble methods would provide prediction intervals essential for risk-aware reservoir management decisions. Aleatoric uncertainty arising from measurement noise could be modeled through learned loss attenuation, while epistemic uncertainty from limited training data could be captured through variational inference over network parameters.

Fourth, extension to multivariate output prediction (simultaneous porosity, permeability, and water saturation) would provide comprehensive petrophysical characterization from well log inputs. Multi-task learning with shared encoder and task-specific decoder heads could exploit correlations between petrophysical properties while maintaining parameter efficiency.

Finally, incorporating geological domain knowledge through physics-informed constraints could improve interpretability and extrapolation beyond training data distributions. Petrophysical relationships (e.g., porosity-permeability correlations, Archie’s law for resistivity-saturation) could be encoded as soft constraints in the loss function, regularizing predictions to remain consistent with established geological principles.

Conclusion

Conventional deep learning models for well-log-based porosity prediction often fail to simultaneously preserve multi-scale geological features and support efficient training. These limitations stem from inadequate capture of hierarchical patterns across scales and the sequential processing bottlenecks inherent in recurrent architectures such as LSTMs. The proposed Dual-Attention Residual U-Net overcomes these challenges through the integration of a U-Net encoder-decoder topology with ResNet blocks. This architecture effectively preserves multi-scale features from bed-scale to formation-scale while enabling fully parallelized processing of depth-windowed sequences, resulting in a 7 times training speedup over LSTM-based methods and improved accuracy via stabilized gradient flow.

A second critical issue is the limited robustness of existing methods to lithological heterogeneity and measurement artifacts, which commonly causes unstable predictions and high sensitivity to outliers. The proposed framework addresses this limitation by incorporating dual attention mechanisms together with Huber loss. The dual attention modules dynamically weight features according to local lithological variations and global stratigraphic context, while Huber loss enables robust optimization that achieves tight convergence in clean intervals and strong resistance to outliers induced by logging tool errors.

Finally, many state-of-the-art baselines provide insufficient statistical gains and suffer from high computational costs that prevent practical deployment of advanced workflows such as ensembles, uncertainty quantification, and hyperparameter optimization. Comparative evaluation against four competitive baselines (CNN-Transformer, PPTransformer, U-Net-LSTM, and PSO-GBDT) demonstrates that the proposed model achieves statistically significant improvements with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d > 0.8). With performance metrics of R² = 0.9912 ± 0.0008, RMSE = 0.0042 ± 0.0003, and MAE = 0.0031 ± 0.0002 on carbonate reservoir test data, combined with markedly improved computational efficiency, the framework enables the practical use of ensemble methods, uncertainty quantification, and hyperparameter optimization workflows that were previously infeasible under sequential processing constraints.
