Orbital apex cavernous hemangiomas are rare, benign vascular malformations located in one of the most anatomically complex and surgically challenging regions of the orbit.1,2 Despite their indolent nature, lesions in this area can lead to progressive visual disturbances, diplopia, and retro-orbital pressure due to compression of critical neurovascular structures.3,4 While traditional open approaches such as orbitotomy or craniotomy remain standard in many centers, they often require extensive dissection with an increased risk of morbidity and poor cosmetic outcomes.5,6

In recent years, endoscopic endonasal techniques have emerged as minimally invasive alternatives, offering enhanced visualization, reduced disruption of surrounding tissues, and faster recovery.7–9 Among these, the ipsilateral mononostril transethmoidal approach provides a direct route to the medial orbital apex, enabling safe dissection while preserving nasal and orbital integrity.8,9 Herein, we report a rare case of an orbital apex cavernous hemangioma treated through this technique, with emphasis on surgical nuances and a brief literature review supporting its efficacy and safety.

Illustrative Case

A 60-year-old woman presented with a 1-month history of progressive retro-orbital pressure described as a deep-seated heaviness without pain, swelling, or proptosis. Symptoms were more prominent on the left and accompanied by binocular diplopia, particularly noticeable during leftward gaze. Ophthalmological examination revealed preserved visual acuity and no visual field deficits. Mild abduction deficit was observed in the left eye, consistent with a left sixth cranial nerve palsy.

MRI with gadolinium enhancement demonstrated a well-circumscribed, 10 × 8–mm hyperintense lesion in the left posterior extraconal orbital apex, encasing the inferior rectus muscle. The lesion showed homogeneous contrast enhancement on T1-weighted images and was suggestive of a cavernous hemangioma10 (Fig. 1A–C). CT confirmed intact bony structures. Given the lesion’s location and the patient’s progressive symptoms, surgical intervention via an endoscopic ipsilateral mononostril transethmoidal approach was recommended, with shared decision-making after discussing both resection and stereotactic radiosurgery options with the patient.11

Surgical Technique and Postoperative Course

Preoperative MRI and CT datasets were fused using a neuronavigation system. Under general anesthesia, the patient was positioned supine with the head secured in a Mayfield clamp. After sterilization and draping, a decongestant-soaked cottonoid was placed lateral to the middle turbinate in the left nasal passage. Following 15 minutes of decongestion, the ear, nose, and throat (ENT) team initiated the approach via the left nostril. The surgical steps are shown in Video 1. Partial resection of the middle and superior turbinates exposed the bulla ethmoidalis and uncinate process, allowing for identification of the maxillary sinus ostium. A standard ethmoidectomy was performed to create a transethmoidal corridor.The lamina papyracea (LP) was exposed, and its mucosal layer was removed. Neuronavigation confirmed the trajectory toward the lesion.

The neurosurgical team then proceeded with orbital entry. The LP was removed using a blunt curette and enlarged with rongeurs. After incising the periosteum, a reddish-purple, well-encapsulated mass was visualized. Using a combination of ring curettes and endoscopic forceps, we carefully dissected the lesion and removed it en bloc. Hemostasis was achieved using a hemostatic matrix, and a synthetic dural substitute was placed to support the medial orbital wall (Fig. 2). Intraoperative MRI confirmed gross-total resection (Fig. 1D–F). The patient was extubated and transferred to recovery without complications. Postoperatively, her retro-orbital pressure resolved immediately, and diplopia subsided within 1 week. No new cranial nerve deficits were observed, and she was discharged on postoperative day 1.

Discussion

Cavernous hemangiomas are the most common benign orbital tumors in adults, typically affecting women in their 3rd to 5th decades of life.1–3 These lesions are usually slow-growing and may remain asymptomatic until they begin to compress adjacent structures. When located in the orbital apex, they can cause retro-orbital pressure, diplopia, or progressive visual decline due to involvement of cranial nerves II, III, IV, and VI.4,5

Diagnosis is primarily radiological. On MRI, cavernous hemangiomas appear T2-hyperintense and show homogeneous enhancement after contrast administration.10 Thin-section MRI and CT are essential not only for diagnosis but also for surgical planning, particularly when considering minimally invasive approaches.10,11

Although conservative management may be appropriate for asymptomatic or small lesions, resection is preferred in symptomatic patients or those with progressive findings.12,13 Traditional open orbitotomies and craniotomies allow access to the orbital apex but are associated with greater morbidity due to the confined anatomy and proximity to critical neurovascular structures.5,6

The evolution of endoscopic skull base surgery has transformed access to deep-seated lesions of the medial and inferior orbit.7 Specifically, the ipsilateral mononostril transethmoidal approach allows for direct exposure of the medial orbital apex while preserving nasal and orbital integrity.8,9 This technique minimizes external scarring, reduces hospital stay, and avoids disruption of contralateral nasal airflow. Several case series have demonstrated its safety, with gross-total resection and favorable visual outcomes in carefully selected patients.6 ,9

The choice of a mononostril approach offers several advantages. By preserving the contralateral nasal mucosa and avoiding extensive sinonasal disruption, such as middle turbinate resection or posterior septectomy, this technique may reduce the risk of olfactory dysfunction and shorten postoperative nasal recovery. However, working through a single nostril may limit exposure and restrict bimanual instrument maneuverability, potentially affecting surgical precision. Comparative studies suggest that while the binostril approach allows greater instrument freedom and facilitates complex tumor manipulation, the mononostril route minimizes sinonasal trauma and better preserves postoperative quality of life in appropriately selected patients.14,15 Adequate exposure in mononostril cases heavily depends on individual nasal anatomy. For surgeons with limited experience in mononostril techniques, the binostril approach may be more suitable in complex cases or when rapid intraoperative maneuverability is necessary.

For patients who are not suitable surgical candidates, or when the lesion’s location makes surgery high risk, radiosurgical treatments such as Gamma Knife radiosurgery (GKRS) and fractionated stereotactic radiotherapy (FSRT) have emerged as viable alternatives. GKRS has shown mean tumor volume reductions exceeding 80%, with significant improvements in visual acuity and field defects in the majority of patients.16 Similarly, FSRT has demonstrated favorable outcomes, with consistent tumor control and symptom relief, particularly in deep-seated orbital lesions, and without major complications.17

In our case, the lesion’s medial extraconal location made it well suited for an endoscopic ipsilateral mononostril approach. The use of intraoperative navigation allowed for safe dissection and complete resection without complications. Although we did not encounter adhesions or difficulties during dissection, previous studies have reported that orbital apex cavernous hemangiomas may form early adhesions to adjacent neurovascular structures, potentially complicating surgical removal.12 The patient experienced rapid symptomatic relief and was discharged the next day. This outcome is consistent with growing evidence supporting the utility of endoscopic approaches for select orbital apex lesions.7,6

Finally, the success of this technique depends on multidisciplinary collaboration, typically between the ENT and neurosurgical teams. Angled endoscopes, neuronavigation, and four-handed techniques improve access and control in this anatomically constrained space. However, these procedures require a high degree of anatomical familiarity, coordination, and surgical precision.

Observations

This case highlights the feasibility and effectiveness of the endoscopic ipsilateral mononostril transethmoidal approach in managing a deep-seated orbital apex cavernous hemangioma. While traditional management options for such lesions include open orbitotomy, conservative observation, or radiosurgery,13,16,17 the endonasal corridor provides a minimally invasive yet direct route to the medial orbital apex, allowing for precise dissection and tumor removal.8,9

Despite the anatomical complexity of the region and the technical demands of the approach, gross-total resection was achieved without complications or additional cranial nerve deficits. The patient’s visual symptoms improved promptly, and postoperative recovery was rapid, supporting the use of this technique in carefully selected cases. This case adds to the limited body of evidence demonstrating the utility of modern endoscopic skull base techniques in the management of orbital vascular lesions.6,9 Importantly, resolution of the lateral gaze palsy following surgery suggests that the impairment was due to mass effect rather than direct nerve invasion, highlighting how clinical signs may not always correlate with the lesion’s precise anatomical location.

Lessons

Orbital apex cavernous hemangiomas, although benign and slow-growing, pose significant clinical and technical challenges due to their proximity to critical neurovascular structures.1,4 While total resection remains the definitive treatment for symptomatic lesions, traditional open surgical approaches often involve substantial morbidity.5,6

The endoscopic ipsilateral mononostril transethmoidal approach provides a less invasive alternative, particularly for medial orbital apex lesions. This technique allows for enhanced visualization, reduced tissue manipulation, and avoidance of external incisions—factors that contribute to shorter hospital stays and faster recovery.6–9 However, the approach requires a high level of technical expertise and is best performed using a four-handed technique involving both the neurosurgery and ENT teams.6, 9

This case also emphasizes a subtle clinical nuance: lesions located medially or inferiorly within the orbital apex may still present with symptoms suggestive of lateral involvement. In tightly confined anatomical spaces like the orbital apex, indirect mass effect on cranial nerve VI or mechanical restriction of extraocular muscles may lead to lateral gaze palsy. Such presentations should not be dismissed based solely on lesion location.

Successful outcomes depend on careful preoperative planning, detailed anatomical understanding, and appropriate patient selection. As illustrated in this case, gross-total resection can be safely achieved with favorable functional results. Nevertheless, patients should be thoroughly counseled regarding the risks, expected benefits, and alternatives to ensure informed decision-making in the context of this highly specialized surgical strategy.
