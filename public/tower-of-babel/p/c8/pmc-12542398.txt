Adult children migration and perceived discrimination among Indian older adults: evidence from longitudinal ageing study in India
Authors: Valatheeswaran Chinnakkannu, Shreyantika Nandi, Vandita Ranjan, Harihar Sahoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe migration of adult children significantly affects older adults’ lives in various ways. This study explores the association between adult children’s migration and perceived discrimination among older adults left behind in India.MethodsThe study utilises nationally representative data from the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI) Wave 1 conducted during 2017-18. The final sample included 24,747 older people aged 60 & above living with non-migrant, internal migrant, and international migrant children. Bivariate analysis was used to estimate the prevalence of perceived discrimination. Relative risk regression has been performed to show the association between migration status and perceived discrimination among older adults. Additionally, interaction terms were incorporated to examine whether the effect of a child’s migration on perceived discrimination is moderated by gender and place of residence.ResultsThe study highlighted that 18.53% of older adults with internal migrant children reported experiencing perceived discrimination in their daily life, followed by 17.48% of those with non-migrant children and 14.69% with international migrant children. Older adults with internal and international migrant children had a lower relative risk of perceived discrimination (RRR = 0.91,95% CI = 0.91–0.91), (RRR = 0.81,95% CI = 0.81–0.81) compared to those with non-migrant children. However, older women with internal migrant children (RRR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.03–1.03) and older adults living in urban areas with internal migrant children (RRR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.20–1.20) exhibit a higher relative risk of perceived discrimination. Additionally, being employed, dissatisfaction with living arrangements, low subjective social position, depression and experience of ill-treatment were all strongly associated with a high risk of perceived discrimination.ConclusionThe internal migration of adult children tends to intensify feelings of social exclusion and perceived discrimination, especially among older women and those living in urban areas. An integrated socioeconomic and gender-inclusive framework is necessary to address the social cost of migration on older adults left behind in India.Keywords: Child migration, Ageing, Perceived discrimination, LASI, India

Introduction

The share of the ageing population is rising globally at an unprecedented pace due to increasing life expectancy and declining fertility rates [1]. The United Nations (UN) estimated that the global population aged 65 and above has tripled since 1980, from 260 million to 761 million in 2021, and this figure is expected to reach 1.6 billion by 2050 [2]. While almost all developed countries have transitioned into a high level of ageing societies, most developing countries are rapidly acquiring this pattern [3]. India, the world’s most populous country, is not exempt from this demographic shift [4]. The number of older people in India doubled from 76 million in 2001 to 149 million in 2022, accounting for around 10.5% of the total population. It is expected to increase to 227 million (15%) by 2036 and 347 million (20.8%) by 2050 [5].

The rising ageing population in the country creates new concerns regarding care, dignity and well-being, especially within rapidly changing family and social structures. One of the critical social issues is the everyday discrimination experienced by older adults within the family and community settings [6, 7]. It encompasses a wide range of behaviours and attitudes, such as neglect, disrespect, social exclusion, and unfair treatment based on age, gender, caste, economic status or physical appearance [8]. A recent study indicates that 10.3% of Indian older adults experienced discrimination, citing age as one of several contributing factors [5].

In traditional Indian society, older adults are expected to live with their adult sons, while daughters typically relocate to their marital homes after marriage [9, 10]. The intergenerational co-residence system has long been the cornerstone of older adult care in India, with adult children expected to provide emotional, physical, and financial support to their parents [11]. However, this traditional family arrangement has been steadily eroding in recent decades due to changes in socioeconomic landscapes, rapid urbanization, and increasing migration rates among the younger generation for employment [12–14]. India had nearly 600 million internal and 18 million international migrants in 2020 [15, 16]. Unemployment, underemployment, low wages, poverty at the place of origin, higher wages, better job opportunities, and improved living conditions in destination areas prompt people to move out, often resulting in spatial separation between older adults and their adult children [17, 18]. Consequently, older adults living alone or only with their spouse rose from 22% in 2011 to 27% in 2017-18 [5].

The impact of migration of adult children on the well-being of older adults is multifaceted. Existing studies indicate that the absence of co-residing children contributes to emotional distress, loneliness, social isolation, and diminished support networks, especially among older women, widowed, single-child, and those in rural or low-income settings [19–24]. Moreover, older adults who are no longer economically active or lack ownership of property or assets are sometimes perceived by family members as a burden [25]. This perception can further reduce their status within the family, contributing to strained familial relationships and making them more susceptible to everyday discrimination within the family or communities [26]. Such discrimination may take various forms, including exclusion from household decision-making, neglect in healthcare and caregiving, or treatment with a lack of respect and dignity. In many instances, inadequate institutional support mechanisms further exacerbate these vulnerabilities [27, 28].

Concurrently, a growing body of literature reports that the financial remittance from migrant children is likely to positively impact older adults’ well-being by improving economic stability and access to healthcare, reducing labour demand, and enhancing their diet and leisure [29–31]. Some studies highlight that older adults with migrant children experience improved physical health, greater independence, and enhanced social status, mainly when remittances are regular and substantial [32–34]. Moreover, modern communication technologies and periodic visits can help sustain emotional ties, mitigating the negative psychological impacts of children’s absence [35, 36]. In extended family settings, the presence of other family members may buffer the adverse effects of children’s migration [37]. Nonetheless, research suggests balancing economic benefits and emotional and social losses is delicate. While financial support may alleviate material hardship, it often fails to substitute for the daily care, companionship, and social validation that children provide in co-residence arrangements [38–40]. Studies in India have noted that empty-nest older adults are more likely to report depression, poor self-rated health, and chronic illness due to the absence of daily emotional support [41–45].

Although a considerable number of studies examined the prevalence and drivers of discrimination and its effect on physical and mental health among older Indian adults [7, 46, 47], there remains a significant gap in understanding how children’s migration shapes perceived everyday discrimination among older adults. This study addresses this gap by exploring the effects of adult children’s migration on discrimination faced by older adults in India. Moreover, the study compares older adults across three groups: those whose children have migrated internally, those whose children have migrated internationally, and those whose children have not migrated. The findings have important implications for policies promoting dignity, inclusion, and successful ageing.

Data and methods

Data source and study population

The present study used the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI), Wave 1, a nationally representative survey of 73,396 older adults aged 45 and above conducted in 2017–18. The LASI used a multistage stratified area probability cluster sampling design, with a three-stage design in rural regions and a four-stage design in urban areas. The first stage in each state/UT consisted of selecting Primary Sampling Units (PSUs), which are sub-districts (Tehsils/Talukas). The next step included the selection of villages in rural regions and wards in urban areas within the specified PSU. In the third and final stage, households were selected from specific selected villages in rural areas. However, sampling in urban locations covers an additional stage. A single Census Enumeration Block (CEB) was randomly assigned to each urban area during the third stage. The fourth and final stage was the selection of households from this CEB. There is a comprehensive methodology section in the LASI survey report containing all the details regarding the survey design and the data collection [5]. For the present study, older persons aged 60 & above having adult male children were considered. After dropping missing cases from the dataset, the final analytical sample came around (n = 24,747; males = 11,760 and females = 12,987). The sample selection flowchart is shown in Fig. 1.

Main explanatory variable

The present study examines the association between the migration status of adult children and the perceived discrimination of older adults aged 60 & above. Specifically, the research focuses on the type of migration undertaken by the adult children to capture a deeper understanding of the issue . Hence, the migration status of children is considered the primary explanatory variable and is classified into three categories: non-migrant, internal migrant, and international migrants. The study evaluates the migration of at least one adult male child aged 18 and above. Children living in the household are classified as “non-migrants” (coded as 1), while those who are not household members living within the village/city, within the state, and outside the state are classified as “internal migrants“(coded as 2). Lastly, those living outside the country are classified as “international migrants“(coded as 3). Traditionally, in India, daughters generally migrate for marital purposes and settle in in-laws’ residences in accordance with patrilocal traditions [48, 49]. Hence, we have considered the migration of at least one adult male child to construct the main explanatory variable of the study.

Outcome variable

The outcome variable for the study is perceived discrimination. LASI provides a separate section that addresses perceived discrimination, which consists of six statements. This is a shorter version of the perceived discrimination scale (EDS) [50]. Following are the statements asked to determine perceived discrimination: 1) you are treated with less courtesy or respect than other people, 2) You receive poorer service than other people at restaurants or stores, 3) People act as if they think you are not smart, (4) People act as if they are afraid of you, (5) You are threatened or harassed, (6) You receive poorer service or treatment than other people from doctors or hospitals. Each statement was given six response categories viz. 1 = Almost every day, 2 = At least once a week, 3 = A few times a month, 4 = A few times a year, 5 = Less than once a year, and 6 = Never. The final variable of “perceived discrimination” has been categorized into “no” and “yes”. Those who responded with “6” were grouped into “no” (coded as 0), and those who responded with “1–5” were grouped into “yes” (coded as 1). Cronbach’s alpha check was performed to check the reliability of the statements, and the value (α = 0.86) indicates a good reliability of the measure.

Covariates

The present study focuses on a population aged 60 and above who are classified as “elderly” or “senior citizens” in India [51]. Hence, the age classification was done by dividing them into three categories, i.e., “60–69”, “70–79”, and “80 & above”. The respondents’ sex was grouped as “male” and “female”. Marital status was categorised as “currently married” and “currently not married”. The caste of the household was grouped into “non-Scheduled Caste/Scheduled Tribe (SC/ST)” and “SC/ST”. Household religion was grouped into “Hindu”, Muslim” and “Others”. The level of education was categorised as “no education”, “up to primary”, “secondary & above”. The work status has been coded as “never worked”, “currently working”, “currently not working” and “retired”. The household monthly per capita consumption expenditure (MPCE) quintile was categorised as “rich”, “middle” and “poor”. For the present study, the place of residence of the respondents was classified as “rural” and “urban”. The country region was categorized as “Southern”, “East & North-east”, “North & Central” and lastly “Western”.

The number of children has been categorised as “single child” and “two or more children”. Satisfaction with living arrangements was categorised as “satisfied”, “neutral”, and “not satisfied”. Life satisfaction was assessed based on the question, “Think about your life as a whole. How satisfied are you with it?” The responses include “completely satisfied”, “very satisfied”, “somewhat satisfied”, “not very satisfied”, or “not at all satisfied”. The first three responses were coded as “yes” and the rest as “no”. Subjective social position was grouped into three categories such as “high”, “middle”, and “low”. Difficulty in activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) were categorized into “no” and “yes”. The self-rated health (SRH) of the respondents has been categorized into “good”, “average” and “poor”. Morbidity conditions were categorized into three groups “no condition”, “single condition”, and “multiple conditions”. The variable of social participation of the respondents was formed by taking six variables, namely “frequency of meeting friends”; “visiting friends/relatives”; “frequency of attending organizations, clubs, or society’s meetings/gatherings”; “attending cultural performances, shows, or cinema”; “attending political, community or organization group meetings”; “attending religious functions/events such as bhajan, Satsang or prayer”; and then categorized the score into “high” and “low”. Lastly, depression status was coded as “not depressed” and “depressed”, which was measured using the CIDI-SF depression scale. Receiving ill-treatment was assessed using the following question: Have you felt that you were ill-treated in the past year? The responses were coded as “no” and “yes”.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics have been used to show the sample characteristics. As the prevalence of perceived discrimination is above 15%, relative risk regression was performed to examine the association between migration status and type with perceived discrimination among older adults aged 60 and above. Furthermore, interaction terms have been included in the regression to check whether the effect is moderated by gender and place of residence. The results of the regression have been represented in Table 3 . Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) has been used to check the multicollinearity among the explanatory variables. The results of VIF are shown in Fig. 2. The mean value of VIF is 1.53, which suggests a small incidence of multicollinearity among the explanatory variables—it is not an issue of concern [52, 53]. Individual weight was applied during the analysis to make the results more generalizable to a larger population. Several studies have highlighted the importance of using weights to increase the precision of estimates and reduce the chances of errors due to over and underrepresentation of the population, especially in the field of public health [54–56].

Results

Sample characteristics

Table 1 gives a snapshot of the general profile of the sample based on background characteristics. Most older adults fall within the 60–69 age group (59.7%), followed by 29.8% in the 70–79 group, and 10.5% aged 80 and above. Females constitute 52.5% of the sample, and 64.97% are married. Regarding social group affiliation, 66.6% of respondents belong to non-SC/ST communities, while 33.4% belong to SC/ST communities. Regarding educational attainment, 54.5% of older adults have no formal education, 24.5% have completed primary education, and 21% have completed secondary education or higher. Employment status indicates that 28.8% are currently working, 8.2% are retired, and 35.7% are not working at present. Nearly 68.3% of the sample population lives in rural areas, while only 31.7% are from urban areas. Regionally, most of the samples are from North & Central regions (33.7%). Additionally, 41% of the total sample comes from poor quintiles. Family structure data show that 96.7% of older adults have two or more children. Satisfaction levels are generally high, with 77.8% expressing satisfaction with their living arrangements and 87.7% reporting satisfaction with life. However, 34.8% of older adults have low subjective social positions and 44.7% report difficulties in daily activities. Nearly 23.2% of older adults reported poor self-rated health. In the case of morbidity conditions, 29.7% reported having a single morbidity, while 24.6% reported multiple morbidities. About 67.9% of older adults have low social participation. In the case of depression, 6.9% of older adults have major depression symptoms, while 5.7% receive ill-treatment.

Prevalence of perceived discrimination

Table 2 depicts the prevalence of perceived discrimination among older adults across different migration categories. It shows that older adults with internal migrant children (18.48%) mostly experience discrimination in their daily lives compared to those living with their children (17.48%) and those whose children migrated abroad (14.69%). Among the age groups, older adults aged 80 & above (18.71%) and those aged 60–69 (18.69%) with internal migrant children report higher levels of perceived discrimination. At the same time, perceived discrimination among older adults with non-migration children is higher in the 60–69 age group (18.08%) but tends to decline with increasing age. Gender differences are also prevalent, where older women with no migrant (18.09%) and with internal migrant children (21.07%) experience higher levels of discrimination. Similarly, 21.07% of currently not married older adults with internal migrant children and 18.81% with non-migrant children experience discrimination. Across social groups, 20.07% of older adults with internal migrant children and 18.17% with non-migrant children report perceived discrimination. Notably, older adults who are Muslim (25.44%), currently working (31.17%) or without formal education (22.87%) face higher levels of discrimination, particularly when their children have migrated internationally. Among those not currently working, 21.19% of older adults with internal migrant children and 19.22% with non-migrant children report experiencing everyday discrimination. Place of residence also plays a role—19.26% of urban older adults with internal migrant children report discrimination, compared to 18.27% of rural older adults with non-migrant children. Regionally, 23.03% of older adults from the southern region with no migrant children experience perceived discrimination. In contrast, in the northern and central regions, 21.81% of those with internal migrant children report similar experiences.

About 18.87% and 17.45% of older adults with non-migrant and international migrant children, respectively, who belong to the wealthiest quintile, reported experiencing perceived discrimination. In contrast, 20.78% of older adults living with internal migrant children and belonging to the poorest quintile reported the same. Similarly, 21.44% of older adults with a single child who migrated internally experienced discrimination in their daily lives, while 15.02% of those with more than two children who migrated abroad reported similar experiences. About 30.92% of older adults with no migrant children who are not satisfied with their current living arrangements report experiencing perceived discrimination, compared to 29.93% of those with internal migrant children. Among those satisfied with life, 14.78% of older adults with internationally migrated children report perceived discrimination, whereas 30.26% of those dissatisfied with life and having internal migrant children report similar experiences. Among older adults with internal migrant children, 23.04% of those with a low subjective social position report experiencing perceived discrimination. In contrast, only 11.29% of those with international migrant children and a medium subjective social position report the same.

Regarding functional limitations, 23.77% and 21.58% of older adults with internal migrant children experiencing difficulties in ADL and IADL, respectively, report higher levels of perceived discrimination. About 17.22% of older adults with non-migrant children who report good self-rated health face perceived discrimination, compared to 20.46% of those with internal migrant children and poor self-rated health. Similarly, 18.06% of older adults with non-migrant children who have multiple morbidities report perceived discrimination, while the figure rises to 21.81% among those with internal migrant children. Among older adults with high social participation, 26.11% of older adults living with internal migrant children and 20.17% of those with non-migrant children report experiencing perceived discrimination. Similarly, in the non-migrant child category, 29.45% of older adults with symptoms of depression and 45.60% of those who report experiencing ill-treatment face perceived discrimination. These figures rise to 33.32% and 48.29% among older adults with internal migrant children. In contrast, only about 14.22% and 14.23% of older adults with international migrant children who report no depression and no ill-treatment, respectively, experience perceived discrimination.

Migration status of adult male child and perceived discrimination

Table 3 shows the association between the migration status of adult male children and perceived discrimination among older adults aged 60 & above. The results show that older adults with internal and international migrant children have a lower relative risk (RRR = 0.91,95% CI = 0.91–0.91), (RRR = 0.81,95% CI = 0.81–0.81) of facing perceived discrimination compared to those with non-migrant children. Older women have a lower relative risk (RRR = 0.93,95% CI = 0.93–0.93) of facing perceived discrimination than men. Similarly, older adults living in urban areas are less likely to face discrimination (RRR = 0.86,95% CI = 0.86–0.86) than those in rural areas. However, female older adults with internal migrant children (RRR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.03–1.03) and urban older adults with internal migrant children (RRR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.20–1.20) show a higher risk of perceived discrimination. In contrast, urban older adults with international migrant children have a significantly lower relative risk (RRR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.30–0.73). Notably, urban older females overall show a slightly higher risk (RRR = 1.07, 95% CI: 1.07–1.07), but this risk decreases for those with internal (RRR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.43–0.58) and international migrant children (RRR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.21–0.87).

Older adults aged 70–79 and 80 + have a lower relative risk (RRR = 0.97,95% CI = 0.97–0.97), (RRR = 0.96, 95% CI = 0.96–0.96) of facing perceived discrimination compared to the 60–69 age group. Currently not married older adults have a slightly higher risk (RRR = 1.01, 95% CI = 1.01–1.01) of facing perceived discrimination than those who are married. Older adults from the SC/ST community have a slightly lower relative risk (RRR = 0.99,95% CI = 0.99–0.99) of facing perceived discrimination compared to those from non-SC/ST communities. Muslims and members of other religions have a lower relative risk (RRR = 0.93,95%CI = 0.93–0.93) (RRR = 0.27,95%CI = 0.24–0.30) of facing discrimination compared to Hindus. In terms of educational attainment, older adults with primary education and secondary or higher education have a lower relative risk (RRR = 0.90,95%CI = 0.90–0.90), (RRR = 0.84,95%CI = 0.84–0.84) of facing perceived discrimination compared to those with no formal education. Employment status indicates that those currently working and not working have a higher relative risk (RRR = 1.15,95%CI = 1.15–1.15) of facing discrimination than those who have never worked. In comparison, retired older adults show a significantly lower risk (RRR = 0.42,95% CI = 0.37–0.47). Older adults living in the north and central face a higher relative risk (RRR = 1.07,95%CI = 1.07–1.07) of perceived discrimination. In contrast, those in the east and north-east regions and western region have a lower relative risk (RRR = 0.75,95%CI = 0.75–0.75), (RRR = 0.89,95%CI = 0.89–0.89) compared to those in the southern part.

In terms of consumption quintiles, older adults from middle and poor quintiles have a lower risk of relative risk (RRR = 0.99,95%CI = 0.99–0.99), (RRR = 0.94,95%CI = 0.94–0.94) of facing perceived discrimination than those from the richest quintile. Older adults with two or more children have a higher relative risk (RRR = 1.01, 95% CI = 1.01–1.01) of facing perceived discrimination compared to those with only one child. Satisfaction with living arrangements indicator shows that older adults who are neutral or dissatisfied with living arrangements have a higher relative risk (RRR = 1.32,95%CI = 1.32–1.32), (RRR = 1.27,95%CI = 1.27–1.27) of facing perceived discrimination compared to those who are satisfied. The same pattern holds for life satisfaction – those not satisfied with life and those with low subjective social position have a higher relative risk (RRR = 1.08,95%CI = 1.08–1.08), (RRR = 1.52,95%CI = 1.52–1.52) of facing perceived discrimination. Older adults with difficulties in ADL and IADL have a higher relative risk (RRR = 1.11,95%CI = 1.11–1.11) (RRR = 1.16,95%CI = 1.16–1.16) of facing perceived discrimination compared to those without such challenges. Those with average and poor self-rated health have a lower relative risk (RRR = 0.89,95%CI = 0.89 = 0.89) (RRR = 0.81,95%CI = 0.81–0.81) of experiencing perceived discrimination than those with good health. Older adults with multimorbidity have a slightly elevated risk (RRR = 1.03,95%CI = 1.03–1.03) of facing perceived discrimination compared to those with no morbidity. Mental health status indicators reveal that older adults experiencing depression and those who report ill-treatment have a higher relative risk (RRR = 1.32,95%CI = 1.32–1.32), (RRR = 1.92,95%CI = 1.92–1.92) of facing perceived discrimination compared to their counterparts without such problems.

Discussion

The results reveal that older adults with internal and international migrant children have a lower relative risk of reporting perceived discrimination compared to those with non-migrant children. A plausible reason for diminished discrimination could be the receipt of remittances, which may contribute to better management of household chores, improved mental and physical health conditions and enhanced well-being for the left behind older adults [20, 38, 57]. Moreover, remittances may uplift the social standing of these older people within their communities, garnering greater respect [58]. However, this finding contradicts several studies asserting the adverse health outcomes of older adults left behind [19, 39, 42, 59]. Although older women and those living in urban areas exhibit a lower relative risk of perceived discrimination, their joint effect presents a higher relative risk. Notably, older women with internal migrant children and those in urban areas seem to have a higher relative risk of perceived discrimination. One probable reason could be the effect of being female and having an internal migrant child exacerbating adverse outcomes [60]. Conversely, older women living in urban areas with internal or international migrant children appear to experience a lower relative risk of discrimination. These contrasting outcomes suggest the influence of complex, intersecting social determinants. Existing studies have shown how multiple axes of social identities can intensify or moderate the effects of discriminatory experiences [46, 62–64]. For instance, females receive greater social and emotional support in old age due to stronger social capital and structural support systems than men [65, 66]. On the other hand, some studies reveal that urban-dwelling elders have a reduced chance of facing discrimination compared to their rural counterparts [61, 67].

Our study further reveals that several socio-economic factors - such as age, marital status, education, employment and household wealth - are significantly associated with perceived discrimination. These findings are consistent with several other studies that assert the contributions of socio-economic factors responsible for perceived discrimination [7, 46, 68, 69]. This could be because of differential treatment faced by individuals regarding social exclusion, isolation, and limited financial opportunities, elevating their vulnerability towards health-related difficulties, caregiving, emotional strain, and several other lifestyle disorders [19, 65]. At the household level, factors such as living in poverty, absence of close family members, and current work status are also significantly associated with perceived discrimination. Employment may ensure economic stability but can also be exposed to increased social and financial conflicts and stressful situations, while supportive living arrangements can facilitate social participation and provide emotional and caregiving support [37, 70, 71].

Psychosocial factors like depression and receiving ill-treatment have a higher relative risk for perceived discrimination. This finding is consistent with several other studies that show how discrimination is positively associated with a higher risk of depression or psychological distress [72–74]. One plausible explanation could be that the issue of discrimination is highly personal and can provoke emotional and physical stress through individual or group-level stigmatization [75, 76]. Similarly, verbal or physical mistreatment increases feelings of exclusion and discrimination. On the other hand, higher social participation lowers the relative risk of perceived discrimination. Studies show how social integration and community engagement not only elevate self-esteem but also provide emotional outlets and coping mechanisms, particularly for left-behind older adults facing psychological stress [77–80]. Consistent with previous studies, this study also shows that older adults with multimorbidity or difficulties in performing instrumental and basic daily activities have a higher relative risk of perceived discrimination. One plausible reason could be that older adults with physical limitations often face challenges in social participation and access to care, leading to increased vulnerability [81–83].

Conclusion

The present study examines the association between adult children’s migration and perceived discrimination among older adults in India. The study contributes to the empirical understanding of how the migration of adult children affects the psychological well-being of older adults left behind. The migration of adult children is a common phenomenon in India, with significant implications for older adults’ emotional and social well-being. Moreover, factors such as poor health, depression, financial insecurity, and lack of family support can further increase the chances of perceived discrimination. Addressing this issue requires an inclusive and holistic approach comprising diverse experiences shaped by intersecting social identities. These considerations become even more essential in a socio-culturally diverse country like India. Enhancing community support programs, expanding pension schemes, and ensuring legal protections for older adults may help to combat situations.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the results should not be generalized to a broader population due to the data’s cross-sectional nature and the country’s vast socio-cultural diversity. Second, our study does not account for remittances, which certainly has some effect on the outcome variable of interest. Migration is a multidimensional phenomenon influenced by several factors, many of which were beyond the scope of the study. Future research should incorporate additional dimensions, such as intersectionality, to understand better the relationship between adult children’s migration and perceived discrimination among older adults.
