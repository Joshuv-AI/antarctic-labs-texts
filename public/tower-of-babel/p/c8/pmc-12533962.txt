Targeting macroautophagy to combat neurodegenerative disease: strategies and considerations
Authors: Glenn M. Duncan, Ai Yamamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUpon demonstration that basal macroautophagy plays an essential role in maintaining protein homeostasis in the mammalian CNS, there has been excitement around modulating this form of autophagy as a therapeutic strategy to combat neurodegenerative disease. Nonetheless, the initial genetic studies that spawned this excitement did little to reveal the complex physiology of autophagy regulation in neural cells, or the predicament of compartment-specific events upon which these cells rely. Pursuit of therapeutic strategies further highlighted how this intricacy extends across the different organs of the body, raising question as to how we may harness the power of macroautophagy for good while minimizing the bad. Fortunately, since these early studies, the field has made significant gains toward understanding the molecular, cellular and physiological basis of macroautophagy. Together with technological advances, they have refueled the exploration into how this powerful pathway may provide the much-needed therapeutic advances for these yet untreatable diseases. In this review, we will contextualize the insights gained over the last decade with the traditional and novel strategies that have been explored to combat disease-associated events such as abnormal protein accumulation. In addition, we will discuss key considerations and strategies that can influence how a therapeutic approach might be designed.

Introduction

Since its original description as an emergent pathway in response to severe nutrient deprivation or neuronal injury, it has been clear that autophagy is essential for vertebrate survival. Nonetheless, it was not until studies demonstrated that basal macroautophagy is essential for maintaining protein homeostasis in the mammalian CNS that the potential therapeutic value for neurodegenerative disease emerged. The initial surge of excitement gave way, however, as the complex physiology of autophagy regulation, the predicament of compartment-specific events, and the differential reliance on autophagy across different cell types and organs made targeting this essential pathway incredibly difficult. Moreover, a growing number of studies implicated autophagic dysfunction as a potential contributing feature across neurodegeneration[1–6], raising question as to whether the power of this pathway can be efficiently targeted. Fortunately, despite these potential hurdles, studies have continued to pursue the molecular basis of autophagy regulation in the brain and neurodegeneration, thereby revealing novel targets that may finally permit the specificity that is required. Although the final answers are still pending, studies suggest that scientists are getting closer to harnessing autophagy for good and minimizing the bad. In this review, we will provide a brief overview of key events in macroautophagy that may lend itself to therapeutic targeting, and discuss key considerations for designing therapeutic strategies.

Macroautophagy: at which point can we intervene?

To maintain cellular homeostasis, the pathways of autophagy may be the most potent: the breadth of cargoes that can be degraded represents virtually every organelle. This potency is derived from the lysosome, which can recycle all cellular macromolecules, coupled with the ability of autophagy to deliver seemingly limitless cytosolic cargo. Autophagic cargo can be transported into the lysosomal lumen directly via chaperone mediated autophagy (CMA)[7], as well as indirectly by first packaging the cargo into an autophagosome via macroautophagy[8]. Although CMA is a target of interest for the treatment of neurodegenerative disease[7,9], in this review, we will focus on macroautophagy, because its ability to degrade complex macromolecules has made it the more prevalent form of autophagy explored for therapeutic potential in adult-onset neurodegenerative diseases.

After cargo is captured by the autophagosome, it then fuses into the endolysosomal system to permit degradation (Figure 1). The process of macroautophagy can be broken down to discrete steps which are indicative of the status of the autophagosome itself, starting with initiating autophagosome membrane formation, followed by cargo capture and membrane growth and closure, and ending with the fusion of the newly formed autophagosome with other endocytic structures and ultimately the lysosome for degradation. Each of these stages can be defined by a discrete set of molecular complexes[10], some of which lay the foundation for targetable entities to augment or diminish macroautophagic function. Autophagosome biogenesis and formation

The most frequently explored use of macroautophagy to combat neurodegeneration is to augment the de novo formation of the autophagosome, to increase the likelihood that the desired cargo will be degraded. The primary kinase complex to induce autophagosome formation is based on the mammalian orthologues of yeast Autophagy 1 (Atg1) known as Unc-like kinases 1 and 2 (ULK1/ULK2)[10], either of which can form a multimeric kinase complex with ATG13, RB1 inducible coiled-coil 1/FAK family interacting protein 200kDa (RB1CC1/FIP200), and ATG101. Through a combination of autophosphorylation and modification by other kinases, activation of this complex can lead to the recruitment of the autophagic machinery to the site of autophagosome formation.

The most recognized and earliest activator of autophagy is rapamycin, which inhibits the serine/threonine kinases mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), a negative regulator of autophagy[11]. Since these classic studies, it is now understood that mTOR exists in two distinct complexes, mTOR complex 1 (mTORC1) and mTOR complex 2 (mTORC2)[12]. Rapamycin inhibits mTORC1 and alters the phosphorylation status of the ULK1 complex thereby promoting autophagy. Although some controversy exists over the potency of mTORC1 inhibition on macroautophagy activation in primary neurons[13,14], subsequent studies in mice showed that inhibition of this central nutrient-sensing kinase can have a clear impact on a broad range of autophagy-dependent functions in cells of the brain such as in autophagy-dependent dendritic remodeling, maintaining the intrinsic excitability of neurons, and synaptic homeostasis[15]. In mouse models of neurodegenerative disease, studies suggest that treatment with rapamycin and more stable analogs (rapalogs) may be beneficial by increasing the clearance of abnormally accumulated Tau, α-Synuclein and mutant Huntingtin[16,17]. Nonetheless, given the ability of rapamycin to inhibit CAP-dependent translation and the ectopically expressed promoters used to create the transgenic models, it is difficult to differentiate if the benefit is derived from increased degradation of protein aggregates via autophagy or due to decreased translation of the disease-causing transgene itself. Despite the questions around the use of rapamycin, other early inducers of autophagosome biogenesis that modify ULK1 phosphorylation have shown some similar success, further implicating the therapeutic potential for autophagy. One kinase that may be especially relevant for activating autophagy in neurons is the central kinase involved in energy sensing, Adenosine Monophosphate-Activated Protein Kinase (AMPK). For example, the use of 5-Aminoimidazole-4-carboxamide ribonucleotide (AICAR), an activator of AMPK, can lead to similarly protective effects as mTORC1 inhibition[18]. FDA approved compounds, such as carbamezapine, an anticonvulsant, and nilotinib, an inhibitor of cellular Abelson tyrosine kinase (c-ABL), has also been shown to activate autophagy via AMPK activation, and has led to modest efficacy in mouse models of AD, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and Parkinson’s disease (PD)[19–21]. Increasing cytosolic calcium (Ca2+) levels to activate Ca2+ dependent kinases to phosphorylate ULK1 (as well autophagosome maturation, as described below) has been another approach by which the induction of autophagosome formation has been used to elicit protective effects[18,22–24]. Overall, these studies suggest that the induction of autophagosome biogenesis to increase potential macroautophagic capacity can slow the onset of neurodegenerative outcomes in model systems. Nonetheless, the timing of the intervention might be key: increasing autophagy activity later in diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) may worsen outcomes since lysosomal functioning may already be impaired, leading to an accumulation of autophagosomes[25]. In contrast, studies in ALS mice suggest that early autophagy inhibition accelerates pathogenesis, whereas late autophagy inhibition may be protective[26,27]. Thus, autophagy dysregulation might change throughout the course of a disease, and that course may differ between diseases. Furthermore, as carbamezapine and nilotinib showed, the pleiotropic nature of these interventions make it difficult to attribute any outcome solely to increased autophagic turnover, thereby limiting their efficacy, not to mention evoke undesirable side effects due to long term use[28–30]. Thus, in contrast to the treatment of different cancers, this early flurry of studies using small molecules has not led to any further clinical trial in the United States explicitly examining autophagy activators to combat neurodegenerative disease, as indicated on clinicaltrials.gov.

As opposed to the early attempts at general activation of autophagy, more recent studies focus on the creation of the membrane phospholipid, phosphatidyl inositol 3-monophosphate (PI3P) as a more selective approach to induce autophagosome formation (Figure 1). PI3P is created by the Class III PI3P kinase (PI3K) Vacuolar Protein Sorting 34 (VPS34) complex, which is bound to the accessory partners, Vacuolar Protein Sorting 15 (VPS15) and the Atg6 homolog Beclin1 (BECN1)[31,32] (Figure 2). To generate PI3P for macroautophagy, VPS34-VPS15-BECN1 is bound to ATG14L, and known as Complex I, whereas for endocytosis, it is bound to UV radiation resistance associated gene (UVRAG) and Rubicon (RUBCN), and known as Complex II[33–36]. Complex I is further regulated by Nuclear Receptor Binding Factor 2 (NRBF2), which binds directly to ATG14L and augments activity[37,38], whereas RUBCN-like/Protein Associated with UVRAG (RUBCNL/PACER) antagonizes RUBCN to favor autophagy[39]. Evolving from the early mechanistic studies that focused on the, albeit inefficient, inhibition of Vps34 itself[40], recent studies suggest that targeting the balance between Complex I and II by changing expression of distinct binding partners can swing PI3P levels to selectively increase macroautophagy[41]. For example, NRBF2 overexpression increases autophagy and promotes the degradation of amyloid precursor protein C-terminal fragments (APP-CTF), diminishes amyloid beta (Aβ) levels in AD models and improves memory deficits[42,43]. Similarly, decreasing RUBCN levels can push the equilibrium toward Complex I, thereby increasing autophagosome biogenesis[35,36,44–49]. Although increasing autophagy in this manner may augment aggregate turnover[45], studies also suggest that decreasing RUBCN expression can increase the secretion of APP/Aβ in an autophagy independent manner manner[50] and thereby may be a problematic approach for those at risk for AD. In postmortem AD brain, changes in the balance between Complex I and II has been reported, with NRBF2 levels diminished and RUBCN levels increased[43]. Given how changes in endocytosis occur early in AD[51], this may reflect how favoring endocytosis may be an early protective event. Nonetheless, increased RUBCN levels may be a general feature of ageing[45], and thus may be a shared feature across different neurodegenerative diseases. Despite the concerns in AD, tipping the balance away from endocytosis toward autophagy may be beneficial across other neurological diseases. Nonetheless, like other autophagy-based interventions, a key question left unanswered is whether this approach would still be beneficial after symptomatic onset, which is when most patients will seek intervention even for heritable diseases. As mentioned above, this may depend on which disease is under study. However, generally for proteinopathies, it is still unclear when abnormal protein accumulation begins, and further interpretation of prevention studies are stymied by not knowing when an intervention should begin clinically[52–55]. Studies from the direct conversion of patient fibroblasts hint that protein accumulation may occur in closer proximity to symptomatic onset rather than early in development[56]. Thus, for therapeutic purposes, it remains likely that patients will receive an intervention only after protein accumulation has occurred. For RUBCN inhibition, studies by Hurley and colleagues have identified the structure of a large portion of the protein[57], making rational drug design and therefore potential follow-up studies pursuing this important therapeutic question possible. For all of these compounds, given the importance of autophagy and endocytosis in different neural cell types of the adult brain, changing autophagy activity in the adult brain at the potential cost of endocytosis, raises another key question of whether this is sustainable[8]. For example, overexpression of RUBCNL/PACER may be detrimental to the endolysosomal system, and may inhibit autophagic degradation[27]. Should the rate of protein accumulation be slow and autophagy-mediated turnover of protein aggregates efficient, periodic activation of autophagy may be sufficient to maintain protein homeostasis while diminishing untoward effects. This may similarly help to abrogate another key concern of chronic autophagy activation: untoward outcomes in peripheral organs. For example, decreasing RUBCN levels in kidney can lead to metabolic disease[49], and augmenting autophagy in muscle leads to muscle wasting[45,46,58]. Given that the elderly population represents the target population for most neurodegenerative diseases, general autophagic activation may clear the proteinopathy, but in turn may increase the vulnerability of the patients in other ways, worsening patient outcomes, potentially explaining the failures that have been observed thus far [30].

Cargo capture

Driving the initiation of autophagosome biogenesis may increase autophagic activity, however, considering the breadth of cargoes autophagy degrades, capture of the desired cargo remains somewhat to chance. Instead, an alternative or complementary approach may be provided by understanding how autophagy sorts and prioritizes substrates for degradation. Unlike the ubiquitin proteasome system for which substrate targeting is better understood, in the study of macroautophagy, we are still in the early stages of research. Nonetheless, it is exceedingly clear that adaptor proteins play a central role in the ability of macroautophagy to direct degradation of discrete cargoes[8,59,60]. Adaptor protein-mediated degradation of cargo has been broadly termed as selective macroautophagy, which arises from early studies suggesting that macroautophagy predominantly degrades in a bulk, nonselective manner due to emergent stress such as prolonged nutrient deprivation. These studies, in which macroautophagy was first identified and named, arose from observational studies in mammalian organs such as liver[61,62]. Although the study of selective macroautophagy in mammalian cells has only increased over the last two decades, the mammalian implication of findings in Saccharomyces cerevisiae made decades earlier was overlooked for many years[63]. Known as cytoplasm to vacuole targeting (Cvt), yeast rely on this pathway to deliver a multimer of the pro-enzyme for aminopeptidase 1 (Ape1), and other enzymes, into the yeast vacuole[64,65]. Although some similarities with macroautophagy were observed, such as the formation of a multilamellar autophagosome-like structure known as the Cvt vesicle, Cvt was considered separate from macroautophagy because it occurred during unstarved, nutrient rich (and presumably mTOR active) conditions. Additionally, Cvt led to the packaging of a specific cargo rather than filling with bulk cytosol[64]. Appreciation that Cvt was indeed a selective form of autophagy was solidified when it became clear that the formation of the Cvt vesicle and autophagosome was identical. Simultaneously, a growing number of studies indicated that the selective capture of cargo in an mTORC1-independent manner may be a common event in mammalian cells[66], and selective uptake of cargo by autophagy was conserved across phyla[67,68]. These early observations were punctuated by a specific naming system that has since persisted, which contrasts the discrete cargoes selectively degraded by autophagy, such as mitophagy for mitochondria, xenophagy for invading pathogens and aggrephagy for protein aggregates. Unfortunately, now that selective autophagy is well accepted, more recently this naming-system has led to the confusion that the “−phagies” are distinct from autophagy, rather than a field-specific shorthand that signifies which cargo is being eliminated (Figure 3). For cargo to be successfully captured and degraded, cargo must undergo labeling, recognition, targeting, sequestration and encapsulation by the growing autophagosome[69,70]. The core mechanism of selective cargo capture in mammalian cells is conserved from Cvt, relying on adaptor proteins that scaffold cargo with the autophagic machinery and the growing autophagosome membrane (reviewed in[64]). The core group of adaptor proteins, known as selective autophagy receptor proteins (SARs) are defined by the presence of a ubiquitin binding domain (UBA) and a motif that binds to the mammalian ATG8 (mATG8) proteins that decorate the growing autophagosome membrane known as an autophagy interacting motif (AIM) or microtubule associated protein 1light chain 3 (LC3)-interacting region (LIR)[8,71]. Much like the Atg19 adaptor protein of Cvt, many SARs can also bind to RB1CC1/FIP200[72–75]. The most frequently studied SARs in the context of neurodegenerative diseases are sequestosome-1 (SQSTM1/p62), neighbor of BRCA1 gene (NBR1), calcium binding and coiled-coil domain 2 protein (CALCOCO2/NDP52), tax1-binding protein (TAX1BP1), Toll-interacting protein (TOLLIP) and optineurin (OPTN). Mechanistically, many of these SARs, such as NBR1 and SQSTM1/p62[76] have been implicated for the degradation of multiple cargoes, leaving some mechanistic questions regarding whether these adaptor proteins are cargo-specific or part of a larger mechanism through which substrates are recognized, consolidated and captured by the autophagosome[8,77,78].

In addition to SARs, additional adaptor proteins have been identified which may ensure cargo selectivity. For the clearance of protein aggregates, one such adaptor protein is the WD repeat and FYVE domain containing 3/Autophagy linked FYVE protein (WDFY3/Alfy), a large multidomain scaffold that is essential for the autophagic clearance of aggregated proteins[79,80]. Notably, it possesses a LIR that binds only to the GABARAP family of mATG8 proteins and a WD40 domain that binds to ATG5 and thereby the E3-like ligase for membrane conjugation of mATG8 in general[79,81,82]. In contrast however, WDFY3/Alfy does not have a ubiquitin binding domain but instead may bind to other adaptor proteins to interact with cargo or may even act in a ubiquitin independent manner like OPTN[79,83]. Trans-2,3-enoyl-CoA reductase pseudogene (TECPR1) has also been shown to coordinate LC3C-positive autophagosomes filled with aggregated proteins for autophagic degradation[84], whereas more recently, TRIAD3A (RNF216), an E3 ubiquitin ligase, multimerizes cargo with K11 and K63 ubiquitin conjugations together to help traffic proteins such as tau for autophagic degradation[85]. Taken together, specificity may be achieved by an overlay of different molecular layers to ensure the unique needs of the different cargoes.

This potential to harness autophagic degradation of specific cargoes has made selective autophagy especially attractive, and significant efforts have been and continue to be made in this area[60]. In a recent study, a rare variant of WDFY3 was associated to the delayed age of onset of Huntington’s disease (HD) by up to 23 years[86]. The study found that the variant increased WDFY3/ALFY expression, which was sufficient to augment turnover of aggregated proteins and confer protection in mouse models of HD, PD and tau accumulation, suggesting that increasing adaptor protein expression or activity is sufficient to autophagic activity selectively[86]. Thus, the mechanistic insights to promote selective cargo capture continue to emerge, and therapeutic approaches exploiting this knowledge are continuing to evolve. Perhaps unsurprisingly, phosphorylation has again emerged as a post-translational modification of SARs (such as SQSTM1) and their binding partner (mATG8 proteins) to regulate cargo capture and degradation[87]. Several kinases have been implicated, including ULK1 and Tank binding kinase 1 (TBK1)[50,88–94]. Given the importance of adaptor protein mediated uptake in xenophagy, it is somewhat satisfying that TBK1 complements its central role in innate immunity with autophagy. Loss-of-function mutations in TBK1 as well as OPTN and SQSTM1/p62 has also been identified for ALS[3], thereby making it and PD two disorders in which cargo capture, especially of mitochondria, might be specifically dysregulated[8,93,95]. The interest of TBK1 loss-of-function revealed another potential consideration important in the context of the adult CNS – which cell type will be most affected when altering kinase function, neuronal or glial cells[96]. Nonetheless, the general induction of these pathways using a small molecule approach is still beset with pleiotropic effects.

One avenue of growing momentum that may overcome this issue is the use of targeted protein degradation (TPD) technology (Figure 4)[97]. These technologies rely on creating a bifunctional molecule that induces proximity of cargo to a functional moiety such as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, to increase the likelihood of interaction and thereby accelerate the process. This approach may be especially valuable for non-enzymatic targets or for those that are beyond the scope of traditional small molecules. Initially developed for promoting cargo recognition by E3 ubiquitin ligases to promote efficient proteasomal degradation, the proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs)-based approaches have inspired designs to also target cargoes via other degradation pathways including autophagy[98]. Autophagy-targeting chimeras (AUTACs)[99], autophagosome tethering compounds (ATTECs)[100], and AUTOphagy-TArgeting Chimera (AUTOTAC)[101,102] are designed heterobifunctional small molecules that bind the target of interest to the inner surface of forming autophagosomes or to promote interaction with an SAR. These different compounds take advantage of different markers of cargo, such as ubiquitinated substrates, and the functional moiety such as mATG8 proteins, or SARs such as SQSTM1[102]. Given the molecularly defined nature of these approaches, the development of TPD-based approaches for autophagy further highlight the importance of delineating the molecular basis for cargo selectivity, to ensure only desired cargoes are captured for elimination. The specificity of these approaches is especially exciting for neurodegeneration, given that they may be designed to be specific for each disease. For example, targets of interest may be either the soluble or aggregated form of a disease-associated protein, or specifically fragmented mitochondria. Although this work is still in its infancy, several PROTAC-based approaches are finding their way into clinical trials for cancer[103]. As with many innovative approaches for combatting brain diseases, however, the main limitation for TPD approaches is delivery, an active area of study[104–106].

Autophagic flux and degradation

The final and perhaps most important step in autophagy is successful degradation of cargo, which is accomplished by fusion of the autophagosome to the lysosome. Upon autophagosome closure, for which multiple protein complexes are implicated, most notably the ESCRT machinery and the VPS34 Complex I (reviewed in[107,108]), ensuring complete degradation may be critical for any therapeutic approach for autophagy. This may be especially important for diseases such as lysosomal storage diseases and AD, where overwhelming the lysosome may lead to an accumulation of the autophagosomes themselves[2]. Moreover, the accumulation of substrates in autophagosomes may drive unconventional secretion by autophagosome exocytosis, and thereby a release of content into the extracellular space. This may be dire not only for viruses[109], but the spread of Lewy and tau pathology[110].

Central to the coordination of the autophagy-lysosomal pathway is Transcription Factor EB (TFEB), a member of the microphthalmia (MiT/TFE) family of leucine zipper transcription factors that is a central transcriptional regulator that coordinates transcription of everything from the adaptor protein p62 to lysosomal components[111–113]. Targeting TFEB has been protective experimentally across models of neurodegenerative disease, especially where lysosomal dysfunction has been previously implicated, such as for Parkinson’s disease[114–116]. In separate studies, excessive activation has been implicated in cancer, leading to renal toxicity and other off target events[117], suggesting caution when considering the prolonged treatment strategies that may be necessary for the treatment of neurodegenerative disease. Strategies such as periodic treatments that can diminish proteotoxic stress might be beneficial and avoid these untoward outcomes.

Intracellular Ca2+ as a means to activate TFEB and autolysosome fusion has been noted. One example is the mucolipin TRP cation channel 1 (MCOLN1)[118,119]. Studies in mouse neurons demonstrate that depletion of MCOLN1 results in autophagy impairment characterized by a buildup of p62/ubiquitin positive inclusions[120], suggesting this channel may play an essential role in autophagy. This channel has been tested as a therapeutic target in the context of ALS and its increased activation has been shown to exert neuroprotective effects pathologically and behaviorally[121]. Two Pore Channel 2 (TPC2) is another ion channel that has been identified for its importance with pH homeostasis[122,123]. Although there have been pharmacological advancements to make these channels druggable targets for neurodegenerative disease, questions regarding ability to achieve high specificity for cargo with these approaches remain unanswered[124]. This furthers the notion that utilizing more than one approach to layer different molecular targets might be necessary to capitalize on both cargo specificity and degradation efficiency.

Conclusion

The landmark studies implicated autophagy as fundamental for maintaining protein homeostasis in the brain led to an almost immediate excitement that augmenting this highly conserved pathway may represent the missing link between neurodegenerative disease and a cure[125–127]. Nonetheless, given the central role autophagy plays in maintaining the homeostasis of all cells, early attempts were disappointing. Since then, however, not only have we gained greater insight into the molecular basis of cargo capture, representing a new handle to harness the power of autophagy selectively, but new technologies such as TPD have emerged that may allow novel ways of pursuing non-traditional targets. Overall, whereas these developments are promising, key observations are still missing that demonstrate sufficiency of targeting autophagic activity to therapeutically impact the course of neurodegenerative disease. For example, although increasing autophagic activity genetically or chemically prior to the onset of symptoms can be preventative, whether therapeutic value can still be achieved in symptomatic animals, the time at which most patients would seek treatment largely remains unclear. Moreover, given that age is a significant variable for both neurodegenerative disease as well as autophagic activity, how aging might impact the potency of autophagy and its ability to be modified should be more actively considered in experimental design. Gaining answers to questions such as these will allow us to not only better contextualize how we might harness the power of autophagy to combat these devastating diseases but ensure better design of the therapeutic strategies to be employed.
