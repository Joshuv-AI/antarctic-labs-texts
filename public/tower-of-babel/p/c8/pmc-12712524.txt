Frailty Matters—But Contextualize the Clinical Decision
Authors: Krithika Mohan, Kenneth J Woodside
Published: 2025

See Clinical Research on Page 3810

The global population is rapidly growing older. This demographic shift has resulted in a growing prevalence of chronic conditions such as diabetes and hypertension—conditions which are the most common causes of chronic kidney disease worldwide. Kidney transplantation offers an improved quality of life (QoL) and longevity in patients with end-stage kidney disease. The number of older patients that are pursuing kidney transplant (KT) is increasing steadily. Although beneficial for the elderly, it is also associated with unique challenges. These patients are often frail and have coexisting medical conditions that increase the risk of complications affecting long-term graft outcomes. Frailty is a clinical condition where the individual is in a vulnerable state and at increased risk of adverse health outcomes when exposed to a stressor such as a major vascular surgery such as kidney transplantation (Figure 1). Despite this risk, intervention may be necessary, because the patient will do worse without it.1, 2, 3, 4

In the older dialysis patients, frailty is a result of a complex interplay between aging, chronic comorbid conditions, and impact of dialysis on the physical and mental health. Frailty can be physical, psychological, or a combination of both that varies with time. The impact of frailty on QoL is profound and can affect clinical outcomes post–kidney transplantation. Identifying and addressing those at risk is crucial to achieving positive outcomes. Most studies so far have either looked at the effect on frailty on KT candidates (not specific to older patients) or have looked at the QoL with no mention of frailty.2

This prospective longitudinal study by Thind et al.,5 investigated the impact of frailty on QoL and clinical outcomes in 210 older KT candidates on the waitlist as well as recipients. The primary outcome was the patient-reported outcome changes after KT. Secondary outcomes included a description of patient-reported outcome while on waitlist and after KT, as well as the difference between the 2 clinical outcomes. The Edmonton frail scale, which assesses cognition, general health status, functional independence, social support, medication use, nutrition, mood, continence, and functional performance was used. In their study, frailty was the strongest predictor of poorer posttransplant outcomes. Frail patients had a higher incidence of delayed graft function and lower 1 year allograft function when compared with nonfrail KT recipients. However, there was no significant difference between death-censored graft survival or patient survival at 1 year. In nonfrail KT patients, there was an initial worsening of physical and mental QoL that improved over time but did not recover to pretransplant levels. In contrast, frail KT recipients reported stable or improved physical QoL, with worsening of mental QoL at 1 year posttransplant. Among the patients on the KT waitlist, nonfrail patients reported a stable depression as well as physical and mental QoL scores. Frail patients reported poorer scores with marked fluctuations in their scores while on the waitlist. They were more prone to infections resulting in ≥ 1 episode of suspension from the waitlist although it did not change the likelihood of being transplanted.5

These results should be contextualized in the broader frailty literature. In a study by Pérez-Sáez et al.,6 449 KT candidates (aged 60.4 ± 14.1 years) were evaluated for frailty by using the Physical Frailty Phenotype and the FRAIL scale. Those who did not receive a transplant were more frequently frail. Nonfrail patients had fewer lower admissions during the first year after listing and fewer cardiovascular or major infectious events. In KT candidates, prefrailty and frailty, according to both scales, were associated with poorer results while listed; findings that are similar to the current study. In contrast to the current study’s findings, McAdams-DeMarco et al.,7 demonstrated that frail patients experienced a greater rate of improvement in physical QoL, kidney disease–specific health-related QoL but not mental QoL. A systematic review by Wang et al.8 including 44 studies comprising 6929 KT recipients showed that patients reported a higher health-related QoL after KT than pretransplant and patients receiving dialysis regardless of waitlist status. Although there was an improvement in QoL posttransplant, and sometimes even to pretransplant levels, continuation of that QoL was not necessarily consistent. Finally, Tsarpali et al.,9 found that older kidney recipients had a significant and sustained improvement of generic and kidney-specific QoL when compared with those on dialysis or transplant waitlist in a 3-year health-related QoL follow-up study.

The study has many strengths. It included a large number with ethnic diversity exploring several aspects of QoL and detailed descriptive patient-related outcomes, enabling a better understanding of the patient outcome. In addition, the authors are to be praised for focusing their discussions on interventions to address frailty and vulnerability. Often, the temptation is to forget that the true comparison is a patient with a given condition with a transplant or remaining on dialysis and instead interpret data as a transplant with a patient with and without the condition; thereby not transplanting the higher-risk patients with the condition.

However, the study has its limitations. The combination of frailty and vulnerability is not unreasonable given the study parameters, but it broadens the groups in a disproportionate way. Because the study was conducted in the time of COVID-19, the impact of the disease, and enforced physical and social isolation on QoL cannot be differentiated, nor can it be discounted. It is even perhaps fair to hypothesize that the lower-than-expected posttransplant QoL results might be tied to the study timing. In addition, there were a small number of living donors included in the study, with disproportionate numbers between the groups favoring the nonfrail group. Given that living donor outcomes are better, regardless of frailty status, that factor cannot be discounted. Both of these factors could impact QoL scores, particularly for the frail. Finally, mean graft estimated glomerular filtration rate was lower in the frail patients than in nonfrail patients, likely because of their vulnerability to more delayed-graft -function and rejection episodes. Again, living donor status may impact the estimated glomerular filtration rate through a variety of mechanisms. Nevertheless, the difference reflects real-world practice, where lower donor kidney quality for higher risk patients because they cannot wait as easily, is a fact of life.

Frailty is a significant concern for elderly patients undergoing dialysis and for those on the KT waitlist, causing a diminished QoL. Although dialysis is life-saving and essential in patients with end-stage kidney disease, it often comes with trade-offs in terms of physical and emotional well-being. It is therefore essential to assess for and treat frailty in this population. Because a KT in the frail elderly end-stage kidney disease population did not show significant and sustained improvement of QoL at 1 year, it is important to discuss the risks, benefits, patient expectations, and goals before enrolling on the KT waitlist, but contextualized to what the real comparison is—the same patient on dialysis or with a KT, not the same transplant with a frail versus nonfrail recipient. Pretransplantation interventions to decrease frailty may impact posttransplant QoL and should be aggressively pursued.
