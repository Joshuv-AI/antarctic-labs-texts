Coefficient of variation–defined glycemic variability is associated with ICU mortality in critically ill influenza patients
Authors: Ramon C Fluit, Sjoerd van der Bie, Johannes P C van den Akker, Steven F L van Lelyveld, Kirsty Short, Susanne Stads, Peter Spronk, Carina Bethlehem, Romy Takken, Corstiaan A den Uil, Jantine van Holten, Rutger van Raalte, Jurre Kuipers, Marc Schluep, Matty Koopmans, Louise Urlings-Strop, Esther K Haspels-Hogervorst, Nina E Disseldorp, Jan Elderman, Jasper Roos, Robbert G Bentvelsen, Tobias R Neijzen, Dorien Kiers, Klaas de Groot, Ingeborg Harks, Ashley De Bie, Peter de Jager, Angelique C M Jansen, Petronella E Deetman, Emily Thieme Groen, Lenneke E M Haas, Rob Wilting, Hetty Kranen, Charlotte H S B van den Berg, Karlijn J van Stralen, Merijn Kant, Diederik Gommers, Evert-Jan Wils, Henrik Endeman, Marco Goeijenbier

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfluenza continues to impose significant strain on intensive care units (ICUs), especially in seasons of capacity stress. Diabetes mellitus has a strong interaction with influenza, showing a high additional increased risk of severe complications and increased mortality. Glycemic variability (GV) may independently contribute to adverse outcomes in critically ill patients, but its role in influenza-associated critical illness remains unexplored. This study investigates the association between GV and clinical outcomes in critically ill patients hospitalized with influenza.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective multicenter cohort study involving adult patients admitted with confirmed influenza to 23 ICUs across the Netherlands between November 2023 and March 2024, with at least 5 glucose measurements. GV was assessed using the coefficient of variation (CV) of all glucose measurements during ICU admission. Outcomes included ICU mortality, hospital mortality, insulin therapy, corticosteroid use and invasive mechanical ventilation. Associations were analyzed using competing risk models and Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusting for age, APACHE and diagnose of diabetes.ResultsA total of 279 patients were included. ICU mortality was similar in the lowest three CV quartiles (14.5%, 18.3% and 18.8%), but higher (32.9%) in the highest CV group. In patients without diabetes, each 10-percentage-point increase in CV was independently associated with a higher risk of ICU mortality, as well as treatment with insulin and corticosteroid therapy. No significant associations were observed in patients with pre-existing diabetes.ConclusionHigher CV was associated with increased ICU mortality in patients with influenza, in our cohort particularly among those without pre-existing diabetes mellitus. These results, if confirmed, underscore the need for prospective studies to further evaluate the clinical utility of GV and CV as a prognostic marker.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13221-w.Keywords: Glycemic variability, Glucose, Influenza virus, Diabetes mellitus, ICU

Background

Seasonal influenza outbreaks pose a significant public health challenge every year, especially in seasons of capacity stress [1]. Some influenza patients develop severe complications, such as pneumonia or respiratory failure, requiring intensive care treatment [2, 3]. In intensive care units (ICUs), patients with severe influenza are treated with supportive care, including invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV), oxygen therapy and antiviral medication, to manage respiratory distress and to prevent further complications. The most severe cases can even require extracorporeal membrane oxygenation [4–7]. Research indicates that 16% to 23% of the hospitalized influenza patients in western hospitals require admission to the ICU [8–10]. Their in-hospital mortality rate is between 6% and 9% [6, 8–11], and can rise to 37% to 44% for those admitted to the ICU [6, 7, 11, 12]. This emphasizes the considerable burden of influenza on both patients and ICUs.

Diabetes mellitus is a risk factor for a more severe course of influenza [13]. Research indicates that diabetes triples the risk of hospitalization and quadruples the risk of ICU admission once hospitalized for influenza [14]. Moreover, diabetes leads to two to four times higher mortality rates in influenza patients as compared to patients without diabetes [15, 16].

This increased disease severity of influenza in diabetic patients may be partly explained by several factors, including hyperglycemia, underlying comorbidities and glycemic oscillations [17]. Over the last few years, more research has been performed on these glycemic oscillations, more often described as glycemic variability (GV). The GV is defined as the measurement of fluctuations in blood glucose levels or other related parameters of glucose homeostasis over a specified time interval [18]. Previous research has shown that GV in the general critically ill population has a stronger predictive value for ICU and hospital all-cause mortality than mean blood glucose levels [19]. Additionally, a randomized trial has found that intensive glucose control aimed strictly at normoglycemia increased mortality in critically ill adults [20]. These findings raise the question if GV might serve as a better target for glucose management in the ICU.

Research has shown that ICU mortality is elevated in patients with high GV [21, 22]. Among general critically ill patients without diabetes, those with high GV had a threefold higher ICU mortality rate compared to those with low GV. This association was similar, though less pronounced, among patients with diabetes [23]. In COVID-19 patients, increased GV was associated with an increased risk of mortality, ICU admission and intubation for mechanical ventilation [24–27]. This included patients with and without pre-existing diabetes. A study in septic patients showed that those with increased GV had nearly a five-fold rise in the odds of hospital mortality [28]. Another study showed a 30-day mortality rate of 36.7% in septic patients with high GV, compared to 26.6% in patients with low GV [29]. In critically ill patients with cerebrovascular disease, a two-fold increased risk of in-hospital mortality has been shown [30]. An association between GV and mortality was also shown in critically ill oncologic patients [31].

Despite the existing studies on GV in various critically ill patient populations, the role of GV among critically ill patients with influenza remains limited for patients both with and without diabetes. While previous data have suggested that increased GV is associated with higher mortality in critically ill patients with conditions such as COVID-19 or sepsis, there is lack of evidence examining GV’s impact on critically ill influenza patients. Furthermore, although research has suggested that increased GV may contribute to a higher risk of severe influenza in those with diabetes, these findings have thus far been confined to animal models and have not been confirmed in human populations [32].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine whether GV is independently associated with morbidity and mortality among patients admitted to the ICU with influenza, irrespective of pre-existing diabetes mellitus.

Methods

Study design and study site

This retrospective observation cohort study is a substudy of a large influenza study [33] and includes all adult patients of 23 hospitals in the Netherlands who were admitted to the ICU with laboratory-confirmed influenza A or B infection between November 1st 2023 and March 17th 2024. The Institutional Review Board of the Spaarne Gasthuis approved the general study on April 4th 2024. Ethic statement and consent are explained under the Ethical approval and consent to participate declaration at the end of this manuscript. The study used an opt-out design for consent to participate. In brief, participants who were discharged alive were informed about the use of their data for the study and were given the opportunity to opt-out. The substudy was approved on June 25th 2024.

Patients

Patients were considered to have an influenza A or B virus infection when RNA was detected in a respiratory sample (naso- and/or oropharyngeal swab, sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid). Each center employed their regular PCR assay for detecting respiratory pathogens; assays were not standardized across the study. Patients who did not have a minimum of five measured glucose levels during ICU admission were excluded, as GV metrics are unreliable when calculated from sparse measurements. Therefore, a minimum number of 5 observations was chosen to ensure stable estimates of variability.

Data collection

Patients’ parameters were collected from their electronic medical record. The collected parameters include: (1) demographic features (sex, age, body mass index (BMI)); (2) length of ICU stay and hospital stay; (3) ICU mortality and post-ICU in-hospital mortality; (4) the use of invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV); (5) all blood glucose values during ICU stay; (6) medical history and comorbidities (cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), respiratory disease other than COPD, chronic kidney disease, neurodegenerative disease, solid or hematologic malignancy, autoimmune disease, diabetes mellitus); (7) the use of insulin and corticosteroids during ICU stay. Diabetes status was extracted from the electronic patient record as a documented medical history of diabetes. No additional diagnostic tests were used to classify patients.

Glucose and glycemic variability

To calculate the glycemic variability, all glucose measurements during ICU stay were collected from the electronic medical record. The glucose values were either tested in the hospital’s chemical laboratory or were obtained via point-of-care testing (POCT) or arterial blood gas (ABG). Since the collection of blood glucose values varies between different ICU centers, the frequency of the measuring was done on a case-dependent basis. The timing of patients’ measurement and the total measurements were not standardized.

There are multiple metrics to describe, calculate and compare the GV, including standard deviation (SD), the coefficient of variation (CV) and the mean amplitude of glycemic excursions (MAGE) [34]. For this analysis, the CV was used, which is the ratio of the SD to the mean of all glucose measurements [19, 30]. The formula used in this study is: CV = (SD / mean) x 100% [34, 35]. By expressing variability relative to the mean glucose value, CV allows comparison between patients with different baseline glucose concentrations, such as those with and without diabetes. In contrast, SD alone is influenced by the absolute glucose level and may therefore be less suitable for comparisons across patients with differing mean glucose levels [36]. All participating ICUs used intermittent glucose measurements [34], limiting the applicability of indices such as MAGE, which was originally developed for continuous glucose monitoring data and is most reliably calculated from high-frequency glucose profiles [37]. CV can be calculated from intermittent clinical measurements and is an often-used GV index in studies of critically ill populations [34]. Therefore, CV was considered the most appropriate and clinically interpretable measure of GV in this cohort. Since there are no internationally accepted cut-off values, the patients were divided into four quartiles, following the methodology of previous research [38]. Group 1: CV ≤ 16.5%, group 2: CV 16.6% − 21.3%, group 3: CV 21.4% − 28.5%, group 4: CV ≥ 28.6%.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. All continuous variables were found to be non-normally distributed, as confirmed by Shapiro-Wilk normality tests. Baseline characteristics and patient outcomes were compared between groups using Kruskal-Wallis tests for the continuous variables and Chi-square tests for the categorical variables.

To assess the impact of excluding patients with fewer than five glucose measurements, patients with 2, 3 or 4 glucose measurements were reclassified into group 1. A sensitivity analysis was then performed to evaluate the effect on the association between CV and ICU mortality.

As the glucose measurement method may influence CV calculations, a sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the impact of measurement technique. CV and ICU mortality were compared between patients who had exclusively ABG-measurements and patients who had exclusively POCT-measurements. These groups were selected as nearly all measurements in the cohort were obtained using either ABG or POCT.

We only had information on in-hospital mortality. Therefore, if patients were discharged home they were censored from the analyses. As this violates the survival analysis assumptions, we performed a competing risk analysis, to determine the association between the CV and ICU mortality for the Kaplan-Meier analysis [39]. This analysis calculated the cumulative probabilities of both ICU mortality and non-ICU mortality, allowing for the determination of cumulative ICU mortality across the four groups. To enhance the reliability of the survival estimates, curves were censored for the final 10%.

A Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to assess whether any co-variates influenced the relationship between the CV and various outcomes. Stratified analyses were performed for exploratory purposes, based on clinical considerations. An interaction term between diabetes status and CV was formally tested for effect modification. Baseline co-variates were included in the regression model if they showed substantial differences across the four groups. Age was included due to its potential relationship with the groups, APACHE was included because of its association with ICU mortality. Diabetes mellitus was included in an additional model because of its influence on GV. A causal model with all the baseline characteristics and their possible influence is provided in the Supplementary Materials (supplemental Fig. 1).

Additionally, two separate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to assess the influence of post-admission variables on the relationship between the CV and ICU mortality. These variables, insulin and corticosteroids, were considered because of their influence on CV. Moreover, they exhibited an association with the four CV groups, with their incidence increasing stepwise and progressively across the groups. For the regression models, the CV percentage was divided by 10 to present the risks in a more interpretable form. Variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated for all covariates included in the regression models in order to assess potential multicollinearity. The proportional hazards assumption for all Cox regression models was tested using partial residuals. All confidence intervals (CI) denote a 95% CI.

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS statistics version 26.0. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value of ≤ 0.05. Reporting of this study follows the STROBE guidelines. A completed STROBE checklist is provided in the Supplementary Materials, along with a statement indicating which analyses were pre-specified and which were post hoc.

The remaining 279 patients were included in the final analysis. ICU mortality was higher in patients with higher CV (14.5% in group 1 to 32.9% in group 4). Overall mortality during ICU stay was 21.1%, additional in-hospital mortality after ICU stay was 5.4%. In the total cohort, ICU mortality in people with diabetes (20.8%) is nearly the same as in people without diabetes (21.3%).

Glucose measurements

After reclassifying patients with fewer than five glucose measurements into group 1, the mortality rate in this group increased slightly from 14.5% to 15.5%. However, the difference remained statistically significant (χ² = 8.1, p = 0.04) and the results were consistent with the original analysis. ICU mortality was similar between included and excluded patients (21.1% and 21.7%, p = 0.99), indicating no statistically significant difference. All baseline characteristics and mortality rates of the excluded patients are included in supplemental Fig. 4.

An independent-samples t-test was performed to compare the number of glucose measurements between patients with ICU-mortality (mean = 41.9, SD = 61.6) and without ICU-mortality (mean = 39.2, SD = 57.7). Levene’s test indicated equal variances between the groups (F = 0.13, p = 0.72). The results showed no statistically significant difference in the number of measurements between the mortality and non-mortality groups: t(259) = -0.31, p = 0.76.

In the sensitivity analysis, comparing patients with exclusively ABG measurements and patients with exclusively POCT measurements, CV did not differ significantly between the groups (Mann-Whitney U = 727, p = 0.74). ICU mortality was 10.5% in the ABG group, versus 12.5% in the POCT group, without a statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.79).

CV and ICU mortality

Figure 1 illustrates the relationship between CV and ICU mortality across the four CV groups, as depicted by the CICR curves (p = 0.03). The cumulative incidence during the first 30 days of ICU admission is shown in panel A, a zoomed view of the first 7 days is shown in panel B. These curves show a divergence in mortality rates between the groups over time. A significant separation occurs early, with the mortality curve for group 4 diverging from the other groups by day 4 and subsequently rising to a risk of 0.33 (CI 0.24–0.46) by day 32, reflecting a higher ICU mortality over time. On day 8, the curve for group 3 rises above those of the lower groups as well. The line continues to rise, reaching a mortality risk of 0.19 (CI 0.12–0.31) on day 24. The cumulative risk curves for groups 1 and 2 remain relatively similar throughout the observation period, with the highest mortality risks observed at 0.13 (CI 0.07–0.24) and 0.17 (0.10–0.28) respectively.

Group 1 (CV ≤ 16.5%) reached an ICU mortality risk of 0.13, Group 2 (CV 16.6–21.3%) 0.17, and Group 3 (CV 21.4–28.5%) 0.19. The curves for groups 2 and 3 show considerable overlap, whereas the curve for group 4 separates distinctly. An additional logistic regression analysis showed that a combination of group 1, 2 and 3 had significantly lower ICU mortality risk compared to group 4 (odds ratio = 0.425, CI 0.230–0.787, p < 0.01), corresponding to 2.35-fold higher odds of ICU mortality in group 4. To assess potential non-linearity, a quadratic term for CV was added to the logistic regression model. Although the overall model was statistically significant (χ² = 8.752, df = 2, p = 0.01), only the linear CV term was significant (p = 0.04), while the quadratic term was not (p = 0.15), providing no clear evidence of a non-linear association.

CV and IMV

An analysis depicting the relationship between CV and the likelihood of transitioning to IMV during ICU stay did not show substantial variance in the risk of IMV escalation between high and low CV groups. The cumulative risk of transitioning to IMV / endotracheal intubation during ICU admission showed a similar trend, but was not statistically significant (supplemental Fig. 5). In short, cumulative risk reaches 0.38 (CI 0.28–0.51) for group 1 by day 30. The curve of group 4 follows closely, reaching a cumulative risk of 0.46 (CI 0.35–0.59). The curves of group 2 and 3 show similar patterns, reaching a maximum risk of 0.51 (CI 0.40–0.64) and 0.59 (CI 0.48–0.72) respectively.

CV and various outcomes

Table 3 presents the Cox proportional hazards regression models for the influence of CV on various outcomes, comparing results across three groups: the entire cohort, people with diabetes and people without diabetes. Although the interaction term between diabetes status and CV was not statistically significant (p = 0.25), stratified analyses were performed for exploratory purposes. The first column of the table shows the regression model that evaluated the CV without adjusting for covariates. The second column presents the model adjusted for age and APACHE score, the third column includes additional adjustment for diabetes in the full cohort. All VIF values for the covariates were below 10, indicating that there was no multicollinearity among the covariates. Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective study of critically ill influenza patients, glycemic variability, quantified by the coefficient of variation, was associated with ICU mortality, particularly among patients without diabetes mellitus. These findings suggest that glycemic variability may represent a relevant prognostic marker in this population and warrant prospective validation.

ICU mortality

Our study demonstrates a strong association between elevated CV and increased ICU mortality, particularly among patients without diagnosed diabetes mellitus (DM). This finding aligns with prior research showing that GV is a significant predictor of poor outcomes in critically ill patients [29, 40]. A particularly striking finding in our study is that patients without diagnosed diabetes but with elevated CV exhibited a higher mortality risk compared to their counterparts (i.e. with diagnosed diabetes).

Moreover, in our cohort, the proportion of patients with DM who died in the ICU is comparable to that of patients without DM. In groups 3 and 4, 84 of 139 people (60.4%) did not have a diagnosis of DM, yet these groups exhibited the highest CV and the highest ICU mortality rates.

One plausible explanation is that these patients, despite lacking a formal diabetes diagnosis, may actually have undiagnosed diabetes.

We did not find a statistically significant association between CV and ICU mortality in persons living with diabetes. This finding aligns with prior studies indicating a similar pattern in critically ill populations [21, 41]. This supports the suggestion posited by Krinsley et al., who also found that GV was associated with an increased risk of mortality among patients without a diagnosis of DM [23]. They proposed that lower glycemic target ranges may be more appropriate for patients without diabetes, as those with diabetes may benefit from a higher glucose target range. Chao et al. noted that GV in patients with DM could reflect underlying variations in insulin secretion or sensitivity, whereas in patients without a DM diagnosis, GV might represent a survival response [29].

In our cohort, ICU mortality was primarily driven by patients in the highest GV group, whereas mortality in the lower CV groups were largely comparable. This pattern may suggest that the association between CV and ICU mortality may be more pronounced at higher levels of variability.

Insulin treatment

High CV was also associated with insulin therapy use in both the overall cohort and patients without DM, independent of covariates. Adjustment for insulin dose did not attenuate the association between CV and ICU mortality; instead, mortality risk increased and remained significant. This may reflect adverse effects of insulin therapy, such as hypoglycemia [22, 42, 43], or confounding by indication, whereby higher insulin requirements indicate greater illness severity and metabolic dysregulation [44]. Further studies are needed to clarify whether insulin contributes causally to mortality or serves primarily as a marker of disease severity.

Corticosteroid treatment

In patients without diabetes, CV was significantly associated with corticosteroid use. Corticosteroids are known to impair glucose homeostasis and may increase glycemic variability through enhanced gluconeogenesis and reduced peripheral glucose uptake [45]. However, it remains uncertain whether elevated glycemic variability reflects corticosteroid effects or greater disease severity prompting corticosteroid treatment. Importantly, the association between CV and ICU mortality persisted after adjustment for corticosteroid use. As only corticosteroid exposure was assessed without detailed dosing information, further studies are needed to clarify the contribution of corticosteroids to glycemic variability and outcomes in critically ill influenza patients. The role of corticosteroids in severe influenza remains controversial, with prior evidence suggesting no clinical benefit and a potential increase in mortality [46].

Glucose monitoring

The strong association between CV and ICU mortality may suggest a potential benefit of improved glycemic monitoring in critically ill influenza patients, particularly if used as a biomarker for severe disease. Our retrospective findings indicate that CV could serve as an early warning sign, helping clinicians identify patients at greater risk of adverse outcomes before they become clinically apparent. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems could provide real-time, high-resolution data on glucose fluctuations, supporting more precise management of blood glucose levels and enabling earlier interventions when necessary.

CGM has a proven benefit in patients with DM, allowing for more effective glucose management [47]. However, it might also be of interest for patients without DM, as it could help clinicians detect stress-induced hyperglycemia and other metabolic derangements more effectively. Monitoring CV closely could guide treatment adjustments, such as insulin therapy, to prevent excessive glucose fluctuations that may contribute to poor outcomes. However, the role of CGM in ICU settings requires further investigation. We recommend both a prospective study to explore its potential to improve outcomes in patients with high CV, particularly those without pre-existing DM, and a retrospective study examining the differences between centers in the management of severe influenza. At the same time, it would be useful to investigate whether similar trends in CV and outcomes are observed in cases of ARDS caused by other viral pathogens, to assess the generalizability of our current findings.

Strengths and limitations

This study benefits from a large, multicenter cohort of critically ill patients across 23 hospitals, which enhances the generalizability of our findings. However, there are some important limitations. First, the retrospective design of the study and the variability in glucose measurement protocols across centers may introduce biases. The absence of standardized glucose measurements across all centers may have affected the precision of our analysis, since the heterogeneity could distort calculated CV and may influence the comparability across patients and centers. To address this potential source of bias, we performed a sensitivity analysis. We observed no significant differences in CV or ICU mortality between ABG measurements in the laboratory and POCT measurements, suggesting that differences in measurement methodology are unlikely to account for the observed association between CV and ICU mortality. Nevertheless, laboratory-based methods generally provide higher analytical precision than POCT devices, particularly in critical care settings [48]. Therefore, residual confounding related to differences in measurement accuracy cannot be completely excluded.

Additionally, we excluded patients who had fewer than five glucose measurements, which may have affected the accuracy of our CV data and mortality risk assessments. The large number of excluded patients may also introduce selection bias. However, we performed a sensitivity analysis to assess the potential impact of the exclusion of these patients. The results indicated that the exclusion did not have a substantial effect on the observed association between CV and ICU mortality.

Another limitation is the lack of detailed information on corticosteroid exposure, including dosage, timing, and duration, which limits our ability to fully assess the relationship between corticosteroid use, CV, and mortality and precludes causal interpretation. In addition, influenza subtype data were unavailable, preventing analyses of potential differences between influenza A and B. Lastly, although APACHE scores were included as a covariate, these scores primarily reflect severity at ICU admission and may not capture dynamic changes in clinical status over time. Future studies should consider incorporating longitudinal SOFA scores to better characterize illness trajectory and improve mortality prediction.

Despite these limitations, our study provides exploratory evidence that glycemic variability, as reflected by CV, is associated with ICU mortality in critically ill influenza patients, particularly among those without diabetes. Given the observational design, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study reveals a significant association between elevated GV and ICU mortality in critically ill influenza patients, particularly among those without a prior diagnosis of DM. If future studies confirm a causal relationship, prospective evaluation of GV interventions may be warranted, while changes to current clinical practice based on this study alone are not recommended.
