Association between oral frailty and locomotive syndrome among community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Eiko Sato, Takashi Ohi, Takamasa Komiyama, Moe Sato, Yoshinori Hattori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association between oral frailty and locomotor function, as well as the mechanisms underlying this relationship, remains unclear. This study aimed to examine the association between oral frailty and locomotive syndrome among community-dwelling older Japanese adults, exploring the roles of dietary intake and frequency of going outside in this association.MethodsThis study included 439 community-dwelling older adults (mean age: 77.7 years) who underwent health examinations in Miyagi and Akita prefectures, Japan. Oral frailty was assessed using the Oral Frailty Six-Item Scale, comprising four objective measures (number of remaining teeth, masticatory ability, oral diadochokinesis, and tongue pressure) and two subjective measures (self-reported masticatory performance and swallowing function). Locomotive syndrome, a domain of intrinsic capacity, was evaluated using the stand-up test, two-step test, and 25-item Geriatric Locomotive Function Scale. Covariates included age, sex, education, smoking status, alcohol consumption, handgrip strength, cognition, and comorbidities.ResultsAmong the participants, 321 (73.1%) had locomotive syndrome, and 111 had oral frailty. Multivariate modified Poisson regression analysis showed that individuals with confirmed oral frailty had a significantly higher prevalence of locomotive syndrome than those without oral frailty (prevalence ratio, 1.21; 95% confidence interval, 1.10–1.34). Structural equation modeling showed a significant indirect effect of going outside on this association, whereas the dietary variety score did not mitigate this association.ConclusionThis cross-sectional study demonstrated a significant association between oral frailty and locomotive syndrome among community-dwelling older adults. Moreover, frequency of going outside may partially explain this association.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08602-1.

Introduction

Population aging is a global phenomenon. By 2050, the proportion of older adults is estimated to increase to 17%, and the number of individuals aged ≥ 80 years is projected to triple compared to 2021 levels [1]. Therefore, maintaining and enhancing intrinsic capacity is fundamental for achieving “healthy aging” [2]. Locomotor function is a constitutive domain of intrinsic capacity [3].

The Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) has proposed the concept of “locomotive syndrome (locomo),” referring to older adults who require long-term care services due to problems with locomotor organs, as well as those with risk factors that may lead to a future need for such services [4]. This concept emphasizes the decline in mobility associated with aging or the dysfunction of the locomotive organs, which are responsible for a wide range of motor functions, including standing, walking, and activities of daily living [5–7]. Advanced stages of locomotive syndrome are strongly associated with functional disability and mortality [5]. Furthermore, a study of patients with rheumatoid arthritis reported that 95.6% of those at the most severe stage of locomotive syndrome had pre-frailty or frailty [8]. Consequently, locomotive syndrome has become an important indicator of declining physical function, and this concept has been adopted internationally [9, 10]. The previous report suggests that a healthy diet and social factors may help prevent locomotive syndrome [11], and that oral health plays a vital role in preventing adverse health outcomes, including frailty, functional disability, and mortality [12, 13]. An increasing number of studies have investigated the association between individual oral health indicators—such as the number of remaining teeth, masticatory ability, swallowing function, and periodontal disease—and locomotive syndrome [9, 14–17]. However, evidence regarding the association between oral frailty, a cumulative and multidimensional indicator of oral health, and locomotive syndrome remains limited. Moreover, few studies have examined whether dietary intake and homebound status mediate this association, although good oral health has been associated with greater dietary diversity and more frequent going outside [18, 19]. Oral frailty may impair masticatory and swallowing function, leading to reduced dietary variety and nutritional imbalance, which in turn may contribute to musculoskeletal decline. Similarly, oral functional decline may reduce social participation and frequency of going outside, resulting in disuse of skeletal muscle and increased risk of locomotive syndrome. Based on these pathways, we hypothesized that oral frailty is associated with locomotive syndrome, and that this association is explained by dietary intake and frequency of going outside.

This cross-sectional study aimed to examine the association between oral frailty and locomotive syndrome among community-dwelling older adults and to evaluate the potential role of dietary intake and going outside in this association.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional study included community-dwelling older adults recruited through municipal health checkups conducted between June and August 2024 in 10 municipalities across Miyagi and Akita Prefectures, Japan. A total of 446 individuals applied for and participated in the health checkups. After excluding seven individuals who had difficulty walking independently, had a pacemaker or implantable cardioverter-defibrillator, had a prosthetic limb, or declined participation, 439 participants were included in the final analysis. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tohoku University Graduate School of Dentistry (approval no. 35745). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Data collection

Assessment of oral frailty

Trained dental professionals, including dentists and dental hygienists, conducted oral examinations. To minimize measurement errors, all examiners discussed and standardized the examination procedures and assessment methods in advance. Oral frailty was assessed using the Oral Frailty Six-Item Survey (OF-6) [18], which comprises oral functional tests and a questionnaire. OF-6 includes six components: (i) number of remaining teeth, (ii) masticatory ability, (iii) oral diadochokinesis (“ta”), (iv) tongue pressure, (v) self-rated masticatory performance, and (vi) self-rated swallowing function. Oral frailty was operationally defined as the presence of three or more of these components [20]. The number of remaining teeth was defined as the total number of erupted teeth, excluding residual roots and teeth with grade 3 mobility. A reduced number of remaining teeth was defined as fewer than 20 [20]. Masticatory ability was evaluated using color-changing chewing gum designed to assess mixing ability (XYLITOL; Lotte Co., Japan). Participants were instructed to chew the gum for 60 s and then expectorate it. The color of the chewed gum was measured using a colorimeter (CR-13; Konica-Minolta Co., Tokyo, Japan) by determining the a* value in the CIE Lab* color space [21]. As previously described, decreased masticatory ability was defined as an a* value of < 14.2 for males and < 10.8 for females [20]. Tongue pressure was measured using a JMS tongue pressure measurement device (GC, Tokyo, Japan), and the maximum value from three trials was recorded [22]. Decreased tongue pressure was defined as < 27.4 kPa in males and < 26.5 kPa in females [20]. Oral diadochokinesis was assessed by instructing participants to repeat the syllable /ta/ as rapidly as possible for 5 s. The number of repetitions per second was recorded using a digital counter (T.K.K.3350; Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Niigata, Japan) [23]. Reduced articulatory oral motor skill was defined as < 5.2 repetitions/s in males and < 5.4 repetitions/s in females [20]. Self-rated masticatory performance and swallowing function were assessed using a self-administered questionnaire. Participants were asked: “Do you feel it is more difficult to eat hard foods compared with six months ago?” and “Do you experience choking when drinking tea or soup?” Participants who responded affirmatively were classified as having impairments.

Assessment of locomotive syndrome

The outcome of interest was locomotive syndrome, which was evaluated using the “Locomotive Syndrome Risk Test” proposed by the Japanese Orthopaedic Association [24]. This assessment consisted of three components: the two-step test, the stand-up test, and the 25-item Geriatric Locomotive Function Scale (GLFS-25). Participants classified as stage 0 were considered to have no locomotive syndrome, whereas those classified as stages 1–3 were defined as having locomotive syndrome.

In the two-step test, participants took two maximal strides, and the two-step value was calculated as the total stride length divided by the participant’s height. Values of < 0.9, 0.9 to < 1.1, 1.1 to < 1.3, and ≥ 1.3 were defined as stages 3, 2, 1, and 0, respectively [25]. The stand-up test was used to assess lower-limb muscle strength and was conducted according to standardized procedures [24]. Participants were first instructed to stand up from a 40-cm-high chair using both legs. Those unable to do so were classified as stage 3. Participants who succeeded were then instructed to stand up from the same chair using only one leg. If unsuccessful, they were asked to stand up using both legs from progressively lower chair heights.

The lowest height at which participants could stand successfully was used to determine the stage of locomotive syndrome. Participants unable to stand on one leg from a 40-cm chair but able to stand using both legs from a 20-cm chair were classified as stage 1; those unable to stand using both legs from a 20-cm chair but able to stand from a 30-cm chair were classified as stage 2; and those unable to stand using both legs from a 30-cm chair were classified as stage 3. The GLFS-25 consists of 25 items related to physical function and daily activities, with total scores ranging from 0 to 100. Scores ≥ 24, 16–23, 7–15, and 0–6 were defined as stages 3, 2, 1, and 0, respectively [26]. The final locomotive syndrome stage was determined based on the highest stage identified across the three assessments [24].

Covariates

Information on age, sex, educational attainment, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and medical history was collected using a self-administered questionnaire. Trained staff were available to assist participants as needed to minimize missing responses. Educational attainment was defined by the age at completion of the highest level of education and dichotomized as < 18 years or ≥ 18 years. Smoking status was classified as smokers (current or former) and nonsmokers (never). Drinking status was similarly categorized as drinkers (current or former) and nondrinkers (never). Comorbidities included osteoporosis, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and joint disease.

Handgrip strength was measured using a digital handgrip dynamometer (T.K.K.5401; Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Niigata, Japan). The participants performed two trials with each hand, alternating hands, and the maximum value for each hand was recorded. The mean of the maximum values for both hands was used as the final handgrip strength value [27]. A rest period of at least 30 s was allowed between measurements. Cognitive function was assessed using the cognitive domain of the Kihon Checklist. Participants who answered positively to at least one of the three questions were considered to have cognitive impairment.

Dietary intake and frequency of going outside were also assessed to examine their potential roles in the association between oral health and locomotive syndrome. Dietary intake was evaluated using the Dietary Variety Score (DVS), an indicator of dietary diversity among older adults [28]. One point was assigned to each of 10 food groups (meat, fish and shellfish, eggs, soybean products, milk and dairy products, green and yellow vegetables, other vegetables, fruits, seaweed, and oils and fats) if consumed “almost every day.” The total score ranged from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating greater dietary diversity and better nutritional balance. A score < 7 was defined as poor dietary diversity [28]. Frequency of going outside was assessed using a question from the Kihon Checklist: “Compared with last year, do you go out less frequently?” Participants who responded “yes” were classified as having a decreased frequency of going outside [29, 30].

Statistical analysis

Participant characteristics according to locomotive syndrome status were compared using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables. Associations between oral frailty, each component of oral frailty, and locomotive syndrome (absence: stage 0; presence: stages 1–3) were examined using modified Poisson regression to estimate prevalence ratios (PRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 was additionally adjusted for educational attainment, smoking status, drinking status, handgrip strength, cognition, and comorbidities (osteoporosis, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and joint disease). To examine potential roles, Model 3 was further adjusted for DVS, and Model 4 was further adjusted for going outside status, in addition to the covariates included in Model 2.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted to investigate the potential mechanisms linking oral frailty, dietary intake, going outside, and locomotive syndrome. Total, direct, and indirect effects were estimated after adjusting for age, sex, educational attainment, smoking status, drinking status, handgrip strength, cognition, and comorbidities (osteoporosis, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and joint disease).

Three sensitivity analyses were performed: (1) the Oral Frailty Five-Item Checklist (OF-5) [31, 32], a brief questionnaire-based assessment, was used for analysis instead of OF-6; (2) analyses using the severity of locomotive syndrome as the outcome; and (3) analyses in which the number of oral frailty components replaced the exposure variable. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata SE version 19 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05.

Results

The mean age (± standard deviation) of the participants (N = 439) was 77.7 ± 5.5 years, and 357 (81.3%) were women. Overall, 321 participants were classified as having locomotive syndrome, and 111 were classified as having oral frailty. As shown in Table 1, participants with locomotive syndrome were older and had higher body mass index, lower handgrip strength, and cognitive impairment, and were more likely to have decreased frequency of going outside and oral frailty than those without locomotive syndrome. Participants with oral frailty were significantly older, had lower handgrip strength, had cognitive impairment, and were more likely to be nondrinkers, have hypertension, have decreased frequency of going outside, and have locomotive syndrome than those without oral frailty (Additional File 1).

The structural equation modeling illustrated the relationships among oral frailty (OF-6), DVS, going outside, and locomotive syndrome (Fig. 1). The total effect of oral frailty on locomotive syndrome was significantly positive (β = 1.74, P = 0.004). A significant indirect effect through frequency of going outside was observed (β = 1.15, P = 0.030), whereas the indirect effect through DVS was not significant. In the first sensitivity analysis, using OF-5 instead of OF-6 showed a significantly higher prevalence of locomotive syndrome (Additional File 2). In the second sensitivity analysis using the severity of locomotive syndrome as the outcome, participants with oral frailty had a significantly higher prevalence of severe locomotive syndrome (Additional File 3). Additional adjustment for going outside slightly attenuated the association between OF-6 and locomotive syndrome severity. When the exposure was defined as the number of oral frailty components (OF-6 and OF-5), the prevalence ratio for locomotive syndrome increased significantly with each additional component (Additional File 4). This association was again slightly attenuated after adjustment for going outside.

Discussion

This cross-sectional study demonstrated a significant association between oral frailty and locomotive syndrome, the locomotion domain of intrinsic capacity, among community-dwelling older Japanese adults, even after adjusting for sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, handgrip strength, cognition, and medical history. The association was slightly attenuated when frequency of going outside was taken into account, and the sensitivity analyses were consistent with the primary results.

Previous studies have reported associations between individual oral health indicators and locomotive syndrome. Akahane et al. showed that lifestyle factors, including smoking, number of remaining teeth, and periodontal disease, were associated with locomotive syndrome in Japanese older adults [14]. Saito et al. conducted a cross-sectional study of 1,142 Japanese adults and reported that fewer remaining teeth and impaired subjective masticatory performance were associated with locomotive syndrome [15]. In a cross-sectional study of 936 community-dwelling older adults in Japan, Nakamura et al. demonstrated that a subjective decline in masticatory function and difficulty swallowing were associated with locomotive syndrome [16]. Similarly, Tominaga et al. conducted a cross-sectional study of 1,084 older Japanese adults and reported that declines in both objective and subjective masticatory ability, as well as in the number of remaining teeth, were associated with locomotive syndrome [17].

In addition, Nagilla et al. conducted a cross-sectional study of 500 older patients attending a dental clinic in Mahabubnagar, India, and found that those with more missing teeth were more likely to have locomotive syndrome [9]. These findings align with the present study, which demonstrated associations between locomotive syndrome and indicators of oral function, including the number of remaining teeth, subjective masticatory difficulty, and swallowing problems. Importantly, our study extends existing evidence by demonstrating associations with objective oral function measures, such as tongue pressure and masticatory ability, as well as with oral frailty, a multidimensional, cumulative indicator of oral health. However, longitudinal studies examining oral frailty and locomotive syndrome are scarce, underscoring the need for further investigation of their causal mechanisms.

We also examined the potential roles of dietary intake and social factors. Our findings suggest that decreased frequency of going outside may partially explain the association between oral frailty and locomotive syndrome, whereas dietary intake did not appear to mitigate this association. Previous studies have linked declines in masticatory and swallowing function to dietary imbalance [18] and poor nutritional intake [33, 34], which have been associated with locomotive syndrome [11]. A possible explanation for the lack of a dietary role in this study is the high proportion of female participants, who may have greater nutritional knowledge and confidence in meal preparation than men [35]. Further studies in more diverse populations are warranted. Another possible explanation is that the DVS, while useful as a simple indicator, primarily reflects frequency of food group consumption and may not adequately capture nutrient quality, portion size, or protective nutrients such as vitamin D and protein, which have been reported to reduce musculoskeletal risk [36]. Although healthy dietary patterns such as the Mediterranean diet may positively affect muscle maintenance [36], our measure may not have been sensitive enough to detect associations between dietary patterns, evaluated by DVS, and decline in musculoskeletal function. In Japanese older adults, more direct indicators of nutrition, rather than dietary patterns, may be more appropriate. Previous research suggests that impaired oral function may limit the intake of protein-rich foods, consequently contributing to loss of muscle mass and sarcopenia, even in individuals who are not underweight [37]. Future research should investigate whether nutritional intake—particularly animal protein intake—mediates an association between oral health and locomotive syndrome. In addition, socioeconomic status (e.g., income, occupation) and psychological factors (e.g., depression, social isolation) could influence both oral health and the ability to maintain a diverse diet, and should be considered in future studies. In contrast, frequency of going outside may represent an important pathway linking oral frailty and locomotive syndrome. Oral functional decline, including unclear speech, altered facial expression, and aesthetic concerns such as anterior tooth loss, may lead to reduced social participation and avoidance of going out [38–42]. Reduced social engagement may, in turn, result in disuse of skeletal muscle, contributing to the development of decline in musculoskeletal function [11].

Although evidence linking oral frailty to locomotor function is growing, longitudinal and interventional data remain limited. In particular, it remains unclear whether interventions targeting oral frailty can effectively prevent or delay the onset of locomotive syndrome. Addressing this gap could further establish oral frailty as a modifiable risk factor for declining locomotor capacity [43]. From a practical perspective, these findings highlight the importance of incorporating oral frailty screening into community-based health programs, where early detection and preventive interventions can be implemented. Interprofessional collaboration among dental professionals, physicians, nutritionists, and rehabilitation specialists may be essential to address oral function, nutrition, and mobility in an integrated manner. Furthermore, maintaining oral health may reduce barriers to going outside, thereby preventing disuse of skeletal muscles and helping to maintain mobility. Strategies that encourage social participation—such as community activities and outdoor engagement—could complement oral health interventions. Ultimately, the absence of effective strategies for maintaining oral health may lead to an oral–locomotion cycle, characterized by a reciprocal relationship between oral health, access to dental (medical) care, and decline in musculoskeletal function.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. In addition, a bidirectional relationship between oral frailty and locomotive syndrome is plausible. Specifically, locomotive syndrome may hinder access to dental care. Therefore, the observed indirect effect of going outside should be interpreted as an associative pathway rather than evidence of mediation. In the future, longitudinal studies with repeated assessments of oral and locomotive function, considering potential lagged effects, are needed. Second, participants were recruited through health checkups and may represent relatively healthy, independent older adults, potentially introducing selection bias and limiting generalizability. However, such bias may have underestimated the observed associations. Additionally, the high proportion of female participants may have influenced the observed relationships, as females have a higher prevalence of locomotive syndrome and frailty [26, 44] and are generally more health-conscious and socially active [45]. Third, due to the limited sample size and statistical power, we were unable to conduct stratified analyses by sex. Therefore, sex-specific mechanisms should be investigated in larger-scale populations in future studies. Finally, a more comprehensive assessment of social factors could help clarify the mechanisms linking oral health and decline in musculoskeletal function.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study of community-dwelling older Japanese adults suggests that oral frailty is associated with reduced locomotor capacity and that decreased frequency of going outside partially mitigates this association. These findings highlight the potential importance of comprehensive oral assessments and integrated oral care within a multidisciplinary framework to prevent declining musculoskeletal function. Future longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to confirm causality and to inform preventive strategies.
