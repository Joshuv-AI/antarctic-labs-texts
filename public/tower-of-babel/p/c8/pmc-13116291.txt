Informal Caregivers As True Partners In Mental Health Recovery: Is It Time For A Complete Rewrite?
Authors: Shyhrete Rexhaj, Jocelyn Deloyer, Philippe Golay, Laurence Fond-Harmant, Jérôme Favrod, Charles Bonsack, Yasser Khazaal, Camille Gaglio, Alexandra Nguyen, Krzysztof Skuza, Margarita Maraitou, Hélène Wilquin, Romain Rey, Spyridon Zormpas, Christian Burr, Aurélie Clement-Perritaz, Romuald Droczinski, Monique Corbaz, Christian Pfister, André Decreane, Donatella Marazziti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This viewpoint highlights the indispensable role of informal caregivers in mental health recovery. The authors note that mental disorders affect around one in eight people worldwide, with major impacts on family quality of life and substantial health system costs. Informal caregivers are advocated not as peripheral supporters, but as key partners in care pathways, to be recognized in public policies and clinical practice. Scientific evidence shows their involvement reduces relapses, isolation, and hospitalization rates.This viewpoint identifies significant psychosocial risks facing informal caregivers and stresses the need for tailored support measures. The authors underscore the need for comprehensive support measures, clearer institutional recognition, ethically balanced information-sharing practices, structured educational initiatives, and more rigorous research and evaluation frameworks. Five priority areas are proposed for transforming care models: recognition, support, ethics, education, and research. Neglecting informal caregivers weakens mental health systems, whereas including them helps build more humane, effective, and resilient approaches.

Introduction

Mental disorders are one of the greatest public health challenges of our time. Nearly one billion people worldwide are affected, accounting for approximately one in eight individuals (World Health Organization, 2025). The burden is substantial: high prevalence, major consequences for quality of life, enduring impacts on families, reduced life expectancy by 15 to 20 years due to medical comorbidities and suicide and significant costs to health system (Laidi et al., 2023; Observatoire suisse de la santé, 2025; World Health Organization, 2025). Up to 75% of mental disorders begin before the age of 25, resulting in lifelong disparities in education, employment, and social participation (McGorry et al., 2024; World Health Organization, 2022).

The World Health Organization’s (WHO) Mental Health Action Plan 2013–2030 outlines four priorities: strengthening leadership and governance; scaling up integrated community-based services; promoting prevention; and improving information, research, and data (World Health Organization, 2022). Central to this agenda is the recognizing of patients and their families as key actors in care systems, within a human rights framework that emphasizes continuity and participation. This paradigm reframes informal caregivers as active partners rather as peripheral supporters. The 2024 Eurocarers report echoes this vision by operationalizing the European Pillar of Social Rights, highlighting informal caregivers as essential contributors to sustainable health systems (Eurocarers, 2024). Similarly, the World Psychiatric Association emphasizes that strong collaborations between professionals, families, and communities are essential for lasting responses to mental illness (Wasserman, 2024). While these frameworks present a compelling vision, their full implementation remains a distant goal, with many of the proposed shifts still far from being realized in practice.

Despite this compelling vision, implementation remains limited. The role of relatives continues to be undervalued and underutilized. Their own well-being and support needs are frequently overlooked, calling for a fundamental shift in how mental health systems recognize, integrate, and care for those who care.

Recovery and the role of informal caregivers

In mental health, recovery is not merely defined as the absence of symptoms, but as the process of living a satisfying and meaningful life, regardless of symptom presence (Anthony, 1993; Deegan, 1988). Recovery involves self-redefinition, flourishing, and exercising rights. Evidence indicates that aligning psychosocial care with the recovery paradigm model necessitates the active involvement of informal caregivers, as full partners, not as marginal resources (Monteiro et al., 2022; Petkari et al., 2021).

The recovery movement was initiated by users of mental health services, who challenged traditional clinical models and advocated for autonomy, dignity, and the centrality of lived experience in care. Today it is clear that similar recognition must be extended to informal caregivers, whose experiential knowledge and emotional labor are equally vital to recovery-oriented systems (Rexhaj, Nguyen, et al., 2023). However, clear guidelines for incorporating informal caregivers into services are scarce, creating a gap between scientific recommendations, financial concerns, and practice.

Informal caregivers as key actors

Informal caregivers play a critical role in early detection, access, continuity, and relapse prevention. The evidence is unequivocal: their involvement reduces relapse, hospitalization, and social isolation while improving quality of life (Okafor & Monahan, 2023; Rexhaj, Martinez, et al., 2023; Rodolico et al., 2022; Tham & Solomon, 2024; Wyngaerden et al., 2022). They provide practical support, emotional stability, and advocacy, and often identify changes in well-being earlier than professionals.

However, informal caregiving carries risks. It is associated with psychological distress, anxiety, and depression (Cham et al., 2022; Coloni Terrapon et al., 2023; Fleitas Alfonzo et al., 2022), often compounded by insufficient information, exclusion from decision-making process, and overly restrictive applications of confidentiality policies (Petkari et al., 2021; Slade et al., 2007). Despite the robust evidence, interventions that empower informal caregiver remain limited, fragmented, and rarely scaled up (Hansen et al., 2022; Rexhaj, Martinez, et al., 2023).

Promising innovations exist. The Ensemble program, for example, is a brief, personalized intervention, five-session intervention that has been shown to significantly reduce informal caregiver burden, increase optimism, and decrease psychological distress, while strengthening empowerment compared to usual support (Rexhaj, Martinez, et al., 2023). These findings underscore the urgent need to develop informal caregiver-inclusive models of psychosocial care. Without adequate support, informal caregivers risk becoming the next patients themselves due to emotional depletion, exhaustion, and clinical distress, leading to a situation where those who care must be cared for.

Call for action

Drawing on databases such as PubMed, Cairn, and PsycINFO, as well as reports from organizations like the WHO and Eurocarers, professional guidelines, and leading thematic journals in the field, we conducted a narrative review of recent international scientific literature on the role of informal caregivers in mental health. This review identifies innovative models, robust results, or critical analyses of public policy. Our approach aims to provide an expert and up-to-date synthesis or supports reflection and offers practical guidance for professionals and researchers. Special attention was given to studies evaluating the impact of interventions for caregivers on recovery and quality of life for affected individuals.

Toward systemic change

Recent scientific literature indicates five priority areas essential for transforming psychosocial care into a model that fully includes informal caregivers (figure 1).

Toward Systemic Change

Axis 1: recognition of informal caregivers

Institutional recognition of informal caregivers is a fundamental lever for improving their well-being and engagement in the psychosocial rehabilitation of those they support. According to international literature, there is a growing trend of recognition through legislation, such as the Adaptation of Society to Aging Act in France, which defines and regulates the status of informal caregivers (Faye-Ropaul & Darriet, 2023). This recognition is also reflected in the increasing incorporation of the topic into public policies and in support for associations, which authors cite as a kay factor to combating stigma and valuing the role of families in care provision. Governments are encouraged to promote visibility actions and support for associations to better integrate informal caregivers into the care pathway (Männikkö et al., 2024).

Axis 2: support and respite

Support for informal caregivers, particularly through access to respite care and support groups, has been shown to effectively reduce perceived burden, prevent mental fatigue, and improve quality of life. Structured programs, such as the BREF program in France or psychoeducation programs, have significantly reduced depressive symptoms among caregivers, while fostering their participation in a collaborative and educational environments (Hansen et al., 2022; Okafor & Monahan, 2023; Rey, 2023).

BREF is a single-family, in-person, program designed to provide caregivers with early and systematic support and basic knowledge while engaging them in a graduated support pathway (Rey et al., 2023).

Additionally, emotional, social, and sometimes financial support is essential for counteracting the isolation and stigma often reported by informal caregivers. Tailored interventions, such as Ensemble program, that focus on the informal caregiver’s experiences and resources, have been shown to empower them in their situation (Rexhaj, Martinez, et al., 2023). The ongoing availability of these services is internationally recommended to ensure continuity of care for patients and support for informal caregivers (Haute Autorité de Santé, 2022).

Early identification of the support needs of informal caregivers is essential to prevent their burden and to strengthen the quality and sustainability of their involvement in care, taking in account their personal characteristics (e.g. age, relationship status, employment, family configuration and role…) (Coloni Terrapon et al., 2023; Lacey et al., 2024; Plessis et al., 2024).

Axis 3: ethical issues and confidentiality

Collaboration between informal caregivers and healthcare professionals is indispensable but challenged by ethical concerns related to confidentiality and autonomy. Balancing the rights of the person in care with the legitimate needs of caregivers for information remains a delicate ethical task. Evidence highlights the importance of providing caregivers with minimal but essential information that enables them to offer safe and adequate support, without breaching patient confidentiality. Shared-decision-making tools and clear communication frameworks can assist professionals navigating these tensions (Männikkö et al., 2024). A recovery-oriented approach requires recognizing informal caregivers as autonomous actors with their own rights and needs. Their systematic inclusion in care teams and decision-making processes should be promoted, regardless of the patient’s diagnosis or level of participation in treatment (Morin et al., 2023). Furthermore, the involvement of informal caregivers is recognized as essential in recovery-oriented practices. Professionals should not only disseminate pertinent and recent recovery practices evidence but also actively involve informal caregivers in decision-making processes regarding their experiential knowledge and capacity to provide an informal support to patients (Mekuriaw et al., 2025). Accelerating the development of integrated perspectives would be ethically preferable, as it can lead to more robust and reliable recovery-oriented outcomes. It is time to give equal attention to all informal caregivers regardless to patient’s diagnosis. Even, if some of informal caregivers experience mental health problems themself, excluding them from caregiver-based interventions assessments is no longer acceptable solution as often been the case until now (Hasan & Van Belkum, 2019). Furthermore, professional support addressed to informal caregivers should be assessed independently to patients’ participation in programs or treatments (Zhang et al., 2023). Informal caregivers have the right to be supported in their role.

Axis 4: education and training

The literature emphasizes the positive impact of psychoeducation programs, family-focused support, professional support, skills development/therapy, tailored interventions, dyadic-focused programs and multimodal approaches for informal caregivers, which reduce psychological burden, stigma and relapse (Hansen et al., 2022; Hasan & Van Belkum, 2019; Rexhaj, Martinez, et al., 2023; Rey, 2023). However, the programs differ in content, and the outcomes are heterogeneous in studies, which limits their generalizability.

Psychosocial interventions need to cover the continuum from early signs of distress in the community, to the hospitalization context and the recovery-oriented continuum of care. They must also be adapted to the context of health service systems. There is a need to optimize the effectiveness of care pathways and to strengthen professionals’ skills to promote the well-being of both caregivers and care recipients, which requires innovative solutions (Ali et al., 2024; Hasan & Belkum, 2019; Rexhaj, Martinez, et al., 2023; R Rey, 2023; Zhang et al., 2023).

A gap persists between existing professional skills, necessary system transformations, and investment in mental health services (AÉSIO mutuelle, Chaire Transitions démographiques, & Transitions économiques, 2021; World Health Organization, 2025). Therefore, it is essential to train healthcare professionals in recovery-oriented practices and partnership approaches to foster attitudes consistent with the necessary system transformations. Additionally, specific training on the role of informal caregivers and on concrete interventions, such as emotional management, problem-solving, detecting signs of relapse, positive communication, empowering the informal caregiver’s role and co-designing clinical solutions, should be rapidly implemented.

Axis 5: research and evaluation

International analyses highlight the urgent need to bolster empirical research and the evaluation of innovative measures aimed at informal caregivers, particularly through effectiveness studies and economic analyses. A systematic review from University of Birmingham emphasizes that demographic changes necessitate the development of specific evaluation tools to better target the most cost-effective and psychosocially beneficial programs. Researchers recommend systematically investigating the long-term impact on mental health of informal caregivers and care recipients, using robust and multidisciplinary assessment methods (Ali et al., 2024).

These five axes, supported by the available literature, are essential conditions and forward-looking perspectives for the evolution of public policies and the advancement of innovative practices for informal caregivers in mental health.

Conclusion

Although literature provides a strong foundation and promising directions, the persistent gaps between evidence, policy, and practice reveal that incremental adjustments are insufficient. What is needed is not a rewrite of scientific knowledge but a complete rewrite of how it is implemented in real-world systems—one that fully embraces informal caregivers as true partners in mental health recovery, with recognition, support, and inclusion at every level of care (Bonsack et al., 2025).

Informal caregivers remain the invisible backbone of mental health systems worldwide. Their daily commitment sustains recovery, alleviates pressure on service, and improves lives. Neglecting them is both ethically indefensible and systemically short-sighted. If left unsupported, they risk becoming the next patients. By placing informal caregivers at the core of psychosocial interventions, we can build more humane, resilient, and effective mental health systems. This must be done with care and precision, as overlooking their needs directly harms those on whom the system relies most.

Future directions

Strengthening informal caregiver inclusion in mental health recovery now depends on concrete implementation. Training programs, policy frameworks, and research agendas must systematically integrate the voice and expertise of informal caregivers. Moving from isolated initiatives to structural change will ensure that caring for those who care becomes a defining principle of 21st-century mental health systems.
