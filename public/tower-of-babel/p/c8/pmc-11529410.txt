De Novo Missense Variations of ATP8B2 Impair Its Phosphatidylcholine Flippase Activity
Authors: Hiroyuki Takatsu, Narumi Nishimura, Yusuke Kosugi, Haruo Ogawa, Kazuhisa Nakayama, Estelle Colin, Konrad Platzer, Rami Abou Jamra, Silke Redler, Clément Prouteau, Alban Ziegler, Hye-Won Shin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractP4-ATPases comprise a family of lipid flippases that translocate lipids from the exoplasmic (or luminal) to the cytoplasmic leaflet of biological membranes. Of the 14 known human P4-ATPases, ATP8B2 is a phosphatidylcholine flippase at the plasma membrane, but its physiological function is not well understood. Although ATP8B2 could interact with both CDC50A and CDC50B, it required only the CDC50A interaction for its exit from the endoplasmic reticulum and subsequent transport to the plasma membrane. Three de novo monoallelic missense variations of ATP8B2 were found in patients with intellectual disability. None of these variations affected the interaction of ATP8B2 with CDC50A or its localization to the plasma membrane. However, variations of either of two amino acid residues, which are conserved in all P4-ATPases, significantly reduced the phosphatidylcholine flippase activity of ATP8B2. Furthermore, mutations in the corresponding residues of ATP8B1 and ATP11C were found to decrease their flippase activities toward phosphatidylcholine and phosphatidylserine, respectively. These results indicate that the conserved amino acid residues are crucial for the enzymatic activities of the P4-ATPases.Keywords: Lipid bilayer, P-type ATPase, flippase

Introduction

In eukaryotic cells, the lipid bilayer of biological membranes exhibits asymmetric lipid distributions. Phosphatidylserine (PS), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and phosphatidylinositol are found mostly in the cytoplasmic leaflet, while phosphatidylcholine (PC), sphingomyelin, and glycolipids are enriched in the exoplasmic/luminal leaflet.1–3 Members of the P4-ATPase family translocate membrane lipids from the exoplasmic to the cytoplasmic leaflet in an ATP-dependent manner and regulate the transbilayer lipid asymmetry.4,5 The lipid asymmetry is critical for the homeostasis of cells and organisms, and disruption of the asymmetry is related to many pathophysiological conditions.6,7 Mutations in the P4-ATPases that are responsible for genetic diseases have been identified. Progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis (PFIC) 1 result from defects in ATP8B1,8 while cerebellar ataxia, mental retardation, and disequilibrium syndrome (CAMRQ) 4 results from defects in ATP8A2.9 ATP10A is related to type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance, and diet-induced obesity, and ATP10D is related to plasma glucosylceramide accumulation, cardiovascular diseases, and atherosclerosis.10–12 Defects in ATP11C cause congenital hemolytic anemia.13 A de novo heterozygous variation of ATP11A induces a gain-of-function phenotype that causes developmental delay and neurological deterioration.14

We previously demonstrated that ATP8B1, ATP8B2, and ATP10A translocate PC, ATP10D translocates glucosylceramide, and ATP11A and ATP11C translocate PS and PE at the plasma membrane.15–18 Moreover, we revealed that a defect in the PC flippase activity of ATP8B1 is responsible for the phenotypes of PFIC1 patients. We also demonstrated that enhanced PC-flipping activity at the plasma membrane drives inward membrane curvature and inhibits cell adhesion and spreading.15,19,20

ATP8B2, which is included in the P4A clade, localizes mainly to the plasma membrane and interacts with the CDC50 proteins (CDC50A and CDC50B), which are β-subunits of most P4-ATPases.21–23 ATP8B2 seems to be expressed ubiquitously and highly expressed in the brain, uterus and aorta,24,25 but its physiological function is currently unknown.

In the present study, we demonstrated that ATP8B2 requires its interaction with CDC50A, but not with CDC50B, to exit the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and to transport it to the plasma membrane. In addition, we report three patients with intellectual disability who carried a de novo heterozygous missense variant of ATP8B2. The affected amino acid residues were found to be conserved in all P4-ATPases. Notably, two of these variations decreased the PC flippase activity of ATP8B2. Similarly, the corresponding mutations in ATP8B1 and ATP11C decreased their PC and PS flippase activities, respectively.

Results

ATP8B2 is transported to the plasma membrane from the ER in complex with CDC50A, but not with CDC50B

To understand the biochemical characteristics and physiological function of ATP8B2, we first sought to resolve the conflicting findings regarding the requirement of CDC50B for ATP8B2 to localize to the plasma membrane. We and others previously demonstrated that ATP8B2 can interact with both CDC50A and CDC50B.21–23 Our study showed that only CDC50A is required for ATP8B2 to exit the ER and localize to the plasma membrane.21 Consistently, another study reported that exogenous expression of CDC50B prevents CDC50A-dependent localization of ATP8B2 to the plasma membrane.22 However, the other study reported that both CDC50A and CDC50B promote transport of ATP8B2 to the plasma membrane although CDC50B is less efficient for ATP8B2 transport.23

To revisit the interaction of the CDC50 proteins with ATP8B2 and the requirement of the interaction for the ATP8B2 localization, we co-expressed C-terminally HA-tagged ATP8B2 with either of the CDC50 proteins with an N-terminal FLAG tag in HeLa cells (Figure 1A). As controls, we co-expressed ATP8B1-HA or ATP11A-HA with FLAG-CDC50s. The lysates prepared from the co-expressing cells were subjected to immunoprecipitation using an anti-HA antibody, followed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting analysis with an anti-DYKDDDDK antibody recognizing the FLAG epitope or anti-HA antibody. As shown in Figure 1A, ATP8B2-HA co-immunoprecipitated both FLAG-tagged CDC50A and CDC50B, with markedly higher levels of CDC50B than CDC50A (Figure 1A, compare lanes 9 and 13). Furthermore, the interaction of ATP8B2 with CDC50A appeared to be much weaker than that of ATP8B1/ATP11A with CDC50A (Figure 1A, lanes 9–11). CDC50A was co-immunoprecipitated with ATP8B2 migrated as smeared bands with relatively weak intensity suggestive of heterogenous glycosylation (Figure 1A, lane 9; also see Figure 1C, lane 20), while CDC50B was co-immunoprecipitated with ATP8B2 as an intense single band of around 50 kDa (Figure 1A, lane 13; also see Figure 1E, lane 26). ATP8B1 co-immunoprecipitated both CDC50A and CDC50B (Figure 1A, lanes 10 and 14), while only CDC50A was co-immunoprecipitated with ATP11A (Figure 1A, lanes 11 and 15) as previously reported21–23 and appeared as smeared bands (Figure 1A, lanes 10, 11, and 14, indicated by brackets). The appearance of two bands in ATP8B2 lanes remain elusive (Figure 1A, lanes 1, 5, 9, and 13). This might arise from potential glycan modifications or tight association with unidentified factors.

Next, we examined which CDC50 protein(s) is required for the plasma membrane localization of ATP8B2 in cells transiently expressing ATP8B2-HA in combination with FLAG-tagged CDC50A or CDC50B. When expressed alone, the majority of ATP8B2 colocalized with the ER marker, protein disulfide isomerase (PDI) (Figure 2A, a–a′′′′) but not with the plasma membrane protein, ATP1A1 (Figure 2B, a–a′′′′).22,23 In the presence of co-expressed CDC50A, ATP8B2 localized to the plasma membrane and colocalized with ATP1A1 (Figure 2B, b–b′′′′). By contrast, in the presence of co-expressed CDC50B, ATP8B2 localized to the ER instead of the plasma membrane (Figure 2A and B, c–c′′′′), despite its stronger interaction with CDC50B than with CDC50A (Figure 1A, lanes 9 and 13). We calculated the proportions of cells in which the P4-ATPases (ATPB2, ATP8B1, and ATP11A) localized to the plasma membrane plus the ER and the ER only (Figure 2C, bars 1–9). The P4-ATPases localized to the plasma membrane without co-expression of the CDC50 protein in some cells due to the presence of the low level of endogenous CDC50 (Figure 2C, bars 1, 4, and 7). The proportion of cells in which ATP8B2 localized to the plasma membrane greatly increased in the presence of co-expressed CDC50A and decreased in the presence of co-expressed CDC50B, compared with cells expressing ATP8B2 alone (Figure 2C, bars 1–3); overexpression of CDC50B might hamper the interaction of ATP8B2 with endogenous CDC50A. Consistent with previous reports, the proportion of cells in which ATP8B1 localized to the plasma membrane dramatically increased in the presence of co-expressed CDC50A or CDC50B (Figure 2C, bars 4–6), while the proportion of cells in which ATP11A localized to the plasma membrane dramatically increased only in the presence of co-expressed CDC50A (Figure 2C, bars 7–9).21–23

We then investigated whether CDC50A is truly necessary for the transport of ATP8B2 from the ER to the plasma membrane. We used CDC50A-knockout (KO) cells established from HeLa cells using the CRISPR/Cas9 system as described previously.26 We transfected CDC50A-KO cells with expression vectors for HA-tagged P4-ATPases alone or together with an expression vector for FLAG-tagged CDC50A or CDC50B and observed the cellular localization of the P4-ATPases (Figure 2D to F). When ATP8B2, ATP8B1, or ATP11A was expressed alone in CDC50A-KO cells, they hardly localized to the plasma membrane (Figure 2D to F, a–a′′′′ and Figure 2C, bars 10, 13, and 16). By contrast, the P4-ATPases were able to localize to the plasma membrane in some parental control cells (Figure 2C, bars 1, 4, and 7), suggesting that endogenous CDC50A allowed the P4-ATPases to localize to the plasma membrane. Given that ATP8B1 fails to localize to the plasma membrane in CDC50A-KO cells (Figure 2E, a–a′′′′ and Figure 2C, bar 13), HeLa cells express virtually no CDC50B.23 ATP8B2 as well as ATP11A localized to the plasma membrane in the presence of co-expressed CDC50A, but not in the presence of co-expressed CDC50B, in CDC50A-KO cells (Figure 2D and F, b–c′′′′, and Figure 2C, bars 10–12 and 16–18). Conversely, ATP8B1 localized to the plasma membrane in the presence of co-expressed CDC50A or CDC50B in CDC50A-KO cells (Figure 2E, b–c′′′′, and Figure 2C, bars 13–15). These results demonstrate that exit of ATP8B2 from the ER and its transport to the plasma membrane require its interaction with CDC50A, although ATP8B2 can interact with both CDC50A and CDC50B. We also confirmed that N-terminally HA-tagged ATP8B2 (HA-ATP8B2) exhibits similar behavior to that of C-terminally HA-tagged ATP8B2; although it interacts with both CDC50A and CDC50B, only CDC50A is required for ATP8B2 localization to the plasma membrane in control and CDC50A-KO cells (Supplementary Figure S1).

CDC50A co-immunoprecipitated with ATP8B2 is endoglycosidase H (endo H)-resistant, whereas CDC50B co-immunoprecipitated with ATP8B2 is endo H-sensitive

CDC50A undergoes N-glycosylation as indicated by the smeared bands, which indicate complex glycosylation occurring in the Golgi complex (Figure 1A, lanes 10 and 11).21,22,27 CDC50B has evolutionarily conserved N-glycosylation sites and is purified as an N-glycosylated form.28 However, its glycosylation has not been confirmed by biochemical analysis. We examined the sensitivity to endoglycosidase H (endo H) of CDC50A and CDC50B co-immunoprecipitated with P4-ATPases to assess their transport from the ER to the Golgi complex (Figure 1B to F and Supplementary Figure S2). High-mannose-type N-linked oligosaccharide chains are hydrolyzed by endo H, while complex-type oligosaccharides are resistant to this enzyme. Therefore, N-linked oligosaccharide chains of glycoproteins present in the ER are cleaved by endo H, while those that have reached the medial-Golgi compartments are resistant to endo H. The input and immunoprecipitated data for each P4-ATPase are shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

The smeared bands of CDC50A co-immunoprecipitated with ATP11A were endo H-resistant (Figure 1B, lane 18, bracket), but appeared as a core form (∼40 kDa) upon treatment with peptide: N-glycosidase F (PNGase F), which hydrolyzes all N-linked oligosaccharides (Figure 1B, lane 19, arrow), indicating that CDC50A bound to ATP11A has complex-type N-oligosaccharides as reported previously.21 Upon endo H treatment, a faint band of ∼40 kDa of CDC50A appeared in the co-immunoprecipitate (Figure 1B, lane 18, arrow), suggesting the presence of a limited amount of an ER-resident form. These results support that the majority of the ATP11A–CDC50A complex is transported to the plasma membrane via the Golgi complex.

The oligosaccharide chains of the CDC50 proteins complexed with ATP8B1 or ATP8B2 underwent inefficient cleavage by the glycosidases under nondenaturing conditions but were efficiently hydrolyzed under denaturing conditions (Figure 1C to F), while CDC50A co-immunoprecipitated with ATP11A was efficiently cleaved upon glycosidase treatment under nondenaturing conditions (Figure 1B). The smeared bands of CDC50A co-immunoprecipitated with ATP8B2 were resistant to endo H (Figure 1C, lane 21, bracket) but sensitive to PNGase F (Figure 1C, lane 22, arrow). However, a band of ∼50 kDa (Figure 1C, lane 20, asterisk) disappeared and a new band of ∼40 kDa appeared upon endo H treatment (Figure 1C, lane 21, arrow). These results indicate that the ATP8B2–CDC50A complex is transported to the plasma membrane via the Golgi complex, although some proportion is retained in the ER.

A single band of CDC50B co-immunoprecipitated with ATP8B2 (Figure 1E, lane 26, asterisk) was sensitive to both endo H and PNGase F, and a band of ∼40 kDa appeared upon either treatment (Figure 1E, lanes 27 and 28, arrow). These results indicate that CDC50B bound to ATP8B2 is primarily retained in the ER and are consistent with the finding that the localization of ATP8B2 to the plasma membrane requires only CDC50A, as shown in Figure 2B and C and previous reports.21,22

The smeared bands of CDC50A co-immunoprecipitated with ATP8B1 were resistant to endo H (Figure 1D, lane 24, bracket) but cleaved by PNGase F (Figure 1D, lane 25, arrow), indicating that it has complex-type N-oligosaccharides. These results indicate that the ATP8B1–CDC50A complex is transported to the plasma membrane via the Golgi complex.

Smeared bands of CDC50B co-immunoprecipitated with ATP8B1 were visible under nondenaturing conditions (Figure 1A, lane 14, bracket), although they were hardly detectable under denaturing conditions (Figure 1F, lane 29, bracket). However, an ∼40 kDa core protein band was detected upon endo H treatment under denaturing conditions (Figure 1F, lane 30, arrow), likely due to a minor proportion of the ER-resident form of CDC50B in complex with ATP8B1. By contrast, an intense single band of ∼40 kDa was detected upon PNGase F treatment (Figure 1F, lane 31, arrow). These results indicate that CDC50B in complex with ATP8B1 undergoes complex-type N-glycosylation and are consistent with the finding that localization of ATP8B1 to the plasma membrane requires CDC50A or CDC50B (Figure 2C and E).

Amino acid residues affected by the identified de novo variations in ATP8B2 are conserved in all P4-ATPases

Although the physiological function of ATP8B2 remains unknown, it appears to be widely expressed in the human brain (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ENSG00000143515-ATP8B2/brain). Moreover, proteomic analysis revealed that Atp8b2 was primarily detected in the mouse brain24 and mRNA expression of human ATP8B2 is rather ubiquitous but high in the brain.25 To further investigate this, we analyzed Atp8b2 expression in various mouse tissues by quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR). Its expression was found to be particularly high in the brain, although it was widely expressed in the other tissues examined (Figure 3A). These data suggest that ATP8B2 has a distinct role in brain function.

In a cohort of 3,871 affected individuals, a patient with intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorder was identified to have a de novo heterozygous missense variation (Asn817Ser) in ATP8B2 (Figure 3B).29 Through a collaborative international effort facilitated by GeneMatcher, 30 we identified two additional de novo heterozygous missense variations in ATP8B2 (Arg549Gln and Gly759Ser) from other patients with intellectual disability (Figure 3B). Notably, the Gly759 and Asn817 residues in ATP8B2 are conserved in all members of the P4-ATPase family, and the Arg549 residue is also conserved in this family, although the corresponding residue is replaced with Lys in some members of the family (Figure 4A and B). In the 3D structures of ATP8B1 and ATP11C, the corresponding Gly803 and Asn896 residues in ATP8B1, and Gly753 and Asn819 residues in ATP11C are in close proximity to the DKTGT and DGET/S motifs (Figures 4C, 6Fa, and 7Fa).28,31 The Asp residue of the DKTGT motif in the phosphorylation domain (P-domain) is transiently phosphorylated during the ATPase catalytic cycle, and the Glu residue of the DGET motif in the actuator domain (A-domain) is critical for dephosphorylation of the phosphoaspartate residue (Figure 4C). The corresponding Arg (Lys) residue is found in the nucleotide-binding domain (N-domain), which is positioned apart from the catalytic center (Figure 4C).

The ATP8B2 variants retain the ability to interact with CDC50A and localize to the plasma membrane

We first examined whether the ATP8B2 variations affect its interaction with CDC50A by transiently co-expressing any of the C-terminally HA-tagged ATP8B2 variants and FLAG-CDC50A in HeLa cells. The lysates prepared from the cells were then subjected to immunoprecipitation using an anti-HA antibody (Figure 5A). All the ATP8B2 variants co-immunoprecipitated CDC50A as well as CDC50B at levels comparable to ATP8B2(WT) (Figure 5A, lanes 13–24 in lower panels).

We then established HeLa cells stably expressing individual ATP8B2 variants to examine whether they are transported to the plasma membrane. All the ATP8B2 variants colocalized with the plasma membrane marker ATP1A1 in immunofluorescence analysis (Figure 5B). In addition, surface biotinylation analysis confirmed that these variants reach the plasma membrane when stably expressed in HeLa cells (Figure 5C, lanes 31–36 in the upper panel). Therefore, none of these variations affected the interaction of ATP8B2 with CDC50A or its transport to the plasma membrane.

Some of the missense variations in ATP8B2 abolish its PC flippase activity

We next investigated whether the variations affect the flippase activity of ATP8B2. Our previous study showed that ATP8B2 specifically translocates NBD-PC.18 To assess the flippase activity, we performed experiments with NBD-PC, NBD-PS, and NBD-SM in cells stably expressing ATP8B2 variants (Figure 5D to F). As previously reported, ATP8B2(WT) specifically flipped NBD-PC but not NBD-SM.18 In addition, the ATPase-deficient mutant ATP8B2(E171Q) did not exhibit PC-flipping activity (Figure 5D and Supplementary Figure S3A). The NBD-PS flipping activity was rather decreased in cells expressing either ATP8B2 or ATP8B2(E171Q) mutant suggesting that the decrease is not due to the enzymatic activity of ATP8B2 (Figure 5E). Importantly, the PC-flipping activity was almost canceled in cells expressing the G759S or N817S variants compared to cells expressing ATP8B2 (WT), whereas the specific PC-flipping activity was not significantly changed in cells expressing the R549Q variant (Figure 5D and Supplementary Figure S3A and B). Although the PC-flipping activity of cells expressing R549Q variant was significantly higher than in control cells, it was slightly lower than in cells expressing the WT at later time points (Supplementary Figure S3A).

The conserved residues are critical for the flippase activities of other P4-ATPases

Given the conservation of the mutated residues across the P4-ATPase family (Figure 4A), we hypothesized that these residues are important for the flippase activities of other members of this family. ATP8B1 is closely related to ATP8B2 and both specifically flip PC. To explore the hypothesis, we substituted the corresponding Gly803 and Asn896 residues with Ser and substituted the Arg608 residue with Gln in ATP8B1 (Figures 4 and 6, Supplementary Figure S4A), and investigated whether these mutations affected its interactions with the CDC50 proteins. C-terminally HA-tagged ATP8B1 mutants and N-terminally FLAG-tagged CDC50A or CDC50B were transiently co-expressed in HeLa cells, and the lysates prepared from the cells were subjected to immunoprecipitation using an anti-HA antibody followed by immunoblotting analysis using anti-DYKDDDDK or anti-HA antibody (Supplementary Figure S4). The ATP8B1(R608Q) and ATP8B1(N896S) mutants co-immunoprecipitated both CDC50A and CDC50B (Supplementary Figure S4, lanes 15, 17, 21, and 23), although expression of the ATP8B1(G803S) mutant was not detected (Supplementary Figure S4A and B, lanes 4, 10, 16, and 22 in upper panels). The G803S mutation may reduce the protein stability (see discussion). Moreover, expression of the ATP8B1(N896S) mutant appeared to a lesser degree than that of ATP8B1(R608Q) in the presence of either CDC50A or CDC50B (Supplementary Figure S4A and B, lanes 5, 11, 17, and 23).

We then established HeLa cells stably expressing each ATP8B1 mutant to assess their localization to the plasma membrane. Immunofluorescence and surface biotinylation analyses revealed that, like ATP8B1(WT), both ATP8B1(R608Q) and ATP8B1(N896S) mutants were present at the plasma membrane although ATP8B1(N896S) appeared to a lesser degree (Figure 6A and B, lanes 6–10 in the upper panel). ATP8B1(WT) specifically flipped NBD-PC but not NBD-PS and NBD-SM, and its ATPase-deficient E234Q mutant did not flip NBD-PC, as previously described (Figure 6C to E).18 The PC flippase activity of ATP8B1 was significantly decreased by the N896S mutation but was unaffected by the R608Q mutation (Figure 6C). These results are consistent with those of the corresponding ATP8B2 mutants shown in Figure 5. However, we cannot exclude the possibility of a lesser expression of ATP8B1(N896S) mutant on the cell surface (Figure 6B, lane 9).

Upon analyzing the 3D structure of ATP8B1, we discovered that Asn896 forms a hydrogen bond with the phosphorylated Asp454 residue in the DKTGT motif, which is critical for the ATPase activity (Figure 6Fa). Therefore, the N896S mutation is predicted to destabilize the phosphorylated intermediate and inhibit the flippase activity. Given that the expression level of the N896S mutant was lower than WT, the mutation may also affect the stability of ATP8B1. Arg608 is located in the N-domain and is not expected to affect the ATPase activity (Figure 4B).

Since some corresponding mutations affected protein stability of ATP8B1, we subsequently substituted the corresponding Gly756 and Asn822 residues with Ser and substituted the Lys555 residue with Gln in ATP11C (Figures 4 and 7, Supplementary Figure S4C). To examine whether these mutations affect its interactions with CDC50A, HeLa cells transiently co-expressing any of C-terminally HA-tagged ATP11C mutants and FLAG-CDC50A were subjected to immunoprecipitation using an anti-HA antibody followed by immunoblotting analysis using anti-DYKDDDDK or anti-HA antibody (Supplementary Figure S4C). All the ATP11C mutants co-immunoprecipitated CDC50A (Supplementary Figure S4C, lanes 31–36 in the lower panel), suggesting that the interaction with CDC50A was unaffected by the mutations, similar to ATP8B2. We then established HeLa cells stably expressing individual ATP11C mutants to examine whether they are transported to the plasma membrane. Immunofluorescence and surface biotinylation analyses confirmed that all the ATP11C mutants localized to the plasma membrane (Figure 7A and B, lanes 7–12 in the upper panel). ATP11C(WT) specifically flipped NBD-PS but not NBD-PC and NBD-SM, and its ATPase-deficient mutant E184Q did not flip NBD-PS, as described previously (Figure 7C to E).18 The PS flippase activity was significantly reduced by the N822S or G756S mutation as compared with WT. The K555Q mutation did not impact flipping of PS (Figure 7C). These results are consistent with those of the PC flippase activities of the corresponding ATP8B2 (Figure 5) mutants.

Similar results have been obtained by analysis of the 3D structure of ATP11C. Asn819 in ATP11C (which corresponds to Asn822 in the N-terminal splice variant used in this study) forms a hydrogen bond with BeF bound to the Asp409 residue (Figure 7Fa), as seen in ATP8B1 (Figure 6Fa), indicating that the residue is vital for stabilizing the phosphorylated intermediate. In addition, the Asp409 residue is located on a β strand (Figure 7Fb) and Gly753 (Gly756) is positioned at the boundary between the β-sheet (β1-β4 strands) and α-helix structures. The flexibility of Gly753 (Gly756) enables the nearly vertical directional change from the β-sheet to the α-helix, but the G756S mutation stiffens the boundary, which may disturb the β-sheet.

Discussion

The human ATP8B subfamily (ATP8B1–ATP8B4) proteins are closely related among P4-ATPase family members.5 All ATP8B proteins require CDC50 proteins as β-subunits for trafficking to the plasma membrane from the ER. Both ATP8B1 and ATP8B4 interact with CDC50A or CDC50B, and either interaction is required for their plasma membrane localization.21–23 The β-subunit of ATP8B3 has not been identified. While ATP8B2 interacts with both CDC50A and CDC50B, only CDC50A promotes its plasma membrane localization (Figures 1–3). The affinity between ATP8B2 and CDC50A appears to be lower than that between ATP8B1/ATP11A and CDC50A (Figure 1B). The ATP8B2–CDC50B complex mainly resides in the ER (Figures 1–3), and it is unknown whether it plays a role in the ER. However, ATP8B2–CDC50A exhibits PC-flipping activity at the plasma membrane.

P4-ATPases are conserved in all eukaryotes and classified into three groups, P4A, P4B, and P4C clade.5 Saccharomyces cerevisiae contains five members (four P4A and one P4B clade) and Arabidopsis thaliana contains 12 members, all belonging to the P4A clade, except for ALA2 (P4C).5 Humans contain 14 members, including 12 P4A and two P4B ATPases clade. All P4A clade members interact with CDC50 family proteins but P4B ATPases do not. P4A ATPases require the interaction with CDC50 to target to the appropriate cellular destinations, while the role of CDC50 in enzymatic activities of P4-ATPases is unknown. In S. cerevisiae, the N-terminal cytoplasmic region of Lem3p (a CDC50 family protein) extends to the actuator domain of Dnf1p (a P4A ATPase) and is likely to contribute to substrate binding.32

Members of the ATP8B subfamily display variable affinities and specificities toward CDC50 proteins and play diverse roles in vivo. ATP8B1 is expressed in all tissues, and its mutations have been linked to PFIC1, with over 50 mutations reported.8 Several ATP8B1 mutations decrease its stability and expression and perturb its localization, while other mutations affect its PC flippase activity.18,33–35 Atp8b3 is expressed in germ cells in the testes and plays a role in sperm capacitation in mice.36,37 Atp8b5 is specifically expressed in testes and is involved in spermatogenesis in mice, but is not found in the human genome.38 ATP8B4 has been hypothesized to be associated with the development of Alzheimer’s disease and systemic sclerosis.39–41 ATP8B2 is highly expressed in the brain (Figure 3A),24 but its physiological function is unknown.

The identification of impaired PC flippase activity associated with two (Asn817Ser and Gly759Ser) of the three de novo monoallelic missense variations in patients with intellectual disability and autism spectrum disorder suggests that ATP8B2 may be implicated in brain development and function. ATP8B2 is associated with plasmalogen homeostasis,42 which is abundant in brain tissue. A de novo heterozygous variation of ATP11A results in a gain-of-function phenotype that causes neurological deterioration.14 The ATP11A variant exhibits flippase activity towards PC, in addition to its inherent substrate PS. However, given that variations of Asn817 and Gly759 in ATP8B2 impair PC flippase activity but do not acquire PS flippase activity (Figure 5E), suggesting that a gain-of-function effect is unlikely. The likelihood of ATP8B2 haploinsufficiency contributing to pathogenicity is low, as indicated by the probability of loss-of-function intolerance (pLI) score close to 043 implying that loss-of-function of ATP8B2 gene may be tolerated. By contrast, the missense Z-score of ATP8B2 is 2.87,43 suggesting a relatively high intolerance to missense variations. The presence of a discrepancy between the missense Z-score and pLI score in combination with the clustering of the two variants suggests that these variations may have a dominant negative effect.44

It is noteworthy that the amino acid residues of ATP8B2 mutated in the patients are highly conserved in the P4-ATPase family, with the Asn and Gly residues playing important roles in the enzymatic activity. When analyzing the 3D structures of ATP8B1 and ATP11C, we found that the Asn residue forms a hydrogen bond with one phosphate from the aspartyl phosphate (Figures 6Fa and 7Fa). This finding suggests that the Asn residue plays a role in stabilizing the phosphorylated intermediate and/or contributing to access to the DGET/S motif for subsequent dephosphorylation. P4-ATPases have four major conformations: E1, E2, and their phosphoenzyme intermediates E1P and E2P (Supplementary Figure S5). The E2P conformation appears to be important for exit from the ER and transport to cellular compartments.45 Since the Asn mutations in the three P4-ATPases do not affect their transport to the plasma membrane, they may form an E2P-like conformation. The flexible Gly residue is positioned at the boundary between the β-sheet and subsequent α-helix, enabling directional changes in the α-helix and its crucial role in stabilizing the β-sheet structures where the Asp residue is situated (Figures 6Fb and 7Fb). Therefore, the conserved Asn and Gly residues in other P4-ATPases, such as ATP8B1 and ATP11C, may have similar functions. The Arg608 residue in ATP8B1 and the Lys552 residue in ATP11C are both located in the N-domain and exposed to the outside of the molecules; therefore, they are not predicted to contribute to the enzymatic activities. Indeed, mutations of these residues did not significantly affect the PC or PS flippase activity (Figures 6 and 7). Based on the primary sequence similarity, Arg549 in ATP8B2, like Arg608 in ATP8B1 and Lys552 in ATP11C, is expected to be located in the N-domain and exposed to the outside of the molecule. Thus, the R549Q variation of ATP8B2 is not expected to influence the enzymatic activity, consistent with the results that PC-flipping activity in cells expressing ATP8B2(R549Q) was comparable with that in cells expressing ATP8B2(WT) (Figure 5D), although it was slightly lower at a later time point (Supplementary Figure S3A).

Although expression of the G803S mutant of ATP8B1 was not detected by immunoblot analysis (Supplementary Figure S4), expression of the corresponding Gly mutants of ATP11C and ATP8B2 was detected, suggesting that the ATP8B1(G803S) mutant is much less stable than the latter two mutants. The autophosphorylated Asp454 residue is located on a β strand and Gly803 is positioned at the boundary between the β-sheet (β1-β4 strands) and α-helix (Figure 6Fb), similar to ATP11C. There is an insertion of 33 amino acid residues after the α-helix following the Gly803 residue in ATP8B1, but not in ATP11C or ATP8B2 (Figure 4A and Supplementary Figure S6). Destabilization of the β-sheet and α-helix structures by the Gly mutation, followed by disruption of the extension, could disrupt the entire structure of ATP8B1. In addition, the N-terminal auto-inhibitory loop of ATP8B1 is positioned adjacent to the Gly803 residue (Supplementary Figure S6).28,46 Destabilization of the β-sheet and α-helix structures by the Gly mutation may lead to dissociation of the auto-inhibitory loop and subsequent disruption of the entire structure of ATP8B1.

Our findings provide insight into the putative role of ATP8B2 in the brain, although more patient case analyses are needed to support its involvement in an autosomal dominant form of intellectual disability. In addition, further investigations are warranted to elucidate the relationship between the flippase activity of ATP8B2 and brain function. The identification of ATP8B2 variations emphasizes the importance of the Asn residue in stabilizing the phosphorylated Asp intermediate, as well as the role of the flexible Gly residue located between the β-sheet and α-helix structures, the former where the Asp residue is located. These structural insights could improve understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the enzymatic activities of the P4-ATPases.

Materials and Methods

Antibodies and reagents

Sources of antibodies used in the present study were as follows: monoclonal rat anti-HA (3F10), Roche Diagnostics; monoclonal mouse anti-DYKDDDDK (1E6), Wako Pure Chemical Industries; monoclonal mouse anti-PDI (1D3), Enzo Life Sciences; monoclonal rabbit anti-ATP1A1 (EP1845Y), Abcam; AlexaFluor-conjugated secondary antibodies, molecular probes; and Cy3- and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories. PNGase F and endo H were purchased from New England Biolabs and Roche Diagnostics, respectively. The NBD-labeled phospholipids NBD-PS (1-oleyl-2-[6-[(7-nitro-2-1,3-benzoxadiazol-4yl)amino]hexanoyl]-sn-glycero-3-phosphoserine), NBD-PC (1-oleyl-2-[6-[(7-nitro-2-1,3benzoxadiazol-4-yl)amino]hexanoyl]-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine), NBD-SM (N-[6-[(7-nitro-2,3-benzoxadiazole-4-yl)amino]hexanoyl]sphingosine 1-phsphocholine) were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids.

Plasmids

Expression vectors encoding C-terminally HA-tagged P4-ATPases and N-terminally FLAG-His6-tagged human CDC50A and CDC50B were constructed as previously described.18,21 Partial cDNA fragments of ATP8B2 were obtained from Kazusa (ORK04226), and the 5′ fragments of ATP8B2 were obtained from total RNA of HeLa cells by RT-PCR using the following primer pair: sense, 5′-GGCGTCGACACCATGGCAGTGTGTGCAAAAAAGCGC-3′, and antisense, 5′-CCAGAGGATTGAAGGAGAAGTC-3′. The complete cDNA of human ATP8B2 (NM_001370597) was inserted into pENTR3C (Invitrogen) and then transferred to expression vectors using the Gateway system (Invitrogen). To construct P4-ATPase mutants, each mutation was introduced into ATP8B2, ATP8B1, and ATP11C cDNAs via site-directed mutagenesis using a QuikChange II XL Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies) and/or the SLiCE cloning method.47 The mutations were confirmed by sequencing analysis. The pMXs-neo vectors and the pEF-gag-pol plasmid were kind gifts from Toshio Kitamura (The University of Tokyo) and the pCMV-VSVG-RSV-Rev plasmid (RDB04393) was kind gift from Hiroyuki Miyoshi (RIKEN BioResource Center). Some plasmids for expression of CDC50s and P4-ATPases used in this study have been deposited in Addgene (209223, 209224, 209209, 209210, 209218, 209226, 209227, 209253, 209254, 209237, 209263). For N-terminally HA-tagged ATP8B2 construct, partial cDNA fragment of ATP8B2 was amplified by PCR using the following primer pair: sense 5′-GGCCTCGAGCCATGGCAGTGTGTGCAAAAAAGCGC-3′ and antisense 5′-GAACCAGGACAGGGAAGAGATC-3′. The PCR products were then digested with XhoI and BglII. The digested PCR products and BglII/EcoRI-digested ATP8B2 in the pENTR3C vector were inserted into pCAG-HA digested by XhoI and EcoRI.

Cell culture, transfection, and establishment of stable cells

HeLa cells were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM, Nacalai Tesque), supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). HeLa cells stably expressing C-terminally HA-tagged P4-ATPases using retroviral system which were described previously.18,21 For retroviral production, pMXs-neo-derived vectors for expression of HA-tagged P4-ATPases were cotransfected with pEF-gag-pol and pCMV-VSVG-RSV-Rev into HEK293T cells. The resultant retroviruses were concentrated and used to infect HeLa cells to establish stable cells. The infected cells were selected in medium containing G418 (1 mg/mL). A mixed population of drug-resistant cells was used for further studies.

The CDC50A gene was disrupted in HeLa cells using the CRISPR/Cas9 system which was described previously.26 Complementary oligonucleotides (for CDC50A, gRNA-1, 5′-caccgactcggagaccggataaca-3′ and 5′-aaactgttatccggtctccgagtc-3′; target sequences are underlined) were synthesized and introduced into the PX459 (Addgene plasmid #48139). To edit the CDC50A gene, we used a previously described method,48 which involved the cotransfection of the plasmid containing the CDC50A target sequences and the Cas9 gene with a donor plasmid (Addgene plasmid #80766) referred to as Donor. Both plasmids were transfected into HeLa cells using the X-tremeGENE9 DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche Diagnostics) and selected in medium containing G418 (1 mg/mL). Cell clones were isolated using the SH800S cell sorter. Genomic DNA was extracted and PCR was performed to amplify the region of interest. The KO was confirmed by direct sequencing of the amplified PCR product.

To transiently express P4-ATPases, HeLa cells were transfected with a pCAG-HA-based vector carrying human P4-ATPase cDNA and a pcDNA3-FLAG-His6-based vector carrying human CDC50A or CDC50B cDNA using X-tremeGENE9 or polyethyleneimine (Polysciences, Inc.) 21. Two days later, the transfected cells were fixed for immunofluorescence analysis. HeLa cells stably expressing each C-terminally HA-tagged P4-ATPase or mutant were established using retroviral infection as described previously.18 Immunostaining was performed as previously described49 and visualized using an Axiovert 200MAT microscope (Carl Zeiss). Some images were captured with the microscope equipped with Apotome 3 (Carl Zeiss).

Immunoprecipitation

HeLa cells were transiently transfected using polyethyleneimine (Polysciences, Inc.) with different combinations of expression vectors encoding C-terminally HA-tagged P4-ATPases and N-terminally FLAG-tagged CDC50 proteins, and grown for 2 days. The cells were then lysed in lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 1% Nonidet P-40) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Nacalai Tesque) at 4 °C for 30 min. The lysates were centrifuged at maximum speed for 20 min at 4 °C in a microcentrifuge to remove cellular debris and insoluble materials. The supernatant was incubated at 4 °C for 15 min with an anti-HA antibody and further incubated at 4 °C for 16 h with protein G-coupled Dynabeads (Invitrogen). After washing, beads were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h in SDS sample buffer, and the supernatant was subjected to SDS-PAGE. Immunoblot analysis was performed using rat anti-HA and mouse anti-DYKDDDDK antibodies. Immunoblots were developed using ImmunoStar (Wako) and recorded on an ImageQuant 800 instrument (Amersham).

Glycosidase treatment

Immunoprecipitation was performed as described above. In the nondenaturing condition, the immunoprecipitates were treated with endo H (Roche Diagnostics) or PNGase F (Roche Diagnostics) in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 5.8, at 37 °C for 3 h. In the denaturing condition, the immunoprecipitates were incubated in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 5.8, containing 10 mM EDTA, 0.1% SDS, and 1% β-mercaptoethanol at 45 °C for 30 min or at 75 °C for 10 min. After incubation, Nonidet P-40 (final 1%) was added before the samples were treated with endo H and PNGase F.

Exome sequencing

In the study of the patient with the Gly759Ser mutation in ATP8B2, genomic DNA of the proband and his parents was extracted from whole blood samples at the University Hospital of Angers. Library preparation was performed by IntegraGen SA. Target region capture was performed with a SureSelect Kit (SureSelect XT Clinical Research Exome, Agilent). Paired-end 75 base massively parallel sequencing was performed on a HiSeq 4000 instrument (Illumina) according to previous work.50 This study was performed in accordance with the ethical principles for medical research outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Consent for publication of clinical data including genetic analyses was obtained from the family. The exome sequencing study of the patient with the Arg549Gln mutation was approved by the ethics committee of the University of Leipzig (approval code: 402/16-ek). Library preparation was performed using Nextera DNA Flex Pre-Enrichment LibraryPrep with Illumina Nextera DNA UD Indexes (Illumina) while strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions and standard operating procedures. Target enrichment was achieved using Human Core Exome hybridization probes from Twist Bioscience. Paired-end next-generation sequencing was then performed on a NovaSeq 6000 instrument using an S1 Reagent Kit (300 cycles, Illumina). Coverage of 10× was achieved in 98.7% (index), 96.9% (mother), and 98.7% (father) of the targeted sequence. The raw data were analyzed using Varfeed software (Limbus), and the variants were annotated and prioritized using Varvis software (Limbus). The generated sequences were aligned and compared with the reference human genome (GRCh38/hg38). Variants were prioritized based on their inheritance pattern, frequency in gnomAD, and in silico prediction. The consequences of the ATP8B2 variants were interpreted based on isoform NM_001370597.

RT-PCR and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells and mouse (C57BL/6N) tissues using Isogen (TOYOBO) and subjected to RT-PCR analysis using a SuperScript III One-Step RT-PCR System (Invitrogen). For RT-qPCR, total RNA was subjected to reverse transcription using ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (TOYOBO). The resultant cDNA was used as a template for PCR using the THUNDERBIRD SYBR qPCR Mix (TOYOBO). Fragments of mouse ATP8B2 were amplified using the following primer pair: forward, 5′-CCCAAACCAGTTCCGGTTTGTG-3′, and reverse, 5′-GGCTTCTCAGCTCCGCCACTG-3′. Fragments of mouse β-actin were amplified using the following primer pair: forward, 5′-GCACCACACCTTCTACAATGAG-3′, and reverse, 5′-CGTAGATGGGCACAGTGTGG-3′.

Flippase assay

NBD-phospholipid incorporation was analyzed using flow cytometry as previously described.18 In brief, HeLa cells were detached from dishes in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 5 mM EDTA and then harvested by centrifugation. Cells (1 × 106 per sample) were washed and equilibrated at 15 °C for 15 min in 100 µL of Hank’s balanced salt solution (pH 7.4) containing 1 g/L glucose (HBSS-glucose). An equal volume of 2 µM NBD-phospholipid in HBSS-glucose was added to the cell suspension and incubated at 15 °C. At each time point, 200 µL of the cell suspension was mixed with 200 µL of ice-cold HBSS-glucose containing 5% fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (BSA, Wako) to extract NBD-lipids incorporated into the exoplasmic leaflet of the plasma membrane, as well as unincorporated lipids. Next, the cells were analyzed with an Accuri C6 Plus instrument (BD Biosciences) to measure the fluorescence of NBD-lipids that had been incorporated and translocated to the cytoplasmic leaflet of the plasma membrane.

Surface biotinylation

The cell surface biotinylation assay was performed as previously described.18,51 Cells were washed three times with chilled PBS containing 0.1 mM CaCl2 and 0.1 mM MgCl2 (PBS++), and then incubated at 4 °C for 30 min with 2 mM sulfo-NHS-LC-biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in PBS++. To stop biotinylation, the cells were washed three times with chilled PBS++ containing 100 mM glycine and 0.3% BSA, and then twice more with chilled PBS. The cells were then lysed in lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 1% Nonidet P-40) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Nacalai Tesque) at 4 °C for 30 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 ×g at 4 °C for 20 min in a microcentrifuge to remove cellular debris and insoluble materials. To precipitate biotinylated proteins, the supernatant was incubated with streptavidin-agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) pre-equilibrated with lysis buffer for 4 h at 4 °C. The protein-bound streptavidin beads were washed three times with lysis buffer, twice with high-salt buffer (20 mM HEPES-KOH, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.5% Nonidet P-40), and once with 20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.4). Proteins were eluted from the beads with SDS-PAGE sample buffer, denatured at 37 °C for 2 h, and subjected to immunoblot analysis. Immunoblots were developed using ImmunoStar reagents (Wako) and recorded on an ImageQuanti 800 instrument (Amersham).
