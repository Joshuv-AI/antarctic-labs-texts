A field-deployable high-resolution SNP genotyping platform for rapid surveillance of Yersinia pestis
Authors: Ying Liu, Qiuying Huang, Yarong Wu, Jiahao Zheng, Mingzhu Xu, Liu Guo, Fang Liu, Mengnan Cui, Yan Guo, Xiujuan Zuo, Jianyun Li, Xin Wang, Huaiqi Jing, Ruifu Yang, Yujun Cui, Qingge Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTYersinia pestis, the causative agent of plague, poses a persistent global health threat due to its rapid transmission and high mortality. To enable rapid, field-deployable surveillance, we developed a high-resolution, culture-independent genotyping assay for precise identification of Y. pestis lineages and sublineages. We curated and validated 25 canonical single nucleotide polymorphisms (canSNPs) to resolve 24 global lineages, along with 12 region-specific canSNPs distinguishing 9 predominant sublineages circulating in Inner Mongolia, China. By integrating ARMS-HANDS PCR with multicolor melting curve analysis, we established a hierarchical multiplex assay capable of simultaneously genotyping 37 canSNPs in three real-time PCR reactions. The system exhibited high sensitivity (detection limit: 50 genome copies per reaction) and achieved 100% accuracy in a double-blind evaluation of 166 Y. pestis strains. Incorporated into a portable “sample-in, result-out” device, the assay enabled direct lineage identification from infected gerbil liver samples within 120 min, accurately detecting the epidemiologically critical sublineage 2.MED3.1.4 in field-collected specimens. This robust and deployable genotyping platform addresses key limitations in current plague surveillance efforts, offering a transformative solution for real-time outbreak investigation and epidemiological tracking in resource-constrained settings.

Introduction

Plague, caused by Yersinia pestis [1], remains a significant zoonotic threat despite substantial reductions in global mortality over the past century [2]. The pathogen persists in enzootic foci across Africa, Asia, and the Americas, occasionally spilling over into human populations and triggering localized outbreaks. Although over 95% of recent human cases have occurred in Africa [3,4], sporadic cases continue to be reported in China, Mongolia, Brazil, Peru, and the southwestern United States, underscoring the pathogen’s enduring global presence [5,6]. Owing to its high transmissibility, potential for antimicrobial resistance, and historical role in pandemics, Y. pestis has been classified by the World Health Organization as a high-priority emerging pathogen, necessitating enhanced surveillance and rapid response capabilities [7,8].

Accurate and timely surveillance of Y. pestis hinges on the ability to differentiate circulating lineages and trace transmission dynamics during outbreaks [9,10]. Traditional methods, such as biovar classification, lack the phylogenetic resolution required for modern epidemiological investigations [2,11]. While molecular techniques have improved discriminatory power, they vary in reproducibility and evolutionary reliability [12–17]. Among them, variable number tandem repeat (VNTR) [18–20] analysis and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) profiling offer the highest resolution. However, VNTR-based methods are limited by high mutation rates and poor cross-laboratory standardization. In contrast, SNPs offer exceptional evolutionary stability [21] and inter-laboratory consistency, making them ideal for defining population structure and elucidating transmission pathways. Despite these advantages, SNP-based genotyping remains largely confined to centralized laboratories due to its dependence on cultured isolates, specialized infrastructure, and the lack of a standardized, field-adaptable SNP panel capable of capturing both global and regional diversity [22–24]. These limitations are particularly pronounced in remote or resource-limited regions, where rapid pathogen identification and lineage tracking are critical for effective public health response.

Inner Mongolia, bordering Mongolia and Russia, is one of the most active plague-endemic regions in China. It accounts for approximately one-quarter of the country’s natural plague foci and has reported over 80% of national human plague cases in the past 5 years, a rising trend over the last decade [25,26]. In response, the Inner Mongolia Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has established 59 Plague Field Monitoring Stations across all administrative divisions. However, these stations are equipped only for basic sample collection and sterilization, having limited facilities for bacterial culture or molecular testing. This situation highlights an urgent need for a portable, culture-independent, lineage-resolving genotyping platform that enables rapid, near real-time surveillance in the field.

In our previous work, we identified 2,298 SNPs within the core genome of Y. pestis by analyzing 133 genomes, leading to the definition of 24 globally distributed lineages [27]. More recently, through a comprehensive analysis of 585 Y. pestis genomes from Inner Mongolia and 1,436 genomes from other regions and countries, we identified 8,246 SNPs and observed a pronounced dominance of three primary phylogroups in Inner Mongolia: 2.MED3, 0.PE4, and 2.ANT3 [28]. Building on these findings, the present study refines the SNP panel to a minimal yet highly informative set capable of differentiating both global lineages and regional subclades. To enable practical field deployment, we developed a novel multiplex SNP genotyping method using ARMS-HANDS PCR (A-H PCR) [29] and integrated it into a portable, “sample-in, result-out” device. This system was successfully validated using rodent liver specimens collected directly from Plague Field Monitoring Stations, without the need for bacterial culture. The resulting platform offers a rapid and accurate solution for high-resolution Y. pestis genotyping in remote, resource-limited, and outbreak-prone settings, within and beyond Inner Mongolia.

Materials and methods

Selection of canSnps

The 25 global canSNPs were selected from the 2,298 non-homoplastic core-genome SNPs identified by Cui et al. [27], in which the core genome was defined using a 100% presence threshold across 133 Y. pestis genomes. The 12 canSNPs targeting predominant sublineages circulating in Inner Mongolia were derived from a separate population genomic dataset based on a 95% core-genome definition [28]. For both datasets, only SNP loci present in 100% of strains within the target lineage were retained for canSNP selection.

The selection criteria were as follows: (1) A single SNP was chosen per branch, with priority given to synonymous SNPs, followed by intergenic SNPs, and finally nonsynonymous SNPs; (2) SNPs were required to be flanked by sequences without repeats or insertions within 500 bp upstream or downstream to ensure conservation and facilitate primer design; (3) Preference was given to SNPs within housekeeping genes, as these are highly conserved, genetically stable, and less prone to reversion mutations. After selection, the canSNPs were validated in silico using a dataset of over 3,000 Y. pestis strains [30].

Genomic DNA (gDNA) of strains and synthetic plasmids

The assay establishment and analytical performance evaluation were conducted using stored gDNA from various sources, including 42 Y. pestis strains with diverse lineages of China, 24 Y. pestis strains specifically collected from Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region of China (Table S1), 22 Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, 11 Yersinia enterocolitica, 2 Yersinia kristensenii, and 2 Yersinia frederiksenii (Table S2). Additionally, plasmids (Table S3) containing target alleles not detected in the above strains were synthesized by Sangon Biotech. Each insertion sequence was cloned into a pUC57 vector, and the resulting plasmids were individually transformed into Escherichia coli TOP10 competent cells for propagation.

PCR and melting curve analysis

PCR and melting curve analysis were performed using a 25-µL reaction mix containing 1× PCR master buffer, 6.0 mM MgCl2, 0.4 mM dNTPs mixture (cat. no. N1101-N1104, Linglan Biotech), 2.5 U Taq HS DNA polymerase (cat. no. CM0008, Accurate Biology), 5 µL of Y. pestis gDNA or water, primers, and probes (sequences and concentrations are listed in Table S4-6). PCR and melting curve analysis was performed using a SLAN 96S real-time PCR system (Hongshi Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The assay was performed in a closed‑tube format without post‑PCR manipulation, thereby minimizing the risk of amplicon contamination. The thermal cycling program was as follows: 95°C for 2 min; 6 cycles of 95°C for 4 s, 58°C for 8 s, and 72°C for 10 s; 12 cycles of 95°C for 4 s, 70°C for 8 s, and 72°C for 10 s; 38 cycles of 95°C for 4 s, 68°C for 8 s, and 72°C for 10 s; followed by 95°C for 1 min, 45°C for 2 min, and finally, a temperature increase from 45°C to 85°C (0.16°C/step). Fluorescence from six detection channels (Atto 425 at 450 nm, FAM at 510 nm, HEX at 565 nm, ROX at 620 nm, Cy5 at 665 nm, Quasar 705 at 705 nm) was recorded at each step of the continuous increase during melting curve analysis. Melting curves were plotted as the negative derivative of fluorescence vs temperature (-dF/dT), and melting temperatures (Tm) were automatically calculated using the real-time PCR thermocycler software. The results were analyzed using MeltPro Manager (Xiamen Zeesan Biotech Co., Ltd.), which embedded an automatic judgment rule for lineage classification based on the multicolor melting curve analysis (MMCA) readout.

Analytical studies

The analytical sensitivity of global Y. pestis genotyping assays (Reactions 1 and 2) was evaluated using gDNA from three Y. pestis strains representing distinct lineages (separately belonging to 0.PE4, 2.ANT1, and 2.MED2). The analytical sensitivity of Inner Mongolia Y. pestis genotyping assay (Reaction 3) was evaluated using gDNA from nine Y. pestis strains (2.ANT3.1, 2.ANT3.2, 0.PE4.3.2, 2.MED3.1.1, 2.MED3.1.2, 2.MED3.1.3, 2.MED3.1.4, 2.MED3.1.5, and 2.MED3.1.6). gDNA copy numbers were calculated based on the Y. pestis CO92 genome size (4.65 Mb) using the formula: copies/μL = (concentration in ng/μL × 10−9 × Avogadro´s number)/(genome size in bp × 650). The concentration of each gDNA stock was first measured using an ND-2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA) before serial dilution. All gDNA samples were serially diluted 10-fold in nuclease-free water to concentrations ranging from 104 to 1 copy/μL. Samples at concentrations of 104, 103, 102, 101, 10° copies/μL were analyzed in 1, 1, 2, 4 and 4 technical replicates, respectively. The limit of detection (LOD) was defined as the lowest concentration that repeatedly yielded correct genotypes across all targets.

Reproducibility of Tm measurement was assessed for Reactions 1–2 and Reaction 3 using gDNA from seven Y. pestis strains (0.PE4, 1.ORI2, 1.ORI3, 2.ANT1, 2.MED2, 3.ANT1, and 0.PE7) and nine Y. pestis strains (2.ANT3.1, 2.ANT3.2, 0.PE4.3.2, 2.MED3.1.1, 2.MED3.1.2, 2.MED3.1.3, 2.MED3.1.4, 2.MED3.1.5, and 2.MED3.1.6), respectively. Plasmid DNA (Table S3) and the gDNA of one Y. pseudotuberculosis strain was used for the absent melting peaks. Samples at 100 copies/μL were analyzed on three independent instruments, each with three technical replicates. Standard deviation (SD) of the Tm value was calculated for each melting peak.

Specificity was assessed using gDNA from Yersinia species other than Y. pestis: 22 Y. pseudotuberculosis, 11 Y. enterocolitica, 2 Y. kristensenii, and 2 Y. frederiksenii with a concentration of 1000 copies/μL.

Double-blind validation of the MMCA assay

A double-blind analysis of the MMCA assay was performed using 166 gDNA of Y. pestis strains, with the quantity of approximately 500 copies per reaction. The validation set included: 42 reference strains sourced from China, 24 reference strains collected from Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region of China, 65 additional strains from diverse plague foci across China, and 35 additional strains from Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region of China (Table S1). The lineage of each of the 166 gDNA samples had been previously characterized by whole-genome sequencing (WGS) followed by phylogenetic positioning based on the established global canSNP framework [27,28]. All WGS data were generated on Illumina platforms and assembled against the CO92 reference genome. All gDNA samples were anonymized by unique numerical codes, and genotype information was withheld from the personnel performing the assays.

Subtyping of Y. pestis from field-collected Mongolian gerbil liver specimens

Nine liver specimens were collected post mortem from nine individual Mongolian gerbils (Meriones unguiculatus) confirmed to be infected with Y. pestis by bacterial culture (organ impression plating on Yersinia-selective agar) followed by plague phage lysis assay, according to routine plague surveillance protocols used by the Otog Banner Plague Field Monitoring Station. These naturally deceased wild gerbils were collected in July 2024 from the grassland managed by Otog Banner Plague Field Monitoring Stations, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region of China, with no specific owner. Their collection was conducted in compliance with the relevant regulations of the monitoring station for plague surveillance purposes. Because the specimens were obtained from naturally deceased wild animals as part of a statutory monitoring activity and the study involved no experimental procedures on live animals, no specific animal ethics approval was required. Tissue fragments (10–15 mg) were homogenized in 200 µL sterile water using a Dounce tissue grinder and then boiled at 100°C for 10 min. After standing for 10 min, 100 µL of the supernatant was transferred into the cartridge of the Sanity 2.0 (Xiamen Zeesan Biotech Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China). Sanity 2.0 is a portable closed system platform integrating DNA extraction and transfer, PCR amplification, melting curve analysis, and automated lineage determination within a sealed cartridge workflow. The instrument uses the same six-channel fluorescence configuration and thermal cycling protocol as the SLAN 96S system and can simultaneously process up to four samples per run. The Sanity 2.0 device can accommodate up to four DNA extraction cartridges and 24 reaction tubes, enabling the simultaneous processing of four samples in a single batch. The reaction solutions were lyophilized and stored in sealed containers at ambient temperature, protected from light and humidity. For comparison, the same samples were also analyzed using a standard SLAN 96S system, following DNA extraction with the Lab-Aid 824s system (Xiamen Zeesan Biotech Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyzes were performed using SPSS software version 27.0 (SPSS Inc.).

Results

Lineage classification of Y. pestis using canSnps

canSNP analysis provides a robust tool for defining phylogenetic clades of Y. pestis, from major phylogenetic division to individual strain [22]. Our analysis of 133 diverse Y. pestis genomes identified a total of 2,326 SNPs [27]. Based on the initial phylogenetic structure inferred from variable number tandem repeats and different region analysis data, each SNP was mapped onto specific phylogenetic branches. To minimize diagnostic redundancy, SNPs providing identical phylogenetic information were consolidated, resulting in a smaller set of SNPs for clade differentiation. Through this refinement process, 25 canSNPs were selected to differentiate 24 global Y. pestis lineages (Figure 1(A) and Table S7). These canSNPs were validated through testing against over 3,000 Y. pestis strains to confirm their canonical designation [30]. Design and establishment of the MMCA assay

To enable multiplex SNPs-based subtyping, we combined A-H PCR with MMCA for multiplexed canSNP detection. In A-H PCR, low concentrations of tailed primers initiate amplification, and a high concentration Tag primer extends along matched strands, discriminating mismatched alleles (Figure 2(A)). During melting curve analysis, self-quenched probes, designed to match one allele, anneal to the target DNA and denature based on their Tm values as temperature increases (Figure 2(A)). Analytical studies

To evaluate the sensitivity of the MMCA assay, strains were serially diluted from 104 to 1 copy/μL. The sensitivity study showed that melting peaks were detected for template concentrations as low as 10 copies/μL, with the LOD determined as 50 copies/reaction (Fig S2).

Reproducibility of Tm values was assessed by detecting gDNA from Y. pestis and plasmids in triplicate across three instruments. The SD of Tm values ranged from 0.051°C to 0.407°C, with no interference between adjacent melting peaks (Fig S3).

The specificity of the assay was evaluated by SNP profiling of closely related strains, including 22 Y. pseudotuberculosis, 11 Y. enterocolitica, and other non-Y. pestis genomes (Figure 3). All non-Y. pestis strains yielded no detectable signals for the three IPCs (3a, caf1, and pla). Fourteen Y. pseudotuberculosis strains exhibited corresponding melting peaks for the 24 SNPs of Reactions 1–2 due to homologous sequences. However, the SNP s283 showed a characteristic Tm shift (ΔTm = 1.2–2.4°C). In Reaction 3, all Y. pseudotuberculosis lost signal at s3323. In contrast, other Yersinia species (Y. enterocolitica, Y. kristensenii, and Y. frederiksenii) yielded no detectable signals for most SNPs in all three reactions, confirming minimal cross-reactivity. Double-blind analysis of 166 Y. pestis strains

To validate the accuracy of the MMCA assay, we conducted a double-blind analysis of 166 Y. pestis gDNA samples with pre-characterized lineages (Figure 4(A)). Among them, 161 strains were positive for both IPCs and SNPs. However, the other five strains exhibited partial loss of IPC signals: strain 0142 lost both pla and caf1, strain 0320 lost pla, and strains 95014, 95013, and 92016 each lost 3a (Figure 4(B)). The genotyping results exhibited complete consistency with lineage assignments obtained via WGS. Reaction 1–2 accurately discriminated 17 phylogenetic lineages (Figure 4(C)), while Reaction 3 correctly resolved all regional strains: 59 strains from Inner Mongolia were categorized into three lineages, including 40 strains as 2.MED3, 12 as 2.ANT3, and 7 as 0.PE4 (Figure 4(C)). Subtyping of Y. pestis in field-collected Mongolian gerbils

The MMCA assay was applied to livers of nine Mongolian gerbils collected from Otog Banner (Figure 5(A)). Liver homogenates were directly tested using Sanity 2.0 (Figure 5(B)). All nine specimens produced signals for the 37 canSNPs and three IPCs (Figure 5(C) and Fig S4). The assay yielded results for four samples (one batch) within 80 min. The genotyping assay resolved all specimens belonged to the 2.MED3 lineage (Reaction 1–2) and the 2.MED3.1.4 sublineage (Reaction 3). All genotyping results obtained using the Sanity 2.0 device were validated in parallel using a standard SLAN 96S system in our central laboratory, demonstrating 100% concordance. These assignments aligned with regional plague patterns, which showed that the predominant sublineages of Inner Mongolia were 2.MED3.1.2 (55.73%) and 2.MED3.1.4 (16.97%) during 1948–2021 [28]. Discussion

In this study, we leveraged the genomic diversity of Y. pestis to establish a novel canSNP genotyping assay capable of resolving both global phylogenetic lineages and regional sublineage diversification in Inner Mongolia. Using A-H PCR for targeted SNP amplification and MMCA for genotyping in a closed-tube format, we successfully genotyped 25 global and 12 Inner Mongolia-specific canSNPs within three reactions. The assay demonstrated a LOD of 50 copies/reaction and exhibited high specificity, with no cross-classification observed across Y. pseudotuberculosis, Y. enterocolitica, Y. kristensenii, or Y. frederiksenii. Validation using 166 China and 59 Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region of China-derived Y. pestis strains confirmed the accuracy of lineage/sublineage discrimination. Importantly, direct genotyping from liver specimens of nine Mongolian gerbils underscores the assay’s adaptability for culture-independent pathogen surveillance.

To minimize cross-reactivity, we selectively targeted SNPs that distinguish Y. pestis from its closest relatives, Y. pseudotuberculosis, Y. enterocolitica, Y. kristensenii, and Y. frederiksenii. For non-Y. pseudotuberculosis species, few SNPs were detected. For Y. pseudotuberculosis, the closest strains have a sequence variation at the probe hybridization area of s283, resulted in a shifted melting peak. Meanwhile, the inclusion of three Y. pestis-specific genes as IPC effectively prevented misclassification. The 12 Inner Mongolia-specific canSNPs further resolved 9 epidemiologically distinct sublineages, highlighting the value of region-specific downstream markers for high-resolution local outbreak investigation. To evaluate the genomic stability of the selected markers, we compared the positions of all 37 canSNPs against the 45 mutational hot regions identified by Wu et al. [30] and found that none overlapped these regions. We further examined two loci reported to exhibit elevated mutation density – the aspA codon 363 hotspot [31] and the YPO0623 gene [32]—and confirmed that none of the 37 canSNPs were located within or adjacent to either locus. This hierarchical SNP identification strategy permits flexible expansion to address emerging sublineages through additional downstream marker incorporation.

The assay’s exceptional sensitivity (50 copies/reaction) is attributed to the A-H PCR mechanism [29], which minimizes primer-dimer accumulation through the formation of pan-handle structures. In contrast to conventional Y. pestis subtyping methods that require 4–100 ng input DNA [14,18,20,33], our protocol operates efficiently with sub-nanogram quantities, allowing direct analysis of uncultured, field-collected specimens. This advancement was crucial for rapidly identifying the Y. pestis subtype in infected Mongolian gerbil, thereby facilitating effective plague surveillance at field monitoring stations. These stations are equipped to monitor wild rodent activity, collect specimens, detect pathogens, provide timely warnings, and implement control measures; however, they often lack the infrastructure for bacterial culture. To our knowledge, this represents the first culture-independent platform capable of achieving lineage-level resolution for Y. pestis.

The novel streamlined workflow addresses key limitations of conventional subtyping methodologies. Fragment-based approaches (insertion sequences [34]/different region analysis [15,35]/Multiple Loci Variable number of tandem repeats Analysis [18]) demand post-amplification open-tube manipulations, increasing carry-over contamination. Sequencing-dependent strategies (16S analysis [36]/multilocus sequence typing [37]/Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats [14,16]) exhibit insufficient resolution for Y. pestis subtyping or require high cost for the necessary fragment sequencing. Alternative SNP detection platforms, such as denaturing high performance liquid chromatography [11,38], matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry [38,39], melt mismatch amplification mutation assays [40], real-time TaqMan PCR [41], either require specialized instrumentation or suffer from limited multiplexing capacity. In contrast, our MMCA assay offers a streamlined, one-step closed-tube workflow with automated result interpretation, significantly reducing operational burden and contamination risk. The assay is optimally performed on real-time PCR instruments equipped with six fluorescence detection channels and melting curve analysis capability, including platforms such as the SLAN 96S and Sanity 2.0 (with six‑channel upgrade). For laboratories using instruments that do not support all six required channels, the assay can be adapted by relabeling probes with fluorophores that are detectable on the available channels and, if necessary, distributing the targets into additional reaction tubes. This strategy preserves genotyping accuracy, because probe binding specificity is determined by the sequence rather than the fluorophore; consequently, the melting peak patterns are expected to remain consistent. Although throughput per run may be reduced, the method remains applicable across a broader range of real‑time PCR platforms.

Validation using field-collected liver specimens confirmed operational practicality. When deployed on an integrated extraction-detection device, the assay generated concordant results with conventional thermocyclers within 80 min. The assay offers three key advantages for resource-limited settings: (i) it allows untrained personnel to perform genotyping after simple pre-treatment of fresh samples, facilitating timely identification and reporting of local lineages/sublineages; (ii) it eliminates manual steps required for DNA extraction and liquid transfer, enabling rapid subtyping without well-equipped facility or laboratory; and (iii) its portable nature, ease of use, and low cost would facilitate its implementation in sentinel stations for routine Y. pestis surveillance, particularly in resource-limited settings where rapid diagnosis is crucial for mitigating the risk of Y. pestis transmission. This field-deployable capability addresses a critical gap in remote plague foci, where transport of specimens to central laboratories may require days. Rapid on-site lineage determination enables timely public health interventions, including targeted rodent control, insecticide spraying, and community alerts, while centralized confirmatory testing proceeds in parallel. Such an approach complements rather than replaces centralized genomic surveillance by providing rapid first-line epidemiological information during outbreak investigations. Beyond Y. pestis monitoring, our platform is adaptable for other emerging infectious diseases [20], including “Disease X” as classified by the World Health Organization, making it a valuable tool for outbreak response and bioterrorism preparedness.

In the double-blind validation, five strains showed partial loss of internal positive-control signals, including loss of pla in two strains (0142 and 0320, both belonging to 2.ANT3.1), loss of caf1 in strain 0142, and loss of 3a in three strains (95014, 95013, and 92016, all belonging to 2.MED3). WGS assembly analysis indicated that the absence of pla in strains 0142 and 0320 resulted from complete loss of the pPCP1 plasmid, whereas the absence of caf1 in strain 0142 resulted from loss of the pMT1 plasmid. The 3a-negative phenotype in the three 2.MED3 strains was caused by chromosomal deletion of the 3a locus. Previous studies have shown that Yersinia plasmids may be unstable and readily lost during prolonged storage or repeated laboratory passaging [42–44]. Importantly, these events did not interfere with genotyping of the 37 canSNPs because the pla, caf1, and 3a targets are independent of the SNP markers.

Although the global canSNP panel enables robust phylogenetic placement across major Y. pestis lineages, its resolution is limited for fine-scale source tracking in endemic regions where the same major lineage may circulate across multiple countries or natural foci. Consequently, isolates sharing an identical canSNP profile should not be interpreted as necessarily deriving from the same recent transmission event. In such settings, finer phylogenetic resolution requires additional region-specific downstream canSNPs, as exemplified here for Inner Mongolia, or WGS integrated with geographic, temporal, and epidemiological information.

In summary, we present a highly specific, sensitive, and rapid Y. pestis subtyping platform based on A-H PCR and MMCA that bridges the gap between phylogenetic resolution and plague endemic foci application. The assay requires minimal DNA input, operates with a streamlined workflow, and demonstrates robust performance from strain subtyping to the analysis of field-collected specimens. These attributes make it a valuable tool for pathogen surveillance in resource-limited environments and a scalable solution for emerging infectious disease control.
