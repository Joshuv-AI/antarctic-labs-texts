Do carbon policy shocks move stocks? evidence from China’s carbon market
Authors: Jiachao Peng, Yuxin Zeng, Shuke Fu, Jiali Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs carbon policies expand under net zero goals, policy news in carbon markets can send fast signals for equity prices. Using daily data for more than 1,600 listed firms from July 2021 to December 2025, we build carbon policy shocks from carbon price moves on 12 national ETS release days and estimate their link to stock returns. Carbon price changes on release days have a much stronger link to stock returns than changes on other days. Channel tests suggest the response is tied to shifts in short-run risk conditions and changes in trading conditions around release days. The effect is stronger when the carbon market is more mature and more active, and it differs by ETS coverage status, policy type and firm carbon exposure. The findings show how transition risk is priced in China and inform ETS communication and market depth.Keywords: Carbon prices, Stock returns, Carbon policy surprise, China’s national ETS, Transition risk pricing

Introduction

Since China’s national emissions trading system (ETS) started in July 2021, the carbon market has moved from an early start to more regular trading. During this period, central agencies released many official policy documents and notices. These texts set or adjust key rules, such as allowance allocation, compliance and surrender, trading rules, supervision, and plans to expand coverage. Each text has a clear release date and a clear scope, so it brings new information about future carbon limits and how rules will be applied. At the same time, the allowance price is set in the market and updated each trading day. The price reflects what traders and firms expect about future compliance pressure and carbon related costs under the current rules. With this setting, this paper asks a clear question: Do ETS policy releases affect stock prices? If yes, do they work through the carbon price, and how large is the effect on firm value and stock returns?

This question matters because transition risk is widely seen as a priced financial risk, but it is hard to isolate policy driven effects. Carbon prices and stock returns can move together for many reasons that have nothing to do with new policy information, so simple links are not informative about policy transmission. If policy news is priced, the equity response should be stronger when policy information arrives, rather than being spread evenly across normal day to day co movement.

Existing evidence follows two broad paths. The first is exposure based. Firms are ranked by emissions or carbon intensity, and studies report return differences between high and low exposure firms, often read as a carbon risk premium [13]. This approach is useful for cross firm patterns, but it is less suited for time series tests of policy shocks because exposure changes slowly and low frequency variation cannot cleanly separate policy news from other shocks. The second path shifts attention from who is exposed to when policy news arrives. It uses tight event windows around policy communication to capture the information shock more cleanly. Recent work for Europe shows that carbon futures move around regulatory events can be used to build policy shock measures that are closer to surprise news, and these shocks can be traced into macro and asset price outcomes [37, 49]. Newer work also suggests that climate policy announcements may bundle multiple types of information, and distinguishing between news effects and value effects is important for understanding market reactions [35]. This view motivates treating the carbon price as an information signal in asset pricing. If carbon price moves around policy releases reflect updated beliefs about future rules, then the carbon price can help explain equity returns beyond standard energy measures. This is consistent with evidence from the UK that carbon prices co-move with firm value at least as much as major energy commodities and may even become the most important energy or environmental variable for corporate valuation [19]. Event day effects may also depend on who trades and what information becomes material, as shown by announcement day reactions in related disclosure policy settings [30]. Studies that focus on channels also show that transition risk pricing is not only about expected compliance costs. It can work through market conditions. Evidence links climate policy uncertainty to weaker market liquidity and to tighter funding and capital flows [69, 70]. Evidence from China’s carbon market points to risk spillovers and shows that low liquidity can amplify cross market transmission [5]. Cross country evidence further shows that policy uncertainty spillovers into financial and energy markets can change over time and can be stronger in stressed periods [52].

Despite this progress, a key gap remains for China. We still lack clear high frequency evidence on whether ETS policy news is transmitted into Chinese stock prices through carbon market reactions. Such evidence is also limited for developing countries in general. Filling this gap matters because China’s ETS is centrally guided and still evolving, while its equity market is large and diverse. The sign and size of transmission are not obvious. Policy news may shift expected cash flows, but it may also shift risk pricing and trading conditions. Heterogeneity is likely to be central. Carbon market maturity and market scale may shape how well carbon prices reflect policy information, and coverage rules may shape whether investors read releases as direct cost pressure or as a wider signal about tighter future rules. In our setting, such heterogeneity refers to differences in the event day slope, not a claim that the overall effect of carbon prices on firm value must change in one fixed way.

This paper addresses the gap with three research questions. First, is the link between carbon price changes and stock returns stronger on ETS policy release days than on other days? Second, are return responses linked to shifts in short horizon risk conditions and trading conditions after policy news? Third, do market conditions and coverage status shape the response in a systematic way, including possible sign differences in the event day slope across settings?

To answer these questions, we build a daily policy event indicator from 12 key national ETS policy releases and test several event windows for robustness. We map each policy release date to the relevant stock and carbon trading day. We match this indicator with firm level daily stock returns and daily carbon price changes. The sample runs from July 2021 to December 2025 and covers more than 1,600 listed firms across 17 main industries. We use a panel regression design to identify how carbon policy is linked to firm value. We control for firm fixed effects and calendar time controls at the month level, together with common market and financial factors, to limit shared shocks. We then compare the return sensitivity to carbon price changes on normal trading days with the added sensitivity on policy event days, and we treat this added sensitivity as the policy day carbon signal, which we call CPS. To shed light on channels, we test whether CPS is linked to shifts in short horizon risk outcomes and to changes in trading conditions. Furthermore, we introduce interaction terms to examine how the baseline effects vary across carbonintensity, policy nature, regulated versus non-regulated industries, carbon market maturity and size, and we conduct a range of robustness checks.

This study contributes in three ways. First, it adds shock based high frequency evidence on transition risk pricing in China, which speaks to when equities absorb policy information rather than only which firms look more exposed on average. Second, it shows that the carbon market can carry policy day information into equity prices, so carbon price reactions are part of the transmission from policy releases to valuation. Third, it links valuation effects to channels and context by testing risk and trading conditions and by documenting heterogeneity across maturity, market scale, and coverage status, which helps explain why the same type of policy news can lead to different market outcomes across segments of China’s capital market.

Several limitations are worth noting. Our event set is based on public national documents and may miss informal guidance or local signals. Carbon price moves on event days may still include non policy components, and our measures of risk conditions and trading conditions are imperfect. These limits call for care when extending the results beyond listed firms or beyond our sample period. They also point to future work using richer policy text measures, higher frequency trading data, and more direct data on allowance allocation and compliance costs.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Sect. "Literature Review and Research Hypotheses" reviews the related literature and develops testable hypotheses. Sect. "Model" describes the data and variable construction and outlines the empirical strategy. Sect. "Results" reports the results, including baseline estimates, channel tests, and heterogeneity analyses. Sect. "Discussion" discusses the findings, the evidence for channels, robustness checks, and limitations. Sect. "Conclusion" concludes and presents policy implications.

Literature review and research hypotheses

Carbon policy events and equity pricing responses

When a regulator releases a key rule or notice, new policy information often arrives on a clear date. Carbon prices can move sharply around these dates, and such moves are more likely to reflect policy news than routine demand or commodity shocks [37, 49]. In related work, changes in climate policy uncertainty also link to stock returns and to the way risk shifts across assets, which suggests that policy news can trigger fast re pricing through investor trading [2, 25, 71].

A policy surprise can affect firm value in two basic ways. First, it can change expected compliance cost, product demand, and clean investment, which shifts expected cash flows [21]. Second, it can change uncertainty about future rules and business risk, which can change required risk pay and discount rates [8]. Both channels can show up in stock prices soon after the policy news arrives.

A key challenge is identification. On ordinary days, carbon prices and stock prices can move together for many reasons, such as macro demand, energy shocks, or changes in risk appetite. This makes the daily link hard to read as policy news [8]. Event timing helps. If we focus on carbon price moves that occur on policy event days, we are closer to the policy related part of the carbon signal, and we can test whether stocks respond more on those days than on other days [31].

This logic leads to a clear prediction. If markets learn from policy releases, then the stock return response to carbon price changes should be stronger on event days than on non event days. The response should also be stronger for firms with higher exposure to carbon cost and to demand shifts toward low carbon goods [21, 45]. We therefore state:

Hypothesis H1

Carbon policy events release new information that is priced in equity markets. Compared with non-event periods, stock returns are more sensitive to carbon price changes during policy event windows, which reflects the policy related component of the carbon price signal.

Policy events are not all the same. Some events tighten scarcity or enforcement, while others mainly clarify process. Because these types can change expected shortage pressure, compliance stress, coverage scope, and trading frictions in different ways, the event day return sensitivity can differ in sign and size across event types.

Time varying risk and risk premium channel of carbon policy shocks

Even when a policy change does not raise current cost right away, it can still change beliefs about the future path of rules, tech, and demand. This can change how investors see the range of future outcomes, especially downside risk. As a result, policy shocks can move stock prices not only through expected cash flows, but also through changes in perceived risk and required risk pay.

Prior evidence shows that climate-policy factor can be priced in stocks [29]. It also shows that exposure to climate policy uncertainty relates to expected returns, which is consistent with a risk premium for policy uncertainty [67]. From a time series view, changes in climate policy uncertainty can reshape how risk and volatility move across the market, with significant effects on Chinese stock market volatility that vary across sectors [25, 71]. When broad policy uncertainty rises, the way carbon risk links to stocks can also vary more over time and across sectors [68].

These ideas imply a testable channel: policy shocks should be linked to shifts in short horizon risk outcomes, and these shifts should help explain return moves. We therefore propose:

Hypothesis H2

Carbon policy shocks affect stock returns not only directly, but also by changing time varying risk and required risk pay through shifts in future operating and compliance uncertainty. As a result, policy shocks are associated with systematic changes in short horizon risk outcomes and return reactions.

Liquidity channel and policy related trading conditions

Policy shocks can also move prices by changing trading conditions. When a major rule is announced, investors may disagree more at first and adjust positions quickly. This can create large order flow that is not fully tied to same day firm news. If liquidity is thin, a given order imbalance can move prices more, so the same policy news can lead to larger price moves when trading is harder [1, 20].

Related evidence also suggests that policy related information can affect trading patterns. Rules and signals tied to climate risk can change market making incentives and future liquidity, and policy or energy shocks can shift co movement across green and carbon intense assets, which can lead to fast re allocation [31, 45]. In addition, higher policy uncertainty can tighten risk limits and trading conditions at the same time, which can slow liquidity supply and amplify spillovers [8]. These points motivate a second channel test:

Hypothesis H3

Carbon policy shocks are associated with changes in stock trading conditions. These changes are another channel through which policy shocks affect stock returns.

Heterogeneous impacts by carbon exposure

The policy effect should differ across firms and across market settings. Firms with higher emissions intensity often face higher expected cost pressure under tighter carbon rules, and may have less room to adjust. Their cash flows can be more sensitive to carbon prices, so their valuation can react more [13]. In an ETS, the same carbon price rise can also mean different things depending on allowance coverage and expected shortfall. A firm that expects to be short of allowances can face stronger cost pressure, while a firm with better coverage may face less near term stress [14, 51].

At the same time, market structure can shape how much information carbon prices carry. When the carbon market is larger and more active, prices can reflect shared belief more clearly, so the equity response to the policy related carbon signal can be stronger. This helps explain why a carbon return or carbon factor can become more visible when transition risk is priced more clearly [73]. Related industry evidence also points to variation with carbon market uncertainty and green innovation [74]. Crucially, as market structures more efficiently broadcast these policy signals, investors naturally penalize firms most vulnerable to future compliance costs. We then test two channels, risk conditions and trading conditions, and we examine whether exposure and market structure shape the strength of the response. Model

Data

Our study combines data from China’s National Carbon Emissions Trading Market, firms’ stock-market outcomes, and firm-level carbon information. Our baseline panel covers over 1,600 A-share-listed firms from July 2021 to December 2025, spanning the period since the launch of China’s national carbon market. To construct the policy-event datasets, we collected all policy documents related to China’s national ETS released since market opening from the official website of the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of the People’s Republic of China. From this full set of documents, we identify and select 12 key policy event days that are most likely to affect carbon prices. Carbon price data are obtained from the CEA1 trading market, and we source firm-level high-frequency stock market variables from the Wind database. At the firm level, we exclude daily observations for suspended, delisted, and ST/*ST firms, and we further exclude firms with less than one year of listing history to ensure sample validity. Some variables are not available for all firm-day observations, resulting in varying effective sample sizes across tables. For each regression specification, we use the largest possible panel dataset that includes valid values for the dependent variable and all covariates. Observations are excluded only if the dependent variable or any required regressor is missing. The number of observations for each regression is reported in Appendix Table 11.

Variables

Carbon policy surprise

Based on the release dates of the policy documents, we define a regulatory event-day dummy variable (Event) that equals 1 on policy event days and 0 otherwise. Notably, if a policy announcement occurs on a weekend or holiday, carbon prices cannot adjust immediately, so we map such events to the next trading day to align the timing with the market’s responsive period. We then construct a daily measure of carbon policy surprise (CPS) following the approach of Känzig [44] to proxy the carbon policy shock. Specifically, we treat the regulatory event days as the time when policy information converges, and use the carbon price change on these event days as a proxy for “policy shock”. In contrast, the risk premium in the carbon market changes more at a low frequency, making the carbon price jumps on event days more likely to reflect “surprises brought by policy news”. Nevertheless, may still be affected by other shocks, as it is essentially constructed using carbon price changes (), which are susceptible to unobserved demand factors.

where denotes the daily change in the carbon price, measured as the difference between the closing price on day t and that on day t-1. This construction follows Känzig [44], who argues that the carbon-market risk premium mainly shifts over longer periods and is unlikely to fluctuate drastically within a single day. It also presumes that the timing of regulatory announcements is largely independent of other same-day market shocks and is difficult to forecast. Based on these assumptions, reflects policy-driven carbon price surprises rather than typical market fluctuations.

Carbon intensity

Specifically, we use firm-level carbon emission intensity from ESG reports to assess exposure to these shocks. These emissions are reported in line with standard accounting practices and cover Scope 1 and Scope 2. We exclude Scope 3; this concern aligns with the broader MRV2 literature, which stresses that carbon metrics can vary with accounting and measurement choices and that validation is crucial for comparability across entities [27]. Also, take into account its frequent missing values and inconsistent measurements across firms.

From the perspective of model specification logic, we use lagged carbon emission intensity as the proxy for firm-level carbon exposure for two reasons. First, the lagged indicator can effectively mitigate reverse causality between policy shocks and current carbon emission intensity, thereby enhancing the validity of causal identification in the estimation results. Second, carbon emission intensity directly depicts the dependence of firms’ production and operational activities on carbon emissions, serving as an essential variable for capturing firms’ exposure to climate-related shocks [66] and showing that changes in carbon pricing regimes are closely linked to firm-level emission intensity responses [50].

To align with the frequency of firm-level financial data and our policy shock analysis, we linearly interpolate the annual carbon emissions data to a quarterly frequency [10]. This interpolation-based frequency conversion is commonly used to address frequency incompatibilities,allowing researchers to retain the information in low-frequency series while aligning it with higher-frequency data [55].

Mechanism variables

The first mechanism works through shifts in firms’ risk conditions. We proxy this risk channel using the 5-day rolling realized volatility (RV5) of firm returns, which is closely connected to time-varying expected returns and risk premia [4, 16]. Evidence for the risk channel arises when CPS moves RV5 in the first step, and RV5 helps explain returns in the second step, with the CPS coefficient weakening once RV5 is included.

The second channel is liquidity. We proxy liquidity using volume ratio (VolRatio), motivated by the idea that major policy announcements can change trading activity and trading frictions. Liquidity risk is known to be priced in stock returns [1, 3, 58]. Consistent evidence for the liquidity channel occurs when carbon policy stock moves the liquidity proxies in the first step, and these proxies are associated with returns in the second step.

Control variables

To address omitted-variable concerns when estimating how carbon price shocks relate to firm returns, we use a small set of daily controls that capture three basic forces that can move returns on the same day as the carbon market. First, we control for firm size using the daily log market value (Size). Size is a simple and widely used proxy for differences in risk and expected returns across firms, and it also helps absorb day-to-day changes in firm value that may be correlated with return sensitivity in a large panel [39]. Second, we control for the daily dividend yield (DY) and the 10-year government bond yield (Y10). Both variables summarize the level of discount rates and valuation conditions faced by investors. When discount rates change, broad equity pricing can shift even if firm cash flows do not, so failing to account for these movements can disadvantage the estimated response to carbon price news. Recent work that links equity values and returns to cash-flow and discount-rate forces, and that uses dividend and yield information to track these forces, supports treating these variables as meaningful state measures for valuation and expected returns [32, 33]. Relative to changes in government yields, equity return behaviour across markets is closely tied, which motivates controlling for daily movements in long rates in our setting [72]. Third, we use the CSI 300 daily amplitude (MktAmp) to control market risk. Volatility changes over time and often moves with risk premia and returns, so MktAmp helps capture shifts in market risk that could otherwise be linked to the carbon variables [9, 60]. Finally, We include USD/CNY and Brent as controls because the RMB exchange rate and global oil prices capture key macro and cross-market shocks that can move China’s stock returns. Controlling for them helps reduce bias from common shocks and external energy and demand conditions [24, 28, 36]. The detailed definitions of the variables are presented in Table 1.

(2)Policy event heterogeneity

Policy event heterogeneity

In our baseline specification, we treat the 12 key ETS policy dates as same event days. While this simplifies the analysis, it may mask important heterogeneity across events. ETS announcements can shape expected carbon costs in qualitatively distinct ways: some tighten regulatory stringency and increase anticipated compliance pressure, whereas others ease requirements or primarily clarify procedural details. If we pool events with opposite direction, the average event effect can be small even when the market reacts strongly to each type. For this reason, we extend the baseline design and allow policy events to be heterogeneous [48, 54, 63].

We classify the 12 ETS policy events into three groups: Tight, Loose, and Ambiguous. A Tight event is a document that is expected to strengthen ETS constraints, such as stricter MRV and enforcement, clearer or stricter surrender rules, or signals of higher allowance scarcity. A Loose event is expected to ease constraints, such as more flexible compliance rules, softer enforcement, or rules that lower trading and compliance costs. An Ambiguous event mainly relates to process and coordination and does not clearly change scarcity or enforcement, so the direction is not clear. Each event day belongs to one and only one category, and the date-by-date mapping and short text-based reasons are reported in Appendix Table 12. This three-way split helps us avoid forcing procedural events into Tight or Loose, and it reduces classification error [34, 43]. However no policy event in our sample period can be clearly classified as Loose, we only estimate heterogeneity for Tight and Ambiguous events.

Carbon market maturity

We also expect the market’s maturity to matter because policy shocks may transmit differently when the trading system is still being tested and refined than when key institutional pieces are in place. We therefore divide China’s national ETS into two maturity stages, defined by major, clearly identifiable institutional milestones. The split follows both the literature and observable policy changes, and it is consistent with a structural break in carbon price returns. Prior studies describe the early national ETS as still developing. Liu et al. [47] notes that the national ETS started in July 2021 but was still relatively immature, and that key parts of the system, such as rules, MRV, and trading design, were still being improved. A review by Huang et al. [41] also shows that China’s ETS moved from pilots to a national market in a gradual way, with rules and market practice updated over time [41]. Policy texts also show a clear change in the main policy focus and in rule strength. In the early stage, major policy events mainly built the basic system of control and compliance. For example, the Ministry of Ecology and Environment issued notices that strengthened data quality checks and set rules for allowance surrender in the first compliance cycle3 . These documents aimed to improve accounting and review, clarify compliance steps, and raise the cost of noncompliance. Around 2024, policy tools became stricter and the plan for wider coverage became clearer. First, the State Council issued and put into force the Interim Regulation on the Administration of Carbon Emissions Trading , which raised the legal level and stability of the ETS4. Besides, the Ministry issued more detailed and frequent rules on allocation, checking, and surrender, and the system moved toward a more regular annual compliance cycle5 . Taken together, these institutional milestones suggest a natural dividing line around early 2024. However, one might still worry that this cutoff is ultimately a chosen date rather than a genuine regime change in the market. Third, to address this concern, we test for a structural break in the carbon price return series. Using the first trading day of 2024 as the candidate break date, we apply a Wald test for a break in an AR(1) return specification. The null hypothesis of no structural break is rejected, indicating a clear change in returns around early 2024 and lending data-based support to our institutional split. We therefore treat the period from July 2021 through December 2023 as the early stage, and the period from January 2024 through December 2025 as a later stage characterized by stricter rules and a clearer path toward broader coverage. Then, we estimate the baseline effect separately in two period and compare the results.

Results

The evolution of China’s carbon price

Since the market opened in July 2021, the carbon price has mostly moved in a narrow range of about 45–60 RMB per ton (see Fig. 4). The price level is fairly stable. In this early stage, the key task was maintaining smooth market operations and rolling out the compliance system, so the price mainly reflected gradual adjustments in supply, demand, and expectations.

China’s carbon price evolution

From the second half of 2023, the price level rose sharply. It rose to 70 and 80 RMB per ton and reached a peak around 2024. In terms of historical background, this period overlapped with the gradual progress in allowance allocation and the concentrated compliance in 2021 and 2022. Stronger compliance pressure and higher trading demand helped push prices up, showing a stronger pricing role of the market. After the second half of 2024, the carbon price fell from the high level and moved into a range with ups and downs. It once dropped to around 60 RMB per ton and then partly recovered. This pullback may be linked to a short-term rebalance of supply and demand and a re-pricing of policy and compliance expectations. Meanwhile, the daily percentage change in the carbon price moves around zero and shows “spikes” and “fat tails”: on most days the change is small, but on a few days it jumps sharply. This suggests that in the short run, the carbon price is sensitive to policy news, compliance dates, and market sentiment. Finally, near the end of the sample period, the expected market expansion became stronger. In 2025, the Ministry of Ecology and Environment released a plan to include steel, cement, and aluminium smelting. It set 2024 as the first control year and required the first compliance to be finished by the end of 2025. This expansion may provide a new long-term driver for the carbon price.

Robustness check

Standard error control

Standard error control

Given that the carbon variables and policy indicators take the same value for all firms. That means returns can be strongly correlated across firms on the same day. If we ignore this “same-day common shock,” standard errors can look too small [12, 59]. We therefore use two-way clustered standard errors, which allow for arbitrary correlation within a firm over time and across firms on a given day [22, 23].

(2)Short window test

Short window test

It is worth noting that a one-day measure can be noisy if prices adjust with a short delay or if there is micro-structure noise. We therefore construct an alternative short-window measure, CPS*, using the lag and lead of carbon price changes around the event day. This specification captures spillovers into the adjacent trading days while avoiding potential same-day micro structure noise. The main results remain qualitatively unchanged. Using short windows around news events is common in event-based return studies [48].

(3)Purged shock test

Purged shock test

Our baseline identification treats carbon price changes on policy event days as policy-driven surprises. A key concern is exclusivity: on the same day as an ETS announcement, other common shocks may also affect the carbon market and stock returns. If these same-day shocks drive part of the carbon price change, then the baseline may mix policy news with non-policy information. (5)Alternative Carbon Intensity Constructions

Alternative Carbon Intensity Constructions

To address concerns that linearly interpolating annual carbon intensity from annual to quarterly frequency may introduce artificial smoothing, measurement error, and spurious serial correlation, we conduct two complementary robustness checks [53, 61]. First, we re-estimate our baseline specification using only the original annual carbon-intensity measure. Specifically, we assign each firm’s carbon intensity from year y − 1 to all observations of that firm in year y. This approach preserves the original annual reporting frequency and avoids imposing any artificial within-year variation in carbon intensity, thereby providing a clean benchmark that is free from interpolation-induced smoothing [61].

Second, we reconstruct quarterly carbon intensity using a firm-specific cubic spline interpolation. Specifically, for each firm, we estimate a cubic spline function based solely on its annual carbon-intensity observations and then predict the corresponding quarterly values. Unlike linear interpolation, this approach allows for flexible, non-linear dynamics between annual observations and therefore mitigates concerns that our findings are driven by the particular functional form implied by mechanical linear smoothing [53, 64]. Reassuringly, the estimated coefficients of interest remain qualitatively and quantitatively similar across these alternative constructions, suggesting that our main conclusions are not sensitive to the interpolation procedure [53, 61]. Discussion

This paper studies whether carbon policy shocks move stock returns, and how this happens. We use 12 keys national ETS policy release dates and treat the carbon price move on those dates as the policy day carbon signal. We then discuss what the main estimates imply, why risk and trading conditions matter, how the response varies across market states and firms, and what the robustness tests say about identification.

Policy Day carbon signal and incremental pricing information

The baseline results show a clear difference between normal days and policy event days. On normal days, stock returns move with daily carbon price changes (∆CP) in a way that can reflect many shared forces. On policy event days, the sensitivity changes in a distinct way. The term we call CPS captures this extra sensitivity on event days, relative to normal days, after controlling for firm fixed effects, year month fixed effects, and daily controls.

This is the key economic point: we are not claiming that any daily carbon price change is a clean policy effect. Instead, the evidence comes from a slope difference. The fact that the event day slope is significantly different from the normal day slope suggests that event timing adds information that is not present in routine co-movement. In practice, the carbon market appears to concentrate policy news into the carbon price on those dates, and the equity market reacts to that policy related component.

Why risk conditions and trading conditions both matter

We test two channels in a cautious way. The first is risk conditions, proxied by short horizon realized volatility (RV5). The second is trading conditions, proxied by the volume ratio (VolRatio). In both cases, CPS is related to the channel variable, and the channel variable is related to returns. At the same time, CPS remains important when the channel variable is included. This pattern suggests that these channels help explain part of the return response, but they do not fully account for it.

The sign of the response deserves careful interpretation. A fall in after policy day carbon signals can mean several things. One possibility is that the policy release reduces short run uncertainty by clarifying rules, especially when the document removes ambiguity about compliance steps or enforcement. Another possibility is that markets reprice quickly and then settle, so realized volatility falls even if risk premia move. We therefore interpret the evidence as a shift in short run risk conditions rather than as a single, hard claim about disagreement.

For trading conditions, we find stronger trading activity around policy day carbon signals and a positive link between that activity and returns. Higher volume does not automatically mean better liquidity. It can also reflect two-sided trade, belief gaps, or forced re balance. What the evidence supports more safely is that policy news is a trading event, not only a valuation event. When policy information arrives, investors trade more, and the trading environment becomes part of the return response.

Taken together, the mechanism results support a two-part reading of policy day pricing. Policy signals are not only cash flow news. They are also news that moves short run risk conditions and changes how trading takes place when the information arrives.

Interpreting heterogeneity and linking it to the hypotheses

The heterogeneity results matter because they tell us when is most informative and for whom it is most material. We structure the interpretation around three dimensions; each tied to a specific mechanism in the theory section.

First, firm carbon exposure shapes the response. When we interact with firm carbon intensity, the marginal effect of becomes stronger as intensity rises. This is exactly what we expected if policy day carbon signals represent transition pressure that is more relevant for firms with higher exposure. It supports the hypothesis that exposure amplifies the valuation response to policy shocks.

Second, regulatory scope changes the meaning of the same carbon signal. The ETS coverage test shows that the response differs between covered and non-covered firms. This pattern supports a mixed channel view. For covered firms, part of the response can reflect direct compliance pressure and compliance planning. For non-covered firms, the response points to a broader policy signal that changes beliefs about the future path of regulation and transition pressure. The key takeaway is that the carbon market signal is not confined to the formal compliance set. Policy news can spill over through expectations.

Third, market structure changes how well carbon prices carry policy information. In the market size tests, we use both a stage-based approach and a continuous interaction approach. The stage approach uses break points in trading amount to split the sample into regimes. The continuous approach uses daily market activity measures directly. Even though the two methods are different, they point in the same direction: when the carbon market is larger and more active, the marginal effect of CPS on stock returns is stronger. The maturity split tells a similar story from a time perspective. In the earlier phase, the day-to-day carbon signal is weaker and the policy day response is less stable. In the later phase, the carbon signal is clearer and the stock response is more systematic. Importantly, this is a statement about the policy day incremental sensitivity captured by , not a claim that the overall effect of carbon prices on firm value flips in a mechanical way.

Finally, policy events are not all the same. When we separate events by type, the sign and strength of differ across categories. We classify events into Tight and Ambiguous in our sample period, and we do not have events that can be cleanly classified as Loose. The result implies that markets react to content rather than to labels. Tight events tend to be priced as higher pressure. Ambiguous events can be priced differently, likely because they change process, clarity, or trading frictions. This helps explain why pooled averages can look misleading. It also strengthens the interpretation of as an information variable that depends on what the policy says.

Overall, these heterogeneity patterns support the full set of hypotheses in a coherent way: event timing matters (H1), risk and trading conditions move with the shock (H2 and H3), and exposure shape the size and sometimes the direction of the pricing response (H4).

What the robustness checks imply for identification

A core identification concern is exclusivity: on the same day as an ETS policy release, other shocks could also hit both carbon prices and stock returns. Another concern is inference: because ∆CP, Event, and CPS are common to all firms on a given day, residuals can be strongly correlated across firms.

Our robustness checks address these concerns in a direct way. Stricter standard error methods reduce the risk that results are driven by cross firm correlation on event days. The leave one industry out exercise makes it less likely that a single large sector is driving the baseline effect. The short window measure makes it less likely that the result is an artifact of exact timing within one trading day.

Most importantly for exclusivity, we build a purged version of the carbon price move by removing the part explained by observable macro and financial factors, and we then re run the main design using the purged signal on event days. The key estimates remain similar in sign and relevance. This makes it less likely that what we call is simply a proxy for oil shocks, exchange rate moves, or broad market news that happens to land on the same day.

None of these tests can remove every possible concern, but together they support the main claim: the policy day carbon signal is distinct from routine daily carbon price changes and is economically meaningful for equity pricing.

Limitations and next steps

This study has limits that matter for interpretation. First, our event list is based on major national policy documents. Informal guidance, local actions, or slow expectation build up before release dates may not be captured. Second, even on event days, carbon price moves can include noise, and our channel proxies are not perfect measures of beliefs, risk premia, or true liquidity. Third, we focus on listed firms with matched daily data, so the results may not map one to one to private firms or to parts of the economy with different disclosure and trading patterns.

Future work can build on this design in three ways. One is to measure policy content more directly, so we can link specific rule changes to specific valuation channels. A second is to use higher frequency market data to tighten event timing and separate immediate reactions from delayed adjustment. A third is to add more direct data on allowance allocation and compliance positions, which would help connect the policy day carbon signal to firm cash flows more cleanly.

Conclusion

This paper asks a clear question. When China releases major policy news on the national ETS, do stock prices react in a clear and repeatable way, and does the carbon price help carry that news into the stock market? This matters because the ETS is meant to guide firm behavior, and we need to know whether financial markets read and price its signals. To answer the question, we use the timing of official ETS policy releases and the carbon price move on those days as our policy shock, and we link that shock to daily stock returns for listed firms.

The stock market does not treat carbon price moves the same on all days. On normal days, carbon prices and stock returns may move together for many reasons. On policy event days, the link becomes much stronger. This change in the slope shows that the carbon price move on a policy day contains extra policy information, and the stock market reacts to that information right away.

These results add up to a clear contribution. First, we offer a simple design that separates normal carbon equity co-movement from the extra pricing that appears when policy news arrives. Second, we show that the carbon market in China can act as an information channel for the stock market, and that this role grows as the market becomes more mature. Third, by linking the response to risk, to trading conditions, and to key forms of heterogeneity, we show when the pricing effect is stronger and why it is stronger.

Based on these results, we give practical policy implications for ETS regulators, market operators, and listed firms.

Because stock returns react most on ETS notice days, regulators can reduce avoidable swings by making both timing and meaning easier to read. Publish an annual calendar with expected release windows for allocation, compliance, and MRV items. For each notice, add a short plain note that states the main direction, the scope it affects, and the start date. Use one stable question and answer form and one clear time line for follow up, so the market does not fill gaps with guesswork.

To make the carbon price a more trusted signal, strengthen MRV and enforcement in ways that the public can verify. Use one online MRV form, require third party checks with some random audits, and publish clear rules for late reports and late surrender. After each compliance cycle, release a short summary of verified emissions, total allowance supply, and the share of firms that have finished surrender. This reduces uncertainty and limits sharp moves on notice days.

Since market activity raises the impact of policy shocks in our results, reduce basic trading frictions that block price discovery. Broaden eligible participation within current rules, lower fees, shorten settlement time, and publish simple daily data on volume, value, and best quote depth. Where allowed, add market making duties to keep quotes in place when news arrives.

The firm level tests point to where rule design matters most: high exposure firms and firms with tight allowance positions. Set an early warning rule based on verified emissions and planned allocation, and use staged surrender dates to avoid end of year pressure. Phase benchmark updates by sub sector in small steps, so changes are easier to plan and easier to price.

If coverage expands to new sectors, publish a clear transition path before the first binding year. State baseline years, the allocation method, the first benchmarks, and a step plan for tightening. A clear path reduces mixed signals and lowers the chance of abrupt re pricing.

For listed firms, replace broad calls for better disclosure with a short standard table in annual and mid-year reports. Require items that investors can compare: direct emissions and power use, carbon intensity, allowance holdings, expected allowance gap, a simple profit test for a given carbon price move, and the main risk action. This gives the market usable facts, especially near policy windows.

Finally, treat planned ETS releases as market risk windows. Add simple carbon policy shocks to stress tests for high exposure sectors, and monitor liquidity and volatility around the calendar dates. Where rules allow, give clear guidance on risk limits and basic hedge use so policy day risk is managed without extra stress in the spot market.

Firm-level stock market data are collected from the Wind database, and firm-level carbon emission intensity data are compiled from companies’ ESG reports. Policy event information is collected from official documents released by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment of the People’s Republic of China.Due to licensing restrictions of the Wind database and firm-level ESG disclosures, the datasets generated and analyzed during the current study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.
