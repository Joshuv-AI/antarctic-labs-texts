The role of insulin resistance in the longitudinal progression from NAFLD to cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic disease
Authors: Chao Hou, Xiangling Yuan, Min Peng, Xuan Shi, Dahong Yang, Fang Wang, Ke Song, Gelin Xu, Jianzhong Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveThis study aimed to elucidate the role of insulin resistance in the progression from non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) to cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) diseases, and to explore the potential influence of lifestyle factors.MethodsThis study included participants free of NAFLD and CKM diseases from the UK Biobank. Insulin resistance was assessed using the TyG index. CKM diseases include ischemic stroke (IS), ischemic heart disease (IHD), type 2 diabetes (T2D), and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Markov multi-state models were used to assess the associations between the TyG index and disease transitions.ResultsAmong 377,757 participants (median age 57 years), 4949 developed NAFLD over a median follow-up of 13.6 years. Of these, 12.65% progressed to T2D, 9.11% to IHD, 8.63% to CKD, and 4.61% to IS. Elevated TyG levels significantly accelerated transitions from NAFLD to first-onset CKM disease (HR = 1.25, 95% CI 1.05–1.49), double CKM disease (HR = 1.46, 95% CI 1.19–1.80), and triple CKM disease (HR = 1.81, 95% CI 1.28–2.55). Similar progression risk increases were observed for individual CKM components: T2D (HR = 1.41, 95% CI 1.11–1.78), CKD (HR = 1.37, 95% CI 1.08–1.75), and IS (HR = 1.70, 95% CI 1.27–2.27). A J-shaped dose–response relationship was identified. Transitions risk remained pronounced among individuals with healthy sleep and regular physical activity.ConclusionsInsulin resistance independently accelerated the progression from NAFLD to CKM diseases, particularly ischemic stroke. Introduction

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome is a systemic disorder involving interconnected metabolic dysfunction, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [1, 2]. It represents a continuum where metabolic abnormalities drive cardiovascular events and further deterioration [3]. However, both guidelines and existing studies have largely focused on type 2 diabetes (T2D) as the key metabolic disorder driving cardiovascular diseases [1–3].

In contrast, growing evidence demonstrates that NAFLD—as an abnormal accumulation of liver fat—also exerts systemic metabolic effects [4–6]. NAFLD not only markedly increases the risk of T2D but is also associated with vascular events [6–9]. With its prevalence rising sharply alongside economic development and lifestyle changes, NAFLD has become one of the most common metabolic disorders worldwide [10, 11]. This underscores the importance of evaluating NAFLD in a more integrated framework of CKM framework. More importantly, research on the well-established associations among NAFLD, T2D, CVD, and chronic kidney disease (CKD) has predominantly focused on cross-sectional prevalence and inter-disease correlations. The longitudinal trajectory—from an initially disease-free state to NAFLD, and subsequently to CKM disease or multimorbidity—remains poorly characterized.

Meanwhile, early identification of high-risk individuals and predictive markers is critical for CKM disease prevention. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, a simple surrogate for insulin resistance that reflects underlying metabolic dysfunction [12, 13], has been linked individually to NAFLD, CVD, and CKD risk [14–17]. However, its predictive value in the NAFLD-to-CKM diseases progression pathway remains unclear. Moreover, lifestyle factors such as physical activity, diet, and sleep quality are critical modulators of metabolic health [18]. Yet it is unknown whether the association between TyG and transition risk differs across lifestyle profiles.

To address existing gaps, we leveraged a large-scale, healthy-baseline cohort to delineate the dynamic trajectories of NAFLD progression toward CKM diseases, evaluate the associations between TyG index and disease transitions, and explore the modifying effects of lifestyle factors. This study was designed to elucidate the under-recognized pivotal role of NAFLD within the CKMD framework and to clarify the contribution of insulin resistance to disease progression.

Methods

Study design and participants

The study utilized data from the UK Biobank, a large prospective cohort that enrolled approximately 500,000 volunteers aged 40 to 69 years from England, Scotland, and Wales between 2006 and 2010. Socio-demographic, medical, and lifestyle information was collected via standardized questionnaires, interviews, and linkage to health records. The UK Biobank study obtained written informed consent from all participants and ethical approval from the Northwest Multicenter Research Ethics Committee [19].

We included participants who were free of both NAFLD and CKM disease at baseline. CKM diseases included ischemic stroke (IS), ischemic heart disease (IHD), T2D, and CKD. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) missing data necessary for calculation of the TyG index; (2) loss to follow-up or withdrawal; (3) missing key covariate information.

Calculation of TyG index

The TyG index was calculated using fasting blood glucose (FBG) and triglyceride (TG) levels measured at baseline. The formulas used was: TyG index = Ln [TG (mg/dL) × FBG (mg/dL)/2].

Outcomes

The primary outcomes included the development of NAFLD and the subsequent onset of CKM diseases, encompassing IS, IHD, T2D, and CKD. Outcomes were identified and classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revisions (ICD-9 and ICD-10) codes. The detailed diagnostic codes for these diseases are provided in Table S1. The first CKM disease was defined as the initial diagnosis during follow-up of any of the following conditions: IHD and IS, T2D, or CKD. Double CKM disease was defined as diagnosis of any two of these conditions, while triple CKM disease was defined as diagnosis of three or more CKM conditions [20, 21]. Patients were followed until the onset of diseases, death, or October 31, 2022, whichever occurred first.

Lifestyle factors

Lifestyle factors included smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, dietary, and sleep quality, all of which may influence the progression from NAFLD to CKM disease.

Smoking status was categorized as never, former, or current. Physical inactivity was defined based on self-reported types and frequency of physical activity during the past four weeks [22], with participants considered inactive if they engaged in no or only light activity once per week or less. Healthy sleep was defined as a sleep duration of 7–9 h per day [23]. Dietary quality was evaluated using a 10-component score based on established cardiovascular health guidelines and comparable to the Mediterranean diet [24, 25]. The score incorporated intake of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, fish/shellfish, dairy products, vegetable oils, refined grains, processed and unprocessed meats, and sugar-sweetened beverages. One point was assigned for each component if the participant`s intake met the recommended level.

Covariates

Potential confounding variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex, ethnicity, education level, and socioeconomic status), clinical indicators (blood pressure, BMI, lipid profile, liver enzymes, renal function, and inflammation levels), as well as relevant medication use.

The Townsend Deprivation Index was used to assess the level of socioeconomic deprivation at the regional level. Educational attainment was dichotomized, with favorable education defined as having an academic or other professional degree. Liver function was assessed using enzymes including alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). Lipid profiles included low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and triglycerides (TG). Renal function was evaluated using serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were also recorded as a marker of systemic inflammation.

Statistics analysis

Baseline characteristics were categorized according to tertiles of the TyG index. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, or as median with interquartile range (IQR) if skewed. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages). Between-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed variables, the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables, and the chi-square test for categorical variables.

In this study, multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to investigate the associations between the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and the risk of NAFLD and CKM disease and components. Models were adjusted for key covariates, including age, sex, ethnicity, Townsend Deprivation Index (TDI), educational level, LDL-C, HDL-C, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, BMI, liver enzymes (AST and ALT), eGFR, CRP, and medication use. To evaluate potential nonlinear dose–response relationships, restricted cubic spline regression with four knots was applied.

A multistate Markov model was used to evaluate the risk of transitions from a metabolically healthy state to NAFLD, subsequent progression to CKM disease and multimorbidity [20, 26]. For events occurring on the same day, standard methods were applied by assigning the preceding event 0.5 days before the subsequent one (e.g., CKM diseases 0.5 days before death) [20, 21]. Adjusted transition-specific HRs were estimated to assess the TyG index’s effect on disease progression. Covariates adjusted in the multistate model were consistent with those used in the Cox regression analyses. To elucidate the role of lifestyle factors, we further conducted stratified subgroup analyses. Finally, restricted cubic spline models were applied to assess the dose–response relationship between TyG levels and the risk of disease transition.

To test the robustness of findings, three sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, participants with multiple state transitions on the same day were excluded to reduce timing bias. Second, those who developed outcomes within the first 2 years were excluded to minimize reverse causality. Third, the spectrum of cardiometabolic diseases was expanded to include atrial fibrillation, heart failure, and peripheral vascular diseases (Table S1). The multi-state model was reapplied, and consistency in HRs and confidence intervals was used to assess the reliability of associations.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 377,757 participants free of CKM conditions and NAFLD were included in the final analysis (Fig. S1). The median age was 57 years, and 44.52% were male (Table 1). The proportions of participants with favorable physical activity, diet, and sleep were 91.86%, 25.46%, and 68.41%, respectively. The median TyG index was 8.66. Participants with higher TyG levels tended to have poorer lifestyle behaviors, as well as higher blood pressure, elevated inflammatory markers, and reduced kidney function.

Progression trajectory from NAFLD to CKM disease

Over a median follow-up of 13.6 years, 4949 participants developed NAFLD. Among them, 20.79% progressed to initial CKM disease, 10.53% to double CKM diseases, and 3.23% to triple CKM diseases (Fig. 1A). Mortality risk increased with the number of CKM conditions. Among specific CKMD subtypes, the highest progression risk was observed for T2D (12.65%), followed by IHD (9.11%), CKD (8.63%), and IS (4.61%) (Fig. 1B). Notably, IS was associated with the highest mortality risk.

TyG index and progression trajectory from NAFLD to CKM multimorbidity

Cox regression models revealed that higher TyG levels were significantly associated with increased risks of both CKM multimorbidity and individual CKM components (Table 2). A similar pattern of risk elevation was observed among participants with NAFLD. Restricted cubic spline analyses further indicated that the association between TyG and disease risk generally followed a J-shaped dose–response pattern (Fig. S2).

TyG index and progression trajectory from NAFLD to CKMD components

We further examined the influence of the TyG index on transitions from NAFLD to specific components of CKM disease. Although progression from NAFLD to IS was the least frequent, it demonstrated the strongest risk association with elevated TyG levels. In Trajectory 2, which traced transitions from NAFLD to individual CKM components and subsequently to death, a higher TyG index was significantly associated with an increased risk of progression to IS (HR = 1.70, 95% CI 1.27–2.27; p < 0.001). Elevated TyG also predicted higher risks of progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD) (HR = 1.37, 95% CI 1.08–1.75; p = 0.011) and type 2 diabetes (T2D) (HR = 1.41, 95% CI 1.11–1.78; p = 0.004), while the association with ischemic heart disease (IHD) was marginal (HR = 1.24, 95% CI 0.98–1.56; p = 0.071). Additional analyses based on TyG tertiles showed that, compared with the low TyG group (median 8.15, 95% CI 7.96–8.30), participants with the high TyG level (median 9.23, 95% CI 9.05–9.48) had the greatest risk of transition from NAFLD to ischemic stroke (HR 2.13, 95% CI 1.24–3.69), followed by progression to CKD (HR 1.89, 95% CI 1.17–3.05) (Table S2). Figure 2 illustrates a J-shaped dose–response relationship between the TyG index and the risk of progression from NAFLD to various CKM conditions.

Sensitivity analyses

The adverse impact of the TyG index remained consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses. This included the exclusion of individuals who experienced transitions between disease states on the same day (Table S4), as well as the removal of participants who developed NAFLD or CKM diseases within the first 2 years of follow-up (Table S5). Furthermore, even when the definition of cardiometabolic diseases was broadened, higher TyG levels continued to be associated with an increased risk of transitioning to CKM disease (Table S6). We additionally examined the progression risk from CKM diseases components toward NAFLD (Table S7). We found that higher TyG levels significantly increased the risk of developing NAFLD among individuals with pre-existing IHD (HR 1.45, 95% CI 1.23–1.70) and CKD (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.19–2.22). These results underscore the role of elevated TyG levels in the bidirectional progression of cardiometabolic diseases and further support NAFLD as a central nodal condition within this spectrum.

Discussions

In this community-based prospective cohort, NAFLD patients most frequently progressed to type 2 diabetes (12.65%), followed by ischemic heart disease (9.11%), chronic kidney disease (8.63%), and ischemic stroke (4.61%). Participants with higher TyG levels were statistically more likely to transition from NAFLD to CKM diseases, particularly to IS, which exhibited the highest subsequent mortality rate during follow-up. Elevated TyG levels significantly accelerated the progression from NAFLD to CKM multimorbidity, with progressively higher risks observed as the number of concurrent CKM conditions increased. The adverse effects of elevated TyG levels were more pronounced among individuals who had healthy sleep patterns, engaged in regular physical activity, or reported an unhealthy diet.

CKM diseases have been increasingly recognized as an integrated management concept [1, 2]. Although current guidelines and most prior studies have highlighted T2D as the central metabolic driver of cardiovascular risk [1–3], accumulating evidence indicates that NAFLD—characterized by ectopic fat deposition in the liver—also contributes to systemic metabolic dysregulation [4–6]. NAFLD not only predisposes individuals to T2D but also shows independent associations with cardiovascular and renal outcomes [6–9]. With its rapidly increasing prevalence worldwide, NAFLD has emerged as a major metabolic disorder shaped by economic and lifestyle transitions, with the global prevalence estimated at 65.33%—rising from 55.86% in 1990–2004 to 68.81% in 2016–2021 [10, 11]. These findings underscore the need to explicitly integrate NAFLD into the CKM disease framework. Our study is the first to quantify this trajectory in a community-based population.

Previous studies have established the TyG index as a reliable surrogate marker of insulin resistance and a significant predictor of various individual cardiometabolic diseases, including T2D, CVD, and CKD. For instance, a large prospective cohort study from Korea reported that individuals in the highest TyG quartile had a 3.67-fold increased risk of incident T2D compared to the lowest quartile [27]. Similarly, Da Silva et al. found a positive association between the TyG index and the prevalence of symptomatic CAD [28]. In the context of kidney disease, individuals with a higher TyG index exhibited a significantly greater cumulative incidence of kidney outcomes, and the high-TyG group had a 79% increased risk of CKD progression [29]. While previous studies have shown the value of TyG in predicting single diseases, our study is the first to examine its role in the progression from NAFLD, a liver sign of systemic metabolic dysfunction, to broader cardiovascular, kidney, and metabolic conditions. We found that higher TyG levels may speed up the shift from early liver-related issues to complications in other organs. This suggests that TyG is not only a useful biomarker, but also a possible driver of disease spread across organs in people with NAFLD.

Importantly, although IS was the least frequent initial CKM manifestation, it demonstrated the strongest association with elevated TyG levels and was linked to the highest mortality risk. This finding suggests a distinct high-risk vascular phenotype in individuals with NAFLD. A potential explanation may lie in the fact that chronic insulin resistance induces subclinical injury within the neurovascular unit. It promotes the proinflammatory transformation and metabolic reprogramming of astrocytes, leading to blood–brain barrier disruption and enhanced infiltration of circulating myeloid cells [30]. In parallel, microglia exhibit heightened activation, characterized by NLRP3 inflammasome upregulation and increased fractalkine/CX3CR1 signaling [30, 31]. Beyond glial effects, insulin resistance also drives vascular endothelial dysfunction, including aberrant nuclear migration and aggregation [32], as well as excessive mitochondrial autophagy [33]. Together, these mechanisms may render the brain more vulnerable to ischemic injury, providing a plausible biological basis for the elevated IS risk we observed.

Interestingly, the J-shaped restricted cubic spline analyses in Figs. 2F and S2B indicate that individuals with lower TyG also carry a potential risk of IS, consistent with the concept of reverse epidemiology. In chronic disease–associated catabolic states or long-term malnutrition, reduced nutrient intake, catabolic stress, and impaired metabolic regulation may result in persistently low glucose and lipid levels [34–36], which manifest as lower TyG values. Despite such biochemical profiles, these patients remain at elevated risk of vascular events, including IS, IHD, and CKD. Therefore, integrating TyG with nutritional and frailty assessments may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of vascular risk.

Our analysis further showed that 20.79% of participants transitioned to initial CKM, 10.53% progressed to dual-organ involvement, and 3.23% developed triple-organ multimorbidity. The TyG index emerged as a strong, independent predictor of both incident CKM and accelerated progression to multimorbidity. Previous studies have linked TyG to individual outcomes such as cardiovascular disease [37] and CKD [38], our findings extend this evidence by demonstrating its prognostic utility across CKM combinations. Mechanistically, TyG reflects an integrated signature of insulin resistance and ectopic lipid deposition, cored processes that underlie multi-organ metabolic dysfunction. Particularly, its predictive strength for IS progression exceeded that for other CKM components, suggesting that even moderate elevations in TyG may warrant early cerebrovascular surveillance and intervention.

Moreover, our study revealed a notable phenomenon: among NAFLD patients with unhealthy lifestyle behaviors—such as smoking, physical inactivity, poor dietary patterns, or abnormal sleep duration—the adverse impact of elevated TyG levels on the progression to cardiovascular-metabolic-renal diseases was significantly amplified. Although positive behaviors such as regular exercise and adequate sleep may mitigate some metabolic risk, they may not fully offset the pathological influence of persistently elevated TyG. Among lifestyle components, diet appeared to play a disproportionately important role: diets rich in added sugars and saturated fats sustain hepatic lipotoxicity and insulin resistance, perpetuating TyG elevation and CKM progression [39, 40]. This underscores the importance of dietary modification as a central strategy in halting metabolic deterioration among individuals with NAFLD, even beyond general lifestyle improvement.

This study has several limitations. First, disease definitions were primarily based on ICD codes, lacking imaging and laboratory confirmations, which may affect diagnostic accuracy. However, considering the large sample size of the UK Biobank, it is practically unfeasible to dynamically track a wide range of clinical and biochemical indicators to comprehensively evaluate the progression of CKM syndrome over the life course. Second, lifestyle factors were only measured at baseline, and changes during follow-up were not captured. Nevertheless, baseline assessments may help reduce reverse causality caused by lifestyle changes following disease onset. Third, the UK Biobank cohort consists mainly of individuals of European ancestry and volunteers, who may be healthier than the general population, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings. Finally, as lifestyle data were collected only at baseline and may not fully capture behavioral changes during the 13-year follow-up, potentially leading to misclassification.

Conclusions

Insulin resistance independently accelerated the progression from NAFLD to CKM diseases, particularly ischemic stroke. Baseline healthy sleep and regular physical activity failed to offset this detrimental impact. Longitudinal follow-up is needed to clarify the impact of lifestyle changes on insulin resistance-related adverse outcomes.
