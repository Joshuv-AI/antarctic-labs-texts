Serum markers and gastric cancer: an umbrella review
Authors: Afra Darvishi, Amirhosein Dadashzadeh Asl, Pouya Golshaniniya, Ali Alipour, Amirreza Naseri, Sina Hamzehzadeh, Hanieh Salehi-Pourmehr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeEarly detection of gastric cancer (GC) is crucial for lowering the mortality risk and health burden. Since various biomarkers have been studied for the early diagnosis of GC, this umbrella focuses on the serum markers valuable in GC, for whether detecting or predicting the risk.MethodsFollowing the PRISMA statement and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched before August 31, 2023. English-language systematic reviews that assessed the association between serum markers and the presence of and/or predicting the risk of GC with or without meta-analyses were included in this umbrella review. The chosen reviews assessed serum markers in GC, and the quality assessment was conducted by the JBI critical appraisal tool for systematic reviews.ResultsFrom 1441 records that remained after removing the duplicates, only seven were selected. The investigated serum markers in the studies included SOD activity, selenium, adiponectin dickkopf-1, pepsinogens, and autoantibodies. The bias risk in most of the included reviews was appropriately low. Overall, the SOD activity, circulating adiponectin, and selenium are suggested to be lower in patients with GC. Serum dickkopf-1 and serum pepsinogen are also proposed as potential biomarkers for GC. Considering the great heterogeneity between the studies, the possibility of publication bias, and the limited number of the included studies further investigation is required.ConclusionThe explained serum biomarkers could be helpful for diagnosis and predicting GC; however, further well-designed large-scale investigation is needed to ensure their clinical applications.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most prevalent cancer and fourth most widespread cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1], with the highest occurrence in East Asia [2, 3]. Recent studies indicate a higher risk of all-cause mortalities in females aged 50–59 and in black males [4]. The main reason contributing to GC’s high mortality rate is delayed detection due to an absence of early symptoms [5]. Hence, many patients with early GC do not receive proper treatment until the disease advances significantly [6, 7]. The current 5-year survival rate of people diagnosed with GC is about 70% [8]. Various factors such as environmental aspects, genetics, and unhealthy lifestyles are considered critical causes of cancer [9, 10]. Because early stages of GC are generally curable [11], multiple Asian countries have initiated cancer screening programs [12, 13]. Screening programs should be able to decrease the burden of the disease and have already been administered in high-risk countries such as Japan [11]. The primary methods of GC diagnosis are endoscopy and biopsy [14, 15]. However, these procedures rely on the operator’s expertise and cause patient discomfort [16]. Biomarkers are measurable traits objectively and are evaluated as a characteristic of normal biological processes and pathogenic changes or pharmacological responses to therapeutic interventions [17]. They have advantages such as minimal invasion and repeatability [18]. Currently, some advancements in biomarkers for the early diagnosis and prediction of GC have offered benefits. Tiantian Jiang et al., shed light on the potential of circulating tumor cells (CTCs), long non-coding RNAs, cell-free DNA, microRNAs, and exosomes [19]; however, most serum-based biomarkers are not recommended for GC detection because of the limited specificity and sensitivity in the early stages of GC [20]. Lack of evidence-based approach in previous conducted reviews, suggested an umbrella review, to provide an evidence-based conclusion concerning this topic. Since various systematic reviews have been held to investigate different biomarkers, we aimed to summarize and organize the results of these reviews and create a clear image of all of these biomarkers to offer a one-stop high-level evidence summary for clinicians, therefore this umbrella review desires to collect evidence regarding the potential serum biomarkers of GC.

Methods

This umbrella review was carried out following the methods mentioned in the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Manual for Evidence Synthesis [21] and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement [22].

Eligibility criteria

English-language systematic reviews that studied the association between serum markers and the presence of and/or predicting the risk of GC, in any stages or any subtypes, with or without meta-analyses were included in this umbrella review. Non-systematic reviews, systematic reviews of animal studies, unpublished studies, original studies, and congress-presented articles were excluded.

Information resources and search strategy

PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases were searched for systematic reviews investigating the association between serum markers and GC presence published before August 31, 2023. We used Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and “Stomach Neoplasms” and “Systematic Review” keywords and the synonyms, based on the MeSH database. The search strategy for PubMed was: (“stomach neoplasms“[MeSH Terms] OR (“stomach“[All Fields] AND “neoplasms“[All Fields]) OR “stomach neoplasms“[All Fields] OR “gastric cancer“[All Fields])) AND (“systematic review“[Publication Type] OR “meta-analysis“[Publication Type])). The reference lists of the included articles and the citations were also checked for any possible missed reviews.

Selection process

After removing the duplicates in EndNote 20, all articles were screened in two stages: title/abstract and, if necessary, full-text review by two independent authors (A.N and A.D), and disputes were settled by consensus. If necessary, a third researcher (H.S-P.) adjudicated any unresolved discrepancies and established the study’s exclusion or inclusion.

Data collection process and risk of bias assessment

The data from eligible reviews were independently retrieved by two authors (P.G and A.D) using a standardized data extraction form: author’s name, publication year, search databases, date of last search, detection methods, association between biomarkers and GC, effect metrics, heterogeneity, publication bias, and risk of bias. Two researchers independently evaluated the risk of bias in each study using the JBI Critical Appraisal Tools for systematic reviews [23]. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. A third author (A.D) arbitrated any unresolved conflicts.

Synthesis methods

Following the data extraction, any reported statistics regarding the research question from corresponding reviews were employed to examine the relationship between GC and serum biomarkers and the findings of the reviews were narratively synthesized.

Results

After duplicate removal, 1441 records were found by the systematic search. By scanning the title/abstract and the full texts, [24–31], if necessary, seven articles were finally selected for our umbrella review and one study was excluded in this stage [24]. Results of risk of bias assessments

Assessing the risk of bias via the JBI appraisal checklist for systematic reviews, two reviews [25, 27] met all the criteria mentioned in the checklist. The study conducted by Wei, Ye et al. needed to be clarified on two out of eleven questions (The appropriation of criteria for appraising reviews and recommendations for policy and practice) [30]. The study conducted by Liang et al. met nine of the questions correctly but needed to be more explicit about the methods to minimize data extraction errors and appropriately present specific directions for future research [28]. The study by Huang, Yu et al. answered seven questions correctly but needed to be more explicit about the methods to minimize data extraction, and the clarity of the review question, the inclusion criteria, and the proper search strategy still needed to be met [26]. The study conducted by Terasawa, Nishida et al. met five of the questions, but the other study did not meet the first three questions, and the critical appraisal was not held by two reviewers independently; the utilized methods to combine the studies were not convenient, and the likelihood of publication biased was not evaluated [29]. The last study by Werner et al. met four of the questions properly. Still, the appropriation of inclusion criteria and search strategy, the sufficiency of the resources for search, and the assessment of the probability of publication bias still needed to be met in this study [31]. Also, the study required more precise information about the appropriation of methods to combine studies, appraisal criteria, and whether the appraisal was done by two reviewers independently. Superoxide dismutase activity

A study completed on the significance of SOD activity in GC showed its capability to be used as a diagnostic and monitoring biomarker. Patients with GC demonstrated reduced activity of SOD compared to healthy people. There was notable heterogeneity in studies because of unmatched gender and age in some included original studies. In detail, subgroup analyses based on the distribution of SOD in erythrocytes and male participants were associated with decreased heterogeneity. Also, sensitivity analysis was performed by eliminating a survey, which was not associated with a significant change in the pooled estimate. In addition, the authors reported differences in methods of measurement for both SOD activity and clinical outcomes as a limitation of evidence. This study covers all the quality checklist questions properly [27].

Selenium

Regarding the link between the serum level of selenium and GC, a meta-analysis showed that serum selenium might have an inverse association with GC. The heterogeneity was reported to be high in the study and in multiple tries to address the source, subgroup analysis based on the duration of follow-up (less than 5 years and more than 5 years) was associated with decreased heterogeneity; while, study design, race, sex, sample collection, and smoking did not report to affect the heterogeneity. This study covers all the quality checklist questions properly and the risk was estimated by OR, based on the quartiles of selenium levels [25].

Adiponectin

Adiponectin, derived from adipocytes, is a potential biomarker for early diagnosis of various cancers, as its levels are decreased in cancer patients. Tai Wei et al.’s study, aimed to investigate the association between circulating adiponectin levels and carcinogenesis and found only three case-control studies that reported the association between adipocytes and GC, two of them reported significantly lower levels in patients with GC in the Japanese population [32, 33], however, the other study in Turkish population, did not find a significant association in this regard [34]. Considering the limited number of the included studies, this study did not perform a meta-analysis for GC and only the lower levels of adiponectin in patients with various cancers were reported. The study meets 9 out of 11 questions on the checklist, considered a highly qualified one [30].

Dickkopf-1

Serum dickkopf-1 is also suggested as another potential biomarker for early diagnosis of GC. Bin Liang et al. aimed to dissect the diagnostic value of dickkopf-1 in gastrointestinal cancers, and in subgroup of three studies of GC, dickkopf-1 was found to be better biomarker for GC than hepatocellular carcinoma and pancreatic cancer. This study could be regarded as valid because of answers to 9 out of 11 questions on the JBI checklist for quality assessment [28].

Pepsinogens

A study carried out the serum pepsinogen as a possible biomarker for GC. Based on 15 studies, serum pepsinogens were reported to have great potential as a noninvasive, screening tool in GC. Since the heterogeneity and publication bias were notable, the results are not confident. To address the source of heterogeneity, study sample size was suggested as a major source of heterogeneity; however, the study design, detection method, and measurement of serum pepsinogens were not sources of heterogeneity based on performed meta-regression. This study answers 7/11 checklist questions [26].

Antibodies

Serum autoantibodies are the other possible biomarkers for early GC diagnosis. Based on a systematic review conducted by Simone Werner et al., single autoantibodies showed a high specificity (median: 99.15%) but low sensitivity (median: 12.35%). P53, NY-ESO-1 (CTAG1B), CTAG2, DDX53, MAGEC1, MAGEA3, Astrocyte Elevated Gene-1 (AEG-1), and GRP78 were evaluated. Early detection of GC with assessment of P53, as the most evaluated factor, as well as NY-ESO-1 (CTAG1B) and AEG-1, were suggested. The study meets 4 of the questions presented in the JBI critical appraisal checklist for systematic reviews [31].

Predicting the risk of GC

Teruhiko Terasawa et al.‘s systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies revealed the significance of pepsinogen the predicting the development of GC in Eastern Asians. Since this study covers 5 of 11 questions in the JBI critical appraisal checklist and the publication bias might exist, the results may be attributed to a high risk of bias. The researchers also investigated the predictive value of Helicobacter pylori seropositivity in Eastern Asians and found the potential to stratify middle-aged presumptively healthy adults [29].

Discussion

In this umbrella review, we synthesized the results of mainly seven systematic reviews on the association of GC and serum biomarkers. Among several studied biomarkers, the limited evidence suggested that SOD activity, selenium, circulating adiponectin, dickkopf-1, pepsinogens, Helicobacter pylori Seropositivity, and autoantibodies (mainly P53, NY-ESO-1 (CTAG1B) and AEG-1) may have the potential as GC biomarkers. As a considerable limitation of the evidence, most of the included meta-analyses in this study were associated with a high level of heterogeneity which harms the validity of the findings. Significant heterogeneity between the studies highlights a considerable variation in the included original studies [35], which can be attributed to multiple factors including sex [27], duration of follow-up [25], and sample size [26]; which suggest more studies on this topic to reach for evidence-based clinical and/or policy-making recommendations.

Gastrointestinal cancer presents an important cause of malignancy-related mortality in the world due to the absence of specific and sensitive screening techniques. Early diagnosis of gastrointestinal cancers is vital for patient prognosis. Thus, a large number of studies on the search for ideal biomarkers for gastrointestinal cancers are still ongoing [28]. Despite the reduced incidence rate of GC observed all over the world, its prognosis remains poor. To effectively improve the survival rate of GC, improved screening tools for early diagnosis of GC and the identification of people at high risk must be developed [36]. Endoscopy is a well-known tool for screening and diagnosing GC [37]; however, in addition to the risks such as infection and perforation, there are considerable concerns regarding the cost-effectiveness of upper endoscopy for GC screening in the general population [38]. Double-contrast upper gastrointestinal barium radiography (UGI-XR) is also an easy-to-access and painless screening tool; however, misdiagnosis due to multiple factors, including gastric lumen filling, barium concentration, affect its detection rate and restricted usefulness in GC screening [39]. In this condition, histopathology remains the most remarkable tool for diagnosing GC, with significant immunohistochemical parameters [40]. Although the data is limited, using the biomarkers for detecting GC is easy to access and it is not associated with considerable harm for the patients which contributes to clinical application. Current evidence does not declare the definite roles of the biomarkers and future diagnostic investigations might produce the essential significance for clinical application.

Evidence suggested reduced SOD activity, an enzyme that plays a crucial role in neutralizing the harmful effects of free radicals, as a potential screening tool for GC [27]. This finding might be explained by decreased antioxidant levels that increase osmotic fragility. In-vitro evidence reported increased SOD activity in malignant gastric adenocarcinoma tissues [41]. Dincer et al. offered that the change in energy metabolism and the production of oxygen-derived free radicals from dysfunctional mitochondria in malignant GCs affect SOD activity and that decreased activity might be interpreted as a changed expression of the genes which encode SODs during tumorigenesis. Raised ROS generation, resulting from either an abnormal tissue function or external stimulus, might cause the accumulation of SOD as a physiological protective mechanism [42]. Assessment of SOD activity has garnered increasing interest in diagnosing patients with GC, particularly in the context of H. pylori infection [43]; however, there is a necessity for further studies to determine the specific mechanisms causing changes in enzyme activity and assessing the definitive diagnostic value. On the other side, changes in SOD activity in different medical conditions [44–46], may result in considerable false-positive cases as a screening tool in GC in the real world.

Gong HY et al. found an inverse association between selenium level and GC risk [25]. Previous studies declare that the serum level of selenium remarkably was associated with the location of GC [47]. As an important cofactor for several antioxidant enzymes, recent evidence suggested a U-shaped association between selenium intake and the risk of multiple cancers, including the GC [48]. Animal studies suggested that supplementation with vitamins and selenium led to H. pylori recovery from 17% of challenged animals, compared to 43% of those fed a control diet [49]. However, in a recent review, the researchers suggested that the effects of selenium supplementation on cancer risk were inconsistent, and no persuasive evidence proposed that selenium supplements may help prevent cancer in humans [50]; however, considering the latest evidence [51], there is still considerable limitation in the evidence for recommendation selenium as a detecting marker in GC.

Adiponectin, an adipokine synthesized by adipose tissue, levels tend to decrease as the tumor stage develops in GC [32]. Findings show that adiponectin is not only correlated with cancer risk but also associated with tumor progression [30]. Circulating adiponectin levels are influenced by several factors, including dietary factors, medicines, inflammatory conditions, genetic factors, and hormonal changes. A possible explanation for reduced adiponectin levels in malignancies is the sustained inflammatory status of patients leading to reduced proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, which are all reported to suppress adiponectin transcription and translation in adipocytes [52, 53]. The expression levels of AdipoRs range from malignant tissues to their peritumoral regular counterparts. The upregulation of AdipoR1 and AdipoR2 is reported in GC [54]. Yabushita et al. concluded that poor expression of AdipoR1 is correlated with lymph node metastasis and tumor invasion [55]. Adiponectin inhibits leptin-induced metastasis by downregulating the JAK/STAT3 pathway, showing an inverse correlation with cancer development. Adiponectin-induced phosphorylation of p53, a tumor suppressor, renders cell cycle apoptosis and arrest in cancer cell lines [56, 57]. Considering the limitation of the evidence, the diagnostic value of adiponectin in GC is still controversial which should be addressed in future research.

Dickkopf-1, one of the four members of a family of secreted extracellular Wnt inhibitors, blocks signaling from plasma membrane Wnt-receptor complexes [58–60]. Because the Wnt pathway plays an essential role in cancer, dickkopf-1 may also play a crucial role in cancer. dickkopf-1 messenger RNA is normally expressed at low levels in most normal human tissues except the placenta [61, 62]. Recent studies have suggested that an elevated serum dickkopf-1 level could serve as a valuable biomarker in cancer diagnosis. While the diagnostic role of dickkopf-1 is not yet fully understood, its potential accuracy for detecting gastrointestinal cancers is intriguing and warrants further investigation [28, 63]. The limited available evidence proposes the potential application of serum dickkopf-1 as a biomarker for GC diagnosis.

Serum pepsinogen level is highly correlated with both predicting and detecting GC [26, 29]. Serum pepsinogen is a biomarker used to identify atrophic gastritis, a gastric precancerous lesion, and its potential utility in the diagnosis of gastrointestinal diseases has been proved by numerous studies [64–66]. Subsequently, cancer screening programs in Japan have accepted the serum pepsinogen measurement as a noninvasive screening test for GC [67]. The Prostate, Lung, Colorectal, and Ovarian (PLCO) Cancer Screening Trial in America, also suggested positive pepsinogen to identify individuals at higher risk of non-cardia GC [68]. Regarding the relationship between serum pepsinogen or pepsinogen Ⅰ/Ⅱ ratio and GC, conflicting results have been published in recent studies [69–73]. Given the current inconsistency in research findings, there is a pressing need for additional high-quality studies to fully understand the relationship between GC and serum levels of pepsinogen. Different methods for assessing serum pepsinogen and various cut-offs make clinicians unsure about considering it as a valuable biomarker in GC. In addition, a combination of serum pepsinogen with other markers is also a novel field of interest. Susumu Aikou et al. showed that the combination of serum tetrathiafulvalene-3 (TFF-3) and serum pepsinogen statistically significantly improved tumor diagnosis as compared to TFF-3 or serum pepsinogen alone [74]. A combination of pepsinogens and Helicobacter pylori also show promise as biomarkers of GC risk [75].

Evidence shows that antibodies against tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) such as p53, AEG-1, and NY-ESO-1 (CTAG1B) are known to be overexpressed in GC [31]. These markers also showed potential as a prognostic marker in GC [76–79]. Other studies [80, 81] have also found similar results, which increase the validity of using these antigens. The use of a combination of autoantibodies can highly improve the sensitivity and specificity of GC detection. Further research is necessary to understand the correlation between autoantibody combinations and tumor diagnosis, type, and metastasis. The abundance of autoantibodies in patients with cancer might be explained by mutation, overexpression, aberrant expression, or abnormal posttranslational modification of the corresponding tumor-associated antigens [10, 82, 83]. Detection of autoantibodies, even at early stages, confirms the high potential of autoantibodies as early detection markers for GC despite their generally low sensitivity. It is possible to combine autoantibody markers and tumor markers, such as CEA or carbohydrate antigen19-9, to enhance the accuracy of early detection. This underscores the clinical relevance of autoantibodies in the early diagnosis of GC.

Best of our knowledge, the current study is the only try for a comprehensive analysis of the detecting and/or predicting serum markers of GC, based on the highest level of evidence. The prognostic role of serum markers in GC was previously investigated in an umbrella review. Cen Zhou et al. [84] analyzed the association between different serum biomarkers as prognostic markers of GC and reported that the association between platelet count and GC overall survival is supported by solid evidence and the potential of Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC), Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), Neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR), foxp3 + Treg lymphocytes (both 1- and 3-year OS), Carbohydrate antigen 19 − 9 (CA 19 − 9), and Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) as prognostic markers of GC is also suggested.

Other serum biomarkers that have not been evaluated in the systematic review studies can also be effective in diagnosing GC. The circular RNA hsa_circ_0000190, programmed death-ligand 1 positivity (PD-L1), expression of minichromosome maintenance complex component 7 (MCM7), Human leukocyte antigen G (HLA-G), macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), SERPINB5, tRF-27-FDXXE6XRK45, cholinesterase, and gastrin [85–94] are some of the suggested markers which should be further evaluated in both original and systematic review studies. As one of the important examples of the studies, which was out of our scope to be included in this umbrella review, Camargo MC et al. identified increased GC risks among women with exposure to the antiestrogenic agent tamoxifen and decreased risk with a longer duration of fertility or exposure to hormone replacement therapy [24]. Tamoxifen, as a selective estrogen receptor modulator, has both estrogenic (e.g., bone) and antiestrogenic (e.g., breast tissue) effects [95]. In a mouse model of GC, tamoxifen upregulated estrogen-responsive pathways and prevented GC development [96]. Chronic infection with Helicobacter pylori is the primary reason for GC. World Health Organization (WHO) has designated this bacterium a Class I carcinogen [97]. Numerous evidential findings claim that estrogens may protect against GC, such as (I) estrogens increase expression of a gene family called trefoil factor encoding products that protect the gastric mucosa from exogenous and endogenous insults [98]; (II) estrogens enhance the immune response to bacterial pathogens by direct blocking the expression of caspase-12 [99]; (III) estrogens interact with receptors in various forms of normal, precancerous, and cancerous gastric cells [100, 101]; (IV) estrogens increase apoptosis in human GC cells in vitro [102]; (V) CpG islands in the estrogen receptor gene promoters become hypermethylated with aging, causing reduced expression with effects on tumor suppressor activity [103] and these relate to the unraveled role of estrogen in GC. Direct evaluation of the levels of estrogen and various drugs altering the estrogen receptors could be a novel direction for further investigation [24].

The umbrella review is essentially a collection of separate evidence summaries rather than an integrated analysis of a single question. This study provides evidence for the significance of specific biomarkers in GC prediction and detection. Nevertheless, the evidence has considerable limitations including, small number of the included reviews, a high level of heterogeneity, and possible publication bias, therefore the results should be interpreted carefully. In addition, there are considerable concerns regarding the generalizability of the findings of the included systematic reviews. More well-designed large-scale studies in different populations must be conducted to achieve a definite conclusion. In addition, naturally, this umbrella review relies on the quality of included systematic reviews and original evidence, which can be another source of different types of bias. Lack of prospective registration of the study was another limitation. Reporting different effect metrics in the included reviews limits the possibility of comparing the findings. In addition, included reviews were published In 2019 or earlier, and their conclusions might be superseded by more recent findings, which were not assessed in more-updated systematic reviews. On the other hand, the exclusion of non-English papers was our limitation during the review process.

Conclusion

Evidence elucidated that some biomarkers, including SOD activity, selenium, circulating adiponectin, dickkopf-1, pepsinogens, Helicobacter pylori Seropositivity, and autoantibodies (mainly P53, NY-ESO-1 (CTAG1B) and AEG-1) have potential for application early diagnosis of GC. While this umbrella review summarizes the available data for potential serum biomarkers, the limitations and lack of solid data for many biomarkers provide room for more high-quality original studies and reviews, to reach the goal of finding clinically applicable biomarkers for GC early detection. Future well-designed original and reviews, can give strength the evidence on this topic. By investigating more on the appropriate plasma biomarkers for GC, a great contribution to diagnostic medicine is made, as we hope to see this improvement in diagnostic tools, affecting the life of patients suffering from GC.
