Pricing Policy for Medical Oxygen and Potential Savings

Introduction

Medical oxygen is recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO) as an essential medicine with no substitute [1]. Although its use is vital in health care, its production and storage have been a challenge since the beginning of its utilization in the 18th and 19th centuries and even more so when this treatment was used on a large scale on the battlefields of the First World War [2, 3, 4, 5]. Today, access to medical oxygen is still challenging and very inequitable as highlighted by the recent Lancet Global Health Commission on medical oxygen security [6].

Medical oxygen plays a crucial role in the treatment of various medical conditions, ranging from surgical interventions to chronic disease management, obstetric complications, and clinical management of pneumonia, the leading causes of mortality in children under 5 years of age. Recent health crises, such as COVID‐19, have accentuated the need to improve the efficiency and sustainability of medical oxygen supply chains [1, 7, 8, 9, 10]. However, the pricing policy and costs associated with the production, distribution, and supply of medical oxygen not only influence the management of health facilities but also the access and quality of care provided to patients. Furthermore, medical oxygen expenditures represent a significant portion of the overall budget of health facilities, impacting financial and operational decisions. Controlling these costs is therefore crucial to ensure the economic viability of health services while guaranteeing equitable and quality access to this essential treatment [11, 12, 13].

In Quebec's healthcare system, the procurement of medical oxygen is shaped by a quasi‐monopolistic (i.e., oligopoly) market structure dominated by a few suppliers, primarily Praxair by Linde, Air Liquide, and Vitalaire for current contracts. These companies are the main suppliers of medical oxygen to public healthcare facilities, which explains their central role in the current procurement landscape. Generally, health sector procurement is centrally oriented toward achieving the lowest possible price, often through volume consolidation strategies and the use of group purchasing organizations (GPOs) [14, 15]. This procurement approach is further constrained by a strict legal and regulatory framework specific to Quebec, which limits institutional flexibility and reinforces cost‐driven decisions at the expense of value‐based purchasing [16, 17]. These factors deeply influence pricing mechanisms, contractual arrangements, and the competitive environment within the public healthcare system.

Despite its importance, there is however, little comprehensive research on medical oxygen pricing policies in Canada, particularly in Quebec, as well as on their financial impact on health institutions. This lack of detailed data poses challenges for making informed decisions regarding the supply and management of medical oxygen costs. This research aims to fill this gap by describing the pricing policy practiced for medical oxygen in the public healthcare system in Quebec. The main objective was to analyze the current structure of pricing policies and to understand the price formation process in this sector, by identifying the actors involved and the existing variations. In addition, this research aimed to reveal the particularities of the policies, costs and operational dynamics related to the supply of medical oxygen in Quebec. Achieving this objective made it possible to provide concrete recommendations that can guide public policies and improve the management of the medical oxygen supply, thus contributing to better preparation and response to future health care needs.

Methods

For this study, we used a triangulation methodology combining a literature review, an analysis of administrative and financial documents, as well as administrative data collected from key stakeholders in the public Quebec health and social services network. This approach allows us to corroborate information from different sources and provide a more complete and nuanced vision of the problem studied.

The literature review was narrative and non‐exhaustive, focusing on relevant sources identified by specific criteria. The databases used included Embase, MEDLINE, CINAHL, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. The use of the thesaurus was favored when applicable, in conjunction with Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) and the truncation strategy (∗) to ensure a precise and relevant search (e.g., Oxygen AND Supply AND (Price OR Pricing policy)). The search also included grey literature, consulting Canadian government agency websites, specialized databases (OpenGrey, Grey Literature Report, GreyNet International), Praxair, Air Liquide, as well as institutional archives of university libraries. References of included studies were reviewed to identify relevant articles not initially captured.

Inclusion criteria for this study were: research articles, systematic reviews, meta‐analyses, editorials, research dissertations, and grey literature; publications in French, English or Spanish; inclusion period was from January 2008 to December 2024; and articles focusing specifically on pricing policy or direct and indirect costs related to the production, distribution and supply of medical oxygen in Canada in particular, but also outside Canada. Recent data on potential solutions for oxygen savings were also analyzed. Exclusion criteria included: articles not explicitly addressing medical oxygen in the context of healthcare; studies that do not provide specific information on the pricing policy, costs or price formation process for medical oxygen; and publications prior to 2008.

Requests for access to medical oxygen supply and consumption documents were made to the main health institutions in Quebec, as well as a search of the financial documents available on the website of the Quebec Ministry of Health and Social Services (MSSS). In many cases, these documents were refused due to legal confidentiality clauses relating to prices and quantities consumed. As a result, the data obtained do not provide an exhaustive picture of the situation, but they do reveal the broad outlines of the current pricing policy in Quebec.

In parallel, a form about medical oxygen supply processes was sent to key players in Quebec. This form was completed by two representatives of supply management in university health institutions (including one institution specialized in pneumology), two clinicians specialized in respiratory care, and a member of the Government Acquisition Centre. If the data provided was incomplete, email exchanges could be requested to get more details. Participants sought permission to provide information from their managers. The questions were structured around several core themes in the supply of medical oxygen in Quebec and the pricing policy practiced. Specifically, these themes were: procurement procedures, pricing mechanisms, contractual arrangements, delivery and storage logistics, and perceived barriers to optimal oxygen supply management. The information was collected between December 2023 and March 2024.

The information collected by these different collection strategies was organized by themes to facilitate comparison. To do this, we used the conventional content analysis framework of Hsieh and Shannon [18]. Coding categories were applied and were revised to ensure their consistency and completeness, then emerging themes were identified by grouping similar codes. While some analytical categories were informed by preliminary readings and the administrative data (e.g., procurement and pricing), the final themes presented in the results emerged through an inductive process using conventional content analysis. This approach allowed us to refine our interpretation while remaining open to unexpected or emerging insights.

Clinical data that were used to discuss potential savings with changes in medical oxygen supply and use processes come from a clinical study conducted by one of the co‐authors and was granted ethics approval from his institution (Ethics Board of the Institut Universitaire de Cardiologie et de Pneumologie de Québec‐Université Laval #2023‐4002). Key stakeholders who completed the form were informed that information could be made public. Anonymity of participants was also guaranteed.

Results

This section presents the main results of our analysis and is divided into four parts. It begins with a brief literature review that contextualizes the current challenges and strategies related to medical oxygen. The second part compares medical oxygen costs across regions, with a focus on Quebec. The third part highlights potential cost savings from improved oxygen therapy practices. Lastly, we present findings from a content analysis of contracts and administrative data, identifying key factors influencing pricing and procurement.

Literature Review

The COVID‐19 pandemic has highlighted major challenges in medical oxygen production, storage, and distribution. This review examines production methods, costs, pricing regulations, and challenges in medical oxygen distribution, focusing on lessons learned and recommendations for improving future supply.

There are several methods for producing and storing medical oxygen (Figure 1). Air Separation Units (ASUs) use cryogenic distillation to separate air into its components, producing oxygen with a purity of 99.5% to 99.8%. These plants can produce up to 5000 tons of oxygen per day, mainly for industries, but also crucial for hospitals [19, 20, 21]. Pressure Swing Adsorption (PSA) units use molecular sieves to adsorb nitrogen from compressed air, allowing oxygen to pass through, with a purity ranging from 93% to 99%. Suitable for hospital facilities, PSAs are particularly useful during periods of high demand [21].

Oxygen produced by ASUs is stored as a liquid in vacuum‐insulated cryogenic tanks. These tanks maintain oxygen at high pressure and extremely low temperatures to keep it in a liquid state, and transport is done via specialized tanker trucks, certified for medical use, which deliver the oxygen to healthcare facilities where it is vaporized for use in gaseous form. In contrast, the oxygen produced by PSA units is typically stored in buffer tanks. This oxygen can be delivered directly to patients via central medical piping systems or compressed into oxygen cylinders (i.e., cylinders) for mobile or emergency use. PSA systems require regular maintenance management to ensure the purity and pressure of the stored oxygen [21].

Installation and operating costs vary considerably between different oxygen sources. PSA systems are particularly cost‐effective when operating at full capacity. Their efficiency and low operational cost make them suitable for medium‐sized hospitals where continuous and reliable production is required. In contrast, ASUs, although more expensive to install and operate, are better suited for large‐scale production. ASUs are capable of producing very large volumes of high‐purity oxygen, making them essential for large medical centers and high‐demand regions. These facilities require a large initial investment due to the complexity of cryogenic distillation technology, as well as the high energy costs to maintain the low temperatures required for gas separation [12, 21].

The COVID‐19 pandemic has revealed major logistical challenges for the supply of medical oxygen in several countries, including Canada, where the Northwest Territories and Manitoba have had to increase their orders and face shortages of medical devices [22]. In India, transportation and storage problems have led to shortages and a black market, requiring emergency measures such as the conversion of industrial facilities [8, 13]. In Bangladesh and some sub‐Saharan African countries, WHO and UNICEF have deployed oxygen concentrators and funded equipment to increase local production [23, 24]. Médecins Sans Frontières has also set up oxygen plants in countries such as Yemen, Burkina Faso and Haiti to ensure continued supply [25]. These examples show that oxygen shortages and logistical challenges are global problems requiring international efforts to strengthen the resilience of health systems to future health crises [26].

Medical oxygen prices vary depending on several factors, such as billing practices, internal policies, contractual clauses, and distribution terms. Production costs are influenced by the method used, each requiring specific equipment and energy resources. The cost of distribution depends on the distance and taxes associated with transportation, while oxygen storage, requiring pressurized containers, also adds to the total cost. In addition, limited competition in the medical gas market can lead to higher prices due to the ability of producers to set prices without significant competition [8, 27, 28].

The literature review reveals that medical oxygen is a critical resource whose efficient management is essential, particularly in times of crisis. During emergencies, sudden spikes in demand—such as pandemics, mass casualty incidents, or natural disasters—can quickly overwhelm supply systems, and bulk oxygen infrastructure may fail if manufacturing or delivery is disrupted [29]. Although the COVID‐19 pandemic highlighted this issue, similar challenges have been documented during influenza pandemics and mass casualty events, where oxygen supply systems struggled to keep up with demand [30]. On‐site production methods such as PSA facilities, price regulations, and technological innovations are essential to ensure adequate and continuous supply. Better coordination between governments and oxygen conservation strategies can alleviate shortages and improve responses to future health crises. This information provides a global vision of the operational and financial dynamics linked to medical oxygen.

Medical Oxygen Costs and International Comparison

The available information suggests that medical oxygen prices vary considerably by region and distribution method (Figure 2). This assessment from our literature review is also supported by the recent publication of the Lancet Global Health Commission on medical oxygen security [6]. However, the method used by this commission to approximate medical oxygen costs significantly attenuates the true disparity by providing costs by major regions, extrapolating some of their data, and omitting to provide data for North America (see section 2.2.3. of Appendix 1 in Graham et al. [6]). It shows that the average cost per cubic meter of medical oxygen ranges from USD 0.8 (South Asia) to USD 5.1 (Sub‐Saharan Africa) in 2024 [6]. However, in the United States, the price of bulk oxygen was estimated to be much lower, at around USD 0.33 per cubic meter, while in China, the price was also lower than its reference group at USD 0.46 per cubic meter. These figures are based on recent commercial data, using an oxygen density of 1.429 kg/m3 under standard conditions, and published in December 2023 and September 2024 [31]. However, this commercial data poorly reflects prices that can be much higher depending on the mode of medical oxygen distribution and the context [32]. For example, in Rwanda, the cost of cylinder (or bottle) oxygen is much higher, at around USD 53 per cubic meter, according to a 2020 study, reflecting the supply and logistics challenges in low‐resource settings [33]. And during the COVID‐19 pandemic, the price for a cylinder in India was able to reach peaks of over USD 1355 [32], which is approximately USD 200 per cubic meter for a J cylinder, reflecting a 10‐fold increase in the black market price in several developing countries [34]. Although cylinder prices are not directly comparable to bulk prices, these differences are notable and highlight the importance of supply infrastructure.

Estimated worldwide variability in oxygen cost in US dollars/m3. Prices are for 2024 (white bars) [6, 25], except for Rwanda [27] and India [26] where they were collected during the SARS‐Cov 2 pandemic in 2020 (black bars).

In Quebec, data on medical oxygen prices are not always publicly available. However, the information collected and the analysis of administrative and financial documents made it possible to establish an apparent (or facial) price in 2024 and for the next 5 years at USD 0.19 (exchange rate of CAD 1.35 to USD 1) per cubic meter for hospitals filling their oxygen tanks. However, it is necessary to add to this price the cost of renting storage tanks, which doubles the real price of bulk medical oxygen, for a total price of USD 0.41 per cubic meter. This figure is consistent with data from other high‐income countries regarding bulk prices, although transparency in pricing practices remains an issue. However, for the price per cubic meter when medical oxygen is supplied in cylinders in Quebec, the weighted average that we were able to calculate on nearly 9,500 cylinders of different formats consumed was USD 5.82, that is more than 14 times the bulk price, and this is without considering the rental costs of cylinders that are not always returned to suppliers on time (i.e., additional delay costs that we were unable to measure, but which constitute a significant additional cost according to the stakeholders' responses).

Potential Cost Savings From Optimizing Oxygen Therapy

Practices optimizing oxygen consumption are a promising avenue for reducing costs in healthcare settings. Recent studies, including those by Nielsen et al. [35] and Poder et al. [36], demonstrate that adjusting oxygenation targets can have a direct impact on the amount of oxygen consumed. Interestingly, SpO2 targets recommended for hypoxemic patients vary from 92 ± 2% to 96 ± 2% [37, 38]. For example, Nielsen et al. showed that reducing oxygenation targets in the care of COVID‐19 patients could generate significant cost savings by reducing oxygen overuse without compromising clinical outcomes [35]. In a recent study, Lellouche et al. [39] demonstrated that adjusting oxygen saturation targets (SpO2) and choosing specific pulse oximeters can reduce oxygen consumption by up to 15‐fold, representing significant potential for cost savings in healthcare settings, particularly by optimizing oxygen therapy management (Figure 3). Recommendations of SpO2 targets of 90%–94% in low‐ and middle‐income countries have been recommended recently [40]. This oxygenation target is a reasonable trade‐off even if targets could be higher in patients with dark skin pigmentation [41]. In addition, the use of automated oxygen therapy systems, such as the FreeO2, allows for better control of oxygen delivery, improving the efficiency of care while reducing consumption [39, 42, 43]. These benefits may be further increased with the utilization in closed‐loop devices of SpO2 sensors that do not underestimate SpO2 [39, 44].

Impact of pulse oximeters calibration and SpO2 target on potential oxygen savings. In addition to savings related to oxygen production/transport (leakage), oxygen storage (leakage), oxygen monitoring/protocols to provide oxygen only when needed, other clinical solutions exist to significantly reduce oxygen consumption. When pulse oximeters are available, the choice of pulse oximeters and the choice of the SpO2 target greatly influences the oxygen consumption (expressed here in the lifespan of an E cylinder [33]). The duration of an oxygen cylinder E can easily be calculated with the following formula: duration (in minutes) = (Cylinder Pressure (psi) × Cylinder Factor) ÷ Flow Rate (L/min). The cylinder factor for Type E cylinder is 0.28. This type of oxygen cylinder contains ~700 liters of oxygen at full capacity with filling pressures at 2200 PSI (pounds per square inch). The oxygen flow for the combination of SpO2 target and pulse oximeter were found in Lellouche et al. study [33].

In an economic analysis by Poder et al. [36], automated oxygen therapy in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) showed substantial cost‐saving potential. This technology not only reduced the amount of oxygen used, but also allowed for optimization of hospital resources, reducing the need for frequent manual interventions by medical staff. The study found a cost reduction of 20.7%, representing an average savings of USD 2192 per patient over a 180‐day period. This highlights the economic and clinical effectiveness of this technology, especially when used for patients hospitalized for acute COPD exacerbations [36].

Content Analysis

The results of the content analysis combine administrative information and the various documents consulted. The main themes that emerged from this analysis include the variability of medical oxygen prices, the factors influencing these prices and the procedures for purchasing and managing contracts, including the rental of equipment and facilities required for oxygen distribution.

Theme 1: Price Variability

The analysis revealed that medical oxygen prices vary little between health care facilities in Quebec. Indeed, prices mainly depend on the grouping of facilities to obtain better purchasing power, although certain specific gases can be purchased individually by each facility according to their specific needs. Medical oxygen prices are thus set at the provincial level through a public call for tenders managed by the Government Acquisition Centre, which allows for a certain uniformity. However, marginal differences may still exist depending on suppliers and regions.

Medical oxygen prices can also vary within a facility based on billing practices and internal policies. For example, some facilities charge private clinics for the use of medical gases, but this practice is not uniform and depends on the facilities' internal policies. As a result, private clinics using hospital infrastructure may be billed differently, leading to cost variations within a facility.

Theme 2: Factors Affecting Prices

Contractual clauses allow for price adjustments based on market fluctuations. In addition, medical gas contracts include annual increases of 2%–3%.

Delivery terms, such as order format and distribution frequency, also influence costs. Prices vary based on the quantity ordered and the distribution format. For example, the cost of delivering cylinders may be different than in bulk (i.e., in a liquid tank oxygen for commercial or industrial storage).

Another important factor is that the medical gas market is characterized by limited competition, which affects prices. The market can therefore be described as oligopolistic, with little competition between suppliers. In Quebec, there are only three suppliers and the consolidation of company activities, such as the acquisition of Praxair by Linde, has further reduced competition.

Theme 3: Procurement and Contract Procedures

The Government Acquisition Centre plays a central role in managing medical oxygen supply contracts. Contracts are negotiated by this agency for all public health facilities, allowing for economies of scale and centralized management.

The rental of equipment and facilities, such as gas cylinders and storage tanks, represents an additional cost for facilities. Most facilities process the supply of oxygen and the rental of equipment on two separate purchase orders, which does not facilitate visibility of real prices. In addition, several facilities do not properly manage cylinders, which results in additional costs in rental fees. Empty cylinders are therefore not returned to suppliers on time, which generates unnecessary daily rental costs.

In summary, the analysis of information provided by representatives and administrative and financial documents shows that the variability of medical oxygen prices is influenced by factors such as contractual clauses, delivery terms, facility management, and limited competition. The centralization of purchases by the Government Acquisition Centre allows for competitive price negotiations, although variations remain depending on the internal practices of facilities and market conditions. Annual price adjustments are common, and flexibility in orders allows facilities to manage their specific needs while benefiting from the advantages of centralization. In addition, contracts often include the rental of equipment and facilities required for oxygen distribution, which adds an additional dimension to overall costs.

Discussion

This study aimed to understand the variability of medical oxygen prices in Quebec by identifying the main factors influencing prices and examining purchasing and contract management procedures. The results of the analysis revealed variability in medical oxygen prices between and within healthcare facilities, influenced by factors such as contractual clauses, distribution terms, and limited competition. Centralization of purchases by the Government Acquisition Centre and equipment rental also play a crucial role.

Price variability, both inter‐ and intra‐facilities, complicates cost standardization and budget planning. Contractual clauses help stabilize prices despite market fluctuations, but distribution terms and limited competition remain major challenges.

Our results are consistent with several studies that also show a significant influence of contractual clauses and limited competition (oligopoly) on medical gas prices [8, 12, 45]. The COVID‐19 pandemic has exacerbated these challenges, as demonstrated by studies by Mirza et al. [13], Ismail et al. [46], and Bhuyan [47]. Indeed, during this crisis, profound lack of oxygen was apparent in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [48, 49, 50]. However, this problem was also described in high‐income countries [51, 52] and exists beyond the COVID‐19 pandemic [26, 53].

Supply chain fragilities, especially during public health emergencies, have also contributed to medical oxygen shortages. These disruptions are often due to a combination of unexpected surges in demand and breakdowns in logistics, manufacturing, or distribution processes [54].

To optimize medical oxygen costs, it is essential to include flexible but protective contractual clauses, improve delivery and storage management, and to implement efficient equipment management. Promoting healthy competition and investing in local oxygen production facilities would also reduce dependence on external suppliers. Finally, improving coordination between healthcare facilities and government agencies would ensure centralized and efficient management of purchases and contracts.

The use of data triangulation and rigorous content analysis is a major strength of this study. It should be noted, however, that obtaining information seemed politically sensitive and several so‐called public documents could not be obtained. In addition, the scope of the study was about public healthcare facilities, and no information was extracted for medical oxygen used outside of public medical facilities such as patient use at home or with ambulation, which may represent a different perspective with its own specificities.

To optimize its use, several sources of oxygen savings have already been well recognized, including the reduction of oxygen leakages during production, transport and oxygen storage, the improvement of clinical oxygen delivery with oxygenation monitoring and utilization of protocols to provide oxygen only when needed [55, 56, 57, 58].

We identified other potential sources of oxygen savings through (i) optimization and reduction of oxygenation targets, which is safe according to recent data [59], (ii) optimization of pulse oximeters that do not underestimate oxygen saturation [39, 44], and (iii) utilization of automated oxygen titration that showed reduced oxygen use in comparison with manual titration [43]. In addition to the differences in the cost of medical oxygen, environmental considerations are now part of the overall thinking [60]. Indeed, the production of oxygen used for the industrial and medical sectors is an energy‐intensive process, with a significant carbon footprint that represents approximately 2% of a country's total carbon emissions [61]. However, it depends heavily on the electricity mix (coal vs renewable) and the recommendations cannot be generalizable. Healthcare institutions should locally assess the entire life‐cycle emissions—including generation, transport, consumption, and disposal—to quantify oxygen's environmental cost accurately [11, 62]. It has been estimated that the production of one kilogram of liquid tank oxygen was associated with the emission of 260 grams of CO2, which was doubled with cylinder‐delivered oxygen [11, 63]. Cryogenic air separation (ASU), especially when combined with transport (cylinders or bulk delivery), generates the highest CO2 emissions per unit of medical oxygen. On‐site PSA generation minimizes emissions substantially and is the most sustainable option when electricity comes from a low‐carbon mix [11, 64]. However, it is desirable to have more data to provide more precise recommendations on this aspect.

For an international audience, this study highlights the need for more transparency in contractual clauses and the development of greater competition among medical gas suppliers. It also reinforces the importance of innovation in how to use efficiently medical oxygen, especially in periods of crisis when supply chain fragilities are exacerbated. The various examples provided in this discussion will help in this way.

In conclusion, this study highlights the complexity of managing medical oxygen costs in Quebec and offers practical recommendations to improve the efficiency and resilience of health systems. It is crucial that decision‐makers and managers collaborate to implement these recommendations to ensure a stable and affordable supply of medical oxygen.
