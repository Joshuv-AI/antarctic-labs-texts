Abnormal Fetuin-A levels in obese horses are associated with activated TLR4/NF-ƙB/MAPK axis and depleted FBXW7 E3 ubiquitin ligase
Authors: Lynda Bourebaba, Mikołaj Panek, Lilibeth Carolina Cárdenas Piedra, Nabila Bourebaba
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Fetuin-A (FetA) is a multifactorial glycoprotein primarily synthesized by the liver with additional expression in adipose tissue, the abundance of which is tightly regulated by the FBXW7 E3 ubiquitin ligase. Recently, FetA has been implicated in the pathogenesis of insulin resistance and associated metabolic failures in humans through its potent and selective inhibition of insulin receptor tyrosine kinase activity, however, no studies have yet directly investigated its role in the development and progression of equine obesity. In this investigation, FetA levels were measured in serum, liver, and adipose tissue derived from healthy and obese horses using quantitative PCR, ELISA and Western blotting to assess gene expression and protein levels. Immunohistochemistry was used to detect FBXW7 and FetA in liver tissue, while immunoblotting assessed insulin receptor (INSR) phosphorylation and FetA protein levels in liver and adipose tissues following FBXW7 supplementation. Obesity-affected tissues exhibited significantly elevated FetA expression at both protein and mRNA levels compared with healthy samples. Moreover, increased FetA was accompanied by activation of the TLR4/NF-ƙB axis and its downstream MAPK targets including JNK1 and p38, as well as elevated circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α. Interestingly, liver FBXW7 levels inversely correlated with high FetA concentrations, and were significantly downregulated under obesity condition. The treatment of liver and adipose tissue biopsies with exogenous FBXW7 protein markedly reduced total FetA expression, which subsequently restored insulin signalling through increased INSR phosphorylation. Overall, horses affected by obesity exhibit abnormally high FetA levels and dysregulated TLR4/NF-ƙB/MAPK pathway, accompanied by suppressed FBXW7 expression, which may represent a potential therapeutic target for restoring insulin sensitivity in metabolically compromised horses.

Introduction

Equine obesity is defined as the excessive accumulation of body fat in horses to the extent that it negatively impacts their health. Typically, horses are considered obese when their body fat accounts for more than 20% of their total body weight, which often corresponds to a body condition score of 7 or greater on the Henneke 9-point scoring system. This condition represents a pathological state of adiposity that predisposes horses to multiple health risks, including metabolic disturbances, insulin dysregulation, laminitis, impaired athletic and reproductive performance, increased susceptibility to arthritis and respiratory problems, and overall reduced welfare. It often results from a chronic positive energy balance, where energy intake exceeds expenditure over time, leading to excessive fat deposition both externally and internally in various body regions, such as the neck crest, withers, ribs, and rump (Rendle et al. 2018; Pratt-Phillips and Munjizun 2023).

Obesity is a major risk factor for equine metabolic syndrome (EMS), a complex endocrine disorder characterized by insulin dysregulation and an increased risk of laminitis. In EMS, the dysfunctional adipose tissue, especially visceral fat deposits, exhibits adipocyte hypertrophy along with increased secretion of proinflammatory cytokines and adipokines such as leptin, which contribute to systemic insulin resistance. This adipose tissue dysfunction induces chronic low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress, further exacerbating metabolic imbalance (Geor 2008; Stefaniuk-Szmukier et al. 2023). As a consequence, obesity-induced insulin dysregulation extends beyond adipose tissue, involving other metabolically active organs such as the liver. In obese horses, lipid accumulation in the liver impairs insulin signaling pathways, contributing to hepatic insulin resistance. This dysfunction prevents the liver from properly suppressing glucose production, potentially contributing to systemic insulin resistance. Additionally, obesity-related hepatic lipotoxicity triggers oxidative stress and inflammatory responses within the liver, further compromising its metabolic function. These alterations worsen the overall metabolic imbalance by promoting dysregulated glucose and lipid homeostasis, which can precede or contribute to metabolic syndrome development in horses (Johnson et al. 2009; Kaczmarek et al. 2016; Daradics et al. 2021; Pratt-Phillips 2024).

Obesity in horses represents a complex metabolic disorder in its own right, and several of its consequences affect multiple physiological systems. Its prevalence is steadily increasing, and in many cases, obesity appears to be the earliest detectable sign of metabolic disturbance (Ragno et al. 2019). In humans, obesity is similarly recognized as an early and prominent feature of metabolic syndrome (MetS), which in adults comprises a group of risk factors including hypertension, abdominal obesity, insulin resistance and fatty liver that increase the risk of developing type 2 diabetes (T2DM), cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), and related mortality (Mohamed et al. 2023). Among the molecular markers associated with human metabolic syndrome, the α2-Heremans-Schmid glycoprotein (AHSG), also known as Fetuin-A (FetA), has been identified as a key serum protein primarily secreted by adipose and liver tissues. Its involvement in the development of MetS highlights its potential as a risk biomarker for chronic inflammatory and metabolic diseases (Komsa-Penkova et al. 2018).

FetA is a multifunctional protein that plays a role in many metabolic processes such as vascular calcification, insulin signalling, inflammation, bone metabolism and adipogenesis (Bourebaba and Marycz 2019). Increased FetA plasma levels have been correlated with reduced glucose tolerance and insulin resistance, which may contribute to the development of metabolic disorders such as T2DM, MetS and CVDs. Moreover, increased secretion of FetA protein by visceral and subcutaneous adipose tissue has been shown in obese animals, suggesting a relationship between obesity, insulin resistance and elevated FetA levels (Pérez-Sotelo et al. 2017). Furthermore, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), frequently associated with insulin resistance and MetS has been linked to increased circulating FetA, highlighting its role as both a hepatokine and adipokine in the pathogenesis of MetS in humans (Ou et al. 2012).

Although the exact molecular mechanisms by which FetA contributes to obesity and MetS are not fully understood, it appears to reduce insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues, including the liver and adipose tissue. Indeed, FetA directly inhibits insulin-mediated insulin receptor tyrosine kinase activity and insulin receptor substrate protein (IRS-1), disrupting the subsequent autophosphorylation/phosphorylation cascade. This results in blocked glucose transporter 4 (Glut4) translocation and protein kinase B (Akt) activation, leading to decreased glucose uptake and ultimately, to persistent hyperglycaemia and insulin resistance (Goustin et al. 2013; Chekol Abebe et al. 2022). FetA may also promote insulin resistance via toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) inflammatory signalling pathway and downstream NF-ƙB/MAPK axis activation (Öner-İyidoğan and Koçak 2025). In addition, the treatment of C57BL/6 mice with FetA triggered an increased expression of IL-6 and TNF-α in adipose tissue, both of which are cytokines known to lower adiponectin production (Lihn et al. 2003; Hennige et al. 2008).

Earlier studies have reported that hepatic FetA protein turnover is regulated by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway (UPP), specifically through the F- box and WD repeat domain containing protein 7 (FBXW7), an SCF E3 ubiquitin ligase that mediates the ubiquitylation of phosphorylated FetA for its proteasomal degradation (Ren et al. 2019). Beyond FetA, FBXW7 also targets sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBP1), making it a key player in maintaining glucose and lipid balance. However, recent findings reveal that FBXW7 levels drop significantly in obese individuals, coinciding with higher FetA amounts and increased insulin resistance. This connection points to FBXW7 as a potential therapeutic target for the efficient management of metabolic disorders (Zhao et al. 2018; Mohammed et al. 2021).

Considering the fundamental role of FetA in the initiation and progression of metabolic disturbances in humans and other species, and given the relevance of obesity and EMS in disrupting equine metabolism, this investigation aimed to determine whether horses with obesity exhibit abnormal FetA levels, and if they coincide with activated TLR4/NF-ƙB/MAPK pathway. Moreover, ex-vivo FBXW7 treatment has been proposed as a potential strategy for defective insulin signalling restitution.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

All reagents and chemicals used in the present study were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Poznań, Poland). Tissue culture media and reagents were purchased from BioWest (VWR International, Gdańsk, Poland). The other reagents and chemical specificities if any, have been highlighted in the methods description.

Tissue samples collection

Liver and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT) samples were collected post-slaughter from Polish Coldblood horses (n = 12) at an accredited local slaughterhouse (Targowa, Rawicz, Poland). All animals were slaughtered for reasons unrelated to this study. The study population consisted of healthy horses (HE, n = 6) and obese horses (OB, n = 6), aged between 8 and 10 years. All animals were evaluated and qualified by an experienced veterinarian on the basis of their clinical profiles and medical history, following previously described procedures and established criteria (Morgan et al. 2015; Durham et al. 2019; Counotte et al. 2022). The evaluation included body weight, body condition score (BCS, 1–9), cresty neck score (CNS, 1–5), fasting insulin, and leptin levels (Table 1). BCS and CNS were assessed through visual inspection and palpation of key body regions, including neck, withers, ribs, tailhead, and flank. Healthy horses typically exhibited a BCS of 4–5 and a CNS of 1–2, whereas obese horses generally showed a BCS ≥ 7 and a CNS ≥ 4, reflecting generalized and regional adiposity, respectively. These scores, together with metabolic parameters, were used to classify horses as healthy or obese. Fasting insulin and leptin concentrations were measured in serum using commercially available ELISA kits: Equine Insulin ELISA (10-1205-01, Mercodia AB, Uppsala, Sweden) and Equine Leptin (LEP) ELISA Kit (orb1665077, Biokom, Janki, Poland). Blood samples were collected between 7:00 and 9:00 a.m. following a 10-hour fasting period, and all assays were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions at a local veterinary diagnostic laboratory.

Collected tissue explants were washed with Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) supplemented with 1% of penicillin/streptomycin solution (P/S) and immediately placed in transport medium containing Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) with 1 g/L glucose, supplemented with 1% P/S. Immediately after collection, the materials were transported to the laboratory facility for processing according to the procedures described in the subsequent sections. Blood samples were collected via jugular venipuncture immediately prior to slaughter into anticoagulant-free vacutainers. Samples were left at room temperature for approximately 30 min to allow clotting, then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Resulting sera were transferred to sterile polypropylene tubes and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. All blood samples were collected by the attending veterinarians as part of routine clinical care. No additional procedures were performed for the purposes of this study, and the samples were only made available for this investigation.

Determination of Fetuin-A, Interleukin-1β and TNF-α levels

Fetuin-A levels were measured in tissue and serum samples using a Horse Fetuin-A ELISA kit (BT Lab, Cat. N° E0158Ho, Zhejiang Province, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, Liver and adipose tissue fragments (100 mg) were washed with cold PBS, homogenized on ice and then centrifuged at 12 000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Proper volumes of samples (tissue homogenates and serra) and provided standard at various concentrations were then introduced into microtiter wells, and mixed with the Horse Fetuin-A antibody and streptavidin-HRP. The plate was incubated for 60 min at 37 °C. Then, all wells were washed 5 times with the 1x Wash Buffer. Substrates were afterwards added to each well and incubated for 10 min at 37 °C in the dark. A stop Solution was subsequently added, and absorbances were measured using a spectrophotometer (Epoch, Biotek, Bad Friedrichshall, Germany) at 450 nm. Similarly, levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines were evaluated in healthy and obese tissues using Horse Interleukin 1 Beta ELISA Kit (BT Lab, Cat. N° E0079Ho, Zhejiang Province, China) (Giercuszkiewicz-Hecold et al. 2025) and Equine TNF-alpha DuoSet ELISA (R&D Systems, Cat. N° DY1814, Minneapolis, MN, USA) (Mukhopadhyay et al. 2023) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

To validate assay performance, the limit of detection (LOD) for each kit was calculated as the mean absorbance of eight blank wells plus three times the standard deviation. The lowest standard concentration tested in each case produced an absorbance signal clearly above this threshold, confirming LOD values of ≤ 6.25 µg/mL for Fetuin-A, ≤ 1.25 pg/mL for IL-1β, and ≤ 15.6 pg/mL for TNF-α. Spike recovery was assessed by spiking known amounts of recombinant protein into both healthy and obese horse samples. Recovery values ranged from 81.6% to 108.8% for Fetuin-A, from 80.1% to 104.9% for IL-1β, and from 85.53% to 109.9% for TNF-α. All results fell within the generally accepted recovery range of 80–120%, indicating satisfactory assay accuracy and minimal interference from the sample matrix. Intra-assay precision was determined by calculating the coefficient of variation (CV%) across duplicate sample wells. CVs ranged from 0.17% to 3.97% for Fetuin-A (mean ~ 0.9%), from 0.06% to 1.86% for IL-1β (mean ~ 0.63%), and from 0.07% to 2.58% for TNF-α (mean ~ 0.82%), reflecting acceptable assay repeatability. Standard curve replicates showed CVs within acceptable ranges (0.3% to 3.8%) across all three assays. Overall, the ELISA assays used demonstrated adequate sensitivity, precision, and recovery for quantifying target proteins in horse sera and tissue homogenates.

Evaluation of serum lipid profil

Circulating cholesterol and triglycerides were measured using an automated A25 biochemical analyzer (Biosystems S.A., Barcelona, Spain) with commercial enzymatic colorimetric assay kits: Cholesterol CHOD-PAP (Borpol Sp. z o.o., Cat. No. 12505, Sosnowiec, Poland) and Triglycerides GPO-PAP (Borpol Sp. z o.o., Cat. No. 12528, Sosnowiec, Poland), following the manufacturer’s protocols in a local Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory (Vetlab sp. z o.o., Wrocław, Poland). Results were expressed in mmol/L.

Profiling of proteins expression using western blot

Total proteins were isolated from liver and adipose tissue samples using ice-cold RIPA buffer containing Proteases and Phosphatases Inhibitors cocktail (1: 1000), following sequential homogenization on ice. Insolubilized materials were removed by centrifugation at 6000 × g, 20 min, 4 °C, and protein concentrations from remaining tissue lysates were measured with the Pierce ™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, Warsaw, Poland). A final concentration of 20 µg of proteins was used for each sample, denatured in a 4× Laemmli loading buffer (Bio-Rad, USA) for 5 min at 95 °C, subjected to SDS-PAGE electrophoresis at 100 V for 90 min in Tris/glycine/SDS buffer for proteins separation, and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio-Rad, Warsaw, Poland) at 100 V, 250 mA for 1 h at 4 °C in a Tris/glycine/methanol buffer. The obtained membranes were incubated in either 5% non-fat milk or 5% BSA solution in 1x TBST for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: β-actin (1:1000, orb10033, Biorbyt), Fetuin-A (1:1000, orb574837, Biorbyt), FBXW7/Cdc4 (1:1000, NBP1-59631, Novus Biologicals), phospho-Insulin receptor (p-Insr Y1361) (1:500, orb393084, Biorbyt), and phospho-NF-kappaB p65 (T254) (1:1000, orb304547, Biorbyt). Excess antibodies have been washed out using 1× TBST and membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (dilution 1:1000 in TBST) and washed with 1x TBST. Chemiluminescent detection of labelled proteins was performed with the SuperSignal™ West Pico PLUS Substrate (ThermoFisher Scientific, Warsaw, Poland) and signals were captured using the ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Warsaw, Poland). Obtained results were further analysed with the Image Lab Software (Bio-Rad, Warsaw, Poland).

Localization of liver Fetuin-A and FBXW7 by immunohistochemistry staining

Fragments of fresh healthy and obese livers were thoroughly washed in PBS and fixed with 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) for 24 h at room temperature. Fixed tissues were afterwards washed, dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-tissue blocks were sectioned at 4 μm thickness using a Microm HM 340E rotary microtome (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), dewaxed and rehydrated in graded ethanol solutions and subsequently incubated in Tris-buffer for 20 min at 96 °C for antigen retrieval. Liver endogenous peroxidase and phosphatase activity was then quenched using the Dako REAL Peroxidase-Blocking Solution (Dako Cytomation Poland, Gdynia, Poland) for 10 min. Tissue sections were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies diluted in appropriate diluent for 1 h at room temperature. The antibodies used included anti-AHSG (C-terminal region; 1:250; catalog no. OAAB00112; Aviva Systems Biology) and anti-FBXW7/Cdc4 (1:500; catalog no. NBP1-59631; Novus Biologicals). Labelled antigens were detected using the Dako Real EnVision + System HRP Labelled Polymer Anti-Rabbit and its associated Liquid DAB + Substrate Chromogen System (Dako Cytomation Poland, Gdynia, Poland), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclei were counterstaining using Hematoxylin solution modified acc. to Gill III (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany), and specimens were mounted with glass coverslips using the Dako Faramount Aqueous Mounting Medium (Dako Cytomation Poland, Gdynia, Poland). The stained tissue sections were observed using an Axio Imager A1 light microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), and photomicrographs captured with a Canon PowerShot camera.

Blinded microscopic examination was conducted at 40× objective magnification. To ensure unbiased assessment, each specimen was randomly assigned an anonymized code (A, B, C, etc.), with healthy and obese samples intermixed so that no grouping could be inferred from the coding. For each section, 10 representative fields were randomly selected across nearly the entire tissue, including areas near central veins, periportal regions, and parenchyma. Staining intensity and subcellular localization including cytoplasmic, perinuclear, and perivascular patterns characteristic of hepatocytes and periportal regions were evaluated visually evaluated on a 0–4 scale according to the manufacturer’s recommendations: 0, no staining; 1+, delicate staining; 2+, average staining; 3+, intense staining; and 4+, bright staining. The analysis included negative controls to confirm staining specificity. The scoring approach was informed by methodologies described in previous studies and used for reference and comparison (Fedchenko and Reifenrath 2014; Meyerholz and Beck 2018; Blondy et al. 2022). Examination was performed by a trained evaluator, who received formal training in histopathology and immunohistochemistry at the Department of Pathology, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, University of Life Sciences in Wrocław (UPWr).

Analysis of relative gene expression

Total RNA was extracted from homogenized tissue samples. Liver and fat tissues were washed twice in PBS, homogenized in TRIzol reagent and centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble materials. The resulting clear supernatant from each sample was then transferred to anew Eppendorf tube, mixed with chloroform and incubated at room temperature for 3 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, the upper clear aqueous phase was carefully collected, and RNA was precipitated by adding cold 100% isopropanol. The resulting RNA pellet was washed twice with 70% ethanol and finally resuspended in DEPC-treated water. The concentration and purity of isolated RNAs were determined using a nano spectrophotometer (Epoch, Biotek, Bad Friedrichshall, Germany). A total of 150 ng of RNA was reverse-transcribed using the commercially available PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Gdańsk, Poland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions in a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, Warsaw, Poland). The Real-Time PCR was performed with a CFX ConnectTM Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Warsaw, Poland) using the SensiFAST SYBR Green Kit (Bioline, London, UK) with the following cycling conditions: 95 ° C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95 ° C for 15 s, annealing for 15 s, and elongation at 72 ° C for 15 s. The sequences of the primers used are listed in the Table 2. All results were normalized to glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) expression. The relative expression level was calculated using the RQMAX log2 scale algorithm.

Ex-vivo liver and adipose tissue treatment with exogenous FBXW7 ligase

Healthy and obese liver and adipose tissue explants were diced into small pieces (< 0,5 cm), washed three times with PBS containing 1% P/S solution and placed into 6-well plates containing Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) with 1 g/L glucose and 1% P/S solution alone (For control groups) or supplemented with 1 µM FBXW7 protein (for treated groups) following insulin stimulation of tested tissues (100 nM for 30 min). All samples underwent an incubation of 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% of humidity, and used for further analysis as described above.

Statistical analysis

The obtained results were analysed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the GraphPad Prism software (version 8.20, San Diego, CA, USA). Differences between groups were determined using either the parametric unpaired Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons or Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and when normality assumptions were not met, the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test was applied. Statistically significant results were indicated by asterisks as follows: * p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation from six biological replicates, each measured in technical triplicates.

Results

Fetuin-A levels are elevated in the liver, adipose tissue, and serum of obese horses

In order to determine whether obese horses exhibit higher levels of FetA glycoprotein, liver, subcutaneous adipose tissue, and serum samples were subjected to protein quantification using a specific ELISA assay. As illustrated in Fig. 1.A, all obese materials where characterised by significant increased levels of FetA over samples collected from healthy horses (p < 0.01). Notably, both healthy and obese adipose tissues appeared as the most concentrated in FetA protein compared to liver and serum specimens. Circulating FetA was also more abundant than liver-tissue FetA. To further confirm the differences in FetA protein among healthy and obese animals, its relative expression was assessed by Western Blot. Obtained data (Fig. 1.B) highlighted substantial overexpression of FetA protein in liver and adipose tissues affected by obesity, in opposition to tissues obtained from normo-metabolic equines (p < 0.001 and p < 0.01 respectively), whereas liver-FetA seemed to be more dysregulated compared to adipose tissue in the course of obesity. In order to elucidate the connection between elevated FetA and dysregulated lipids homeostasis, circulating lipids were additionally measured. As depicted in the Fig. 1.D, obese horses were characterized by a significant elevation in the blood cholesterol and triglycerides levels when compared to healthy horses (p < 0.05), as a consequence of the obesity phenotype and underlying lipids metabolism disruption, suggesting that FetA glycoprotein and fatty substances coexist in the blood of horses suffering from obesity at very high levels.

Fetuin-A overexpression is associated with increased NF-ƙB/MAPK effectors expression and increased pro-inflammatory cytokines secretion in obese tissues

FetA has been proposed as an adaptor protein linking FFAs to TLR4 receptor and thus participating in the activation of NF-ƙB inflammatory pathway in humans and mice. To establish if FetA mediates similar signalling pathways in the course of equine obesity, the expression of AHSG (FetA) as well as TLR4, MAP kinases (MAPK8, MAPK9, MAPK14) effectors and NF-ƙB was ascertained using RT-qPCR method. As previously shown at protein level, the expression of FetA at mRNA level was also found upregulated in obese livers and adipose tissues (p < 0.001), as evidenced by the high relative expression of the AHSG transcript (Fig. 2.A). Interestingly, it was associated with the expression of TLR4, a fundamental regulator of the FFAs-mediated immune response, which was overexpressed in both livers (p < 0.01) and adipose tissues (p < 0.001) of the obese group. Likewise, the expression of TLR4-downstream targets including MAPK8 (JNK1), MAPK9 (JNK2), and MAPK14 (p38) that among others initiate the activation of NF-ƙB translocation was found to be dysregulated in obese matrices, by opposition to healthy animals (Fig. 2.A). A similar tendency was noticed for NF-ƙB, for which the difference was apparent at the mRNA level, where the NF-ƙB transcript was significantly more abundant in the liver (p < 0.001) and adipose tissue (p < 0.001) of obese horses. Additionally, the activation of NF-ƙB was also confirmed at the protein level, by assessing the relative expression of phosphorylated p65 (phospho-T254) subunit, which was significantly upregulated in the liver (p < 0.05) and adipose tissue (p < 0.05) of horses with obesity (Fig. 2.B). To further analyse the crosstalk between increased FetA/TLR4/NF-ƙB axis activation and inflammatory response initiation, the levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines has been measured. As shown in the Fig. 2.D, a significant upregulation of IL-1β and TNF-α was detected at the mRNA level in obese livers and adipose tissues (p < 0.05; p < 0.001). As a consequence, the protein levels of both cytokines appeared to be also notably increased in both tissues under obesity condition (Fig. 2.E), suggesting the unleashing of a process chain encompassing the overproduction of FetA and the activation of NF-kB axis, that ultimately mediates excess cytokines release and inflammatory responses.

Increased Fetuin-A is accompanied by FBXW7 ubiquitin ligase depletion in obese liver tissue

Insofar, quantitative and functional FetA homeostasis is normally maintained by the highly conserved ubiquitin-proteasome system involving the FBXW7 E3 ligase, tissue expression of FBXW7 has been determined together with FetA in order to elucidate whether abnormal FetA levels relate to FBXW7 loss. As demonstrated in the Fig. 3.A, tissue localization of both FBXW7 and FetA evidenced a substantial differential expression of both proteins among healthy and obese livers. Indeed, healthy liver sections stained for FetA protein were characterized by a delicate punctual cytoplasmic staining in nearly 80% of cells evaluated at an intensity of 1+, by contrast to livers derived from obese horses that displayed punctual cytoplasmic staining of all cells with an estimated intensity of 4+; moreover, highly positive stained cells were localized around the blood vessels. On the other hand, FBXW7 labelling resulted in the appearance of 4 + strong positive cells with granular staining pattern of the cytoplasm localized around the blood vessels of healthy livers. Specimens from obese horses showed low FBXW7 signal with medium positive cells diffused in all of the stained area, and similar granular staining located around the nucleus and estimated at an intensity of 2+ (Fig. 3.A). These findings have been further confirmed by the protein profiling analysis (Fig. 3.B), that brought out a significant decrease in FBXW7 protein expression in obese liver lysates when compared to normo-metabolic samples (p < 0.01). Correspondingly, the FBXW7 gene expression (Fig. 3.D) was also found to be downregulated under obesity condition (p < 0.01), indicating a collapse in the hepatic ubiquitin-proteasome system failing to properly regulate FetA balance under metabolic distress.

Minor differences in hematoxylin counterstaining were observed between samples, likely reflecting inter-individual variability in tissue fixation or preservation.

FBXW7 treatment lowers Fetuin-A levels and restores insulin receptor phosphorylation in response to insulin stimulation in obese equine tissues

Recent findings proved that FBXW7 is a pivotal factor that maintains metabolic balance and prevents the development of glucose intolerance and insulin resistance. In this context, the potential influence of exogenous FBXW7 treatment on FetA dynamic and its inhibitory effect on insulin receptor tyrosine kinase activity has been investigated in the liver and adipose tissue of obese horses. Interestingly, the applied treatment significantly lowered FetA protein levels, in both livers (p < 0.001) and adipose tissues (p < 0.001) isolated from obese animals, and normalized the amount of the protein that appeared similar to those measured in healthy tissues (Fig. 4.A). What is more, FBXW7 application at a final concentration of 1 µM also restored insulin receptor autophosphorylation capacity, as evidenced by the augmentation in the relative expression of p-Insr(Y1361), which was noticeably decreased in dysmetabolic livers and adipose tissues, as a consequence of an existing insulin response dysregulation. Taken together, these data highlight the significance and therapeutic potential of FBXW7 in regulating FetA-mediated metabolic imbalance.

Discussion

Fetuin-A glycoprotein has been largely implicated in the pathogenesis of insulin resistance and related metabolic disruptions in humans and rodents. Being a direct inhibitor of insulin receptor’ kinase activity, the insulin desensitizing effects of the glycoprotein have been suggested to be closely mediated by the TLR4/NF-ƙB pathway (Bourebaba and Marycz 2019). Similar to mechanisms observed in other species, equine obesity has been shown to involve insulin dysregulation as a key pathological factor contributing to persistent metabolic dysfunction and low-grade inflammation in horses (Durham 2016; Ribeiro et al. 2020; Hallman et al. 2023). In the present study, we demonstrated for the first time, that like in humans and rodents, obese horses exhibit elevated circulating and tissue levels of FetA, that are associated with dysregulation of the NF-ƙB-TLR4-MAPK pathway. Moreover, we propose the use of FBXW7 E3 ubiquitin ligase as a new drug candidate for the regulation of FetA levels and the subsequent restoration of proper insulin signal transduction.

α2-Heremans-Schmid glycoprotein (AHSG)/FetA is a serum protein belonging to the cystatin protease inhibitors superfamily, primarily produced and secreted by the liver and adipose tissue, and widely recognized as a potent multifunctional molecule involved in a broad range of metabolic processes, including energy expenditure, appetite regulation, insulin signalling, and adipogenesis (Ix et al. 2006). Human gene mapping revealed the location of AHSG gene on the chromosome 3q27, which has been further reported as a type 2 diabetes and obesity-metabolic syndrome susceptibility locus (Kissebah et al. 2000; Siddiq et al. 2005). Elevated tissue and circulating levels of FetA have already been correlated to various metabolic-related defects. Khadir et al. (2018) reported the significant high serum concentrations of FetA in non-diabetic metabolically unhealthy obese subjects, and the critical increased levels of the protein within the subcutaneous adipose tissue of diabetic obese patients compared to the non-diabetic obese cohorts. Similarly, Haukeland et al. (2012) demonstrated a positive correlation between increased FetA levels and NAFLD independently from body mass index, age, sex, fasting glucose, and triglycerides; where patients with liver distress were characterized by elevated FetA expression at both mRNA and protein levels, compared to healthy controls. Moreover, the polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), which is commonly associated to insulin resistance has also been linked to high circulating FetA levels, being however positively correlated with BMI, TG, LDL-C, HOMA-IR, LH, and DHEA-S in affected women (Liu et al. 2020).

In the present investigation, altered FetA levels have also been highlighted in horses affected by obesity. Consistent with previous studies across other species, we demonstrated that obese horses display a significant increase in FetA production, which potentially participates to insulin dysregulation onset and/or progression, and subsequent metabolic failure. Compared to healthy horses, obese animals exhibited increased circulating levels of FetA, together with elevated expression of the glycoprotein in the liver and adipose tissue at mRNA and protein levels. These observations align with a recently published study reporting that circulating FetA levels are elevated in obese horses, supporting a potential role for this glycoprotein in insulin dysregulation and metabolic alteration in equids (Espinosa-López et al. 2025). Together with our results, these studies represent the only evidence to date linking FetA with equine obesity. Although FetA is known to be elevated in multiple metabolic conditions in humans, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, NAFLD, and PCOS, our study provides the first evidence of increased FetA levels in obese horses. This suggests that FetA may play a conserved role in metabolic dysregulation across species. While our findings focus on obesity, it is possible that FetA could also be involved in other equine metabolic disorders, such as EMS or laminitis, although this remains to be investigated.

While little is known about the exact mechanisms leading to FetA overexpression, tissue accumulation of FFAs has been proposed as one of the key prominent hallmarks of FetA upregulation. Notably, Type 2 diabetes-associated fatty liver has been associated with increased FetA protein levels and its mRNA expression, whereas a decrease in liver fat content in humans correlated with a decline in plasma FetA (Stefan et al. 2008). Moreover, the levels of non-esterified FFAs and FetA have been shown to simultaneously increase in the serum of db/db mice (Dasgupta et al. 2010). Therewith, elevated serum FetA level has been evidenced in high-fat-fed animals and obese diabetic subjects (Lin et al. 1998; Stefan et al. 2006), while mice lacking FetA protein have been found to be less prone to develop both obesity and insulin resistance phenotypes (Mathews et al. 2002). Our findings are in agreement with these observations, as obese horses with high FetA levels were also characterized by increased cholesterol and triglycerides concentrations. Moreover, statistical analysis of the obtained data demonstrated an association between serum free lipids and FetA; confirming the existence of a crosstalk between these two components.

Abnormal lipid metabolism and excess FFAs have been previously reported as natural activators of toll-like receptors including TLR4, which is considered a key initiator triggering a cascade of cellular signals, involving the p65 subunit phosphorylation and culminating in NF-κB activation (Suganami et al. 2007; Kuzmicki et al. 2013). Once activated, NF- κB translocates to the nucleus to further target and stimulate FetA promoter and significantly augment FetA gene and protein expression (Bhattacharya et al. 2012). In a model of NF-κBnull HepG2 cells, exposure to palmitate did not induce any significant increase in FetA levels, while enhancing the secretion of the glycoprotein by over fourfold in native cells. What is more, forced expression of NF-κB in those cells resulted in a robust overexpression and secretion of FetA independently from palmitate challenging (Dasgupta et al. 2010).

In this investigation, liver and adipose tissue sampled from obese horses displayed an altered NF-κB/TLR4/MAPK axis. The analysis of gene expression evidenced a sharp overexpression of both TLR4 and NF-κBp65 transcripts in obese horses compared with healthy horses. Likewise, protein profiling revealed an increased phosphorylation of the NF-κBp65 subunit, suggesting the initiation of its translocation to the FetA promotor, which has been confirmed by the observed upregulation of the AHSG (FetA) gene expression. Interestingly, the expression of TLR4 downstream effectors was also found to be upregulated in obese tissues. FFAs-TLR4 complex contributes to the mobilization of downstream mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signalling cascades including p38 and JNK1/2, that subsequently initiate the activation of various transcription factors, such as NF-κBp65 (Wang et al. 2008). As a consequence of MAPKs and NF-κB pathways stimulation, proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α are massively secreted, leading to excessive inflammatory responses and insulin resistance exacerbation (Xiao et al. 2020). In this research, activated MAPK/NF-kB pathway was accompanied by a substantial increase in the expression of both IL-1β and TNF-α at mRNA and protein levels in liver and adipose tissue explants of obese horses, suggesting the interconnection between elevated FFAs/FetA levels and NF-κB/TLR4/MAPK-mediated inflammatory bias. Our findings are consistent with the earlier study of Pal et al. (2012), which highlighted the combined influence of FetA, TLR4 and FFAs on inflammatory processes. In their study, AHSG knockout in a mouse model of high-fat diet-induced insulin resistance (HFD-IR) resulted in a marked suppression of TLR4-mediated NF-κB activation, as well as IL-6 and TNF-α production. Conversely, intravenous injection of FetA protein triggered FFA-induced activation of NF-κB and elevated expression of these proinflammatory cytokines, that occurred only in the presence of FetA, compared to wild-type mice under the same dietary regimen.

Previous studies have demonstrated the critical role of FetA in the loss of insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues. Elevated Human FetA has been thus shown to specifically inhibit insulin receptor tyrosine kinase activity in muscle and liver, whereas suppression of mouse AHSG gene expression resulted in improved glucose clearance and insulin sensitivity, resistance to weight gain, and decreased dyslipidaemia (Reinehr and Roth 2008). Recent findings suggest that FetA protein levels are tightly regulated by the ubiquitination machinery involving the FBXW7 E3 ubiquitin ligase, that mediates the specific recognition and degradation of Ser305 and Ser309 phosphorylated FetA, highlighting the major role of FBXW7 in maintaining glucose and insulin homeostasis (Yan et al. 2021). Indeed, Zhao et al. (2018) indicated a marked downregulation of FBXW7 protein and mRNA levels in the liver derived from obese patients. Likewise, they demonstrated that liver-specific FBXW7 knockout in mice significantly augmented circulating and hepatic FetA levels, and promoted hyperglycaemia and hyperinsulinemia compared to wild type mice. Similarly, Tu et al. (2012) reported a noticeable loss in FBXW7 at both mRNA and protein levels in the livers of C57BL/6J mice following 8 weeks of high fat diet-induced NAFLD-like model. Our findings are in agreement with these observations, as obese equine livers displayed lowered levels of FBXW7 ligase over FetA protein, suggesting that the observed increased FetA may be partly attributable to disruption of the FBXW7/FetA regulatory axis in the course of obesity in horses.

Recent preclinical studies have emphasized the therapeutic potential of FBXW7 protein in regulating hyperglycaemia, hepatic inflammation and insulin resistance, and has therefore been proposed as a promising therapeutic target in liver-related metabolic disorders (Zhao et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2019). Accordingly, forced expression of FBXW7 in obese and/or NAFLD livers resulted in a substantial normalization of blood glucose and insulin levels, ALT activity, and hepatocytes size, accompanied by a marked reduction in vacuolization, fat accumulation, and infiltration of pro-inflammatory cells in NAFLD livers. Moreover, overexpression of FBXW7 significantly mitigated glucose intolerance and systemic insulin resistance in HFD-fed mice, thought the restoration of insulin signal transduction involving an increase in p-Akt (Ser473) and p-GSK3β (Ser9) proteins levels (Zhao et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2019). Similar outcomes were achieved in our investigation following the treatment of livers and adipose tissues sourced from obese horses with 1 µM of exogenous FBXW7 protein. We first found that a 24 h treatment significantly lowered the tissue levels of FetA protein, confirming the ability of FBXW7 to orchestrate the degradation and regulation of the glycoprotein abundance. We further demonstrated that the E3 ligase-mediated reduction of FetA consequently enhanced insulin signalling cascade by augmenting the phosphorylation of its receptor in both obese tissues in response to insulin stimulation, suggesting that FBXW7 represents a valuable pharmacological tool for the improvement of insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues, via the restoration of the insulin receptor tyrosine kinase activity under obesity condition.

While this study provides novel and valuable insights into the role of FetA and the TLR4/NF-κB/MAPK signalling pathway in obesity-affected horses, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size, though adequate for exploratory analyses, may not capture the full biological variability within the equine population. Second, only obese horses were included, which limits the generalizability of our findings. Future studies should expand the scope to include additional and more complex endocrine clinical phenotypes, such as EMS, lean insulin-dysregulated horses, and PPID, either alone or in combination with obesity, to better define the role of FetA across the spectrum of equine metabolic and endocrine disorders. Third, although strong associations between FetA, inflammatory mediators, and FBXW7 were demonstrated, the observational nature of the study limits conclusions regarding causality. Finally, while the ex vivo treatment with FBXW7 provided promising results regarding FetA downregulation and restoration of insulin receptor phosphorylation, further in-depth studies are necessary to assess its effects on downstream insulin signaling targets such as AKT and Pi3K, and the final impact on glucose and lipid metabolism. Ideally, these investigations should be extended to isolated primary hepatocytes to allow for more controlled and mechanistic evaluation, followed by in vivo studies to validate the therapeutic efficacy and translational relevance of FBXW7-based interventions.

Conclusion

Fetuin-A organokine has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various metabolic dysfunctions in humans and rodent experimental models, through its ability to inhibit insulin receptor tyrosine kinase activity and its pro-inflammatory effect. In this study, we demonstrated that similarly to humans and other animal models, horses with obesity displayed elevated FetA levels in blood, liver and adipose tissue. Moreover, increased FetA levels were associated with activation of the TLR4/NF-κB/MAPK signalling pathway and the consequent elevation of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TFN-α and IL-1β. Interestingly, livers derived from obese animals were characterized by suppressed FBXW7 E3 ligase protein and gene expression, whereas treatment of liver and fat explants with exogenous FBXW7 efficiently reduced the abundance of FetA protein and restored the autophosphorylation capacity of the insulin receptor, suggesting that FBXW7 represents a potential new drug candidate targeting FetA glycoprotein for the prevention and the proper management of equine and human metabolic disorders.
