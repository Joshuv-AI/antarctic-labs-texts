Light polarization-based electro-optic memory
Authors: Quanzhen Wan, Shi Zhao, Baoshan Tang, Zhi Gen Yu, Zihang Fang, Jin Feng Leong, Zefeng Xu, Yong-Wei Zhang, Evgeny Zamburg, Aaron Voon-Yew Thean
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The integration of light polarization into non-volatile memory enables angle-resolved information processing, unlocking new photonic channels for communication, computation and imaging. Yet practical polarization-sensitive memory remains rare. Here, we report a 2D rhenium disulfide (ReS2)/hafnium zirconium oxide (Hf0.5Zr0.5O2, HZO) ferroelectric field-effect transistor in which field-driven charge separation realizes polarization-resolved memory. The redistribution of photo-generated carriers at the heterostructure interface establishes an interfacial electrostatic field that modulates HZO ferroelectric domains and encodes non-volatile states. We also find that interfacial compressive stress induced by lattice mismatch shortens the Re-Re bond, which enhances the Re-Re chain anisotropy by 3.7x (from 2.67 to 9.98). Integrated into arrays for photonic neural networks, the device attains >93% accuracy on a transformer model. Leveraging the cumulative switching property of HZO with sequential optical signals, the device enables in-situ multiplication and accumulation of inputs over time, achieving 4x area saving with <1% accuracy loss. Beyond amplitude and phase, the demonstrated electro-optic device enables optical polarization as an additional information read-out, which significantly increases the information density of photonic-based computing.

Introduction

Photonic neural networks (PNNs) leverage low dissipative properties of light to enable computational parallelism as an alternative to electronic-based neural networks, which suffer from limitations due to resistive losses1–4. Without a practical photonic-based memory that co-locates with optical components, the energy efficiency of photonic networks will be limited by a memory wall that results from the data transfers between signal processing and electronic memory units5. This motivates innovations to integrate non-volatile electronic memory with optical sensors and modulators, such as phase change materials Ge2Sb2Te56, ferroelectric Hafnium-Zirconium oxide on lithium niobate-on-insulator (LNOI)7,8, and ferroelectric-defined MoTe2 homojunctions9. Most of these devices respond to either optical intensity or spectral content10,11. On the other hand, optical polarization, another fundamental property of light waves, has yet been extensively explored as an information carrier. Harnessing optical polarization to encode and decode information may significantly expand the limitations of transmission bandwidth, density, and efficiency12,13. Such polarization-encoded signals, propagating in low-loss polarization-maintaining fibers, enable high-fidelity photonic interconnects with significantly higher information density than what’s being used today14. A key enabler of such a technology would be polarizer-based electro-optic modulators and demodulators.

One approach for such an electro-optic demodulator (sensor) would be based on heterostructure materials with pronounced crystallographic anisotropy: exhibiting strong optical interactions while generating robust interfacial electrostatic fields that can be read electrostatically. Perovskite nanowires exhibiting strong linear dichroism that produce an excitonic response to illumination can produce polarization-dependent charge accumulation and a memory effect15. Similarly, organic supermolecular J-aggregates, when coupled with n-type polymer (Cl2-NDI), exhibit polarization-dependent excitonic transitions and trap states16. However, both material systems show limited environmental stability and require stringent molecular alignment; moreover, their short exciton lifetimes restrict operation to transient synaptic behavior. In contrast, many two-dimensional transition metal dichalcogenides (2D TMD) exhibit strong light-matter interactions, producing long-lived excitons and trions17. Among them, rhenium disulfide (ReS2) with strong 2D crystallographic anisotropy is a promising candidate for polarization-sensitive photonic applications18,19. ReS2, characterized by its distorted 1 T (d1T) phase at room temperature and Re4 chain structures, features a thickness-independent direct bandgap, and exceptional photoresponse characteristics20. Its remarkable photoresponsivity (up to 88600 A/W)21, external quantum efficiency (3168%)22, and broadband responsivity (ultraviolet to near-infrared) further highlight its photodetector potential23. Although the in-plane anisotropy of ReS2 enables polarization-dependent carrier dynamics, it lacks an intrinsic mechanism for non-volatile polarization-state retention, hindering its use in computation within photonic neural networks.

In this work, an integrated electro-optic memory platform is demonstrated using an anisotropic van der Waals (vdW) heterostructure consisting of multilayer ReS2 and ferroelectric HZO. By leveraging the anisotropic interfacial electrostatic coupling under polarized light, the device achieves non-volatile, polarization-selective programming of ferroelectric states. We identify that the interfacial coupling between ReS2 and the orthorhombic HZO induces a significant lattice reconstruction, shortening the Re-Re bonds and markedly enhancing the intrinsic transport anisotropy. This strain-mediated enhancement enables the discrimination of polarization states with a high optical anisotropic ratio of 9.98. The observed memory behavior is rationalized through a self-consistent framework combining Landau-Khalatnikov (LK) dynamics and first-principles calculations, identifying the spatial separation of photo-excited carriers and the resulting interfacial potential as the primary drivers for ferroelectric domain switching. Finally, the scalability and computational efficiency of this platform are validated through the implementation of a simulated FeFET array performing complex transformer-model inference, highlighting its potential for area-efficient, polarized in-sensor computing.

Results

Device and Material Structures

Anisotropic ferroelectric field-effect transistors (FeFETs) were realized by integrating mechanically exfoliated multilayer ReS2 flakes onto HZO gate dielectrics. The characteristic vibrational mode splitting and excitonic signatures of ReS2 were resolved via spectroscopic characterization (Supplementary Fig. 1). The ferroelectric orthorhombic phase of the HZO film was stabilized through rapid thermal annealing24. XPS analysis validated stoichiometric Hf:Zr ratio (≈1:1), while HZO-based capacitor demonstrated a robust ferroelectric polarization (2 Pr) of 46 µC/cm2, with sub-nA leakage current, endurance exceeding 108 cycles and stable non-volatile retention (Supplementary Figs. 2, 3).

FeFET mini-arrays with high device-to-device uniformity were developed based on this platform (Supplementary Fig. 4). Each unit comprises a van der Waals ReS2 channel on an orthorhombic HZO dielectric above a tungsten (W) back gate (Fig. 1a), enabling the co-localization of optical sensing, ferroelectric programming, and electrical readout within a single device footprint. Cross-sectional TEM (Fig. 1b) reveals an atomically sharp ReS2/HZO interface free of amorphous interlayers or voids, providing the structural foundation for efficient interfacial electrostatic coupling. Source and drain electrodes were diagonally aligned with a 30° angular separation to investigate polarimetric discrimination (Fig. 1c). Identification of the Re-Re chain orientation for each flake via polarization optical microscopy ensured precise crystallographic alignment across the array (Supplementary Fig. 5). Anisotropic interfacial charge redistribution is triggered by polarized illumination of the ReS2 channel, facilitated by the high-quality heterostructure interface. The density of this charge accumulation scales with incident power and varies as a function of the angle between the polarization vector and the Re-Re chains. An optically modulated interfacial potential establishes a local electric field, driving polarization-state modulation in the adjacent HZO layer (Fig. 1d). Successive optical pulses lead to the incremental accumulation of interfacial charge, advancing the polarization state for non-volatile multi-level storage. The interfacial field strength is enhanced at higher optical intensities or upon alignment with the high-absorption axis, driving more polarization evolution and expanding the memory window (Fig. 1e).

Device operations

Source-drain angle-resolved Id-Vg characteristics confirm that the maximum on-current (Id,max) aligns with the Re-Re chains (Supplementary Fig. 7). This orientation is consistent with the mobility maximum derived from the Peierls distortion in the d1T-ReS2 lattice25,26. The devices exhibit n-type transport with an on/off ratio of 108, facilitated by the 1.35 eV bandgap of ReS2 and the high dielectric quality of the HZO film. Photoresponse evaluated at λ = 380 nm and 808 nm shows a monotonic increase in drain current with light intensity (Supplementary Fig. 8). The non-volatile stability of the device is confirmed by negligible current degradation over 104 s and stable operation beyond 108 cycles, correlating with the robust ferroelectric properties of the HZO layer (Supplementary Fig. 12). The observed anisotropic memory window is consistent with polarization-selective charge generation in ReS2, which modulates the interfacial potential and controls the extent of ferroelectric domain switching in HZO. Stable and polarization-selective states are maintained under both dark and illuminated conditions (Supplementary Fig. 13a). Furthermore, successive optical pulses enable deterministic multi-level programming, advancing the conductance toward saturation (Supplementary Fig. 13b, Fig. 2f). A measurable and non-volatile conductance update is achieved within a single 1 μs optical pulse. The measured pulse-number-dependent conductance evolution for the 32 optical writing states is provided in Supplementary Fig. 13c, from which the potentiation deviation was extracted. The device resolves up to 32 distinguishable states (5-bit), demonstrating the capability for controllable photonic weight updating in high-density in-memory computing (Fig. 2g). Benchmarking against other 2D-based FET and FeFET platforms (Supplementary Table 4) confirms the competitive performance of this architecture.

Characterization of polarization-sensitive electro-optic memory

To elucidate the interfacial coupling mechanism, piezoresponse force microscopy (PFM) was performed on the ReS2/HZO heterostructure. The scan included both the ReS2-covered region and the adjacent bare ferroelectric HZO surface. Under illumination, the ferroelectric response is unambiguously enhanced and exhibits a distinct light polarization dependence (Fig. 3a–d). At an intensity of 10 mW/cm2, the phase contrast between the ReS2-covered and bare regions is 4.2° for 0° polarization, which decreases to 1.7° for 90° polarization. Increasing the intensity to 50 mW/cm2 amplifies this difference to 8.5°and 5.6°, respectively, demonstrating that the interfacial modulation is cooperatively governed by both light intensity and polarization orientation. The written polarization states remain stable over time, with phase distributions showing negligible change after a 1 h delay. Steady-state PL spectra exhibit a redshift and linewidth broadening with increasing temperature, driven by exciton-phonon coupling (Fig. 3g). In contrast, the power-dependent evolution shows a monotonic intensity increase with negligible peak shift (Fig. 3h), confirming that optical excitation modulates the population density without altering the band structure31,32. Polarization-resolved spectra reveal that the relative intensities of the trions and excitons evolve with the incident polarization angle (Supplementary Fig. 16). This anisotropic redistribution of the charge population is governed by the intrinsic in-plane anisotropy of ReS2. Furthermore, the orthorhombic HZO interface, compared to SiO2 or monoclinic HZO, induces blue shifts and intensity enhancements in both excitonic and trion emissions (Supplementary Fig. 17). These results indicate that polarized illumination not only generates long-lived photo-carriers in ReS2 but also modulates their population and redistribution through the intrinsic crystallographic anisotropy of the material. As a result, different carrier densities at the ReS2/HZO interface are produced, leading to the formation of distinct interfacial electrostatic potentials that subsequently influence the ferroelectric polarization state.

The observed optical response indicates that illumination redistributes interfacial charge carriers, and the critical question is how this redistribution modulates the ferroelectric switching dynamics. To establish the ferroelectric response, the experimental P-V hysteresis was first quantitatively fitted using a multi-domain Landau-Euler expansion (Supplementary Note 2, Fig. 18a). In this framework, the fitted Landau coefficients remain fixed, while illumination modifies the effective driving field (Eeff=Eext+Eint). The interfacial field Eint arises from the spatial separation of photo-generated carriers at the ReS2/HZO heterostructure. As derived in Supplementary Note 4, this field is self-consistently coupled to interfacial electrostatics, where the high-quality vdW interface facilitates a vertical dipole moment. The calculated polarization dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 18b) demonstrate that the separated photocharge-associated interfacial potential produces the dominant modification of the switching trajectory, driving the deterministic transition between polarization states.

The microscopic origin of this coupling is further elucidated by first-principles calculations. Charge-density-difference calculations visualize the interfacial charge redistribution in the ReS2/HZO heterostructure (Fig. 3i, j). Under the dark condition, only weak charge rearrangement is observed at the interface, corresponding to a small interfacial dipole moment of 0.2 e·Å. In contrast, photo-induced carrier accumulation produces pronounced electron depletion and accumulation across the ReS2/HZO interface, increasing the dipole moment to 1.3 e·Å. This more than sixfold enhancement indicates that illumination substantially strengthens the vertical electrostatic potential across the heterostructure, providing an internal driving field that can couple to the polarization landscape of the adjacent HZO layer. Consistently, the field-dependent band-structure and Bloch-state projections in Supplementary Fig. 19 show that an out-of-plane electric field drives spatial separation of band-edge electron and hole states across the heterostructure. These calculations support that photo-generated carriers are not merely transient recombination products, but instead form a vertically polarized interfacial charge distribution that sustains the electrostatic environment required for light-assisted memory switching35.

Enhanced sensitivity of polarization detection

To rationalize the anisotropic ratio of 9.98, which substantially exceeds the intrinsic value typically reported for ReS2 (below 3), the influence of the gate dielectric crystalline phase on in-plane transport was examined36,37. Polarization angle-resolved transfer measurements of multilayer ReS2 FETs gated by monoclinic HZO yield an anisotropy ratio of 2.67, with Id,max varying from 32 to 12 μA (Fig. 4a). In contrast, ReS2 FeFETs integrated with orthorhombic HZO exhibit nearly an order-of-magnitude variation in Id,max, corresponding to an enhanced anisotropic ratio of 9.98 (Fig. 4b). This pronounced disparity indicates that the orthorhombic phase of the gate dielectric acts as more than a mere electrostatic modulator, playing a central role in amplifying transport anisotropy beyond the intrinsic limit.

Given that the intrinsic anisotropy of ReS2 originates from directional Re-Re bonding, the enhanced device response suggests a modification of the ReS2 lattice induced by interfacial coupling36,38. Raman spectroscopy provides evidence for such lattice modification (Fig. 4c). Coupling to orthorhombic HZO induces blue shifts of ~1–2 cm-1 across four in-plane Eg-like phonon modes relative to the monoclinic reference. These shifts are consistent with in-plane compressive strain that shortens and strengthens Re-Re bonding. Similar results were found in GaP and MoS239,40. Polarization-resolved Raman spectra further confirm that the in-plane anisotropy, with the axis of maximum Raman intensity remaining aligned with the Re-Re chains. The larger temperature coefficient of the first Eg-like mode on orthorhombic HZO suggests enhanced phonon anharmonicity and robust strain-mediated interfacial coupling (Supplementary Fig. 20)41,42.

First-principles calculations clarify the electronic consequences of this lattice compression. Under 1–4% in-plane compressive strain, the angle-resolved electron effective mass undergoes a marked change, increasing to 3.8 m0 along specific crystallographic directions while remaining stable along parallel axes (Supplementary Fig. 21). This divergence results in a transport anisotropy of 3.65 at 3% strain and 6.38 at 4% strain. Structural relaxation confirms the shortening of the Re-Re bond from 2.8 Å to 2.71 Å in the ReS2/HZO heterostructure (Fig. 4d, g), with the absence of imaginary phonon modes verifying the dynamical stability of the strained configuration (Supplementary Fig. 22). Band structure and projected density of states (PDOS) analysis indicate that strain-induced band-edge variations are driven by the redistribution of Re-d orbital weight, consistent with strain-tunable transport behavior reported previously (Supplementary Fig. 23)43,44.

To further understand this phenomenon, lattice-scale structural evidence was obtained via aberration-corrected, atom-resolved ADF-STEM. Quantitative analysis of Re atomic columns in freestanding ReS2 and ReS2/HZO heterostructures resolves the effect of interfacial strain (Fig. 4e, h). Statistical distribution of in-plane Re-Re distances, extracted through MATLAB-assisted column localization (Supplementary Fig. 24), reveals a reduction in the average bond length from 2.806 Å in freestanding ReS2 to 2.698 Å in the HZO-supported layers (Fig. 4f, i). This contraction corresponds to an interfacial compressive strain of ~3.9%, consistent with the magnitude predicted by first-principles calculations45,46.

The link between lattice modification and transport was further validated through thickness-dependent and material-comparative studies. Increasing the HZO thickness from 8 nm to 16 nm reduces the anisotropy from 9.98 to 5.41, a trend attributed to partial elastic relaxation of the interfacial strain (Supplementary Fig. 25). In contrast, MoS2-based FeFETs exhibit nearly isotropic transfer characteristics (Supplementary Fig. 26), confirming that ferroelectric switching alone does not introduce appreciable in-plane anisotropy. The application of this heterostructure configuration to ReSe2 yields a similar enhancement, with the anisotropic ratio increasing from 2.63 to 5.98 (Supplementary Fig. 27), suggesting that the strain-induced amplification of anisotropy is a general feature of this class of rhenium-based dichalcogenides.

Towards real-time sensing and inference

The angular dependence of the on-current, as demonstrated in Figs. 2d and 2e, follows47:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I(\theta )={I}_{\max }{\cos }^{2}(\theta+\varphi )+{I}_{\min }{\sin }^{2}(\theta+\varphi )$$\end{document}I(θ)=Imaxcos2(θ+φ)+Iminsin2(θ+φ)where Imax and Imin represent the maximum and minimum photocurrents, respectively, and φ denotes the angular offset relative to the Re-Re chain (extracted as 1.72°). This characteristic allows the FeFET to function as an analog in-sensor multiplier, where input values are encoded by optical intensity, and synaptic weights are mapped to discrete polarization states via spatial light modulators (SLM). The drain current represents the product of these two factors. By organizing these devices into a crossbar architecture, vector-matrix multiplication is executed by summing column-wise currents according to Kirchhoff’s current law, enabling core neural network inference operations directly within the optical sensors.

The feasibility of this architecture was verified using a 3 × 3 array prototype to simulate large-scale FeFET arrays for in-sensor MAC/VMM operations. To experimentally demonstrate an exclusive OR (XOR) classification task, binary optical inputs were encoded and multiplied by different programmed conductance states in the array (Supplementary Fig. 28). The calibrated 3 × 3 array mapping successfully reproduces the XOR decision boundary, demonstrating programmable weighted summation for a non-linearly separable task and providing a functional bridge toward device-calibrated large-scale neural-network inference simulations. The input layer of a deep neural network (DNN) trained for MNIST handwritten digit classification was replaced by the FeFET model (Supplementary Figs. 29, 30). The system-level simulations were calibrated using measured conductance states and device-to-device variation rather than idealized linear updates. Weights were quantized into eight discrete polarization states, accounting for a measured device-to-device variation of 5%. The array achieved a projected inference accuracy of 92.9%, representing a negligible 0.32% drop compared to the non-quantized GPU-based baseline (Supplementary Fig. 31a). Similar performance was observed for the Fashion-MNIST (FMNIST) dataset, reaching 79.77% accuracy48.

The enhanced polarization sensitivity and device uniformity further enable large-scale, mixed-precision inference, evaluated using a transformer-based ALBERT model for sentiment analysis. In this workflow, movie reviews were encoded as optical intensities and processed through embedding, multi-head attention, and fully connected layers (Fig. 5a, b). VMM operations for the fully connected layers are computed on the FeFET array (Fig. 5c), with multiplication results stored within the ferroelectric state of each device. Subsequent electrical readout and current summation complete the VMM calculation. Device-calibrated simulations project an accuracy loss of less than 2% across individual Query, Dense, and Pooler layers, and a total drop of 5% when replacing all three layers (Supplementary Fig. 31b). Furthermore, the quasi-linear cumulative nature of ferroelectric switching allows sequential multiplication results to be accumulated within the HZO layer prior to readout. This facilitates time-multiplexed in-memory accumulation of partial sums, effectively trading computation time for a reduced hardware footprint. A single FeFET can support four multiplexed MAC operations, reducing the required area by 75% with less than 1% additional accuracy degradation (Fig. 5d, e). These results establish the FeFET as a scalable platform for photonic in-sensor computing. Projections of power consumption and footprint at a 1 MHz pulse rate provide an estimate of the scaling behavior of this integrated electro-optic memory. In addition, experimentally based estimates of the optical programming energy and electrical operation energy are provided in Supplementary Note 5 together with a comparison against representative memory and in-memory-computing technologies (Supplementary Table 9). Future scaling of ReS2/HZO arrays may benefit from orientation-controlled growth of aligned ReS2 crystals, thereby reducing cell-to-cell variability associated with random crystal orientations and polarization-dependent photoresponse49. In addition, write–verify programming and hardware-aware training strategies could compensate residual conductance variations and support reliable large-scale system implementation.

Discussion

The synergistic integration of light-driven interfacial electrostatics and strain-engineered crystalline anisotropy enables a robust platform for polarization-resolved non-volatile memory. Unlike conventional optoelectronics characterized by volatile photocarrier recombination, the HZO-gated architecture facilitates the transduction of transient optical stimuli into deterministic ferroelectric states through optically induced interfacial potential modulation. As investigated by time-resolved spectroscopy and first-principles calculations, the memory modulation originates from photo-generated carrier redistribution at the van der Waals interface, which establishes an interfacial electrostatic potential and modifies the polarization state of the adjacent ferroelectric layer. The resulting localized charge redistribution establishes an interfacial field that effectively modulates the energy landscape of the ferroelectric layer, lowering the activation energy for domain reversal. The efficacy of this process is further amplified by interfacial strain engineering, where the orthorhombic dielectric phase serves as a structural template to reconfigure the ReS2 lattice. The ~3.9 percent compressive strain, verified by atom-resolved STEM and Raman blue-shifts, induces a shortening of Re-Re bonds and a consequent effective mass renormalization, which enhances the intrinsic transport anisotropy. Furthermore, the demonstrated time-multiplexed multiply and accumulate operations achieve a 75% reduction in hardware footprint, highlighting the scalability of this heterostructure for area-efficient neuromorphic crossbars. This coupling of dielectric-induced lattice reconstruction and interfacial electrostatic control provides a pathway for integrated systems capable of advanced polarimetric sensing, memory and computing.

Methods

Device fabrication

For the metal-ferroelectric-metal (MFeM) capacitor, 30 nm W is first deposited through sputtering (AJA UHV sputtering system) on the top of a Si/SiO2 (280 nm) substrate. 8 nm HZO film is then deposited using the thermal ALD (Picosun ALD system) through Hf[N-(C2H5)CH3]4 (TEMAHf), Zr[N-(C2H5)CH3]4 (TEMAZr) and ozone at 280 °C. 30 nm W top electrode is sputtered again on top of HZO before rapid thermal annealing (Annealsys) at 380 °C for 1200 s under an N2 atmosphere, to activate the ferroelectric orthorhombic phase. Subsequently, the top electrode is patterned with an area of 100 × 100 μm2 by optical lithography (MA8 mask aligner) and wet etching processes.

For FeFET, the bottom W gate is fabricated by sputtering and optical lithography (DMO ML3 Pro microwriter) on a Si/SiO2 (280 nm) substrate. 8 nm HZO is deposited and activated using the same process as that applied in MFeM. Subsequently, the sacrificial layer is completely removed by W etchant solution. Before transferring, the sample is heated at 180 °C for 1200 s under high vacuum to remove the adsorbed molecule. Few-layer ReS2 exfoliated from the bulk crystal (HQ Graphene) was isolated onto a polydimethyl-siloxane (PDMS) stamp and then transferred onto the HZO film at 50 °C. A post-process annealing at 200 °C in forming gas ambient for 2 h, followed by 150 °C in ultra-high vacuum for 45 min, is used to reduce the contact resistance in a self-designed furnace. Next, source/drain electrodes are defined by electron-beam lithography (EBL Jeol 6300FS) and lift-off with Cr/Au (20/30 nm) through electron beam evaporation (AJA Electron Beam Evaporator). The deposition rates of Cr and Au are both selected at 0.1 Å/s to minimize the bombardment damage on the surface of ReS2.

Device measurements

Ferroelectric characteristics of HZO capacitors were measured through Radiant precision multiferroic II precision materials analyzer. The electrical and optical measurements were conducted by Keysight B1500 Semiconductor Parameter Analyzer and Lakeshore Cryogenic probe station with lasers. The waveform used in the endurance test was generated by an Agilent B1530.

Material characterizations

Room-temperature unpolarized Raman spectra were taken on a Renishaw Raman microscope with a 514 nm excitation. Polarized Raman measurements were carried out using a Renishaw InVia micro-Raman system. A 532 nm laser with an 1800 l/mm grating was employed. Low-temperature PL measurements were performed in a He-flow cryostat under vacuum. The cryostat was equipped with a 15 T superconducting magnet in Faraday configuration. Excitation was provided either by a tunable continuous-wave laser (400–2300 nm) or by a femtosecond optical parametric oscillator (345–2500 nm, Radiantis Inspire) pumped by a mode-locked Ti:sapphire laser (80 MHz repetition rate, 100 fs pulse width). TRPL measurements were conducted using a time-correlated single-photon counting system (PicoHarp 300). AFM and PFM images were obtained with Park atomic force microscope under targeted conditions. A Si cantilever tip coated with Pt-Ir (SCM-PIT, Bruker Co.) is applied. Polarized optical microscope images were obtained by OLYMPUS GX71.

Chemical composition of the HZO films was characterized using a Thermo Scientific Nexsa XPS system equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα (1486.6 eV) X-ray source. Survey and high-resolution spectra were collected under ultrahigh vacuum conditions. Charge neutralization was performed using a low-energy electron flood gun, and all spectra were calibrated using the adventitious carbon C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV as the reference. High-resolution scans were acquired for the Hf 4 f and Zr 3 d core levels, and peak fitting was conducted using Thermo Avantage software to extract binding energies and relative atomic ratios.

Atom-resolved scanning transmission electron microscopy was performed using an aberration-corrected transmission electron microscope (JEOL ARM 200 F) equipped with a cold field-emission gun and a probe-side spherical aberration corrector. All images used for quantitative lattice analysis were acquired in annular dark-field (ADF) STEM mode at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV to ensure sufficient spatial resolution and signal-to-noise ratio. The probe convergence semi-angle was 20.6 mrad, with a probe current of ~17 pA and a probe size of ~0.13 nm. ADF signals were collected using an inner collection angle of 54 mrad and an outer angle of 220 mrad, providing strong Z-contrast for Re atomic columns. To minimize sample drift and scan distortion, images were acquired under stabilized conditions with active vibration isolation and electromagnetic field compensation.

STEM image processing and bond length analysis

Quantitative analysis of in-plane Re-Re distances was performed using a fully automated and reproducible image-processing workflow implemented in MATLAB. Raw ADF-STEM images were first converted to double precision and subjected to background suppression using Gaussian filtering to remove low-frequency intensity variations. Contrast enhancement was applied to emphasize atomic-column features while preserving local intensity maxima. Re atomic columns were then identified as regional intensity maxima above a robust statistical threshold, and their positions were refined to sub-pixel accuracy using intensity-weighted centroid fitting within a local window. Regions containing scale bars and annotations were excluded from analysis to avoid spurious detections.

The extracted Re-column coordinates were used to compute nearest-neighbor distances corresponding to characteristic in-plane Re-Re spacings. Distance distributions were constructed from all valid atomic pairs, and statistical metrics, including mean values, were calculated after excluding outliers beyond the upper quantile. To verify detection fidelity, an overlay of the identified Re-column positions on the original STEM images is provided in the Supplementary Information, demonstrating consistent atomic-column localization across the entire field of view.

For the PAW pseudo-potential, we included 5d5 and 6s2 valence electrons for Re; for S, the n = 3 shell is included as a valence configuration (3s2 and 3p4). A 10 × 10 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-point grid was used for monolayer unit cell geometry optimization calculations, and a plane-wave basis set with an energy cutoff of 500 eV. Good convergence was obtained with these parameters, and the total energy was converged within 1 × 10-6 eV per atom. 3×3 mini-array demonstration

For the 3 × 3 mini-array demonstration, XOR classification was used as a compact nonlinear benchmark. A 2 × 3 × 1 neural network was trained in PyTorch using two binary inputs, three hidden neurons with tanh activation, and one sigmoid output neuron. Binary cross-entropy loss was minimized using the Adam optimizer.

To map the hidden layer to the 3 × 3 CIM block, the input vector was augmented with a constant bias term, xaug = [x1,x2,1]T. The hidden-layer computation was therefore expressed as zh = Wh xaug, where Wh is a 3 × 3 matrix. Each row of Wh corresponds to one hidden neuron and each column corresponds to x1, x2, or the bias input.

Signed weights were represented using differential positive and negative conductance components in the device-calibrated mapping. The three hidden activations were then passed to a lightweight readout stage implementing the final output neuron. The trained model was evaluated on the four XOR input patterns, and the predicted probabilities and decision boundary were recorded as the mini-array inference demonstration.

Deep neural network implementation

For both MNIST and FMNIST, a naïve fully-connected DNN (784 × 128 × 10) is trained on a workstation (Intel i9-13900K, NVIDIA 3080Ti, Windows 11) using PyTorch with ReLU activation. Before training, each image is quantized to 2-bit black-and-white image. After training (10 epochs), inference accuracy is measured on the test set. For device-calibrated evaluation, weights in selected fully-connected layers are mapped following the workflow above, using the fitted multiplier levels in Supplementary Fig. 27 and a task-specific global scaling factor (e.g., 4000 for MNIST/FMNIST).
