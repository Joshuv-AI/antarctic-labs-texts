UK consensus statement on priorities to support the process of withdrawal of veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation
Authors: Rebecca Lewis, Emma Jackson, Patrick Collins, Emilia Tomarchio, Christopher Remmington, Lorenzo Giosa, Valentina Camarda, Katherine Cowan, Luigi Camporota, Louise Rose
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with acute severe respiratory failure receiving veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) have high mortality risk. Management of the process of VV-ECMO withdrawal is challenging, especially in awake patients. Yet there is no guidance to guide clinicians to manage these morally distressing and ethically ambiguous decisions. The study aimed to establish consensus-based priorities for management of the process of VV-ECMO withdrawal to reduce practice variability and improve care quality.MethodsOur process for generating priority statements for consideration at our consensus meeting included semi-structured interviews and a scoping review. We recruited healthcare professionals with experience in VV-ECMO withdrawal to participate in interviews and a consensus meeting led by an independent facilitator. Using modified nominal group technique, participants discussed and voted on statements, categorising them as essential, desirable, or not required. A subsequent voting round identified key priorities for future research.ResultsDuring our item generation process, we interviewed 12 healthcare professionals with VV-ECMO expertise and identified 41 publications (21 empiric studies and 20 opinion pieces) focused on ECMO withdrawal. We developed 16 priority statements from this data, of which 17 participants representing seven UK ECMO centres considered eight as essential, six desirable, one not required; one was without consensus. Areas for future research highlighted were developing a standardised framework for managing disagreements; creating tools to enhance communication and documentation of information provided; and designing quality improvement strategies to ensure consistency in the clinical and technical aspects of VV-ECMO withdrawal.ConclusionsUsing rigorous methods, we have established consensus on eight essential and six desirable priorities for management of VV-ECMO withdrawal and three future research priorities. These consensus-based priorities should be viewed as a developmental step towards a standardised approach to support patients, family members, and clinicians through this challenging clinical situation.Keywords: consensus, end-of-life care, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, moral distress, veno-venous-ECMO, withdrawal

Veno-venous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VV-ECMO) is increasingly used as a rescue therapy for patients with acute, severe, but potentially reversible, respiratory failure that is refractory to conventional ventilation, or when ventilation is deemed injurious.1 Although the primary use for VV-ECMO is as a ‘bridge to recovery’, overall mortality remains high.2 In patients who fail to achieve sufficient native lung recovery and who are not candidates for transplantation,3 VV-ECMO enables survival for extended periods, referred to as a ‘bridge to nowhere’.3 This results in a morally, ethically, and emotionally charged situation with patients who can be kept alive, and potentially conscious, with highly invasive supportive technology, but who have limited chance of surviving outside of a specialised ICU.4

Patients requiring VV-ECMO with minimal viable native lung function represent one of the most extreme forms of medical futility.5 Caring for these patients can be morally distressing and ethically challenging,6 especially if the patient is awake and interactive while VV-ECMO dependent. Unlike withdrawal of other life-sustaining support, VV-ECMO withdrawal is perceived somewhat differently as death is immediate.7 However, once a decision is made that continued treatment is futile, ECMO might become a means of prolonging the dying process rather than providing a therapeutic benefit. This prolonged dying process can exacerbate suffering for patients and complicate the grieving process for families.

Given equivocal, uncertain, and variable opinions on the concept of medical futility, it is not surprising that discontinuation of VV-ECMO results in conflict, moral distress, and disagreements among clinical teams, and with patients and their family members.8,9 Conflicts with resultant communication breakdown among and between clinical teams and the patient and family result in loss of trust and can take considerable time and resource to resolve.9 The emotional and psychological impact on clinical teams can be immeasurable and result in poor team morale, moral distress, and burnout resulting in loss of the limited supply of these highly skilled team members.10 Ultimately these ethical conflicts and prolongation of futile interventions contribute to harm, moral distress, impaired decision-making, distress, and resource depletion.

Robust evidence to guide the management of VV-ECMO withdrawal9 is lacking, with limited resources available to support clinicians navigating these complex decisions.11 The primary objective of this study was to establish expert consensus on priorities to guide multidisciplinary ECMO teams in decision-making and care delivery during VV-ECMO withdrawal and end-of-life care. A secondary objective was to identify key research priorities to advance understanding and improve practices in VV-ECMO withdrawal.

Methods

Statement generation

To generate statements for consideration at our consensus meetings, we conducted semi-structured interviews (see Supplementary material for interview guide) followed by a scoping review. For interview participation, we recruited multiprofessional representatives from the UK ECMO network with experience in VV-ECMO withdrawal and end-of-life care. We used purposive sampling and snowballing recruitment methods. Interviews were conducted via Microsoft Teams (Redmond, WA, USA) enabling digital recording and transcription. Transcripts were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis as described.12

To conduct our scoping review, we developed a search strategy with an experienced information specialist, and then searched Ovid MEDLINE, CINAHL, Embase Classic+Embase, APA PsycInfo, and Web of Science (see Supplementary material for our search strategies). We included studies, guidelines, consensus statements, editorials, and opinion pieces focused on withdrawal, end-of-life care, and palliative care for all ECMO types (i.e. veno-arterial and VV) and for other cardiac support/assist devices. We broadened our scoping review to all ECMO types and other cardiac support devices as we hypothesised there would be limited evidence specific to VV-ECMO. For pragmatic reasons, we limited inclusion to studies published in English. We also searched web sites of relevance to ECMO, and professional organisations and societies relevant to intensive care and palliative care. Two reviewers (RL, EJ, PC, ET, CR, LG, VC) independently screened studies to confirm inclusion and subsequently extracted data relevant to our study objectives.

To generate draft priority statements for the consensus meeting, we first established three domains: (1) decision-making process of withdrawal of VV-ECMO support; (2) communication surrounding withdrawal of VV-ECMO support with patients and surrogates; and (3) the process of planned withdrawal of VV-ECMO support that was consistently evidenced in both the semi-structured interviews and our scoping review findings. We then identified key themes within these domains to inform the drafting of statements for consideration at the consensus meeting. We finalised the statements wording through iterative team discussion.

Consensus building

Participants were recruited from eight commissioned ECMO centres in the UK with invitations to participate sent to the ECMO leads at each centre. Before the consensus meeting, participants were sent study packs outlining the meeting purpose, background information, and the proposed priority statements for discussion.

The consensus meeting was led by an independent and experienced facilitator (KC). During the meeting, we used a modified nominal group technique.13 Participants were divided into small breakout groups with a mix of professions and representation from different ECMO centres. Each participant was asked to present three (of the 16) statements they thought most important and three least important to include in the consensus document with a brief explanation. This was followed by a small group discussion to understand different perspectives and to review any statements not yet discussed. Small group membership was then shuffled and this process repeated to allow participants to hear more viewpoints and points raised from discussions in other groups. The groups then came back together for a chance to share reflections and raise questions. All discussions were recorded and transcribed for review by the research team.

Participants were then asked to vote using Google Forms (Google, Mountain View, CA, USA) on each statement as either essential, desirable, or not required. For a recommendation to achieve consensus for inclusion in the final document, a majority of >50% voting as essential was required. If there was no majority, then no consensus on inclusion was met. After voting, these results were presented to the group. Participants were then asked to vote for three statements of those voted essential or desirable, to gain consensus on the priorities for future research.

Ethical considerations

We received ethical approval for the study from the King’s College London Minimal Risk Committee (Registration Number: MRSU-21/22-32299). Written informed consent was obtained from interview and consensus workshop participants.

Scoping review

Our search strategies identified 21 657 references of which we screened 338 at full-text stage, and we identified 41 publications meeting our inclusion criteria (see Supplementary material for PRISMA flow diagram). This included 20 editorials and opinion pieces, 19 observational studies, and two qualitative studies. Of the 21 empirical studies, most (33, 80%) were conducted in the USA (see Table 2 for further characteristics). We identified six themes from the 41 publications: (1) communication and decision-making (10 publications); (2) ethical issues and the need for ethical consultation (10 publications); (3) processes of withdrawal (nine publications); (4) palliative care involvement (eight publications); (5) characterising death (two publications); and (6) impact on healthcare professionals (two publications) (see Supplementary Table 1 for themes and key findings).

Essential recommendations

The following eight statements were considered essential for inclusion in the guidance document.

Statement A. Multiprofessional team involvement is pivotal to impartial and equitable decision-making processes taking into consideration the perspectives of all individuals involved in patient care and distributing the burden or responsibility of decision-making. This involvement was seen to help with support for the decisions made and to facilitate consistent communication with the patient and their family. Participants acknowledged that achieving truly collaborative decision-making is challenging given the numerous professionals involved.

Statement B. Patient and family centrality in the decision-making process resonated throughout discussions with a core value of patient-centred care as a priority. Participants identified that this has a profound impact on patient and family psychological well-being alleviating distress not only at the time of decision-making but also family distress after the withdrawal process.

Statement E. Participants recognised that disagreements between the healthcare team and family members can rapidly descend into an emotive battle. In some centres, early identification of patients who require complex decisions and use of a standardised approach to manage conflict had worked well for reducing conflict. This standardised approach can reduce the emotion and burden from decision-making discussions whilst providing reassurance that the patient’s best interests are being kept at the forefront of decision-making.

Statement H. In the UK, the Mental Health Capacity Act 200514 assumes patients possess capacity unless proven otherwise. Awake VV-ECMO patients can have compromised capacity owing to sedative medications or hypoxaemia which should be evaluated before conversations about treatment goals. Despite these challenges, patient involvement in these discussions was seen as paramount as was provision of a realistic portrayal of the limitations of continuing ECMO support as a treatment strategy when it is no longer a bridge to recovery. Such discussions should be supported by appropriately trained healthcare professionals including palliative care and psychology.

Statement J. There was considerable discussion regarding the optimal timing for initiating conversations with patients and family about managing expectations of VV-ECMO treatment. Participants emphasised the importance of early and consistent communication to pre-empt potential conflicts and disagreements both within the healthcare team and with patients and family members. However, participants acknowledged the challenge of defining what ‘early’ communication meant in practice. Participants also acknowledged the need for careful language selection with terms such as stable having different meaning for patients and their families compared with healthcare professionals.

Statement L. Discussion on the role of palliative care emphasised the importance of their involvement, but more importantly, the timing of when they should be brought into conversations. There was strong consensus that their involvement should occur early, enabling a more proactive role to support patients, families, and staff. Participants felt this early inclusion allowed a more personalised and trusting relationship to develop, thereby facilitating improved communication.

Statement O. Psychological support for patients and family members was viewed as crucial with timing of its introduction an important element to consider. Integrating early psychological support alongside palliative care was seen as a strategy to ensure comprehensive and timely support for both patients and their families.

Statement P. Participants identified the significant moral burden on the multiprofessional team when making withdrawal decisions, with appropriate psychological support for staff also viewed as crucial. Furthermore, there was a consensus that access to psychological support should be standard rather than an exception. Careful and appropriate use of debriefing, coupled with psychological support were considered to play a pivotal role in sustaining the psychological well-being of the team.

Desirable recommendations

The following six statements were considered essential for inclusion in the guidance document.

Statement C. Participants emphasised that transparency and standardisation of transplant eligibility decision-making involving the patient and family are essential across transplant centres, particularly when not co-located with an ECMO centre. There was consensus that, for potentially eligible patients, transplant should be considered as a potential destination therapy. However, participants also acknowledged challenges and inconsistency in the definition of who is an ‘appropriate’ candidate for transplant.

Statement F. Participants stressed the importance of access to an expert second opinion for complex and challenging cases relating to withdrawal. This already exists for discussions regarding determination of VV-ECMO initiation. Some participants discussed the current physician-focused system and the need to capture opinions of various professions during this expert consultation. Participants suggested that a register of VV-ECMO treatment discontinuations could be beneficial as a resource to assist with challenging cases.

Statement I. Early introduction of the concept of futility and possibility of withdrawal generated significant debate. Determining the appropriate timing to introduce this concept was acknowledged as exceptionally challenging, with the consensus being that appropriate timing varied and was case-sensitive. In some centres, the ability to discuss possible withdrawal at the time of consent was not feasible as consent to ECMO treatment was not actively sought but assumed given that without VV-ECMO the patient would die. Participants also acknowledged that at the time of VV-ECMO initiation families usually do not have the emotional or cognitive capacity to comprehend concepts of futility. Participants therefore recommended provision of standardised written information provided on VV-ECMO initiation about the potential for treatment futility.

Statement K. Participants recommended a structured framework for a communication plan accessible to all team members and focusing on patient-centred treatment goals with regular review and consistent communication including active listening as a standard for care. The potential for case managers to ensure communication continuity for families was also suggested though acknowledged as reliant on funding for this role.

Statement M. Using a checklist was considered desirable but not essential, given the constraints of a standardised approach to VV-ECMO withdrawal and the need for individualised patient care. Participants recognised that a checklist could be helpful to ensure all important aspects of care are considered, given the often highly emotive and distressing situation of VV-ECMO withdrawal, particularly in awake patients. They acknowledged a checklist could provide team members with reassurance that they were delivering appropriate care. Participants recommended the checklist to comprise aspects of symptom and circuit management.

Statement N. Participants considered a process for structured debriefing, particularly if psychological support was available as desirable but not essential. Participants considered in-person debriefing sessions more effective, with the possibility of involving family members. Debriefing should aim to address moral distress, provide a platform for team members to express their opinions, address any concerns, and gain insight into the collective experience utilising it as a learning experience.

Not required

One statement was considered not required for inclusion in the guidance document.

Statement G. Regarding the decision-making process for withdrawal of VV-ECMO support, routine involvement of ethics committees was considered not required. Participants highlighted that the intricacies of these cases required a specific and nuanced skill set gained through direct involvement in VV-ECMO care that ethics committee members generally do not possess. Ethics committee involvement was seen as potentially complicating matters rather than providing clarity. Furthermore, when ethics committees had been involved, it was noted that the process was prolonged, causing increased distress for all parties involved. Additionally, not all organisations and ECMO teams have access to an ethics committee, leading to potential disparities in patient care and decision-making.

No consensus achieved

One statement did not gain consensus for inclusion in the guidance document.

Statement D. We did not establish consensus on the statement that the ultimate decision-making regarding appropriateness of withdrawal rests with the medical team using a nationally agreed upon set of criteria. Discussion centred on the current absence of such criteria acknowledging that clear definitions are lacking, particularly regarding what constitutes futility in patients with lung failure and no prospect of organ recovery. Therefore, such criteria were considered difficult to produce. Participants also acknowledged significant variability in how VV-ECMO withdrawal is practised both nationally and internationally, further challenging the establishment of standardised criteria. However, participants agreed that having such criteria in place would alleviate pressures associated with decision-making and help to mitigate moral distress experienced when faced with complex decision-making regarding VV-ECMO withdrawal. Another point of discussion was that although a physician usually assumes the role of team leader, decisions regarding VV-ECMO withdrawal require input from the multiprofessional team.

Discussion

Our multistage consensus-building process identified eight essential priorities and six desirable priorities to provide guidance on ECMO withdrawal and end-of-life care. Essential priorities emphasise the importance of multidisciplinary team involvement and a patient- and family-centred process that integrates clinical judgement or opinions with patient or surrogate wishes. Having a standardised framework was seen as a crucial strategy to manage disagreements among the family and the treating team, including access to external opinions and mediation.

Mental capacity assessment was viewed as fundamental when involving awake ECMO patients in decision-making and should involve palliative, psychiatric, or psychological support as appropriate. Other essential criteria included the need for expectation management when initiating ECMO support with discussion of potential negative outcomes and circumstances when withdrawal of ECMO support would be considered, early palliative care involvement, and psychological support for patients, families, and healthcare professionals. Key areas for future research included: (1) exploring a standardised framework for managing disagreement; (2) improving communication and tools for documenting information provided; and (3) developing quality improvement tools for clinical and technical aspects of VV-ECMO withdrawal to ensure consistency.

Priority statements that were considered desirable but not essential included assessment for transplant suitability, having a national panel to consult for complex cases, including discussion of VV-ECMO time-limited trial during the consent process, early and frequent therapy goal-setting involving patients, family, and the healthcare team, having a standardised approach (i.e. checklist) for withdrawal, and structured debriefing processes. The one statement that was deemed not required was the need for ethics committee consultation. Participants referred to experiences when such consultation had made the process of ECMO withdrawal more distressing and time-consuming. This is in contrast to several studies identified in our scoping review that recommended either early involvement in more complex cases or automatic review by an ethics committee at the time of ECMO therapy initiation.4,15,16 None of these studies were conducted in the UK, and might reflect a system in which access to ethics committees and clinical ethics consultants is more integrated within the healthcare system.17

The statement that decision-making responsibility should rest with the medical team based on an agreed set of national criteria did not achieve consensus. Having national criteria to guide decision-making was deemed important considering there are national criteria for ECMO initiation.18 However, participants felt that consensus on withdrawal would be challenging because of the variation in practice, the lack of evidence on best practice, and difficulty in defining irreversible lung injury and futility.19 The statement that decision-making authority for withdrawal should rest solely with the medical team raised concerns about the moral distress this responsibility could place on a single medical consultant. Furthermore, physicians typically spend the least amount of time at the bedside and thus might have less nuanced awareness of patient and family preferences. This again highlights the importance of shared decision-making.20

The statement that decision-making should involve both clinical assessment and patient or surrogate views, desires, and wishes received the most votes as an essential criterion, and reflects opinions identified in our scoping review.7,16 Discussion emphasised the importance of committing to delivering patient-centred care that prioritises the patient’s best interests, even when this might lead to complex and difficult decision-making.7 The challenge of establishing a patient’s wishes from family members who can offer valuable insights to provide a patient-centred approach while avoiding causing distress to family members was highlighted.

Our participants were strong advocates for early palliative care involvement. Several studies in the scoping review also highlighted the benefits of early palliative care involvement21, 22, 23, 24, 25 and recommended involvement for all patients initiated on ECMO.26 However, participants also highlighted variability in access to palliative care with some UK ECMO centres having palliative care involvement from admission for all ECMO patients and others having limited or no involvement. Given that the aim of this consensus work is to reduce practice variability and improve standard of care, we hope that this essential consensus-based priority statement improves equity of access to palliative care.

The extensive discussion surrounding successful communication strategies, with agreement on the need for early and consistent messaging using terms that are understandable for all in the context of ECMO withdrawal, is unsurprising given historical and ongoing challenges, and is supported by other authors.27, 28, 29, 30 Although many of the priorities identified and discussed are broadly applicable to the withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy within general critical care, several aspects are specific to the unique complexities associated with VV-ECMO, particularly the ‘bridge to nowhere’ scenario in which patients continue to receive ECMO but have no viable path to recovery or transplant. Such situations present distinctive challenges not commonly encountered with other life-sustaining interventions. ECMO patients are also, on an average, younger than other critically ill patients.31 They can also experience being alert, interactive, and feeling relatively well with ECMO keeping them alive but with no hope of recovery.32,33 Furthermore, ECMO management usually involves numerous healthcare professionals who may have varying opinions or perspectives. Uncertainty regarding treatment futility can result in communication breakdown and cause distress among patients and families.28

Limitations

There are several limitations to acknowledge. Our study involved a relatively small group of participants from the UK, and despite attempts to have a wide multidisciplinary representation, 50% of participants were physicians and we were unable to recruit an ethicist to participate. Because of the nature of discussions and online group meeting format, such that we were unable to provide direct emotional support if needed, we did not include perspectives from patients or family members. This limits the diversity of perspectives included and highlights a key area of future research. Although many of the priority statements might have broader relevance, our work is reflective of UK-based practices and policies. Therefore, caution is advised against generalisation to other healthcare systems. Future research should consider a more diverse and internationally representative panel to strengthen generalisability and impact. A further limitation is our decision to use a 50% agreement threshold for identifying consensus. This lower threshold was chosen given we were exploring priority statements as opposed to formal guideline development.

Conclusions

Using rigorous consensus-based methods, we identified eight essential and six desirable priorities to inform the process of VV-ECMO withdrawal and end-of-life care, and established three future research priorities. These priorities should be used to support discussion among clinical teams, patients, and family members rather than viewed as formal guidelines.
