The identification of a key gene highlights macrocyclic ring’s role in trichothecene toxicity
Authors: Susan P McCormick, Rosa E Cardoza, Natalia Martínez-Reyes, Karl Vermillion, Mark Busman, Álvaro Rodríguez-González, Pedro A Casquero, Robert H Proctor, Santiago Gutiérrez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbstractTrichothecenes are toxins produced by certain species from several fungal genera, including Aspergillus, Fusarium, Isaria, Paramyrothecium, Stachybotrys, Trichoderma, and Trichothecium. These toxins are of interest because they contribute to the toxigenicity, plant pathogenicity, and/or biological control activities of some fungi. All trichothecenes have the same core (12,13-epoxytrichothec-9-ene or EPT) structure but can differ from one another by the presence or absence of a macrocyclic ring formed from polyketide and isoprenoid substituents esterified to carbon atoms 4 and 15 of EPT, respectively. Genes required for formation and some modifications of EPT have been elucidated, but almost nothing is known about genes specific to the formation of the macrocyclic ring. Therefore, we used genomic, transcriptomic, metabolomic, and gene deletion analyses to identify genes that are required specifically for the formation of the macrocyclic ring. These analyses identified one gene, TRI24, that is predicted to encode an acyltransferase and that is required for macrocyclic ring formation during biosynthesis of macrocyclic trichothecenes by the fungus Paramyrothecium roridum. In addition, a TRI24 deletion mutant of P. roridum caused less severe disease symptoms on common bean and had less antifungal activity than its wild-type progenitor strain. We propose that the reduced aggressiveness and antifungal activity of the mutant resulted from its inability to produce trichothecenes with a macrocyclic ring. To our knowledge, this is the first report of a gene required specifically for the formation of the macrocyclic ring of trichothecenes and that loss of the macrocyclic ring of trichothecenes can alter the biological activities of a fungus.Key points• TRI24 gene is found in all known macrocyclic trichothecene-producing fungi.• A tri24-deletion mutant exhibits a reduction in antifungal and plant disease activities.• TRI24 is the first described gene specific to macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-024-13297-x.Keywords: Macrocyclic trichothecenes, Paramyrothecium roridum, Trichoverrins, Gene deletion, Trichothecene toxicity

Introduction

Trichothecenes are terpenoid toxins produced by species of diverse fungal genera, including Aspergillus, Fusarium, Isaria, Paramyrothecium, Peltaster, Stachybotrys, Trichoderma, and Trichothecium. These toxins are of interest and/or concern because of the biological activities they impart to fungi that produce them. For example, trichothecene production can contribute to the mycotoxigenic activity and/or plant pathogenesis of some Fusarium species (Cuzick et al. 2008; Desjardins et al. 1996) and to the biological control activities of some Trichoderma species (Malmierca et al. 2012; Ryu et al. 2017). In addition, trichothecene production by Stachybotrys species has been implicated in health problems of humans resulting from water-damaged buildings (Nielsen et al. 1998; Straus 2009). The toxic properties of trichothecenes have led to numerous studies to assess their impact in medicine, agriculture, animal breeding, food safety, and toxicology (Kinser et al. 2005; McCormick et al. 2004; Pestka et al. 2004; Chung et al. 2003).

All trichothecene analogs share a core structure consisting of 12,13-epoxytrichothec-9-ene (EPT) but differ from one another by the presence, absence, and types of substituents attached to various positions of EPT (Supplemental Fig. S1) (Ueno 1977, 1984). The patterns of substitutions are used to classify trichothecenes into different groups. In one binary classification system, trichothecene analogs are classified as either macrocyclic or simple. Macrocyclic trichothecenes have a macrocyclic ring formed from a linear polyketide substituent esterified to carbon atom 4 (C4) of EPT, an isoprenoid substituent esterified to C15 of EPT, and an ether bond that connects the polyketide and isoprenoid substituents (Supplemental Fig. S1). Simple trichothecenes lack a macrocyclic ring. Macrocyclic trichothecenes consist of several multi-analog families, including roridins, verrucarins, and satratoxins (Fig. 1), and are among the most toxic trichothecenes that have been described. There is evidence that the macrocyclic ring itself plays a critical role in the high levels of toxicity (Sato and Ueno 1977; Ueno 1977; Wu et al. 2013). In general, a given fungal species produces either simple or macrocyclic trichothecene analogs as end-products of the trichothecene biosynthetic pathway. However, some simple trichothecene analogs, such as verrucarol (4,15-dihydroxy EPT), trichodermadiene, trichoverrols, and trichoverrins, are likely intermediates formed during the biosynthesis of macrocyclic trichothecenes (Degenkolb et al. 2008; Grove 2007; Jarvis et al. 1981).

Chemical structures of examples of the macrocyclic trichothecenes roridins, verrucarins, and satratoxins (left) and putative intermediates of the macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis (right) (modified from Ueno 1983; Grove 2007)

Most genes and the corresponding enzymes required for the biosynthesis of simple trichothecenes have been identified and functionally characterized in species of Fusarium and/or Trichoderma (Proctor et al. 2020; Cardoza et al. 2024). Because of structural similarities, most simple and macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthetic genes should be the same. For example, biosynthesis of both simple and macrocyclic trichothecenes requires two genes (TRI5 and TRI4) that confer the formation of the core EPT structure. Formation of macrocyclic trichothecenes and most simple trichothecenes also requires a gene (TRI22) encoding an enzyme that catalyzes the hydroxylation of EPT at C4. Likewise, biosynthesis of macrocyclic trichothecenes and many simple trichothecenes requires a gene (TRI17) encoding a polyketide synthase that catalyzes the formation of a linear polyketide and two other genes (TRI3 and TRI8) encoding acyltransferases required for esterification of the polyketide to the 4-hydroxyl. Orthologs of all of these genes (i.e., TRI3, TRI4, TRI5, TRI17, TRI18, and TRI22) occur in macrocyclic and most simple trichothecene-producing fungi that have been examined. As is the tendency for other fungal secondary metabolite biosynthetic genes, trichothecene biosynthetic genes (TRIs) tend to be clustered (Proctor et al. 2018; Semeiks et al. 2014; Trapp et al. 1998; Zhu et al. 2020).

In addition to the genes that are required for the biosynthesis of both simple and macrocyclic trichothecenes, it is likely that there are also genes that are specific to macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis. The structures of macrocyclic trichothecene analogs indicate that these genes encode enzymes that catalyze the following biochemical reactions: (1) hydroxylation of EPT at C15; (2) conversion of a primary metabolic isoprenoid to verrucarinoyl, the isoprenoid intermediate that becomes part of the macrocyclic ring; (3) esterification of verrucarinoyl to EPT via the 15-hydroxyl; and (4) formation of the ether bond between the verrucarinoyl and the polyketide at C4 (Fig. 1). To our knowledge, the genes that confer these macrocyclic trichothecene-specific reactions have not been identified. Although most simple trichothecenes lack an oxygen atom at C15, most Fusarium and Isaria trichothecenes, which are simple trichothecenes, have an oxygen atom at C15. The presence of this oxygen atom is conferred by the TRI11 gene, which encodes cytochrome P450 monooxygenase that catalyzes trichothecene 15-hydroxylation. Macrocyclic trichothecene-producing species that have been examined lack a TRI11 ortholog (Proctor et al. 2018; Semeiks et al. 2014; Zhu et al. 2020). Therefore, macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis must involve a 15-hydroxylation gene other than TRI11.

The objective of the current study was to identify genes that are specific to macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis. By comparing the gene content within and adjacent to TRI clusters in simple and macrocyclic trichothecene-producing fungi, we identified two genes that were present in all the macrocyclic trichothecene producers but absent in simple trichothecene producers. Gene deletion, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses of the roridin-producing fungus Paramyrothecium roridum indicated that one of these two genes contributes to the formation of the macrocyclic ring of roridins. We also found evidence that loss of the ability to esterify verrucarinoyl to the 15-hydroxyl and, therefore, loss of the ability to produce the macrocyclic ring reduced the pathogenicity and antifungal activity of P. roridum. Thus, this study provides insight into the genetic basis for biosynthesis of macrocyclic trichothecenes and, to our knowledge, is the first report on the functional characterization of a gene that is specific to macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture conditions

P. roridum (formerly Myrothecium roridum) strain NRRL 2183 was the wild-type progenitor strain used in the current study. This and all other strains derived from it were grown on CMD_ULE medium (5% yellow corn meal, 0.5% PDB, 2% agar) and incubated at 28 °C in the dark for 7–14 days, depending on the strain.

Trichoderma arundinaceum strain IBT 40837 (Ta37) was used for heterologous expression of the P. roridum TRI24 gene. Ta37 and transformants derived from it were grown by incubation at 28 °C for 5–7 days on a PDA medium prepared from Potato Dextrose Broth (PDB) (Becton Dickinson Co., East Rutherford, NJ, USA) amended with 2% agar (Oxoid Ltd.).

For RNA purification, conidia of P. roridum and T. arundinaceum strains were inoculated in PDB medium at a final concentration of 1 × 106 conidia/mL and incubated at 28 °C and 200 rpm for the time indicated for each experiment.

Rhizoctonia solani strain R43 was used in the antifungal assays. This strain is a highly virulent phytopathogen (Mayo-Prieto et al. 2015) and was obtained as accession PAULER006 from the Pathogens and Antagonists Culture Collection at the University of León, Spain (PAULE). To produce inoculum for antifungal assays, strain R43 was grown on a PDA medium and incubated at 28 °C for 7 days.

The yeast Kluyveromyces marxianus strain CECT 1018, used for antifungal assays, was grown in malt extract-peptone-dextrose (MA) medium (2% malt extract, 0.1% peptone, 2% glucose, 2% agar) at 30 °C for 2–3 days.

Seeds of Phaseolus vulgaris (common bean) variety “Riñón” harvested in 2019 and without visible disease symptoms were selected for this assay. The seeds were surface inoculated with P. roridum spores as described previously (Mayo-Prieto et al. 2015).

Solanum lycopersicum variety “Marmande” (Semillas Battle S.A., Barcelona, Spain) was used for fungal-plant interaction studies.

Plasmid construction

Construction of p∆tri24 plasmid for P. roridum TRI24 gene deletion

Regions adjacent to the P. roridum TRI24 gene were PCR amplified using oligonucleotides PRORI_TRI24_3R_F_KpnI/PRORI_TRI24_3R_R_SmaI and PRORI_TRI24_5R-F_SmaI/PRORI_TRI24_5R_R_BamHI (Supplemental Table S1) to obtain the fragments corresponding to the 5′ (1,028 bp) and 3′ (1,000 bp) regions, respectively. The fragments were digested with SmaI-KpnI and BamHI-SmaI, respectively, and after gel purification, they were ligated to a pBluescript KS + vector (Stratagene, La Jolla, CA, USA) previously digested with BamHI and KpnI endonucleases. The resulting plasmid, pB_T24_3R_5R (4,980 bp), was linearized with SmaI, dephosphorylated with alkaline phosphatase (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and ligated to a 2,710-bp fragment containing the hygromycin (hyg) resistance cassette, which was isolated from pAN7-1 (Punt et al. 1987) by digestion with Ecl136II-HindIII, and treated with Klenow fragment (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). In the resulting 7,695-bp plasmid, p∆tri24, the hyg resistance cassette was inserted between the 5′ and 3′ flanking regions of P. roridum TRI24 gene but in the opposite orientation of TRI24 transcription (Supplemental Fig. S2A). Plasmid p∆tri24 was linearized with XhoI prior to its transformation into P. roridum protoplasts.

Construction of plasmids to express P. roridum TRI24 and TRI25

Open reading frames (ORFs) corresponding to the P. rodidum TRI24 (1,512 bp) and TRI25 (1,371 bp) were amplified from the fungal genome using oligonucleotide pairs PRORI_TRI24_ATG/PRORI_TRI24_end and PRORI_TRI25_ATG/PRORI_TRI25_end, respectively (Supplemental Table S1) and Q5 high-fidelity DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). The resulting amplified fragments were phosphorylated with the polynucleotide kinase (Fermentas, Vilnius, Lithuania), and after gel purification, the fragments were ligated to the pTAcbh plasmid (Cardoza et al. 2015) linearized with NcoI, filled in with Klenow, and dephosphorylated. The resulting plasmids, pTC_PRORI_TRI24a (6,851 bp) and pTC_PRORI_TRI25a (6,701 bp), were digested with EcoRI, filled in with Klenow, and dephosphorylated. The linearized plasmids were ligated to the 1,591-bp fragment containing the ble (bleomycin/phleomycin resistance) cassette, which was isolated from pJL43b1 (Gutiérrez et al. 1997) by digestion with HindIII, filled with Klenow, and then digested with Ecl136II. The final plasmids, pTC_PRORI_TRI24a_ble_a (8,450 bp) (Supplemental Fig. S2C) and pTC_PRORI_TRI25a_ble_a (8,309 bp) (Supplemental Fig. S2D), were linearized with NdeI and NheI, respectively, prior to transformation of protoplasts of the P. roridum tri24-deleted and P. roridum tri25-deleted mutants, respectively. Note that in these plasmids, P. roridum TRI24 and TRI25 genes are expressed under the control of Ptadir promoter region, which corresponds to the promoter of the tadir gene, a highly expressed gene isolated from Trichoderma harzianum, and the transcriptional terminator region of the T. reesei cellobiohydrolase gene (Tcbh2) (Gutiérrez et al. 2021).

Construction of p∆tri25 plasmid for P. roridum TRI25 gene deletion

Regions adjacent to P. roridum TRI25 were PCR amplified using oligonucleotides PRORI_TRI25_5R_F_BamHI/PRORI_TRI25_5R_R_SmaI and PRORI_TRI25_3R-F_SmaI/PRORI_TRI25_3R_R_KpnI (Supplemental Table S1) to obtain the fragments corresponding to the 5′ (1,009 bp) and 3′ (979 bp) regions, respectively. The fragments were digested with BamHI-SmaI and SmaI-KpnI, respectively, and after gel purification, they were ligated to a pBluescript KS + vector (Stratagene, San Diego, CA, USA) previously digested with BamHI and KpnI endonucleases. The resulting plasmid, pB_T24_3R_5R (4,931 bp), was linearized with SmaI, dephosphorylated with alkaline phosphatase (Thermo Scientific), and ligated to a 2,710-bp fragment containing the hyg resistance cassette, which was isolated from pAN7-1 (Punt et al. 1987) by digestion with Ecl136II-HindIII, and treated with Klenow fragment (Thermo Scientific). In the resulting 7,646-bp plasmid, p∆tri25, the hyg resistance cassette was inserted between the 5′ and 3′ flanking regions of P. roridum TRI25 gene in the opposite orientation to TRI25 transcription (Supplemental Fig. S2B). Plasmid p∆tri25 was linearized with ApaI prior to its transformation into P. roridum protoplasts.

P. roridum transformation

After growing P. roridum on CMD_ULE medium at 28 °C for 1–2 weeks, conidia were harvested and added to 100 mL of CM medium (0.5% yeast extract, 0.5% malt extract, 0.5% glucose) to a final concentration of 5 × 106 conidia per mL medium. The resulting cultures were grown in a rotary shaker at 250 rpm and 28 °C. After 18–22 h, 25 mL of the culture was centrifuged at 7000 rpm in a Sorvall™ RC 5C Plus (Kendro Laboratory Products, Newtown, CT, USA) for 10 min at room temperature. The pellet was washed three times with 0.6 M NaCl and then resuspended in 20 mL of 0.6 M NaCl containing 150 mg of the Lysing Enzyme (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 150 mg of Driselase (Sigma-Aldrich), and 1000 units of Zymolyase (ZymoResearch, Irvine, CA, USA). This mixture was incubated at 28 °C for 8–9 h at 80 rpm. Once the protoplasts were released, an equal volume of solution 1 (5% PEG 8000, 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 50 mM CaCl2, dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) 5 µL) was added, leaving a concentration of between 7 × 107 and 1 × 108 protoplasts/mL. The protoplast solution was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C, where they remained stable for a period of up to 7–9 months.

For the transformation, 10 µg of DNA (p∆tri24 or p∆tri25), 100 µL of filter sterilized STC solution (1.4 M sorbitol, 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 50 mM CaCl2), 50 µL of filter sterilized PEG solution (25% PEG 8000, 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.5, 50 mM CaCl2), and 100 µL of the protoplast solution were mixed in a 10-mL tube.

The final mixture was aliquoted and added to new 10-mL tubes, to which 6.7 mL of regeneration medium was added (0.8 M sucrose, 0.1% yeast extract, 0.1% NZY medium (Sigma-Aldrich), 1.6% Bacto-Agar), mixed gently, and poured onto Petri dishes having a base of 20 mL of regeneration medium previously prepared. After 24-h incubation at 28 °C, the surface of each of the resulting plates was covered with 10 mL of 1% agar containing 500 µg hygromycin B per mL. The plates were then incubated again at 28 °C for 5–7 days until the growth of the putative transformants was visible.

Colonies of the putative transformants were transferred to tryptic soy agar medium (TSA, 1.7% casein peptone, 0.3% soya peptone, 0.5% NaCl, 0.25% K2HPO4, 0.25% glucose, pH 7.3) without hygromycin and incubated at 28 °C. After 5 days of growth, the resulting colonies were transferred to TSA medium containing hygromycin at 500 µg/mL and incubated for 7 days at 28 °C. Colonies that grew on the latter selective medium were selected for further analysis.

For complementation studies, the TRI24 or TRI25 overexpression plasmids described above were transformed into the corresponding deletion mutants using the transformation procedure described above, except that phleomycin 500 µg/mL containing medium was used for selection of the transformants.

T. arundinaceum transformation

Transformation of T. arundinaceum protoplasts was carried out as previously described (Cardoza et al. 2006; Proctor et al. 1999), using the plasmid pTCS**_PRORI_TRI24a_ble_a, linearized with the NdeI endonuclease (Supplemental Fig. S2C). Transformants were selected in Czapek + sorbitol medium (3% sucrose, 0.2% NaNO3, 0.05% K2HPO4, 0.05% MgSO4·7H2O, 1 M sorbitol, 1.5% Bacto-Agar) amended with phleomycin (100 µg/mL). Once growth on the selective medium was visible, putative transformants were first transferred to TSA medium without phleomycin and subsequently to TSA medium with phleomycin 150 µg/mL. All incubations were performed at 28 °C for 5–7 days.

Antifungal assays

K. marxianus

Two to three colonies of K. marxianus grown on TSA medium were inoculated in 30 mL of liquid MA medium and incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 220 rpm for 24 h. An aliquot of the resulting culture was diluted to obtain an optical density (O.D.) of 1 at 560 nm (1.1 × 109 cells/mL). A 200-µL aliquot of this dilution was then added to 100 mL of MA medium amended with 1% agar to a final concentration of 2.2 × 106 cells/mL.

To perform the bioassay in Petri dishes, 20-mL aliquots of the K. marxianus MA medium (1% agar), prepared as described above, were poured into 9-cm-diameter Petri dishes. For analysis of culture broths, 60 µL of each sample was added to 7-mm-diameter wells previously performed in the medium. The plates were kept at 4 °C for 1 h and then incubated at 30 °C for 20–24 h.

For the thin-layer chromatography (TLC) bioassay (= antibiogram), the method described by Cardoza and coworkers (2000) was used, with some modifications. Once the TLC plates were developed, they were placed in 241 x 242 x 20 mm square Petri dishes (Nunc™, Thermo Scientific), and then, 200 mL of the K. marxianus MA medium (1% agar) mixture, prepared as described above, was poured over the TLC plate. The plate was then kept at 4 °C for 30 min to allow the medium to solidify and subsequently at 30 °C for 20–24 h.

To aid visualization of the inhibition zones 100 mL of 10% trichloroacetic acid was added to each Petri plate. After 10 min of incubation with gently stirring; then, they were stained with Coomassie blue (0.1% Coomassie blue, 1% acetic acid, 45% methanol) for 20 min; and finally, they were washed with a 1% acetic acid to 30% methanol solution for 20–30 min.

Agar diffusion assay with R. solani

For each P. roridum strain, a 60-µL aliquot of a PDB culture filtrate was loaded into a 7-mm-diameter hole made in the center of PDA medium (1% agar) contained in a 90-mm-diameter Petri plate. Plates were placed at 4 °C for 24 h to allow diffusion of the filtrate into the agar. A 7-mm plug from a 7-day-old PDA culture of R. solani was then placed into the same hole in the PDA into which the culture filtrate had been loaded 24 h before. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for an additional 7–10 days, after which the diameter of the resulting R. solani colony was measured and photographed. Percent of radial growth inhibition was calculated as described previously (Cardoza et al. 2007; Royse and Ries 1978). PDA plates loaded with uninoculated PDB medium were used as a control.

Plant disease assays

Tomato leaf assay

Conidia of the different P. roridum strains used in these assays were obtained from 7- to 14-day-old CMD_ULE cultures. Conidia were collected by flooding the surface of the cultures with 10-mL water and scraping with a sterile glass pipette. The resulting conidial suspensions were diluted to a concentration of 5 × 106 spores/mL in germination buffer (20 mM glucose; 20 mM KH2PO4). Fifteen-microliter aliquots of the suspensions were placed as spots onto detached tomato leaves (two spots/strain on each tomato leaf). Tomato plants were grown for 4 weeks as previously described (Malmierca et al. 2012), and leaves were detached from these plants to determine the phytotoxicity of the different P. roridum strains assayed in this study.

Bean seed assay

These assays were performed as described previously (Rodríguez-González et al. 2020). Briefly, suspensions of P. roridum conidia from the different strains used in this study were prepared as indicated above and then applied to bean seeds using a manual loading Potter tower (Burkard Scientific Limited, High Wycombe, England) (Potter 1952) with an air pressure of 40 kPa. One milliliter of each conidial suspension (1 × 107 spores/mL) was used for each treatment, and five replicates were performed per treatment. After 15 days of incubation, five beans were placed on the surface of PDA medium in a 90-mm-diameter Petri dish and incubated at 28 °C in the dark to detect the fungal growth in the plate and to evaluate bean germination.

Genome sequencing

Genome sequences of two P. roridum deletion mutants, strains ∆tri24.36 and ∆tri25.30, were generated at Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea; https://dna.macrogen.com) using an Illumina platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequence assemblies were generated as previously described (Zanfaño et al. 2024). The processed sequence reads for the two mutants were submitted to the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) as accession SRR28588832 (Bioproject PRJNA1097601) (∆tri24.36) and SRR28589284 (Bioproject PRJNA1097615) (∆tri25.30).

Accession numbers for P. roridumTRI24 and TRI25 genes

Sequences of these two genes were deposited at the NCBI/GenBank repository under the accession numbers PP584324 and PP584325, respectively.

RNA-seq and qPCR analyses

For RNA-seq and qPCR experiments, strains were grown from 24 to 72 h. RNAs were purified, and cDNAs were synthesized as described previously (Lindo et al. 2018). cDNAs were quantified using a Nanodrop ND-1000 (Thermo Scientific). qPCR reactions were carried out on a Step One system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) using the express SYBR green qPCR super-Mix Universal (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) as described by the manufacturer, and oligonucleotides designed for analysis of P. roridum genes in the TRI region, and the gene for α-actin, the latter used as a housekeeping-reference gene (Supplemental Table S1). Finally, the qPCR Ct values were analyzed using the REST©2009 software (Pfaffl et al. 2002) to determine the expression ratios as well as the oligo pair amplification efficiencies. Each measurement was done in triplicate.

Metabolite analysis

TLC

For TLC analysis, two growth and analysis methods were used:

Method 1.1 × 106 conidia/mL was added to 100 mL of CM medium in a 500-mL Erlenmeyer flask, and the resulting culture was incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. After 48 h, 20 mL of the culture was used to inoculate 100 mL of PDB medium, and incubated in the same conditions for 72 h. After this time, mycelia were collected by filtration, and the filtrates were extracted twice with ethyl acetate. The upper phase from each extraction was recovered, evaporated, and the solid debris resuspended in 1/10 of the original volume of acetonitrile. The resulting extracts were loaded onto TLC plates (see below) that were developed in an ethyl acetate: hexane solution (v/v 70:30).

Method 1.1 × 106 conidia/mL was added to 100 mL of CM medium in a 500-mL Erlenmeyer flask, and the resulting culture was incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. After 48 h, 20 mL of the culture was used to inoculate 100 mL of PDB medium, and incubated in the same conditions for 72 h. After this time, mycelia were collected by filtration, and the filtrates were extracted twice with ethyl acetate. The upper phase from each extraction was recovered, evaporated, and the solid debris resuspended in 1/10 of the original volume of acetonitrile. The resulting extracts were loaded onto TLC plates (see below) that were developed in an ethyl acetate: hexane solution (v/v 70:30).

Method 2. Strains were grown at 28 °C on PDA medium for 7 days. Then, seven 7-mm-diameter plugs were collected from the resulting culture for each experimental strain. The plugs were added to 8-mL YEPD medium (5% glucose, 0.1% yeast extract, 0.1% peptone) in 50-mL Erlenmeyer flasks. The resulting cultures were incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 7 days and then extracted with 8 mL ethyl acetate. These extracts were loaded onto TLC plates (see below) that were developed in an ethyl acetate: methanol solution (95%:5%).

Method 2. Strains were grown at 28 °C on PDA medium for 7 days. Then, seven 7-mm-diameter plugs were collected from the resulting culture for each experimental strain. The plugs were added to 8-mL YEPD medium (5% glucose, 0.1% yeast extract, 0.1% peptone) in 50-mL Erlenmeyer flasks. The resulting cultures were incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 7 days and then extracted with 8 mL ethyl acetate. These extracts were loaded onto TLC plates (see below) that were developed in an ethyl acetate: methanol solution (95%:5%).

For both methods 1 and 2, 10–20 µL of culture extracts and 2–4 µL of trichothecene standard solutions were spotted onto pre-coated ALUGRAM Xtra SIL G/UV254 TLC plates (Macherey–Nagel, Düren, Germany) (Smoragiewicz et al. 1993). The plates were developed in 250 × 250 × 70 mm TLC chambers using the solvent systems described above.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with ultraviolet detection (UV)

Twenty microliters of the concentrated P. roridum culture extract obtained as indicated above (Method 1) was applied to a reverse-phase C18 column of 150 mm × 4.6 mm i.d., 4 μm (octadecyl-silica (ODS)) (YMC Europe GmbH, Dinslaken, Germany), and eluted with a 1 mL/min flux of mobile phase ([90% water plus 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid]/10% acetonitrile).

The program started with the conditions described above and reached 100% acetonitrile in 35 min, held for 5 min, and then returned to 10% acetonitrile at the 50-min time point. Detection of eluting compounds was done at 260 nm.

Stains for developing TLC plates

To characterize the compounds present in the P. roridum cultures, two staining methods were used. In the first method, 4-(p-nitrobenzyl)pyridine (NBP) was used, because it interacts with the 12,13-epoxy group of the trichothecene structure (Takitani et al. 1979; Smoragiewicz et al. 1993). For this purpose, once the TLC plate was developed, it was allowed to dry and subsequently dipped in a 2% solution of NBP in acetone for 3–5 min. After that time, the plate was allowed to dry at room temperature and then heated in an oven at 110 °C for 20 min. Finally, the plate was cooled to room temperature before it was dipped in a 10% acetone solution of tetraethylenepentamine (TEP). In this method, compounds on TLC plates appear as purplish-dark blue spots on a white background. In the second method, vanillin was used, because it stains multiple classes of organic compounds, including those of a terpenoid nature as well as macrocyclic trichothecenes, which are detected as grayish-blue spots on TLC plates (Kommedahl et al. 1987). After the chromatography, TLC plates were allowed to air dry, dipped in a vanillin-sulfuric acid–ethanol solution (2.5 g vanillin-50 mL concentrated sulfuric acid-50 mL ethanol), allowed to air dry again, and finally heated in an oven at 110 °C for 20–30 min. In this case, the reaction of the reagents with the metabolites was permanent.

Preparative TLC

For isolation and purification of compounds, preparative TLC plates (Macherey–Nagel™ Standard SIL G Silica Plates on UV254 glass plates, 2 mm deep; Machery-Nagel, Düren, Germany) were used. Samples were loaded as a horizontal line across the width of the plate. Once the chromatography was finished, the compounds were visualized by UV light (254 nm) and the silica from each one was scraped off with a spatula and deposited in 1.5-mL tubes. The silica was resuspended in 0.5 mL of methanol, mixed vigorously with a vortex mixer for 1–2 min, and then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm at room temperature for 15 min to remove the silica from the sample. The resulting supernatant was evaporated in a vacuum concentrator, and once the methanol was removed from the sample, 100 µL of acetonitrile was added to dissolve the purified compounds. Samples were cleaned using polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) filters (0.22 µm pore diameter).

Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR)

NMR spectra were collected with either a Bruker Avance 500 spectrometer (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) (500.11 MHz) using a 5-mm Broad Band Observe (BBO) probe (Bruker) (for spot 4, 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene), or a JEOL ECZ600R spectrometer (JEOL Ltd., Akishima, Tokio, Japan) (600.17 MHz) using a 5-mm BBO probe (for roridin A and roridin E). All samples were dissolved in methanol-d4 and spectra were acquired at 27 °C (spot 4, 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene) or 25 °C (roridin A and roridin E). Chemical shifts are reported as ppm from tetramethylsilane calculated from the lock signal. Spectra were processed with Topspin 1.3 (Bruker BioSpin GmbH, Mannheim, Germany) or JEOL Delta 6.0.0 (Jeol USA, Inc., Peabody, MA, USA), respectively.

Precursor feeding experiments

Six 7-mm culture plugs were collected with an agar-punch (Bürkle GmbH, Bellingen, Germany) of 7 mm diameter from 7-day-old PDA or 7- to 14-day-old CMD_ULE cultures of T. arundinaceum or P. roridum strains, respectively. The plugs were added to 8-mL liquid YEDP medium (5% glucose, 0.1% yeast extract, 0.1% peptone) in 50-mL Erlenmeyer flasks, amended with 100 µL of acetone solution containing 1.5 mg of the fed trichothecene analogs. The resulting cultures were incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 7 days and then extracted with 8 mL ethyl acetate. The resulting extract was analyzed by HPLC–UV.

Results

Detection of two genes specific to macrocyclic trichothecene-producing fungi

Because TRI genes tend to be clustered (Proctor et al. 2018), we surmised that genes specific to macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis would be located within or near TRI clusters in macrocyclic trichothecene-producing fungi. Examination of TRI cluster regions in fungi that produce or are predicted to produce trichothecenes revealed two genes, tentatively named TRI24 and TRI25, that were present in macrocyclic trichothecene-producing species but absent in simple trichothecene-producing species. When present, TRI24 and TRI25 were typically located near other TRI genes (Fig. 2). TRI24 and TRI25 were not near other TRI genes in the genome sequence assemblies of Monosporascus sp. strain 5C6A and Monosporascus cannonballus strain CBS 586.93, but they were located at or near the ends of the contigs on which they occur. Therefore, they may be near other TRI genes in the genomes of these two fungi.

TRI24 and TRI25 are indicated by white arrows. Gray arrows indicated genes presumably not involved in trichothecene biosynthesis or with unknown functions; the numbers above colored arrows indicate TRI designations (e.g., 17 indicates TRI17). MONOS, Monosporascus sp. 5C6A; SCHAR, Stachybotrys chartarum 40,293; PRORI, Paramyrothecium roridum; TBALE, Trichoderma balearicum; TCRYS, Trichoderma chrystalligenum; TOLIG, Trichoderma oligosporum; TPSYC, Trichoderma psychrophilum

In their analysis of Stachybotrys chartarum, Semeiks et al. (2014) referred to TRI24 and TRI25 orthologs as SAT15 and SAT14, respectively, because these genes occurred in strains that produced the macrocyclic trichothecenes satratoxins but not in strains that did not produce satratoxins. Here, we use TRI rather than SAT designations for these two genes because of their location near known TRI genes and the evidence described below that TRI24 plays a role in trichothecene biosynthesis. In addition, use of TRI rather than SAT designations is consistent with the designations given to all previously described trichothecene biosynthetic genes, i.e., TRI1 and TRI3–TRI23.

In BLASTp analysis using the deduced amino acid sequence of the TRI24-encoded protein (Tri24) as a query sequence against the UniProt/Swiss-Prot (The UniProt Consortium 2023) database, the best hits were LolU proteins (36.14% of identity), which were first described as putative transcription factors involved in biosynthesis of loline alkaloids in species of the fungus Epichloë (Schardl et al. 2007), and the TazD-acetyltransferase (22.54% of identity), which is involved in azaphilone biosynthesis in the fungus Aspergillus terreus (Supplemental Table S2). In the same BLASTp analysis with the deduced Tri25 amino acid sequence, the best hits were to acetyltransferases putatively involved in trichothecene (73.03% of identity), siderophore (46.37% of identity), and lysine (46.63% of identity) biosynthesis in multiple fungi (Supplemental Table S3).

Based on their physical linkage to known TRI genes in macrocyclic trichothecene-producing fungi and their absence in simple trichothecene-producing fungi, we hypothesized that the function of TRI24 and TRI25 is specific to the biosynthesis of macrocyclic trichothecene. To test this hypothesis, we carried out the functional analyses of TRI24 and TRI25 described below.

Deletion and complementation of TRI24 and TRI25

To determine whether TRI24 and TRI25 function in macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis, a deletion mutant for each gene was generated using the wild-type P. roridum strain NRRL 2183 and the double cross-over strategy as described in the “Materials and Methods” section. In addition, each mutant was complemented by transformation with a plasmid designed to overexpress TRI24 or TRI25 in their respective deleted mutants.

TRI24 gene deletion

Following the transformation of the wild-type P. roridum strain NRRL 2183 with the TRI24 deletion plasmid p∆tri24 (Supplemental Fig. S2A), we recovered 51 hygromycin-resistant transformants. In a PCR analysis designed to detect double homologous recombination between p∆tri24 and the 5’ and 3’ flanking regions of TRI24, we identified three transformants (strains ∆tri24.36, ∆tri24.43, and ∆tri24.47) that had the expected amplicon pattern (Supplemental Table S1, Supplemental Fig. S3 and S4). Genome sequence analysis of transformant strain ∆tri24.36 confirmed that TRI24 was deleted and replaced by the hygromycin resistance cassette (Supplemental Fig. S4). Therefore, strain ∆tri24.36, hereafter the ∆tri24 mutant, was selected for further analysis.

Complementation of the ∆tri24 mutant

The ∆tri24 mutant was transformed with plasmid pTCS**_PRORI_TRI24a_ble_a (Supplemental Fig. S2C), which was designed for overexpression of the P. roridum TRI24 gene. Twenty transformants were isolated after selection on phleomycin. In PCR analysis, 11 transformants yielded the 997-bp amplicon expected when the TRI24 complementation construct was present (Supplemental Fig. S5). One of these transformants, strain ∆tri24_T24.5 and hereafter referred to as the complemented ∆tri24 mutant, was selected for further analysis.

Deletion of TRI25

Following the transformation of the wild-type P. roridum with the TRI25 deletion plasmid p∆tri25 (Supplemental Fig. S2B), we recovered 52 hygromycin-resistant transformants. In the PCR analysis designed to detect double homologous recombination between p∆tri25 and the 5’ and 3’ flanking regions of P. roridum TRI25, we identified two transformants (strains ∆tri25.30 and ∆tri25.50) that had the expected amplicon pattern (Supplemental Table S1, Supplemental Fig. S6 and S7). Genome sequence analysis of both strains revealed that in strain ∆tri25.30, the TRI25 coding region was deleted and replaced by the hygromycin resistance cassette. Therefore, strain ∆tri25.30, hereafter the ∆tri25 mutant, was selected for further analysis.

Complementation of the ∆tri25 mutant

The ∆tri25 mutant was transformed with plasmid, pTCS**_PRORI_TRI25a_ble_a (Supplemental Fig. S2D), which was designed for overexpression of the P. roridum TRI25 gene. In PCR analysis of 20 phleomycin-resistant transformants, nine of them yielded the 987-bp product expected to be amplified when the TRI25 complementation construct was present (Supplemental Fig. S8). We selected one of these transformants, strain ∆tri25_T25.2, hereafter referred to as the complemented ∆tri25 mutant, for further analysis.

Effect of TRI24 or TRI25 deletion on trichothecene production

HPLC–UV analysis

Multiple peaks were detected in extracts of culture filtrates of wild-type P. roridum, with some variation between runs over the course of this study (Fig. 3, Supplemental Fig. S9). Nevertheless, three peaks were present consistently in analyses of the wild type. These peaks eluted at 21.7–21.8 min (peak 1), another at 19.1–19.2 (peak 2), and the third at 14.1–14.2 min (peak 3). Note that peak 2 was markedly smaller than peaks 1 and 3.

HPLC analysis of P. roridum wild type (upper central panel), ∆tri24 mutant and ∆tri24-T24.5 complemented transformant (lower left panels), and ∆tri25 mutant and ∆tri25_T25.2 complemented transformant (lower right panels) from 72-h broths. The main peaks were pointed by arrows, and labeled with 1–5, for further reference. Those new peaks detected in the ∆tri24 mutant were pointed with blue arrows

In chromatograms resulting from HPLC–UV analysis of the ∆tri24 mutant, peaks 1, 2, and 3 were not present (Fig. 3). However, the chromatograms of the ∆tri24 mutant included two peaks that did not occur in chromatograms of the wild type. One of these peaks was a doublet at 10.9 and 11.17 min (peak 4), and the other peak was a singlet at 17.6 min (peak 5). This finding indicates that the mutant might have produced two metabolites that the wild type did not produce. In the HPLC–UV chromatogram of the complemented Δtri24 mutant, peaks 1 and 3 were present, indicating at least a partial restoration of the wild-type pattern of metabolite. Peaks 4 and 5 did not occur in the chromatograms of the complemented ∆tri24 mutant (Fig. 3).

HPLC–UV chromatograms resulting from analysis of the ∆tri25 mutant and the complemented transformants, ∆tri25_T25.2 and ∆tri25_T25.9, did not differ markedly from those of the wild-type strain, but there were differences in the heights of peaks 1 and 3 (Fig. 3).

TLC analysis of culture filtrates from wild type and ∆tri24 mutant

In TLC method 1, extracts of 72-h-old PDB culture broths of the wild-type strain had three predominant spots that were designated spots 1–3 (Fig. 4). By contrast, extracts of the ∆tri24 mutant did not have spots 1–3, but instead had two other spots, which were designated spots 4 and 5 (Fig. 4). Note that the five spots detected in TLC analysis are consistent with five peaks detected by HPLC–UV (see Fig. 3).

TLC (left panel) analysis of 72-h PDB broths of wild type (wt) and ∆tri24 mutant (∆T24), and (right panel) antibiogram of the TLC plate against K. marxianus

To check the bioactivity of the spots detected in TLC analysis, an antibiogram with the TLC plate was performed with K. marxianus. The results of the antibiogram indicated that spots 1 and 2 had a strong inhibitory activity against K. marxianus, but spots 3, 4, and 5 lacked such activity (Fig. 4).

In TLC method 2, analysis by TLC of culture extracts of 7-day-old YEPD cultures yielded results that were similar to those observed with Method 1, but with some differences. Analysis of extracts of the ∆tri24 mutant yielded spot 4 but not spot 5 (Supplemental Fig. S10). Furthermore, analysis of the extract of the wild type yielded spots 1, 2, and 3, but also a fourth spot, which was designed as spot 6 (Supplemental Fig. S10A). Spot 3 had a retention factor (Rf) close to but not the same as spot 4 (accumulated by ∆tri24 mutant).

Because spots 1 and 6 were not well resolved in TLC Method 2, additional analyses were performed to confirm that they were distinct compounds. Thus, extracts of the wild type were subjected to preparative TLC in which spots 1 and 6 were scraped from the plates. The resulting silica was extracted with methanol, and the resulting extracts were analyzed by HPLC–UV. The chromatogram from the extract of spot 1 included a large peak at 22.03 min (peak 1) and another much smaller peak at 19.63 min (peak 2) (Supplemental Fig. S11). The HPLC–UV chromatogram from the extract of spot 6 included a large unique peak at 17.3 min, which was designated peak 6, and a small peak at 22.03 min (peak 1) (Supplemental Fig. S11).

TLC plates resulting from TLC Methods 1 and 2 were also stained using the NBP/TEPA method, which stains trichothecenes as blue spots (Takitani et al. 1979). Spots 1, 3, 4, and 6 on the TLC plates were stained in blue, which indicates that they are likely to be trichothecene analogs (Supplemental Fig. S10B).

Characterization of trichothecene metabolite produced by TRI24 mutant

Because we lacked a standard for the macrocyclic trichothecene analogs reported to be produced by P. roridum (i.e., roridins), we did a series of TLC, LC–MS, and NMR analyses to confirm that the spots detected on TLC plates (= HPLC–UV peak) corresponded to trichothecene analogs. Thus, spot 4, accumulated by ∆tri24 mutant, was identified as a mixture of 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene isomers A and B, which differ in the stereochemistry of the polyketide substituent esterified at C4 (Fig. 5; Supplemental Fig S12). Analyses of extracts from cultures of wild-type P. roridum indicated that spots 1 and 6 corresponded to roridins E and A, respectively (Supplemental Fig. S13). Further, although spot 3, which was also detected in wild-type cultures, was not identified by NMR, its molecular formula (C29H38O9), deduced from the TIMS-tof–MS analysis, was consistent with another roridin analog.

Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (A) and carbon-13 nuclear magnetic resonance (carbon-13 NMR spectroscopy) (B) of spot 4 (15-hydroxy trichodermadiene)

The NMR spectra of spot 4 suggest a basic trichothecene structure. The carbon signal of the characteristic epoxide (13-C) was under the solvent peak but showed in the heteronuclear single quantum correlation (HSQC) spectrum (Supplemental Fig. S12) at 47.4 ppm, with the protons at 3.10 and 2.89 ppm. The protons of the CH2OH at 3.85 and 3.51 ppm showed correlations in the heteronuclear multiple bond correlation (HMBC) spectrum (not shown) to 5-C, 6-C, 7-C, and 11-C but not to the carbonyls at 166.2 ppm. This was sufficient to assign the CH2OH to position 15, in place of the missing methyl. The HSQC spectrum showed the positions 7 and 8 protons as CH2 pairs, suggesting a macrocyclic type D trichothecene (Supplemental Fig. S1). The CHOH (4-H) proton showed HMBC correlations to the two carbonyl carbons at 166.15 and 166.17 ppm. The two closely spaced carbon peaks suggest two isomers.

There were a number of unsaturated positions showing HMBC correlations to the carbonyls. Beginning with the clean but somewhat broad doublet at 5.69 ppm, one could follow the chain in the correlation spectroscopy (CoSY) spectrum (not shown) to see four unsaturated positions: the doublet (H-18) at 5.69 ppm coupled to two barely resolved overlapping triplets (19-H) at 6.72 ppm. These coupled to two triplets (20-H) centered at 7.65 and 7.62 ppm, which lead to the two doublets of doublets (21-H) centered at 6.19 and 6.13 ppm. From these unsaturated positions, the correlations led to a pair of triplets (22-H) 4.07 and 4.03 ppm, which were coupled to partially obscured multiplets (23-H) at 3.74 and 3.69 ppm, which then terminated in a pair of methyl doublets (24-H) 1.16 and 1.13 ppm.

Thus, with a combination of CoSY and HSQC, the shifts of positions 18 through 24 could be assigned as a chain beginning with a carbonyl with two conjugated double bonds, followed by an epoxide and then terminating with a methyl. The cis/trans isomers of the epoxide at C-22 and C-23 were evident by the doubling of the peaks in the proton and carbon spectra of all positions of the chain.

LC–MS data were also consistent with the presence of 15-hydroxy trichodermadienediol (= trichoverrol) in cultures of the ∆tri24 mutant (Fig. 1). Thus, a substantial amount of a compound with a molecular mass of 420 was present in the extract of cultures of this mutant. The compound 420 had a chemical formula of C23H32O7 based on accurate mass measurements for the [M + H] + (m/z 421.22135), [M + Na] + (m/z 443.20323), and [M-H2O + H] + (m/z 403.21063) ions. In tandem MS, the M + H + ion readily lost a fragment with a m/z of 171.1702 (assumed as C8H11O4), which is consistent with the structure 6,7-dyhydroxy-2,4-octadienoyl, the polyketide substituent at C4 of the previously characterized analog 15-hydroxy trichodermadienediol. Loss of the m/z 171.1702 fragment from the m/z 420 metabolite resulted in a product of m/z 249, which further degraded into a very typical set of trichothecene-derived fragments. The accurate mass for the m/z 249 fragment corresponded to a trichodermol-like fragment with the molecular formula of C15H21O3 (Supplemental Fig. S14). Together, these data indicated that the m/z 420 metabolite was 15-hydroxy trichodermadienediol, which is also known as trichoverrol.

The finding that the ∆tri24 mutant produced 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene and 15-hydroxy trichodermadienediol rather than roridins indicates that the Tri24 enzyme is required for the formation of the macrocyclic ring of roridins. The absence of a 15-verrucarinoyl substituent (Fig. 1) in structures of both 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene and 15-hydroxy trichodermadienediol suggests that Tri24 catalyzes esterification of verrucarinoyl to the 15-hydroxy of a trichothecene biosynthetic intermediate during production of roridins. Further, restoration of the wild-type roridin production profile in the complemented ∆tri24 mutant (Fig. 3) confirmed the role of TRI24 in the formation of the macrocyclic ring of roridins.

Heterologous expression of P. roridumTRI24 in T. arundinaceum IBT 40837

To further investigate its function, P. roridum TRI24 was heterologously expressed in T. arundinaceum Ta37, which is a simple trichothecene producer and, therefore, lacks an endogenous TRI24 homolog. Fourteen putative transformants were recovered following the transformation of T. arundinaceum with the heterologous expression plasmid pTCS**_PRORI_TRI24a_ble_a (Fig. 2C). In PCR analysis with oligonucleotides PRORI_T24_ATG/Tcbh2 (Supplemental Table S1), three of the transformants (Ta37_T24.1, Ta37_T24.2, and Ta37_T24.3) yielded the 1,658-bp product expected to be amplified from transformants with TRI24 (Supplemental Fig. S15).

Precursor feeding with 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene (compound 4)

Based on the results described above, we hypothesized that the Tri24 enzyme catalyzes the esterification of verrucarinoyl to the 15-hydroxyl group of 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene or a structurally similar trichothecene analog. To test this hypothesis, we did a precursor feeding study in which 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene was fed to two T. arundinaceum transformants (strains Ta37_T24.1 and Ta37_T24.2) carrying P. roridum TRI24 expression construct. It is noteworthy that the wild-type T. arundinaceum produces harzianum A (HA), a trichothecene that lacks a 15-hydroxyl group (Cardoza et al. 2011). In TLC and antibiogram analyses initiated 8 days after YEPD cultures of the transformants were fed 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene, we did not find evidence for other trichothecene analogs on the TLC plates (Supplemental Fig. S16). However, the large spot on the TLC plates corresponding to HA could have masked any new spot corresponding to other trichothecenes with a similar Rf (Supplemental Fig. S16).

In HPLC–UV analysis, a chromatogram of 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene had two closely eluting peaks at 11.3 and 11.6 min. Chromatograms from culture extracts of transformant strains Ta37_T24.1 and Ta37_T24.2 that were fed 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene also had the peaks at 11.3 and 11.6 min, but they also had two additional pairs of closely eluting peaks: one pair at 14.8 and 15.2 min and the other at 18.3 and 18.7 min (Fig. 6). The two pairs of peaks were not present in the chromatograms derived from feeding experiments with the wild-type T. arundinaceum and, therefore, are likely related to the expression of TRI24 in the transformants. We hypothesized that the two peaks of each pair could correspond to two diastereomers (A and B). Previous studies revealed that several macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthetic intermediates, i.e., trichoverrol, trichoverrin, and 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene, occur as diastereomer pairs (Ueno 1983; Jarvis et al. 1981). This raised the possibility that the two pairs of peaks detected in the current study could be formed by Tri24-catalyzed modification of 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene A/B.

Precursor feeding analysis with 15-acetyl trichodermadiene

A feeding experiment was also done with 15-acetyl trichodermadiene A/B (15-acetyl 4) and one of the T. arundinaceum transformants (strain Ta37_T24.2) carrying the P. roridum TRI24 expression construct (Supplemental Fig. S17).

The HPLC–UV chromatograms of 15-acetyl 4 had two very closely eluting peaks at 22.58 and 22.88 min. When 15-acetyl 4 was fed to either strain Ta37_T24.2 or wild-type T. arundinaceum, the peaks at 22.58 and 22.88 min were not present in the resulting chromatogram. Instead, the chromatograms had two other closely eluting peaks at 14.52 and 14.86 min. Because the latter two peaks were present in chromatograms derived from both the transformant and wild-type T. arundinaceum, the formation of the corresponding metabolites was independent of TRI24. These results indicate that 15-acetyl 4 is not a substrate for the Tri24 enzyme.

Antibiogram of TLC plates indicated that 15-acetyl 4 has antimicrobial activity against K. marxianus (Supplemental Fig. S18), but at a lower level than roridins (compounds 1, 2, and 6).

Effect of TRI24 and TRI25 deletion on gene expression and biological activities

Effect of TRI24 gene deletion on expression of genes located in the TRI cluster region

In P. roridum, all known TRI genes, including TRI24 and TRI25, are clustered, but in addition, four genes, designated g3, g4, g5, and g6, were located within the cluster between TRI18 and TRI22. Whether the genes g3–g6 are involved in trichothecene biosynthesis is not known, but such a role seems unlikely based on their functional prediction from BLASTx comparison against the non-redundant NCBI database (data not shown), which indicated that closely related homologs of these genes are not associated with the TRI cluster in other trichothecene-producing fungi.

qPCR analysis

In order to determine the effect of the TRI24 gene deletion on the expression of genes located in the TRI cluster region (Fig. 2), a qPCR analysis was performed using RNAs extracted from mycelia grown for 24, 48, and 72 h. Analysis of RNA samples extracted from 72-h cultures indicated that deletion of TRI24 upregulated the expression of all TRI genes analyzed except for the two pathway-specific regulatory genes, TRI6 and TRI10 (Supplemental Fig. S19).

The expression of genes g3, g4, g5, and g6 differed from the expression of TRI genes in that the relative level of expression of g3–g6 decreased over time (Supplemental Fig. S19).

qPCR analysis of the complemented ∆tri24 mutant

Expression of TRI genes differed markedly in the ∆tri24 mutant and in the complemented ∆tri24_T24.5 transformant. Thus, expression levels of 10 TRI genes that were examined were significantly (p[H1] < 0.05) lower in the complemented transformant compared to the ∆tri24 mutant by ratios ranging from 0.017 fold (p[H1] = 0.000) to 0.185 (p[H1] = 0.000) for TRI17 and TRI18, respectively (Supplemental Fig. S20).

RNA-seq analysis

To further characterize the effect of TRI24 deletion on the fungal transcriptome, an RNA-seq experiment was performed using mycelia from cultures grown for 24, 48, or 72 h. However, based on the qPCR results summarized above, we focused on results from the 72-h samples. In agreement with qPCR data, all TRI genes analyzed, except the regulatory gene TRI10, were upregulated in Δtri24 mutant versus the wild-type strain (Fig. 7). Furthermore, TRI genes were among the genes with the highest relative expression among all the P. roridum genes whose level of expression was significantly affected by TRI24 deletion (Supplemental Table S4). In addition, five genes downstream of TRI25 were also significantly upregulated, genes 11260–11264 (Fig. 7). However, whether these five genes are involved in trichothecene biosynthesis remains to be determined (Supplemental Table S5).

Effect of TRI24 gene deletion on the antifungal activity and plant pathogenesis

Antifungal activity

Antifungal assays were carried out with R. solani R43 and K. marxianus. The TRI24 gene deletion resulted in a marked reduction of the antifungal activity of culture filtrates from 24- to 72-h cultures of P. roridum in an agar diffusion assay (Supplemental Fig. S22). In the case of the inhibitory activity against K. marxianus, a very weak antifungal activity was observed for the ∆tri24 mutant 72-h culture filtrate (Supplemental Fig. S23). Together, these results indicated that Tri24 is involved in the production of a metabolite with antifungal activity.

Transformants obtained by complementation of the ∆tri24 mutant exhibited greater antifungal activity against both K. marxianus and R. solani than the ∆tri24 mutant. Thus, a preliminary analysis of the antifungal activity of 7 out of the 11 selected complemented transformants was performed against K. marxianus using the agar diffusion assay. Results indicated that most of the transformants exhibited higher levels of antifungal activity that the ∆tri24 mutant but typically less activity than the wild-type P. roridum strain (Supplemental Fig. S24). The antifungal activity against K. marxianus was also demonstrated using filtrates from 72-h culture of the complemented transformant ∆tri24_T24.5 (Supplemental Fig. S23).

Similar changes in antifungal activity were observed against the phytopathogen R. solani in the agar diffusion assay (Fig. 8A). According to the radial inhibition (RI) values obtained in the assay, the activity of the ∆tri24 mutant was reduced by 80%, and the activity of the complemented ∆tri24_T24.5 transformant was increased by 5% compared to the wild-type strain (Table 1).

Plant pathogenesis

The pathogenicity of P. roridum strains to tomato leaves and bean seeds was determined as described in the “Materials and Methods” section. The ∆tri24 mutant exhibited a marked reduction in toxicity to tomato leaves compared to the wild type (Fig. 8B). These results were analogous to the results of the antifungal assays and were consistent with TRI24 being required for the production of a toxic secondary metabolite with such activities. It is important to note that roridins, which are the major trichothecene analogs produced by wild-type P. roridum, are highly phytotoxic (Ueno 1983). A similar result to this was observed when analyzing the toxicity to bean seeds of ∆tri24 mutant compared to the wild type. The wild-type strain completely blocked bean seed germination, while ∆tri24 mutant inhibited germination to a lesser degree (Supplemental Fig. S25).

Furthermore, when the toxic activity against tomato leaves was tested with conidia of the complemented ∆tri24_T24.5 transformants, the phytotoxicity reached levels similar to the wild type (Fig. 8B), indicating that the production of the metabolite/s responsible for that activity has been restored.

Effect of TRI25 deletion on the antifungal activity

TRI25 gene deletion did not negatively affect the antifungal activity against R. solani or K. marxianus when culture filtrates from 72-h cultures of the ∆tri25.30 and ∆tri25.50 complemented strains (Supplemental Figs. S26 and S23; Table 1). Thus, these results indicated that TRI25 is not essential for the antifungal activity of P. roridum. There were two possibilities to explain these data: (i) Tri25 is not involved in the biosynthesis of trichothecenes, or (ii) it is involved in the biosynthesis, but the trichothecene biosynthetic intermediate that is the substrate of the Tri25 enzyme is as toxic as the end-product of the trichothecene biosynthetic reaction in P. roridum.

Nine transformants from the complementation analysis of ∆tri25 mutant strain ∆tri25.30 exhibited levels of antifungal activity against K. marxianus that were similar to the activity of the wild type and the ∆tri25 mutated strains (Supplemental Fig. S27).

Antifungal activity of metabolites purified from ∆tri24 mutant and wild-type strains

In antifungal assays, compounds 1, 2, and 6, which were produced by wild-type P. roridum, had detectable levels of antifungal activity against K. marxianus. However, compound 3, which was produced by the wild type, and compounds 4 and 5, which were produced by the ∆tri24 mutant, lacked activity against K. marxianus (Supplemental Fig. S28).

Discussion

Structural features that are common to simple and macrocyclic trichothecenes indicate that the early steps in the biosynthesis of these two groups of metabolites are the same (Degenkolb et al. 2008; Grove 2007; Semeiks et al. 2014). The finding that simple and macrocyclic trichothecene-producing fungi have multiple TRI genes in common indicates the enzymes that catalyze these early steps are also the same (Proctor et al. 2018; Semeiks et al. 2014). However, the ether bond connecting the verrucarinoyl-derived 15-acyl substituent and the polyketide-derived 4-acyl groups is unique to macrocyclic trichothecenes. In addition, the verrucarinoyl-derived 15-acyl substituent is unique to macrocyclic trichothecenes and some of their simple trichothecene intermediates. This structural uniqueness suggests that the biosynthesis of macrocyclic trichothecenes requires some enzymes/genes that are not required for simple trichothecene biosynthesis. Thus, the goal of the current study was to identify genes unique to macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis.

The physical association of TRI24 and TRI25 with previously characterized TRI genes and their occurrence in macrocyclic trichothecene-producing but not in simple trichothecene-producing fungi (Fig. 2; and Semeiks et al. 2014) indicated that TRI24 and TRI25 might be unique to macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis. The loss of roridin production in the Δtri24 mutant but not in the Δtri25 mutant of P. roridum provided further evidence that TRI24 is required for the biosynthesis of roridins, a subset of macrocyclic trichothecenes. In contrast, the finding that roridin production in the ∆tri25 mutant was unchanged relative to the wild-type P. rodidum strain indicated that TRI25 is not required for roridin biosynthesis.

Two lines of evidence from the current study indicated that TRI24 is required for 15-O-acylation of an EPT-derived intermediate during roridin biosynthesis. First, the predicted Tri24 protein shares sequence homology, albeit low-level homology, with fungal acetyl/acyltransferases. Second, the Δtri24 mutant does not produce roridins but instead produces 4 and trichoverrol (15-hydroxy trichodermadienediol), both of which lack a 15-acyl substituent but have a 4-acyl substituent (Figs. 1 and 5). These findings are largely consistent with the previously proposed roridin biosynthetic pathway based on biogenic and structural hypotheses (Degenkolb et al. 2008; Grove 2007; Ueno 1983). Although the order of some reactions in this proposed pathway is not clear, the pathway requires the following steps: 4- and 15-hydroxylation of EPT; acylation of the 4-hydroxyl with 6,7-dihydroxy-2,4-octadienoyl; acylation of the 15-hydroxyl with verrucarinoyl; and formation of the ether bond via the 5-hydroxyl of the verrucarinoyl substituent and the 6-hydroxyl of the 6,7-dihydroxy-2,4-octadienoyl substituent (Fig. 1).

We have proposed a revised roridin biosynthetic pathway (Fig. 9) after considering the trichothecene production phenotype of the ∆tri24 mutant in the context of previously proposed macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthetic pathways. The pathway includes two alternative schemes. In one scheme, both 4 and trichoverrol are biosynthetic intermediates, and in the other, only trichoverrol is an intermediate. The rationale for a pathway in which 4 is not an intermediate is provided below.

Proposed biosynthetic pathway for macrocyclic trichothecenes in P. roridum. Two alternative pathways are proposed. In the first alternative, 15-hydoxy trichodermadiene (4) is a precursor of trichoverrol (solid arrows), and in the second alternative, 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene is not a precursor (dashed arrows). If the second alternative is correct, then 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene would likely be a shunt product produced only in the absence of a functional Tri24 enzyme. The order of roridins E, D, and A formation was proposed by Tamm (1977)

Numerous macrocyclic trichothecene analogs and proposed biosynthetic intermediates have been reported from chemical analyses of P. roridum and other fungi (Tamm and Breitenstein 1980; Jarvis 1991). To our knowledge, the current study is the only report of 4. This raises the question, is 4 a true biosynthetic intermediate or a shunt product formed when macrocyclic ring formation is blocked? If 4 is a true biosynthetic intermediate, it is possible that it is a precursor of trichoverrol or that the ether bond of the macrocyclic ring is formed from an interaction of the epoxide of the 6,7-epoxy-2,4-octadienoyl substituent and the 5-hydroxyl group of the 15-verrucarinoyl substituent. To our knowledge, its production by the Δtri24 mutant in the current study is the only report of 4. This suggests that 4 could be a shunt product produced only in the absence of a functional Tri24 enzyme. However, the trichothecene analog known as trichodermadienediol, which consists of EPT with a 6,7-dihydroxy-2,4-octadienoyl substituent at C4 and no substituent at C15, has been reported as a naturally occurring metabolite. The existence of trichodermadienediol as a naturally occurring metabolite suggests that 4 might also occur naturally. If this is the case, a possible explanation for why 4 has not been reported previously is that it might be rapidly converted to another biosynthetic intermediate (e.g., trichoverrol) in wild-type strains of macrocyclic trichothecene-producing fungi (Fig. 9).

In our proposed roridin biosynthetic pathways (Fig. 9), the product of the Tri24-catalyzed reaction is trichoverrin, which consists of EPT with a 6,7-dihydroxy-2,4-octadienoyl substituent at C4 and a verrucarinoyl substituent at C15. Even though trichoverrin has acyl groups at both C4 and C15, it is classified as a simple trichothecene because it lacks a macrocyclic ring. We rationalize, therefore, that trichoverrin is unique to macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis rather than an end-product of a simple trichothecene biosynthetic pathway. Based on functional analyses in T. arundinaceum, TRI17 and TRI18 are likely required for the formation of the 6,7-epoxy-2,4-octadienoyl substituent found at C4 of 4 (Lindo et al. 2019; Proctor et al. 2018). In contrast to TRI24, we rationale that TRI17 and TRI18 are not unique to macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis because these genes are also required for the biosynthesis of simple trichothecenes, such as harzianum A, that are end-products of a biosynthetic pathway (Lindo et al. 2019; Proctor et al. 2018).

The main compound accumulated in the ∆tri24 mutant was 4, which was co-purified with trichoverrol A/B (= 15-hydroxy trichodermadienediol A/B) (Fig. 9). Thus, Tri24 most likely acts as an acyltransferase putatively required for C15 acylation of the intermediate(s) 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene or trichoverrol with verrucarinoyl to yield trichoverrins (Fig. 9).

In the HPLC–UV chormatograms, 4 appeared as a double peak (Figs. 3 and 6; Supplemental Fig. S9). This is consistent with NMR data showing differences in the stereochemistry of the epoxide of the 6,7-epoxy-2,4-ocatadieneoyl substituent of 4 (Fig. 9). Thus, 4 likely consists of two stereoisomers A and B of 15-hydroxy trichodermadiene. The presence of these two isomers is consistent with previous reports that biosynthetic intermediates of macrocyclic trichothecenes occur as mixtures of two diastereomers (Ueno 1983; Jarvis et al. 1981). In some previous studies, the ratio of the levels of the A to B diastereomers was 1:2 (Ueno 1983; Jarvis et al. 1981), which is similar to the ratio of the two diastereomers of 4 observed in the current study (Fig. 3).

The structural similarities of 4 to trichoverrol, trichoverrol to trichoverrin, and trichoverrin to roridins are consistent with the proposal that 4, trichoverrol, and trichoverrin are intermediates required for the formation of the macrocyclic ring of roridins. Furthermore, feeding studies with trichoverrins A and B to resting cultures of Myrothecium verrucaria resulted in the production of the same array of macrocyclic trichothecenes described for normal cultures of this species, with verrucarins A, B, J, roridins A, and isororidin E being the major metabolites (Jarvis et al. 1981). Following these previous results and the current study, 4 and the 15-hydroxy trichodermadienediol (trichoverrol) co-purified with it would undergo an acylation process to esterify verrucarinic acid to the 15-hydroxy moiety. This is most likely the reaction catalyzed by Tri24. Accumulation of 4 and trichoverrol in cultures of the ∆tri24 mutant supports this hypothesis. However, with the currently available data, and to address the question of whether 4 is a shunt product only accumulated when the formation of the macrocyclic ring is blocked or if it is an intermediate required for the formation of roridins and other macrocyclic trichothecenes, we proposed two alternative hypotheses: (1) Tri24 catalyzes acylation of the 15-hydroxyl of 4 with verrucarinoyl to yield trichoverrins; and (2) Tri24 is responsible for 15-acylation of C15-acetyl 4 with verrucarinoyl to produce trichoverrins. If hypothesis 2 is correct, in the absence of Tri24, the compound C15-acetyl 4 would accumulate, and the C15-acetyl moiety could be removed by a non-specific/specific esterase. Similar findings to that of hypothesis 2 have been described in Fusarium, where in addition to T2 toxin, production of diacetoxyscirpenol and neosolaniol was detected, the two latter lacking the C3-acetyl moiety, which is removed by the action of Tri8 (McCormick and Alexander 2002). Also, in T. arundinaceum, deletion of TRI18 resulted in the accumulation of trichodermol instead of its immediate precursor, the C4-acetylated analog trichodermin (Lindo et al. 2019).

In order to test these two hypotheses, we carried out two experiments in which purified 4, or 15-acetyl trichodermadiene A/B (C15-acetyl 4) were fed to cultures of T. arundinaceum transformants expressing P. roridum TRI24. Feeding 4 resulted in a low rate of conversion to two sets of compounds, visualized as two pairs of HPLC–UV peaks, which only appear in TRI24 overexpressing transformants fed with that compound. These peaks have not been identified yet, but they do not match any other peaks observed in the current study, including those corresponding to roridin analogs (compounds 1, 2, and 6). These data, together with the low rate of conversion to the newly observed peaks, indicate that 4 may not be the optimal substrate for Tri24 at least in a heterologous background. Secondly, no new peaks were detected in feeding studies with C15-acetyl 4, indicating that it is not a substrate for Tri24 in the same conditions as those used above.

Early in this study, we hypothesized that Tri24 was a regulatory protein because it has sequence homology to LolU, which was previously proposed to regulate the biosynthesis of the alkaloid loline in Epichlӧe species (Schardl et al. 2007). This hypothesis was our rationale for the initial analyses of gene expression in the Δtri24 mutant. Although subsequent analyses indicated that Tri24 is a biosynthetic enzyme, qPCR and RNA-seq analyses indicated that other TRI gene exhibited marked upregulation in the Δtri24 mutant but not in the complemented Δtri24 mutant (Fig. 7). Given that Tri14 is more likely to be a trichothecene biosynthetic enzyme than a regulatory protein, it is unclear why deletion of TRI24 had such a marked effect on expression of other TRI genes.

The role of Tri24, as an acyltransferase instead of a regulatory protein, was also supported by antifungal and plant disease analyses. Deletion of TRI24 resulted in a drastic reduction in the antifungal and disease symptoms on beans. In fact, 4 lacked antifungal activity in the TLC bioassay (Supplemental Fig. S28). Furthermore, C15-acetyl 4 exhibited little but detectable activity against K. marxianus, which agrees with previous results, by which the higher complexity of the trichothecene chemical structure is correlated with the higher levels of toxicity (Sato and Ueno 1977; Ueno 1977; Wu et al. 2013). That is, macrocyclic trichothecenes tend to be more toxic than simple trichothecenes. In the present case, a single modification, as it was the acetylation at C15 of 4, the latter lacking antifungal activity, resulted in C15-acetyl 4, with an already detectable antifungal activity. Thus, the more complex structure is correlated with higher antifungal activity. Furthermore, in vitro toxicological assays indicate that the macrocyclic ring plays an important role in the toxicity of these compounds. For example, trichoverrins A and B are close to 20-fold less toxic than roridin E and isororidin E (Ueno 1983). These data agree with the current study, showing the lack of activity of 4 against K. marxianus and the low toxicity of C15-acetyl 4. Considering the high intensity of the later spot in the TLC and the small diameter of the inhibition area, the antifungal activity of this compound should be much lower than the activity described for compounds 1, 2, and 6, corresponding to three different roridins.

In contrast to deletion of TRI24, deletion of TRI25 did not affect roridin production or antifungal activity of P. roridum. Despite these results, the involvement of Tri25 in the trichothecene biosynthetic pathway cannot be ruled out. The location of TRI25 adjacent or close to TRI24 in all macrocyclic trichothecene producers examined led us to surmise that TRI25 could be involved in a trichothecene biosynthetic reaction that has not yet been described. However, the phenotype of the Δtri25 mutant indicates that the putative reaction does not affect the antifungal activity or the roridin production profile of P. roridum. The function of TRI25 could be analogous to the trichothecene biosynthetic gene TRI14 and fumonisin biosynthetic genes FUM15 and FUM16 in Fusarium, because deletion of these genes in Fusarium species does not affect the trichothecene or fumonisin production profiles (Hao et al. 2024; Butchko et al. 2006).

Production of roridins and other macrocyclic trichothecenes almost certainly requires genes in addition to TRI24 that are specific to the biosynthesis of macrocyclic trichothecenes. Presumably, there is an enzyme that catalyzes trichothecene 15-hydroxylation, an enzyme(s) that catalyzes the conversion of the Tri17-derived polyketide to 6,7-dihydroxy-2,4-octadienoic acid (or 6,7-epoxy-2,4-octadienoic acid), and another enzyme that catalyzes the formation of the ether bond that creates the macrocyclic ring. In Fusarium species, Tri11 catalyzes trichothecene 15-hydroxylation, and in T. arundinaceum, Tri23 catalyzes the conversion of the Tri17-derived polyketide. Because macrocyclic trichothecene-producing fungi lack orthologs of the TRI11 and TRI23 genes, other genes with the same or similar functions must be required for macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis. It is not clear why these other genes were not apparent using the comparative genomics approach used to identify TRI24. However, possible explanations are suggested by previous studies indicating that some TRI genes have changed functions during their evolutionary histories, and that different TRI genes confer the same trichothecene biosynthetic reaction in different fungi. Thus, it is possible that the functions of one or more previously characterized TRI genes have changed so that they confer reactions that are specific to macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis. Alternatively, it is possible that different genera of macrocyclic trichothecene-producing fungi have different genes that confer the same macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthetic reactions. Thus, the approaches used in the current study will have to be adjusted to identify additional genes specific to macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis. Because TRI24 deletion upregulated the expression of previously characterized TRI genes (Fig. 7), one possibility is to search for additional genes that are upregulated in the mutant. Such upregulated genes could then be analyzed to determine whether they are required for macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis.

In conclusion, molecular genetic and biochemical studies over the last three decades have identified most of the genes required for biosynthesis of structurally diverse simple trichothecenes but, to our knowledge, no genes that are unique to macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis. Thus, the evidence from the current study that TRI24 is required for the formation of roridins is the first reported characterization of a gene that is unique to macrocyclic trichothecene biosynthesis. The characterization of TRI24 also provided evidence that the macrocyclic ring of roridins is required for high level of aggressiveness of P. roridum on bean seeds and for its antifungal activity against two distantly related fungi. Together, these findings suggest that the macrocyclic ring can play an important role in the ecology of macrocyclic trichothecene-producing fungi. In addition, the demonstration of the role of TRI24 in trichothecene biosynthesis adds to the body of knowledge on the diversity of enzymes that contribute to the tremendous structural variation of trichothecene toxins. Such information has the potential to contribute to efforts to design detoxification enzymes to control Fusarium-incited crop diseases in which trichothecenes contribute to pathogenesis and mycotoxin contamination. Likewise, this knowledge has the potential to aid efforts to modulate the toxicity of trichothecenes in Trichoderma species for which trichothecene production contributes to biological control activity.
