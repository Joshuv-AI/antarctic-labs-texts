Reducing inequities in access to postpartum hemorrhage prevention: Reframing advance distribution of misoprostol as a test case for health system responsiveness and adaptation
Authors: Hannah Tappis, Naoko Doi, Chirag Bhola, Yapouloucé Bamba, Eva Lathrop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvance distribution of misoprostol is an evidence‐based strategy for preventing postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) at births without skilled health personnel present. Despite its lifesaving potential, initial momentum behind this intervention has slowed over the last decade and has not been renewed since WHO released updated guidelines in 2020 or the global Roadmap to combat postpartum hemorrhage between 2023 and 2030 was launched in 2023. The present article argues for renewed consideration of advance distribution of misoprostol in comprehensive PPH prevention strategies and reframing of policy dialogues around the intervention to reflect current health system realities and public health imperatives. Drawing on experiences of the Accelerating Measurable Progress and Leveraging Investments for Postpartum Hemorrhage Impact (AMPLI‐PPHI) project across six diverse health systems, we position advance distribution of misoprostol as a litmus test for health system responsiveness and call on health sector stakeholders to critically examine all available opportunities to optimize PPH prevention strategies and accelerate efforts to reduce inequities in preventable maternal mortality.Keywords: community health, maternal health, maternal mortality, misoprostol, postpartum hemorrhage, scale‐up, uterotonic

INTRODUCTION

Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), or excessive blood loss in the first 24 h after childbirth, is the most common cause of maternal death worldwide. 1 To reduce risk of PPH, it is recommended that every woman giving birth receives medication, a uterotonic, to make the uterus contract immediately after delivery. 2 However, for most of the 18.5 million women giving birth each year without skilled health personnel, 3 access to lifesaving uterotonics remains out of reach.

The first‐line uterotonic recommended for PPH prevention, oxytocin, is a parenteral medicine that must be administered through an intramuscular or intravenous injection by skilled health personnel. Most alternative uterotonics, recommended for use when oxytocin is not available or quality cannot be assured, also require administration by skilled health personnel. 4 As access to care expands and more women give birth in health facilities, prophylactic uterotonic coverage will increase; however, complementary strategies are needed to ensure every woman, regardless of where she gives birth, has access to quality medicine for PPH prevention.

Evidence‐based recommendations for all contexts

WHO uterotonic recommendations 5 , 6 provide a foundation for PPH prevention strategies at all stages of the obstetric transition 7 or Integrated Maternal, Newborn and Stillbirth Transition Framework. 8 As Figure 1 shows, these recommendations include provisions for health systems with well‐regulated medicine markets and well‐functioning supply chains, as well as options for settings where these preconditions are not met, and where women are not able to access facilities with skilled health personnel able to provide an injectable uterotonic.

WHO recommendations on use of misoprostol for postpartum hemorrhage prevention.

Misoprostol, a heat‐stable and water‐soluble uterotonic in tablet form, is currently the only uterotonic option for PPH prevention when skilled health personnel are not present. WHO guidelines support both advance distribution of misoprostol to community health workers that will be present during or immediately after childbirth, and advance distribution to pregnant women for oral self‐administration, provided that distribution is accompanied by appropriate training, counseling, and monitoring of commodity use. 9 For advance distribution to pregnant women, WHO also remarks that misoprostol should ideally be provided during an antenatal care visit in the third trimester (typically as part of a safe delivery kit), and instructions for use accompanied by information on danger signs and emphasis on the importance of giving birth in a health facility. 9

FIGO and the International Confederation of Midwives (ICM) also support use of misoprostol as a safe and effective strategy for reducing PPH in low‐resource settings where women give birth at home or in facilities that lack basic supplies and infrastructure. 9 , 10 Despite these recommendations, and inclusion of misoprostol on the Essential Medicines List in many countries with high maternal mortality burden, few have adopted strategies for community health worker, lay health worker, or self‐administration of misoprostol for PPH prevention at home birth. 11

Decades of optimism, implementation pilots, and resistance

When researchers in the 1980s discovered that misoprostol, a medicine initially licensed for the prevention and treatment of gastric ulcers, could be used off‐label as a uterotonic agent, maternal health advocates heralded the discovery as revolutionary and game‐changing. 12 , 13 By the 2000s and 2010s, pilot studies were conducted in at least 25 low‐ and middle‐income countries (File S1) to generate evidence on the safety, effectiveness, feasibility, and acceptability of advance distribution of misoprostol for PPH prevention. Multiple reviews were conducted to support advocacy efforts, and at least seven countries (Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Ethiopia, India, Mozambique, Nepal, Niger) made strides in national strategy development and foundations for scale‐up. 14 , 15 , 16

At the same time, since its introduction for obstetric use, efforts to expand availability of misoprostol beyond health facilities have been contentious. Misoprostol can also be used to induce or augment labor and to induce abortion, and the latter has fueled social and political debate. 12 , 17 Reviews show that the vast majority of women provided with misoprostol use the medicine as instructed, and concerns that advance distribution of misoprostol would hinder efforts to promote facility births are not supported by evidence. 9 , 18 Nevertheless, these concerns have led authorities in some contexts to place stricter controls on misoprostol than on other medicines with higher risk profiles. 19 For example, in many health systems, advance distribution of insulin for blood glucose control or anticoagulants to decrease thrombotic risk are routinely given to patients for self‐administration at home without the presence of a health worker. 20 , 21 When managed according to instructions, these medications can be lifesaving; when managed incorrectly, for example by doubling a dose, mistiming a dose, or omitting a dose, they can lead to serious complications such as hypoglycemia, hyperglycemia, severe bleeding, or even death. 22

Implementing safe and effective advance distribution of misoprostol requires more than simply providing the drug—it requires both health worker and community awareness and acceptance, clear counseling for women and families, robust training of skilled and lay health workers, and reliable supply chain and monitoring systems. Even where these programmatic safeguards are in place, concerns often remain that distributing misoprostol at community level could facilitate unsupervised use for labor induction or abortion, particularly if regulatory oversight is weak. As a result, discussions related to advance distribution of misoprostol have too often been transformed into platforms for larger sociopolitical concerns and disagreements about trust, autonomy, and women's agency. 23 These polarizing debates tend to overshadow the central public health question: "How to ensure timely access to uterotonics for women giving birth outside facilities, while countries work toward universal skilled birth attendance".

A changing landscape

The global health landscape has shifted in the last decade since many of these early pilot studies were published and donor‐funded initiatives were launched. In this time, many countries have progressed along the maternal, newborn, and stillbirth transition pathway, with the proportion of births attended by skilled health personnel increasing from 61% in 2000 to 87% in 2024. 24 Concurrently, governments are investing in community health workforce professionalization and integration into national health systems, enhancing continuity of care in underserved areas. 25 , 26 Advances in digital health technologies are improving capacity for real‐time monitoring, data use, and accountability for health services and commodities. 27 , 28 , 29 Additionally, in recent years, self‐care has emerged as an accepted strategy for expanding access to essential health services. 30 , 31 However, challenges and inequities in access to care persist.

The advocacy priorities identified in AMPLI‐PPHI project staff reflection workshops have begun to move beyond emotionally charged sociopolitical debates to practical challenges of sustaining or scaling advance distribution of misoprostol beyond donor‐funded projects in select geographic areas. While project staff have noted that narratives highlighting perceived risks of commodity availability influencing induced abortion and skilled birth attendance rates are persistent, the more daunting challenges identified are health system bottlenecks—system‐wide health workforce, supply chain, monitoring, and accountability gaps—that are not unique to maternal health initiatives.

Community health workforce

In many countries, the potential for advance distribution of misoprostol to bridge the gap in ensuring all women giving birth have access to uterotonic medicine to prevent PPH will depend on the status of the country's community health workforce. Most past pilots have been implemented in countries early in the integrated mortality transition, characterized by both low antenatal care and institutional delivery coverage (File S1). In these contexts, it is not surprising that reviews find PPH prevention programs that allowed misoprostol distribution by community health workers (CHWs) and during home visits achieved greatest distribution and coverage, potentially more than double the coverage achieved by programs with distribution by health workers or as a part of antenatal care services. 14

Although some governments are reluctant to authorize CHW distribution or administration of any medicines, others have demonstrated that well‐regulated and supervised community distribution systems can be safe, effective mechanisms for reducing inequities in access to care. 32 , 33 WHO guidelines on optimizing CHW programs acknowledge that legal and regulatory barriers, lack of recognition (and remuneration) of CHWs as a part of the formal health system, unclear scopes of practice, and professional opposition frequently constrain task sharing, particularly with regard to medicine distribution. 34 Rather than singling out specific drugs for exceptional scrutiny, ministries could instead establish criteria for CHW distribution or administration of medicines across health areas. Such frameworks would not only improve consistency and safety standards but also facilitate integrated delivery of essential medicines for malaria, child health, family planning, and maternal care.

More integrated community health governance frameworks can also have implications for resource mobilization, particularly where sustainable financing for community health programs remains a persistent challenge. 35 , 36 For example, in Guinea, AMPLI‐PPHI plans to support advance distribution of misoprostol had to be scaled back from two districts to one after malaria program funding for CHWs in one district was discontinued, highlighting the fragility of program sustainability without dedicated long‐term investment.

Commodity management

Similarly, concerns about unused misoprostol typically focus on potential diversion for abortion, which draws attention away from broader operational challenges of forecasting, storage, and safe disposal of unused medicines—all emerging as issues on the global stage. 37 In reviewing publications of past programs (File S1), AMPLI‐PPHI found that few countries have documented efforts to institutionalize advance distribution of misoprostol as part of a national health service package. Only one case study from Mozambique underscores the broader challenges for health system leaders to tackle: after initial universal distribution resulted in a substantial proportion of misoprostol doses being returned by women who gave birth at a health facility, the program shifted to targeted distribution for women deemed most likely to deliver at home. While some applauded this more controlled approach as mitigation of misuse, others felt the eligibility criteria employed was too restrictive, noting that the shift limited program reach to <10% of forecasted home births and resulted in expiration of a large volume of already procured commodities. 38

Managing unused medicines remains a challenge across many community health initiatives, and very few publications on past advance distribution of misoprostol initiatives addressed this topic beyond mentioning that women were encouraged to return the medication to a health worker if not used (File S1). Although many health systems lack mechanisms to retrieve or safely dispose of medicines distributed outside facilities, these gaps are rarely considered sufficient justification for restricting access to antimalarials, antibiotics, or other medications. Mitigating loss, resale and unsafe disposal of unused medication require stronger community engagement and logistics management platforms, not exceptional scrutiny of misoprostol. For example, Ministries of Health could issue public guidance on how and why to safely dispose of any unused medicines, establish standard operating procedures for safe disposal of unused medicines, and invest in data systems—such as community‐based health information systems—to improve tracking and strengthen commodity management within primary healthcare supply chains.

Program targeting

AMPLI‐PPHI's program sites in India illustrate how aggregate statistics mask critical inequities. While Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh report facility birth rates exceeding 90%, 39 both states have districts and subdistrict blocks where a substantial proportion of women still give birth at home. Rapid assessments in tribal areas selected for AMPLI‐PPHI implementation revealed villages where as high as 65% of recent births occurred at home—a surprise to policymakers and stakeholders who typically see state and district level statistics. While AMPLI‐PPHI teams relied on these formative assessments to guide selection of communities for advance distribution of misoprostol, program staff highlighted strengthening subdistrict information systems to capture more granular data (e.g., village or panchayat level) on the prevalence of home births as critical to effective expansion of the approach to other areas.

While newer to maternal health programs, such a localized targeting approach is not unprecedented. Disease control programs for malaria, 40 HIV, 41 and vaccine‐preventable diseases 42 routinely use microplanning, stratified risk mapping, and local surveillance to direct resources where they are needed most. Applying comparable microplanning and accountability frameworks to uterotonic access and shifting attention from feasibility studies to validation and refinement of targeting criteria, would allow Ministries of Health to reach women most likely to give birth outside facilities with a similar data‐driven strategy to that underpinning progress in other areas of health.

Context responsiveness and adaptation

WHO guidelines include advance distribution of misoprostol to pregnant women for self‐administration to prevent PPH as a context‐specific recommendation, noting that this strategy should only be implemented with targeted monitoring and evaluation. 9 However, in many countries with the highest maternal mortality burdens, the same barriers that hinder women's access to skilled intrapartum care limit health system capacity to closely monitor safety, coverage, and effectiveness of services in remote, hard‐to‐reach and insecure areas. To the extent feasible, advance distribution of misoprostol should be accompanied by robust process and outcome monitoring. At the same time, the burden of PPH demands immediate action. In this light, broader‐scale distribution and increased tolerance for uncertainty may be warranted in exchange for public health benefits in countries earlier in the mortality transition.

Ultimately, inclusion of advance distribution of misoprostol in comprehensive PPH prevention and treatment strategies will hinge on governments embracing the notion all women should have access to a quality uterotonic. This tenet can and should drive policy decisions and allocation of resources for reducing barriers to facility births, providing and tracking advance distribution of misoprostol as a risk mitigation approach, and strengthening health information systems to enable more refined targeting of interventions.

REFRAMING THE DEBATE

In today's constrained funding environment, with competing health priorities and political polarization, there is risk that maternal health advocacy will be avoided, or investments sidelined in favor of health issues unrelated to human sexuality and reproduction. 43 In this context, countries that adopt or update policies on advance distribution of misoprostol should be commended, not only for acting on calls for action toward maternal mortality reduction, but for demonstrating a bold commitment to context‐responsive, equity‐driven, people‐centered models of health service delivery. Three shifts in framing advocacy and policy dialogue surrounding advance distribution of misoprostol can help facilitate this:

Shift 1: From a hotly contested innovation to an evidence‐based risk mitigation strategy: Advance distribution of misoprostol should be embraced as a risk mitigation strategy that complements efforts to increase coverage of facility births by expanding PPH awareness, facilitating birth preparedness planning, and promoting births with skilled health personnel at facilities equipped to manage obstetric and newborn complications. Until quality intrapartum care is accessible everywhere, including remote communities impacted by insecurity and climate disasters, the reality is that women giving birth outside of health systems face greater risks of PPH and waiting for universal access to skilled birth attendance means accepting preventable deaths. Framing stakeholder dialogues around where and how to implement advance distribution of misoprostol safely and cost‐efficiently—rather than whether to consider it at all—signals a public health imperative and need for solutions to address inequities in access to lifesaving medicines while system strengthening progresses.

Shift #2: From paternalism to trust and agency: Providing pregnant women with misoprostol and clear guidance for home use in case of birth before arrival at a health facility signals that health leaders trust women and families to make responsible, informed health decisions, as they would for self‐management of gestational diabetes. It aligns with global policy shifts toward self‐care, user‐directed medication, and task‐sharing. This shift is particularly important in contexts where traditional birth attendants remain a primary source of care due to cultural trust, proximity, and accessibility. Counseling, pictorial instructions, and postnatal follow‐up can transform the act of misoprostol distribution into an act of empowerment, enabling women and their families to act in their own best interests when distance, cost, and other access barriers limit access to intrapartum care.

In more than 50 publications describing advance distribution of misoprostol pilots and early‐stage scale‐up efforts across 25 countries, less than half included misoprostol in clean delivery kits and only a few explicitly highlighted advance distribution of misoprostol as one component of a broader PPH strategy (File S1). Countries positioning advance distribution of misoprostol as part of a comprehensive PPH strategy include Nepal and Niger, both of which have been recognized as "exemplars in maternal health" for their successes in maternal mortality reduction. 44 , 45 , 46 This framing positions advance distribution not as a permanent solution but as a targeted, time‐bound strategy that can be adapted to changing health system capacities and progressively scaled back as need decreases.

Many of these arguments were made in reviews nearly a decade ago, at the height of earlier policy debates, yet they remain relevant. AMPLI‐PPHI is working to embrace and model these three shifts, with varying degrees of success. Learning how to respond to unique health system realities and adapt policies and guidelines commensurate with diverse population needs is critical to ensuring all delivering women have access to a lifesaving uterotonic to prevent PPH. We call on health system leadership, professional associations, and other key stakeholders in contexts with suboptimal uterotonic coverage to (re)consider advance distribution of misoprostol as part of comprehensive equity‐oriented strategies to address PPH.
