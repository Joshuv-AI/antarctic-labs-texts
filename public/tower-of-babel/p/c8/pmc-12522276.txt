The role of SPRED1 mutation in melanoma
Authors: Hui Hou, Jiao Li, Jianxiao Xing, Ruixia Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Melanoma is a highly malignant tumor, with its initiation and progression tightly linked to the aberrant activation of the MAPK signaling pathway. As a critical negative regulator of the MAPK pathway, SPRED1 frequently exhibits genomic alteration in melanoma, including gene deletions and mutations, which lead to its functional inactivation. Consequently, the loss of SPRED1-mediated inhibition on the MAPK pathway significantly drives tumorigenesis and progression, enhances invasive and metastatic capacities, and is closely associated with therapeutic resistance. This article systematically reviews the structure and biological functions of SPRED1, its mutation profiles across different melanoma subtypes, and its regulatory mechanisms on the MAPK pathway. Furthermore, it discusses the associations of SPRED1 alteration with tumor malignant progression, as well as resistance to targeted therapy and immunotherapy. This review aims to provide a comprehensive theoretical basis for the precise diagnosis, treatment, and fundamental research of melanoma.

Introduction

Melanoma is a highly malignant tumor originating from melanocytes [1], and its clinical diagnosis and treatment still pose significant challenges. Although it accounts for less than 2% of global cancer diagnoses [2], melanoma has the highest mortality rate among skin cancers [3]. The latest epidemiological data show that the number of new melanoma cases worldwide has exceeded 350,000 in 2023, with over 60,000 deaths, and the incidence is increasing at a rate of 3–5% annually. This growth trend is particularly significant among younger populations [4]. The incidence rate among individuals aged 20–40 has increased by 27% compared to 10 years ago. The malignant biological features of melanoma are primarily characterized by extremely strong invasive and metastatic capabilities, as well as treatment resistance [5]. Even advanced-stage patients receiving standardized treatment have a 5-year survival rate of less than 20% [6]. This tumor can arise in multiple locations, including the skin, eyes, gastrointestinal tract, and reproductive system [7]. Its initiation and progression involve a complex, multi-gene, multi-step process, closely linked to the accumulation of genetic mutations. These mutations drive abnormal biological behaviors such as uncontrolled cell proliferation and enhanced invasiveness [8]. Thus, in-depth investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying melanoma development, along with the identification of novel diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets, remains a critical focus in current melanoma research.

The abnormal activation of the MAPK signaling pathway is a core driving event in the occurrence and development of melanoma [9], and SPRED1 has a functionally abnormal state that is closely related to the malignant progression of melanoma [10]. Since the first discovery of the role of SPRED1 in neurofibromatosis in 2007 [11], more and more studies have confirmed its role as a tumor suppressor in various tumors [12–14]. In melanoma, SPRED1 frequently undergoes gene deletion and mutation, and its functional deficiency is closely related to tumor occurrence and development as well as resistance to MAPK-targeted therapy [15]. This article aims to systematically review the role of SPRED1 in melanoma and its research progress, providing a more comprehensive theoretical basis for the diagnosis, treatment, and basic research of melanoma.

The mutational landscape of melanoma

Melanoma, like other malignant tumors, develops through the accumulation of genetic mutations. These mutations cause cells to proliferate uncontrollably and form an invasive cell phenotype. Melanoma carries the most severe burden of somatic genetic variations among all human tumors [16, 17]. In skin melanomas exposed to sunlight over a long period or intermittently, the most common somatic mutations affect genes that control core cellular processes, such as proliferation (BRAF, NRAS, and NF1), growth and metabolism [phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) and KIT proto-oncogene tyrosine kinase (KIT)], anti-apoptotic ability [tumor protein p53 (TP53)], cell cycle control [cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A (CDKN2A)], and replication lifespan [telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT)] [18, 19]. KIT, NF1, BRAF, NRAS, SF3B1, and SPRED1 are significantly mutated genes. ATRX and SF3B1 mutations are more common in lower-site skin melanomas, while CTNNB1 is present in higher-site skin melanomas. NF1, PTEN, CDKN2A, SPRED1, ATM, CHEK2, and ARID1B are often affected by chromosomal copy number loss, while TERT, KIT, BRAF, YAP1, CDK4, CCND1, GAB2, MDM2, SKP2, and MITF typically undergo amplification [20]. Some less frequent but significant genomic alterations indicate the commonality of signaling networks during tumor development, including MAPK, PI3K, Notch, Wnt/b-catenin, cell cycle, DNA repair, and telomere maintenance pathways. These genomic variations typically lead to abnormal activation of two major signaling pathways in melanoma: the RAS/RAF/MEK/ERK signaling cascade (also known as the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway) and the phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT pathway [21]. The MAPK signaling pathway physiologically participates in the transfer of extracellular signals (such as growth factors and hormones) to the cell nucleus, thereby leading to the expression of genes related to cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival [22, 23]. Moreover, studies have shown that MAPK activation plays a key role in the biological processes of different types of cancers, and it is the most frequently abnormally activated pathway in melanoma [24]. Up to 90% of melanomas exhibit abnormal activation of the MAPK pathway. The PI3K signaling pathway typically participates in the regulation of cell homeostasis and has been proven to play a core role in different types of cancers, including melanoma, where it is the second most frequently abnormally activated pathway. SPRED1 may play a tumor suppressor role in melanoma

Melanoma is a type of malignant tumor that originates from melanocytes [25]. Based on the location of onset and clinical characteristics, it can be classified into various subtypes, among which cutaneous melanoma is the most common. Other subtypes include mucosal melanoma, uveal melanoma, and acral melanoma, etc. [26]. Different subtypes of melanoma exhibit significant differences in molecular characteristics and pathogenesis. The abnormal pattern of SPRED1 also shows subtype specificity.

The human SPRED1 gene is located on chromosome 15q14, and the protein encoded by it consists of 444 amino acids [27]. The SPRED1 protein has three different domains, including the N-terminal EVH1 domain (The Enabled/VASP homology 1), the C-terminal Sprouty-related domain SPR (Sprouty-related), and the central c-kit binding domain KBD (c-Kit binding domain). These three domains have different functions. The EVH1 domain is the N-terminal domain of SPRED1, which binds to other proteins by recognizing proline-rich motifs. The crystal structure shows that this domain forms a globular structure composed of 7 β-strands and α-helices, and the surface hydrophobic pocket is the core site for binding proline [28]. Its core function is to bind to the proline-rich domain (PRD) of neurofibromin protein (NF1), recruiting NF1 to the cell membrane [29], laying the foundation for subsequent inhibition of Ras activity. The SPR domain is specific to the SPRED family and contains multiple conserved cysteine residues, which may participate in protein dimerization or post-translational modifications (such as palmitoylation). The C-terminal of this domain can bind to phosphatidylinositol on the cell membrane, mediating the membrane localization of SPRED1 [30]. This process is a key prerequisite for its Ras inhibitory function, ensuring that SPRED1 can interact with target molecules at the core site of signal transduction (the cell membrane). KBD is a structure necessary for phosphorylation, so it is crucial for the normal function of SPRED1. In recent years, it has been discovered that SPRED1 can regulate important activities, such as cell development, movement, and proliferation, and is involved in pathological physiological processes, such as tumor metastasis and hematopoietic regulation. In wound healing and chemotaxis experiments, overexpression of SPRED1 dissolved actin stress fibers and inhibited chemokine-induced cell migration [31]. SPRED1 is a key regulatory factor for RhoA-mediated cell movement and signal transduction, not only inhibiting cell migration induced by serum growth factors but also inhibiting chemokine-induced cell migration [32]. As a tumor suppressor gene in melanoma, knockdown of SPRED1 leads to ERK activation and increased cell proliferation [10]. SPRED1 is an inhibitory gene for acute myeloid leukemia (AML), and high expression of SPRED1 in newly diagnosed AML significantly reduces its expression, while in patients after treatment, SPRED1 expression increases. Overexpression of SPRED1 inhibits ERK signal transduction and cell proliferation of AML cells, while silencing SPRED1 activates ERK signal transduction and enhances cell proliferation [33]. SPRED1 is an inhibitor of fibroblast growth factor signal transduction and plays a key role in the development of lung branching morphology and its tissues. The expression level of SPRED1 in lung adenocarcinoma patients is low, and high expression of SPRED1 predicts a longer survival period [34]. Other studies have shown that SPRED1 can act as an important tumor suppressor factor in hepatocellular carcinoma, and the reduction of SPRED1 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma promotes the invasion and metastasis of tumor cells, while overexpression of SPRED1 reduces the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of liver cancer cells and the levels of metastasis-related proteins (MMP2 and MMP9) [35]. SPRED1 inhibits ERK activation by forming a complex with Raf, and inhibition of SPRED1 can induce ERK phosphorylation in HepG2 cells, while overexpression of SPRED1 can reduce the level of ERK phosphorylation in MHCC97H cells [36]. The SPR domain of SPRED1 can also bind to Caveolin-1 to regulate the translocation of SPRED1 to the plasma membrane and affect the ERK signaling pathway [37].

Melanoma of the skin is the most common type of melanoma. The GISTIC analysis of 363 human skin melanoma samples from the TCGA database revealed significant genomic alteration in SPRED1 (Fig. 3). The genomic alterations of the SPRED1 gene account for a high proportion in the disease of cutaneous melanoma. The overall deletion frequency of the SPRED1 gene was 24% (q = 0.0076). 6% of the cases had alterations in the SPRED1 gene, including homozygous deletion (2.7%), truncating mutations (1.1%), missense mutations (1.9%), and gene fusions resulting from truncation events (0.28%) [10]. Missense mutations were mainly concentrated in the EVH1 and KBD domains, such as R27W (EVH1), G157R (KBD), etc. These mutations significantly reduced the binding ability of SPRED1 to NF1 or Ras. Nonsense mutations (such as Q123X, R202X) and frameshift mutations (such as c.567delC) led to truncation of the SPRED1 protein, missing key functional domains. Based on these data, it is estimated that the proportion of functional loss alterations of SPRED1 in human skin melanoma is 4.1%, and this change is achieved through deep deletion or truncating mutations. These genomic alterations led to the loss of function of the SPRED1 protein, preventing it from normally exerting its negative regulatory effect on the MAPK pathway, thereby promoting the occurrence and development of melanoma. Mucosal melanoma originates from melanocytes on the mucosal surface [38]. Based on the location of the lesion, it can be classified into three major categories: head and neck, digestive tract, and urogenital tract [39]. Among them, mucosal melanoma in the head and neck region accounts for the highest proportion [40]. Compared with cutaneous melanoma, mucosal melanoma has unique clinical and pathological characteristics. The age of onset is older, with a median age of 70 years, and there are slightly more females than males [41]. The disease progresses rapidly, and most cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage, with a 5-year survival rate of only 14% [42]. At the molecular level, the mutation spectrum of mucosal melanoma is significantly different from that of cutaneous melanoma. The BRAF mutation rate is only 8% [43], while the KIT mutation rate is as high as 10–22% [44]. In addition, there are frequent TERT promoter mutations and TP53 mutations in mucosal melanoma. SPRED1 may play a tumor suppressor role in mucosal melanoma. Among 43 mucosal melanoma samples, 26% (11/43) showed SPRED1 deletion, which includes double allelic inactivation achieved through deep deletion or through truncating mutations combined with the wild-type SPRED1 allele [45]. Whole-genome sequencing of 67 mucosal melanoma samples revealed abnormal SPRED1 phenomena [15]. There is a mutually exclusive trend between SPRED1 deletion and NF1 functional loss mutations, suggesting that SPRED1 deletion and NF1 deletion may play similar roles in the tumor progression of mucosal melanoma. SPRED1 deletion is significantly co-occurring with KIT changes (30%, 7/23 cases). In vitro and in vivo models indicate that in the case of KIT mutation, SPRED1 deletion leads to increased MAPK pathway activity and confers resistance to the KIT tyrosine kinase inhibitor dasatinib. These results lay the foundation for positioning SPRED1 as a tumor suppressor gene, which interacts with activating KIT mutations to maintain MAPK signaling and may make tumors resistant to KIT inhibition therapy. SPRED1 alterations across melanoma subtypes

The mutation of SPRED1 is a new mechanism for melanoma resistance to MAPK-targeted therapy

Melanoma is one of the tumor types with the most significant genetic variations [46]. Its formation is driven by the excessive activation of the MAPK pathway, which is achieved through activating mutations in genes such as BRAF, NRAS, and KIT, or through the inactivation of negative regulatory factors of this pathway (such as NF1) [47, 48]. The BRAF mutation is the most common mechanism of MAPK pathway activation in melanoma [49], with an incidence of approximately 60–70% in cutaneous melanoma [50]. The mutation types and survival curves of 360 human melanoma samples in the TCGA database are shown in Fig. 4. Patients with BRAF mutation have a relatively better prognosis, with a 5-year overall survival rate of approximately 55–60%, significantly higher than those with NF1 mutation and wild-type 3. The 5-year OS rate for NRAS mutation patients is about 45–50%, slightly lower than that of BRAF mutation patients. Patients with NF1 mutation and wild-type 3 have a poorer prognosis, with a 5-year OS rate of approximately 30%, and their survival curves decline the fastest. Besides V600E (accounting for 80%) [51], there are also V600K (10%) [52], V600R (5%) [53] and other mutation types. Approximately 50% of cutaneous melanomas are driven by the BRAFV600E mutation, and specific BRAFV600E inhibitors have shown significant clinical efficacy [54–57]. However, within about 1 year after starting treatment, these targeted therapies always develop resistance [58, 59]. By analyzing copy number variations in human melanomas, it was found that the SPRED1 gene has frequent biallelic deletions [45]. Recent structural studies have further confirmed the physical interactions between SPRED1, NF1, and Ras [29] (Fig. 5). SPRED1 is the core mechanism by which it functions in melanoma [63, 64]. Under normal circumstances, SPRED1 binds to NF1 and recruits it to the cell membrane, bringing the NF1 GAP domain close to the membrane-bound Ras. The KBD domain of SPRED1 directly binds to Ras, stabilizing the GDP-bound state of Ras. SPRED1 inhibits the isoprenylation of Ras, reducing its membrane localization [65]. Enhancing the inhibitory effect of NF1 on Ras thereby inhibits the activation of the MAPK pathway and maintains the normal physiological state of the cells. When SPRED1 has a functional deficiency, this inhibitory effect is weakened, leading to excessive activation of the MAPK pathway. The function of SPRED1 as a tumor suppressor gene in melanoma is significantly background-dependent, especially showing distinct differences in the context of KIT mutations and BRAF V600E mutations. Its absence also represents a new mechanism for resistance to MAPK-targeted therapy. In melanoma driven by KIT mutations, the inactivation of SPRED1 (such as double allele deletion, functional mutation) can significantly accelerate tumor development and enhance cell proliferation [45]. This phenomenon is directly related to the regulatory position of SPRED1 in the MAPK pathway. SPRED1 exerts its function by directly inhibiting Ras activity, with its functional nodes located downstream of KIT and upstream of RAF [65]. Therefore, when KIT is activated, the absence of SPRED1 will greatly enhance the MAPK signal transmitted through wild-type Ras, amplifying the proliferation effect driven by KIT, and becoming an important driver of the progression of melanoma with KIT mutations. Unlike the KIT-driven model, in melanoma with BRAF V600E mutations, the absence of SPRED1 has a smaller impact on tumor initiation or cell growth without treatment. The main reason lies in the fact that BRAF V600E can bypass wild-type Ras and directly activate downstream MEK without relying on Ras signal transmission. Coupled with the fact that SPRED1 may have a weaker MAPK regulatory effect than BRAF V600E, this might also explain why the effect of SPRED1 function deficiency on melanoma cell proliferation only becomes apparent when the MAPK inhibitor is deeply suppressed (i.e., at relatively high dabrafenib concentrations). In the presence of BRAF inhibition, melanoma cells may rely on the basal MAPK signal to survive. The absence of SPRED1 itself will increase the remaining MAPK activity and maintain a low level of cell proliferation, enabling melanoma cells to rapidly resist treatment. By simulating the absence of SPRED1 in adult zebrafish melanoma and human melanoma cell lines, it can enhance resistance to BRAF inhibition, thereby maintaining low-level MAPK signal transduction. In a patient with melanoma, the absence of SPRED1 is associated with acquired resistance to MAPK inhibition. Therefore, the absence of SPRED1 is a new mechanism for melanoma resistance to MAPK-targeted therapy.

Adult zebrafish melanoma and human melanoma cell lines model application of SPRED1 gene mutation

Zebrafish, as an important model organism [67], has been widely applied in melanoma research. By using a rapid tissue-specific CRISPR technique to simulate the genotype related to the inactivation of SPRED1 in zebrafish, it was found that SPRED1 acts as a tumor suppressor. The zebrafish model can well simulate the role of SPRED1 in melanoma, providing an effective tool for an in-depth study of the function and related mechanisms of SPRED1. The zebrafish model can also be used for drug screening to evaluate the therapeutic effects of different drugs on SPRED1-deficient melanoma, providing a reference for clinical treatment.

Human melanoma cell lines are important tools for studying the function and mechanism of SPRED1. In human melanoma cell lines, by using genetic manipulation methods such as CRISPR/Cas9 technology and RNA interference technology, the absence of SPRED1 is simulated to confirm that it can enhance the resistance to BRAF inhibition. In cell line models, the effects of SPRED1 deficiency on cell proliferation, survival, and the activity of signaling pathways, as well as the response to drugs, can be studied more directly. Human melanoma cell lines can also be used for drug sensitivity testing to evaluate the inhibitory effects of different drugs on melanoma cells with SPRED1 deficiency, providing a basis for screening effective therapeutic drugs. In addition, through gene expression profiling analysis, proteomics analysis, etc., molecular changes caused by SPRED1 deficiency can be deeply explored, and new therapeutic targets can be identified.

Conclusions

SPRED1 plays a crucial role in the occurrence and development of melanoma. It exhibits significant genomic alteration in both cutaneous melanoma and mucosal melanoma, including homozygous deletions, truncating mutations, and missense mutations, which result in the loss of SPRED1 function and the inability to effectively inhibit the activation of the MAPK signaling pathway. The loss of SPRED1 function enhances the activity of the MAPK signaling pathway, promoting the proliferation, survival, invasion, and metastasis of melanoma cells, and is also one of the important mechanisms for melanoma to develop resistance to MAPK-targeted therapy. The mutation of SPRED1 is not only found in melanoma, but also in Legius syndrome, acute myeloid leukemia, ovarian cancer, lung adenocarcinoma, gastric cancer, and other diseases [68–71], indicating that the role of SPRED1 in tumor occurrence and development has a certain universality. SPRED1 has significant clinical significance. Its expression level and genomic mutation can be used as indicators for prognosis assessment of melanoma, and it itself may also become a new target for the diagnosis and treatment of melanoma.

Therapeutic strategies targeting SPRED1 deficiency, such as combination with MAPK inhibitors and immunotherapy, show good application prospects. While the combination of BRAF and MEK inhibitors targeting the MAPK pathway has markedly improved therapeutic outcomes, acquired resistance remains a critical challenge. Upon MAPK pathway inhibition, alternative compensatory pathways—such as the PI3K–AKT–mTOR axis—are often activated, serving as a key driver of treatment resistance. Consequently, the development of combination strategies involving MAPK pathway inhibitors and agents targeting other aberrant pathways has emerged as a major research focus. For instance, combinations of BRAF/MEK inhibitors with PI3K inhibitors or mTOR inhibitors have been explored for the treatment of patients who develop resistance to MAPK inhibitors alone. Thus, there is an urgent need to investigate more effective mutation-specific targeted combination regimens. Notably, the combination of MAPK inhibitors and immune checkpoint inhibitors represents another extensively studied cross-modal strategy (targeted therapy-immunotherapy). On one hand, MAPK inhibitors directly suppress tumor cell proliferation; on the other, they can modulate the tumor microenvironment to augment the efficacy of immunotherapy. However, current research still faces substantial hurdles: the mechanism of action of SPRED1 remains incompletely elucidated, detection methodologies require further optimization, and therapeutic strategies need additional clinical validation. Looking ahead, a key direction will involve advancing biomarker development and dissecting melanoma microenvironment markers to enable molecular subtype-guided precision targeted therapy. Leveraging high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatics analyses to define patients’ molecular subtypes, and subsequently developing tailored targeted agents, will provide new avenues to improve the prognosis of melanoma patients.
