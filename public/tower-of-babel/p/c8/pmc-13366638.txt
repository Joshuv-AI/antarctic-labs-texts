Influence of adrenomedullin on the enzymatic activity of dipeptidyl peptidase 3 in vitro
Authors: Ruqayyah Nissen, Amin Yourdkhani, Marie-Louise Hollerbach, Katja Seewald, Karine Santos, Joachim Struck, Andreas Bergmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCirculating Dipeptidyl Peptidase 3 (DPP3) hydrolyses dipeptides from the amino terminus of its substrates and excess levels represent a target for the treatment of cardiogenic shock. Known substrates include Angiotensin II (Ang II), but there might be more. Adrenomedullin (ADM) is a 52-mer peptide regulating vascular tone and integrity, which also is a therapeutic target for the treatment of shock. The pathways leading to shock involving DPP3 and ADM have been considered independent so far. Here, we assessed the influence of ADM on the activity of DPP3 in vitro using an established assay. ADM inhibited DPP3 activity at a similar IC50 concentration (6.5 µM) as the cognate substrate Ang II (6.6 µM). As opposed to Ang II, ADM was barely cleaved by DPP3 in vitro. Stepwise C-terminal truncation of ADM essentially did not change its inhibitory effect. Inhibition of DPP3 activity by ADM was reversed by an anti-N-terminal ADM antibody.Keywords: Dipeptidyl peptidase 3, adrenomedullin, enzyme inhibition

Introduction

DPP3 (EC 3.4.14.4) is a zinc-dependent aminopeptidase, which can hydrolyse dipeptides from the amino terminus of certain small peptides12. It is ubiquitously expressed and mainly localised in the cytoplasm of cells. Intracellularly, DPP3 functions as a regulator of the cellular oxidative stress response pathway, e.g. the Keap1-Nrf2 mediated antioxidative response1. Before and during life-threatening pathological conditions such as septic shock and cardiogenic shock, DPP3 can leak into the blood circulation (designated as circulating DPP3, cDPP3), where it degrades Ang II and leads to hemodynamic instability, myocardial depression, organ failure and eventually death3,4,5. An inhibitory humanised monoclonal anti-DPP3 antibody for the treatment of cardiogenic shock is currently being developed6. The substrate specificity of DPP3 is only partially understood. A few physiological cleavable substrates have been known for a long time. These include Angiotensins, Enkephalins, and Endorphins1,7. More recently, several additional natural peptide substrates have been identified: hemorphin-4, valorphin, Leu-valorphin-Arg, and β-casomorphin8.

ADM, a circulating 52 amino acid-comprising peptide, has been initially described as a potent vasodilator9. Later, multiple functions of ADM have been discovered,10 but the key role of ADM identified by both genetic evidence and experimental data is to maintain endothelial barrier function11,12. Functionally active ADM is generated in sequential maturation steps from a larger precursor peptide, the last step being a conversion of the C-terminal glycine at position 53 from the functionally inactive Adrenomedullin-Glycine (ADM-Gly) into an amide13,14.

Plasma concentrations of ADM and, even more, of ADM-Gly increase under several pathological conditions, where endothelial barrier function is disturbed, reaching peak concentrations in septic shock15,16,14,17,13. Such increase has been interpreted as a compensatory, albeit insufficient mechanism to combat the endothelial disturbance which drives organ dysfunction and eventually leads to death18. A therapeutic approach has been developed to increase levels of circulating active ADM in a compartment-specific way using intravenous administration of a non-blocking humanised monoclonal anti-N-terminal ADM antibody (Enibarcimab)19,20. This antibody induces an increase in plasma ADM concentration, and, importantly, due to its epitope specificity, does not prevent ADM from binding to its receptor and from inducing signal transduction21,19,20. In a phase 2 clinical trial, Enibarcimab was investigated in a septic shock population having elevated pre-dose plasma concentrations of its binding target ADM.19 The 28-day mortality was barely reduced by Enibarcimab, when the Full Analysis Set was analyzed.19 Interestingly, the mortality-reducing effect of Enibarcimab became apparent, when patients with supranormal plasma cDPP3 pre-dose concentrations were excluded from the analysis22,23. A multivariate interaction analysis revealed a significant interaction of cDPP3 levels with Enibarcimab treatment efficacy, even after correction for baseline disease severity22.

The pathways associated with elevation of cDPP3 and elevation of plasma ADM, which lead to septic shock, appear unrelated22. For several types of critical illness, it has been shown that concentrations of cDPP3 and ADM are barely correlated and that both are independently prognostic and therefore add value to each other when combined for outcome prediction24,25,26,18. Whether ADM can influence DPP3 activity has not been investigated so far.

Material and methods

Peptides and proteins

All peptides were chemically synthesised, purified to a grade of at least 90%, and lyophilised (Peptides & Elephants, Hennigsdorf, Germany). Immediately prior to use, peptides were dissolved and diluted as required in pure water.

Native human DPP3 was prepared as described27. Briefly, human DPP3 was purified to ≥95% from blood cell lysate (InVent Diagnostica GmbH, Hennigsdorf, Germany) in a sequential process employing first a pre-clearance step, where the lysate was incubated with Sepharose 4B (Sigma Aldrich) to remove a large portion of lipoproteins and lipids, second, an immuno-affinity chromatography using a specific monoclonal anti-DPP3 antibody (AK2552, produced as described for other anti-DPP3 antibodies28) and third, an ion exchange chromatography step on a HiTrap Q-sepharose HP column (GE Healthcare).

A humanised anti-N-terminal ADM monoclonal antibody (initially named Adrecizumab, later Enibarcimab)29 was purchased from (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, USA). This antibody was based on a mouse monoclonal antibody developed against a peptide representing the first 21 amino acids of ADM, conjugated to a carrier protein (BSA)21. For its use in the DPP3 activity assay, the purchased stock solution (7.6 mg/mL) was diluted at various rates in PBS buffer as follows: 1:5, 1:7.6, 1:15.2, 1:76, 1:760, 1:7,600, 1:76,000, 1:760,000, 1:7,600,000.

Palivizumab (“Synagis”), a humanised monoclonal antibody directed against the F-protein of the RS virus, was purchased at a local pharmacy (Elch Apotheke, Berlin-Frohnau, Germany). For its use in the DPP3 activity assay, the purchased stock solution (100 mg/mL) was diluted at various rates in PBS buffer as follows: 1:300, 1:1,000, 1:10,000, 1:100,000, 1:1,000,000.

DPP3 activity assay

DPP3 activity was measured using a soluble activity assay based on an established method30. In brief, a fluorogenic substrate (Arg-Arg-β-naphthylamide) was incubated with a constant concentration of DPP3, and fluorescence of β-naphthylamine continuously generated by the turnover of the substrate was detected over a period of 60 min. The effect of the addition of various concentrations of ADM-related peptides was investigated.

In more detail, the DPP3 activity was determined as follows:

The following solutions were prepared:

50 ng/mL purified, native human DPP3 in Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.3 at 25 °C);

50 ng/mL purified, native human DPP3 in Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.3 at 25 °C);

ADM-related peptides at the following concentrations in ultra pure water: 830; 400; 100; 40; 10; 4; 1 µM;

ADM-related peptides at the following concentrations in ultra pure water: 830; 400; 100; 40; 10; 4; 1 µM;

125 µM Arg-Arg-β-naphthylamide substrate solution (Bachem #K-1085) in Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.3 at 25 °C).

125 µM Arg-Arg-β-naphthylamide substrate solution (Bachem #K-1085) in Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.3 at 25 °C).

Dilutions of pure βNA (Beta-Naphtylamine; Sigma-Aldrich #31618) in pure water were used as calibrators (0.6; 2; 5; 16; 46; 134 µM). Reactions were run in nonbinding, uncoated, black 96 well microplates (Greiner Bio-One #7635165).

Calibrators: 10 µL of the respective calibrator were pipetted in duplicates, followed by 10 µL pure water and 80 µL Tris-HCl-buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH = 8.3 at 25 °C).

Samples: 10 µL of DPP3 were pipetted in duplicates into one side of the wells, afterwards 10 µL ADM-related peptides were pipetted in duplicates into the other side of the wells (no mixing to minimise possible inactivation of DPP3 due to strong pH changes). As a negative control, 10 µL Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.3 at 25 °C) were pipetted in duplicates into one side of the wells, afterwards 10 µL ADM-related peptides were pipetted in duplicates into the other side of the wells. Afterwards, 80 µL of substrate solution was added to each well.

Immediately after pipetting, the kinetic measurement was started, and the relative fluorescence units (RFU) (excitation at 340 nm and emission at 410 nm) were measured using a fluorescence plate reader (SpectraMax ID5; Molecular Devices).

The measurement was performed at 37 °C from t = 0 every 10 min for 1 h.

Raw data were evaluated as follows: Raw Data (mean RFU over time) were plotted, and the slope [RFU/min] was determined by linear regression fitting (R2 > 0.95). The determined RFU/min were converted to µmol/min as follows: From the β-naphthylamine calibrator solutions, the RFU were measured. By linear regression of these data (forced through origin), it was determined how much RFU are generated per amount of β-naphthylamine, e.g. RFU/µmol. Using this factor, the activity measured for the samples in RFU/min was converted into µmol/min. Calculation of Inhibition: Inhibition (%) = 100-(activity only DPP3/activity DPP3 + Inhibitor) *100. Plots of each ADM fragment dose-dependent inhibition of DPP3 were generated, and the concentration of each ADM fragment leading to 50% inhibition of the maximal DPP3 activity (IC50) was read from the curves.

The effect of an anti-N-terminal ADM antibody and Palivizumab on the ADM-mediated inhibition of DPP3 activity was assessed using the same assay as above. 25 µL of 100 µM ADM and 25 µL of various dilutions in PBS buffer of the anti-N-terminal ADM antibody and Palivizumab, respectively, were pre-incubated for 30 min at 25 °C. Then, 20 µL of each peptide-antibody mixture was pipetted per well, and 10 µL DPP3 (50 ng/mL) and 70 µL substrate solution as above were added. Incubation, detection, and evaluation were done as outlined above.

Assessment of peptide cleavage by DPP3

ADM and Ang II, respectively, were incubated at 0.88 µg/mL with or without DPP3 (12 µg/mL) in 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.8, for 30 min at 37 °C and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Peptides were analysed by mass spectrometry as follows (Proteome Factory, Berlin, Germany): The samples were diluted 1:5 or 1:10 in 0.5% acetonitrile with 0.5% formic acid. LCMS/MS analyses were performed with an Ultimate 3000 nanoRSLC system interfaced to a Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer (ThermoFisher, Bremen, Germany). Diluted samples were applied to nanoLC-ESI-MS/MS after acidification. After trapping and desalting the peptides on an enrichment column (Dionex PepMap C18, 0.3 mm x 5 mm) using 0.5% formic acid solution for four minutes peptides were separated on a Reprosil C18-AQ column (0.075 mm x 500 mm column (Dr. Maisch, Ammerbuch) using an acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid gradient from 12% to 40% acetonitrile. MS survey spectra (R = 70.000) were automatically taken in FT-mode according to the manufacturer’s instrument settings for nanoLC-ESI-MSMS analyses. Peak areas of peptide ions of interest were determined, and areas of respective ions were compared between samples, in which peptides had been incubated in the presence or absence of DPP3.

Statistical evaluation

Both for statistical evaluation and graphical representation of results, GraphPad Prism Software (Version 10.4.2), GraphPad Software, Inc., Boston, USA, was used.

Results

Human ADM was added at increasing doses to a constant concentration of purified human DPP3, and DPP3 activity was measured in an in vitro assay using Arg-Arg-β-naphthylamide as a fluorogenic substrate.

ADM inhibited DPP3 activity in a dose-dependent manner, with an IC50 of 6.5 µM, which was similar to what was observed for Ang II (6.6 µM), a known substrate of DPP3, which served as positive control (Figure 1).

Peptides were incubated with purified human DPP3 in vitro, and analysed by mass spectroscopy. For Ang II, almost complete cleavage by DPP3 into species lacking the first 2 or 4 amino-terminal amino acids was detected. Discussion

Using an established in vitro assay, we demonstrate for the first time that the enzymatic activity of DPP3 can be specifically inhibited by ADM in a competitive manner.

Known substrates of DPP3 are small peptides comprising up to ten amino acids at most33. In this context, it does not appear obvious to assess ADM, a 52-mer peptide, as a possible inhibitor and substrate of DPP3. However, we have been intrigued by the clinical observation of the involvement of both DPP3 and ADM in the pathogenesis of shock, in particular septic and cardiogenic shock: Plasma concentrations of both increase considerably under these conditions34,35,4,15. cDPP3 has been shown to causally contribute to the development of organ dysfunction, shock and death by degrading Ang II and potentially additional mechanisms35. The detrimental effects of supranormal concentrations of cDPP3 can be reduced by an inhibitory anti-DPP3 antibody, which is currently in clinical development6 3. In contrast to cDPP3, the elevation of plasma ADM is considered not to cause progression of disease, but rather to be a compensatory response to cytokine-mediated permeabilization of the endothelial barrier, which leads to reduced tissue oxygenation, followed by organ dysfunction, shock, and eventually death18,36. An anti-ADM antibody is currently in clinical development, which does not inhibit ADM function, but rather supports it in further increasing the circulating concentration of active ADM, thereby fostering repair of the impaired endothelial barrier37.

While the DPP3-related pathway and the ADM-related pathway in shock appear to be independent from each other,18 a possible interaction between DPP3 and ADM, even though unlikely at first glance, cannot be excluded, and the influence of intervening drugs on this have not been addressed so far.

We hypothesised that ADM might influence DPP3 activity. In an in vitro assay employing Arg-Arg-β-naphthylamide as a fluorogenic substrate, we found that ADM dose-dependently inhibited the enzymatic activity of DPP3. Assuming that the interaction between ADM and DPP3 would involve the amino terminus of ADM – analogously to known substrates of DPP3 – we tested a series of C-terminally truncated peptide variants of ADM, the smallest variant comprising only the twelve amino-terminal amino acids of ADM. All of these peptides reduced DPP3 activity in vitro at similar concentrations.

While our results indicate that ADM acts as a competitive inhibitor, the exact mechanism of how ADM conveys the observed inhibition of DPP3 activity is subject to speculation at this stage. The substrate specificity of DPP3 has not been fully elucidated yet. A preference has been described for (a) a positively charged, unmodified, N-terminus, (b) the ability of the substrate to form β-strand secondary structures, (c) hydrophobic amino acid residues at the P1’ position, and d) a proline residue at the position P138,39,40,41,8. ADM does not exhibit such structural features,42 except from a positively charged amino acid at position 2. On this background and regarding its size, it is not surprising that we found ADM to be only marginally cleaved by DPP3 in vitro.

It has to be emphasised that all results reported in our study stem from in vitro experiments. We consider it not excluded that the observed inhibition of DPP3 activity by ADM occurs in vivo. However, it seems unlikely that this effect, if it occurs in vivo, is relevant, at least systemically, neither in health nor in disease. In healthy subjects, reported circulating concentrations of DPP3 and ADM are below 30 ng/mL and 43 pg/mL, respectively43,44. In refractory cardiogenic shock, the median cDPP3 concentration has been reported as 76 ng/mL, and the median ADM concentration as 91 pg/mL34,35. Thus, circulating molar concentrations of ADM make up less than 2% relative to those of DPP3. Local concentrations might differ from systemic concentrations, so that the ratio of ADM to DPP3 might be different there.

Clearly, our study must be considered hypothesis-generating, and additional studies must follow to investigate whether ADM has an influence on DPP3 in vivo as well.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations: The experiments reported have been conducted in vitro, and translatability to the situation in vivo is unclear at this stage (an experimental substrate for DPP3 was used; peptide-, protein- and substrate concentrations might be different in vivo locally and systemically; experiments were conducted with pure components in certain buffer conditions).

Conclusions

ADM has been shown to inhibit the enzymatic activity of DPP3 in vitro. This may indicate that ADM plays a role in vivo in the regulation of the activity of DPP3, while it should be finally evaluated under such conditions.
