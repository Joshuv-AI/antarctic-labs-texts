Mitochondrial cristae remodeling: Mechanisms, functions, and pathology
Authors: Jianglong Yu, Yuanchun Luo, Jiacheng Lin, Zhuofan Li, Zheng Fang, He He, Chaojun Yan, Zhiyin Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial cristae are the principal sites of oxidative phosphorylation and are central to mitochondria-dependent energy metabolism. Rather than static folds, cristae are dynamic bioenergetic compartments that remodel in response to physiological and stress cues. During remodeling, their number, length, width, lateral alignment, curvature/stiffness, and the geometry of crista junctions (CJs) can change. Depending on cellular context, cristae may increase in abundance, tighten or widen, and exhibit opening or closure of CJs, with corresponding effects on respiratory-chain organization and supercomplex assembly. Key regulators include OPA1 (and its proteolytic processing), the MICOS complex that scaffolds CJs, F1Fo-ATP synthase dimerization/oligomerization that shapes high-curvature ridges, and cardiolipin, which stabilizes inner-membrane architecture. Abnormal cristae compromise electron transport, ATP production, and mitochondrial metabolism, contributing to neurodegeneration and metabolic disease etc. In this review, we synthesize current insights into the molecular control of cristae ultrastructure and its impact on mitochondrial metabolism, delineate structural features and quantitative readouts, and highlight mechanisms that govern cristae remodeling under physiological and stress conditions, with an emphasis on diseases arising from aberrant crista architecture.Keywords: Cristae remodeling, MICOS complex, Oxidative phosphorylation, OPA1, F1Fo-ATP synthase, Cardiolipin

Introduction

Mitochondria are double-membrane-bound organelles, consisting of the outer membrane, intermembrane space, inner membrane, and matrix ​(Colaco et al., 2012). The inner membrane undergoes extensive invagination, forming mitochondrial cristae, which greatly increase its surface area, thereby facilitating oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production (Brown et al., 2025; Daumke & van der Laan, 2025).

In recent years, a growing body of evidence has indicated that mitochondrial cristae morphology and remodeling regulate multiple cellular functions, and abnormalities in cristae structure are associated with various human diseases (Daumke & van der Laan, 2025). As mitochondrial cristae belong to the ultrastructural level of organelles, their observation has been largely limited to electron microscopy techniques. Currently, the mechanisms underlying cristae formation and remodeling remain incompletely understood.

With advances in electron microscopy and super-resolution microscopy, future research will focus on elucidating the regulatory mechanisms of mitochondrial cristae remodeling, their biological functions, and the intrinsic relationship between cristae dynamics and human diseases.

Architecture of mitochondrial cristae

The structure of mitochondrial cristae is complex and diverse. The number and length of cristae vary across different tissues, and their arrangement also differs. These arrangements can be categorized into three main types: “lamellar cristae”, “tubular cristae”, and “vesicular cristae” (Hanaki et al., 1985) (Fig. 1(A)). Cristae can be further divided into functionally distinct regions, including cristae junctions (CJs)—elliptical or circular structures that connect the cristae to the inner boundary membrane (IBM)—the cristae tip (CT) at the distal end, and the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) complexes, which are responsible for electron transfer and the establishment of an electrochemical proton gradient that drives ATP synthesis (Fig. 1(B)).

Cristae junctions (CJs)

The essential structures that maintain mitochondrial cristae are called cristae junctions (CJs). These junctions are highly conserved, with similar size and diameter (ranging from 20 to 50 nm) across different species (Fig. 1(B)–(C)). CJs serve as gating switches that separate the mitochondrial intermembrane space from the cristae lumen. They form channels for the diffusion and transport of proteins and metabolites, thereby regulating material transfer and information exchange among the cristae lumen, intermembrane space, and cytosol (Perkins et al., 1997). As the core structures essential for cristae formation and integrity, CJs are assembled and maintained by the MICOS complex (Fig. 1(B)). The MICOS complex is an evolutionarily conserved protein complex localized to the inner mitochondrial membrane. It consists of at least six subunits in yeast (MIC10, MIC12, MIC19, MIC26, MIC27, and MIC60) and seven in mammals (MIC10, MIC13, MIC19, MIC25, MIC26, MIC27, and MIC60). The complex can be divided into two key subcomplexes: the MIC60 subcomplex (containing MIC60, MIC25, and MIC19) and the MIC10 subcomplex (comprising MIC10, MIC13, MIC26, and MIC27). Both are crucial for the stability and function of MICOS. Deletion of any subunit except MIC26 disrupts CJ integrity, leading to detachment of cristae from the inner boundary membrane and resulting in stacked membrane sheets within the mitochondrial matrix (Harner et al., 2011). Furthermore, we propose the existence of a mitochondrial intermembrane space bridging (MIB) complex, which includes both the MICOS and the sorting and assembly machinery (SAM) complex (Fig. 1(B)). The role of MICOS in cristae dynamics and metabolism will be elaborated in later sections. OPA1, a dynamin-family GTPase localized to cristae, not only mediates inner mitochondrial membrane fusion but also helps maintain cristae structure by stabilizing and tightening CJs (Ban et al., 2017).

OXPHOS complexes

OXPHOS (oxidative phosphorylation) is facilitated by protein complexes localized on the mitochondrial cristae, where approximately 94% of Complex III and ATP synthase and about 85% of cytochrome c ​are anchored (Scorrano et al., 2002). From an evolutionary perspective, a molecular link exists between the mechanisms of cristae formation and the OXPHOS system (Muñoz-Gómez et al., 2015), further supporting the view that cristae serve as the primary bioenergetic membrane of mitochondria. The OXPHOS system consists of four distinct complexes, which can assemble into higher-order structures known as supercomplexes. Some studies suggest that Complex II (CII) may insert into the spaces between Complex I (CI) and Complex IV (CIV) to form a closed ring-like structure, referred to as the electron transport chain (ETC) supercomplex (Guo et al., 2017).

Cristae tip (CT)

The cristae tip is the terminal structure of the folds formed by the inner mitochondrial membrane, and its pointed morphology helps to expand the membrane surface area for efficient cellular respiration and ATP synthesis (Fig. 1(B)). F1Fo-ATP synthase (Complex V), located on the CT, is a key membrane protein responsible for aerobic ATP synthesis. In addition, ATP synthase has been identified as a crucial factor in cristae formation (Hahn et al., 2016).

The mechanism of mitochondrial cristae formation

The precise mechanism underlying mitochondrial cristae formation remains unclear. However, Harner et al. proposed two hypotheses based on experiments in yeast combining imaging, biochemical, and genetic approaches. According to cristae morphology, they classified cristae into two types: tubular cristae, which are tube-like structures, and lamellar cristae, which are flat and sac-like (Fig. 1(A)). These two types coexist within the same mitochondrion and are proposed to form via distinct mechanisms (Harner et al., 2016).

Tubular cristae formation involves the aggregation of MICOS complexes at the IBM, where they assemble into CJs. Newly synthesized proteins and lipids are transported to the IBM and extend into the matrix from these CJs to form tubular cristae. Throughout this process, dimerized F1Fo-ATP synthase arranges into a regular, helical pattern along the cristae surface to maintain membrane curvature (Harner et al., 2016) (Fig. 2(A)–a).

Theoretical models of cristae formation.

b Lamellar cristae formation: Two mitochondria approach and fuse with each other, forming contact sites (IM-OM contacts). Their inner membranes subsequently appose and fuse under the remodeling force of Mgm1/OPA1, forming cristae with the assistance of both F1Fo-ATP synthase and the MICOS complex.

c Vesicle budding model: Vesicles bud within the mitochondrial matrix. These vesicles enlarge and eventually fuse with the inner boundary membrane to form cristae.

d Invagination model: Triggered by specific proteins or lipids, the inner mitochondrial membrane spontaneously invaginates and bends to form cristae.

e Balloon model: Precursor CJs on the IBM first form a bud. Matrix-localized proteins then exert a pulling force on this membrane domain, causing it to inflate and finally form a crista.

f Cristae fusion model: Vesicles bud from existing cristae, are released into the matrix, and eventually fuse with the inner boundary membrane to form new cristae.

In contrast, lamellar cristae formation is more complex and requires OPA1 (its yeast homolog Mgm1) and mitochondrial fusion. The process initiates when two mitochondria approach each other: their outer membranes fuse first, establishing inner-outer membrane (IM-OM) contact sites. The inner membranes are then drawn together by OPA1 (Mgm1), fusing to form a sac-like protrusion that extends into the matrix along the IM-OM contact site, eventually developing into a crista. This process also depends on F1Fo-ATP synthase and the MICOS complex. The dimeric ATP synthase stabilizes the curved membrane, while MICOS terminates fusion, preventing excessive expansion and maintaining connections to the IBM, thereby forming cristae and CJs (Fig. 2(A)–b).

Additional hypotheses regarding cristae formation have been proposed:

Vesicle budding model: Vesicles form within the mitochondrion, expand via osmosis, and eventually fuse with the IBM to form cristae (Purdue & Lazarow, 2001; Reggiori, 2006). This idea originated from observations of vesicles in the mitochondrial matrix of patient samples, suggesting a potential role in cristae biogenesis (Frey & Mannella, 2000; Mannella et al., 2001) (Fig. 2(A)–c).

Invagination model: The inner membrane invaginates spontaneously to form cristae, supported by physical principles indicating that the membrane can provide the required energy and curvature (Renken et al., 2002). Initiation may involve specific lipids or protein complexes that induce membrane bending (Strauss et al., 2008) (Fig. 2(A)–d).

Balloon model: Precursor CJs on the IBM form an initial dimple, which is then pulled deeper into the matrix by matrix-localized proteins, eventually forming a crista (Zick et al., 2009) (Fig. 2(A)–e).

Cristae fusion model: Preexisting cristae undergo fission to release vesicles, which subsequently fuse with the IBM to generate new cristae (Meeusen et al., 2006; Zick et al., 2009) (Fig. 2(A)–f).

The various hypotheses of cristae formation are not mutually exclusive but likely represent complementary mechanisms that operate in different cellular contexts. Tubular and lamellar cristae pathways can coexist within the same cell type, with their prevalence adapting to distinct physiological states. For instance, tubular cristae formation may dominate during periods of high energy demand requiring rapid membrane expansion, while lamellar cristae may form during mitochondrial fusion events or in response to specific metabolic cues. The vesicle budding and invagination models may represent early evolutionary mechanisms or specialized contexts such as mitochondrial biogenesis during cell division. Environmental factors including metabolic status, oxidative stress, and interorganellar communication can influence which pathway predominates, with key regulators like MICOS, OPA1, and F1Fo-ATP synthase serving as molecular switches between these formation modes (Fig. 2(B)).

The molecular mechanism of mitochondrial cristae remodeling

Mitochondrial cristae dynamics refers to the process by which the morphology, structure, quantity, and spatial distribution of cristae (the folded structures inside mitochondria) undergo dynamic adjustments in response to changes in the cell's physiological state. At its core, cristae maintain functional homeostasis through “fusion-fission-remodeling”, which in turn regulates mitochondrion's key physiological roles, such as energy production and cell apoptosis (Cogliati et al., 2016; Mannella, 2006; Tábara et al., 2024).

For decades, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was the sole method for cristae visualization, though it is incompatible with live imaging. Recently, structured illumination microscopy (Hessian-SIM) has enabled super-resolution, real-time observation of cristae dynamics in living cells (Huang et al., 2018) (Fig. 1(D)). For 3D structural analysis, focused ion beam/scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM) provides detailed tomographic reconstructions of cristae morphology.

Cristae dynamics include several types of structural changes: Elongation; Shortening; Fusion of two cristae; Division into separate cristae; Detachment from the IBM with loss of CJs; Formation of cristae networks; Inter-mitochondrial cristae contact and communication (Hu et al., 2020; Katahira et al., 2025) (Fig. 3).

Several types of mitochondrial cristae movement.

Unlike mitochondrial dynamics, whose regulatores are well documented, the regulatory mechanisms underlying cristae dynamics-such as elongation, shortening, and fission—remain poorly understood. However, it has been found that dysfunction of certain proteins can lead to an imbalance in these processes. OPA1 is essential for cristae integrity. OPA1 knockout (KO) HeLa exhibit reduced mitochondrial number and disrupted cristae (Fig. 2(C) and 2(D)), accompanied by altered dynamics such as shortening, elongation, and cristae-IBM contact (Diokmetzidou et al., 2025). Yme1L, which cleaves OPA1 at S2 site, also regulates cristae dynamics. Yme1L KO results mitochondrial fragment, fewer and disorganized cristae, increased CJs per crista, and abnormal curvature.

The MICOS complex also plays a key role in cristae dynamics. Deficiencies in MIC10, MIC19, MIC60, or SAM50 reduce dynamics including shortening, elongation, and cristae-crista fusion (Katahira et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2020) (Fig. 2(C) and 2(E)). Additionally, ATAD3A appears to negatively regulate cristae-IBM and cristae-cristae contact. Further regulatory mechanisms will be discussed in later sections (Hu et al., 2020; Katahira et al., 2025).

Mitochondrial cristae are highly dynamic subcellular structures. Their morphology undergoes adaptive changes in response to various stimuli or physiological conditions—a process known as cristae remodeling. This structural dynamic is regulated by multiple proteins, including the dynamin family GTPase OPA1 (Diokmetzidou et al., 2025), MICOS complex, F1Fo-ATP synthase, and cardiolipin (Brown et al., 2025; Daumke & van der Laan, 2025; Tábara et al., 2024) (Fig. 4(A)). OPA1

OPA1, a dynamin-related GTPase located in the inner mitochondrial membrane, plays a central role in mitochondrial fusion and cristae remodeling during apoptosis (Cipolat et al., 2004; Costa et al., 2010; Frezza et al., 2006; Landes et al., 2010; Yamaguchi et al., 2008). It was initially identified through its link to dominant optic atrophy (DOA), a hereditary visual disorder caused by OPA1 mutations (Alexander et al., 2000). OPA1, along with its yeast homolog Mgm1, belongs to the dynamin family GTPases and has eight isoforms resulting from alternative splicing (Delettre et al., 2001).

The N-terminus of OPA1 contains a mitochondrial targeting signal (MTS) that directs it to mitochondria. Upon import, the mitochondrial processing peptidase (MPP) cleaves off the MTS, enabling OPA1 maturation and localization to the inner mitochondrial membrane. Subsequent proteolytic cleavage at sites S1 and S2 by the proteases OMA1 and Yme1L, respectively, generates both long (L-OPA1) and short (S-OPA1) forms. Inner membrane fusion requires the coordinated action of both L-OPA1 and S-OPA1 (Song et al., 2007). These forms can oligomerize on the membrane and act as a gatekeeper at cristae junctions.

Recent structural studies on Chaetomium thermophilum Mgm1 suggest that it assembles into helical filaments on curved membranes, implying a similar mechanism at cristae junctions in mammals (Tábara et al., 2024). Loss of OPA1 leads to widened cristae junctions, reduced metabolic capacity, and disorganized cristae (Kanazawa et al., 2008).

Yme1L, an inner membrane protease responsible for cleaving OPA1 at S2, influences cristae integrity. Yme1L KO causes mitochondrial fragmentation, reduces cristae number, increases cristae abnormalities and junctions, and results in highly dynamic but disorganized cristae.

Additionally, several other regulators influence cristae morphology via OPA1: CHCHD10 stabilizes OPA1 by competing with P32 for YME1L binding; prohibitins modulate OPA1 processing and turnover (Merkwirth et al., 2008; Merkwirth & Langer, 2009); and ROMO1 silencing disrupts OPA1 oligomerization and promotes its cleavage (Norton et al., 2014).

MICOS complex

MICOS complex in HeLa cells and primary skin fibroblasts form periodic clusters predominantly localized at CJs (Jans et al., 2013). Core subunits MIC60 and MIC10 constitute two distinct subcomplexes bridged by MIC19 in yeast (Friedman et al., 2015).

Within the MIC60-centered subcomplex, MIC60, MIC19, and MIC25 are transmembrane proteins localized at CJs. MIC19 and MIC25 contain CHCH (CC-helix-CC-helix) domains, suggesting roles in membrane folding and structural organization. MIC60 possesses a coiled-coil domain and a conserved C-terminal region essential for CJ integrity; its deletion causes cristae detachment from the inner membrane (Zerbes et al., 2012). MIC60 also influences the oligomeric state of F1Fo-ATP synthase, indicating synergistic regulation of cristae morphology (Rabl et al., 2009). MIC60 knockdown destabilizes MIC19, impairing MICOS complex assembly and cristae structure.

MIC10, a ∼10 kDa CJ protein with two transmembrane domains containing conserved glycine motifs, forms large oligomers (Bohnert et al., 2015). Its overexpression increases CJ number and cristae area. Recombinant MIC10 induces membrane bending and tubulation in vitro, demonstrating its role in cristae formation and elongation (Long et al., 2015).

The MICOS complex interacts with multiple regulators:

OPA1 binds MICOS, linking it to inner membrane curvature control (Daumke & van der Laan, 2025; Hu et al., 2020; Noone et al., 2022).

PERK (PKR-like ER kinase), a key endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress sensor, promotes cristae remodeling by activating the OGT (O-linked N-acetyl-glucosamine transferase)-TOM70 axis to enhance the mitochondrial import of MIC19, thereby coupling ER proteostasis to mitochondrial ultrastructural adaptation (Latorre-Muro et al., 2021).

MIRO1/2 (mitochondrial Rho GTPases), regulators of mitochondrial trafficking along cytoskeletal elements, interact with MIC60, MIC19, and SAM50. Their loss disrupts membrane tethering and cristae integrity, linking organelle motility to inner membrane architecture (Shembekar et al., 2025).

COX17, a copper chaperone for cytochrome c ​oxidase assembly, binds MIC60 in a copper-dependent manner, coordinating cristae morphology with the metabolic demand for respiratory complex biogenesis (Chojnacka et al., 2015).

CARD19, a potential outer membrane protein implicated in apoptosis regulation, interacts with the MIB and MIC60-MIC19-MIC25 subcomplex to modulate cristae form in response to cell death signals (Rios et al., 2022).

F1Fo-ATP synthase

F1Fo-ATP synthase, located in the inner mitochondrial membrane, comprises a hydrophilic F1 portion and a membrane-embedded Fo domain. Beyond its canonical role in ATP production, growing evidence indicates its critical involvement in cristae remodeling.

Dimerization and higher-order oligomerization of F1Fo-ATP synthase are key to inducing curvature in the inner membrane, facilitating the formation of tubular cristae structures (Allen, 1995). The regular arrangement of these oligomers is thought to provide structural stability to cristae.

Subunits e and g, though dispensable for catalytic activity, are essential for dimer and oligomer formation (Arnold et al., 1998). Yeast lacking either subunit exhibits abnormal “onion-like” concentric cristae membranes (Bornhövd et al., 2006; Giraud et al., 2002; Paumard et al., 2002), a phenotype also observed upon subunit downregulation. Similarly, mutations in subunit 4 (β-subunit homolog in mammals) disrupt oligomer stability and lead to comparable cristae abnormalities (Soubannier et al., 2002; Weimann et al., 2008). In vivo cross-linking of the F1 moiety reproduces these structural defects, underscoring the importance of oligomerization in maintaining inner membrane architecture (Gavin et al., 2004).

In dimeric and oligomeric forms, F1Fo-ATP synthase generates membrane curvature necessary for cristae formation and maintenance (Dudkina et al., 2005). Its interaction with cardiolipin further promotes cristae remodeling. Notably, the assembly of the OXPHOS system and the MICOS complex occurs independently, with no observed mutual influence (Shi et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2019).

Cardiolipin

Cardiolipin, a major phospholipid of the inner mitochondrial membrane, constitutes 15%–18% of its total lipid content. Deficient cardiolipin levels markedly reduce the stability of respiratory chain complexes III and IV (Claypool et al., 2008; Pfeiffer et al., 2003). Its asymmetric distribution within cristae promotes membrane bending, increasing surface area and shaping membrane curvature (Ikon & Ryan, 2017). This curvature helps reduce membrane tension and stabilizes cristae structure.

Impaired cardiolipin remodeling—such as in Barth syndrome (BTHS), caused by mutations in Tafazzin (TAZ)—leads to aberrant cristae morphology, including concentric or hyperconnected membranes in cardiac tissue (Sniezek Carney et al., 2025). Cardiolipin also facilitates the assembly and integrity of the MICOS complex and promotes dimerization of F1Fo-ATP synthase. Recent work indicates that high concentrations of OXPHOS proteins drive cardiolipin remodeling, ultimately leading to cristae reorganization (Xu et al., 2021).

Upstream regulatory signaling networks of cristae dynamics

Cristae remodeling is finely tuned by upstream signaling networks that sense environmental and metabolic changes, translating these cues into structural adaptations. Key upstream regulators include:

Nutrient availability: Nutrient-sensing pathways such as mTOR and AMPK influence cristae architecture. AMPK activation during energy stress promotes cristae tightening through phosphorylation of MICOS components, while mTOR signaling stimulates cristae biogenesis to support anabolic processes.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS): Moderate ROS levels function as signaling molecules that induce cristae remodeling through oxidation of MICOS subunits and OPA1, enhancing respiratory efficiency. Conversely, excessive ROS promotes pathological cristae disruption via oxidation of cardiolipin and mPTP opening.

ER stress: The ER stress sensor PERK phosphorylates O-linked N-acetyl-glucosamine transferase (OGT), which subsequently glycosylates TOM70 to enhance MIC19 import and cristae formation, representing a direct ER-mitochondria communication axis.

Calcium signaling: Mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake through mitochondrial calcium uniporter complex (MCUC) regulates cristae morphology by modulating the activity of Ca2+-sensitive dehydrogenases and ATP production, creating a feedback loop between energy status and membrane architecture.

Hypoxic response: HIF-1α stabilization under hypoxia transcriptionally represses MICOS components and promotes cristae simplification to reduce oxidative metabolism and adapt to low oxygen conditions.

These upstream pathways converge on the core cristae remodeling machinery, allowing dynamic structural adaptations to diverse cellular states.

Tissue-specific cristae remodeling, environmental feedback and physiological output

At the tissue level, cristae remodeling and environmental feedback influencing cristae dynamics constitute a sophisticated process that is fundamental to cellular energy regulation and metabolic adaptation (Fig. 4(B)). Cristae, the invaginations of the mitochondrial inner membrane, serve as the primary sites for OXPHOS and are essential for ATP generation (Huang et al., 2023). Alterations in cristae architecture impact mitochondrial efficiency and ROS production, collectively determining cellular and tissue homeostasis (Jenkins et al., 2024). Environmental feedback mechanisms, including nutrient availability, heat alteration, hypoxia, and physical stress, significantly modulate cristae dynamics by altering the biophysical properties of the inner mitochondrial membrane (Golombek et al., 2023; Latorre-Muro et al., 2021). These interactions are mediated through signaling pathways involving processes such as mitophagy, fusion, and fission, which collectively influence cristae morphology and organization (Kondadi & Reichert, 2024).

Tissue-specific factors, such as metabolic demands, impose additional constraints on these dynamics (Glancy et al., 2020). For instance, tissues with high energy requirements often exhibit denser cristae to support elevated ATP production rates. Conversely, tissues with lower metabolic activity may display fewer cristae (Kondadi et al., 2020). The interplay between environmental feedback and cristae remodeling exerts significant effects on tissue-level functions (Fig. 4(B)).

Tissue-specific factors impose additional constraints on these dynamics:

Liver: Cristae remodeling plays key roles in maintaining systemic energy balance (Sohn et al., 2023). Mic19 liver-specific knockout (LKO) reduces ER-mitochondrial contacts, disrupts mitochondrial lipid metabolism, impairs fatty acid β-oxidation, and may spontaneously trigger nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and liver fibrosis in mice (Dong et al., 2024).

Brain: Cristae dynamics are critical for maintaining mitochondrial function and preventing neurodegeneration (Ju et al., 2023). Cristae ultrastructure is required to maintain glutamate homeostasis, and disruption of cristae organization accelerates brain aging (Li et al., 2024).

Adipose tissue: Cold stress or β-adrenergic stimulation activates PERK, which phosphorylates OGT. Phosphorylated OGT glycosylates TOM70 on Ser94, enhancing MIC19 import into mitochondria and promoting cristae formation and thermogenic respiration (Latorre-Muro et al., 2021).

Heart: Nutritional restriction enhances cardiomyocyte resistance to ischemia-reperfusion injury by preserving calcium cycling and mitochondrial function (Huang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2012; Xia et al., 2023). Cristae morphology influences oxidative phosphorylation efficiency, mitochondrial calcium homeostasis, and ATP production, directly affecting cardiomyocyte energy supply (Deng et al., 2023; Yang, 2025).

At the tissue level, cristae remodeling and environmental feedback constitute a sophisticated process fundamental to cellular energy regulation and metabolic adaptation. Cristae architecture impacts mitochondrial efficiency and ROS production, collectively determining cellular and tissue homeostasis. Environmental feedback mechanisms, including nutrient availability, thermal stress, hypoxia, and physical strain, significantly modulate cristae dynamics by altering the biophysical properties of the inner mitochondrial membrane. These interactions are mediated through signaling pathways involving processes such as mitophagy, fusion, and fission, which collectively influence cristae morphology and organization.

The intricate interplay between cristae remodeling and environmental feedback at the tissue level is essential for sustaining cellular energy equilibrium and tissue homeostasis.

Function of mitochondria cristae

Mitochondrial cristae and apoptosis

As dynamic bioenergetic compartments, mitochondrial cristae undergo structural changes in response to physiological conditions. Numerous studies have revealed a link between cristae remodeling and apoptosis: the reshaping of cristae facilitates the complete release of cytochrome c ​and subsequent activation of pro-apoptotic factors. Scorrano et al. showed that the pro-apoptotic protein BID induces cristae junction widening (from 18 nm to 56 nm) independently of BAX–BAK oligomerization. Since cytochrome c is primarily localized within cristae, only ∼15% is released upon mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP). Cristae remodeling allows cytochrome c to diffuse into the intermembrane space, and BAX–BAK oligomerization promotes MOMP, enabling cytochrome c release into the cytosol (Karbowski & Youle, 2003; Scorrano, 2005). These findings underscore the role of ultrastructural dynamics in apoptosis and provide a basis for studying cristae remodeling regulation.

Mitochondrial cristae and the electron transport chain

The folding of the inner membrane into cristae increases surface area, accommodating OXPHOS complexes and cristae-shaping machinery. Electron transport chain (ETC) complexes I–IV, which pump protons into the intermembrane space, are enriched within cristae membranes. Cristae remodeling directly influences electron transport efficiency, energy production, and ROS generation (Cogliati et al., 2013). Tightly packed cristae with narrow junctions promote the assembly of respiratory supercomplexes, enhancing electron channeling and reducing electron leakage, thereby minimizing ROS production. Conversely, widened or disrupted cristae impair supercomplex formation, leading to inefficient electron transfer, decreased ATP synthesis, and increased ROS generation. The curvature of cristae membranes also affects the proton gradient establishment, with optimal curvature maximizing the efficiency of the proton motive force for ATP synthesis. These structural-functional relationships demonstrate how cristae architecture serves as a critical determinant of mitochondrial bioenergetic efficiency and redox balance.

Cristae junctions and mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake

Mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake supports Ca2+-dependent dehydrogenases in the TCA cycle, providing reducing equivalents for the ETC and enhancing ATP production (Wan et al., 1989). Excessive Ca2+ levels induce ultrastructural damage and apoptosis (Strubbe-Rivera et al., 2021). Cytosolic Ca2+ enters the intermembrane space via voltage-dependent anion channels (VDACs), and is imported into the matrix by MCUC (Bathori et al., 2006; Rapizzi et al., 2002). MICU1/2 regulate Ca2+ uptake and maintain mitochondrial function (Perocchi et al., 2010; Plovanich et al., 2013). Recent studies show that OPA1 and MICU1 modulate cristae junctions to influence Ca2+ uptake in POMC neurons (Fulop et al., 2011; Gomez-Valades et al., 2021; Gottschalk et al., 2019). OPA1 loss triggers cristae junction opening, cytochrome c release, mitochondrial fragmentation, swelling, and Ca2+ overload, promoting mPTP opening and apoptosis (Arnoult et al., 2005; Baumgartner et al., 2009; Kushnareva et al., 2013). MICU1 deficiency increases apoptotic sensitivity, disrupts cristae junctions, elevates cytosolic and IMS Ca2+, and enhances ER–mitochondria contacts, further amplifying Ca2+-mediated apoptosis (Madreiter-Sokolowski et al., 2021; Mukherjee et al., 2002).

Mitochondrial cristae and human disease

The intricate architecture of mitochondrial cristae is not merely structural but is fundamentally linked to their function as the primary site of OXPHOS. The assembly and efficacy of respiratory chain supercomplexes (RCS) are critically dependent on cristae integrity, with structural modulation directly impacting respiratory efficiency (Cogliati et al., 2013; Gilkerson et al., 2003). While electron transport and ATP production are known to occur within cristae, elucidating the precise spatial dynamics—such as whether these processes are localized to individual cristae or are mitochondrion-wide—requires advanced live-cell imaging technologies capable of mapping pH gradients and complex distributions.

Defects in cristae morphology are increasingly implicated in a wide spectrum of human diseases, including neurodegenerative disorders, metabolic syndromes, and cardiovascular conditions, underscoring their vital role in cellular health (Colina-Tenorio et al., 2020). The biogenesis and maintenance of cristae rely on a core set of evolutionarily conserved factors: the MICOS complex, F1Fo-ATP synthase oligomers, the dynamin-related GTPase OPA1, and the phospholipids cardiolipin and phosphatidylethanolamine (Joubert & Puff, 2021). While mitochondrial dysfunction is often a secondary effect in pathology, a growing number of studies identify primary mutations in cristae-shaping proteins across diverse diseases, suggesting cristae abnormalities can be a primary pathogenic driver (Table 1). Neurological and metabolic disorders from MICOS mutations

MIC13 (QIL1) deficiency: Mutations in the QIL1 gene encoding the MICOS subunit MIC13 cause severe early-onset mitochondrial encephalopathy, characterized by liver disease, cerebellar atrophy, and neurological decline. MIC13 bridges the MIC60 and MIC10 subcomplexes, and its loss disrupts MICOS integrity (Zeharia et al., 2016), leading to cristae destabilization, impaired respiration, and broad metabolic dysfunction. A specific homozygous splice-site mutation (c.260-2A > G) results in aberrant splicing, cristae disorganization, loss of membrane contacts, and defective OXPHOS (Guarani et al., 2016).

MIC19 in type 2 diabetes: Proteomic studies reveal reduced levels of MIC19 in the skeletal muscle of diabetic patients, correlating with mitochondrial dysfunction. This suggests that Mic19 deficiency may contribute to insulin resistance via cristae destabilization, positioning it as a potential contributor to disease pathogenesis and a novel therapeutic target (Hwang et al., 2010).

Neurodegenerative diseases

Parkinson's disease (PD): Cristae integrity is intimately linked to PD pathogenesis. Rare variants in IMMT (encoding MIC60) are associated with familial and sporadic PD. The PINK1/Parkin mitophagy pathway directly regulates cristae stability; PINK1 phosphorylates and stabilizes Mic60, and loss of this interaction disrupts cristae structure (Tsai et al., 2018). Notably, Mic60 overexpression is protective in PD models, mitigating neuronal death and mitochondrial defects caused by dopamine or rotenone.

CHCHD10-related Disorders: Mutations in CHCHD10, a cristae junction protein interacting with the MIC60 subcomplex, are linked to motor neuron disease, cerebellar ataxia, and spinal neuropathy (Bannwarth et al., 2014; Genin et al., 2016). Patient fibroblasts show disassembly of the MIC60-MIC19-MIC25 complex, cristae loss, and respiratory defects (Bannwarth et al., 2014; Genin et al., 2016, 2018). Interestingly, complete loss of CHCHD10 does not recapitulate these defects, suggesting mutations may have a toxic gain-of-function rather than simply loss of function (Burstein et al., 2018; Meng et al., 2017; Zhou et al., 2019).

Leigh syndrome and lipid metabolism

SLC25A46 Mutation: This outer membrane protein, which interacts with MFN2, OPA1, and MICOS, is essential for lipid homeostasis and cristae stability (Bagli et al., 2017). A homozygous missense mutation (c.425C > T) causes Leigh syndrome, with patient fibroblasts showing mitochondrial hyperfusion and ER morphology defects. Overexpression of SLC25A46 restores MICOS stability and phospholipid trafficking, highlighting its role in cristae maintenance (Janer et al., 2016).

Barth syndrome (BTHS) and cardiolipin remodeling

BTHS is an X-linked disorder caused by mutations in TAZ, encoding the cardiolipin-remodeling enzyme Tafazzin. Characterized by cardiomyopathy and skeletal myopathy, BTHS mitochondria exhibit aberrant concentric cristae and severe dysfunction due to deficient cardiolipin levels. This phospholipid is crucial for inducing membrane curvature and stabilizing MICOS subcomplexes (e.g., facilitating MIC10 oligomerization) (Koob et al., 2015; Weber et al., 2013). Although MICOS complex quantity may increase in BTHS, its assembly and function are impaired, underscoring cardiolipin's essential role in cristae morphology.

In summary, while the question of whether cristae defects are a primary cause or a consequential feature of mitochondrial disease remains, the evidence strongly implicates disturbed cristae morphology as a central mechanism in pathology. Targeting the molecular machineries that shape cristae—the MICOS complex, lipid modifiers, and associated regulators—offers a promising strategic avenue for developing therapies for this array of debilitating diseases.

Perspectives

The dynamic remodeling of mitochondrial cristae represents a fundamental regulatory mechanism underpinning cellular energy metabolism, signaling, and survival. This review has synthesized current knowledge illustrating that cristae are not static architectural features but are highly adaptable compartments whose morphology is precisely tuned by a complex interplay of protein complexes and lipids. The MICOS complex acts as a central architect, establishing and maintaining cristae junctions, while F1Fo-ATP synthase dimerization drives the curvature of cristae tips. Simultaneously, OPA1 dynamics and the unique lipid environment provided by cardiolipin are indispensable for membrane fusion, stability, and the continuous restructuring of these inner membrane invaginations. The concerted action of these components ensures that cristae morphology is optimized to meet cellular energetic demands, respond to stress signals, and regulate apoptotic pathways through mechanisms such as controlling cytochrome c ​release and calcium homeostasis.

Therapeutic targeting of cristae remodeling machinery has shown promising results in preclinical models. In PD models, Mic60 overexpression ameliorates neuronal death and mitochondrial defects induced by neurotoxins, preserving cristae integrity and respiratory function (Tsai et al., 2018; Van Laar et al., 2016). Similarly, OPA1 supplementation in cellular models of dominant optic atrophy restores cristae morphology and prevents retinal ganglion cell degeneration. For BTHS, gene therapy approaches aimed at restoring Tafazzin expression improve cardiolipin remodeling and rescue the characteristic concentric cristae pathology in patient-derived cardiomyocytes. Small molecules that stabilize F1Fo-ATP synthase dimers have demonstrated efficacy in enhancing cristae curvature and OXPHOS capacity in age-related mitochondrial dysfunction. These examples underscore the translational potential of targeting cristae remodeling pathways.

Looking forward, the field will be revolutionized by the application of advanced imaging technologies, such as super-resolution live-cell microscopy and volume electron microscopy, which are beginning to allow the direct observation of cristae dynamics in real-time. Future research must focus on elucidating the intricate regulatory networks and signaling pathways that integrate environmental cues, such as nutrient availability and cellular stress, to direct cristae remodeling. A deeper understanding of the tissue-specific variations in these processes will be crucial. Ultimately, deciphering the molecular language of cristae dynamics will not only resolve fundamental questions in cell biology but also pave the way for innovative strategies to treat the growing number of diseases linked to mitochondrial dysfunction.
