Insights into the Cellular and Molecular Mechanisms behind the Antifibrotic Effects of Nerandomilast
Authors: Dennis Reininger, Felix Wolf, Christoph H. Mayr, Susanne L. Wespel, Nadine Laufhaeger, Kerstin Geillinger-Kästle, Alec Dick, Florian Gantner, Peter Nickolaus, Franziska E. Herrmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The quest for innovative pharmacologic interventions in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a challenging journey. The complexity of the disease demands a comprehensive approach that targets multiple cell types and pathways. This study examined the antifibrotic properties of nerandomilast, a preferential phosphodiesterase 4B inhibitor, focusing on its effects on myofibroblasts (MFs) and endothelial cells. Using cytokine-stimulated human IPF lung fibroblasts and RNA sequencing, we assessed the effects of nerandomilast on MF contractility, MF markers, and differentiation mechanisms. In addition, using human microvascular endothelial cells, we assessed endothelial barrier integrity and monocyte adhesion in a three-dimensional microfluidic chip. Our results show that nerandomilast significantly inhibited MF contractility and marker expression in cytokine-stimulated human IPF lung fibroblast cells. Treatment with nerandomilast significantly activated cAMP-associated pathways and G-protein–coupled receptor signaling events while inhibiting mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathways and transforming growth factor β signaling. Nerandomilast also significantly reduced microvascular permeability in cytokine-stimulated human lung microvascular endothelial cells. Finally, in an adeno-associated virus–human diphtheria toxin receptor/diphtheria toxin mouse model of acute lung injury, nerandomilast significantly inhibited total protein in lavage, total macrophages, neutrophils, cell count, and VCAM-1 expression. In summary, our results demonstrate that nerandomilast induces the dedifferentiation of human IPF lung MFs and diminishes their contractility in vitro by interfering with transforming growth factor β, mitogen-activated protein kinase phosphatase-1, and G-protein–coupled receptor signaling pathways. It also mitigates vascular dysfunction by strengthening endothelial junctions and inhibiting adhesion protein expression. These findings highlight nerandomilast’s potential therapeutic use in IPF by providing insights into its cellular and molecular actions.

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a severe and progressive interstitial lung disease characterized by a range of symptoms, including respiratory impairment leading to respiratory failure and mortality within 2–5 years from diagnosis (1, 2). Despite its devastating impact, the precise underlying causes of IPF remain largely unknown (3).

The current understanding of IPF pathogenesis involves initial repetitive alveolar epithelial microinjuries, followed by dysregulated repair mechanisms, resulting in excessive synthesis of extracellular matrix components, driving tissue remodeling, fibrosis, and scarring, ultimately leading to the progressive decline in lung function (4, 5). Although the central role of myofibroblasts (MFs) in IPF pathogenesis has been widely acknowledged, recent studies have also drawn attention to the importance of the underlying microvasculature (6). Increasing evidence suggests that features of endothelial dysfunction and microvascular remodeling, such as increased vascular permeability, coagulation and immune cell infiltration, and loss of endothelial cell type specialization, may contribute to the initiation and progression of IPF (7).

Phosphodiesterase 4 (PDE4) inhibition has emerged as a potential strategy for pharmacologic intervention in fibrotic diseases through elevation of cAMP levels (8). Nerandomilast (BI 1015550) is a novel selective PDE4 inhibitor with a ninefold preference for the PDE4B versus the PDE4D isoenzyme (9). This unique preference offers a promising opportunity for limiting the adverse events associated with pan-PDE4 inhibition without compromising efficacy. Preclinical investigations have shown that nerandomilast exhibits potent antiinflammatory and antifibrotic effects, both in vitro and in vivo (9). In a Phase-I clinical trial in healthy volunteers and patients with IPF, the adverse events associated with nerandomilast were mild or moderate, and mostly gastrointestinal (10). In a Phase-II clinical trial involving patients with IPF and healthy volunteers, nerandomilast stabilized lung function over a 12-week treatment period, with favorable tolerability (11).

Recent findings from a rat model of bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis have demonstrated the antifibrotic properties of nerandomilast (12). The antifibrotic effect is believed to be mediated through modulation of MF function, as well as affecting epithelial and endothelial dysfunction (13). However, despite these initial findings, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the potential therapeutic efficacy of nerandomilast remain to be fully elucidated.

In this study, we aimed to provide valuable insights into the cellular and molecular modes of action of nerandomilast in the context of IPF, focusing on human MFs and human microvascular endothelial cells (HMVECs).

In Vivo Studies

AAV-DTR/DT Mouse Model of Acute Lung Injury.

An adeno-associated virus–human diphtheria toxin receptor/diphtheria toxin (AAV-DTR/DT) mouse model of acute lung injury was performed as previously described (14). In brief, male 8- to 10-week-old C57BL/6JRj mice (Janvier Labs) were housed in individually ventilated cages at 22–25°C, at a humidity of 45–65%, and at a 12-hour:12-hour day:night cycle with free access to water and food. At Day 0, AAV6.2-stuffer or AAV6.2-hDTR was instilled intratracheally under isoflurane anesthesia (catalog no. 1214, Isofluran CP). After 28 days of AAV6.2 instillation, nerandomilast (12.5 mg/kg twice daily, a well-established dose as previously reported) (9) was instilled intratracheally. 1 hour later, 150 ng DT (catalog no. D0564, Sigma-Aldrich) was instilled intratracheally in all experimental groups. Animals were killed 24 hours after DT instillation. Plasma, BAL, and lung tissue were obtained and prepared according to needs for further analysis. Total cell influx as well as cell types were measured in BAL using the Sysmex XT-1800i device. Total protein concentration and VCAM-1 protein levels of BAL fluid were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (catalog no. 23225; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the Mouse VCAM-1 Assay Kit (catalog no. K1527PR-2; Meso Scale Discovery), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Plasma surfactant protein D (SP-D) levels were determined using the Mouse SP-D Quantikine ELISA (catalog no. MSFPD0; R&D Systems) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Study Approval.

Ethical approval was obtained from the regional board for animal care and welfare (Regierungspräsidium; Tübingen, Germany; TVV-18–23-G). Animal studies are reported in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines (15).

Data Analysis and Statistics

All data are presented as mean ± SEM of n donors/animals. For the in vitro assays, we used replicates to ensure reliability. We determined statistical differences between positive and negative controls, using an unpaired t test for parametric data. We analyzed statistical differences for the test groups by using a one-way ANOVA with subsequent Dunnett’s multiple comparison test for parametric data. Statistical significance was accepted at P < 0.05. The tests were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). To control for donor variance, in vitro data were normalized by calculation of percentage inhibition as follows:%inhibition=100–(Y/K1)⋅100,where Y indicates mean rate of nonstimulated, non–compound-treated control wells, subtracted from the mean rate of stimulated, compound-treated wells, and K1 indicates mean rate of nonstimulated, non–compound-treated control wells.

We calculated half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values by nonlinear regression of log (inhibitor concentration) versus percentage inhibition using a four-parametric fitting procedure of the GraphPad Prism software package. (For details for the remaining methods, see the Supplemental Methods in the data supplement.)

Results

Nerandomilast Dedifferentiates MFs by Interference with Several Disease-associated Pathways

Because treatment with nerandomilast elevated global cAMP levels in MFs (see Figures E1A and E1B in the data supplement), which has been described to play an essential role in MF dedifferentiation (16), we assessed contractility as a functional feature and molecular marker of MF properties. Stimulation of human IPF lung fibroblasts (IPF-HLFs) with transforming growth factor β1 (TGFβ1) for 120 hours significantly decreased collagen gel disc size by 0.68-fold (32%), implying increased MF contractility (Figure 1A and see Figure E1C). Addition of nerandomilast for the last 72 hours significantly decreased MF contractility in a concentration-dependent manner up to 38%. For the molecular markers, IPF-HLF were prestimulated with TGFβ1 for 48 hours, followed by nerandomilast for an additional 72 hours in the presence of TGFβ1 (Figure 1B). Nerandomilast significantly inhibited α smooth muscle actin, connective tissue growth factor, and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 protein levels in a concentration-dependent manner up to 36%, 40%, and 41%, with IC50 values of 0.64, 0.02, and 0.03 μmol/L, respectively.

To examine the molecular mechanisms behind MF dedifferentiation, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). IPF-HLFs were prestimulated with TGFβ1 for 48 hours to induce MF differentiation, with nerandomilast or high-concentration prostaglandin E2 (PGE2; commonly used to stimulate cAMP in vitro) added in the presence of TGFβ1 for an additional 24 hours.

To examine the effect of nerandomilast on the TGFβ1-derived MF phenotype, we investigated the changes in mean marker expressions in our RNA-seq data using cell-type–specific marker genes identified from publicly available human single-cell RNA-seq data (see Figures E2A and E2B). The most significantly expressed genes following TGFβ1 stimulation mapped best to human MFs (Figure 1C). On stimulation with TGFβ1, MF marker expression significantly increased, and treatment with nerandomilast or high-concentration PGE2 significantly inhibited MF marker expression up to 46% and 61%, respectively (Figure 1D).

Ingenuity pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes (see Figures E3A and E3B) revealed that stimulation of IPF-HLF with TGFβ1 led to the deregulation of 360 predicted canonical pathways and ∼2,000 predicted upstream regulators, which were filtered for their relevance in the context of IPF pathobiology (see Figures E4A and E4B). Treatment with nerandomilast and high-concentration PGE2 significantly counterregulated the top-scoring canonical pathways and upstream regulators. As expected, treatment with nerandomilast and high-concentration PGE2 significantly activated cAMP-associated pathways and upstream regulators—as well as G-protein–coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling, including G α (i) and (s) signaling events—while inhibiting TGFβ1 signaling, including the canonical effectors mothers against decapentaplegic homologs (SMAD)-2/3/4, implying interference with major molecular pathways that were involved in MF differentiation. In addition, high-concentration PGE2 significantly activated calcium signaling and G α (z) and (q) signaling events.

Given the importance of TGFβ1 signaling in IPF, we examined the regulation of TGFβ1-associated genes in more detail (see Figure E4C). Treatment with nerandomilast significantly counterregulated 6% and high-concentration PGE2 counterregulated 36% of all genes deregulated by stimulation with TGFβ1. Among the individual genes analyzed, nerandomilast showed an effect (99%) similar to that of high-concentration PGE2 in counterregulating genes, but with a less potent and significant effect.

Interference with SMAD3 Promoter Binding Affects TGFβ Signaling

To elucidate the molecular impact of nerandomilast on canonical TGFβ signaling, we treated IPF-HLFs with nerandomilast or high-concentration PGE2 for 30 minutes and then stimulated them with TGFβ1 for 1 hour. Phosphorylation of cAMP-response element binding protein (CREB) significantly increased, whereas phosphorylation and translocation of SMAD3 into the nucleus was not affected by nerandomilast or high-concentration PGE2 (Figures 2A and 2B). Previous studies have suggested competition of phosphorylated CREB and SMAD3 for binding to CREB-binding protein (CBP)/p300, important coactivators modulating gene transcription, in the nucleus (17). Protein coimmunoprecipitation for CBP and subsequent protein analysis of binding partners revealed no significant effect of stimulation with TGFβ1 on phospho-CREB binding to CBP, whereas treatment with nerandomilast and high-concentration PGE2 led to a nonsignificant increase in phospho-CREB binding. On the other hand, stimulation with TGFβ1 led to a significant increase in phospho-SMAD3 binding to CBP, which was not affected by treatment with nerandomilast or high-concentration PGE2 (Figure 2C). Similar results were obtained for protein coimmunoprecipitation of p300 (see Figure E5). Possible interference with SMAD3 promoter binding and subsequent initiation of gene transcription was further investigated using the Cleavage Under Targets & Release Using Nuclease (or, CUT&RUN) assay. We analyzed promoter regions of SERPINE1 and CDKN1A as representative SMAD3 target genes involved in IPF pathogenesis. Stimulation with TGFβ1 led to a significant increase in SERPINE1 promoter binding, which was not affected by treatment with nerandomilast or high-concentration PGE2 (Figure 2D). On the other hand, stimulation with TGFβ1 led to a numeric increase in CDKN1A promoter binding, which was significantly inhibited by the treatment with nerandomilast and high-concentration PGE2.

Stabilized MKP1 Protein Inhibits p38 MAPK for Interference with TGFβ Signaling

As a further molecular mode of action, mitogen-activated protein kinase phosphatase-1 (MKP1; a.k.a. DUSP) is known to dephosphorylate all three mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs)—p38, JNK, and ERK—that play major roles in the regulation of MF differentiation and proliferation on activation by profibrotic stimuli such as TGFβ1 (18, 19). To investigate the effect of nerandomilast on MKP1 expression in MFs, we pretreated IPF-HLFs with nerandomilast or vehicle for 30 minutes and subsequently stimulated them with TGFβ1 for 1 hour. Although stimulation with TGFβ1 did not significantly affect MKP1 protein expression, pretreatment with nerandomilast significantly increased MKP1 protein expression in a concentration-dependent manner up to 66% (Figure 3A). Consequently, we analyzed the activation status of all three MAPKs possibly influenced by elevated MKP1 protein expression. On stimulation with TGFβ1, phosphorylation of p38 significantly increased by 2.2-fold, which was significantly inhibited by nerandomilast concentration dependently up to 84% (Figure 3A). Figure 3B summarizes the potential intervention points of nerandomilast treatment on canonical TGFβ and MAPK signaling cascades that were investigated.

Inhibition of Ca2+ Influx Interferes with GPCR Signaling to Inhibit Fibroblast Proliferation

In addition to TGFβ1, GPCR signaling emerged as a molecular pathway significantly influenced by nerandomilast in our MF RNA-seq data. Thus, we investigated the effect of treatment with nerandomilast on Gq signaling by assessing lysophosphatidic acid (LPA)-induced IPF-HLF proliferation, which is known to be mediated through Gq and G12/13 signaling (20). IPF-HLFs were pretreated with nerandomilast for 30 minutes and subsequently stimulated with LPA for another 28 hours, followed by bromodeoxyuridine for 20 hours. After LPA stimulation, fibroblast proliferation significantly increased by 2.8-fold, which was significantly inhibited by pretreatment with nerandomilast in a concentration-dependent manner up to 85%, with an IC50 value of 0.105 μmol/L (Figure 4A). To further examine this effect on a molecular basis, LPA-mediated Ca2+ influx was measured immediately after LPA stimulation over a period of 10 minutes. Stimulation with LPA significantly increased global intracellular Ca2+ influx in IPF-HLF by 19.7-fold, which was significantly inhibited by pretreatment with nerandomilast in a concentration-dependent manner up to 40%, with an IC50 value of 0.53 μmol/L (Figure 4B).

Nerandomilast Inhibits Vascular Leakage by Stabilizing Endothelial Barrier Integrity

To investigate the functional effects of treatment with nerandomilast on HMVECs, we assessed endothelial tube formation and barrier integrity by the leakage of FITC dextran from the perfusion to the extracellular matrix gel channel of a three-dimensional microfluidic chip under flow (Figure 5A). On stimulation with an IPF-relevant cytokine cocktail (IPFrc) (21), mimicking the cytokine environment in the fibrotic lung, microvascular permeability significantly increased by 2.5-fold compared with unstimulated controls, which was significantly inhibited by treatment with nerandomilast in a concentration-dependent manner up to 41%.

The Simple Western protein analysis revealed that IPFrc stimulation significantly decreased the protein expression of CD31 (platelet endothelial adhesion molecule 1) by 37% compared with unstimulated controls (Figure 5B). Simultaneous treatment with nerandomilast significantly normalized CD31 protein expression in a concentration-dependent manner.

Because elevated cAMP levels are known to act through protein kinase A and exchange protein directly activated by cAMP (or, Epac)-1 to stabilize endothelial barrier integrity, the activity of the downstream signaling molecules vasodilator-stimulated phosphoprotein (VASP) and myosin regulatory light chain 2 (MLC2) was investigated (22). HMVECs were pretreated with nerandomilast for 30 minutes and subsequently stimulated for 1 hour with IPFrc. Simple Western analysis showed that stimulation with IPFrc in the absence of nerandomilast did not affect VASP or MLC2 phosphorylation, compared with unstimulated controls (Figures 5C and 5D). However, pretreatment with nerandomilast significantly increased VASP phosphorylation in a concentration-dependent manner up to 3.4-fold and significantly decreased MLC2 phosphorylation up to 37%.

Nerandomilast Inhibits Immune Cell Infiltration to Injured Endothelial Cells

To further investigate the effect of nerandomilast on vascular dysfunction, immune cell infiltration was assessed by the adhesion of calcein-labeled monocytes (Figure 6A). On stimulation of HMVECs with IPFrc for 24 hours, monocyte adhesion significantly increased by 4.2-fold compared with unstimulated controls. Simultaneous treatment with nerandomilast significantly inhibited monocyte adhesion in a concentration-dependent manner up to 58%.

Because sustained immune cell infiltration is described to be mediated by adhesion molecules, the expression of vascular cell adhesion molecule (VCAM)-1 and E-selectin were investigated (23). Soluble VCAM-1 and E-selectin levels in the supernatants of perfused microvascular tubes significantly increased by 7.5- and 155.5-fold, respectively, on stimulation with IPFrc for 24 hours (Figures 6B and 6C). Both soluble VCAM-1 and E-selectin were significantly inhibited in a concentration-dependent manner by simultaneous treatment with nerandomilast, up to 42% and 73%, respectively.

Nerandomilast Normalizes Features of Vascular Dysfunction in an AAV-DTR/DT Mouse Model

To confirm our findings of improved vascular function after treatment with nerandomilast, we used an AAV-DTR/DT mouse model to assess alveolar–capillary permeability and immune cell infiltration (Figure 7A). On administration of DT, total protein concentration in lavage (used as a marker for alveolar–capillary permeability) and SP-D levels in plasma (used as a marker for epithelial cell damage) significantly increased by 3.1- and 4.3-fold, respectively, compared with controls (Figures 7B and 7C). Treatment with nerandomilast significantly inhibited total protein in lavage by 45% and numerically inhibited SP-D protein in plasma by 38%. Administration of DT significantly increased total macrophages, total neutrophils, and total cell count in lavage by 4.9-, 3-, and 5.5-fold, respectively, compared with controls (Figures 7D–7F). Treatment with nerandomilast significantly inhibited total macrophages and neutrophils by 34% and 39%, respectively, and numerically reduced total cell count by 27%. Furthermore, administration of DT significantly increased VCAM-1 protein levels in lavage by 27.2-fold (Figure 7G). Treatment with nerandomilast significantly inhibited VCAM-1 up to 51%.

Discussion

In IPF, research has primarily focused on targeting fibrotic mediators, single molecules, or cell types. However, current translational research aims for more effective substances targeting multiple cell types and signaling pathways, or combining treatments around specific cell types or pathways.

In the present study, we elucidated the antifibrotic properties of nerandomilast, a preferential PDE4B inhibitor with proven efficacy in a Phase-II clinical trial and investigated the underlying molecular mechanisms, focusing on MFs and endothelial cells (11).

Our findings demonstrate that nerandomilast significantly elevates global intracellular cAMP levels in IPF-HLFs and TGFβ-induced MFs, leading to MF dedifferentiation, which is reflected in the reduction of contractility and MF-associated protein expression. Further investigation is needed to understand how nerandomilast, or altered cAMP levels in general, affect the contractility of MFs on a molecular level. However, the impact of cAMP is well documented in airway smooth muscle cells, where elevated levels also result in decreased contractility (24).

On a molecular basis, RNA-seq indicated that nerandomilast induces MF dedifferentiation by interference with various disease-associated pathways and upstream regulators, such as TGFβ1 and GPCR signaling. It is important to note that the degree of MF dedifferentiation on treatment correlated with the concentration of cAMP levels, which was higher when exposed to high-concentration PGE2. Our data are in line with previously published results for the high-concentration PGE2-mediated dedifferentiation of CCL210- and normal human lung fibroblast-derived MFs, which was also shown to be an essential mechanism for spontaneous fibrosis resolution after bleomycin-induced lung injury in mice (16, 18, 25).

As shown in previous studies (26), cAMP increase (in this case, induced by nerandomilast and high-concentration PGE2) did not interfere with TGFβ-induced phosphorylation or the nuclear translocation of SMAD3. A widely discussed hypothesis suggests that elevated cAMP levels interfere with canonical TGFβ signaling by binding of phosphorylated CREB to CBP/p300, resulting in reduced availability of CBP/p300 for phosphorylated SMAD3 to initiate profibrotic target gene expression (17, 27). However, our coimmunoprecipitation results for CBP/p300 protein could not show any competitive binding for CBP/p300. Furthermore, the activation of SERPINE1 and CDKN1A gene expression after TGFβ signaling and SMAD3 promoter binding has been described in relation to other diseases (28–30). Our observations revealed an inhibition of CDKN1A promoter binding while SERPINE1 remained unaffected, suggesting that the regulation of SMAD3 binding to its promoters is complex and dependent on other co-transcription factors or phosphorylation isoforms of SMAD3 (31, 32). Whether our results regarding a possible interference with SMAD3 promoter binding are relevant to functional effects on MF dedifferentiation, as observed after treatment with nerandomilast, requires further investigation with more holistic approaches, such as chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing.

In contrast to published data for standard-of-care medications (18), treatment with nerandomilast resulted in significant interference with MAPK signaling pathway by stabilization of MKP1 protein expression, leading to the inhibition of p38 MAPK phosphorylation. Previous studies have reported that elevated cAMP levels cause dephosphorylation of MAPKs, playing an important role in inhibiting MF differentiation and promoting MF dedifferentiation, which is in line with our findings (18, 33).

In addition, our study revealed that nerandomilast significantly influenced GPCR signaling by attenuating LPA-induced fibroblast proliferation, a key process in the pathogenesis of IPF (34). Previous research has demonstrated that signaling through Gi, Gq, and G12/13 G-proteins acts as profibrotic regulators of fibroblast activation, whereas Gs G-proteins act as antifibrotic regulators through cAMP signaling (35, 36). Gs-coupled GPCRs have been found to be suppressed in fibrotic conditions, and strategies that activate these GPCRs have been investigated for their antifibrotic potential (34, 37, 38). Further investigation is required to determine the precise molecular mechanisms by which nerandomilast interferes with Gq and G12/13 signaling to suppress LPA-mediated proliferation and Ca2+ influx.

Besides MF dedifferentiation, vascular dysfunction is an emerging field in the pathogenesis of IPF (6, 39). Our in vitro data demonstrate that treatment with nerandomilast significantly improved the barrier integrity of injured lung endothelium by the normalization of intercellular junctions and inhibited immune cell infiltration by downregulation of adhesion molecules. Protective effects of altered cAMP levels on endothelial barrier integrity are well established for inflammatory pulmonary diseases (40). On a molecular level, stimulation with IPFrc did not alter VASP or MLC2 phosphorylation, although this is the proposed mechanism by which nerandomilast counteracts IPFrc to improve barrier integrity. Either IPFrc induces vascular leakage independently of VASP and MLC2, or 1-hour stimulation was not sufficient to induce changes as observed after 24-hour stimulation. Furthermore, elevated cAMP levels are known to decrease proinflammatory mediators and adhesion molecules, with subsequent inhibition of leukocyte recruitment (41), highlighting the potential antiinflammatory properties of nerandomilast in addition to already described effects on peripheral blood mononuclear cells (9).

To validate our in vitro findings, we used an AAV-DTR/DT mouse model of acute lung injury, which confirmed the beneficial effects of nerandomilast on alveolar–capillary barrier integrity and immune cell infiltration (14). Although the injury in this model is specific to alveolar epithelial cells, it causes substantial damage to the neighboring vasculature, as indicated by the readouts reflecting multiple aspects of alveolar–capillary barrier damage. From a clinical perspective, several studies have emphasized the importance of alveolar–capillary permeability as a significant predictor of IPF-associated outcomes and mortality (42, 43), and the analysis of blood biomarkers in a Phase-II study of nerandomilast demonstrated a notable suppression of E-selectin after treatment (44), strengthening the translational relevance of our results to clinical settings.

In conclusion, our data confirm that nerandomilast effectively dedifferentiates human lung MFs and reduces their contractility in vitro by interference with TGFβ and GPCR signaling pathways. Features of vascular dysfunction such as increased vascular permeability and immune cell attachment are effectively reduced on treatment with nerandomilast in vitro and in vivo by enhancing endothelial junctions and inhibiting expression of adhesion molecules. Overall, our findings demonstrate that, in combination with the already published antiinflammatory potential and beneficial effects on epithelial cell injury (12), nerandomilast offers a comprehensive approach to the treatment of IPF by targeting multiple cell types and pathways.
