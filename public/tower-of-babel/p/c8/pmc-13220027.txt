Joint pain and hidradenitis suppurativa: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Kenan Kherallah, Claire S Chung, Chelsea Moon, Raveena Ghanshani, Vivian Y Shi, Katrina H Lee, Jennifer L Hsiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Joint pain was reported by 37% of patients with hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) at our single-centre HS clinic. However, only one-quarter of those reporting joint pain had a diagnosis of arthritis. Presence of joint pain did not differ based on HS disease severity. Patients who reported joint pain had worse Dermatology Life Quality Index scores.

Dear Editor, Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory skin disease marked by painful nodules, abscesses and draining tunnels that often lead to scarring. Joint pain is frequently reported in HS, yet its prevalence and clinical characteristics are understudied.1

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with HS seen at the University of Southern California Keck HS clinic from 1 January to 30 June 2024. Patients were stratified by the presence or absence of self-­reported joint pain recorded in clinician notes. Extracted variables included age, sex, race, ethnicity, disease duration, smoking status, body mass index (BMI), Hurley stage, Hidradenitis Suppurativa Physician’s Global Assessment score (HS-PGA), Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) and use of systemic treatment. Statistical comparisons were made using Fisher’s exact test or the χ2 test of independence for categorical variables, and unpaired t-tests for continuous variables, with significance set at P < 0.05. Fisher’s exact test was applied to all 2 × 2 categorical comparisons, while χ2 tests were used for multicategory variables.

In total, 161 patients with HS met the aforementioned criteria and were included in the analysis. Of the 159 patients with data, 20 (12.6%) had Hurley stage IHS, 87 (54.7%) had Hurley stage II and 52 (32.7%) had Hurley stage III disease. The mean age was 37.1 years (range 14–77) and the mean disease duration was 14.4 years (range 0–57). Most were female (n = 126/161; 78.3%). The racial distribution was 36.7% White (n = 58/158), 20.9% Black (n = 33/158), 7.6% Asian (n = 12/158) and 34.8% mixed or other (n = 55/158); 29.9% (n = 46/154) identified as Hispanic.

Joint pain was reported in 37.3% of patients (n = 60/161). Data on affected sites were available for 54 patients. Sites affected included the knees (n = 28; 52%), hips (n = 11; 20%), hands/fingers (n = 10; 19%), back (n = 10; 19%), shoulders (n = 8; 15%), elbows (n = 6; 11%), feet/toes (n = 6; 11%), ankles (n = 5; 9%), wrists (n = 5; 9%), generalized/whole body (n = 2; 4%) and neck (n = 1; 2%). Only 27% with joint pain (n = 16/60) had previously received a diagnosis of arthritis. Among patients with joint pain who had an established diagnosis of arthritis, diagnoses included rheumatoid arthritis (n = 4/16; 25%), osteoarthritis (n = 3/16; 19%) and unspecified arthritis (n = 9/16; 56%). Compared with those without joint pain, affected patients were older (mean age 42.1 vs. 34.1 years, P < 0.001), had longer disease duration (19.1 vs. 11.5 years, P < 0.001) and had higher DLQI scores (13.2 vs. 8.9, P < 0.01). No significant differences were observed in sex, race, ethnicity, smoking, BMI, Hurley stage, HS-PGA score or current biologic use. Detailed results are available in Table 1.

Despite reported joint symptoms in 37% of patients in this cohort, only one-quarter of these patients had a formal diagnosis of arthritis. The presence of joint pain was not limited to cases of severe HS, as no differences in Hurley stage or HS-PGA scores were observed. These findings highlight the need to incorporate musculoskeletal symptom screening into routine HS care through targeted questions about joint pain, stiffness, swelling or morning symptoms suggestive of inflammation, with referral to rheumato­logy when appropriate.

Although our study was limited in characterizing the proportion of patients specifically with inflammatory arthritis, the chronic systemic inflammation underlying HS is mediated by several cytokines, including tumour necrosis factor-α, interleukin-1 and interleukin-17, which are also known to be central to inflammatory arthritis.2–4 One study found that HS is linked to increased risk of inflammatory arthritis [odds ratio (OR) 3.44], including spondyloarthritis (OR 2.10) and ankylosing spondylitis (OR 1.89), highlighting the need for evaluation in symptomatic patients.5

Interestingly, DLQI scores were worse among patients with joint pain, which is consistent with a previous study demonstrating that quality-of-life impairment in HS is linked to concomitant back pain, as well as higher BMI and waist circumference.6 These comorbidities may contribute to global disability and perceived symptom burden independently of objective disease severity. Screening for comorbidities, as recommended by Garg et al., remains an important component of comprehensive HS management.7 Further studies are warranted to evaluate how quality-of-life scores in patients with HS may correlate with joint pain severity.

Additionally, the positive associations between joint symptoms and both age and HS duration may, in part, reflect cumulative mechanical joint stress over time, underscoring the importance of further investigations to delineate the relative contributions of inflammatory and noninflammatory mechanisms in patients’ joint symptoms.

Limitations of this study include its single-centre design and retrospective nature, and the lack of detailed clinical information on the nature of the joint pain. Specific rheumatological testing, including screening and confirmatory laboratory and imaging studies, were also not routinely performed, limiting differentiation between inflammatory and noninflammatory arthritis. Lastly, the temporal relationship between HS flares and joint pain was not assessed.

In conclusion, joint pain was a common symptom in our cohort of patients with HS and was associated with older age, longer disease duration and poorer quality of life. Screening for joint symptoms should be integrated into routine HS care with follow-up questions to guide rheumatology referral and comanagement. Future studies in clinical practice should evaluate the utility of standardized musculoskeletal screening tools, such as the Early Inflammatory Arthritis questionnaire, Psoriatic Arthritis Uncluttered Screening Evaluation and Musculoskeletal Health Questionnaire, and assess the impact of HS treatments on joint symptoms.
