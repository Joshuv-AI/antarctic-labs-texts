Cucurbitacin E inhibits M1 macrophage polarization and attenuates rheumatoid arthritis: a multi-omics analysis and experimental validation
Authors: Haolan Xiong, Shenglu Cao, Lin Yang, Xiaofang Hu, Gang Wang, Fuming Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease marked by joint inflammation and destruction. Current treatments often have side effects and resistance. Cucurbitacin E (CuE), a natural compound with anti-inflammatory properties, shows therapeutic potential but its role in RA is unclear. This study explores CuE’s mechanisms in RA, focusing on M1 macrophage polarization.MethodsWe used a multi-omics approach, integrating transcriptomics, single-cell sequencing, and network pharmacology. Key steps included immune infiltration analysis, WGCNA, machine learning-based biomarker discovery, and flow cytometry validation. Pseudotime trajectory and cell communication analyses were also employed.ResultsImmune infiltration analysis revealed increased M1 macrophage infiltration in RA patients. WGCNA identified gene modules related to macrophage polarization. Machine learning identified five key biomarkers (CCR2, NFKB1, NT5E, PIK3R1, TYRO3). A diagnostic model based on these biomarkers achieved high accuracy (AUC = 0.94). Pseudotime and cell communication analyses suggested CuE may regulate M1 polarization and signaling networks. Flow cytometry showed high CuE concentrations inhibited M1 macrophage polarization.ConclusionCuE inhibits M1 macrophage polarization and related pathways, offering a promising RA treatment strategy. The identified biomarkers may serve as diagnostic and therapeutic targets. Future research should validate CuE’s clinical effects.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease characterized by joint inflammation and destruction, affecting approximately 1% of the global population [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of RA is complex, involving immune cell infiltration, macrophage polarization (M1 pro-inflammatory macrophages and M2 anti-inflammatory macrophages), release of inflammatory factors, and abnormal proliferation of synovial tissue [3–11]. Current RA treatments mainly rely on anti-inflammatory drugs and biologics, but these medications are often accompanied by side effects, and some patients are either unresponsive to or develop resistance against these treatments [12–14].

Cucurbitacin E (CuE) is a natural compound extracted from plants of the Cucurbitaceae family, known for its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immune-modulating properties [15, 16]. CuE has shown significant therapeutic potential in cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, and inflammatory conditions such as colitis [17, 18]. However, its role in RA is not yet fully understood. Based on CuE’s anti-inflammatory characteristics, it is hypothesized that it may alleviate RA symptoms by modulating macrophage polarization, inhibiting the release of inflammatory factors, and improving immune cell infiltration.

Despite extensive research into the pathogenesis of RA, the dynamic changes in macrophage polarization and its interactions with the immune cell network still require further exploration. The specific mechanisms of CuE in RA treatment are not yet well understood, lacking systematic multi-omics analysis and experimental validation. Current studies are mostly focused on individual molecules or pathways, lacking a systems biology approach to comprehensively analyze the potential role of CuE in RA.

This study aims to systematically explore the therapeutic mechanisms of CuE in RA using multi-omics analysis (transcriptomics, single-cell sequencing, and network pharmacology) combined with experimental validation. By integrating immune infiltration analysis, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), and machine learning algorithms, key biomarkers for RA will be identified. Pseudotime trajectory analysis and cell communication analysis will be employed to reveal the dynamic differentiation of M1 macrophages and their signaling networks in RA. This research will elucidate the mechanisms of CuE in RA, providing new insights into the pathogenesis of RA. It will also offer potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for the diagnosis and treatment of RA, promoting the clinical application of CuE. Ultimately, this work aims to improve the quality of life for RA patients, reduce medical costs, and provide new strategies for the treatment of chronic inflammatory diseases.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The transcriptomic data were obtained from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo), including three datasets: GSE89408 (training set, n = 218 human synovial tissue samples, GPL11154 platform), GSE1919 (validation set, n = 15 human synovial tissue samples, GPL91 platform), GSE184609 (single-cell sequencing data of murine synovial tissue, GPL24247 platform).

All raw data underwent rigorous preprocessing involving quality control (removal of low-expression genes), normalization (FPKM method), and batch effect correction (ComBat algorithm) to ensure analytical reliability.

The SMILES identifier of cucurbitacin E (CC(= O)OC(C)(C)/C = C/C(= O)[C@@](C)([C@H]1[C@@H](C[C@@]2([C@@]1(CC(= O)[C@@]3([C@H]2CC = C4[C@H]3C = C(C(= O)C4(C)C)O)C)C)C)O)O) was acquired from PubChem. Potential targets were predicted through integrated mining of SwissTargetPrediction, STITCH, BATMAN-TCM, and SuperPred databases, yielding 122 targets. Rheumatoid arthritis-associated targets (n = 6,837) were compiled from GeneCards, DisGeNET, and OMIM databases using "Rheumatoid Arthritis" as the search term. Venn analysis via Venny 2.1 identified 83 overlapping targets between cucurbitacin E and rheumatoid arthritis, designated as core therapeutic targets.

Bioinformatics analysis pipeline

Immune Infiltration Profiling: Utilizing the xCell algorithm from the R package "immunedeconv", we meticulously calculated the scores of 39 immune cell types across all samples in the training set. This step was crucial for delineating the immune landscape of RA and understanding the differential infiltration of various immune cells.

WGCNA for Module Identification: Employing the R package "WGCNA", we constructed a weighted gene co-expression network to identify modules intricately related to macrophage polarization. This systems biology approach allowed us to uncover synergistically varying gene sets and their associations with clinical traits.

Gene Intersection Analysis: The R package "ggvenn" was utilized to identify overlapping genes between the module genes derived from WGCNA and the predefined target genes of Cucurbitacin E. This step streamlined the pathway towards identifying key genes of interest by integrating multiple data sources.

Comprehensive Enrichment Analysis: Both GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted using the R package "clusterProfiler". The results were visualized with the R package "ggplot2", providing insights into the biological functions and pathways associated with the identified genes, thereby elucidating their roles in RA pathogenesis.

Machine Learning for Biomarker Discovery: Candidate biomarkers were rigorously screened using two robust algorithms, SVM-RFE and Boruta, implemented in the R packages "e1071" and "Boruta". This dual-method approach enhanced the reliability of biomarker selection by cross-validating the results from different algorithms.

Biomarker Validation Across Datasets: The expression levels of the shortlisted candidate biomarkers were validated across both the training and validation sets. Boxplots generated using the R package "ggplot2" facilitated a clear visualization of expression differences, ensuring the consistency and significance of the biomarkers across datasets.

Diagnostic Model Development: A diagnostic nomogram was meticulously constructed using the R package "rms". The model's performance was thoroughly evaluated using ROC and DCA curves, leveraging the R packages "ROCR" and "ggDCA", to ascertain its diagnostic efficacy and clinical utility.

Network Construction for Interaction Insights: To explore the interactions within the identified genes, TF-mRNA, mRNA-miRNA-lncRNA, and gene-disease networks were constructed. The JASPAR, miRDB, Starbase, and MalaCards databases were utilized, with networks visualized using Cytoscape 3.7.1, providing a comprehensive view of gene regulatory mechanisms. Utilizing the GeneMANIA database (https://genemania.org/) to construct a gene–gene interaction network (GGI) and sort the interactions based on their Score values in descending order.

Single-Cell Data Acquisition and Preprocessing Single-cell RNA sequencing data were obtained from the GSE184609 dataset in the GEO database, which includes synovial tissue samples from RA patients and healthy controls. The data were preprocessed using the Seurat package in R, which involved quality control, normalization, and batch effect correction. The preprocessing step was critical for ensuring the reliability of downstream analyses.

Cell Clustering and Annotation Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to reduce the dimensionality of the data, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were selected for further analysis. Cells were clustered using the "FindNeighbors" and "FindClusters" functions in Seurat, with a resolution of 0.2 to optimize cluster separation.

Pseudotime Trajectory Analysis To explore the dynamic differentiation of M1 macrophages, pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed using the Monocle package. This analysis reconstructs the differentiation trajectory of cells, allowing for the identification of gene expression changes along the trajectory. Cells were ordered based on their differentiation state, and differentially expressed genes were identified to understand the molecular events driving macrophage polarization.

Cell Communication Analysis Cell communication was analyzed using the CellChat package, which quantifies ligand-receptor interactions between different cell types. This analysis helps to understand the signaling networks and interactions that drive the inflammatory response in RA. Key ligand-receptor pairs were identified and visualized to highlight the communication between M1 macrophages and other immune cells.

Flow cytometry analysis

CCK-8 assay to determine the effect of CuE on the viability of THP-1 cells. The detailed process of flow cytometry analysis is as follows: Initially, THP-1 cells are seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells/mL into a 12-well cell culture plate and induced with 0.04 mg/mL PMA for 48 h to differentiate into macrophages. Then, the cells are treated with 100 ng/mL LPS and 20 ng/mL IFNγ for 24 h to induce M1 macrophage polarization. Subsequently, the cells are divided into six groups, including a control group (treated with DMSO only) and five experimental groups (with 0 nM, 1 nM, 3 nM, 10 nM, and 30 nM CuE added, respectively), each group continues to be induced with LPS/IFNγ for an additional 24 h. The staining procedure involves collecting cells that have undergone different treatments, washing and centrifuging with 1 × PBS; discarding the supernatant, adding Zombie AquaTM Dye for live/dead staining for 20 min; washing and centrifuging again with 1 × PBS; discarding the supernatant, adding diluted antibodies for staining for 30 min; finally, washing with 1 × PBS, resuspending the cells, and immediately proceeding to flow cytometry analysis. Markers for M1 macrophage polarization are CD80 and CD86.

WGCNA reveals macrophage-related modules

In accordance with the criteria of the hybrid dynamic tree cutting algorithm, the minimum number of genes in each gene module was set to 200, resulting in a total of 11 modules (Fig. 1D). Through WGCNA, five distinct modules (MEgreen, MEmagenta, MEpurple, MEred, MEturquoise) were identified as being associated with macrophage polarization. These modules collectively harbored 9767 genes, which were significantly enriched in immune responses and inflammatory pathways (Fig. 1E). The module eigengenes exhibited strong correlations with clinical traits, highlighting their relevance to RA progression and severity.

Biomarker discovery and cross-validation

A total of 18 genes were identified through SVM-RFE, namely NR3C2, GBA1, CNR1, EGLN1, HSP90AB1, NT5E, LGALS3, CDK1, TYRO3, ACACA, ADAM10, C5AR1, PIK3R1, CCR2, FPR2, PDE3A, DPP8, and NFKB1. Meanwhile, the Boruta method yielded 20 intersecting genes, which are ACACA, AOC3, BCL6, CCR1, CCR2, CDK1, DPP8, EGLN1, FPR1, FPR2, GBA1, ITGB3, NFKB1, NR3C2, NT5E, PIK3R1, PRCP, PRKAA1, TLR4, and TYRO3. By taking the intersection of these two sets, 12 intersecting genes were obtained as candidate biomarkers, including NR3C2, GBA1, EGLN1, NT5E, CDK1, TYRO3, ACACA, PIK3R1, CCR2, FPR2, DPP8, and NFKB1 (Fig. 2D). Box plots were used to illustrate the expression differences of these candidate biomarkers between disease and control samples in both the training and validation sets, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed between groups (Note: In the validation set, the gene GBA is an alias for GBA1, and the genes EGLN1 and DPP8 were not present) (Fig. 2E and F). Among the candidate biomarkers, five exhibited significant differences and maintained consistent expression trends between groups across both the training and validation datasets. These five candidates (CCR2, NFKB1, NT5E, PIK3R1, and TYRO3) were thus selected for further analysis. Notably, the functional similarity among these biomarkers exceeded 0.4, indicating a moderate level of relatedness in their biological functions. (Fig. 2G).

Discussion

Macrophages play a key role in the pathogenesis of rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and their polarization state is of great significance for the progression and treatment of RA [19–24]. Immune infiltration analysis indicates that the infiltration of M1 macrophages is significantly increased in RA patients, closely associated with disease progression. WGCNA analysis identifies gene modules related to macrophage polarization, which are significantly enriched in immune response and inflammatory pathways. Gene intersection analysis and machine learning identify key genes (such as CCR2, NFKB1, NT5E, PIK3R1, TYRO3) with significant expression differences between RA patients and healthy controls, and these genes are closely related to M1 macrophage polarization. Flow cytometry experiments show that high concentrations of CuE (10 nM and 30 nM) significantly inhibit LPS/IFNγ-induced M1 macrophage polarization, as evidenced by the reduced expression of CD80 and CD86, demonstrating that CuE can inhibit M1 macrophage polarization.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis shows that the expression of key genes (such as Ccr2 and Nfkb1) involved in the differentiation process of M1 macrophages decreases with the differentiation stage, suggesting that CuE may inhibit M1 polarization by regulating the expression of these genes. Cell communication analysis indicates that the signaling communication between M1 macrophages and other cell types is significantly enhanced in the disease group, and CuE may alleviate the inflammatory response by modulating these signaling networks. The constructed TF-mRNA, mRNA-miRNA-lncRNA, and gene-disease networks reveal the key molecular mechanisms regulated by CuE, including autophagy, ferroptosis, and the HIF-1 signaling pathway. GGI network analysis shows that CuE may inhibit M1 macrophage polarization by regulating key genes (such as NT5E, PIK3R1) and their interactions.

Tetracyclic triterpenoid compounds hold significant potential in the treatment of RA [25–27]. CuE, a tetracyclic triterpenoid compound with anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, and anti-tumor properties, carries profound implications for research into RA therapy [15]. By inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization, CuE reduces the release of pro-inflammatory factors (such as TNF-α, IL-6) [28], thereby alleviating the inflammatory response in RA. The immune-modulating effects of CuE may help restore the balance of the immune system in RA patients, reducing autoimmune attacks. Compared with traditional RA treatment drugs, CuE, as a natural compound, has better safety and tolerability, and may reduce the occurrence of side effects.

The five key biomarkers identified in this study (CCR2, NFKB1, NT5E, PIK3R1, TYRO3) can serve as potential diagnostic and therapeutic markers for RA. The diagnostic model constructed based on these biomarkers (AUC = 0.94) shows high accuracy and has potential clinical application value. CuE provides a new strategy for RA treatment by inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization and may become a supplement or alternative to traditional treatment drugs.

Further verify the therapeutic effects of CuE on RA in animal models and clinical trials, especially its long-term impact on M1 macrophage polarization. Conduct in-depth research on how CuE inhibits M1 macrophage polarization by regulating key genes and signaling pathways (such as PI3K-Akt, autophagy, ferroptosis). Explore the combination of CuE with other RA treatment drugs to develop more effective therapeutic regimens. Investigate the administration methods and dosage optimization of CuE to enhance its feasibility for clinical application.

This study reveals the potential mechanisms of CuE in RA treatment through multi-omics analysis and experimental validation, especially its anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting M1 macrophage polarization. These findings provide new insights for the diagnosis and treatment of RA and lay the foundation for future clinical applications based on CuE. Future research should further validate the clinical effects of CuE and explore its mechanisms of action to advance its application in RA treatment.
