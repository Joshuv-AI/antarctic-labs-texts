Medication Exposure Definitions in Perinatal Pharmacoepidemiology: Implications for Pregnancy Studies
Authors: Gabra Nohmie, Anick Bérard, Anton Pottegård, Mette Bliddal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundExposure definitions in real‐world pregnancy studies vary across studies, data sources, treatments and outcomes, yet their impact on prevalence estimates remains unexamined.ObjectivesTo identify the most used exposure definitions during the 1st trimester and evaluate their impact on prevalence estimates:MethodsWe reviewed 400 randomly selected articles on medication use during pregnancy to identify exposure definitions. Using Danish health registers (1997–2024) and the Québec Pregnancy Cohort (1998–2015), we analysed chronic, as‐needed, and acute medication use. Prevalence (per 1000) was calculated per definition, including: (1) ≥ 1 or (2) ≥ 2 filled prescriptions during 1st trimester; filled ≥ 1 prescription anytime between the 1st trimester or (3) 30 days, (4) 60 days, (5) 90 days, (6) 6 months before conception; filled a prescription during 1st trimester or before conception, (7) assuming 1 daily defined dose per day or (8) prescribed days overlapping 1st trimester. Trends across cohorts and medications were compared.ResultsWe analysed 1,524,075 pregnancies in Denmark and 229,000 in Québec, Canada. The most common exposure definitions identified from the literature were Definition 1 (83%), followed by Definition 3 (13%). Prevalence varied across definitions, with differential impacts depending on medication and type of use. Definition 6 (1st trimester or within the 6 months before conception) yielded the highest prevalence; Definition 2 (≥ 2 fills in 1st trimester) the lowest. Broader time‐windows of exposure increased prevalence, with variation by medication. The prevalence of medication use, whether chronic or acute, showed less variation across definitions than that of as‐needed medication.ConclusionsMedication exposure prevalence varied by definition and medication. Using ≥ 1 filled prescriptions in the 1st trimester yielded prevalence estimates comparable to those from more complex definitions, supporting its use as a pragmatic standard. Sensitivity analyses and transparent reporting of exposure definition are essential to ensure replicability and critical appraisal.Keywords: 1st trimester, country comparisons, definitions, medication exposure, trends

Background

Most pregnant individuals take at least one prescription medication [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Some medications have the potential to cross the placenta, raising concerns about their safety for the child. Perinatal pharmacoepidemiologic studies are the cornerstone of our understanding of the effect of such medication exposure. However, operational definitions of perinatal exposure vary greatly among studies. Currently, drug utilisation research has not systematically examined how different exposure definitions influence prevalence and risk estimates. Exposure definitions depend on factors such as healthcare systems, reimbursement policies, data sources, and indications of interest.

The objectives of this study were to (i) identify the most commonly used operational definitions for calculating medication exposures during the first trimester of pregnancy and (ii) evaluate how these distinct exposure definitions impact the estimated prevalence of medication use during the first trimester of pregnancy in two countries with different prescription access and policies.

Methods

To identify commonly used definitions of first‐trimester medication exposure, we conducted a literature review in the fall of 2024. Articles were identified through a PubMed search using the following strategy: “Pregnancy”[Mesh] AND (“Pharmaceutical Preparations”[Mesh] OR “exposure*” OR “first trimester”) AND (“Denmark”[Mesh] OR “Sweden”[Mesh] OR “Norway”[Mesh] OR “Finland”[Mesh] OR “Canada”[Mesh] OR “Asia”[Mesh] OR “United States”[Mesh]). Search results were screened in random order by one reviewer (G.N.). Observational studies that explicitly described the operational definition of first‐trimester medication exposure were eligible, and screening continued until 400 studies were included. We included in our PubMed search countries and regions where pharmacoepidemiologic research is well established and where administrative data sources are commonly used to ascertain medication exposure definitions in perinatal studies.

Using the Danish National Health Registers [6] and the Québec Pregnancy Cohort (QPC) [2], we conducted a population‐based cohort study to examine how exposure definition influences the estimated prevalence of medication use during the first trimester. We selected medications prescribed for both mental illnesses and somatic conditions, encompassing chronic, as‐needed, and acute use, as they follow different prescribing patterns. Denmark and Québec, Canada, were selected because of differences in healthcare systems and prescription refill practices. For instance, chronic prescriptions are typically filled every 30 days in Québec [7], versus every 3 months in Denmark [8]. These differences provide a unique opportunity to assess how prescription patterns influence estimates of medication use in pregnancy in different settings.

Danish Data Sources

We used information from the Danish Medical Birth Register [9] to identify all pregnancies resulting in a live or stillbirth. Using the unique identification number assigned to all residents in Denmark at birth or upon immigration [10], we linked data from the Medical Birth Register to the Danish National Prescription Registry [11] to obtain information on filled prescriptions. Denmark provides tax‐funded, universal maternity care and all healthcare contacts are mandated to be reported, yielding an unselected nationwide cohort.

The Danish Medical Birth Register contains information on all births in Denmark since 1973. It includes maternal characteristics, specific variables related to pregnancy and delivery, and information on outcomes. Additionally, it records the date of delivery and gestational age based on ultrasound scans or, if missing, on maternal recall of the first date of the last menstrual period. Live births are included regardless of gestational age, while stillbirths are only recorded if delivery occurred after 21 full weeks of gestational age [9]. Gestational age was defined as the first day of the last menstrual period (LMP).

The Danish National Prescription Register contains individual‐level data on all dispensed medications at community pharmacies since 1994. All pharmacies in Denmark are community pharmacies. The term ‘community’ is used to distinguish dispensing in primary care settings from hospital‐based medication use. Therefore, the pharmacy data captured in our study reflect medications dispensed exclusively by community pharmacies, not by hospitals. It provides information on date of dispensation, anatomical therapeutic chemical (ATC) classification codes, number of tablets, number of doses per unit in package, number of defined daily doses (DDD) per package and number of packets filled [11].

Québec Pregnancy Cohort

The Quebec Pregnancy Cohort is a prospective population‐based cohort. For this study, data on pregnancies insured by the ‘Régie de l'Assurance Maladie du Québec (RAMQ)’ public drug plan between January 1998 and December 2015 were considered [2]. RAMQ provides medical and pharmaceutical coverage to Québec (Canada) residents who are welfare recipients, employees without a medication coverage from their employer or spouse's employer, and individuals ≥ 65 years old. This cohort was constructed by linking three databases using a unique patient‐encrypted identifier, the mother's and child's dates of birth and names and surnames. RAMQ contains information on dispensed medications from pharmacies, including medication name, prescribed duration, dosage, ATC codes, number of filled pills and dispensing date. Individuals in the QPC are covered by Quebec's public prescription drug insurance plan for at least 12 months before the first day of gestation, and during pregnancy. The first day of gestation is defined as the first day of LMP confirmed by ultrasound for most pregnancies and validated against medical charts (Pearson correlation = 0.970) [12]. The Maintenance et exploitation de données pour l'étude de la clientèle hospitalière (MED‐ÉCHO) contains information on acute care hospitalisations, diagnostic codes and gestational age [2]. The Institut de la Statistique du Québec (ISQ) contains demographic information on the mother and baby, as well as birthweight and gestational age for livebirths and stillbirths [2].

Study Populations

We identified pregnancies in the Danish cohort from 1997 to 2023 and in the Québec Pregnancy cohort from 1998 to 2015. We identified all pregnancies leading to a birth, whether live or stillbirths. The study unit was the pregnancy; thus, each pregnant individual could be included more than once. Pregnancies were included if they had available information on gestational age (≥ 21 + 0 weeks). To ensure complete information on medication use, we restricted to pregnancies in which the pregnant mother was registered as a resident in Denmark from 1 year prior to pregnancy until 3 months postpartum, and for Québec, to pregnancies with continuous public drug insurance plan coverage over the same period.

Exposure Definition and Time Window

Medication use surrounding pregnancy was of interest, with a particular focus on the first trimester, the period of organogenesis. The time window of interest for exposure definitions identified from the literature was the first trimester. We defined the first trimester as the period from the first day of gestation to the 12th completed week of pregnancy, i.e., 84 days (12 + 0) [13, 14].

The definitions retained in this analysis obtained through the literature review were: Definition 1: filled ≥ 1 prescription during the 1st trimester, Definition 2: filled ≥ 2 prescriptions in the 1st trimester, Definition 3: filled ≥ 1 prescription during the 1st trimester or within the 30 days before conception, Definition 4: filled ≥ 1 prescription during the 1st trimester or within the 60 days before conception, Definition 5: filled ≥ 1 prescription during the 1st trimester or within the 90 days before conception, Definition 6: filled ≥ 1 prescription during the 1st trimester or within the 6 months before conception, Definition 7: filled a prescription during the 1st trimester or before conception, assuming DDD overlapping 1st trimester with 1 DDD per day (only applicable in Danish data), Definition 8: filled a prescription during the 1st trimester or before conception with prescribed days overlapping 1st trimester (only applicable in Canadian data).

Medications of Interest

Because variation in prevalence depends on the definition used, we assessed the impact of exposure definitions on selected medications. Filled medications were identified using ATC codes. We studied six chronic medications: selective serotonin‐reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) (N06AB), antiepileptics (N03), β‐blocking agents (C07), other drugs for obstructive airway diseases, inhalants (R03B), levothyroxine sodium (H03AA01), and psychostimulants, agents used for ADHD and nootropics (N06B). We further studied three medications used as needed: anti‐inflammatory and antirheumatic products, non‐steroids (M01A), paracetamol (N02BE01), and opioids (N02A). Finally, we studied one medication used to treat acute conditions: antibacterials for systemic use (J01).

Statistical Analysis

We tabulated the frequency of the different definitions found in our random sample of 400 articles. For each cohort, we calculated the weekly prevalence of medication fills from the year before pregnancy through 3 months post‐delivery. This was done for overall medication use and for each included medication. To better understand the trends of prescription fillings in each cohort during the first trimester, we modelled the prevalence of filling prescriptions (per 1000) per gestational week by fitting a linear regression model for each cohort per studied medication. We report the slope coefficient (β) as the change rate of weekly filled prescriptions (per 1000 pregnancies) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for each model. Gestational lengths were considered, as not all pregnancies contribute to each weekly prevalence; consequently, the denominator changes weekly after Week 21 of gestation.

For each of the eight exposure definitions identified above, we calculated the prevalence proportion of exposed pregnancies (per 1000) within each cohort.

In a post hoc analysis, we quantified the overlap between Definition 1 (filled ≥ 1 prescription during the 1st trimester) and each alternative definition by identifying pregnancies that were classified under both definitions and those unique to either definition.

Sensitivity Analyses

According to the literature, there is variation in the definition of the first trimester [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Therefore, we repeated the analyses, defining the end of the first trimester at Weeks 13 + 0 and 14 + 0.

Missing Data

We excluded pregnancies with missing gestational age. Multiple imputation was not performed.

Ethics Statement

No ethics approval from an Institutional Review Board was required for the conduction of this study. In accordance with Danish legislation, approval and patient consent are not required for studies conducted exclusively using registry data. For Québec Pregnancy Cohort, data linkages were previously authorised by the Québec Comission d'Accès à l'information.

Results

In total, we identified 1,524,075 pregnancies between 1997 and 2023 in the Danish cohort and 229,000 between 1998 and 2015 in QPC (Figure 1). In the Danish cohort, pregnancies with missing gestational age (n = 202, 0.01%), incomplete health coverage (n = 63,399, 4.0%) and invalid gestational age (< 21 weeks) (n = 42,295, 2.7%) were excluded. In the QPC, incomplete public drug plan coverage (n = 20,494 [8.2%]) and invalid gestational age (n = 33 [0.01%]) were excluded. No missing gestational age was recorded in QPC. The most used definition for medication exposure during the first trimester was the binary ‘filled ≥ 1 prescription during the 1st trimester’ (Definition 1, 83%), followed by ‘filled ≥ 1 prescription between 30 days before conception and the end of the 1st trimester(Definition 3, 13%)’, and ‘filled ≥ 1 prescription during 1st trimester or before gestation with prescribed days overlapping 1st trimester’ (Definition 8, 9.8%) (Table 1).

In both cohorts, weekly prescription fills declined in the year before pregnancy (Figure 2). Unlike in QPC, where the trend continued to decrease in the first few weeks of pregnancy, the number of filled prescriptions increased in Denmark during the first 3 weeks of pregnancy. This was driven by the use of gonadotropins and other ovulation stimulants (G03G) (100 per 1000). During the postpartum period, both cohorts experienced a surge in the number of prescriptions filled. In an exploratory post hoc analysis, the increase in medication fillings in Denmark was driven by beta‐lactam antibacterials, penicillin (J01C, 93 per 1000), treatment of haemorrhoids and anal fissures for topical use (C05A, 63 per 1000) and NSAIDs (M01A, 24 per 1000). In Québec, the first 3 weeks postpartum were driven by the use of NSAIDs (M01A, 166 per 1000), hormonal contraceptives for systemic use (G03A, 115 per 1000) and opioids (N02A, 111 per 1000).

Prevalence of filling of any prescription medication by week from 1 year prior to pregnancy to 3 months postpartum.

The prevalence of medication use varied substantially across exposure definitions, with higher proportions observed as the exposure time window widened. Definition 6 (filled ≥ 1 prescription between 6 months before conception and the end of the 1st trimester) yielded the highest estimates for all medications, while Definition 2 (filled ≥ 2 prescriptions in the 1st trimester) consistently produced the lowest (Tables S1–S6).

Chronic Medications Use

Prevalence of weekly filled prescriptions for chronic medications (Figures S1–S6) either decreased or remained stable during the first trimester. For instance, SSRI weekly filled prescriptions showed a consistent decline in both cohorts during the 1st trimester, with greater variation in Québec (from 3.6 per 1000 on Week 1 to 2.4 per 1000 on Week 12; β −0.14 prescription per 1000, 95% CI −0.18 to −0.11) compared to Denmark (from 2.1 per 1000 to 1.4 per 1000; β −0.08 prescription per 1000, 95% CI −0.09 to −0.06) (Figure S1), while antiepileptic use decreased in the QPC (from 1.9 per 1000 to 1.2 per 1000, β −0.08 prescription per 1000, 95% CI −0.11 to −0.05) but remained constant in the Danish cohort (β −0.002 prescription per 1000, 95% CI −0.007 to 0.003) (Figure S2).

Except for Definition 2 (≥ 2 filled prescriptions in the 1st trimester), most definitions produced similar prevalences, with an increase as the exposure window widened (Figure 3). For instance, the prevalence of SSRIs varied from 15.3 per 1000 (Definition 1) to 28.5 per 1000 (Definition 6) and from 17.1 per 1000 to 29.6 per 1000 in Denmark and Québec, respectively (Tables S1 and S4). In our mapping analysis, definitions with longer time windows (Definitions 3–6) identified all exposed pregnancies from Definition 1 (Tables S7 and S8).

Medications Use As‐Needed

Medication use as needed showed notable variation across exposure definitions (Figure 4). NSAIDs use decreased in both cohorts (Denmark from 3.7 per 1000 to 0.4 per 1000, β −0.32 prescription per 1000, 95% CI −0.40 to −0.23; QPC from 3.4 per 1000 to 0.4 per 1000, β −0.32 prescription per 1000, 95% CI −0.42 to −0.23) (Figure S7). In QPC, paracetamol use increased during the first trimester (from 0.5 per 1000 in Week 1 to 0.8 per 1000 in Week 12, β 0.03 prescription per 1000, 95% CI 0.02–0.05), while it decreased in Denmark (from 1.7 per 1000 to 0.8 per 1000, β −0.09 prescription per 1000, 95% CI −0.12 to −0.06, Figure S8). Opioids also showed a decrease in both cohorts (Denmark from 1.2 per 1000 to 0.4 per 1000, β −0.09 prescription per 1000, 95% CI −0.11 to −0.07; QPC from 2.0 per 1000 to 1.3 per 1000, β −0.08 prescription per 1000, 95% CI −0.12 to −0.05) (Figure S9).

Prevalences of as‐needed treatments according to different definitions in Denmark and Québec, Canada. The definition categories 1–8 are described in Table 1.

NSAIDs and paracetamol showed the largest variability when longer exposure windows were considered. In Denmark, NSAIDs prevalence went from 17.5 per 1000 in Definition 1 to 95.3 per 1000 in Definition 6, and paracetamol from 11.6 per 1000 to 41.0 per 1000, respectively. In Québec, NSAIDs prevalence increased from 16.7 per 1000 to 103.6 per 1000, and paracetamol from 6.6 per 1000 to 16.5 per 1000, respectively (Figure 4 and Tables S1 and S4).

In our mapping analysis, the number of exposed pregnancies being added by longer exposure time windows surpassed the number of pregnancies identified by both definitions for all three medication classes (NSAIDs, paracetamol, and opioids) (Tables S7 and S8).

Acute Medications Use

A slight decreasing trend in the prevalence of weekly filled prescriptions for acute conditions, i.e., antibacterials for systemic use, was observed in QPC (β −0.13 prescription per 1000, 95% CI −0.33 to 0.07), whereas an increasing trend was observed in Denmark (from 10.2 per 1000 to 12.0 per 1000, β 0.40 prescription per 1000, 95% CI 0.19–0.62; Figure S10). Except for Definition 2, prevalence of systemic antibacterial use showed large variations across definitions and increased with wider exposure window (Denmark from 116.8 per 1000 in Definition 1 to 294.2 per 1000 in Definition 6; Québec from 102.6 per 1000 to 284.0 per 1000, respectively) (Figure 5 and Tables S1 and S4).

Prevalences of acute treatment (antibacterials for systemic use) according to different definitions in Denmark and Québec, Canada. The definition categories 1–8 are described in Table 1.

In our mapping analysis, definitions with longer time windows (Definitions 3–6) identified all exposed pregnancies from Definition 1 (Tables S7 and S8). Sensitivity analyses confirmed these findings, showing trends similar to those of the primary analysis (Tables S2, S3, S5 and S6).

Comment

Principal Findings

We identified commonly used 1st‐trimester exposure definitions and evaluated how these definitions affected the prevalence of medication use. By far, the most used definition was the filling of at least one prescription within the first trimester. Our findings indicate that, for most selected medications, this easily explained definition yields exposure estimates similar to those from more complex definitions.

Strengths of the Study

A key strength of this study is the use of two large, population‐based cohorts from different healthcare systems. The Danish cohort benefits from nationwide coverage, reducing the risk of selection bias. Another advantage of the Danish cohort was the use of fully updated data until 2023. The QPC includes 36% of all pregnant individuals in the province aged 15–45 during the study period. However, privately and publicly insured pregnancies have similar characteristics [20].

Limitations of the Data

A main limitation of this study is the incomplete capture of all pregnancies, including those ending before gestational Week 21. Therefore, we were unable to capture pregnancies that ended with pregnancy losses and terminations, underestimating the prevalence of exposed pregnancies. Furthermore, filled prescriptions serve as proxies for medication consumption, not actual intake. However, prior studies have found that medications filled during pregnancy are usually taken, particularly for chronic and acute medications, but less so for as‐needed [21]. Therefore, there is a risk of exposure misclassification, especially for as‐needed treatments such as NSAIDs and paracetamol. In Denmark, prescriptions are required to purchase more than 20 pills of paracetamol or NSAIDs [22]. Such restrictions do not exist in Québec, which may lead to an under‐ascertainment of exposure.

It was impossible to calculate prevalences for some definitions across the two study cohorts due to variation in data capture. For example, information on prescription duration is available in Quebec data but not in the Danish Prescription Registry.

Interpretation

These results suggest that the simpler definition can serve as a practical and effective standard, supporting better comparability across studies and improved methodological transparency.

The timing and method used to define exposure are crucial. However, variation in definitions was markedly greater for as‐needed medications compared to those used acutely or chronically. When dosage information was considered (Definitions 7 and 8), the prevalence of exposed pregnancies in the first trimester decreased and more closely aligned with that of Definition 1.

In addition, comparisons across definitions also revealed important methodological implications. Definitions with longer exposure windows (Definitions 3–6) included all pregnancies identified by Definition 1 and captured additional pregnancies with predefined duration. The level of additional exposed pregnancies varied by medication type. We conducted the study in two countries, Canada and Denmark, which reflect different clinical guidelines and prescribing patterns. These differences may be partly explained by distinctions in health care systems, including public coverage and the socioeconomic status of insured people [2, 9]. Overall, we observed a general decline in weekly filled prescriptions during pregnancy across both cohorts, suggesting a change in medication use behaviour during the first trimester once pregnant individuals suspect a pregnancy.

The identified definitions highlight the importance of precisely defining first‐trimester medication exposure in perinatal pharmacoepidemiology. While the dichotomous definitions presented here do not capture exhaustive exposure definitions or exposure during specific gestational weeks, this distinction becomes increasingly relevant for studies that aim to assess outcomes linked to narrower exposure windows (e.g., spina bifida). The choice of exposure definition should align with the study objective, whether describing medication utilisation patterns or estimating comparative effectiveness or safety, and account for potential biases such as immortal time bias, dosage information and timing of exposure.

Conclusions

The choice of exposure definition influences prevalence estimates of medication use during the first trimester of pregnancy. This variation depends on the medication, its indication, and the healthcare system. Defining exposure as ≥ 1 filled prescription during the first trimester yields estimates comparable to those from more complex definitions, supporting its use as a gold standard across data sources to facilitate comparability. Sensitivity analyses using alternative definitions are recommended to assess the robustness of findings. Ultimately, transparency in defining exposure is essential to enable reproducibility and critical appraisal of study design and findings.
