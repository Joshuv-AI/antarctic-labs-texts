All 2D Material Printed Diodes and Circuits on Paper for Sustainable Electronics
Authors: Alessandro Grillo, Khaled Parvez, Jingjing Wang, Zixing Peng, Aniello Pelella, Chaochao Dun, Jeffrey J. Urban, Antonio Di Bartolomeo, Cinzia Casiraghi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sustainable electronics aim to reduce environmental impact by using ecofriendly materials, energy-efficient manufacturing, and recyclable components. However, existing approaches rely on complex, resource-intensive methods, rare metals, or nanomaterials with limited stability as well as plastic substrates, raising sustainability issues. Solution-processed two-dimensional (2D) materials offer a promising alternative: water-based and biocompatible conductive, semiconductive, and insulating 2D material inks can be produced with scalable techniques and are suitable for the fabrication of fully printed devices on low-cost and biodegradable paper substrates. However, 2D material only and fully printed diodes on paper have not yet been reported. Here, we demonstrate fully inkjet-printed 2D material-based diodes on paper using metal–insulator-semiconductor and metal–insulator–metal–semiconductor architectures. Water-based graphene and MoS2 inks, prepared by liquid-phase exfoliation, are used for the metallic and insulating films, while electrochemical exfoliation is used to produce the semiconducting MoS2 ink. The highest forward-to-reverse current ratio obtained is 330 (at ±2 V), while the forward current density is 1 mA/cm2 (at 1 V), making the diode performance comparable to the best solution-processed diodes reported so far. However, in contrast to previous works, fabrication occurs entirely at room temperature and ambient pressure, without using high-pressure sputtering, thermal evaporation, and any precious metal ink. The devices maintain stable performance under bending up to strain of 4% over 10,000 cycles. Finally, the diodes are successfully integrated with other 2D-material based electrical components to realize fully printed RC circuits, differentiators, integrators, and AC-to-DC converters onto paper, hence demonstrating the suitability of our approach for sustainable and disposable integrated circuits.

Printed electronics has emerged as a transformative manufacturing technology by enabling the fabrication of flexible, lightweight, and cost-effective devices well-suited for the Internet of Things (IoTs), where manufacturing costs, flexibility, scalability, and low power consumption are of crucial importance. − While printed electronics offer several advantages compared to traditional silicon-based electronics, there are various sustainability concerns that need to be faced, spanning the whole lifecycle, from material sourcing to device fabrication and their disposal. Most of the printed devices are made on polymer-based substrates, which are nonbiodegradable and thus contribute to plastic waste. Moreover, biodegradable plastics may still release microplastics or other harmful byproducts in the environment. Because of that, biodegradable or recyclable substrates, such as cellulose-based or paper substrates, are more attractive than plastics for the development of sustainable electronics. In particular, paper has gained considerable attention due to its low cost, biodegradability, lightweight nature, and compatibility with scalable printing processes. , Paper’s inherent porosity and better mechanical stability upon heating, as compared to plastic, make it an attractive platform for sustainable and disposable electronics. , However, large-scale adoption of printed devices on paper is still very limited due to several challenges associated with its poor compatibility with common materials and chemicals used in printed electronics, its high surface roughness and moisture sensitivity, as well as its mechanical fragility that can hinder device performance and reliability.

Diodes are fundamental components in printed electronics, as in traditional electronics. However, only a few studies have shown diodes fabricated on paper, using a combination of different materials, − ranging from organic materials, carbon nanotubes, metal oxide semiconductors (MOs), and other inorganic materials. Diodes based on MOs typically require high annealing temperatures and have poor mechanical properties, hence limited stability under bending. Remarkably, transition metal oxides can be directly grown on paper by the hydrothermal method, , which typically requires several processing steps and heating treatments. Furthermore, the paper substrate often needs to be pretreated to enable the growth of the nanomaterial. When looking at organic materials, the most used conductive polymers, e.g., polyaniline or poly­(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrenesulfonate (PEDOT:PSS), when printed on paper typically result in lower conductivities as compared to the same ink printed on plastic substrates. Furthermore, although many diodes have been made on paper, their fabrication often requires magnetron sputtering (for example, for deposition of indium–gallium–zinc oxide) , and metal evaporation for the fabrication of the top contacts. , In general, it is very challenging to fabricate all solution-processed vertical devices due to the intermixing of different materials at the interface. , However, the vertical geometry enables scaling of the semiconductor thickness to the submicron scale, hence decreasing the time required for the carrier to go from one electrode to the other with respect to planar diodes, enabling faster response. To fully leverage the cost-effectiveness and sustainability of paper substrates, it is crucial to use materials and fabrication approaches all compatible with solution-processed materials. On the other hand, the use of metallic inks, such as silver and copper, often used in printed electronics, also poses sustainability issues for disposable electronics, hence the need to look for potential substitutes for scarce or toxic materials.

2D materials are emerging candidates for printed electronics because they can be easily solution-processed by low cost and mass-scalable approaches, such as liquid-phase exfoliation (LPE) and electrochemical exfoliation (ECE). In particular, our group has demonstrated water-based 2D material inks compatible with inkjet printing and suitable for the fabrication of fully printed vertical devices on paper. ,− Inkjet printing is very attractive for disposable electronics because it does not require high-temperature deposition, it is compatible with paper, it shows high resolution among other printing technologies, and it has minimal waste. Despite these advancements, diodes entirely fabricated from solution-processed 2D materials have not been reported so far. Printed diodes on glass based on TiO2 nanosheets in combination with CNTs have been demonstrated; however CNT synthesis and processing remain expensive. In addition, their long-term environmental impact is not fully understood, raising concerns about their use for disposable electronics.

In this work, we use water-based graphene and MoS2 printable inks, both produced by LPE as metallic and insulating components, respectively, of the diode. A second type of MoS2 ink, produced by ECE, is used as a semiconductor to demonstrate fully inkjet-printed and metal–insulator-semiconductor (MIS) and metal–insulator–metal–semiconductor (MIMS) diodes on paper, which can sustain up to 4% bending strain. The MIMS structure enables enhanced rectifying characteristics, which are comparable to the performance of the best solution-processed diodes made with more complex, time-consuming, and high energy consumption processes. We finally show integration of the diodes with other 2D-material-based and printed electrical components, hence demonstrating fully printed functional electronic circuits on paper, such as differentiators, integrators, and RC circuits. Ultimately, we employed the printed diodes to realize a fully printed AC-to-DC converter on paper, showcasing their potential for scalable, sustainable, and disposable electronics.

Results and Discussion

Metal–semiconductor diodes are attractive for high-speed and low-voltage applications due to their fast-switching speed and low forward voltage drop. By incorporating an insulating layer between the metal and semiconductor, MIS diodes provide greater control over electrical characteristics, enabling the optimization of parameters such as leakage current, breakdown voltage, and switching speed. − Furthermore, MIS diodes exhibit enhanced radiation hardness, temperature stability, and reduced sensitivity to environmental variations, making them ideal for high-frequency and low-power applications. −

The all-2D material MIS diode was fabricated by using water-based graphene and MoS2 inks produced by LPE. However, in the case of transition metal dichalcogenides, LPE is known to provide small flakes with poor electronic properties when assembled into thin films. Printed MoS2 films made by LPE have shown insulating behavior with temperature-dependent transport governed by Mott variable-range hopping or nearest-neighbor hopping, hence, behaving as dielectrics. Therefore, a second type of MoS2 ink was produced by ECE, being able to produce large crystals with semiconducting properties. − For example, field-effect transistors using MoS2 made by ECE as a channel have been reported, achieving mobility up to 10 cm2 V–1 s–1.

Figure A shows a schematic of the ECE setup: MoS2 nanosheets were exfoliated from their bulk counterparts using a two-electrode cathodic ECE setup. A platinum foil served as the anode, while a MoS2 crystal acted as the cathode. The electrolyte consisted of 5 mM tetrapropylammonium chloride (TPACl) dissolved in propylene carbonate (PC) (Figure S1A). Initially, a potential of −1.5 V was applied to the cathode for 10 min to wet the bulk MoS2 crystal. Subsequently, the potential was increased to −10 V for 1 h to achieve efficient exfoliation. Due to the TPA+ cation intercalation into the interlayer spacing of MoS2, a substantial volume expansion occurs, leading the MoS2 crystal to exfoliate into smaller fragments that were subsequently suspended in the electrolyte. The resulting dispersion of MoS2 nanosheets exhibited a greenish color (Figure A), indicative of light absorption in the visible range, as confirmed by the UV–vis spectrum (Figure B). This spectral signature is characteristic of the semiconducting (pure 2H phase) nature of the prepared MoS2 nanosheets. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was used to estimate the thickness (T) and lateral size (L) distributions of the as-prepared MoS2 nanosheets (Figure S1B). The histogram obtained from the analysis of over 400 individual nanosheets revealed a broad lateral size distribution with an average flake size of ∼7.41 μm (Figure S1C).

Notably, ∼60% of the flakes exceeded 5.0 μm in size (Figure S1D), with the largest MoS2 nanosheets reaching over 30 μm, a value significantly larger than most previously reported values for MoS2 flakes produced by ECE. ,− This can be attributed to the molecular size of the TPA+ (≈0.75 nm), matching well with the interlayer spacing of MoS2 (≈0.62 nm), hence maximizing the efficient intercalation of the TPA+ ions into the interlayer space of bulk MoS2 crystals and avoiding the excessive charging and structural damages arising from charged intercalants. , Figure C shows a representative Raman spectrum of a MoS2 nanosheet: the characteristic E1 2g and A1 g peaks at 385.1 and 403.5 cm–1, respectively, along with a peak position difference (Δpos) value of ∼18.4 cm–1 and narrow peak widths (fwhm of E1 2g and A1 g: ∼6.2 and ∼4.4 cm–1), confirming production of high-quality single-layer MoS2. Statistical analysis of over 170 Raman spectra revealed that approximately 98% of the as-prepared MoS2 nanosheets consist of 1–3 layers, with 62% being single-layer (Figure S1D). The photoluminescence (PL) spectrum of a single-layer MoS2 nanosheet shows the prominent A and B peaks at 1.82 and 1.96 eV, respectively (Figure D), in agreement with other works. ,, Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) provides further insights into the atomic structure of the MoS2 nanosheets: low-magnification TEM images (Figure E) show low contrast of the nanosheets due to their atomic thickness and the folded edges of the nanosheets. High-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) imaging (Figure S1F) and the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern (Figure G) are signatures of a honeycomb lattice structure, with a typical (100) interplanar spacing of 0.27 nm, hence confirming the high crystallinity of the nanosheets.

To demonstrate the use of the MoS2 nanosheets made by ECE into printed devices, we developed a water-based printable ink as follows: the as-exfoliated MoS2 nanosheets were collected by vacuum filtration and subsequently dispersed in deionized water containing 1-pyrenesulfonic acid sodium salt (PS1), which acts as a stabilizer (see Methods). , The dispersion is then sonicated for 20 h to reduce the size of MoS2 nanosheets well below 1 μm (Figure A), as inkjet printable inks require particle sizes up to 1/20th of the nozzle diameter. , Figure B presents the statistical thickness distribution obtained by AFM of over 100 individual flakes after sonication, revealing an average nanosheet thickness of 3.8 ± 1.7 nm and an average flake size of 870 nm (Figure C).

The rheological properties of the water-based MoS2 dispersion need to be adjusted to match the requirements of the piezoelectric inkjet printer, following the same approach reported in ref (see also Methods), enabling to achieve an inverse Ohnesorge number (Z) of ∼17. Despite exceeding the ideal printability Z-value range (1 < Z < 14), the ink exhibited stable jetting without satellite droplet formation or nozzle clogging, as confirmed by jetting sequence analysis (Figure S1E). These results are also in agreement with previous reports on the printability of 2D material inks with Z ∼ 20. , Figure S1F shows the wetting behavior of the MoS2 ink: the average contact angle was found to be ∼20° on paper. Low contact angle values ensure the good spread of the ink once deposited on the substrate under optimized drop spacing. Figure S1G shows the optical images of the printed lines on paper made with different numbers of printing passes. A linear correlation between the number of printing passes and the resulting line thickness was determined through stylus profilometry (Figure D). To confirm the semiconducting properties of the MoS2 ink produced by ECE, we conducted I–V measurements on printed graphene/MoS2/graphene heterostructures between 80 and 350 K (Figure E). The resistivity, ρ, was obtained from the relation: ρ = R·A/L, where R is the resistance evaluated from the I–V curves, and A and L are the area (900 μm2) and the length (300 nm) of the printed MoS2 film, respectively. Figure F shows the resistivity as a function of temperature, exhibiting an exponential decrease, which is characteristic of semiconducting materials. Similar results, showing a decrease in resistivity with increasing temperature, have been reported before for other MoS2 inks. This behavior is strictly different from the one observed for the MoS2 ink produced by LPE, where the temperature dependence of the resistance is typical of an insulating material, hence confirming that the two MoS2 inks can be used separately to fabricate the insulating and semiconducting films of the diode (Figure S2A).

Figure A shows a schematic of the device structure printed on paper and the electrical configuration used for the measurements. The electrical characteristics of the MIS devices are shown in Figure B: a clear rectifying behavior was observed, with an average on/off ratio of approximately 9, calculated as the ratio of the forward and reverse current measured at ±2 V across 20 samples (Figure S2B). MIS diodes typically exhibit a low on/off ratio due to the relatively low forward current caused by the presence of the insulating film, which limits the flow of electrons even when the diode is forward biased (Figure C). This insulating barrier, while effective at reducing reverse leakage, also restricts forward current, resulting in a low contrast between the on and off states (Figure D).

This behavior has also been observed on graphene/hBN/MoS2 heterostructures made with graphene crystals produced by chemical vapor deposition, which reported on/off ratios below 10 and forward current density of 10–4 mA/cm2 measured at 1 V. By exploiting the ability of the inkjet printer to fabricate heterostructure-based devices of arbitrary complexity, we demonstrate that the MIS diode performance can be improved by simply printing an additional graphene film in the structure to create a MIMS diode.

Figure A illustrates a schematic of a MIMS structure printed on a paper substrate. The thickness and the mean square roughness of the individual layers composing the MIMS structure were measured through stylus profilometry (Figure S2C). The electrical characteristics of the MIMS device as compared to those obtained for the MIS diode are shown in Figure B: the MIMS device exhibits a notably higher forward current and a slightly reduced reverse current, while the forward current density measured at 1 V increases from 0.2 to 1 mA/cm–2 by changing the structure from MIS to MIMS. The additional metal layer in the MIMS structure favors the transport of electrons from the semiconductor to the insulating film, facilitating higher forward current by reducing the effective barrier height for electrons and might also alter the work function differences between the films, facilitating increased electron flow under forward bias (Figure E).

Conversely, in reverse bias, the additional metal layer introduces a reverse MS structure that screens the electric field applied to the insulating layer, effectively reducing the leakage currents and resulting in a decrease of the reverse current (Figure F). In other words, the enhanced rectifying characteristics of the MIMS diode can be explained by modeling the structure as a series combination of a MIM capacitor and a MS Schottky diode, Figure S2D: under forward bias, the applied voltage primarily drops across the capacitor, which facilitates increased current flow. In contrast, under reverse bias, the applied potential mainly drops across the MS diode, resulting in reduced current flow through the capacitor. We note that the band diagrams presented in Figure C–F are purely qualitative and are intended to provide first-order insight into the expected charge transport mechanisms. Due to the disordered nature of the printed films and the residual chemicals adsorbed, parameters such as work function and electron affinity are not known and may differ significantly from those of single-crystal MoS2 or graphene. For comparison, the same MIMS diode structure was also printed on a Si/SiO2 substrate using identical inks and printing parameters. The I–V characteristics (Figure S2E) show a lower rectification ratio (∼10) and a noisier current compared with devices on paper. After thermal annealing at 300 °Capplied either at the end of the printing process or after printing each individual layer (e.g., step-by-step annealing)the rectification ratio improved to ∼23 and ∼49, respectively.

The introduction of an additional metallic middle layer not only enhances device performance but also enables novel functionalities. For example, by applying a small voltage to the intermediate electrode, the current flowing through the device can be effectively modulated: Figure G shows the I–V characteristics recorded under varying bias conditions applied to the middle electrode, demonstrating a reduction in forward current with an increasing electrode voltage.

To quantitatively assess the performance of the MIMS diode, the key performance metrics of a rectifier were extracted from the I–V characteristics of 20 devices (Figure S2F,G). We note that all the tested diodes showed a rectifying behavior and comparable current values. The asymmetry (f ASYM) is defined as the ratio of forward (I F) to reverse (I R) current (f ASYM = I F/I R) and is important for understanding how well a diode functions. Nonlinearity (f NL) is the ratio of the derivative of current with respect to voltage to the ratio of current to voltage (f NL = (dI/dV)/(I/V)) and provides a quantitative measure of the diode’s performance by comparing its small-signal response to its large-signal response, which is fundamental to predict the diode’s behavior under different operating conditions. The ideality factor (n) provides insight into how closely a diode follows ideal thermionic behavior. As illustrated in Figure F, the I–V curve exhibits pronounced asymmetry, with f ASYM increasing to a maximum of 290 at a 1.9 V bias (Figure H). The calculated nonlinearity (f NL) increases with bias voltage, reaching a peak value of 1.7 at 0.5 V (Figure I) and then slightly decreases. The peak value is consistent with the “knee” position of the diode’s I–V curve, indicating the region where the current response is highly sensitive to small changes in voltage. As a first approximation, the ideality factor can be obtained from the standard diode equation. We calculated the slope of the linear region in a plot of the natural logarithm of the current (ln I) versus voltage (V), corresponding to the forward bias regime where current increases exponentially with voltagei.e., where the diode follows the ideal diode eq (Figure S2H). An ideality factor of 5.7 was extracted from this analysis, which is consistent with nonideal charge transport mechanisms commonly observed in printed 2D material-based films. We also assessed the ambient stability of the MIMS diodes by repeating the I–V measurements after 30 days of air exposure. As shown in Figure S2I, only minimal changes were observed, with J ON decreasing of ∼14%, indicating good environmental stability of the devices.

To benchmark the performance of our MIMS devices, we compared their key metrics (f ASYM measured in the range 1–2 V and forward current density, J ON, defined as the current density measured at 1 V in the forward region) with those reported for solution-processed diodes on paper, plastic, and rigid substrates (Tables and S1). While the benchmarking needs to be done on devices fabricated on the same substrate, we also included FP diodes on plastic and rigid substrates, as paper has been hardly used as a substrate in the literature. This also enables one to compare our approach to well-established fabrication strategies used with conventional substrates.

Figure J shows that our fully printed MIMS diodes demonstrate a significant improvement in both asymmetry and forward current density compared to most previously reported diodes, including those on rigid substrates. Notably, only two studies have reported diodes on paper with both high asymmetry (>102) and high current density (>2 mA/cm2); , however, these devices were fabricated either by high-pressure sputtering to deposit silver and gold electrodes and thermal evaporation to produce CdS thin films or by sublimation in high vacuum (<5 × 10–5 mbar) with organic materials grown at elevated temperatures (up to 460 °C). Furthermore, we note that our MIMS diode exhibits the highest current density among all reported fully printed (FP) diodes on paper as well as those on plastic and rigid substrates that do not employ photolithography-based fabrication techniques. Note that the FP diode with the highest on/off ratio (∼105) requires a high turn-on voltage (3 V) and has been tested in an inert atmosphere, while our diode demonstrates reliable operation in ambient air, and therefore it is suitable for practical applications.

To assess the suitability of the MIMS diodes for flexible electronics, their electrical performance has been evaluated after bending conditions. Bending strain (ε) was calculated using the relation ε = (h/2r) × 100%, where h represents the total thickness, including device and substrate (∼300 μm), and r is the bending radius. Figure K shows a schematic representation of the diodes under bending conditions. The MIMS device demonstrates great stability, maintaining an on/off ratio of approximately 250 for 10,000 bending cycles up to a strain of 4% (Figure K). A pronounced drop in the on/off ratio was observed at a strain of 5%, primarily due to an increase in device resistance and a subsequent decrease in forward current.

The circuit schematic, depicted in Figure S3A, comprises a series connection of the MIMS device and a graphene resistor R. We note that the inkjet printing technique allowed for precise control of the graphene resistor’s value by adjusting the number of printed layers. When subjected to an AC input signal (Figure S3B), the MIMS diode rectifies the current, enabling current flow only during the positive half-cycle and effectively eliminating the negative component. Figure B illustrates the input and output signals measured at the resistor’s terminal. As expected, a reduction in peak voltage corresponding to the diode’s turn-on voltage is observed, while the negative current component is suppressed. We note that the low-frequency operation (1–10 Hz) is well suited for several practical applications where printed devices on paper are particularly advantageous. , These include biological signal processing, low-frequency energy harvesting from human motion or vibrations, and real-time environmental sensing.

We further demonstrated the versatility of our platform by utilizing the bottom portion of the device (i.e., graphene/LPE MoS2/graphene) as a standalone MIM capacitor by exploiting the insulating properties of the MoS2 film made by the LPE. A fully printed RC circuit, comprising a graphene-based resistor and a MIM capacitor in series, was fabricated on paper (Figure A) and electrically connected following the circuit schematic shown in Figure S3A. Upon application of an AC signal, the capacitor undergoes charging and discharging cycles in response to the oscillating voltage, resulting in a phase shift between the input and output signals. Figure C illustrates the typical charging and discharging behavior of the capacitor. By analyzing the output curve (Figure S3C), the circuit’s characteristic time constant (τ = RC) was determined, allowing for the calculation of the MIM capacitor areal capacitance, which was found to be 1 nF/cm2, similar to the values reported using hBN as dielectric. , The functional behavior of an RC circuit is primarily determined by the input signal frequency and the output measurement location. By measuring the output across the resistor, both integrator and differentiator functionalities can be achieved. At low frequencies (Figure S3D), the capacitor’s high reactance results in slow charge accumulation, yielding an integrated output signal across the resistor (triangular waveform in response to a square input, Figure D). Conversely, they are at high frequencies (Figure S3E), the capacitor’s reduced reactance enables rapid charging and discharging, producing a differentiated output signal (voltage spikes at each rising and falling edge, Figure E). Consequently, by adjusting the input frequency and measuring the output across the resistor, the RC circuit can operate as either an integrator or a differentiator, showcasing its versatility in signal processing applications.

Finally, to demonstrate the practical application of our developed components, we integrated the MIMS diode and MIM capacitor to create a fully printed AC-to-DC converter. Figures A and S3A show an optical image and the circuit configuration that involves a series connection between the MIMS diode and the MIM capacitor. During the positive half-cycle of the AC input signal, the diode conducts, enabling the capacitor to charge and store energy. Conversely, during the negative half-cycle, the diode blocks the current, preventing significant capacitor discharge. Consequently, a relatively stable DC output voltage is generated, as reported in Figure F. The minimal ripple effect is due to the possible presence of parasitic shunt resistance in the circuit. This fundamental rectification and filtering process is essential for powering various electronic devices that require a stable DC power supply.

Conclusions

By using water-based and printable 2D material inks, we have demonstrated fully inkjet-printed diodes directly on paper substrates, with performance comparable to previously reported solution-processed rectifying devices on paper that, however, required high-pressure sputtering, thermal evaporation, or sublimation in high vacuum. In contrast, our fabrication process is conducted entirely at room temperature and atmospheric pressure, and the devices operate reliably under ambient conditions, making our approach highly attractive for sustainable electronics. High device performance was achieved through an MIMS architecture, which enables metrics comparable to those of fully printed diodes on both flexible and rigid substrates, demonstrating the robustness of our approach.

We went beyond demonstration of the individual device performance by integrating the MIMS diodes into fully printed 2D material-based analogue circuits, including RC filters, differentiators, integrators, and an AC-to-DC converter. All devices were fabricated at room temperature and ambient pressure, underscoring the potential of paper substrates as low-cost, sustainable alternatives to conventional polymer-based substrates.

This work paves the way for the development of scalable and environmentally friendly printed electronics made of 2D materials with performance suitable for biosignal monitoring, low-frequency energy harvesting, and real-time environmental sensing.

Methods

Graphene and MoS2 Printable Inks Made by LPE

Bulk graphite flakes (99.5% purity) were purchased from Graphexel Ltd. Molybdenum disulfide powder (<2 μm, 99% purity) and pyrene-1-sulfonic acid sodium (PS1, >97% purity) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Deionized water (resistivity ∼ 18.2 MΩ cm at room temperature) was prepared using a Millipore Simplicity 185 water purification system. Bulk graphite and MoS2 dispersions in DI water were subjected to bath sonication (Hilsonic, 300 W) for 120 h to exfoliate the material. To minimize nozzle blockages, flakes of appropriate lateral size were isolated through differential centrifugation. The resulting liquid underwent centrifugation (Sigma 1–14k refrigerated centrifuge) at 3500 rpm (903 g) for 20 min, and the supernatant was subsequently collected. Further centrifugation at 15,000 rpm (16,600 g) for 1 h was performed, and the precipitated material was redispersed in a water-based printable solvent developed in a previous work. The concentration of the 2D material was determined via UV–vis spectroscopy. The dispersion was diluted to a concentration of 2 mg/mL, as measured using a PerkinElmer l-900 UV–vis-NIR spectrophotometer, employing absorption coefficients of 2460 L mol–1 cm–1 (at 660 nm) and 3400 L mol–1 cm–1 (at 670 nm) for graphene and MoS2, respectively. No aggregation was observed during the material transfer or printing processes.

MoS2 Printable Ink Made by ECE

Bulk MoS2 crystals were purchased from Manchester Nanomaterials Ltd., while TPACl (98% purity) and PC (99% purity) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. ECE of layered MoS2 crystals was conducted in a two-electrode cell configuration. A MoS2 crystal, affixed to copper tape to ensure optimal contact, served as the cathode, and a platinum foil acted as the anode. Both electrodes were immersed in an electrolyte solution containing 5 mM TPACl dissolved in PC. An initial DC voltage of −1.5 V was applied to the MoS2 crystal for 10 min to facilitate wetting. Subsequently, the potential was increased to −10 V and maintained for 1 h. Subsequently, the delaminated flakes were subjected to bath sonication for 10 min to further exfoliate the material. The resulting MoS2 dispersion was centrifuged at 1500 rpm (166 g) for 5 min to remove unexfoliated material, leaving a supernatant containing thin MoS2 nanosheets.

To formulate the printable ink, the as-prepared MoS2 dispersion was filtered through a PTFE membrane (pore size 0.2 μm, Sigma-Aldrich) and washed with isopropyl alcohol (IPA) to remove residual solvent. The collected MoS2 powder was mixed with 300 mL of deionized water containing 0.3 g of PS1. The mixture underwent sonication at 600 W using a Hilsonic bath sonicator for 20 h. Subsequently, the MoS2 dispersion in water was centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min using a Sigma 1–14k refrigerated centrifuge to remove unexfoliated flakes. The remaining supernatant, containing thin MoS2 flakes, was subjected to two additional centrifugation steps at 15,000 rpm (16,602 g) for 1 h each to eliminate excess PS1 from the dispersion. After washing, the precipitate was redispersed in a water-based inkjet printable solvent prepared as previously reported.

Ink Characterization

A Varian Cary 5000 UV–vis spectrometer was used to measure the absorption spectrum of the dispersion, and the Beer–Lambert law was used to obtain the concentration of the inks by employing the following absorption coefficients: α660 = 2460 L g–1 m–1 for graphene and α672 = 3400 L g–1 m–1 for MoS2 produced by LPE and ECE.

Surface tension measurements were conducted using a Theta Lite 101 optical tensiometer employing the pendant drop method (Biolin Scientific). The pendant drop’s shape, influenced by the interplay of surface tension and gravity, was analyzed to determine surface tension using One Attension analysis software. Dynamic viscosity measurements were carried out by using a Brookfield DVII + Pro viscometer at various shear rates and room temperature. Contact angle measurements were conducted with a VCA Optima XE instrument at ambient temperature. A 2 μL droplet was formed at the end of a syringe and carefully deposited onto the substrate surface. The syringe was withdrawn, and the image of the static contact angle was taken within 3 s of liquid deposition by a charge-coupled device camera. The contact angle was calculated using vendor-supplied software.

Films Characterization

The samples for AFM, Raman, PL and TEM characterizations were prepared by drop-casting the supernatant onto precleaned SiO2/Si substrates followed by annealing at 300 °C for 1 h under vacuum.

AFM measurements were taken with a Bruker Multimode 8 Atomic Force Microscope operating in PeakForce Tapping mode with ScanAsyst-Air cantilevers employed for all measurements. Scan sizes were adjusted to match the dimensions of the nanosheets (approximately 5 μm2), and a scan rate of 0.4 Hz with 1024 samples per line was used. Line profiles were analyzed to determine the apparent thickness of different nanosheet regions.

A Renishaw InVia Raman spectrometer was employed for both Raman and PL measurements. The laser has an excitation wavelength of 514.5 nm, and it is focused on the sample using a 100× (NA = 0.85) objective lens with a laser power of 0.3 mW. For the PL measurements, a laser with an excitation wavelength of 532 nm with a power of 0.3 mW was used to record the spectra from 10 individual MoS2 nanosheets within the energy range of 1.7–2.1 eV. Lorentzian functions were used to fit to the A and B peaks to determine their positions.

High-resolution TEM images were acquired on a JEOL 2100-F microscope with a field-emission gun operated at 200 kV accelerating voltage, providing direct images of the atomic structure.

Devices Fabrication

A Dimatix inkjet printer (DMP-2800, FujiFilm) equipped with a 10 pL cartridge was used. Inks were printed onto coated technical paper (PEL P60, Printed Electronics Ltd.), characterized by a microporous surface treatment, designed to wick away the carrier solvent of the ink while allowing for uniform deposition. A drop spacing of 25 μm was used, and the platen temperature was maintained at 40 °C to promote rapid solvent evaporation. The devices on rigid substrates were printed on doped n-type Si (100) wafers (∼10 Ω cm) with a 300 nm thermally grown SiO2 layer (Siegert Wafer). Prior to printing, the substrates were cleaned by sonication in acetone and isopropanol for 5 min each, followed by 20 s of oxygen plasma treatment to increase surface wettability.

Printed Film Characterization

The thickness and roughness of the printed films were determined by using a Bruker Dektak XT surface profiler. Measurement parameters included a stylus radius of 12.5 μm, a stylus force of 2 mg, a scan speed of 100 μm/s, and a scan resolution of 0.33 μm. Scanning electron microscopy (Zeiss Gemini Ultra55) was employed to collect cross-sectional images of the film.

Electrical Characterization

DC electrical measurements were performed with a Janis probe station (Janis ST-500 probe station) equipped with four nanoprobes connected to a Keithley 4200 SCS (semiconductor characterization system), working as a source-measurement unit with current sensitivity better than 1 pA. AC measurements were performed using an RS PRO AFG21225 function generator as the voltage source and a RS PRO RSDS 1052DL+ digital oscilloscope for signal acquisition. To prevent loading effects, given the high output impedance of the printed circuits, a high-input-impedance buffer stage (≥10 GΩ) was inserted between the device and the oscilloscope.
