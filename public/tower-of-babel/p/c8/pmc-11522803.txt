Catheterization: An Unconventional Diagnostic Route for Obstructive Sleep Apnea
Authors: Kirrat Ahmad, Rishi Thakkar, Sabiha Sultana, Neha Mehta, Jyotpal Singh, Maria Gagarinova, Chase J Ellingson, Payam Dehghani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 55-year-old woman presented with an incidental finding of significant respiratory variation in mean right atrial pressure during cardiac catheterization. Later, she was evaluated and diagnosed with sleep apnea. A significant increase in right atrial pressure with respiration should raise awareness for sleep apnea.Key Words: obstructive sleep apnea, respiratory variation, right atrial pressure, right heart catheterization

History of Presentation

A 55-year-old woman presented to the local hospital with significant fatigue, shortness of breath, pedal edema, and chest pain, which fit NYHA functional classification III to IV. Laboratory work results, including complete blood count, liver and renal panel, and blood glucose, were all within normal values and not a contributory factor to the patient’s symptoms; thus, they were not included. She underwent a computerized tomography scan, transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE), and transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE). Mitral valve stenosis was suggested by the TTE and TEE results. During right heart catheterization (RHC), the patient’s right atrial pressure (RAP) varied significantly (15-45 mm Hg) with respiration.

Past Medical History

The patient has a history of atrial fibrillation, anxiety, smoking, obesity, hypothyroidism, irritable bowel syndrome, and gastroesophageal reflux disease.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included heart failure, pulmonary embolism, mitral valve stenosis, and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA).

Investigations

Initial TTE examination showed normal left ventricular (LV) systolic function and moderate mitral valve stenosis (valve area: 1.6 cm2), with features suggestive of rheumatic disease. A TEE was then performed to determine the patient’s candidacy for valvuloplasty. TEE results showed a mean mitral valve gradient of 10 mm Hg, an area of 1.9 cm2, and a Wilkins score of 8. (A score of 2 was given for each of the following 4 components: leaflet mobility, valve thickness, subvalvular thickening, and valvular calcification.) The patient was in sinus rhythm, had a rheumatic mitral valve, mild aortic regurgitation, and no evidence of pulmonary hypertension. Based on these findings, a mitral valvuloplasty was not recommended, and invasive hemodynamic testing was performed, given the poor correlation between the severity of the patient’s symptoms and moderate mitral valve stenosis.

A mitral valve gradient of 9 mm Hg was obtained on the simultaneous LV and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure assessment. A mitral valve area of 1.2 cm2 was calculated using Gorlin’s formula (Figure 1A). In the LV/pulmonary capillary wedge pressure right atrial tracing, a significant variation in RAP (Δ30 mm Hg) was noted during respiration while the patient was asleep (Figure 1B). Additionally, LV end-diastolic pressure was elevated at 28 mm Hg, moderate mitral valve regurgitation was observed, and there was no evidence of coronary artery disease.

Management

Hemodynamics from RHC confirmed that the patient did not have severe mitral valve stenosis. The 30–mm Hg variation in mean RAP with respiration prompted a referral to respirology for evaluation of OSA. A level 3 sleep study was completed, which confirmed the suspected diagnosis. The patient was initially trialed on continuous positive airway pressure ventilation, which helped prevent obstructive apneic episodes related to the arousals and desaturation but not central events. Thus, the patient was advanced to bilevel positive airway pressure ventilation, as a result of which the apnea-hypopnea index reduced from 80 to 2.4 (Figure 1C), and all apneic episodes were successfully managed.

Discussion

Cardiac catheterization is considered the most accurate diagnostic measure of cardiovascular hemodynamics.1 Diagnostic accuracy of noninvasive testing (history and echocardiogram) was reportedly 86% in comparison to catheterization in patients with valvular heart disease, cardiomyopathy, congenital heart disease, and coronary heart disease, implying that an alternative diagnosis occurred in 14% of the cases following catheterization.1 Additionally, Alpert et al1 found that 3% of management plans were incorrect based on noninvasive testing alone. For our patient, a diagnosis of mitral valve stenosis was suspected based on noninvasive testing alone; however, given the discrepant findings between the patient’s symptoms and imaging findings, cardiac catheterization and sleep study were performed, which confirmed an alternative diagnosis of OSA. This is an important phenomenon for cardiologists to be aware of, because catheterization may yield an alternative diagnosis and independently alter management plans.

Our case is the first documented finding, to our knowledge, of significant variation in RAP with respiration during catheterization, leading to a diagnosis of OSA. Patients with OSA enter a state of hypoxia during obstructive episodes, triggering their sympathetic nervous system to become overactive, thus affecting blood pressure, heart rate, and cardiac contractility.2, 3, 4 During apneic episodes, a sharp increase in negative intrathoracic pressure (above baseline) develops secondary to the inability to inhale air through a narrowed airway, consequently increasing venous return to the heart. A study conducted by Chi et al5 evaluated the effects of OSA on jugular venous hemodynamics5 and found that during resting and waking states, the outflow from the jugular vein is markedly increased in patients with OSA. With an increased outflow from the venous system into the heart, an increase in central venous pressure, synonymous with RAP, occurs.5 Simulation of OSA in awake patients by using the Mueller maneuver (inhalation against an obstructed airway) to measure interatrial pressure gradients results in a 7.0 ± 4.3–mm Hg increase as compared to 0.1 ± 1.4 mm Hg seen during baseline without Mueller maneuver.6 However, this 7.0 ± 4.3–mm Hg increase by Mueller maneuver 6 does not compare to the significant variation in RAP of 30 mm Hg that was observed with respiration in our case. This study also found that RAP increases more than left atrial pressure, which can be attributed to an initial onset of negative intrathoracic pressure.6

Only one study, by Kolluri et al,7 has investigated the relationship between increased central venous pressure (synonymous with RAP) and the prevalence of OSA, with no association found. However, Kolluri et al7 used an echocardiogram to measure RAP during waking states, and the findings are therefore not an accurate reflection of the variations in RAP that can occur with respiration in patients with OSA. Documented elevations in RAP above the normal range from 1 to 7 mm Hg have been seen in patients with heart failure, cardiac tamponade, and pulmonary embolisms.8,9 However, our case provides the first evidence of elevation in RAP in a patient with OSA and suggests that this rise in RAP of 30 mm Hg, as shown by RHC, is influenced by respiratory variations.

Follow-Up

At the 6-month follow-up, the patient’s NYHA functional classification decreased to NYHA functional class I to II, with significant improvement in quality of life.

Conclusions

This case study demonstrates that incidental findings of significant respiratory variation in RAP should raise suspicion for OSA. Cardiologists should always be observant and seek alternative diagnoses during hemodynamic studies in the right clinical settings.
