Tailored management of a melanoma patient with bleeding brain metastases and deep vein thrombosis with thrombectomy
Authors: Antonella Viterbo, Filippo De Angelis, Corrado Ceccarelli, Luca Arezzini, Davide Del Roscio, Ilaria Pastina, Massimo Pieraccini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCase presentationWe describe a 62-year-old man with disseminated melanoma (initially staged pT2aN0M0, AJCC stage IB, 8th edition AJCC) who developed multiple brain metastases with hemorrhagic transformation, pulmonary embolism, retrobulbar progression with eyeball herniation, and deep vein thrombosis (DVT). After cerebral edema improved with corticosteroid tapering and the patient became asymptomatic, he started combination immunotherapy with ipilimumab (3 mg/kg q3w × 4) and nivolumab (1 mg/kg q3w × 4). Despite initial benefit, progression led to radiotherapy for retrobulbar lesion and subsequent targeted therapy with encorafenib (450 mg daily) and binimetinib (45 mg twice daily). Shortly after, the patient developed symptomatic left-leg DVT extending to the femoral vein with segmental pulmonary embolism. Given the contraindication to full anticoagulation, mechanical thrombectomy with the Indigo Aspiration System was performed, followed by prophylactic LMWH at reduced therapeutic dose (enoxaparin 1 mg/kg once daily due to recent thrombectomy and hemorrhagic risk from bleeding brain metastases). During targeted therapy, the patient experienced partial regression of brain, liver, and peritoneal metastases, although intracranial hemorrhage recurred later.ConclusionsThis case highlights the value of a multidisciplinary, tailored approach integrating immunotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and mechanical thrombectomy with no DVT recurrence observed following thrombectomy at 6-month follow-up.

Introduction

The incidence of melanoma is rising globally, particularly in younger individuals. In Italy, it is the third most common cancer among people under 49 years old, while Australia and New Zealand record the highest rates worldwide due to intense UV exposure in fair-skinned populations. In the United States, melanoma is the fifth most common cancer [1–3].

Brain metastases develop in approximately 40%–50% of advanced melanoma cases, severely impacting survival. When associated with venous thromboembolism (VTE), prognosis drops to 2–3 months [4]. Although neurosurgery, stereotactic radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and BRAF/MEK inhibitors have improved outcomes [5], the optimal sequence of therapies remains debated.

VTE, including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is the second leading cause of death in cancer patients [6]. Tumors promote thrombosis by inducing stasis, vascular injury, and hypercoagulability [7]. Immunotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy may further increase risk. Standard management involves anticoagulation, but in patients with brain metastases this may provoke fatal hemorrhage.

Risk assessment models (RAMs), such as the Khorana and HAS-BLED scores, can help stratify bleeding and thrombotic risk but remain insufficient in oncology patients with brain metastases [8]. In selected patients, mechanical thrombectomy offers an alternative, physically removing thrombi while limiting systemic anticoagulation [9]. Although evidence is limited, retrospective studies suggest this approach is safe in cancer patients [10].

Here we present the case of a patient with metastatic melanoma, complicated by hemorrhagic brain metastases, orbital involvement, and DVT, managed successfully with immunotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and mechanical thrombectomy with no perioperative complications.

Case report

A 62-year-old man presented to the emergency department with confusion and bradylalia. Non-contrast CT revealed multiple hyperdense lesions, including one with hemorrhagic features. The patient had a history of cutaneous melanoma diagnosed 4 years earlier (pT2aN0M0, AJCC stage IB, 8th edition AJCC).

A total-body CT showed a subsegmental pulmonary embolism and metastases in the lung, liver, peritoneum, and soft tissue (Figure 1A–C). Brain MRI confirmed multiple metastases, including a bleeding lesion in the brainstem (Figure 2A). Laboratory tests showed hemoglobin 12.8 g/dL, platelets 220 × 109/L, INR 1.0, LDH 450 U/L, and elevated D-dimer 2.5 mg/L. ECOG performance status was 1.

Corticosteroid therapy (dexamethasone 16 mg daily) was initiated for cerebral edema. After tapering steroids and once the patient became asymptomatic, combination immunotherapy with ipilimumab (3 mg/kg q3w × 4) and nivolumab (1 mg/kg q3w × 4) was started.

Initial benefit was observed, including resolution of PE and partial regression of brain and liver lesions. However, after 4 cycles MRI revealed progression of a retrobulbar metastasis causing severe exophthalmos and herniation of the left eyeball (Figure 2B). Immunotherapy was discontinued due to the disease progression and grade 3 diarrhea, managed with methylprednisolone (1 mg/kg).

The orbital lesion was treated with stereotactic radiotherapy (25 Gy in 5 fractions, Elekta Synergy, VMAT technique), resulting in rapid improvement of diplopia and exophthalmos.

Following progression, second-line targeted therapy with encorafenib (450 mg daily) and binimetinib (45 mg twice daily) was initiated. Two weeks later, the patient presented with painful swelling of the left leg. Doppler ultrasound confirmed DVT from the superficial femoral to the popliteal vein; CT also revealed a segmental PE.

Given the bleeding risk from brain metastases, the multidisciplinary team opted for catheter mechanical thrombectomy (Indigo Aspiration System) without perioperative complications, including no worsening of intracranial hemorrhage or new bleeding events post-procedure. Phlebography confirmed extensive thrombosis; thrombectomy restored venous flow with residual partial obstruction in a 7 cm femoral segment (Figure 3A–D). No DVT recurrence was observed at 6-month follow-up (Figure 3E). Prophylactic LMWH (enoxaparin 1 mg/kg once daily due to recent thrombectomy and hemorrhagic risk from bleeding brain metastases) was administered.

The patient’s oncologic treatment produced partial regression of brain, liver, and peritoneal metastases (Figure 1D–F), with clinical improvement in appetite, weight, and activity. Subsequently, a frontal brain hemorrhage occurred, requiring discontinuation of LMWH. Symptoms resolved with conservative care.

After one month of targeted therapy, MRI showed cystic degeneration of several metastases and partial resorption of hemorrhage (Figure 2C). Despite the presence of hemorrhagic brain metastases associated in the literature with poor median survival [4,5], this patient survived 9 months after metastatic diagnosis, experiencing significant periods of well-being. Unfortunately he ultimately developed hemiparesis and aphasia from recurrent hemorrhage in a degenerated cystic lesion, leading to clinical decline and death (Figure 2D).

Discussion

This case underscores the complexity of managing advanced melanoma with concurrent hemorrhagic brain metastases and VTE. Treatment sequencing was tailored to clinical evolution: immunotherapy initiated after corticosteroid taper and recovery from symptoms, radiotherapy for sight-threatening orbital progression, and targeted therapy upon disease progression.

Mechanical thrombectomy was selected to manage DVT without complications, as anticoagulation would have further increased the risk of intracranial bleeding in a patient who had already experienced hemorrhage. The procedure provided rapid symptom relief and allowed only minimal anticoagulation, effectively preventing DVT recurrence during follow-up.

Bleeding risk stratification remains a critical challenge. While Khorana and HAS-BLED scores provide frameworks, they are inadequate in patients with CNS involvement [8]. Our case supports the need for oncology-specific RAMs integrating tumor histology, hemorrhagic events, and treatment exposures.

Alternative strategies such as full-dose anticoagulation, IVC filters, or supportive care were considered but avoided due to the high risk of cerebral bleeding, increased venous pressure, and recurrent thrombosis, respectively [11]. Retrospective data confirm that thrombectomy is a safe and effective option in oncology patients [10].

Histology confirmed melanoma metastases with immunohistochemistry positive for S100, HMB45, and Melan-A. Representative histological images were unavailable due to archival limitations.

Despite poor prognosis associated with hemorrhagic brain metastases, survival was extended by 9 months, with preserved quality of life for much of this time. This highlights the importance of a multidisciplinary approach combining systemic, local, and endovascular therapies.

Conclusions

This case demonstrates that in metastatic melanoma with hemorrhagic brain metastases and VTE, a multidisciplinary and tailored approach can prolong survival and maintain quality of life. Mechanical thrombectomy represents a valuable option when anticoagulation is unsafe, supporting integration of endovascular interventions in oncologic care.
