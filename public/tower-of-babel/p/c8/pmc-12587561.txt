Association between adiposity indices and incident albuminuria: Results from the Brazilian Longitudinal Study of Adult Health (ELSA-Brasil)
Authors: Wagner do Nascimento Carvalho, Gustavo Velasquez-Melendez, Alexandra Dias Moreira, Maria del Carmen Bisi Molina, Luana Giatti, Sandhi M Barreto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlbuminuria is an early indicator of kidney damage, and obesity is one of its main risk factors. Increased adiposity has been shown to be associated with albuminuria. Therefore, the aim of this study was to analyse the association between adiposity indices (BMI, WC, WHtR and WHR) and the incidence of albuminuria in Brazilian adults.MethodsA total of 12,399 participants from the ELSA-Brasil cohort baseline (2008–2010) who were free of albuminuria and attended visit 2 of the study (2012–2014) were included in this study. The outcome was albuminuria (urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥ 30 mg/g), and the adiposity indices were the exposure of interest. Logistic regression models were used to estimate the magnitude of the associations, adjusting for covariates.ResultsThe mean age was 51.70 (± 8.87) years, and the majority were female (54.53%). The incidence of albuminuria was 2.82% during a mean follow-up period of 3.84 (± 0.57) years. The mean BMI, WC, WHtR and WHR adiposity indices were: 26.85 (± 4.61) kg/m², 90.74 (± 12.51) cm, 55.02 (± 7.39) % and 89.25 (± 8.60) %, respectively. A one unit increase in adiposity indices was associated with an increase of one to three percent of odds the albuminuria, these associations remained significant even after adjusting for covariates.ConclusionsIncreased central and global adiposity indices are associated with incident albuminuria. It is vital to implement health interventions to prevent increased adiposity, given that it is a predictor of albuminuria.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04545-x.Keywords: Adiposity, Obesity, Adiposity indices, Albuminuria, Kidney disease

Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a public health problem strongly associated with aging. The global number of deaths attributed to CKD increased from 0.6 per million people (95% CI: 0.56–0.63) in 1990 to 1.4 (95% CI: 1.31–1.52) per million people in 2019. The age-standardized mortality rate for CKD among all causes of death moved from 15th place in 1990 to 10th place in 2019 [1].

In Brazil, the prevalence of CKD at baseline (2008–2010) in a multicenter study of around 15,000 civil servants aged 35–74 years old was 8.9% [2], close to the worldwide prevalence of this disease estimated by the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study worldwide (P = 9.1%; 95% CI: 8.5% − 9.8%) in 2017 [3]. However, individuals with CKD may require renal replacement therapy, which also has been increased in prevalence worldwide [4].

Albuminuria is well-established biomarker of glomerular permeability dysfunction and endothelial damage. As such, it is associated with the progression of kidney diseases [5, 6]. Among the risk factors for albuminuria and CKD is the increase in body adiposity [6–8] and obesity-related metabolic disorders such as diabetes mellitus, arterial hypertension, and metabolic syndrome [8], even though a high BMI is associated with improved survival in patients with end-stage CKD [9], a phenomenon referred to as the “obesity paradox” [10]. Obesity is currently considered a global public health problem, evidenced by its steady increase in prevalence [11].

Cross-sectional data from ELSA-Brasil previously identified associations between adiposity and albuminuria. A higher waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) was associated with higher odds of albuminuria (OR: 1.49; 95% CI: 1.20–1.86), as well as BMI, waist circumference (WC), and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) [12]. However, due to the cross-sectional design, temporal relationships couldn’t be determined.

A recent meta-analysis of 57 studies, 44 studies on albuminuria and 13 studies on proteinuria, with a total sample size of 10,603,067 participants, showed that every component of metabolic syndrome, including obesity, increased the risk of albuminuria, although the total impact of metabolic syndrome on proteinuria was more remarkable than each component separately, with a dose-response association between the number of metabolic syndrome components and albuminuria risk [13].

Given that most longitudinal studies exploring this relationship have focused on European, North American, and Asian populations [14–16], and many didn’t examine different anthropometric indices [6, 17, 18], this study aims to investigate the association between adiposity indices and incident albuminuria in Brazilian adults’ individuals. We hypothesize that higher adiposity indices are associated with an increased odds of albuminuria incidence.

Methods

Study design

Longitudinal study using data from the multicenter cohort ELSA-Brasil (Longitudinal Study of Adult Health). The study participants were civil servants from teaching and research institutions from six Brazilian capital cities (Belo Horizonte, Porto Alegre, Rio de Janeiro, Salvador, São Paulo and Vitória) [19, 20].

The ELSA-Brasil data collection protocol involves interviews, anthropometric measurements, laboratory, imaging tests and storage of biological material. The baseline (visit 01) of the study took place between 2008 and 2010, since then the research subjects have been contacted annually by telephone to collect data on their health status, and every four years they are invited to undergo face-to-face evaluations at the reference research centers to monitor their health status and outcomes [20].

Study sample

The study’s initial sample comprises participants in the ELSA-Brasil cohort, which has at baseline (visit 1, 2008–2010) 15,105 individuals aged between 35 and 74, who were subsequently followed up until the second visit of the study (visit 2, 2012–2014). Of the initial sample cohort, 601 individuals were ineligible for the study because they had prevalent albuminuria, urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR ≥ 30 mg/g), at visit 1. The study did not include 223 individuals who died during the period, 868 individuals who didn’t attend at visit 2 (loss to follow-up), 373 individuals who had missing uACR data at visit 1 and 641 individuals who had missing uACR data at visit 2. A total of 12,399 individuals were eligible for the study and composed the final analytical sample. Figure 1 shows the sample selection flowchart for this study.

Exposure variables

The anthropometric indices were obtained at the first visit of the study and are described below.

Height was measured with the individual barefoot, standing up straight, below the horizontal rod of a stadiometer scale 0.1 cm brand SECA-SE-216 ®, and with their back against the vertical scale of the equipment. Weight was measured on an empty stomach with the individual barefoot using a previously calibrated digital scale Toledo 200 Kg ®. Waist circumference was measured with the waist region free of clothing and using an anthropometric tape brand Mabis model Gulick ®, which was positioned on an equidistant line between the lower margin of the costal arch and the iliac crest. Hip circumference was measured using the same anthropometric tape mentioned above; after asking the individual to stand with their feet together, the tape was positioned over the maximum protrusion of the gluteal muscles (hip) [21].

Using anthropometric measurements of weight, height, waist circumference (WC) and hip circumference, adiposity indices were created: BMI (weight / height², presented in Kg/m²), WC itself in cm, WHtR (waist circumference in cm / height in cm) and WHR (waist circumference in cm / hip circumference in cm) [22, 23].

Outcomes

The study’s outcome variable was incident albuminuria defined as uACR ≥ 30 mg/g [6]. The uACR was obtained from 12-hour nocturnal urine samples collected at the second study visit. The nephelometric method used to calculate urine albumin was the Dade Behring® Nephelometer BNII automated device, and the enzymatic colorimetric method (Jaffé without deproteinization) used to calculate urine creatinine was the Siemens® ADVIAâ 1200 automated device [24]. The incident albuminuria was estimated by uACR categorized as yes (1 = uACR ≥ 30 mg/g) and no (0 = uACR < 30 mg/g) [6].

Covariates

Sociodemographic: age (years), sex (male ─ reference or female), race/color (white ─ reference, brown, black, yellow and indigenous), education (university ─ reference, high school and elementary school) [25].

Health related behaviors: leisure-time physical activity assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [individuals who didn’t perform the minimum amount ≥ 10 min/week of physical activity were defined as practicing low intensity (reference) physical activity, those who reported < 150 min/week of moderate physical activity or < 75 min/week of vigorous physical activity were defined as practicing predominantly moderate-intensity physical activity, and those who practiced ≥ 150 min/week of moderate physical activity and/or ≥ 75 min/week of vigorous physical activity were defined as practicing predominantly high-intensity physical activity], smoking (never smoked ─ reference, ex-smoker, smoker), excessive alcohol consumption (present when consumption in men ≥ 210 g alcohol/week and women ≥ 140 g alcohol/week) [25].

Health (comorbidities) and laboratory history: diabetes mellitus (considered present if the disease was previously diagnosed and/or one of the following criteria was present: use of medication to treat diabetes, fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, glucose tolerance test ≥ 200 mg/dL, glycated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5%), arterial hypertension (considered present if blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg measured using an automatic Omron device model HEM–705 CP ® and/or use of antihypertensive medication), self-reported cardiovascular disease (present when history of acute myocardial infarction and/or, angina and/or, heart failure and/or, previous heart surgery) at the first visit of the study [25] and triglycerides (mg/dL) [26].

All adjustment covariates were considered at Visit 1 of the study.

Statistical analysis

Initially, a descriptive analysis of sociodemographic and clinical covariates was carried out, stratified by the presence or absence of albuminuria. Absolute and relative frequencies were calculated for categorical covariates and the mean and 95% confidence intervals for continuous covariates. The cumulative incidence of albuminuria was estimated during the period analyzed. Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between means and Pearson’s chi-square test to verify the hypothesis of association between categorical covariates.

A correlation analysis between adiposity indices was performed and demonstrated that they are highly correlated (Supplementary Table 1). Binary logistic regression was performed to estimate the association and the magnitudes of associations between each of the adiposity indices (BMI, WC, WHtR and WHR) and albuminuria were estimated by the Odds Ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence intervals.

The models were adjusted for potential confounding variables, as follows: model 1 unadjusted; model 2 adjusted for age, sex, race/color and schooling; model 3 adjusted by model 2 + excessive alcohol consumption and physical activity; model 4 adjusted by model 3 + arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, history of cardiovascular disease and triglycerides.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted considering the recruitment center as an adjustment variable and excluding 472 individuals with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² at the first study visit, but didn’t change in the significance of the adjusted models (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3, respectively).

The results of the hypothesis tests using maximum likelihood estimators with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The Stata software version 14.0 (Stata Corporation, College Station, TX, USA) was used to analyze the data.

Ethics statement

The ELSA-Brasil study was approved by the Research Ethics Committees of the participating institutions and by the National Health Council (CONEP), filed under number 976/2006. Eligible individuals received written and oral information about the study and were given the freedom to decide whether to participate in the study. Individuals who agreed to participate in the study were informed that their participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any time. All participants understood the aims of the study, and they all agreed to participate in the study and signed the informed consent form. The study complies with ethical precepts, in accordance with Declaration of Helsinki’s ethical principles and the recommendations for research with human beings in Brazil presented in Resolution 466/2012 of the National Health Council (CONEP).

Discussion

The results of this multicenter and multi-racial cohort of Brazilian civil service show that different adiposity indices were associated with a greater risk of albuminuria in an average of 3.84 years of follow-up. The coefficients of association were attenuated after adjusting confounding covariates but maintained significance. A one unit increase in adiposity indices is associated with an increase of one to three percent of odds the albuminuria in this study’s sample.

Although associations between adiposity markers and albuminuria have been extensively studied in high-income settings, there is a scarcity of evidence from middle-income countries like Brazil, particularly using a big cohort with standardized laboratory measures. The Brazilian population is characterized by an ethnic admixture, socioeconomic disparities, and dietary transitions — that may contribute to modulate the impact of adiposity on kidney outcomes. In this context, visceral adiposity may exert a greater effect on metabolic and renal risk than generalized adiposity (as measured by BMI). Additionally, the burden of poorly controlled diabetes mellitus, high sodium intake, specificities justify the need for local data to guide risk stratification and preventive strategies in settings with limited health resources.

Associations between adiposity indices and albuminuria were also demonstrated in a cross-sectional study carried out in the European sample of 3,749 individuals aged between 20 and 81 to assess the association between adiposity indices and albuminuria found that individuals with high or low WC had up to a 52% increased chance of albuminuria compared to the average WC quintile (OR: 1.52; 95% CI: 1.16–2.00). High WHtR was associated with an increased chance of albuminuria of up to 33% (OR: 1.33; 95% CI: 1.03–1.74). In the same study, the associations between BMI and albuminuria weren’t significant. The variables used to adjust the models in this study were age, sex, diabetes mellitus and arterial hypertension [27]. These results for BMI differ from the study we conducted, because we found a direct proportional relationship between increased adiposity and incident albuminuria regardless of the adiposity indices assessed.

We identify that longitudinal studies with healthy or relatively healthy populations are scarce. However, the results of this study have been confirmed by other longitudinal studies, as described below. For example, an Asian study with 1,432 adults aged 40 or over, with an average follow-up time of 2.8 years, an albuminuria cumulative incidence of 4.7% and an incidence rate of 16.6/1,000 person-years, found that individuals who had central obesity (increased WC) had a 112% increased risk of albuminuria (RR: 2.12, 95% CI: 1.01–4.44) compared to participants without central obesity after adjusting for sex, age, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity blood pressure, and glycemia [28].

In addition to the studies presented above, a systematic review and meta-analysis carried out with data from 39 cohorts, covering 630,677 individuals aged between 35 and 76 years, with an average follow-up time of 6.8 years, found that obesity, assessed by BMI, increased the risk of developing albuminuria (RR: 1.51, 95% CI: 1.36–1.67). Each unit increase in BMI was also associated with a 2% increase in the risk of albuminuria (RR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.04) [17].

We found that different adiposity indices, which reflect visceral and global fat, are associated with a greater risk of albuminuria. Potential biological mechanisms explaining the associations between adiposity indices and albuminuria include excess adipose tissue, which promotes low-grade inflammation and contributes to endothelial dysfunction and glomerular damage [29]; decreased circulating adiponectin (a lipolytic hormone that acts to decrease insulin resistance) [30]; and increased insulin resistance, which contributes to dysregulation of the renal hemodynamic system, leading to mesangial expansion of the glomeruli, glomerular hypertrophy, and renal fibrosis. All these processes contribute to the development of albuminuria and the progression of kidney disease [31]. On the other hand, obesity can also activate mechanisms related to glomerular capillary hyperfiltration to preserve glomerular filtration in the short term, but in the long term, it leads to kidney damage, such as albuminuria, and the progression of kidney disease [32].

The strengths of this study include: the sample studied consisted of middle-aged adults living in a middle-income country with a very admixed population and differing health conditions. The study used several important confounding covariates as adjustment variables. Different adiposity indices reflecting global and central adiposity levels were analyzed, and all were associated with the incidence of albuminuria. In addition, all variables and covariates in the study were measured in a standardized manner and using calibrated devices.

The limitations of this study were that, despite being a large sample, the cohort isn’t a representative of the Brazilian population, which may underestimate the incidence and strength of association, the follow-up time was relatively short and varied between participants, with an average of 3.84 (± 0.57) years. The albuminuria may be transient due to factors such as intense physical exercise, fever, infections or dehydration, but unfortunately in this study it was only possible to characterize it with albuminuria being characterized based on isolated uACR measurement at both visit 1 and visit 2. However, even considering the short time interval, an independent association was found between adiposity indices and the incidence of albuminuria. Given the limited number of time points of uACR measurement we suggest future studies with longer follow-up to investigate the trajectory of uACR levels and their determinants. Besides, we believe that further longitudinal studies should investigate the role of inflammatory markers in obesity and their association with albuminuria in a sample with a longer follow-up period.

Conclusions

The increase in different indices of central and global adiposity (BMI, WC, WHtR and WHR) was associated in this study with an increase in the chances of incident albuminuria, and the results remained statistically significant regardless of adjustments for potentially confounding covariates. It is important to establish governmental health promotion strategies aimed to avoid overweight and obesity in the populations, since these conditions have been shown to be predictors of albuminuria and kidney disease.
