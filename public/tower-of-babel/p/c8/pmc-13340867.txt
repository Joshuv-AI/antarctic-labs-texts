Ethanol-assisted fabrication of Casein-Ritonavir amorphous dispersions
Authors: Deepika Sharma, Federico M Harte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe study investigates the impact of ethanol content and temperature on the formation of casein–ritonavir amorphous dispersions, aiming to enhance the drug’s effective solubility and delivery. Ritonavir, a poorly water-soluble crystalline antiviral medication, was combined with casein in aqueous ethanol solutions ranging from 0 to 60 % v/v ethanol and 60°C. Solubility tests revealed a sharp increase in ritonavir solubility in 50% ethanol, highlighting that reduced solvent polarity facilitates drug dispersion. After solvent removal, thermal analysis confirmed ritonavir-casein interaction leading to the formation of ritonavir amorphous dispersions. Microstructural analysis revealed a significant decrease in crystalline peaks and spectral shifts in the amide regions, particularly in ethanol-treated samples, indicating changes in the local microenvironment and noncovalent molecular associations of ritonavir within the casein matrix. Zeta potential measurements showed a consistent increase in negative surface charge (−45 mV to −60 mV) with increasing ethanol content during complex fabrication, indicating changes in surface charge characteristics and electrostatic dispersion stability. Therefore, these findings support the formation of stable, amorphous casein–ritonavir complexes with improved solubility and potential for oral delivery applications.Keywords: Casein, Ritonavir, Ethanol-assisted encapsulation, Hydrophobic drug delivery

Introduction

Ritonavir is a protease inhibitor commonly used as an antiretroviral, alone or in combination with other active pharmaceutical ingredients (API), against HIV (Chandwani & Shuter, 2008), hepatitis C (Klibanov, Gale, & Santevecchi, 2015), and COVID-19 (Cao et al., 2020). Despite its medical importance, ritonavir face limitation due to crystallization leading to poor dispersibility in water and low oral bioavailability (Savjani, Gajjar, & Savjani, 2012). Various formulation strategies, including solid dispersions, carrier-assisted dispersion systems, and lipid-based carriers, have been explored to enhance the effective solubility/dispersibility and bioavailability of poorly water-soluble drugs (Choi, et al., 2021; Shin, Cho, Ud Din, Jin, & Choi, 2024; Suk Kim, et al., 2022). For ritonavir, (Deshmukh & Kulkarni, 2014) developed an amorphous solid self-microemulsifying drug delivery system to enhance its solubility in water and 0.1 N HCl. Similarly, (Trasi, Bhujbal, Zhou, & Taylor, 2019) demonstrated that solvent granulation onto a mixture of lactose/microcrystalline cellulose produced amorphous solid dispersions of ritonavir, which exhibited dissolution comparable to that of the marketed Aluvia™ formulation. However, most approaches involve the use of synthetic excipients, aggressive solvents (e.g., ethanol), or complex processing steps that raise health concerns.

Casein, a milk-derived phosphoprotein, offers an attractive natural alternative for drug encapsulation due to its biocompatibility, GRAS status, and amphiphilic nature. In particular, caseinates (sodium-, potassium-), a partially aggregated, low-in-calcium form of casein proteins, have been widely proposed as stabilizers of hydrophobic compounds for food and pharmaceutical applications (Livney, 2010). Caseinates are flexible proteins in solution, exposing both hydrophobic regions and polar functional groups (e.g., –NH2, –OH, –COOH) that facilitate non-covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic binding with poorly water-soluble drugs (Sadiq, Gill, & Chandrapala, 2021).

Previous studies have demonstrated that casein can encapsulate hydrophobic actives, such as curcumin, flavonoids, and pheophorbide (Kim, et al., 2024; Li, Fokkink, Ni, & Kleijn, 2019; Cheema, Hristov, & Harte, 2017; Sahu, Kasoju, & Bora, 2008). Previous studies indicate that ritonavir binds to casein mainly when processed with high-pressure homogenization (Corzo-Martínez, Mohan, Dunlap, & Harte, 2015). While protein–drug interactions have been studied in aqueous systems, the impact of ethanol–water mixtures on enhancing ritonavir dispersibility by influencing protein structure remains unexplored. Ethanol is known to increase the solubility of hydrophobic drugs like ritonavir (Xie, Liu, Li, Yang, & He, 2024), while also inducing partial unfolding and/or quaternary structure dissociation in milk proteins at relatively high temperature, such as whey and casein, which may enhance drug binding by exposing hydrophobic and hydrogen-bonding domains (Feng, Ma, Kong, Chen, & Liu, 2021; Sharma, Ziegler, & Harte, 2024).

In this study, we investigate the preparation of caseinate–ritonavir complexes using various ethanol–water solvent systems and evaluate their structural behavior through thermal, structural, and molecular characterization after solvent removal. The long-term aim is to develop a simple, infant-friendly method to enhance the amorphization and dispersion of ritonavir using casein as a natural dispersant.

Experimental

Materials

Acid casein (Product No. C7078; CAS No. 9000-71-9; pH 4.6) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), and ritonavir (≥ 98% purity by HPLC; CAS No. 155213-67-5) was obtained from Chem Impex International (Wood Dale, IL, USA). Koptec 200-proof ethanol (Decon Labs Inc., King of Prussia, PA, USA) and deionized water (resistivity 18.2 MΩ·cm at 25°C) were used for the complex synthesis. All materials were used without further purification.

Solubility of Ritonavir in ethanol-water mixtures

The solubility of ritonavir in ethanol-water solvent mixtures was assessed by dissolving 1 g of ritonavir in 10 mL of ethanol-water solutions with ethanol content ranging from 0% to 100% v/v. Each mixture was vortexed for 1 minute and incubated at 60°C with constant shaking for 2 hours to reach equilibrium. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 1000 x g for 15 minutes to remove undissolved material. Then, 1 ml of the supernatant was extracted and dried in an oven at 60°C for 24 hours. The solubility was determined based on the amount of ritonavir recovered and compared across different ethanol concentrations.

Preparation of Casein-ritonavir complexes

Casein-ritonavir complexes were prepared by stirring a 10% w/v casein solution in ethanol-water mixtures (0-60 % v/v) at 60°C and pH 10 for 3 hours, as shown in Fig. 1 (Ye & Harte, 2013). NaOH (10 M) was added dropwise under continuous agitation to minimize dilution during pH adjustment and preserve the concentration-dependent casein dispersion characteristics. Then, 10% w/w ritonavir pre-dissolved in a small amount of ethanol was added dropwise. The dispersion was stirred overnight at 60°C to achieve a homogeneous casein dispersion and casein-ritonavir complex formation while remaining below the reported thermal degradation range of ritonavir (Fig. 3). The solution was concentrated by centrifugal evaporation using a CentriVap vacuum concentrator (7810014, Labconco Corporation, Kansas City, MO, USA) at 60 °C and 1500 × g for 3 hours to facilitate ethanol removal. The concentrated dispersions were then cooled to 20°C, frozen at −80°C, and lyophilized at −105°C for 48 hours (FreeZone 72040 1000; Labconco Corporation, Kansas City, MO, USA) to remove water and remaining ethanol, and then gently ground using a mortar and pestle to obtain a uniform fine powder for further analysis.

Thermal characterization

The thermal properties of the dried casein-ritonavir powders were measured from 25 to 150°C at a constant heating rate of 5°C/min using Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC; Discovery, TA Instruments, Newcastle, DE, USA). The samples (6-7 mg) were placed in sealed aluminum pans (Tzero pans and lids, TA Instruments, Newcastle, DE, USA) with an empty pan serving as a reference. The changes in enthalpy (ΔHendo) and peak temperature (Tendo) of the endotherms were determined from the corresponding DSC traces.

Microstructural characterization

Powdered, freeze-dried casein-ritonavir complexes underwent X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis using a Rigaku Ultima IV X-ray Diffractometer (Rigaku Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) operated at 40 kV and 30 mA. The diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 5° to 50° at a scanning rate of 5°/min. Diffractograms of pure ritonavir, casein, and the complexes were compared to evaluate changes in crystallinity after complexation.

Attenuated Total Reflectance-Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) of the complexes was performed using a Bruker Vertex 80 spectrometer (Bruker Optics, Billerica, MA, USA) equipped with a diamond ATR accessory. Spectra were collected over the 4000–500 cm−1 range to analyze the interactions between the functional groups of casein and ritonavir by examining shifts in characteristic absorption peaks.

Zeta potential measurement

The hydrodynamic zeta potential of casein–ritonavir complexes was measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) on a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, Westborough, MA, USA). Samples were dispersed in deionized water and analyzed at 25 °C following the guidelines by the manufacturer. Surface charges were recorded to evaluate dispersion stability and complexation behavior. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and an average was reported.

Results and discussion

Solubility of Ritonavir in ethanol-water solvent mixtures

The solubility of ritonavir was tested in ethanol-water mixtures with varying ethanol concentrations (0-100% v/v) to determine the optimal conditions for forming a complex with casein. It was hypothesized that maximum solubilization/dispersion of both ritonavir and casein was needed to obtain an amorphous solid dispersion after solvent extraction. As shown in Fig. 2, ritonavir exhibited low solubility in pure water but showed a significant increase in solubility as the ethanol content increased to >40% (v/v), with a sharp jump at 50% (v/v) ethanol content, indicating a threshold where the solvent environment greatly improves ritonavir’s solubility. This sharp increase demonstrates the classic co-solvency effect: adding ethanol to water disrupts the water's hydrogen-bonding network, lowers the medium’s polarity, and facilitates the better dissolution of hydrophobic molecules like ritonavir (Sanghvi, Narazaki, Machatha, & Yalkowsky, 2008), and explains why commercial ritonavir formulations contain 40-45% v/v ethanol (Morris, Tisi, Tan, & Worthington, 2019). Above 50% (v/v) ethanol content, solubility continued to increase, reaching its maximum at 70-100% (v/v) ethanol in water. These findings highlight the impact of ethanol concentration on ritonavir solubility, informing the choice of solvent for subsequent complexation with casein.

Thermal properties of casein-ritonavir complexes

The thermal behavior of casein-ritonavir dried complexes prepared using different ethanol-water mixtures (0-60% v/v) was investigated by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and the resulting thermograms are shown in Fig. 3. Samples labeled E-0 to E-60 correspond to increasing ethanol concentration (0-60 % v/v) used during complex preparation (Fig. 3 b). Higher ethanol content (70-100%) was not tested to prevent casein re-aggregation and precipitation, as previously reported (Lewis, Bodinger, & Harte, 2022). All samples exhibited broad endothermic transitions between 60 °C and 130 °C, primarily corresponding to the evaporation of bound water absorbed by casein due to its hygroscopic nature and amorphous structure.

Raw ritonavir exhibited a melting peak (Tm) at 123.7°C with a melting enthalpy (ΔHm) of 93.03 J/g, while E-0 and E-10 displayed peaks at 122.8°C (ΔHm= 5.4 J/g) and 120.95°C (ΔHm= 3.42 J/g), respectively. Since ΔHm is proportional to the crystalline fraction (Kong & Hay, 2002), the reduced ΔHm values indicate a decrease in detectable crystallinity and an increase in ritonavir molecular dispersion within the casein matrix. In contrast, no distinguishable melting peaks of ritonavir were observed from E-20 to E-60, suggesting the absence of detectable free crystalline ritonavir and an increased entrapment within the casein matrix. The interaction between ritonavir and casein is attributed to ritonavir’s solubility in ethanol–water mixtures in the presence of monomeric casein proteins, before solvent removal (Fig. 2). Ethanol reduces the polarity of the solvent system, improving the molecular dispersion of hydrophobic ritonavir. Concurrently, ethanol induces the dissociation of casein micelles into monomeric units and aggregates, exposing hydrophobic regions that interact with ritonavir (Fig. 5) (Corzo-Martínez, et al., 2015; Sharma, et al., 2024). Upon freeze-drying, these interactions facilitate strong entrapment of ritonavir within the protein matrix. The absence of ritonavir’s melting peak in high-ethanol samples confirmed the formation of amorphous, thermally stable complexes, highlighting ethanol’s dual role as a solubilizer and protein-structure modifier, potentially improving ritonavir’s aqueous solubility and bioavailability, which will be investigated in future studies.

Microstructural attributes of casein-ritonavir complexes

The molecular and microstructural interactions between casein and ritonavir were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and attenuated total reflectance Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR), as shown in Figs. 4 and 5, respectively. XRD patterns confirmed the highly crystalline nature of pure ritonavir, displaying multiple sharp peaks between 2θ = 5° and 30° (Moreira Pinheiro, et al., 2022). In contrast, casein showed a broad halo centered around 2θ = 20°, characteristic of its amorphous structure (Karydis-Messinis, et al., 2024). In the E-0 and E-10 casein-ritonavir complexes (prepared with minimal or no ethanol), weak residual diffraction features corresponding to crystalline ritonavir were detectable due to the relatively low ritonavir loading (~10% w/w) within the casein matrix, indicating incomplete encapsulation or phase-separated crystalline ritonavir. In contrast, these features were not distinguishable from E-20 to E-60, suggesting reduced detectable crystallinity and increased molecular dispersion of ritonavir within the casein matrix (Vyas & Harte, 2025).

XRD diffractograms for casein-ritonavir complexes prepared with various ethanol-water solvent systems (0-60% v/v).

FTIR spectra (Fig. 5) further supported these findings. Pure ritonavir exhibited characteristic peaks corresponding to N–H stretching (3322 cm−1), C=O stretching (~1700 cm−1 and 1652 cm−1), and aromatic C=C stretching (~1533 cm−1) (Ershad, et al., 2013). Casein displayed its typical amide I (~1625 cm−1) and amide II (~1512 cm−1) bands (Corzo-Martínez, et al., 2015). In casein-ritonavir complexes, E-0 exhibited blue shifts of the amide I and II bands toward higher wavenumbers of ~1638 cm−1 and ~1525 cm−1, respectively, whereas higher ethanol formulations gradually shifted closer to the native casein positions. In addition, weak ritonavir-assisted C=O stretching features near ~1700 cm−1 were distinguishable in E-0 and E-10 but became less apparent at higher ethanol concentrations. Variations in the amide region are commonly associated with changes in the local microenvironment, intermolecular interactions, and protein organization (Barth, 2007). The observed spectral variations indicate differences in the local microenvironment and the non-covalent organization of the casein-ritonavir systems across the ethanol concentration studies. Overall, FTIR results substantiate the presence of molecular associations between casein and ritonavir within the protein matrix.

Zeta potential of casein-ritonavir complexes

Selected casein-ritonavir formulations were analyzed through zeta potential measurements to assess representative trends in surface charge behavior. The zeta potential of casein–ritonavir complexes became increasingly negative with higher ethanol content, ranging from approximately −45 mV at E-0 to −60 mV at E-60, as illustrated in Fig. 6. Previous studies have reported that ethanol concentration can influence the zeta potential and stability of dispersed colloidal systems (Dan, Kuanjun, & Shaohai, 2009), while protein interactions and aggregate organization can affect the surface-charge behavior of casein-based systems (Wang, Wusigale, & Luo, 2023). Given that higher absolute zeta potential values are generally correlated with increased electrostatic repulsion and superior dispersion stability, these observations imply an improved electrostatic stabilization of casein-ritonavir dispersions, which may potentially facilitate the incorporation of ritonavir into the casein matrix.

Zeta potential of casein-ritonavir complexes prepared with different ethanol-water solvent systems (0-60% v/v) dispersed in aqueous media.

Overall, DSC, XRD, FTIR, and zeta potential analyses collectively suggest that ethanol enhances ritonavir dispersion and promotes partial dissociation of casein aggregates into more dispersed casein monomeric units, thereby facilitating ritonavir incorporation and entrapment within the casein matrix during complex formation.

Conclusion

The ethanol-assisted complexation of ritonavir with casein was evaluated using thermal and microstructural characterization techniques. Thermal and microstructural analyses demonstrated reductions in crystalline peaks and shifts in casein spectral peaks with increasing ethanol content, indicating alterations in molecular organization and non-covalent interactions within casein-ritonavir systems. An increase in negative zeta potential also suggested improved aqueous dispersibility and stability in casein-ritonavir dry powders created by exposing the API and protein to rising ethanol content before solvent removal. Overall, ethanol plays a crucial role in modulating molecular interactions, providing a promising strategy to enhance the effective solubility and bioavailability of hydrophobic active pharmaceutical ingredients such as ritonavir, which will be further investigated in future studies.
