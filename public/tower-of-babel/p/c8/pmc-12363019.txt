Predictive value of combined inflammatory markers for septic cardiomyopathy: a retrospective study
Authors: Xianqi Lan, Huiqiang Mai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeptic cardiomyopathy (SCM) is a common but underdiagnosed complication of sepsis characterized by acute myocardial dysfunction driven by inflammatory and immune dysregulation. This study aimed to investigate the clinical characteristics, inflammatory profiles, and predictive markers associated with SCM, highlighting its diagnostic and prognostic importance.MethodsClinical data from 89 controls and 71 SCM patients were retrospectively analyzed from January 2022 to July 2024. The differences in demographics, clinical characteristics, hematological parameters, inflammatory markers, and cardiac injury markers were compared between the two groups. Additionally, the risk factors for SCM were evaluated via univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. After that, the predictive capacity of each marker for SCM was assessed through the receiver operating characteristics curve analysis.ResultsThe SCM group had significantly higher levels of SOFA score, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), cardiac troponin I (cTnI), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), systemic immune-inflammation index, NT-proBNP, and inflammatory markers such as CRP, TNF-α, PCT, and IL-6 (P < 0.05). Multivariate analysis revealed that cTnI (OR = 4.548, 95% CI: 1.164–19.452, P = 0.041), NLR (OR = 4.231, 95% CI: 2.443–7.328, P < 0.001), and CRP (OR = 1.138, 95% CI: 1.061–1.221, P < 0.001) were independent risk factors for SCM. The combined use of NLR, PLR, and CRP yielded an AUC of 0.962, with a sensitivity of 91.5% and specificity of 93.3%.ConclusionsSCM is associated with pronounced inflammatory activation and myocardial injury. This study identifies cTnI, NLR, and CRP as independent risk factors. Notably, the combined use of NLR, CRP, and PLR significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy, providing a reliable basis for early identification and targeted intervention in SCM.Keywords: Sepsis, Septic cardiomyopathy, Inflammatory factor, Systemic immune-inflammation index

Background

Sepsis is a systemic inflammatory response syndrome caused by infection and is a leading cause of death in hospitalized patients, particularly in intensive care units. The core feature of sepsis is vital organ dysfunction, including the liver, lungs, kidneys, and heart, stemming from an uncontrolled immune response to infection, which can be fatal in severe cases [1, 2]. Sepsis is more than just an infection; it involves an excessive immune reaction to pathogens during infection, which causes uncontrolled inflammation and subsequent tissue and organ damage [3, 4].

In patients with sepsis, septic cardiomyopathy (SCM) is a frequently observed cardiac dysfunction. It refers to acute cardiac dysfunction caused by impaired myocardial contractility due to structural and functional damage to myocardial cells, resulting from dysregulated inflammatory and immune responses during sepsis. More specifically, the immune system imbalance is capable of releasing large amounts of inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin (IL-6), causing direct myocardial cell damage and impaired cardiac function [5]. The precise mechanism of SCM remains to be fully understood, but current research indicates that oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are also implicated in the pathogenesis of SCM. Excessive oxidative stress can keep myocardial cells in a heightened inflammatory state, worsening cardiac dysfunction [6, 7]; besides, it also damages the mitochondria in myocardial cells, causing insufficient energy supply, which in turn affects myocardial contraction and relaxation [8]. Changes in hemodynamics caused by sepsis can lead to microcirculatory dysfunction, resulting in insufficient myocardial blood supply, which subsequently induces myocardial ischemia and impairs cardiac function. SCM is a significant contributor to the higher mortality observed in patients with sepsis. Cardiac dysfunction worsens organ hypoxia and hypoperfusion, which can further result in multiple organ failure [8]. Early identification of SCM and timely implementation of appropriate treatment measures can help improve patient outcomes and reduce sepsis-related mortality.

The SCM occurrence and progression are strongly tied to the homeostasis of the inflammatory microenvironment, and inflammatory markers are the best indicators of the severity of inflammation. The association of SCM with serum inflammatory factors is a focus in sepsis research. During sepsis, the body generates large quantities of inflammatory factors, including TNF-α, C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), and IL-6 [9]. These factors not only play a role in controlling infection but can also contribute to myocardial damage through mechanisms like oxidative stress and apoptosis. Monitoring these inflammatory markers dynamically aids in early diagnosis and intervention of SCM, offering insights for future targeted therapy [5]. Despite ongoing research, comprehensive studies on the correlation between inflammatory factors and SCM are still lacking, and clinical evidence remains insufficient [10]. Currently, studies have evaluated the predictive value of single biomarkers, such as troponins or procalcitonin, for SCM [5, 11]. However, these approaches often lack sensitivity and specificity when applied in clinical settings. Multi-marker predictive models for SCM remain underexplored, and existing models have limitations [12]. Our study addresses these gaps by developing and validating a combined biomarker approach (NLR, PLR, CRP), which significantly improves predictive accuracy and provides a novel strategy for early SCM detection.

Methods

General information

This was a retrospective observational study conducted at Zhongshan City People’s Hospital between January 2022 and July 2024. Clinical data of 160 sepsis patients were retrospectively reviewed and analyzed. In accordance with the absence or presence of SCM, patients were stratified into the SCM group and the Control group. Approval for this research was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Zhongshan City People's Hospital (Approval No.2024 − 127). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee because the study was retrospective and involved only anonymized data.

The inclusion criteria included: (1) patients aged ≥ 18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of sepsis; (2) diagnosis of septic cardiomyopathy (SCM) based on one or more of the following echocardiographic abnormalities: ① right ventricular systolic myocardial velocity < 12 cm/s; ② left ventricular systolic myocardial velocity < 8 cm/s or left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 50%; ③ early diastolic mitral annular velocity < 8 cm/s or an early mitral inflow velocity to early diastolic mitral annular velocity ratio (E/e’) > 15 [13, 14]; (3) Patients with no prior history of cardiovascular disease were included; cardiovascular conditions identified during hospitalization were documented as part of clinical data collection for completeness; (4) Comprehensive clinical records. The patient selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of patient selection. A total of 160 patients with sepsis were admitted to Zhongshan People’s Hospital from January 2022 to July 2024. Of these, 129 patients were excluded for the following reasons: pre-existing cardiac disease (n = 41), no echocardiography within 24 h of admission (n = 39), incomplete clinical records (n = 27), readmission unrelated to sepsis or SCM (n = 12), and atrial fibrillation (n = 11). Finally, 71 patients with septic cardiomyopathy (SCM) and 89 control patients were included in the analysis

Exclusion criteria were specified as follows: (1) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (2) atrial fibrillation; (3) a history of cardiovascular disease (e.g., chronic heart failure, previous myocardial infarction, cardiomyopathy, or valvular heart disease); (4) readmission during the study period for non-sepsis-related reasons (e.g., trauma, malignancy, or non-infectious conditions); and (5) absence of an echocardiogram within 24 h of admission. For readmission cases, patients were included if the readmission was related to recurrent sepsis or SCM; otherwise, they were excluded to avoid confounding.

After fully informing the patients and obtaining informed consent from them or their legal guardians, data were collected, including gender, age, comorbidities (diabetes, coronary artery disease), body mass index (BMI, kg/m²), sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score, cardiac troponin I (cTnI, mg/L), infection site (respiratory, urinary, hepatobiliary, peritoneal, skin and soft tissue, central nervous system, or others), N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP, ng/L), diastolic and systolic blood pressure (mmHg), and heart rate (beats/min). Potential confounding factors, such as infection source, antimicrobial therapies, and fluid balance, were recorded where possible and included in the multivariate analysis to minimize their impact on the results.

Data collection

Complete blood count

Hematological features were assessed by collecting whole blood samples from all study participants. Then, a complete blood count was performed using an automated hematology analyzer. Key parameters measured included white blood cell count (4.0–10.0 × 10⁹/L), hemoglobin (120–160 g/L), monocyte count (0.2–0.8 × 10⁹/L), red blood cell count (4.0–5.5 × 10¹²/L), platelet count (100–300 × 10⁹/L), neutrophil count(2.0–7.5 × 10⁹/L), and lymphocyte count (1.0–3.0 × 10⁹/L). Additionally, calculation was conducted on the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR). The SII was calculated by multiplying the platelet count by the neutrophil count divided by the lymphocyte count [15].

Detection of the inflammatory markers

The levels of serum inflammatory markers were measured using standardized laboratory methods to assess the intensity of the inflammatory response in patients with sepsis. CRP and PCT were measured by chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA) using the Roche Cobas e601 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Germany, limits of detection: CRP, 0.3 mg/L; PCT, 0.02 ng/mL), while IL-6 and TNF-α were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (R&D Systems, USA, limits of detection: IL-6, 0.12 pg/mL; TNF-α, 6.23 pg/mL) according to the manufacturers’ protocols.The markers measured encompassed PCT (ng/L), IL-6 (pg/mL), TNF-α (pg/mL), and CRP (mg/L) [16]. Reference ranges were as follows: CRP < 5 mg/L, PCT < 0.05 ng/mL, IL-6 < 7 pg/mL, TNF-α < 8 pg/mL, cTnI < 0.04 mg/L, and NT-proBNP < 125 ng/L (for patients < 75 years old). All baseline laboratory and clinical data—including cardiac biomarkers (cTnI, NT-proBNP) and inflammatory markers (CRP, PCT, IL-6, TNF-α)—were collected within the first 24 h of ICU admission and before the diagnosis of septic cardiomyopathy.

Statistical analysis

SPSS version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was employed for the statistical analysis of all data. Mean ± standard deviation was utilized for expressing normally distributed data, and the independent samples t-test was applied for comparisons between groups. The Mann-Whitney U test was applied for analyzing non-normally distributed data, which were presented as median (P25, P75). To present categorical variables, frequency and percentage were utilized, with group comparisons analyzed via Fisher’s exact test or chi-square test. The risk factors for SCM were identified through univariate analysis, and to further confirm independent risk factors, multivariate logistic regression was applied. The impact of variables was assessed via the odds ratio (OR) as well as its 95% confidence interval (CI). To evaluate the predictive capacity of each indicator, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was employed, with diagnostic performance quantified by the area under the curve (AUC). As the AUC approached 1.0, the predictive capacity of the indicator became stronger. P < 0.05 represented the statistical significance threshold. To evaluate diagnostic performance, logistic regression models were constructed using different combinations of NLR, PLR, and CRP, and the AUC was calculated for each model. Pairwise Delong tests were performed to statistically compare the three-marker model with all two-marker combinations. Partial correlation analyses adjusted for SOFA score were conducted to explore associations between inflammatory markers (NLR, CRP, PLR) and cardiac injury indicators (cTnI, NT-proBNP). To avoid multicollinearity and model overfitting, we used a stepwise forward logistic regression approach. Additionally, we assessed the variance inflation factor (VIF) for each included variable, confirming that all final predictors had VIF < 5, indicating low collinearity. In terms of missing data, a complete-case analysis was performed. Relevant clinical variables were assessed as potential confounders and adjusted for in multivariate logistic regression models to account for their effects on SCM risk. Patients with missing values in key variables (e.g., inflammatory markers or cardiac function indicators) were excluded from the final analysis. The overall proportion of missing data was less than 5%, and missingness was assumed to be at random. Given the small extent of missing data and the retrospective nature of the study, this method ensured minimal bias and maintained the validity of the statistical results.

Results

Demographics and clinical baseline characteristics

The SCM group and the Control group did not differ significantly in body mass index, gender, age, comorbidities (including coronary artery disease and diabetes), infection site (respiratory system, urinary system, etc.), diastolic blood pressure, and heart rate (P > 0.05). However, the SCM group displayed markedly higher SOFA scores (P = 0.047), cTnI (P < 0.001), and NT-proBNP (P = 0.004) levels in compared to both the Control group and the reference ranges, alongside remarkably lower systolic blood pressure (P < 0.001). These findings suggested more severe impairment in cardiac function and overall organ function in SCM patients(Table 1).

Analysis of potential risk factors for SCM

SOFA score, cTnI, NT-proBNP, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, PLR, MLR, NLR, CRP, IL-6, PCT, SII, and TNF-α were all significant risk factors for SCM, as presented by univariate logistic regression analysis (P < 0.05). Identification of independent risk factors for SCM was conducted through multivariate logistic regression analysis(Table 4), including cTnI (OR = 4.548, 95% CI: 1.164–19.452, P = 0.041), NLR (OR = 4.231, 95% CI: 2.443–7.328, P < 0.001), and CRP (OR = 1.138, 95% CI: 1.061–1.221, P < 0.001). Although several variables such as PLR, PCT, IL-6, and TNF-α showed significance in univariate analysis, they were not included in the final model due to either overlapping predictive information or multicollinearity with other variables. We employed stepwise forward logistic regression for variable selection. Furthermore, we assessed the multicollinearity using variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis, and all variables retained in the final model (cTnI, NLR, and CRP) had VIF values less than 5, indicating low collinearity and good model stability.

ROC curve analysis for predicting SCM

The predictive capacity of CRP, PLR, and NLR combined, as determined by ROC curve analysis, resulted in an AUC of 0.962, with sensitivity and specificity values of 91.5% and 93.3%, respectively. The optimal cut-off value for the combined prediction was 0.635, determined using the Youden index. The ROC curve of the combined model showed a steep rise with minimal false positives, indicating excellent discriminative ability. For PLR, NLR, and CRP, the AUCs were 0.734 (95% CI: 0.648–0.819), 0.927 (95% CI: 0.888–0.966), and 0.804 (95% CI: 0.734–0.874), respectively. Discussion

In this retrospective study, we found that patients with septic cardiomyopathy (SCM) exhibited significantly higher levels of inflammatory markers (CRP, PCT, IL-6, TNF-α), immune dysregulation indices (NLR, PLR, SII), and myocardial injury markers (cTnI, NT-proBNP) compared to controls. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified cTnI, NLR, and CRP as independent risk factors for SCM. Moreover, the combination of NLR, PLR, and CRP demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy for predicting SCM. These findings highlight the central role of inflammatory activation and immune imbalance in SCM pathogenesis and underscore the potential clinical utility of combining inflammatory markers for early diagnosis and risk stratification.

SCM is characterized by significantly worse cardiac dysfunction compared to general sepsis. The reduced systolic blood pressure in SCM patients indicates impaired cardiac output and poor organ perfusion, highlighting the importance of early detection and timely interventions, including hemodynamic support, myocardial protection, and control of systemic inflammation [17]. In addition, elevated CRP may further exacerbate endothelial dysfunction and microvascular thrombosis through complement activation, amplifying myocardial ischemia. High NLR reflects neutrophil-driven oxidative stress and lymphocyte depletion, both indicative of immune dysregulation and contributing to myocardial injury. The release of cTnI can be interpreted as a marker of cardiomyocyte membrane damage secondary to these inflammatory processes. These interrelated mechanisms collectively provide a plausible pathological basis for the observed biomarker changes in septic cardiomyopathy. The elevated inflammatory markers observed in our study are consistent with previous reports suggesting that excessive immune activation plays a critical role in SCM development [5, 12].

Pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly TNF-α and IL-6, activate downstream pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK, which disrupt calcium homeostasis, promote mitochondrial dysfunction, and induce cardiomyocyte apoptosis, ultimately impairing myocardial contractility [18]. Our findings of increased NLR and CRP further support this hyperinflammatory state, aligning with evidence that these markers reflect immune imbalance and correlate with myocardial injury [11, 19].

Previous studies focusing on single markers, such as troponins or procalcitonin, often reported limited predictive value in heterogeneous sepsis populations and lacked external validation [9, 11]. Multi-marker predictive models for SCM have been proposed in a few small-scale studies, but these models showed inconsistent performance and were not widely adopted clinically due to insufficient robustness and lack of rigorous validation [10]. Our study addresses these gaps by integrating NLR, PLR, and CRP into a unified predictive model. This approach reflects the multidimensional nature of the inflammatory response in SCM and significantly enhances predictive performance (AUC = 0.962, sensitivity 91.5%, specificity 93.3%), outperforming previous two-marker combinations and single-marker models.

CRP, beyond serving as an inflammatory marker, may directly damage endothelial cells by activating complement pathways and promoting microvascular thrombosis, thereby exacerbating myocardial ischemia [20, 21]. Similarly, PCT reflects the severity of bacterial infection-induced inflammation, while elevated NLR indicates neutrophil predominance and lymphocyte depletion, both markers of immune dysregulation in sepsis [9]. These observations reinforce prior reports linking these inflammatory markers to SCM severity and prognosis [22]. Systolic blood pressure was significantly lower in SCM patients, likely reflecting vasodilation and impaired myocardial performance [23, 24]. Hypotension, a hallmark of cardiovascular dysfunction in sepsis, worsens tissue hypoperfusion and is particularly detrimental during septic shock [25, 26]. Our analysis suggests that maintaining stable systolic blood pressure may reduce myocardial strain, sustain organ perfusion, and improve outcomes in SCM patients [25, 27].

Although no significant differences were observed in anemia and leukocyte counts, SCM patients had elevated neutrophils and reduced lymphocytes and monocytes, indicative of immune dysregulation. High MLR, NLR, PLR, and SII further confirm the imbalance between pro-inflammatory and immune-suppressive responses [28, 29].

Importantly, combining NLR, PLR, and CRP improved diagnostic accuracy for SCM, reflecting multiple dimensions of inflammation and immune status. This multi-marker approach, together with cTnI, provides a valuable tool for early SCM detection and risk stratification [30, 31]. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that targeting inflammatory cascades or preserving mitochondrial function through therapies such as cytokine inhibitors and antioxidants may represent promising approaches to mitigate myocardial injury in SCM [11].

Although this study provides valuable insights into the association between inflammatory markers and septic cardiomyopathy, it also has several limitations. The results of this single-center retrospective study may lack generalizability due to its small sample size. The retrospective design is inherently prone to selection bias and limits the ability to establish causal relationships between inflammatory markers and septic cardiomyopathy. Potential unmeasured confounders, such as differences in pathogen type, antimicrobial regimens, and fluid management, may also affect the robustness of our findings. Moreover, reliance on a single 24-hour sampling period and the absence of comprehensive control over these factors could introduce residual confounding in identifying SCM risk factors. Although a formal post-hoc statistical power analysis was not performed, the highly significant P values and large effect sizes of key variables (e.g., NLR, CRP, cTnI) suggest adequate power to detect meaningful differences. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger sample sizes and standardized protocols are warranted to validate these results, strengthen causal inference, and enhance statistical rigor.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings indicate that septic cardiomyopathy (SCM) is closely associated with pronounced inflammatory activation and myocardial injury. We identified cTnI, NLR, and CRP as independent risk factors for SCM. Notably, the combined use of NLR, CRP, and PLR yielded superior diagnostic accuracy compared to single markers. This multi-marker predictive model, based on laboratory parameters, may facilitate early detection, risk stratification in the ICU, and prognostic monitoring in clinical practice.
