Interparental conflict and children’s sleep quality in the chinese family context: the serial mediating roles of emotional insecurity and subjective well-being
Authors: Hongxia Hou, Yaxin Guan, Tiantian Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep problems in children are an important risk factor for cognitive, emotional, and behavioral development. Previous studies have shown that interparental conflict is closely associated with poorer sleep quality in children. However, the internal psychological variables involved in this association, particularly how they may be understood within the Chinese family context, remain insufficiently examined. Based on a sample of Chinese children, this study examined the association between interparental conflict and poorer sleep quality in children, and it tested the mediating pathways of emotional insecurity and subjective well-being(SWB).MethodsThis study employed a quantitatively driven explanatory sequential mixed-methods design combining questionnaire survey data with child interviews. The quantitative component included 900 upper-grade primary school children (M = 10.58, SD = 0.81). Correlation analyses and a serial mediation model were used to examine the associations among interparental conflict, emotional insecurity, SWB, and children’s sleep quality. In the qualitative component, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 23 children, and thematic analysis was used to supplement and contextualize the quantitative findings.ResultsInterparental conflict was significantly associated with poorer sleep quality in children (r = 0.39, p < 0.001), and this association remained significant after emotional insecurity and SWB were included as mediators in the model (β = 0.11, p = 0.006). The statistical indirect effects through emotional insecurity, through SWB, and through emotional insecurity and SWB in sequence, were all significant, with effect values of 0.014, 0.019, and 0.004 respectively. The qualitative findings showed that the interviewed children incorporated interparental conflict into their understanding of family relationships, their own situations, and daily life circumstances. Within the Chinese family context, these responses were more clearly reflected in a contextualized organization of meaning around family harmony, parent–child connection, and relational responsibility.ConclusionsEmotional insecurity and SWB are important psychological variables for understanding the association between interparental conflict and poorer sleep quality in children. At the same time, these processes may acquire specific meanings within the Chinese family context.

Introduction

At a developmental stage when children should be sleeping soundly, an increasing number are instead experiencing chronic sleep insufficiency and poor sleep quality [1, 2]. Notably, this trend has not been alleviated by improvements in material living conditions; rather, it appears to have intensified in the context of mounting academic pressure and changing lifestyles [3]. As a critical process for neural restoration and information integration, sleep is involved not only in memory consolidation and cognitive development, but also in emotional recovery and regulation [4]. For children in this key developmental period, impaired sleep can adversely affect cognition, emotion, and behavior, and may substantially increase the subsequent risk of depression [5]. A national survey on sleep among Chinese children has further shown that the average sleep duration of children aged 6–12 years falls significantly below recommended levels, with common problems including difficulty falling asleep, nocturnal awakening, and instability in overall sleep quality [6]. Against this background, a core question demands urgent attention: what factors are persistently undermining children’s sleep quality?

Among the many factors that shape children’s sleep, the family environment plays a foundational role. Systematic review evidence indicates that children’s sleep is influenced not only by individual biological rhythms and daily behavioral habits, but also by family structure, family stress, coparenting, parent–child interaction, and parental behavior [7]. Among these factors, interparental conflict, as a salient manifestation of tension within the family system, has been identified as an important risk factor for children’s sleep problems. Previous studies have consistently documented a stable association between interparental conflict and children’s sleep difficulties with this relationship commonly explained through pathways such as emotional responses [8] and parenting behaviors [9]. However, this line of research has tended to emphasize children’s exposure to external family stress, while paying relatively less attention to how children subjectively interpret and make sense of interparental conflict.

Nevertheless, subsequent research has shown that this process is not entirely homogeneous. Similar levels of interparental conflict may yield markedly different sleep outcomes across children. Some studies have found that children’s emotional reactivity and self-regulatory capacity can shape this association: children with higher emotional reactivity are more likely to experience difficulty in falling asleep and through nocturnal awakenings, whereas those with stronger regulatory capacities may maintain relatively stable sleep even under comparable levels of interparental conflict [8, 10]. Taken together, these studies suggest that the consequences of interparental conflict also depend on how children respond to such conflict. More importantly, these response patterns are not determined solely by children’s immediate psychological states; rather, they are gradually shaped through long-term lived experience. In the course of family interaction, children continuously learn how to understand relationships, how to interpret the meaning of conflict, and, through repeated experience, develop relatively stable patterns of response.

In this sense, children’s understanding of interparental conflict is also embedded in the family and social contexts in which they live. Compared with cultural orientations that place greater emphasis on individual boundaries and autonomous development, Chinese culture tends to understand and construct the self within family relationships. Accordingly, children’s self-perception, emotional security, and evaluations of daily life are often closely connected with the overall state of the family [11]. The family is not only a living unit, but also a meaningful structure that carries relational responsibilities and emotional norms. Previous research has suggested that Chinese culture emphasizes the wholeness and harmony of family relationships, and that family stability is an important value foundation [12]. Against this background, children may be more likely to understand interparental conflict in relation to family stability, parent–child connection, and the overall atmosphere of family life.

However, much of the existing research has been based on Western examples and relatively limited attention has been paid to how children understand interparental conflict across different cultural contexts. At the same time, although previous studies have examined the association between interparental conflict and children’s sleep, less is known about how this association is reflected in children’s everyday experiences and becomes linked to sleep problems. In addition, existing research has focused more on adolescents with relatively insufficient attention to school-aged children, especially those in primary school. Moreover, most studies have relied primarily on quantitative analyses and have provided limited insight into children’s subjective understanding of interparental conflict within specific family contexts.

Children aged 9–12 years are at an important developmental stage in which family experiences, psychological feelings, and sleep development are closely intertwined. Children in this age group are already able to form basic understandings and evaluations of interparental conflict, including judgments about threats posed by the conflict, their own responsibility, and the possibility of coping with these [13]. They are also able to perceive and gradually internalize cultural values related to family norms, parental expectations, and parent–child responsibilities through everyday family interactions [14]. Meanwhile, sleep in this age group remains developmentally sensitive and is closely related to emotion regulation and behavioral adjustment [15].

Based on these considerations, the present study focuses on upper-grade primary school children in the Chinese family context and uses a quantitatively driven explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. The quantitative component was used to test the statistical associations and indirect pathways among interparental conflict, emotional insecurity, subjective well-being(SWB), and children’s sleep quality. The qualitative interviews conducted were used to present children’s ways of understanding interparental conflict and their own emotional experiences, thereby providing a contextualized interpretation of the quantitative findings.

The relationship between interparental conflict and sleep quality

Sleep quality is typically defined as a multidimensional indicator reflecting children’s regularity of sleep timing, adequacy of sleep duration, sleep onset latency, frequency of nocturnal awakenings, occurrence of sleep disturbances, and daytime functioning [16]. Research grounded in the ecological model of human development has identified a range of factors influencing children’s sleep quality across individual, peer, and family domains, among which adverse family environments have repeatedly been shown to constitute an important risk factor for children’s sleep problems [17]. Family systems theory conceptualizes the family as an integrated whole composed of interdependent relational subsystems with the interparental relationship functioning as the core subsystem. The functioning of this subsystem may affect children’s developmental adjustment through emotional spillover, interactional transmission, and systemic linkage [18]. From this perspective, children’s sleep problems may represent an individual-level manifestation of imbalance within the family relational system.

Among family-related factors, interparental conflict is regarded as one of the most stressful contexts for children. Interparental conflict generally refers to verbal or behavioral conflict arising from disagreements between partners that might include overt arguments, hostile interactions, and a persistent atmosphere of tension [19]. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that children’s cognitive, emotional, behavioral, and physiological responses to interparental conflict are generally associated with adjustment problems at small to moderate effect sizes, suggesting that interparental conflict may represent an important family risk factor linked to children’s psychological and physiological regulation [20].

Comparable conclusions are supported in literature on the topic of sleep. A meta-analysis found that negative family relational factors are associated with poorer sleep quality in children and adolescents [21]. Longitudinal research has further shown that individuals exposed to higher levels of interparental partner conflict during adolescence exhibit lower sleep efficiency and longer periods of nocturnal wakefulness in emerging adulthood [22]. These findings suggest that in family contexts characterized by higher levels of interparental conflict, children may be more likely to experience heightened vigilance and difficulty relaxing before sleep, which may be associated with poorer sleep quality. Accordingly, this study proposes Hypothesis 1 (H1): Interparental conflict is significant in identifying factors positively associated with children’s sleep problems.

The mediating pathway of emotional insecurity

Emotional insecurity refers to a vulnerable psychological state that develops when children experience persistent concern about the stability and predictability of family relationships in adverse family environments such as interparental conflict. It is typically manifested in the heightened vigilance of children toward family disorder, producing anxiety, fear, and responses of involvement or avoidance [23]. Emotional security theory posits that the enduring impact of interparental conflict on children lies in its capacity to undermine children’s basic sense of whether family relationships are reliable, thereby rendering the family, which is expected to function as a source of security for children, to be perceived by them to as being less dependable as a relational context [19].

Previous research supports this perspective. Studies show that interparental conflict is consistently associated with children’s emotional insecurity at family level, and that the interparental relationship, the parent–child relationship, and emotional insecurity are linked to a range of developmental outcomes [24, 25]. At the same time, the spillover hypothesis suggests that negative affect and conflict within the marital relationship can spill over into parent–child interactions, weakening parental emotional support and making it more difficult for children to derive stable support from the parent–child relationship, thereby deepening their experience of uncertainty regarding family relationships [26].

Children who remain in a prolonged state of emotional insecurity often maintain a heightened level of vigilance in everyday life and continue to monitor environmental changes closely. This vigilance does not necessarily dissipate at night; instead, it may manifest at bedtime as repetitive thinking about family situations create difficulty in relaxing, thereby interfering with sleep onset. In addition, sustained emotional arousal may disrupt sleep maintenance during the night, making sleep more easily fragmented. Prior research has shown that marital conflict can further influence children’s sleep problems through elevated emotional insecurity [27].

The above reasoning suggests that interparental conflict may be associated with poorer sleep quality through higher emotional insecurity, which is characterized by heightened vigilance, worry, and emotional arousal. Accordingly, this study proposes Hypothesis 2 (H2): Emotional insecurity mediates the relationship between interparental conflict and children’s sleep quality.

The mediating pathway of SWB

The association between SWB and sleep quality can be understood from its global evaluative function and psychological resource function. SWB reflects children’s overall evaluation of whether their lives are satisfactory, stable, positive, and worthy of optimism. As an important cognitive component of SWB, life satisfaction represents individuals’ overall evaluation of their quality of life according to their own standards [28]. For children, this overall evaluation is often reflected in whether they feel that daily life is stable, family life is satisfactory, support is available, and life presents positive experiences and expectations.

From the perspective of SWB homeostasis theory, individuals usually maintain SWB through a relatively stable affective baseline and positive cognitive bias. When persistent stress weakens this homeostatic protection, individuals’ positive evaluations of life circumstances may decline, and be accompanied by reductions in positive affect and life satisfaction [29, 30].

From this perspective, higher SWB may indicate that children are more likely to experience daily life as stable, positive, and worth anticipating, enabling them to enter a relatively calm and restorative psychological state at the end of the day. In contrast, lower SWB may indicate a decline in children’s overall evaluation of life circumstances, fewer positive experiences, and an insufficient sense of psychological recovery that may be associated with difficulty falling asleep, restless sleep, and poorer overall sleep quality. A longitudinal meta-analysis further shows a stable bidirectional association between adolescents’ positive well-being and sleep, with positive well-being also predicting subsequent sleep outcomes [31].

Taken together, interparental conflict may be linked to lower positive evaluations of daily life and lower SWB. Lower SWB may indicate weaker overall psychological adjustment and recovery, making it more difficult for children to maintain a stable emotional foundation conducive to good sleep. Accordingly, this study proposes Hypothesis 3 (H3): SWB mediates the relationship between interparental conflict and children’s sleep quality.

Serial mediating pathway of emotional insecurity and SWB

The association between interparental conflict and children’s sleep quality can be understood as a possible sequential process in which perceived family threat is linked to lower overall life evaluation, which is in turn associated with poorer sleep quality. Emotional insecurity may alter children’s ongoing judgments about family support, relational reliability, and the predictability of daily life [32]. When children remain vigilant and worried about family disharmony over time, their attention may become more focused on potential threat cues, negative information in their daily experience may be amplified, and positive experiences may become relatively reduced. As a result, their overall evaluation of whether life is stable and worth looking forward to may also decline [33]. Neurodevelopmental research also suggests that chronic stress may affect brain regions involved in threat processing, emotion regulation, and adaptive responses, including the prefrontal cortex, amygdala, and hippocampus, through stress-regulation systems [34].

These findings suggest that persistent insecurity may lead children to perceive family relationships repeatedly in a vigilant and defensive manner, thereby weakening their sense of family stability, positive daily experiences, and future expectations. In this way, security-related concerns that initially focus on family relationships may gradually extend to a more negative evaluation of overall life circumstances, which is reflected in lower SWB. This decline in overall well-being may further be associated with sleep processes. Sleep regulation is closely intertwined with emotion, arousal, and attentional systems, whereby children’s sleep develops in conjunction with daytime emotional states and neuroregulatory functions [35]. Therefore, when emotional insecurity is associated with lower SWB over time, children may not only experience instability in family relationships, but may also find it more difficult to maintain a positive, relaxed, and restorative daytime psychological state, which may in turn be linked to difficulties in sleep onset and nighttime sleep maintenance.

Taken together, interparental conflict may first be associated with children’s concerns about the safety and predictability of family relationships, thereby contributing to emotional insecurity. Persistent insecurity may then be related to lower evaluations of daily life stability, pleasantness, and positive experiences, which may be reflected in lower SWB. Lower SWB may further indicate weaker daytime emotion regulation and psychological recovery and may therefore be associated with poorer sleep quality. Because the association between SWB and sleep quality may be bidirectional, this pathway does not exclude other possible orderings of the variables. This study statistically tests this model on the basis of existing theory linked to the present research question. Accordingly, this study proposes Hypothesis 4 (H4): Emotional insecurity and SWB jointly form a serial mediating pathway in the association between interparental conflict and children’s sleep quality.

Methods

Research design

This study employed a quantitatively driven explanatory sequential mixed-methods design. Following a quantitative-then-qualitative sequence, it examined the association between interparental conflict and children’s sleep quality as well as the psychological processes that may be involved. The basic logic of this design was, firstly, to use questionnaire survey data and statistical models to test the associations among variables and the relevant indirect pathways, and then to use child interviews to provide experiential supplementation and contextual interpretation of the quantitative findings. Compared with a single-method design, a mixed-methods approach not only allows for the testing of relationships among variables but also helps to illuminate the psychological processes and contextual meanings underlying statistical patterns thereby enhancing both the interpretive depth and contextual sensitivity of the findings. In this present study, the quantitative component was used to identify whether stable associations existed among the focal variables and to estimate the statistical roles they played within the proposed model; the qualitative component was used to capture children’s experiential accounts in everyday family contexts, thereby enriching understanding of the concrete psychological processes underlying these statistical associations.

The study followed a quantitative-first, qualitative-follow-up design. The first phase consisted of the quantitative study in which questionnaire data was collected and analyzed using correlation analysis, regression analysis, and mediation analysis to systematically examine the relationships among interparental conflict, emotional insecurity, SWB, and children’s sleep quality, as well as the serial mediating roles of emotional insecurity and SWB. This phase was designed primarily to address whether these variables were associated and how those associations were structured.

The second phase introduced the qualitative component. On the basis of the quantitative findings, a subset of children was selected for semi-structured interviews. The interviews focused on Chinese children’s subjective understanding of interparental conflict, as well as the emotional experiences and life perceptions associated with such conflict. The qualitative inquiry was intended to illuminate how children interpret family conflict in specific lived contexts and how such interpretations extend into emotional experience and broader evaluations of daily life, thereby providing an experience-based complement to the quantitative findings.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Education, Qinghai Normal University (Approval No. JY20240618002; approval date: June 18, 2024). As the participants were minors, written informed consent was obtained from their guardians through the school before the questionnaire survey and interviews were conducted. The children were also informed about the study in age-appropriate language and provided their own assent before participation. All participant information was anonymized during data organization and analysis, and the data were used only for this study.

Quantitative study

Participants

According to the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child, a child is defined as an individual under 18 years of age. Within the Chinese educational system, the primary school years generally correspond to children aged 6–12 years. This developmental period is marked by rapid change in sleep rhythms, emotion regulation, and self-evaluation, and is therefore of considerable theoretical and empirical significance. Accordingly, the present study focused on children in Grades 3–6 from a primary school in China.

A cluster sampling strategy was used with classes serving as the sampling units. Paper questionnaires were distributed in the classes and students completed them anonymously during class time. In order to reduce the potential influence of social desirability bias, they were explicitly informed that there were no right or wrong answers. A total of 941 questionnaires were collected. After spoiled and incomplete questionnaires were excluded, 900 valid cases remained, yielding an effective response rate of 95.6%. Participants ranged in age from 9 to 12 years (M = 10.58, SD = 0.81). The sample size was considered to be adequate and the demographic characteristics were relatively well distributed across categories. Measures

Self-developed demographic questionnaire

The questionnaire included items on students’ sex, grade level, only-child status, whether they lived with grandparents, whether they had their own bedroom, and whether they slept alone. Left-behind child status referred to situations in which one or both parents did not live with the child for an extended period because of migrant work, employment, or other long-term reasons. Class-cadre status referred to whether the child held a formal student leadership or service position in the class, such as class monitor, study representative, subject representative, or other class-management/service roles, either appointed by the teacher or selected by the class.

Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)

Children’s sleep quality was assessed using the PSQI [36] based on a Chinese adaptation validated for use with children [37].The PSQI assesses seven components of sleep, including subjective sleep quality, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Higher total scores indicate poorer sleep quality. In the present study, the model fit indices for the scale were as follows: χ2/df = 5.84, RMSEA = 0.06, NFI = 0.93, RFI = 0.85, IFI = 0.94, TLI = 0.87, and CFI = 0.94. Cronbach’s α was 0.81, indicating acceptable reliability and validity.

Index of well-being (IWB)

SWB was measured using the IWB developed by Campbell and colleagues [38]. The scale consists of 9 items rated on a 7-point scale. The first 8 items are weighted 1.0, and the final item is weighted 1.1. Higher scores indicate greater SWB. In the present study, the model fit indices were as follows: χ2/df = 5.39, RMSEA = 0.06, NFI = 0.97, RFI = 0.95, IFI = 0.98, TLI = 0.96, and CFI = 0.98. Cronbach’s α was 0.92, indicating good reliability and validity.

Children’s perception of interparental conflict scale (CPIC)

Interparental conflict was assessed using the CPIC [39] with a Chinese adaptation suitable for children [40]. The scale comprises 38 items rated on a 4-point scale ranging from “completely inconsistent” to “completely consistent.” Higher scores indicate perceived interparental conflict that is more severe. The scale has been shown in multiple studies to be suitable for Chinese children. In the present study, the model fit indices were as follows: χ2/df = 5.66, RMSEA = 0.08, NFI = 0.85, RFI = 0.83, IFI = 0.89, TLI = 0.82, and CFI = 0.87. Cronbach’s α was 0.89, indicating acceptable reliability and validity.

Security in the interparental subsystem scale (SIS)

Emotional insecurity was measured using the SIS [41], based on a Chinese adaptation [42]. The scale consists of 17 items rated on a 4-point scale and is used to assess children’s emotional insecurity following interparental conflict. Higher scores indicate greater emotional insecurity. In the present study, the model fit indices were as follows: χ2/df = 5.55, RMSEA = 0.06, NFI = 0.89, RFI = 0.85, IFI = 0.91, TLI = 0.87, and CFI = 0.91. Cronbach’s α was 0.89, indicating good reliability and validity.

Data analysis

Data was first entered into Excel and subsequently imported into SPSS 26.0 (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences 26.0) and AMOS 24.0 for statistical analysis. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were found to satisfy the assumption of normality. AMOS 24.0 was used to examine the validity of the measurement scales. In SPSS 26.0, independent-samples t tests and one-way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) were conducted to examine demographic differences; Pearson product-moment correlations were used to assess associations among the study variables; and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations among interparental conflict, emotional insecurity, SWB, and children’s sleep quality. Covariates were selected on the basis of previous research and on the theoretical model of the present study. Priority was given to demographic and family background variables that could plausibly be associated with interparental conflict exposure, children’s psychological states, and sleep quality. On this basis, parental divorce status, left-behind child status, sleeping alone, co-residence with grandparents, class-cadre status, and grade were included as control variables in the regression analyses and mediation model. Finally, the PROCESS macro was used to test the serial mediating pathway of emotional insecurity and SWB in the association between interparental conflict and children’s sleep quality. A two-tailed p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Qualitative Study

Interview procedure

The interviews were conducted individually in a quiet setting within the school. Given the age characteristics of the participants, the interview questions were phrased in a simple manner and closely tied to children’s everyday experience and with appropriate follow-up probes used in response to their answers. Each interview lasted approximately 10–20 min. Audio recording was conducted only after written consent had been obtained from each child’s guardian and oral assent had been obtained from the child. The interview material was used solely to provide contextualized interpretation of the quantitative findings.

To enhance the reliability of the qualitative analysis, quality control was implemented at three stages: sampling, data collection, and analysis. At the sampling stage, key demographic differences were incorporated to broaden sample coverage; at the data collection stage, a standardized interview guide was used and interviews were conducted in relatively consistent settings; and at the analysis stage, two researchers independently coded the transcripts and then reached agreement through discussion, thereby improving coding consistency.

Design of the interview protocol

A semi-structured interview guide was used in this study. It was developed primarily on the basis of emotional security theory and family systems theory. The interview format was designed to encourage children to describe family interaction processes from the standpoint of their everyday experience with particular attention to how they perceive family relationships in concrete life contexts, how they interpret interparental conflict, and how these interpretations develop into more enduring subjective experiences.

Guided by this objective, the interviews encouraged children to elaborate on several aspects of their experiences. First, the interviews explored children’s overall understanding of their family relational context, including their perceptions of familial atmosphere, relationship stability, and patterns of interaction among family members. Second, they focused on children’s subjective interpretations of interparental conflict. Third, the interviews examined children’s understandings of everyday family norms, including family relationships, family harmony, and parent–child responsibilities, and they explored how these understandings might be associated with their interpretations of interparental conflict. Finally, the interviews further examined how these understandings and interpretations extended to children’s broader perceptions of their own life circumstances and were reflected in specific domains, such as daily emotional experiences and sleep-related states.

Because the participants were children aged 9–12 years, the questioning style of the interviews relied on contextualized and concrete language and avoided direct use of abstract concepts. Questions were anchored in daily life experience and used specific situations to invite narration. For example, children were asked questions such as “What is your family usually like?” and “When do you feel that your family is especially good?” in order to elicit their basic perceptions of family relationships. These were combined with prompts based on specific events and situational follow-up questions, such as “When your parents argue, what do you usually think?” and “How does your mood change during those days?”, to guide them in describing their emotional experiences and the ways these unfolded over time. On this basis, continued probing was used to elicit richer experiential information, so that the interview material would capture, as fully as possible, the continuity of children’s lived experience.

Qualitative data analysis

Upon completion of the interviews, the research team first transcribed all audio recordings verbatim and anonymized all personally identifying information. The resulting transcripts were then imported into NVivo 14 for coding and analysis. Because the interviews in this study were intended primarily to supplement and interpret the quantitative findings rather than to generate an entirely new theory, the qualitative data was analyzed using theory-informed thematic analysis, combining both deductive and inductive coding strategies.

In the first step, an initial coding framework was established on the basis of the core variables and hypothesized pathways in the quantitative model. This framework included categories such as perceived interparental conflict, emotional insecurity, SWB, and sleep quality, as well as content related to the perceived importance of family relationships, expectations of family harmony, and concerns about family discord. In the second step, the transcripts were read repeatedly and subjected to open coding, with additional labels assigned to children’s concrete experiences, emotional responses, and meaning-making processes in order to identify themes that extended beyond the initial framework. In the third step, conceptually similar codes were grouped and integrated into higher-order themes. To enhance the reliability of the analysis, two researchers independently coded a subset of the interview transcripts, then compared their coding, discussed areas of disagreement, and refined the framework until a consensus coding system was achieved.

Sampling strategy and interview participants

Following the questionnaire survey, the study proceeded with semi-structured interviews. At the end of the questionnaire, students were asked whether they would be willing to participate in a follow-up interview; 178 students indicated willingness to do so. Given that the qualitative interviews were designed to serve the explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, their purpose was to present the possible psychological processes underlying the association between interparental conflict and poorer sleep quality, rather than to make statistically representative inferences about the full sample. At the same time, to avoid having the interview data reflect only the experiences of a single high-risk group, the study prioritized the inclusion of children with relatively high perceived interparental conflict and poorer sleep quality, while also including other types of children as contrasting and supplementary cases.

A combination of purposive sampling and maximum variation sampling was used. Potential interviewees were classified into two types according to their level of perceived interparental conflict and sleep quality status. Type 1 consisted of children with relatively high perceived interparental conflict and poorer sleep quality. Type 2 consisted of children with other experience profiles, including children with relatively high perceived interparental conflict but no obvious sleep problems, as well as children with low or moderate levels of perceived interparental conflict and varying sleep quality. Because this study focused on how interparental conflict may be associated with sleep quality through children’s psychological experiences, Type 1 children were prioritized during participant selection. At the same time, Type 2 children were also included to retain space for comparison across different experience profiles. Differences in life contexts, such as sex, school grade, and whether the child slept alone, were also considered to enhance sample heterogeneity. After screening, a list of 39 potential interviewees was formed. The guardians of these children were contacted individually and informed about the study purpose and ethical safeguards. Among them, 34 guardians agreed to their child’s participation and provided written informed consent.

Data organization and preliminary analysis were conducted alongside the interviews, and information saturation was used as the criterion for discontinuing sampling. Saturation was considered to have been reached when additional interviews no longer generated new core themes or relational patterns. In the actual study, after the 23rd interview was completed, the data had become largely repetitive and no new key concepts emerged and, therefore, further sampling was stopped. Among the final 23 interviewed children, 15 were Type 1 children and 8 were Type 2 children (see Table 2). Quantitative findings

Common method bias

Because the present study assessed interparental conflict, emotional insecurity, SWB, and sleep quality using questionnaire data collected through the same method and in the same assessment context, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to examine the possibility of common method bias. An unrotated exploratory factor analysis extracted 17 factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, and the largest factor accounted for 19.89% of the total variance, which was below the commonly used threshold of 40%. These results suggest that serious common method bias was unlikely to be present in the current data.

Children’s self-involvement and responsibility-oriented understanding of interparental conflict

The interviews showed that, when describing interparental conflict, the interviewed children tended to incorporate it into a self-related interpretive framework, reflecting clear patterns of self-involvement and responsibility-oriented understanding. When interparental conflict occurred, the children in this Chinese sample tended to understand it as a major family event closely related to their own state. For example, one child said, “When they argued, I felt confused inside, and I was always distracted when doing my homework. I just could not calm down.” (P3). Another child stated, “When the atmosphere at home was bad, I did not really want to talk, and I became a little careful. I did not dare to act as I usually did.” (P11). In addition, when explaining interparental conflict, some children attributed its causes to themselves, showing a tendency toward self-blame. For instance, one child said, “Sometimes I wondered whether it was because I had done something wrong that they became more likely to argue.” (P21).

The interviews also showed that this self-involving interpretation was grounded in children’s strong expectations of family harmony. When describing an ideal family, many children emphasized not material conditions, but “harmony,” “no arguments,” and “being happy together.” For example, one child said, “I think if people in the family are always arguing, then it does not count as a good family. It feels like you would not be happy staying there.” (P6). This suggests that, in the everyday understanding of the interviewed children, harmony, the absence of arguments, and a pleasant family atmosphere served as important reference points for evaluating the state of the family. When interparental conflict occurred, children were more likely to understand it as a disruption to the normal state of family life.

Persistent threat-related interpretations of conflict and the emergence of emotional insecurity

The interviews further showed that the interviewed children extended their responses to interparental conflict into future-oriented threat expectations. For example, one child said, “Even after they finished arguing during the day, I still thought about it at night. I was afraid they would argue again tomorrow, or that it would happen again a few days later. I did not know when things would really be okay.” (P8). This suggests that, in the understanding of many interviewed children, conflict did not automatically end when the argument stopped. Instead, it was further interpreted as a family risk that might recur.

Some children also understood repeated conflict as a sign that family relationships were deteriorating or might even break down. For example, one child said, “What I was most afraid of was not how badly they argued this time, but that they would always be like this in the future. Then I would feel that this family was becoming less and less like it used to be, and wonder whether it might really fall apart one day.” (P12). On this basis, the insecurity triggered by conflict also extended to children’s concerns about their own situation. For instance, one child stated, “When they argued badly, I would start thinking too much. What if neither of them wanted to take care of me anymore? Would no one look after me the way they used to?” (P15).

This interpretive pathway, moving from the current event to future risk and then to uncertainty about one’s own situation, indicates that children’s emotional insecurity involved a persistent sense of loss of control in everyday life. Overall, many interviewed children tended to extend interparental conflict into ongoing concerns about the future state of the family and the stability of their own care and daily life.

The formation of negative life evaluations and their extension into the sleep process

The interviews also showed that the emotional insecurity associated with interparental conflict may gradually extend into children’s overall negative evaluation of their own life circumstances. The effects of conflict appeared to lead some children, for a period of time, to interpret their daily life more negatively, describing their experiences as “not very good these days,” “nothing goes smoothly,” or “not having any energy.” For example, one child said, “During those days when they were unhappy, I felt gloomy all day. I could not really listen in class, and when I got home, I did not want to do anything. I just felt that nothing was interesting.” (P10).

Furthermore, some children used family conflict as a basis for evaluating whether their own life was happy. For instance, one child stated, “When they argued, I felt that everything was wrong during that period. It seemed like other families were all fine, but my family was always like this, and I also felt that my own life was not going well.” (P16). This suggests that, among the interviewed Chinese children in this study, some children connected family disharmony with a decline in their own life circumstances. Considering the emphasis on family harmony and relational stability within the Chinese family cultural context, this finding indicates that interparental conflict may become incorporated into children’s overall evaluation of whether daily life is stable, pleasant, and worth looking forward to.

When this overall negative evaluation of life persisted, its effects could extend into the pre-sleep period at night. For example, one child said, “After I lay down at night, those unhappy feelings from the daytime would come back, and the more I thought about them, the less I could fall asleep.” (P20). Another child mentioned, “I was about to sleep, but I was still worried about their arguing, and I would also have nightmares.” (P13).

Discussion

The preceding quantitative analyses showed that interparental conflict was not only significantly associated with poorer sleep quality in children, but also operated through emotional insecurity and SWB. The qualitative interviews further revealed that these processes were embedded in children’s concrete understandings of family relationships, their own situation within the family, and their broader life circumstances. Particularly within the Chinese family cultural context, family harmony is more readily regarded by children as a precondition for a stable life and emotional well-being; accordingly, interparental conflict is more likely to be interpreted as a signal of disrupted family order and, potentially, imbalance in life more broadly.

Descriptive findings on children’s sleep quality

The results shows that the mean sleep quality score in the present sample was 3.64, which is lower than the mean score of 5.05 reported in a previous study of Chinese children in Grades 3–6 [45]. Using a PSQI score greater than 7 as the criterion for identifying sleep problems, 87 children in the present study (9.7%) met the cut-off for poor sleep quality, a proportion lower than the 14.2% previously reported in Chinese samples of children under 13 years of age [46]. Overall, the children in the present study appeared to have relatively good sleep status; nonetheless, nearly one in ten met the screening criterion for sleep problems, indicating that sleep difficulties in primary school children remain a matter of concern.

The sample used in this study was drawn from a single school, and the observed prevalence of sleep problems was lower than that reported in prior studies, suggesting that the sample may have been relatively healthy. This feature may, to some extent, limit the generalizability of the findings. Methodological research has shown that when the range of variation on key variables is relatively restricted, correlations and effect estimates among variables may be attenuated [47]. Accordingly, although the associations among interparental conflict, emotional insecurity, SWB, and sleep quality reached statistical significance in the present study, the magnitude of these effects should be interpreted in light of the relatively healthy profile of the sample.

Interparental conflict and sleep quality

The present study found that interparental conflict was significantly associated with poorer sleep quality in children (r = 0.39, p < 0.001). Moreover, after exercising control for relevant variables, the regression coefficient for interparental conflict remained statistically significant (β = 0.11, p = 0.006). This suggests that interparental conflict is an important correlate of children’s sleep problems, but not the sole determinant; the development of children’s sleep quality remains jointly shaped by multiple family-level and individual-level factors. This finding is consistent with previous research [21].

The interview data further indicated that interparental conflict may be associated with sleep by placing children in a state of psychological preoccupation that persists into the night. Across the interviews, children repeatedly described relationship-related concerns that did not disappear after the conflict had ended. This suggests that the association between interparental conflict and sleep may first be reflected in the persistence of relational threat. Although the conflict may have ended at the behavioral level, children’s monitoring of family stability and relational predictability may continue. For this reason, nighttime may become a context in which these unresolved worries continue to operate.

This persistence of relational threat may also be intertwined with children’s self-contraction and responsibility-oriented adjustment after conflict. Chinese family culture emphasizes parent–child connection, family responsibility, and a shared order of everyday life, and children’s self-understanding is often embedded in family relationships. Within this relational interpretive framework, interparental conflict may be more readily understood by children as an event related to their own behavior, family position, and relational responsibility. Therefore, after conflict, some children may actively self-adjust in an attempt to reduce the possibility that family relationships will continue to deteriorate. Some children may become more cautious and silent, worrying that their words or actions might trigger further conflict between their parents. Others may partly attribute parental arguments to themselves and try to maintain the family atmosphere by speaking less, avoiding trouble, or behaving better. These accounts suggest that children may actively regulate their own behavior in an attempt to reduce further deterioration in family relationships, thereby taking on relational responsibilities that they should not have to bear.

From the perspective of sleep, such self-contraction and responsibility-oriented adjustment may increase children’s cognitive load and emotional burden before sleep. Before falling asleep, children may find it difficult to psychologically withdraw from the conflict context. They not only have to face uncertainty about whether the parental relationship has recovered, but also internal pressure about whether they should do something. In this way, interparental conflict may make it difficult for children to complete the transition from daytime tension to nighttime relaxation through persistent vigilance, relational scanning, and responsibility-oriented thinking, and may therefore be associated with sleep experiences such as difficulty falling asleep, restless sleep, or nightmares. This interpretation is also consistent with previous research emphasizing the roles of feeling caught, threat appraisal, and impaired family functioning in children’s subsequent adjustment after family conflict [48].

The mediating pathway of emotional insecurity

The results of the present study show that the indirect effect of interparental conflict on children’s sleep quality through emotional insecurity was 0.014, with a 95% confidence interval of [0.008, 0.021], indicating that this mediating pathway was statistically significant. This pathway accounted for 26.4% of the total effect, suggesting that emotional insecurity has substantial explanatory value in the association between interparental conflict and children’s sleep quality. Given that the direct effect remained significant, emotional insecurity may be understood as functioning as a partial mediator: that is, the association between interparental conflict and children’s sleep quality includes both a direct component and an indirect component transmitted through diminished emotional security. In this way, the link between interparental conflict and children’s sleep problems can be understood not merely as an association between family events and child outcomes, but as a more specific psychological process. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Keller and El-Sheikh [27] and is also in line with the core propositions of emotional security theory [49], thereby lending support to its applicability in a sample of Chinese children.

The interview data showed that, for the interviewed children, emotional insecurity associated with interparental conflict disrupted their basic judgments about the predictability of family relationships. Children began to question whether the family remained stable, whether relationships were still reliable, and whether their lives were still grounded in a dependable environment. This suggests that interparental conflict may activate a broader sense of uncertainty in children regarding the future of the family, the continuity of relationships, and their own situation.

The interview findings also suggested that this sense of uncertainty may persist because children repeatedly attend to emotional cues in the family after conflict. Children may use subtle signals such as parents’ facial expressions, tone of voice, silence, and the overall family atmosphere as the basis for judging whether “the conflict has really ended” and whether “the parental relationship has been repaired.” If parents remain cold in tone, silent toward each other, or if the family atmosphere remains tense after an argument, children may perceive that the conflict has not truly passed and that family relationships remain uncertain. In this process, the family context, which normally carries a sense of stability, may temporarily become an object that children need to observe repeatedly, continuously evaluate, and anticipate.

In light of the earlier discussion of the Chinese family cultural context, this attention to relational cues and emotional atmosphere may be more salient in a context that values family harmony, parent–child connection, and relational stability. It may further strengthen children’s ongoing attempts to confirm whether the family remains stable and secure. In this way, emotional insecurity may enter children’s subsequent adjustment processes through persistent relational vigilance and a sense of uncertainty in daily life, and it may further extend into pre-sleep relaxation and sleep experiences.

The mediating pathway of SWB

The results show that the indirect effect of interparental conflict on children’s sleep quality through SWB was 0.019, with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval of [0.014, 0.026], indicating that this mediating pathway is statistically significant. This pathway accounted for 35.8% of the total effect, which is larger than the separate mediating pathway through emotional insecurity. In addition, SWB shows a relatively strong correlation with sleep quality (r = −0.51, p < 0.001), and its standardized path coefficient for sleep quality is the largest in the model (β = −0.412, p < 0.001). Within the present model, these findings suggest that SWB is one of the psychological variables most closely associated with children’s current sleep status, and that its role is more proximal to sleep outcomes.

This finding indicates that the association between interparental conflict and children’s adjustment may be reflected in children’s overall evaluation of their life circumstances. This is consistent with previous research on adolescents [50]. The present study further suggests that this association is also observable in a Chinese child sample. More specifically, the relatively strong indirect pathway between interparental conflict and sleep problems appears to be reflected mainly in children’s reduced sense of life stability, pleasantness, and positive future expectations.

However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Previous research suggests that the association between SWB and sleep quality is likely bidirectional. Better sleep may predict higher subsequent positive well-being, and higher positive well-being may also be associated with better subsequent sleep [51, 52]. Therefore, a more cautious interpretation of the present findings is that SWB constitutes a psychologically meaningful pathway in the association between interparental conflict and children’s sleep quality. At the same time, SWB homeostasis theory may provide auxiliary theoretical support for this pathway [29], but it is insufficient to establish a unidirectional effect of SWB on sleep quality. The directionality of this association still needs to be further examined in longitudinal research.

The interview material further suggests that, when children described interparental conflict, they were expressing how family conflict gradually changed their sense of what constituted a normal state of daily life. What children experienced was a shift in the family environment from one that should have been supportive, relaxing, and dependable to a living context characterized by persistent tension, emotional suppression, and a lack of harmony. This shift led some children to extend their experiences of family conflict into a negative interpretation of their overall quality of life.

This process can also be understood from the perspective of children’s construction of the self within family relationships. Previous research has suggested that children’s understanding of the self is often embedded in parent–child relationships, family responsibilities, and the shared order of everyday life [53]. The stability of the parental relationship may be closely related to children’s basic confirmation of whether they meet expectations for the self and whether they are loved and accepted. Accordingly, interparental conflict may undermine children’s self-confirmation and sense of order derived from family relationships, thereby lowering their SWB and making them more likely to feel that life is no longer stable, smooth, or worth looking forward to.

Sleep depends, in part, on whether individuals can temporarily withdraw from daytime stress experiences at night and restore a relatively calm and relaxed psychological state [54]. Therefore, when interparental conflict continuously reduces children’s positive feelings about family and daily life, the fatigue, emotional suppression, and sense of disorder that has accumulated during the day may carry over into the night. This may make it more difficult for children to enter a relaxed state before sleep and may be further associated with pre-sleep distress and poorer sleep quality.

The serial indirect pathway of emotional insecurity and SWB

The present study has found that the serial indirect effect linking interparental conflict to poorer sleep quality through the pathway of emotional insecurity → SWB was 0.004, with a 95% confidence interval of [0.001, 0.007], indicating statistical significance. However, in terms of the extent of relative effect, this serial pathway accounted for only 7.5% of the total effect, which is substantially smaller than the two specific indirect pathways through emotional insecurity alone (26.4%) and SWB alone (35.8%). This structural difference suggests that the sequential pathway is more appropriately understood as a supplementary extension of existing psychological processes.

The current model remains theoretically meaningful because the research question for this study focuses on children’s poorer sleep quality. According to emotional security theory, interparental conflict is associated with children’s concerns about the predictability and safety of family relationships. Such emotional insecurity may undermine children’s overall evaluation of the stability, pleasantness, and their future expectation of daily life. Lower SWB, in turn, may indicate insufficient positive experiences and psychological recovery resources, and may be associated with poorer pre-sleep relaxation and sleep quality. Therefore, within the framework of the present study, SWB is positioned as an evaluative psychological pathway linking insecurity in family relationships with poorer sleep quality, rather than being directly equated with proximal sleep-related mechanisms such as pre-sleep arousal or rumination.

At the same time, this study also acknowledges that another ordering of the variables is theoretically plausible. Interparental conflict may firstly contribute to sleep disturbance through emotional insecurity, and persistent sleep problems may then further reduce children’s positive outlook, daytime functioning, and life satisfaction, thereby leading to lower SWB. Indeed, the association between SWB and sleep quality may be bidirectional, and poorer sleep quality itself may become an important source of impaired quality of life and reduced well-being in children. Therefore, the current cross-sectional data cannot determine the causal direction between the two pathways of SWB → sleep quality and sleep quality → SWB. The serial mediation model reported in this study should be understood as a theoretically proposed and statistically supported possible pathway, rather than as evidence that rules out competing models.

Future research should adopt longitudinal designs, cross-lagged models, or multiple-time-point mediation models to further compare the theoretical plausibility and empirical support for different orderings of these variables. In addition, it should be noted that the results of this study may also be influenced by unmeasured common factors. The associations among interparental conflict, emotional insecurity, SWB, and sleep quality may partly reflect the influence of shared antecedents. For example, parents’ own mental health status and family socioeconomic stress may increase the likelihood of family conflict while also undermining children’s psychological adjustment and sleep. Moreover, the possible role of more stable family vulnerability factors cannot be completely ruled out. Therefore, the serial mediation findings identified in this study are better understood as a theoretically consistent explanatory framework, rather than as a definitive confirmation of a single mechanism among the variables. Future studies should further include variables such as parental mental health and family stress, and they should examine these alternative explanations using longitudinal or more rigorous research designs.

The interview material provides more concrete support for this interpretation. In their narratives, children first showed persistent concern about the stability of family relationships. On this basis, this relational uncertainty gradually extended into broader evaluations of daily life, so that the perception that “something was wrong in the family” was further transformed into a sense that the overall state of life was no longer normal. In this sense, the explanatory value of the serial indirect pathway does not lie in establishing a strict temporal sequence, but in revealing a process of experiential extension: interparental conflict is first associated with children’s judgments about the reliability of family relationships, and these judgments are then carried into their overall evaluation of daily life, which is further associated with sleep problems. Therefore, this pathway is more appropriately understood as a continuous process extending from relational evaluation to life evaluation. This finding is generally consistent with previous research on the associations of family conflict with positive affect, life satisfaction, and overall well-being [50, 55].

Children’s interpretations of interparental conflict in the Chinese cultural context

Previous research based on non-Chinese samples has shown that children’s worry, vigilance, self-blame, and sleep disturbance following interparental conflict are not responses unique to Chinese culture [56]. Accordingly, the purpose of discussing the Chinese family context in this study is not to demonstrate that Chinese children show different types of responses, but to explain how these general responses may be organized, intensified, and given concrete meaning within the Chinese family cultural structure.

Developmental psychological research suggests that children actively interpret conflict situations. The cognitive-contextual framework indicates that, when children are exposed to interparental conflict, they evaluate whether the conflict is threatening, why it occurred, whether they themselves are involved, and whether they are able to cope with it; these appraisals may further shape their emotional and behavioral responses [13].The dynamic constructivist approach to culture and cognition suggests that cultural knowledge can be activated in specific situations and participate in individuals’ construction of event meaning [57]. Accordingly, how children understand interparental conflict may be influenced by culturally embedded assumptions about the self, parents, and family relationships. Children aged 9–12 years are already capable of being influenced by family norms and cultural values. For example, a study of Chinese students in Grades 4–6 shows that children in this age group can express cultural beliefs related to parent–child responsibility, parental expectations, and family roles [58].

From a cultural perspective, Chinese and Western contexts differ in the cultural logics through which the self is formed and understood. Different cultures vary in the extent to which they emphasize individual autonomy and relational connectedness [59]. The modern Western conception of the individual has developed within a long history of religious, moral, and institutional traditions, and tends to construe the self as an independent entity with relatively clear boundaries, autonomy, and internal attributes [53]. By contrast, in the Chinese cultural context, which can be traced to Confucian ethical traditions, individuals’ emotions, cognitions, and behaviors are more closely embedded in family, kinship, and intergenerational relationships, and the self is more likely to be understood through the relational network between the self and the family, and between the self and others.

This cultural difference is also reflected in children’s understanding of parental roles. In many Western cultural contexts, children are more often positioned within a developmental framework of future independent personhood, and parenting tends to support the gradual development of children’s capacity for self-choice, self-expression, and independent action. In contrast, Chinese culture tends to position children as members of family relationships through family ethics, kinship networks, and everyday parenting practices. Parental roles are therefore more closely embedded in educational investment, behavioral guidance, parent–child responsibility, and family expectations [60].

Additionally, the value placed on harmony and conflict management in Chinese culture may also shape how children understand interparental conflict. The concept of harmony in Chinese culture influences how individuals understand and manage conflict, particularly because the orientation toward avoiding relational rupture is closely related to conflict management [61].

Furthermore, individuals in East Asian cultures tend to attend not only to the target person, but also to background figures, interactional contexts, and the relational atmosphere when judging others’ emotions. By contrast, individuals in Western cultures tend to rely more strongly on the target person’s explicit emotional expression [62]. These findings suggest that emotion perception in East Asian cultures is characterized by greater contextual sensitivity. Combined with the interview data of the present study, what deserves attention in the Chinese family cultural context is how children may understand interparental conflict not merely as a specific argument, but as a signal that the order of family life has been disrupted. In a context that emphasizes family harmony, parent–child connection, role responsibilities, and a shared order of everyday life, the parental relationship constitutes an important reference point through which children understand their own situation and daily life circumstances. Therefore, when parents are in conflict, some children experience the family as “no longer the way it used to be,” and feel that the familiar state of everyday life has been interrupted. This transformation of meaning extends interparental conflict from a localized relational event to children’s broader judgment about the overall functioning of the family.

More specifically, this transformation of meaning may also change children’s understanding of their own position for action within the family. Interparental conflict may lead children to begin thinking about how they should act within such a tense relational context. In the interviews, some children mentioned that they become more careful and silent, worrying that saying or doing something wrong might cause their parents to continue arguing. This suggests that interparental conflict enters children’s judgments about their own roles and behavioral boundaries. Children begin to connect “what I should do” with “whether the family can become better,” thereby forming a responsibility-oriented form of self-adjustment. This is precisely the point that requires further explanation within the Chinese family cultural context: children do not simply attribute parental conflict to themselves; rather, they incorporate their own behavior into their consideration of whether family relationships can become less tense.

In addition, the psychological implications of interparental conflict may be sustained through children’s renewed understanding of their own position within the family. The more children connect their own behavior with the family atmosphere, the more difficult it may be for them to fully disengage from the conflict. Even after the argument has ended, they may continue to observe their parents’ attitudes, adjust their own words and actions, and try to avoid triggering further tension. This sustained self-restraint transforms interparental conflict from an event that has already passed into a relational task that still needs to be managed in daily life. In this sense, the Chinese family cultural context may provide an interpretive framework through which worry, vigilance, and self-blame are organized as an ongoing responsibility to maintain family relationships.

This responsibility-oriented understanding also helps explain why interparental conflict may be associated with SWB and sleep experiences. When a child begins to understand her/himself within the family as someone who needs to maintain a harmonious atmosphere, avoid conflict, and bear the emotional consequences, family life loses part of its original sense of ease and restorative function. Children’s interview expressions such as “I did not want to talk,” “I did not really dare to act as I usually did,” and “nothing felt interesting” indicate that their daily lives were covered by a persistent form of self-monitoring. Therefore, the discussion of the Chinese family cultural context in this study ultimately aims to explain how these general responses may be reorganized within the Chinese family cultural structure: worry is organized as a continuous confirmation of whether family harmony can be restored; self-blame is organized as reflection on whether one’s own behavior meets family expectations; vigilance is organized as continuous observation of parents’ tone of voice, facial expressions, and the family atmosphere; and sleep disturbance becomes the continuation at night of relational stress that could not be resolved during the day.

Theoretical and practical implications

This study not only advances the understanding of the association between interparental conflict and children’s sleep quality from a general family risk–outcome relationship to a more specific psychological process, but it also suggests that this process is culturally contextualized. In this regard, the present study provides new empirical support for understanding how family conflict may be associated with child development within a specific cultural context.

The findings also suggest that interventions for children’s sleep problems should not be limited to sleep hygiene education or behavioral training, but they should also take into account the quality of family relationships and the related psychological processes. For parents, reducing destructive conflict is certainly important; however, it is equally important to repair children’s sense of security after conflict occurs. Parents should help children to understand that interparental conflict is not caused by them and that they should not be responsible for maintaining family balance, thereby reducing responsibility-oriented self-regulation and persistent worry. At the same time, parents’ love and care should be conveyed through stable emotional responses, clear relational reassurance, and consistent companionship. Children need to feel clearly that they are always loved, accepted, and cared for, that interparental conflict does not mean the disappearance of family support, and that they do not need to take responsibility for reconciling parental relationships.

For schools and mental health professionals, attention should be paid to the family contexts underlying children’s sleep difficulties. In particular, practitioners should be alert to signs of emotional insecurity, reduced SWB, repetitive thinking, and difficulty disengaging from family tension before sleep. Support may include age-appropriate explanation and reassurance after conflict, encouragement of emotional expression, stable bedtime transition routines, and explicit confirmation of security. These strategies may help reduce children’s psychological involvement before sleep and nighttime vigilance.

Compared with interventions that focus only on sleep behaviors, approaches centered on repairing family relationships, supporting children’s sense of security, and restoring positive daily life experiences may be more targeted and developmentally appropriate.

Limitations and future directions

This study combined questionnaire survey data with child interviews to examine the psychological processes through which interparental conflict may influence children’s sleep quality via emotional insecurity and SWB, and it has further sought to provide a contextualized interpretation of these associations within the Chinese cultural context. To some extent, the findings enrich research on the family-related psychological mechanisms underlying children’s sleep problems and provide empirical support for related intervention efforts. Nevertheless, given the constraints of the research design and scope, several limitations should be acknowledged.

(1) Sample source. The study used cluster sampling, and all participants were recruited from the same primary school. The geographical and educational context of the sample was therefore relatively concentrated. This design helped reduce potential interference from school-level differences, but it also limits the generalizability of the findings to children from different regions, different types of schools, and different family backgrounds. In addition, the study has focused on upper-grade primary school children aged 9–12 years. The findings should therefore be interpreted within this specific developmental stage. Given that children at different ages may differ in sleep development, emotion regulation capacity, and their understanding of family relationships, the results of this study should not be directly generalized to younger children or to adolescents.

(2) Research method. All variables in this study were derived from children’s self-reporting collected at a single time point. Although procedural remedies and Harman’s single-factor test were used as preliminary controls, systematic error associated with a single method cannot be fully ruled out in the absence of multi-informant data. Accordingly, the present findings are better understood as supporting the existence of associations among the variables, whereas the strength of these associations should still be interpreted with caution.

(3) Research design. Although the proposed sequence of interparental conflict → emotional insecurity → SWB → sleep quality has a certain theoretical basis, the present cross-sectional design means that the findings are more appropriately interpreted as statistical associations consistent with this theoretical ordering rather than as evidence for strong causal inference. The temporal ordering of this continuous process remains to be examined in future longitudinal research. At the same time, alternative explanatory models cannot be ruled out. For example, poorer sleep quality may in turn undermine children’s SWB and sense of relational security, thereby heightening their sensitivity to interparental conflict.

(4) Cultural mechanism. Although this study interpreted the psychological effects of interparental conflict on children within the Chinese family cultural context, it did not directly assess the extent to which children had internalized the relevant cultural values. Therefore, the cultural mechanism proposed here has not been directly and empirically verified. As such, the conclusions regarding culture should be understood primarily as contextualized interpretations rather than as strict empirical demonstrations.

Building on these limitations, future research may be extended and deepened in several directions. First, in terms of sample selection, future studies could broaden the sampling frame to include children from different regions, different types of schools, and different socioeconomic backgrounds, thereby improving the external validity of the findings and allowing comparison of similarities and differences in the relationship between interparental conflict and children’s sleep quality across groups. Second, with respect to data sources, future work could integrate parent reports, teacher ratings, peer information, and objective sleep indicators (e.g., sleep diaries) to construct a multi-informant, multi-method assessment system, thereby further reducing common method bias and strengthening the robustness of the findings. Third, in terms of research design, longitudinal follow-up or cross-lagged designs could be employed to examine more rigorously the temporal ordering and dynamic relationships among interparental conflict, emotional insecurity, SWB, and sleep quality, as well as being used to compare other plausible alternative models. Fourth, regarding research content, future studies could directly quantify the extent to which children have internalized cultural values such as family harmony and relational responsibility, thereby providing a more in-depth account of the culturally contextualized mechanisms through which family conflict affects child development.

Conclusion

This study shows that the association between interparental conflict and children’s sleep quality unfolds through children’s internal psychological experiences. Specifically, the present study situates this association within an explanatory framework extending from impaired emotional security to reduced SWB. The qualitative materials further suggest that, for children, the meaning of interparental conflict is not limited to a single episode of parental argument. Some children continue to monitor whether family relationships have been restored, whether their own situation remains stable, and whether daily life is still secure after the conflict. The significance of the Chinese family cultural context lies in its usefulness for understanding how these relatively general child responses may be organized around family harmony, parent–child connection, and relational responsibility.

This study provides new empirical support for understanding the psychological processes through which family conflict is associated with children’s development, as well as the cultural conditions under which these processes may acquire specific meanings. Future research may build on this study by adopting longitudinal designs and directly measuring children’s internalization of cultural values such as family harmony and relational responsibility. Such work would help further examine the temporal ordering and boundary conditions among interparental conflict, internal psychological experiences, and sleep quality. This would not only deepen the theoretical explanation of these associations, but it would also provide a stronger basis for culturally responsive family interventions.
