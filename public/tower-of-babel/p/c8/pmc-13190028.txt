Automated identification of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and asthma using impulse oscillometry combined with quantitative computed tomography parameters and radiomics features
Authors: Ruiping Qiao, Wenxiu Zhang, Fengxiang Huang, Yunqi Zhang, Jintao Liu, Qilong Wang, Lu Zhou, Huanqin Wang, Xiaoyun Liang, Lijun Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma are two common chronic inflammatory diseases that are difficult to distinguish clinically. While pulmonary function tests (PFTs) are considered the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) standard for diagnosing these two diseases, they have inherent drawbacks, such as heavily relying on patient cooperation. This study aimed to explore the ability of impulse oscillometry system (IOS), quantitative computed tomography (QCT) imaging and radiomics features for differentiating between COPD and asthma.MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 151 patients, including 67 COPD patients and 84 asthma patients who completed inspiratory high-resolution computed tomography (CT) scans with recorded demographics, PFTs and IOS data from January 2021 to September 2023 from The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University. The NeuLungCARE-QA software (Neusoft Medical Systems Co., Ltd., Shenyang, China) was used to extract QCT parameters. The radiomics features were calculated using the Pyradiomics package. After features selection, the logistic regression method was used for model construction. An additional 37 patients were recruited as the external validation set to validate the model’s generalization.ResultsThere were significant differences in PFT parameters (P<0.001), IOS indices (P<0.05), and emphysema parameters (P<0.001) between COPD and asthma groups. Model 6, which combined IOS indices, QCT parameters, and radiomics features, achieved the best performance with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values of 0.959 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.918–0.989], 0.960 (95% CI: 0.909–0.999) and 0.949 (95% CI: 0.871–0.999) in the training set, testing set, external validation set, respectively. The DeLong test comparing the fusion features-based Model 6 with the PFTs-based Model 1 showed no significant differences in training set, testing set and external validation set, respectively.ConclusionsThe IOS indices, along with quantitative CT imaging and radiomics features, could provide a reliable tool for differentiating asthma from COPD patients. This approach could serve as an alternative and/or a complement method for patients who find it difficult to perform PFTs.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Our study has demonstrated that significant differences in pulmonary function tests (PFTs), impulse oscillometry system (IOS), and quantitative computed tomography (QCT) parameters exist between the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) group and the asthma group. Furthermore, the model integrating IOS, QCT, and radiomics parameters can distinguish COPD from asthma, achieving the best performance with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve values of 0.959 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.918–0.989], 0.960 (95% CI: 0.909–0.999) and 0.949 (95% CI: 0.871–0.999) in the training set, testing set, and external validation set, respectively.

What is known and what is new?

• Compared with PFTs, IOS requires minimal effort and cooperation, making it suitable for a broader range of patients. However, IOS is less commonly used to differentiate COPD from asthma. Chest computed tomography (CT), by contrast, is widely used for lung cancer screening and diagnosis, and its role in differentiating between COPD and asthma is underestimated.

• This study innovatively constructs a model to differentiate COPD from asthma by integrating IOS, QCT, and radiomics parameters.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This model has the potential to act as an alternative or supplementary tool for patients with COPD and asthma who are unable to undergo conventional PFTs.

• For patients who experience difficulty in undergoing PFTs, clinicians may consider utilizing IOS and CT for the differential diagnosis of COPD and asthma.

Introduction

Chronic inflammatory diseases of the airways, represented by chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma, are common diseases of the respiratory system (1), affecting over 500 million people worldwide (2). They consume a large amount of healthcare resources every year and bring a heavy burden to patients and society (3,4). Asthma and COPD exhibit several similar clinical features including symptoms and physiologic impairments (5). In addition, there is also partial overlap in the pathological characteristics of the two diseases, such as promotion of airway hyper-reactivity, common susceptibility genes, lung growth during the fetal stage/early childhood and respiratory dysfunction (6), further increasing the difficulty of distinguishing between the two diseases clinically. A previous study revealed that around 15–45% of patients with COPD may have co-existed asthma-COPD (7). The 2024 Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) reported that asthma may be a risk factor for the development of chronic airflow obstruction and COPD (8). Therefore, accurate differential diagnosis of the two diseases and the development of reasonable treatment measures are crucial for improving patient symptoms and reducing the burden of the disease.

Pulmonary function tests (PFTs) are considered the gold standard for the clinical diagnosis of COPD and asthma (9). However, PFTs rely on patient cooperation, have limited application scope, and exhibit poor reproducibility (10). The impulse oscillometry system (IOS) is an independent technique that can be used during spontaneous tidal breathing or respiratory support ventilation, recording gas flow velocity and airway resistance at different frequencies (11). Compared with PFTs, IOS requires minimal effort and cooperation, making it suitable for a wide range of patients including children, elders, and people with disabilities (12). Many studies have explored the clinical value of IOS technique. For example, Li et al. found that IOS indices were more sensitive in detecting small airway dysfunction than spirometry in subjects with chronic respiratory symptoms and preserved pulmonary function, and they correlated better with symptoms (13). Chen et al. identified the potential roles of IOS indices, such as airway resistance and reactance, in predicting COPD for chest physicians and clinicians (14). Additionally, Sugawara et al. reported that IOS indices were significantly correlated with the severity of asthma, suggesting that could be an effective tool for selecting inhaled corticosteroids and evaluating asthma phenotype (15). Xu et al. demonstrated that IOS indices had good long-term repeatability in both asthma and COPD (16). Therefore, IOS, as a non-invasive test, provides rich information that can sensitively reflect dynamic changes in respiratory status and mechanics. While these studies have primarily focused on a sole disease, the ability of IOS indicators to differentiate COPD from asthma warrants further exploration.

Increased airway resistance, narrowing, and remodeling are important manifestations of the progression of chronic respiratory diseases (17). Computed tomography (CT) scanning is a non-invasive and highly reproducible tool for the objective assessment of changes in lung structure. Recently, the quantitative computed tomography (QCT) imaging technique has been proposed to explore the pathophysiology, diagnosis and outcomes in chronic respiratory diseases, which could provide the quantitative analysis including the degree of emphysema, airway inflammation and remodeling (18,19). QCT measurements have been shown to correlate strongly with pulmonary function tests and predict exacerbation risk and mortality in patients with chronic respiratory diseases (20). In both COPD and asthma research, many studies have demonstrated the clinical significance of QCT technique. QCT technique plays a critical role in COPD management by improving early detection, addressing disease heterogeneity, and monitoring progression, as noted by the Asian Society of Thoracic Radiology (21). For asthma, particularly severe asthma, QCT technique helps identify imaging-based phenotypes and shows a good correlation with lung functional decline, thereby guiding clinicians in understanding the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying these phenotypes (22). Additionally, QCT parameters can reflect the similarity and disparity in lesions, such as airway remodeling, air trapping, and emphysema, between COPD patients and asthma patients (23,24). While QCT method provides valuable insights, radiomics has emerged as a complementary method that can extract rich information from medical images, bridging medical imaging and precision medicine (25). Radiomics has been applied in COPD research for early prediction (26,27), identification and severity staging (28,29), and prognostic analysis in recent years (30). However, we found that only one study explored the value of radiomics for staging patients with asthma, demonstrating that the radiomics model could offer promising method for the clinical diagnosis of asthma patients (31). Thus, the effectiveness of radiomics features in differentiating asthma from COPD remains to be determined. This underscores the critical need for further research in this area to improve clinical decision-making and enhance patient outcomes.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore the diagnostic value of IOS indices, QCT parameters, and radiomics features in distinguishing COPD from asthma, and seek an optimal method for the patients who find it difficult to perform PFTs. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2558/rc).

Methods

Participants

This study involves a retrospective analysis of patients who presented at The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University between January 2021 and September 2023, exhibiting chronic respiratory symptoms primarily characterized by chest tightness, cough, and sputum production. The inclusion criteria and exclusion criteria are as follows. Inclusion criteria: (I) the presence of non-fully reversible airflow obstruction [i.e., forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) <0.7 post-bronchodilation] measured by spirometry, confirming the diagnosis of COPD according to Global Strategy for The Diagnosis, Management, and Prevention of COPD (2024 Report), which can be found on the GOLD website (www.goldcopd.org); (II) the diagnosis of asthma is mainly based on typical clinical symptoms and reversible airflow restriction according to Global Strategy for Asthma Management and Prevention (2024 update), available on the GINA website (www.ginasthma.org); (III) all patients were diagnosed in our hospital and have complete PFTs data and inspiratory CT scans. Exclusion criteria: (I) patients with insufficient clinical data; (II) patients who have not undergone IOS or CT examinations; (III) patients with asthma-COPD overlap.

Finally, a total of 151 patients were included as the discovery set in this study, which were divided into COPD groups with 67 patients and asthma groups with 84 patients. Additionally, we enrolled an external validation set of 37 patients which included 21 patients with asthma and 16 patients with COPD. The framework for patient enrollment is shown in Figure 1.

This study conformed with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the ethics committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (No. 2024-KY-1413-001) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

CT imaging protocol

All participants underwent CT scans while placed in the supine position during end-inspiration. Patients with breathing difficulties received guidance and consultation before the scan, and all scans were performed after breath-hold practice. The discovery set comprised CT scans with a thickness of 1.00 mm, obtained using four CT systems: SOMATOM X. cite and SOMATOM go. Top (Siemens Healthineers AG, Forchheim, Germany); as well as CT 6000 and IQon-Spectral CT (Philips Medical Systems, Best, Netherlands). The external validation set included CT scans with a thickness of 1.25 mm, obtained using Revolution CT (GE Healthcare; Somatom Force, Siemens). The CT imaging protocol involved a voltage of 120 kV, a tube current ranging from 30 to 100 mA, and a field of view of 512 mm × 512 mm.

PFT and IOS parameters

The Jaeger MasterScreen PFTs system (Jaeger Co., Wurzburg, Germany) was used to quantify PFTs and IOS parameters, with the operating procedure following the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society guidelines (32). The following PFTs parameters were evaluated: FEV1, predicted FEV1 (FEV1%_pred), FEV1/FVC, maximal expiratory flow at 50% (MEF50%), maximal expiratory flow at 75% (MEF75%), predicted MEF50% (MEF50%_pred) and predicted MEF75% (MEF75%_pred). For IOS indices, the evaluated parameters included resonant frequency (Fres), respiratory resistance at 5 Hz (R5), predicted R5 (R5_pred), respiratory resistance at 20 Hz (R20), predicted R20 (R20_pred), the difference between R5 and R20 (R5–R20), and respiratory reactance at 5 Hz (X5). Each measurement was repeated three times, and the average values were used for subsequent analyses.

Assessment of emphysema and airway change

The Quantitative CT metric for airways and emphysema were automatically computed using the NeuLungCARE-QA software (Neusoft Medical Systems Co., Ltd., Shenyang, China) (33). The software analyzes the degree of emphysema by assessing lung parenchymal density, including percentage of low attenuation areas of the whole lung and each lobe including right upper lobe, right middle lobe, right lower lobe, left lower lobe, and left upper lobe, defined as lung tissue with a density ≤−950 or −910 Hounsfield unit (HU) on inspiratory CT (LAA_950 or LAA_910), and the CT attenuation at the 15th percentile of the CT histogram (perc15). In addition, this software provides the cross-sectional measurement of 0–4 generation airways characteristics, including the maximum, mean, and minimum wall thickness, as well as the maximum, mean, and minimum wall diameters. It also measures square root of wall area of a hypothetical airway with an internal perimeter of 10 mm (Pi10), and normalized airway metrics such as the percentage of airway wall area (WA).

Radiomics feature extraction

The U-net (R231) deep learning model (https://github.com/JoHof/lungmask) was used for automatic lung segmentation (34). The PyRadiomics (version 3.0.1) package for Python (version 3.10.0) was used for the extraction of radiomics features from CT imaging. First, we converted the image format from Dicom to Nii.gz. Second, the images were normalized to a scale of 100 and resampled to 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm, with a bin-width set to 25. The original image features included 14 shape features, 18 first-order histogram-based features, and 75 texture features. Additional features derived from Laplacian of Gaussian images with sigma values of 3.0 and 5.0, and wavelet-transformed images filtered into eight subbands using a 3D wavelet filter, were computed, resulting in a total of 1,037 radiomics features extracted from each patient.

Feature analysis

A two-stage feature selection strategy, including F-test and LASSO regression, was employed to reduce the risk of model overfitting. The F-test was used to determine whether the variability between groups was greater than the variability within group observations. We combined the 50 clusters bootstrapping with F-test to selected features with P<0.05 for next step feature selection, ensuring reliable outcomes. To avoid selecting 0 features in the first step method, we will appropriately reduce the number of bootstrapping clusters. Then, L1 regularization of LASSO method combined with five-fold cross validations was used to select the final features.

Model development and validation

The discovery set was stratified into a training set and a testing set at a ratio of 7:3. First, the training set data were standardized using Z-score normalization to eliminate the dimensional influence between indices. After features selection, and 6 models were constructed to compare the classification ability of different feature sets. The validity of the model built by the selected features is tested in 30% of the testing set, and the results of the external validation set to verify the generalization of the model. The six models are defined as follows: Model 1 based on PFTs parameters; Model 2 based on IOS indices; Model 3 based on QCT parameters; Model 4 based on radiomics features; Model 5 based on both QCT parameters and radiomics features; Model 6 based on IOS indices, QCT parameters and radiomics features. The external validation set of 37 patients, which included 21 patients with asthma and 16 patients with COPD, was used to assess the robustness of the six models. All the analyses were performed using the “Scikit-learn” package in Python (https://scikit-learn.org/stable/index.html).

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk method. Continuous variables that followed a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while those that did not follow a normal distribution were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages (N, %). Continuous and categorical variables were compared using two samples t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, or Chi-squared tests, as appropriate. Five evaluation metrics including the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, and F1-score, were used to evaluate the reliability of models. The DeLong test was used to analyze the significant differences between the AUC values of different models. A nomogram and decision curve analysis (DCA) were drawn to illustrate the effectiveness of model. All the statistical analyses were two-tailed, and a P<0.05 was considered to be significant.

The process of feature selection

After two-step feature selection, the optimal features were determined for model construction as shown in Figures S1-S5. For PFT parameters, seven features were selected using the ANOVA F-test with 50 bootstrap iterations (Figure S1), and three of them were subsequently selected using LASSO with five-fold cross-validation (Figure S2). Using the same feature selection approach, three IOS indices (Figure S1 and Figure S3) and two QCT parameters (Figure S1 and Figure S4) were independently selected. For radiomics feature selection, ANOVA F-test with 10 bootstrap iterations followed by LASSO were employed to select two features (Figure S1 and Figure S5).

Comparison of performance in distinguishing COPD from asthma in different models

As shown in Table 4 and Figure 2, Model 1 based on PFTs parameters achieved the highest AUC in training set, testing set, and external validation set, indicating that PFTs parameters had the excellent ability for distinguishing COPD from asthma. Model 2 based on IOS indices obtained AUCs of 0.925 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.855–0.967], 0.958 (95% CI: 0.915–0.994), and 0.932 (95% CI: 0.864–0.987) in the training set, testing set, and external validation set, respectively; the DeLong test results in the testing set and external validation set showed no significant difference compared to Model 1 (P=0.24 and 0.27). This means that IOS indices also have good ability for distinguishing COPD from asthma.

However, the performance of Model 4 based on radiomics features was relatively poor with AUCs of 0.762 (95% CI: 0.695–0.829), 0.727(95% CI: 0.559–0.878), 0.753 (95% CI: 0.612–0.912) in the training, testing, and external validation sets.

Model 5 combined QCT parameters and radiomics features improved the prediction results in the testing set compared to Model 3 based only on QCT parameters and Model 4 based only on radiomics features (Model 5: 0.785 vs. Model 3: 0.746 vs. Model 4: 0.727). When not considering PFTs parameters, Model 6 combining IOS indices, QCT parameters, and radiomics features achieved the best results. In the training set, the accuracy, recall, precision, F1-score, AUC of Model 6 were 0.886, 0.809, 0.927, 0.864, and 0.959 (95% CI: 0.918–0.989) respectively. In the testing set, the accuracy, recall, precision, F1-score, AUC of Model 6 were 0.891, 0.800, 0.941, 0.865, and 0.960 (95% CI: 0.909–0.999), respectively. In the external validation set, the accuracy, recall, precision, F1-score, AUC of Model 6 were 0.838, 0.813, 0.813, 0.813, and 0.949 (95% CI: 0.871–0.999), respectively. The DeLong tests comparing Model 6 with Model 1 showed no significant difference in all sets (P=0.11, 0.31, and 0.39 for training, testing, and external validation sets).

Discussion

The current study assessed the differences in PFTs parameters, respiratory mechanics, emphysema parameters, and airway parameters between COPD patients and asthma patients. It was found that patients with COPD had worse airflow limitation, higher resistance values, more severe emphysema and airway lesions compared to asthma patients. In addition, we constructed the six models to automatically identify the COPD and asthma, among which the Model 2 based on IOS indices obtained AUCs of 0.925, 0.958, 0.932 in the training, testing, and external validation sets, demonstrating that IOS indices had the good ability for the differential diagnosis between asthma and COPD. In particular, the multi-feature fusion Model 6 including IOS indices, QCT parameter and radiomics features achieved the best AUC (training set: 0.959; testing set: 0.960; external validation set: 0.949) and the DeLong test indicated no significant differences between this model and Model 1 based on PFTs in the training, testing, and external validation sets; this demonstrated that the multi-feature fusion model may be a clinically feasible tool for COPD and asthma classification.

Previous studies showed that the predominant airway disorders in asthma and COPD were central and peripheral, respectively, and IOS can reflect the difference in airway disorders by R5 (central component) and R5–R20 (peripheral component) between asthma and COPD (35). Kanda et al. explored the differences in physiological properties of the airways between asthma and COPD using IOS. In that study, it was shown that the IOS indices R5–R20 increased in the COPD group compared with those in the asthma group; R5 increased in the COPD and asthma groups compared to the control group (36). Kim et al. found that IOS indices of Fres, R5, R20, and R5–R20 were higher, whereas X5 values were lower in asthmatic patients than in non-asthmatic patients (37). These remarkable pathological and physiological changes correlated with the severity of airways inflammation, which were consistent with our finding that higher Fres, R5, R20, R5–R20 and lower X5 were identified in COPD patients compared to asthma (Table 1) due to the more severe airways inflammation in COPD patients in our study. From a physiological perspective, the lesion of COPD always originates from the small airways, and a significant increase in small airway resistance will be observed when extensive lesions occur in the small airways, likely leading to the increase in total airway resistance in COPD patients (38). In COPD patients, as alveoli continue to expand, lung tissue overexpansion leads to decreased lung compliance. Therefore, the sum of elastic resistance and inertial resistance of the lung and thorax, i.e., X5 value, was significantly reduced. Asthma is generally considered to be a disease mainly involving the atmospheric tract, so during the acute episode of asthma, IOS parameters, such as R20 that reflect the dysfunction of the atmospheric tract, may be abnormal. Specifically, Kitaguchi et al. found that compared with COPD patients, R20 significantly increased in asthma patients with persistent airflow limitation (39). However, in the present study, the R20 levels in the COPD group were significantly higher than those in the asthma group, which may be related to the fact that most subjects in the asthma group were mild patients seeking outpatient treatment. These findings indicate that IOS can reflect the pulmonary characteristics of respiratory physiology and mechanics in patients with COPD, which plays an important role in the evaluation of COPD and asthma. In addition, a previous study has also demonstrated the clinical value of the IOS model in distinguishing COPD from asthma (40).

Furthermore, the evaluation of asthma and COPD could be performed using QCT parameters. Specifically, Hartley compared airway remodeling, air trapping, and emphysema between asthmatic patients and those with COPD; the relationships with airflow limitation were investigated using QCT parameters and it was shown that emphysema was largely restricted to patients with COPD, while asthmatic patients exhibited greater proximal airway remodeling compared to the COPD patients (23). In our study, the COPD group showed greater WA, smaller thickness values and diameters values in the 3rd to 4th generations airways compared to asthma patients. The emphysema parameters including perc 15, LAA_950, and LAA_910 in total lung and each lung lobe increased in COPD patients compare with those in asthma patients. These findings were consistent with previous studies, supporting the view that airflow limitation and obstruction in COPD may be attributed to a combination of small-airway obliteration and emphysema (41). To further explore the application of QCT parameters in distinguishing COPD from asthma, Moslemi et al. identified optimal QCT parameters to distinguish COPD from asthma, including the emphysema parameter of LAA_950, as well as some airway parameters such as outer airway perimeter, inner airway perimeter, total airway count, and low-attenuation cluster total hole count (42). Their model achieved an accuracy of 80%, but lacked external validation of its generalization. In contrast, our QCT model was not only validated on an external validation set, but also calculates AUC to comprehensively evaluate model performance, obtaining AUCs of 0.815, 0.746, and 0.836 in the training, testing, and external validation sets, further demonstrating that the QCT model can distinguish COPD from asthma.

Notably, radiomics have been demonstrated to be capable of conducting early prediction, identification and severity staging, prognostic analysis of COPD (26-30). Chen et al. achieved good results in predicting clinical staging of asthma patients based on radiomics features (31). Small airway dysfunction (fSAD), a precursor of COPD and asthma, was detected using radiomics feature, and the model showed satisfactory predictive performance (43). However, there is still a lack of research on the differentiation between COPD and asthma using radiomics features. Our study aimed to this research gap and the constructed radiomics model achieved good predictive performance in identification of COPD and asthma (AUCs of 0.762, 0.727, 0.753 for the training, testing, and external validation sets, respectively). Radiomics features can be categorized into three distinct types including first order features, shape features and texture features, which provide information about local and global heterogeneity of the region of interest (44). Specifically, as shown in Figure S5, the radiomics model, log-sigma-3-mm-3D_glrlm_GrayLevelNonUniformity and wavelet-LLL_glcm_Imc1 were the most discriminating signature in our study. GrayLevelNonUniformity is a second-order feature that reveals the distribution uniformity of different gray levels in the region of interest of an image and Imc represents an Information Measure of Correlation, which reflects the correlation between gray levels in the region of interest of the image. Wavelet transformation and the Laplacian of Gaussian were two spatial image filter that provide higher-order features that capture more complex texture patterns (45). Different features display the heterogeneity and complexity of lung disease in different ways, thus providing complementary value in the model (46).

Importantly, the present study considered the comprehensive information extracted from the IOS indices, QCT parameters, and radiomics features. Specifically, two selected radiomics features were further combined with three IOS indices and two QCT parameters to construct a more competent fusion Model 6, which achieved the best AUC (training set: AUC =0.959, testing set: 0.960, external validation set: AUC =0.949) compared to a single feature model, as well as the CT imaging model (QCT and radiomics features). Through multi-dimensional feature synergy, the Model 6 achieves efficient discrimination between COPD and asthma. It not only overcomes the limitations of single-parameter diagnostics but also offers a new technological pathway for the precise classification of chronic respiratory diseases.

There are several limitations in our study that merit mention. First, our study was performed at a single center and the sample size was relatively small, especially in the external validation cohort, which may introduce bias and limit the robustness and transportability of our model. Hence, future studies should include a larger sample size of patients from multiple centers for further analysis. Second, discrepancies may exist in QCT and IOS parameters between patients with severe asthma and those with mild-to-moderate asthma. In this study, the majority of asthma patients were outpatients with mild symptoms, and no comprehensive analysis was conducted on patients across different severity levels; this may limit the generalizability of the model. In addition, patients with COPD are older than those with asthma, and there are differences in gender composition between the two groups. In future studies, we will recruit a more diverse and balanced cohort of participants to develop a more comprehensive predictive model. Third, it is unlikely to explore the long-term outcomes of the patients in the present study; thus, further stratified analysis and longitudinal follow-up studies are need to demonstrate the ability of the fusion features model, including IOS indices combined with QCT parameters and radiomics features.

Conclusions

Our study has demonstrated that significant differences in PFTs, IOS, and QCT parameters were identified between the COPD group and the asthma group. Furthermore, the model constructed by integrating IOS, QCT, and radiomics features s has been demonstrated to be able to distinguish COPD from asthma, which has the potential to serve as an alternative and/or a complement tool for COPD and asthma patients who cannot tolerate routine PFTs in clinical practice.
