Epstein-Barr Virus Positive Diffuse Large B-Cell Lymphoma with Epidermotropism
Authors: Ga Hyun Lee, Gil Jae Pyo, Ki Bum Myung, Eun Sun Kwon
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Primary cutaneous B-cell lymphomas are rare diseases comprising nodular to diffuse lymphoid cell infiltration with an overlying grenz zone and no epidermal involvement upon histological analysis. Diagnostics can become challenging when lymphomas exhibit the characteristics of both B and T-cells. Differential diagnoses may include reactive proliferations, cutaneous composite lymphomas, and transformed mycosis fungoides. Immunohistochemistry and gene arrangement tests may be beneficial to clarify the diagnosis. Herein, we report a rare case of epidermotropic EBV-positive cutaneous B-cell lymphoma along with a literature review.

INTRODUCTION

Primary cutaneous B-cell lymphomas (PCBCLs) are B-cell neoplasms of the skin without any evidence of extracutaneous involvement. The typical histologic characteristics of B-cell lymphomas include nodular to diffuse B-cell infiltrates, absence of epidermal involvement, and presence of a grenz zone1. On the other hand, cutaneous T-cell lymphomas such as mycosis fungoides (MF) characteristically present epidermotropism, which is defined as the intraepidermal clustering of cells such as lymphocytes2. However, when a biopsy specimen exhibits histological features of both B- and T-cell lymphomas, diagnosis becomes challenging. If the characteristics of both B-cell and T-cell lymphomas are observed in a single specimen, we have to differentiate cutaneous composite lymphomas, reactive proliferation (pseudolymphoma), cutaneous CD4+ small/medium T-cell lymphoproliferative disorders, and various types of B-cell lymphomas3. In such cases, immunophenotyping and gene rearrangement studies are critical for differentiating among them.

Herein, we report a rare case of Epstein-Barr virus-positive primary cutaneous B-cell lymphoma (EBV+ PCBCL) with epidermotropism.

CASE REPORT

The patient was an 88-year-old female who presented with a 15 days history of an erythematous, pruritic, rapidly enlarging skin lesion on the right nasolabial fold. The patient underwent incision and drainage at a local clinic, but no discharge was found. The patient had a history of hypertension and angina. A physical examination revealed a 2.5×2.0 cm, round, slightly elevated, erythematous infiltrated plaque with scales and accompanied tenderness (Fig. 1). We received the patient’s consent form about publishing all photographic materials. All laboratory test results, including complete blood cell counts, liver function tests and renal function tests, were within the normal range. In the bacterial culture test no growth was observed for 48 hours. Facial magnetic resonance imaging revealed an approximately 26-mm--sized mass with inhomogenous signal intensity and mixed diffusion restriction on the right cheek.

Histologically, the specimen from the right nasolabial fold exhibited dense and diffuse infiltrates of inflammatory cells through the superficial to deep dermis at a low magnification (Fig. 2A). High magnification showed clustered epidermotropic atypical lymphocytes through the epidermal-dermal junction to the mid epidermis. In addition, vacuolization of the basal layer was observed (Fig. 2B). In the dermis, atypical cells invaded structures of blood vessels and any granulomatous change were founded (Fig. 2C). Lymphocytic infiltrates of varying sizes and atypia were present in the dermis. Of these, medium-to large-sized cells with rounded nuclei and prominent nucleoli were dominant. Atypical lymphocytes showed immunoblastic features with centrally located prominent single nucleolus and moderate to abundant basophilic cytoplasm. Also, centroblastic featured lymphocyte were detected which contained vesicular nuclei, vesicular chromatin, 2 to 3 small nucleoli and moderate amount of cytoplasm (Fig. 2D).

According to immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, the pan-B-cell marker CD20 exhibited diffuse positivity through the entire dermis, and clustered CD20+ B-cells were detected in the epidermis (Fig. 3A). Another B-cell marker, PAX5, showed diffuse positivity (Fig. 3B). MUM1 was diffuse positive whereas expression of germinal center markers BCL-6 and CD10 were scattered positive (Fig. 3C~E). Also, BCL-2 showed positive result (Fig. 3F). The analysis was positive for the T-cell markers CD4, CD8, and CD5 (Fig. 3G~I). However, the density of these cells was lower than that of B-cell marker-positive cells. Both epidermotrophic CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells were scattered in epidermis (Fig. 3G, H). Ki-67 staining showed a 90% growth fraction of the cell population (Fig. 3J). The lambda and kappa chain stains exhibited no predominance of κ or λ (Fig. 3K, L). The cells were negative for CD56 and CD30 (Fig. 3M, N). According to in site hybridization, the specimen expressed the EBV antigen, which is an oncogenic virus (Fig. 3O). Oligoclonal T-cell proliferation was observed using the TCR-γ gene rearrangement test. In IgH gene rearrangement test, monoclonal B-cell proliferation was detected.

Based on these findings, the right nasolabial lesion was diagnosed as EBV+ PCBCL with epidermotropism. Especially, according to World Health Organization classification of lymphoid neoplasms, this case was consistent with EBV+ diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, not otherwise specified (EBV+ DLBCL, NOS). Moreover, by Hans criteria, it was classified as a non-germinal center type DLBCL. The patient was treated with 5 mg/wk methotrexate for 6 weeks and the lesion significantly decreased in size compared with the initial visit.

DISCUSSION

Cutaneous B-cell lymphomas usually present with single or few indurated plaques or nodules that are preferentially located on the trunk or head and neck4. Although there are some histological differences among subtypes, B-cell lymphomas generally present nodular to diffuse infiltration patterns of lymphocytes, presence of characteristic ‘grenz zone’ and absence of epidermal involvement5. In contrast, MF usually presents as multiple, scaly, erythematous patches or plaques on unexposed areas during the early stage. In the late stage, a smooth or ulcerated, brown-colored tumor is most commonly found on the face and body folds6. Histologically, early-stage MF shows lichenoid infiltrates in the upper dermis composed of reactive or neoplastic T-lymphocytes. In addition, the atypical T-cells present epidermotropism with the formation of intraepidermal Pautrier microabscesses6.

Nonetheless, the specimen from this report presents histological features of both B- and T-cell lymphomas, which poses diagnostic challenges. Differential diagnoses include cutaneous composite lymphoma, pseudolymphoma, transformed MF, and other variant types of T- or B-cell lymphomas7. IHC and gene rearrangement tests are necessary for an accurate diagnosis. In this case, the initial diagnosis was MF with reactive proliferation according to H&E staining. However, we considered possibility of cutaneous composite lymphoma or a rare type of cutaneous B-cell lymphoma since the specimen showed positive results for both T-cell markers (CD4 and CD8) and B-cell markers (CD20 and PAX5) with a predominance of CD20+ cells. Moreover, more than 90% of the cells were positive for Ki-67 and EBV. For an accurate diagnosis, we performed T-cell receptor gene rearrangement test and IgH gene rearrangement test. The results showed oligoclonality in T-cells and monoclonality in B-cells. Therefore, we diagnosed the patient with as the EBV+ PCBCL with epidermotropism.

To clarify the subtype of lymphoma, we focused on the cytomorphological features of the specimen which showed immunoblasts, centroblast and centrocyte like cells with angionecrosis. And, additional IHC stains were conducted. MUM1 showed diffuse positive whereas CD10 and BCL-6 showed scattered positive. This indicated non-germinal center phenotype of DLBCL according to Hans algorithm. Finally, we diagnosed as EBV+ DLBCL, NOS with epidermotropism. Patient of EBV+ DLBCL, NOS were older with the median age of 70 years compared with all other DLBCLs. Moreover, extranodal involvement is more common and have poorer overall prognosis8.

Few cases have been reported of cutaneous B-cell lymphoma showing histologic features of T-cell lymphomas (Table 1)249101112131415. Chui et al.4 reported a patient with pruritic rashes on the trunk, upper and lower limbs. Histological findings showed dense band-like infiltration of small lymphocytes with abundant cytoplasm and distinctive epidermotropism, without spongiosis. Similar to the present case, the initial diagnosis was considered to be MF. However, IHC revealed that most dermal and intraepithelial lymphocytes expressed CD20, B-cell marker, but were negative for CD23 and CD10. Finally, the patient was diagnosed with marginal zone B-cell lymphoma and treated with chemotherapy.

Chiang et al.9 described a patient with pruritic rashes on the chest, abdomen and thigh. Skin biopsies were performed at all three sites and commonly showed dense atypical dermal infiltrates of lymphocytes and scattered lymphocytes in the dermoepidermal junction and epidermis. The atypical lymphocytes were positive for CD20 and BCL-2 but negative for CD43 by IHC staining. The gene rearrangement test did not identify a clonal T-cell population. These findings corresponded to low grade cutaneous B-cell lymphoma with epidermotropism.

Wu et al.10 reported three cases presenting polymorphic EBV+ lymphocytes, including centroblasts, immunoblasts and sheets of immunoblastic cells. In addition, the tumors showed diffuse expression of CD20, CD19, CD79a, and PAX5. The cells were negative for both CD10 and Bcl6. All three patients were diagnosed with epidermotropic EBV+ DLBCL. This case was more uncommon because previous reports of epidermotropic B-cell lymphomas were all marginal zone B-cell lymphomas.

The mechanism of epidermotropism of B-cell lymphomas is unknown, but Kakinuma et al.16 suggested a distinct interaction between the neoplastic B cells and particular epidermal chemokines and growth factors, similar to the mechanisms of epidermal involvement in T-cell lymphomas. Furudate et al.17 described a case of DLBCL with epidermotropism involving the umbilicus and found that malignant lymphoid cells expressed matrix metalloproteinase-9 in relation to decreased E-cadherin expression in the surrounding epidermis. This suggest that these changes promote migration of neoplastic lymphocytes into the epidermis.

The current report describes a rare case of EBV-positive DLBCL with epidermotropism resembling MF histologically. The histological and immunohistochemical findings shown here are consistent with previous reports. When differentiating cutaneous lymphomas, histopathological findings may be helpful for diagnosis but can be misleading. Therefore, it is important to perform immunohistochemistry and gene rearrangement tests if cutaneous lymphoma is suspected.

Although, there are some reported cases of composite lymphomas which coexist with T-cell lymphoma and DLBCL or EBV+ DLBCL without epidermotropism in South Korea181920. However, to our knowledge, a case of EBV+ DLBCL, NOS with epidermotropism has not been reported in South Korea to date. Herein, we report a rare case of EBV+ DLBCL with epidermotropism with a review of the literature to emphasize the significance of immunohistochemistry and gene rearrangement tests for the precise diagnosis when specimen showing epidermotropism.
