16S rRNA gene sequencing reveals altered composition of gut microbiota in patients with polycystic ovary syndrome
Authors: Lian Gao, Ying Zhang, Qiong Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the causes of endocrine disorders and infertility in women of childbearing age, which seriously affects women’s health. The etiology of PCOS is still unknown, and metabolic abnormalities are the focus of current research. A large number of studies have shown that gut microbiota is related to metabolic abnormalities. This project intends to study the relationship between gut microbiota and PCOS and analyze the molecular mechanism of its impact on PCOS. The project is designed to conduct 16S rRNA sequencing and analysis using feces from patients with polycystic ovary syndrome. 48 samples were included in the study and divided into 3 groups (NOR, POS1, POS2), Venn diagram shows that there are 372 operational taxonomic units in total, and 96, 43 and 20 unique to the 3 groups respectively. PLS-DA analysis shows that there are significant differences among the 3 groups. Species abundance analysis shows that there are differences among the 3 groups at the level of class, family, genus, order, phylum and species. There are more lachnoanaerobcaulum and Klebsiella in POS1 group, and more enterococcaceae and enterococcu in POS2 group. The correlation analysis showed that Klebsiella and Lachnoanaerobaculum were positive associated with BMI, and LH level, respectively, and Enterococcus was negative correlated with the levels of LH and testosterone. This study can provide reference basis for clinical accurate and personalized dietary intervention and drug treatment of PCOS.

1. Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a multifactorial endocrine and metabolic disorder characterized by heterogeneous clinical manifestations. As the most common cause of endocrine dysfunction and ovulatory infertility in women of reproductive age, it affects 6% to 10% of the global population. Since the implementation of China’s 3-child policy, the couples of reproductive age with the demand for fertility have continued to rise. PCOS exhibits distinct regional epidemiological patterns and is often associated with hyperandrogenemia, oligo- or anovulatory menstrual cycles, polycystic ovarian morphology, as well as metabolic abnormalities such as obesity and insulin resistance. These factors collectively elevate the risks of infertility, gestational diabetes, adverse pregnancy outcomes, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases.[1] The etiology of PCOS remains incompletely understood, involving genetic predisposition, lifestyle factors, and intrauterine influences, and metabolic dysregulation emerging as a key research focus in recent years.

There is a profound interplay between human metabolic homeostasis and the gut microbiota. The gut microbiota, comprising over 10 trillion microbial cells (of which > 99% are bacteria), represents a vital ecosystem within the human intestine. These microorganisms play critical roles in maintaining health,[2,3] regulating body weight, digestion, immune responses to infections and autoimmune diseases, and even modulating therapeutic drug efficacy. Notably, gut microecology is closely linked to metabolic disorders such as obesity and insulin resistance. Dysbiosis alterations in the structural and functional composition of gut microbiota can disrupt systemic metabolic balance.

In this study, we aim to investigate the relationship between gut microbiota and PCOS, to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying its pathogenesis. By comparing the gut microbiota profiles of healthy reproductive-aged women (NOR), untreated PCOS patients (POS1), and post-pharmacological intervention PCOS patients (POS2), we seek to identify microbiota signatures associated with PCOS. This research is expected to provide a scientific foundation for developing precise, personalized dietary strategies and targeted therapies in clinical practice.

2. Methods

2.1. Participant enrollment

This study enrolled PCOS patients from the gynecology outpatient clinic in the People’s Hospital of Longhua, Shenzhen. Inclusion criteria: first-time diagnosis of PCOS according to the Rotterdam consensus criteria; reproductive-aged women with fertility needs; no prior use of hormonal therapies; treatment with Diane-35 (containing 2 mg cyproterone acetate and 0.035 mg ethinylestradiol); Regular dietary and lifestyle habits.

Exclusion criteria: Antibiotic treatment within the past month; use of contraceptives within the past month; use of antidiabetic medications (e.g., metformin) within the past month; BMI <18 were excluded to minimize clinical heterogeneity; current gastrointestinal diseases or infections.

The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the People’s Hospital of Longhua, Shenzhen (Approval No. LHRY-1907005).

2.2. Sample collection

Materials collected: Fecal samples: At least 2 samples per participant, one before treatment initiation and another 3 months posttreatment, after signing the clinical research informed consent form; BMI; Serum testosterone, FSH, and LH levels; Menstrual cycle records; Hirsutism evaluation; personal information, including age and contact details.

Fecal samples were collected using the MGIEasy stool sample collection kit (BGI genomics). Sample collection, transportation, storage, usage, and disposal complied with national regulations and ethical standards. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

2.3. Sample testing

Fecal DNA extraction, library preparation, and 16S rRNA (V3–V4) sequencing were performed by the BGI Genomics Institute. Biochemical analyses of blood and urine samples were conducted by the hospital’s clinical laboratory.

2.4. Gut microbiota profiling

After DNA sample qualification, library construction was performed as follows: Target amplicon fragments were purified, and sticky ends were repaired to blunt ends using T4 DNA Polymerase, Klenow DNA Polymerase, and T4 PNK. An “A” base was added to the 3’ end to enable ligation with adapters containing a “T” overhang. Alternatively, fusion primers with sequencing adapters and dual indices were designed for PCR amplification using genomic DNA. Amplified fragments were selected using magnetic beads, and qualified libraries were subjected to cluster generation and sequencing.

Raw sequencing data were filtered to remove low-quality reads, retaining high-quality clean data for downstream analysis. Overlapping reads were assembled into tags, clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% similarity, and annotated against reference databases. Species diversity, intergroup differences, and functional predictions were analyzed based on OTUs and taxonomic annotations. Clinical parameters (e.g., BMI, serum testosterone, FSH, LH) were correlated with microbiota features using Spearman rank correlation and PERMANOVA to identify species associated with environmental factors.

3. Results

3.1. General characteristics of participants

A total of 48 samples were enrolled and divided into 3 groups: the normal control group (NOR, N = 21, mean age 23.56 ± 5.10 years), the untreated PCOS group (POS1, N = 19, mean age 24.14 ± 3.15 years), and the posttreatment PCOS group (POS2, N = 8, mean age 24.56 ± 3.09 years). No significant differences in age were observed among the 3 groups.

3.2. OTU clustering analysis and PLS-DA analysis

DNA extracted from fecal samples underwent quality control, amplification, library preparation, and sequencing on the HiSeq platform. Raw sequencing data were filtered, and paired-end reads were merged into tags using fast length adjustment of short reads (FLASH, v1.2.11). Tags with ≥97% similarity were clustered into OTUs. Venn diagram analysis revealed 372 shared OTUs across the NOR (normal control), POS1 (newly diagnosed PCOS), and POS2 (posttreatment PCOS) groups, with 96, 43, and 20 unique OTUs in each group, respectively. The results indicated a higher OTU diversity in the normal control group (Fig. 1A). The OTU accumulation curve approached saturation, suggesting that the sample size was sufficient for subsequent analyses (Fig. 1B). The partial least squares discrimination analysis (PLS-DA) results demonstrated distinct intergroup differences among the 3 groups along both the horizontal (x-axis) and vertical (y-axis) coordinate (Fig. 1C).

3.3. OTU taxonomic abundance analysis

OTUs were taxonomically classified through alignment with reference databases. Species abundance analysis demonstrated distinct differences among the 3 groups at the class (Fig. 2A), family (Fig. 2B), genus (Fig. 2C), order (Fig. 2D), phylum (Fig. 2E), and species (Fig. 2F) levels.

3.4. Differential microbiota analysis

The analysis revealed significantly higher abundances of Klebsiella and Lachnoanaerobaculum in the POS1 group, while the POS2 group exhibited increased levels of Enterococcaceae (family) and Enterococcus (genus) (Fig. 3). These findings indicate that Diane-35 treatment significantly altered the gut microbiota composition in PCOS patients. No statistically significant differences were detected between the NOR group and either the POS1 or POS2 groups.

LEfSe analysis LDA plot. LDA = linear discriminant analysis, LEfSe = Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size.

3.5. Clinical-microbiome correlation analysis

The results of correlation analysis (Fig. 4) showed that an increased prevalence of Klebsiella and Lachnoanaerobaculum in POS2 were positive associated with BMI, and LH level, respectively. The increased prevalence of Enterococcus in POS2 was negative correlated with the levels of LH and testosterone.

4. Discussions

4.1. Gut microbiota dysbiosis and its association with reproductive endocrine disorders

Dysregulation of gut microbiota is closely linked to vaginal microenvironment alterations, and modulating gut microbiota holds therapeutic potential for female reproductive endocrine disorders. Gut microbiota imbalance plays a critical role in hyperandrogenemia, insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation in PCOS.

Studies have shown that transplanting gut microbiota from obese mice into germ-free mice induces obesity in the recipients,[4,5] highlighting the role of gut microbial dysbiosis in metabolic disturbances.[6] Hyperandrogenemia, a hallmark of PCOS, disrupts follicular maturation and promotes excessive follicular wall hyperplasia, leading to anovulation. Kelley et al[1] demonstrated in a letrozole- induced PCOS mouse model that hyperandrogenemia reduced bacterial diversity in the colon, suggesting its profound impact on gut microbiota. Markle et al[7] observed that altering gut microbiota in nonobese diabetic (NOD) mice influenced sex hormone levels; transplanting gut microbiota from mature male mice into immature female mice elevated serum testosterone in the latter.

4.2. Key findings of this study

In our cohort of 48 samples, Venn diagram analysis revealed 372 shared OTUs across the 3 groups (NOR, POS1, and POS2), with 96, 43, and 20 unique OTUs, respectively. The NOR group exhibited higher OTU diversity than the other groups, sharing 487 OTUs with POS1, significantly exceeding other intergroup overlaps. PLS-DA analysis demonstrated clear separation between NOR and the PCOS groups (POS1/POS2), while POS1 and POS2 clustered indistinctly, with POS1 showing tighter OTU aggregation. Taxonomic abundance analysis identified distinct differences at class, family, genus, order, phylum, and species levels among the groups. The correlation analysis showed that Klebsiella and Lachnoanaerobaculum were positive associated with BMI, and LH level, respectively, and Enterococcus was negative correlated with the levels of LH and testosterone.

4.3. Gut microbiota and metabolic/endocrine interactions

Poutahidis et al[8] reported elevated serum testosterone in mice supplemented with Lactobacillus, linking gut microbiota modulation to hormonal and metabolomics changes and pancreatic inflammation. Insulin resistance, present in 50% to 70% of PCOS patients, is central to PCOS pathogenesis. Bäckhed et al[9] first established the gut microbiota-insulin resistance connection in 2004, showing that germ-free mice colonized with conventional microbiota developed increased adiposity and insulin resistance. Qin et al[10] further linked gut dysbiosis to insulin resistance in type 2 diabetes through metagenomics sequencing. Org et al[11] identified associations between gut microbiota and fasting serum metabolites (e.g., fatty acids, amino acids, lipids, glucose) in 531 men with metabolic syndrome.

4.4. Differential microbiota in PCOS groups

Our differential analysis revealed significantly higher abundances of Klebsiella and Lachnoaneerobcaulum in POS1 (untreated PCOS), while POS2 (posttreatment) showed enrichment in Enterococcaceae (family) and Enterococcus (genus). No significant differences were observed between NOR and the PCOS groups. Notably, Lachnoanaerobaculum (a genus linked to celiac disease[12]) and Klebsiella (an Enterobacteriaceae member associated with bacteremia[13]) may reflect dysbiotic states in untreated PCOS. Conversely, Enterococcaceae and Enterococcus (implicated in inflammatory bowel disease[14] and diabetic foot infections[15]) emerged posttreatment, suggesting microbiota shifts induced by Diane-35. It was reported that alterations in the gut microbiota community are associated with childhood obesity and precocious puberty,[16] which is agree with the positive association of Klebsiella and BMI. short-chain fatty acid regulated GnRH to increase LH level,[17] and short-chain fatty acid was reported to associate with Lachnoanaerobaculum,[18] these make the positive correlation Lachnoanaerobaculum and LH reasonable. There is a lot of evidence supported that Enterococcus was negative correlated with the levels of LH[19] and testosterone.[20,21]

4.5. Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the relatively small sample size, particularly in the posttreatment PCOS group (POS2, N = 8), may limit the statistical power of the analyses and reduce the generalizability of the findings. Second, as an observational study, it cannot establish causality between Diane-35 treatment and the observed changes in gut microbiota. Third, the assessment of gut microbiota was based on 16S rRNA sequencing, which, while informative, does not provide the resolution needed to characterize microbial communities at the species or strain level with high precision. Additionally, although distinct microbial differences were identified, the potential influence of unmeasured confounding factors (e.g., dietary habits, lifestyle, or genetic background) cannot be entirely ruled out. Finally, the use of self-reported or retrospectively collected clinical data may introduce measurement bias or unaccounted variability.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the altered composition of gut microbiota among normal individuals, untreated PCOS patients, and post-treated PCOS patients. Specifically, untreated PCOS patients exhibited significantly higher abundances of Klebsiella and Lachnoanaerobaculum, while Diane-35 treatment led to a notable shift characterized by increased levels of Enterococcaceae and Enterococcus. Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed that these microbial changes were associated with key clinical parameters: Klebsiella and Lachnoanaerobaculum were positively correlated with BMI and LH levels, respectively, whereas Enterococcus was negatively correlated with LH and testosterone levels. These findings suggest that Diane-35 intervention may ameliorate PCOS-related endocrine and metabolic disturbances by modulating the gut microbiota, highlighting the potential role of microbial-targeted therapies in PCOS management, providing a foundation for developing personalized dietary and pharmacological interventions targeting gut microbiota in PCOS management.. However, further studies with larger sample sizes and mechanistic investigations are warranted to validate these observations and elucidate the underlying pathways.
