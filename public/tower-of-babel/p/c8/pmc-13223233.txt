X-ray computed tomography images of wheat kernels and reconstructed three-dimensional shapes
Authors: Zhixiao Shi, Mingxu Wang, Yingxin Xie, Bingyan Gao, Tongwei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGrain kernel morphology strongly influences aerodynamic loads, breakage susceptibility, and processing performance. Conventional two-dimensional projection imaging and manual measurements lack the accuracy and completeness needed to represent the true three-dimensional structure of kernels. We present an open X-ray computed tomography dataset of wheat kernels that includes axial slice stacks and three-dimensional shape models for 100 individual kernels, together with per-sample morphometrics including principal-axis lengths, elongation index, flatness index, convexity, and sphericity. The dataset supports statistics of kernel shape, enables analysis of internal microstructure from the provided slice stacks, and facilitates high-fidelity discrete element modelling with realistic particle geometry.Subject terms: Plant sciences, Mathematics and computing

Background & Summary

Wheat is one of the world’s most important food crops and a staple for billions of people. The geometric characteristics of wheat kernels play a key role in downstream processing, including storage, conveying, milling, and phenotyping and cultivar selection1–3. The inherent irregularity and anisotropy of kernel shape strongly influence behavior. Compared with spherical particles, non-spherical kernels exhibit richer kinematics and orientation-dependent interactions. To gain a deeper understanding of grain motion and flow during processing and to build more realistic and reliable numerical simulations, high-resolution and faithful three-dimensional shape data are essential4,5.

Traditionally, the morphology of wheat kernels has been characterized from two-dimensional images, for example by measuring length, width, and projected area. Although such methods can be effective for certain tasks, they are limited in accuracy and information content and cannot capture the true threedimensional structure of particles. In recent years, researchers have increasingly adopted three-dimensional reconstruction to model particle shape in fine detail, with notable applications to mineral particles such as chromite6 and coal7, and to non-cereal seeds such as cottonseed8 and sunflower seed9. Some studies have not only achieved high-resolution reconstructions but have also released well-structured databases that provide valuable resources for shape analysis and modeling. However, high-resolution three-dimensional databases for cereal grains remain scarce, and publicly accessible and well-structured datasets are lacking, which limits deeper research and modeling in agricultural engineering and grain processing.

X-ray computed tomography is a non-destructive imaging technique in which X-rays transmitted through a specimen generate contrast. By illuminating the specimen from multiple orientations to achieve complete volumetric coverage and applying computational reconstruction, X-ray CT recovers a stack of crosssectional images, from which three-dimensional volumes and surfaces can be obtained using software for image processing. X-ray CT has been widely adopted across disciplines, from comparative anatomy to computational imaging and granular materials research10,11. In the plant sciences, domain-specific repositories such as Quantitative Plant12 and X-plant13 have been established to host and share such quantitative imaging data. Recently, several publicly available datasets related to the three-dimensional reconstruction of cereal grains have emerged, such as wheat point cloud datasets14 obtained via close-range photogrammetry and three-dimensional reconstruction datasets15 based on multi-view volume carving techniques. Although these optical and multi-view imaging techniques can rapidly generate three-dimensional point clouds or surface models of grains, they are limited to capturing external geometric features and cannot provide information regarding the internal structure of the kernels. Furthermore, constrained by the inherent limitations of optical occlusion or outline reconstruction principles, these methods often have limited capability in handling complex concave structures. On the other hand, although prior studies have applied X-ray CT to grain analysis16–20, these studies typically only present two-dimensional images or statistical results in publications, and rarely share the raw CT imaging data or three-dimensional models as open source. Consequently, publicly accessible datasets remain scarce. Aberystwyth University has released some raw micro-CT datasets of wheat21,22, but their data consists of scans of intact, unthreshed wheat spikes. Their primary objective is to preserve the spatial positional information of the grains on the spike to extract macroscopic phenotypes, rather than focusing on individual, threshed kernels. Overall, publicly available datasets targeting individual grain kernels that simultaneously contain raw tomographic slices and ready-to-use, high-fidelity three-dimensional surface models remain extremely scarce. We therefore present and openly release a CT-based three-dimensional shape dataset of wheat kernels that contains axial slice stacks and the corresponding three-dimensional surface models for one hundred kernels, together with morphometrics including principal-axis lengths, elongation index, flatness index, convexity, and sphericity. The dataset is provided as an open resource intended to supply standardized three-dimensional data for research in agricultural engineering, granular mechanics, computer vision, and plant phenotyping. It enables analysis of kernel morphology, construction of high-fidelity discrete element models, development of segmentation algorithms, and exploration of cultivar classification and quality prediction based on three-dimensional shape23–25.

Methods

Sample preparation and labelling

Kernels of common wheat (Triticum aestivum, cultivar Jimai 22) were sourced from northern Anhui, China, from a single source batch. Following GB/T 5497-1985, a 10 g subsample was oven-dried at 105 °C and weighed at 30-minute intervals until two consecutive measurements differed by less than 0.005 g, yielding a wet-basis moisture content of 10.2% for the batch. From this batch, one hundred intact and uniformly plump kernels were selected. This selection was conducted subjectively via visual inspection, purposely excluding kernels exhibiting mechanical damage, insect-induced defects, or severe shriveling. The kernels were divided into twenty groups for handling and tracking. Within each group five kernels were inserted into a low-density foam support that was affixed to the specimen stage to minimise micromotion during rotation and, together with flat-field correction, to reduce the risk of artefacts. Within each group the kernels were labelled a–e for traceability. X-ray CT scanning

All scans were performed on a Bruker SKYSCAN 1275 laboratory CT system. The tube voltage was 48 kV and the tube current was 70 μA. The exposure time per projection was 100 ms and the isotropic voxel size was 10 μm. A short-scan protocol with an angular step of 0.3 degrees was used, yielding 705 projections over an angular range of 211.5 degrees. Short-scan coverage on a circular trajectory provides sufficient sampling within a reduced angular span, balancing sampling density and scan time, and the small angular step improves angular sampling uniformity. Dark-field and flat-field images were acquired during scanning and used for projection-domain pre-processing to correct pixel offset and gain non-uniformity. Tomographic reconstruction was performed in NRecon under a uniform reconstruction protocol; all samples used identical geometric calibration and algorithm settings with beam-hardening correction and ring-artefact suppression enabled to improve grey-level uniformity and geometric fidelity. The reconstructed data were exported as axial slice stacks.

Segmentation and surface reconstruction

Three-dimensional views of wheat kernels and the corresponding two-dimensional slice images are shown in Fig. 2. Panels 2a–2 d present three-dimensional renderings, and panels 2a1–2d1 show the matched axial slices, where internal anatomy such as the bran tissues, embryo, endosperm, scutellum, and surface pores can be visualised. In this dataset we reconstructed the external surface of each kernel rather than segmenting internal tissues, because whole-kernel surface shape is the target for our downstream analyses. The CT slice series were imported into the open-source 3D Slicer for segmentation and reconstruction. Segmentation and surface reconstruction were performed manually under operator control. The reconstruction workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1c. Threshold-based segmentation guided by the greyscale histogram was applied. Within each group, connected-component labelling was used to delineate the five kernels as independent regions of interest (ROI) with unique labels, which were then processed individually. Morphological opening and closing were used to remove small islands and to close small holes, followed by gentle smoothing, with the relative changes in volume and surface area each limited to at most one percent. For each kernel an isosurface was extracted to generate a closed triangular mesh, which was exported as STL with linear units in millimetres. For traceability, the per-kernel slice stacks obtained after ROI separation were exported and saved as 8-bit TIFF images.

Morphometric parameter extraction

Based on the 3D reconstruction results, we calculated several morphological parameters, including principal-axis lengths, elongation index, flatness index, convexity, and sphericity. These metrics provide a quantitative basis for analyzing variation in kernel shape across samples.

Principal-axis lengths

Each kernel was first translated so that its centroid coincided with the origin. Principal component analysis was then applied to the coordinates of the surface vertices to obtain three mutually orthogonal principal directions. The mesh was rigidly rotated to align these directions with the X, Y, and Z axes. The extents along the three axes were taken as the principal-axis lengths and ordered L1≥L2≥L3, reported as the major, intermediate, and minor axes26,27.

It should be noted that the PCA-based method provides a purely geometric alignment driven by the spatial variance of the mesh vertices, rather than an alignment based on anatomical landmarks. While the major principal axis (L1) generally aligns macroscopically with the longitudinal direction of the ventral crease due to the inherent elongation of the kernel, there is no strict guarantee that the intermediate (L2) and minor (L3) axes will consistently orient with respect to specific anatomical features. This mathematical orientation naturally varies from kernel to kernel owing to the inherent biological asymmetry of the samples.

Elongation index and flatness index

Elongation and flatness quantify axislength ratios along the three PCA-aligned principal axes. The elongation index is EI=L2/L1 and flatness index is FI=L3/L228,29. These dimensionless metrics summarise overall form and are invariant to rigid motion and uniform scaling.

Convexity

Convexity describes how closely a particle resembles its convex hull. The volume-based convexity is defined as Cv=Vp/Vh, where Vp is the volume of the particle and Vh is the volume of the three-dimensional convex hull enclosing the particle30,31. The convex hull is computed from the mesh vertices, after which its volume is measured.

Sphericity

Sphericity quantifies how close a particle’s three-dimensional morphology is to a sphere and serves as a core descriptor of overall form. It is widely used in particle engineering and, together with anisotropy indices such as principal-axis ratios, provides a complementary characterisation of particle geometry. Data Records

The dataset comprises tomographic slice stacks, three-dimensional surface meshes, a model-processing data file, and a plain-text Readme.txt file. All files are publicly available on Figshare at 10.6084/m9.figshare.3014585834. Slices.zip comprises tomographic slice stacks for one hundred wheat kernels, organised into twenty groups. Within each group the kernels are labelled a-e, and each kernel is stored in an individual directory. All slice stacks are provided in TIFF format. Stl.zip provides the reconstructed three-dimensional surface meshes for one hundred kernels in STL format. Data.xlsx is a workbook comprising two sheets: “Morphometrics”, which records the computed morphometric parameters for each kernel, and “Metadata”, which details the plant cultivar, handling dates, grain moisture, storage conditions, and X-ray CT imaging parameters.

Technical Validation

All data were acquired and processed under unified experimental conditions and a consistent analysis workflow, which supports data integrity and crosssample comparability. All kernels were scanned on the same X-ray CT system at the same voxel size. The projection data were reconstructed into tomographic slices using the same software and identical settings. Slice continuity was checked during reconstruction, and complete image series were produced for all samples with no missing or corrupted files. Surface meshes generated from the slices were required to be closed and manifold with consistent face normals. These checks ensure that the three-dimensional models faithfully represent the external geometry of the kernels and can be compared across samples.

Each surface mesh was required to be closed and two-manifold with consistent normal orientation. Non-manifold edges, self-intersections, and degenerate faces were identified and repaired. To assess the impact of post-processing on morphometric indices, a subset of samples was recomputed before and after mesh optimisation. We recalculated volume, surface area, and principal-axis lengths, and derived elongation index, flatness index, sphericity, and convexity, with the relative changes in volume and surface area each constrained to at most one percent. Principal-axis ratios and sphericity showed only minor variation, indicating that the meshes provide a smooth and complete representation of the external geometry of the kernels.

Usage Notes

The slice stacks use micrometres for voxel spacing and the mesh coordinates use millimetres. All meshes have been aligned by principal components so that the major, intermediate, and minor axes correspond to the positive X, Y, and Z directions. When importing the meshes into downstream software it is recommended to verify units and to disable any automatic rescaling. For discrete-element simulations the meshes may be downsampled by quadric decimation while monitoring the relative changes in volume and surface area. Keep these changes within predefined tolerances, for example not more than one percent, and adjust the triangle count by comparing the recomputed morphometrics with those provided in the dataset. The dataset provides surface meshes that capture only whole-kernel external geometry, which is the level of detail needed for our subsequent studies. Researchers who require anatomical substructures such as the embryo, endosperm, or bran tissues, or who wish to study internal fissures, should use the slice stacks to perform their own volumetric segmentation and reconstruction.

Furthermore, data users should be aware of the sampling bias in this dataset. During sample preparation, the kernels were subjectively selected via visual inspection to be intact, plump, and free of macroscopic physical damage in order to establish a baseline morphological model. Consequently, they do not encompass the full natural variance of wheat grain morphology, such as shriveled or deformed kernels resulting from environmental stress or disease. Researchers interested in a broader range of grain morphologies, including non-ideal or stressed grains, should consider this limitation and may need to complement this dataset with models of defective kernels.
