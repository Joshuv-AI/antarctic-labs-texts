Cardiovascular Hospitalizations and Resource Use Following Atrial Fibrillation Ablation
Authors: Mehak Dhande, Amr Barakat, Ann Canterbury, Floyd Thoma, Suresh Mulukutla, Ahmet Sezer, Konstantinos N. Aronis, Aditya Bhonsale, Krishna Kancharla, Andrew H. Voigt, Norman C. Wang, Alaa Shalaby, N. A. Mark Estes, Samir Saba, Sandeep K. Jain
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOver the next few years, atrial fibrillation (AF)–related morbidity and costs will increase significantly. Thus, it is prudent to examine the impact of AF treatment on health care resource use. This study examined the impact of AF ablation on hospitalization, length of stay, and resource use for patients undergoing AF ablation in a multihospital system.Methods and ResultsIn an observational analysis, outcomes of total, cardiovascular, and AF hospitalizations, emergency department visits, and length of stay were compared for 3417 patients between 12 months before and 24 months following AF ablation. Use of electrical cardioversions and antiarrhythmic use were also compared 1 year before to 2 years after AF ablation. There were fewer total (0.7±1.3 versus 0.3±0.7; P<0.001), cardiovascular (0.7±1.2 versus 0.2±0.6; P<0.001), and AF (0.6±1.1 versus 0.1±0.3; P<0.001) hospitalizations and emergency department visits (0.8±2.1 versus 0.4±0.9; P<0.001) per patient‐year for the 2 years following AF ablation compared with 1 year before. Average length of stay per patient‐year (1.4±7.9 versus 3.6±5.3 days; P<0.0001), the percentage of patients on antiarrhythmic therapy (21.2% versus 58.5%; P<0.0001), and those undergoing electrical cardioversions (16.1% versus 28.1%; P<0.0001) were lower 2 years following AF ablation versus 1 year before.ConclusionsWe noted a decrease in total, cardiovascular, and AF hospitalizations and health care resource use during the 2‐year period after index AF ablation, compared with the 1 year before. AF ablation may portend a decline in patient morbidity and health care costs.

Catheter‐based ablation for atrial fibrillation (AF) has an increasing role in management as technology develops to enhance safety and efficacy. 1 Although AF ablation does not impact mortality in a broad population, it is associated with improved quality of life. 2 , 3 , 4 Less is known about the ramifications on health care resource use for patients undergoing AF ablation. Published claims data suggest that AF ablation decreases the number of all‐cause hospitalizations and those for arrhythmic causes. 5 Studies from large hospital systems could corroborate the relationship between AF ablation and resource use with the addition of vital clinical details only available in an institutional database. We sought to evaluate the impact of AF ablation on hospitalizations, emergency department (ED) visits, antiarrhythmic use, and use of cardiovascular diagnostic or therapeutic procedures within a real‐world setting in our multihospital health care system.

Methods

We performed a single‐arm, observational, retrospective cohort study of 3417 consecutive patients undergoing their first AF ablation procedure at 1 of 5 sites of a multihospital health care system from January 2013 to December 2020. Patients who underwent atrioventricular junction ablation were excluded. Baseline characteristics, patient demographics, and clinical outcomes were extracted from electronic health records, as well as administrative and clinical databases integrated within the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center Clinical Data Warehouse. A full list of the search terms used is provided in Table S1. Both International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision and Tenth Revision (ICD‐9 and ICD‐10) and Current Procedural Terminology codes were used to determine clinical outcomes as well as hospitalizations and procedures performed. A hospitalization was determined to be cardiovascular if the billing codes referenced in Table S1 were 1 of the top 3 discharge codes. To determine patient comorbidities, we looked back at the closest electronic health record encounter within a year preceding the date of the AF ablation. In addition, we looked as far back in our electronic health record encounters as the first diagnosis of AF, which was a median of 486 days.

The discharge diagnoses and billing codes were determined by the discharging physician. Patients included in the cohort were observed at 1 of 40 hospitals and 400 outpatient clinics of the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center health care system. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Pittsburgh, and informed consent was not deemed necessary for the study.

We compared primary outcome variables of total hospitalizations, cardiovascular hospitalizations, AF readmissions, ED visits, average hospital length of stay (LOS), and intensive care unit (ICU) stay between a 12‐month period before first ablation (or time from AF diagnosis, if time from diagnosis to ablation was <12 months) and a 24‐month period after the first AF ablation. The hospitalization for AF ablation was considered a cardiovascular hospitalization and included in the postablation hospitalization metrics for number of hospitalizations, LOS, and ICU LOS, if applicable. For secondary outcome variables, we compared use of cardiac echocardiograms, stress tests, and electrical cardioversions by number of patients undergoing the above procedures or interventions 1 year before with 1 and 2 years after the index AF ablation. We also compared the number of patients being treated with antiarrhythmics at the time of ablation with 1 and 2 years after ablation. Finally, we analyzed primary end points for the subgroup of patients who required repeated ablation procedure(s). The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean±SD, and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages for baseline characteristics. Outcomes of preablation versus postablation use of echocardiograms, stress tests, electrical cardioversions, and antiarrhythmic medications were compared using the McNemar test. Total hospitalizations, cardiovascular hospitalizations, AF readmissions, and ED visits were presented as number per patient‐year of follow‐up. Average hospital LOS and ICU stay were presented as days per patient‐year of follow‐up. Hospitalization and LOS outcomes were compared per patient between 1 year before and 2 years after index ablation using paired t‐test, indexed to the duration of follow‐up for each patient. Zero inflated negative binomial model was used to evaluate effect of the baseline characteristics on total hospitalizations, cardiovascular hospitalizations, AF readmissions, and ED visits during the postablation period. A 2‐sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant for comparisons. Variables tested included the following: age >65 years, female sex, race, hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, congestive heart failure (CHF), coronary artery disease, peripheral vascular disease, stroke, chronic kidney disease, end‐stage renal disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and obesity. All statistical analyses were performed on SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). In longitudinal studies, the number of repeated events (such as admissions) may depend on the follow‐up duration for the patients. Ignoring the difference in exposure time may result in biased estimates for the parameters in the model. In this study, not all patients had the same follow‐up duration. To overcome this, we added an offset term, log of follow‐up time, as an explanatory variable in to the zero inflated negative binomial model.

Results

We included 3417 consecutive patients in the present analysis. Most patients were men (66%), with an average age of 63 years (SD, ±10 years). The mean follow‐up was 28.3 months before and 40.8 months following the index ablation. Obesity was the most common cardiovascular risk factor among the cohort (65%), followed by hypertension (58%), hyperlipidemia (55%), and diabetes (17%). Among the study population, 23% of the patients had CHF, 23% had coronary artery disease, and 24% had obstructive sleep apnea. The average Congestive heart failure, Hypertension, Age, Diabetes mellitus, prior Stroke or transient ischemic attack or thromboembolism, Vascular disease, Age, Sex category (CHA2DS2‐VASc score) was 2.1±1.5, and 92% of the patients were on anticoagulation agents at baseline (Table 1). Standard AF ablation techniques (ie, cryoballoon or radiofrequency ablation) were used per provider preference. Our primary outcomes analysis showed that for the 3417 patients studied, there were fewer total hospitalizations (0.7±1.3 versus 0.3±0.7 per person‐year; P<0.001), cardiovascular hospitalizations (0.7±1.2 versus 0.2±0.6 per person‐year; P<0.001), AF readmissions (0.6±1.1 versus 0.1±0.3 per person‐year; P<0.001), and ED visits (0.8±2.1 versus 0.4±0.9 per person‐year; P<0.001) 2 years following index AF ablation when compared with the year before (Figure 1). The average length of hospital stay per patient‐year was shorter (1.4±7.9 versus 3.6±5.3 days; P<0.0001) 2 years after ablation compared with the 1 year before ablation. The average number of days spent in the ICU was higher 1 year before compared with 2 years following index ablation (5.4 versus 3.0 days; P=0.008).

Analysis of patient characteristics or comorbidities that could independently predict clinical outcomes revealed that age >65 years, female sex, and history of CHF were predictors of AF readmissions; age >65 years, CHF, and peripheral vascular disease were significant predictors of cardiovascular hospitalizations; CHF and history of stroke were significant predictors of ED visits; and age >65 years, CHF, chronic kidney disease, and type 2 diabetes were significant predictors of total hospitalizations (Table S2). Secondary outcome analysis revealed that the percentage of patients undergoing echocardiograms, cardiac stress tests, and electrical cardioversions for AF was significantly lower at the end of 2 years following AF ablation compared with the 1 year before (Table 2). The number of patients on any antiarrhythmic treatment was significantly lower at 21.2% 2 years after AF ablation, compared with 58.5% at the time of ablation (Table 3). Similarly, there were significantly fewer patients being treated with amiodarone therapy 2 years after AF ablation compared with the time of AF ablation (4.8% versus 20%).

During a 2‐year follow‐up period after the index AF ablation, 369 (10.8%) patients underwent at least 1 additional ablation procedure. The average time to repeated ablation was 244±202 days. A total of 101 patients underwent a repeated ablation >1 year after the index ablation. For the subgroup of patients undergoing repeated ablations within the observed 2‐year period, there were fewer total hospitalizations 2 years following ablation compared with 1 year before (0.43±0.59 versus 0.89±1.54; P<0.001), as well as fewer cardiovascular hospitalizations (0.37±0.53 versus 0.86±1.48; P<0.0001) and AF readmissions (0.28±0.45 versus 0.70±1.31; P<0.001) (Figure 2). The number of ED visits per patient‐year were also lower over the 2 years following the index ablation relative to 1 year before (0.75±1.53 versus 0.39±0.76; P<0.0001) (Figure 2). The average LOS 2 years after index ablation was lower when compared with the LOS 1 year before the index hospitalization, per patient‐year (2.57±5.98 days versus 0.98±2.05 days; P<0.0001). Average length of ICU stay per patient‐year did not differ significantly from 1 year before AF ablation to 2 years after the ablation (1.86 versus 0.76; P=0.16) for this subgroup of patients.

Discussion

The impact of AF ablation on improvement in patient quality of life is well known. 2 However, there are few clinical studies examining the downstream effects of AF ablation procedures on hospitalization and cardiovascular diagnostic and therapeutic interventions in the time following the index ablation with considerable detail, although noteworthy observations from claims data have shown a decline in total and arrhythmic hospitalizations following AF ablation. 4 , 5 To our knowledge, this is the first study using validated clinical outcomes made available through an extensive institutional database, to analyze post‐AF ablation outcomes compared with prior reports of claims‐based data. Our data suggest that in a population with significant cardiovascular comorbidities, AF ablation is associated with a decline in total and cardiovascular hospitalizations, AF readmissions, and ED visits; and a decrease in the need for interventions, such as electrical cardioversions. This finding is true despite counting the AF ablation hospitalization and associated LOS or ICU stay toward the postablation metrics. Although the number of echocardiograms performed was lower in the postablation period, this may be because most patients would be likely to undergo initial workup, including stress testing or echocardiogram, soon after their AF diagnosis rather than in the years following the AF ablation, which could account for the decline in number of echocardiograms and stress tests noted among our cohort. The median time from AF diagnosis to ablation was 486 days.

Comparing our real‐world results with the health care use metrics depicted as baseline characteristics in the CABANA (Catheter Ablation versus Antiarrhythmic Drug Therapy for Atrial Fibrillation) trial, we had a lower percentage of patients on antiarrhythmic medications at baseline (81.9% versus 58.5%), possibly because of a shift in practice to using AF ablation as a first‐line therapy over the past few years. We noted a comparable percentage of patients with a history of electrical cardioversion at baseline in the CABANA trial and in the 1 year before ablation for our cohort (36.8% versus 28.1%). On the basis of our raw data, the percentage of patients hospitalized for AF over the preablation year was 38.6%, which was also comparable to the baseline AF admissions reported in the CABANA trial (39.7%). 4

As did the authors of a national registry study by Guo et al, we noted a decline in total and cardiovascular hospitalizations following ablation. We noted a decline in AF hospitalizations, a metric similar but not identical to arrhythmic hospitalizations in the cited study. 5 Although the results cannot be compared directly because of differences in methods and statistical analysis between the 2 studies, our findings were aligned with the registry study. 5 We also noted that older age (>65 years) and a history of CHF were independent predictors of AF readmissions. These findings are similar to the predictors of arrhythmic hospitalization reported by Guo et al, albeit the age cutoff for our study was 65 years versus 55 years in the cited article, and we did not detect obstructive sleep apnea as a significant predictor of AF readmissions, possibly because of differences in sleep apnea severity or treatment between the patient cohorts in the 2 studies. 5

We also assessed cost savings from AF readmissions and ED visits in context of AF ablation cost. On the basis of the 2021 national average medicare reimbursement for AF ablation, the unadjusted outpatient reimbursement was $21 464 (Current Procedural Terminology codes 93 656 and 93 653), and the unadjusted inpatient reimbursement was $22 231 (diagnosis‐related group codes 273 and 274). Using weighted reimbursement (86% outpatient, and 14% inpatient), we calculated a resultant average reimbursement or cost of AF ablation as $21 571. The average cost of an AF hospitalization is determined to be $21 906. 6 Applying this information to our raw data for AF readmissions 1 year before ablation (0.47 per patient) versus AF readmissions 1 year after ablation (0.15 per patient), the net savings would be $6847 per patient per year. Similarly, considering 1 ED visit costs ≈$4100.80, the cost savings for our cohort would be $1231 per patient per year. 7 Thus, the combined savings for AF readmissions and ED visits would be $8078 per patient per year for a 1‐time cost of $21 571. Notably, cost savings from outpatient antiarrhythmic medications were not captured in our study; thus, the savings are likely underestimated. In addition, if one were to project the savings to the future, after 30 months postablation, one would anticipate $8078 in cost savings per patient per year.

We report a decline in the percentage of patients on antiarrhythmic agents in the 2 years following AF ablation, which, considering limited patient eligibility, significant toxicities, and adverse effects of antiarrhythmic medications, is a favorable observation. We also note a similar decline in the number of total and cardiovascular hospitalizations, AF readmissions, and ED visits among patients undergoing >1 AF ablation, as we did in the pooled cohort, suggesting that the positive impact on resource use and patient hospitalization holds true regardless of the need for repeated therapy following the index ablation.

The incidence of AF is increasing in the United States along with the aging population, conferring significant burden of morbidity and mortality. 8 In addition, the diagnostic and therapeutic interventions performed for the treatment of this complex disease impose notable costs on the US health care system. A study performed in 2011 quoted an incremental national cost of $26 billion for inpatient, outpatient medical, and outpatient pharmacy expenses attributable to AF. 9 Management strategies for AF could entail rate control, rhythm control using antiarrhythmic agents, or rhythm control using pulmonary vein isolation or AF ablation procedures. 1 , 10 Recent studies have demonstrated that regardless of symptoms, an early rhythm control strategy with antiarrhythmic agents or AF ablation in patients with concomitant cardiovascular conditions is associated with a lower risk of death from cardiovascular causes, stroke, or hospitalization for heart failure or acute coronary syndrome. 11 In specific patient populations, such as those with heart failure and reduced ejection fraction on optimal medical therapy and symptomatic AF, catheter ablation has been shown to decrease the composite end point of death from any cause or hospitalization for worsening heart failure compared with medical therapy. 12 In line with the findings of the CASTLE‐AF (Catheter Ablation for Atrial Fibrillation with Heart Failure) study, although not all patients in our cohort had failed antiarrhythmic treatment before ablation, we found that patients who carried a diagnosis of CHF had more total, cardiovascular, and AF recurrence hospitalizations as well as ED visits compared with patients without a diagnosis of CHF, as measured by 2‐sample t‐test (Table S3). 12 In addition, catheter ablation is recommended for patients with AF with symptomatic, drug refractory disease, young age, first episode of AF, tachycardia‐mediated cardiomyopathy, and patient preference, within confines of procedural candidacy. 10 , 13 Thus, it is no surprise that over the past 2 decades, the number of AF ablations being performed has increased and will likely continue to increase with improvements in procedural techniques, operational efficiency, and safety profile. 13 , 14 , 15 It is therefore prudent to analyze the impact of catheter ablation therapy on resource use, health care costs, and patient quality of life in addition to cardiovascular outcomes, especially as, increasingly, ablation is being considered as a first‐line rhythm control therapy.

Our study contributes to the body of knowledge supporting the hypothesis that AF treatment with ablation, in addition to correlating with a better quality of life, may also provide a health care cost benefit. In addition to fewer hospitalizations and direct resource use in the form of ED visits and electrical cardioversions, the observation that fewer patients are on antiarrhythmic therapy following AF ablation is significant because antiarrhythmic initiation often requires a hospital admission for surveillance and monitoring and places patients at risk for subsequent life‐altering adverse effects. Although it is plausible that patients with highly symptomatic and drug‐resistant forms of AF are the ones most likely to undergo AF ablation and thus also likely to demonstrate decreased resource use after ablation, this is a significant finding nonetheless because such patients are the most vulnerable to higher morbidity and mortality from AF, and perhaps contribute most significantly to the national health care costs. Prior data do report that incremental annual costs accrued following AF ablation fall within conventionally accepted thresholds for value in the US health care system, although a decline in health care costs following ablation has not yet been demonstrated. 2 , 15 Studies such as the present analysis, wherein detailed clinical metrics are collected prospectively for each ablation procedure performed within a large integrated multicenter hospital system, are then retrospectively extracted and analyzed to assess patient outcomes and resource use surrogates, and they lay an important observational foundation for further scientific inquiry. Further studies dedicated to cost analysis comparing various AF treatment modalities may reveal a clear health care cost benefit associated with AF ablation, in addition to favorable clinical end points for patients with AF. Beyond the decision to perform an AF ablation, such studies could impact timing of AF ablation, treatment, and referral policies, as well as management guidelines.

Our study has some limitations. First, the electronic health records data were derived from a single hospital system, introducing the possibility of selection bias, albeit the 5 centers included in the study span the entire spectrum of clinical practice from a community setting to a quaternary care referral center within a large multihospital health care system and include 12 clinical cardiac electrophysiology operators. Second, our analysis does not account for any hospitalizations or cardiovascular interventions that may have occurred outside of the studied health network; however, most patients in our cohort also received their primary care within the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center system, suggesting most patients in the study, when seeking urgent care for AF, would likely be within driving distance of 1 of the numerous inpatient, outpatient, and emergency care University of Pittsburgh Medical Center facilities spread throughout western Pennsylvania. Third, this is a single‐arm retrospective, observational analysis without a control group; thus, the results establish correlation without causation.

Conclusions

We demonstrate a decrease in health care resource use after AF ablation based on an observational analysis. Specifically, we found a decrease in total hospitalizations, cardiovascular hospitalizations, AF hospitalizations, ED visits, hospital LOS, and use of electrical cardioversions in addition to demonstrating a decline in antiarrhythmic agent use during a 2‐year follow‐up after the index ablation procedure, compared with the 1 year before ablation. Thus, AF ablation as a treatment strategy may portend a decline in patient morbidity and health care resource use. Proper understanding of this aspect of care is important to inform clinical decision‐making, institutional policies for treatment and referral, as well as published management guidelines that afford better patient outcomes, including quality of life, while also minimizing health care costs incurred in the management of this complex disease with considerable population‐wide disease and cost burden.
