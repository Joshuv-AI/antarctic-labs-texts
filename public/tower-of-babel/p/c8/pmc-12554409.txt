Cardiovascular Mortality in Incarcerated Populations: An Example of Health Inequalities Within Health Inequalities
Authors: Benjamin J. Gray, Stephanie E. Perrett
Published: 2025

In this era of mass incarceration, not only do deep‐rooted social and health inequalities persist, but they are widening. 1 , 2 , 3 Those in contact with the justice system often experience poorer health than their community peers, commonly originating from a combination of complex social circumstances, adverse childhood experiences, and higher rates of smoking and alcohol and substance use. 4 , 5 Some European countries even view the health of justice‐involved individuals as part of the public health umbrella, 6 and there have been increasing calls in the United States by some academics to also adopt this mantra. 3 , 7

One of the most substantial public health challenges facing this population is that of cardiovascular disease (CVD). 3 , 6 In some countries, carceral facilities have been deemed as obesogenic in nature due to cramped conditions and limited opportunities for physical activity. 8 There is even the suggestion that incarceration may be an independent cardiovascular risk factor, 8 and exposure to incarceration may increase the susceptibility of an individual to develop CVD at a younger age. 9 , 10 Despite the known cardiovascular risks among carceral populations, and increasing cardiovascular mortality, 3 , 6 , 7 , 9 little is known about the use of prison health care in terms of prevention, screening, diagnosis, and treatment before a cardiovascular event in prison.

Eberly and colleagues 11 help to somewhat fill this evidence gap in this issue of the Journal of American Heart Association (JAHA). Eberly and colleagues 11 sought to understand trends of cardiovascular mortality and use of health care before death and considered the deep‐rooted inequalities of mass incarceration, stratifying their analysis by race and geography. To do this, data contained within the Bureau of Justice Deaths in Custody Reporting Program from 2001 to 2019 were analyzed. In addition to the cause of death, the Death in Custody Reporting Program collects sociodemographic characteristics, the circumstances of death, information about the death, and details of any medical evaluation or treatment received. Completion of a standardized form detailing this information is a requirement under the Death in Custody Report Act and is usually completed by prison administrators and correctional medical staff.

In the study period there were 65 021 deaths reported in state prisons, of which 18 277 (28.0%) were attributed to CVD. Ischemic heart disease was the most common cardiovascular condition reported and accounted for around three‐quarters of the cardiovascular deaths. After a period of decline in the initial years of the study, the age‐standardized cardiovascular mortality rates have increased since 2012 and were 128.2 per 100 000 population in 2019. Most deaths occurred in those individuals who had been incarcerated for ≥10 years. The proportion of cardiovascular deaths increased concomitantly with age; however, ≈40% occurred in those ≤54 years of age. In Black, Hispanic or Latino, and American Indian/Alaska Native populations, this proportion was closer to 50%. Those incarcerated are often referred to as older once they reach 50 years of age 10 ; however, these numbers still illustrate an abundance of premature mortality due to CVD.

Perhaps one of the most concerning findings from Eberly et al 11 is the high proportion of people who were not medically evaluated or in receipt of diagnostic testing or treatment for CVD before their death. Black patients were found to be less likely to be evaluated by a physician or medical staff, although multivariate analysis found the odds of receiving care or treatment before death did not differ significantly between White or Black individuals. Differences were instead found by geographic region. These findings pose more questions about the access to, and equitable provision of, prison health care across the country. These findings suggest factors influencing health‐seeking behavior among those in custody should be further explored, examining the impact of incarceration on health‐seeking as well as by racial and ethnic groups. 12 Poor health literacy coupled with stigma may prevent people presenting to prison health care services.

Early engagement, detection, and diagnosis are key community strategies to prevent premature cardiovascular mortality and need to be equally available for imprisoned populations. Nearly a quarter of all the cardiovascular deaths in the study by Eberly and colleagues 11 were from a condition that developed after admission to prison. This suggests that screening should be intensified at admission to prison and offered regularly thereafter. A recent systematic review looking at interventions for the detection, monitoring, and management of chronic noncommunicable diseases in the prison population was inconclusive in the most effective way to do this. 13 The review authors recommended that more research is needed to determine the most effective interventions for improving chronic disease management in prisons and how these should be implemented to ensure optimal success. 13 Couple these recommendations with the findings by Eberly et al, 11 and there is a key research gap that could be addressed. Determining the most effective interventions for different ethnicities is also important to try and address the inequalities within inequalities that were observed by Eberly et al. 11

It should not be overlooked that the data reported in this present study by Eberly and colleagues 11 were collected before the COVID‐19 pandemic. The public health community is fully aware that the pandemic exacerbated many existing health inequalities, and the prison environment did not escape this. 14 It is reasonable to assume that cardiovascular treatment and care in US prisons has worsened since 2019. This is likely not just an isolated issue for the United States. Our own evidence from the United Kingdom demonstrates a high prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors in the prison population. 15 Thus, if the study were to be replicated in the United Kingdom, the access to cardiovascular treatment and outcomes could be akin to the findings of Eberly and colleagues.

Eberly et al 11 acknowledge their findings are limited to prison settings, having excluded jails (typically settings used to serve sentences <1 year), and not following individuals on release. Continuity of care should be an idea of future work and a sensible development to the research by Eberly and colleagues. In the forthcoming years, it will be interesting to follow the emerging insights from the ongoing JUSTICE (Justice‐Involved Individuals Cardiovascular Disease Epidemiology) prospective cohort study. 16 The JUSTICE study involves 500 individuals with known cardiovascular risk factors recruited within 3 months of release from incarceration. The study seeks to estimate the direct and indirect latent effects of incarceration on cardiovascular risk factors and will also examine any mediation. 16

The most recent evidence from the JUSTICE cohort, which was published in the September 17, 2024 issue of this journal, highlighted a burden of uncontrolled cardiovascular risk factors in those released from carceral facilities. 17 Control of cardiovascular risk factors was found to be worse in those released from jail compared with prison, but the authors of the study expressed that there is a real need to address cardiovascular health in all those who have experienced incarceration. 17 Therefore, if health improvements are really to be made among incarcerated communities, cardiovascular care should be prioritized from pre‐entry (among communities at high risk of imprisonment), at entry into any carceral facility, throughout the custodial sentence, and continued upon release.

With the right design, carceral facilities may have factors protective of CVD, for example, where facilities are smoke free 18 and have a health‐promoting regime. 19 We acknowledge such designs are far from universal and remain an aspiration for most nations. Due to the increasing churn of individuals through the justice system and increasing numbers globally of those incarcerated, there will be community health dividends 6 from efforts to improve the health of incarcerated populations, with the opportunity to impact national rates of CVD disease. Health on reentry to the community is also important. Those on release who are healthier, both physically and mentally, improve their chances of employment, have better family relationships, and ultimately reduce the chances of recidivism. 20

To summarize, the research by Eberly and colleagues 11 provides a timely spotlight on the cardiovascular health needs of justice‐involved populations. This research is ably complemented by the emerging evidence from the JUSTICE cohort study. 16 , 17 Combine these research findings with an evolving political landscape, and ultimately, we are reminded of the challenges that remain in reducing the health inequalities experienced by those in carceral facilities, not just in the United States, but across the globe.
