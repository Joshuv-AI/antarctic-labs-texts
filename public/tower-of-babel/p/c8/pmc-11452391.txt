Evidence of the association between asthma and lung cancer risk from mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Lingling Ye, Fen Wang, Hao Wu, Yihang Yuan, Quan’an Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsthma and lung cancer are both significant public health concerns worldwide. Previous observational studies have indicated a potential link between asthma and an increased risk of lung cancer, whereas the causal relationship remains uncertain. We aimed to investigate the potential causal relationship between asthma and lung cancer risk utilizing Mendelian randomization (MR) design.The present study employed a two-sample MR analysis utilizing summary statistics from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) with European descent of asthma and lung cancer. The MR analysis was performed using inverse variance weighting (IVW), supplemented with MR-Egger regression and weighted median method to investigate the potential causality between asthma and lung cancer. Furthermore, Sensitivity analyses were also conducted to ensure the reliability of the findings. The MR analysis showed that genetically predicted asthma had suggestive causal association with the elevated risk of lung cancer [odds ratio (OR), 1.05 (95%Cl,1.01–1.09), P = 0.01]. The consistent direction of effects observed in the three methods further supported this finding. In addition, sensitivity analyses demonstrated the reliability of the results. This study provided potential evidence supporting a causal association between asthma and lung cancer. These findings highlighted the importance of early detection and prevention strategies for lung cancer in individuals with asthma. Further research was needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms linking asthma and lung cancer.Keywords: Asthma, Lung cancer, MR analysis, GWAS, Causal associationSubject terms: Cancer, Diseases, Risk factors

Introduction

Lung cancer accounts for the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide (18.0% of all cancer deaths) and ranks as the second most commonly diagnosed malignancy (11.4% of all cases)1. It can be divided into two main histologic subtypes: small cell lung cancer(SCLC), which accounts for only 15–17% of all cases, and non-small cell lung cancer(NSCLC), which accounts for 85%2.The majority of lung cancer patients (48%) were diagnosed with distant metastases, and the 5-year relative survival rate for these patients was about 8%, while the 5-year survival rate for individuals diagnosed with localized lung cancer was ≥ 60%3. In addition, research has indicated that patients with lung cancer pose a significant economic burden on the health service in terms of direct and indirect costs4. A number of studies have explored the factors influencing lung cancer, and smoking5, environmental exposures (biomass fuels, arsenic, radon, industrial carcinogens and air pollution)6, and genetic factors have been identified as contributing to the increased incidence of lung cancer7.

Asthma is a prevalent chronic disease affecting roughly 300 million people globally8. Characterized by chronic inflammation, variable airflow obstruction, and bronchial hyperresponsiveness9,10, asthma is associated with various comorbidities, including metabolic syndrome, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and cardiovascular diseases11,12. Additionally, studies have demonstrated that there is a correlation between asthma and an elevated risk of developing various cancers, such as lung cancer, breast cancer, and stomach cancer13,14, which may be related to the chronic inflammation in asthma patients15. Chronic inflammation is known to create a microenvironment conducive to cancer development, characterized by the sustained production of inflammatory compounds, cytokines, and growth factors16. This environment supports tumor cell survival, proliferation, invasion, and angiogenesis, thereby facilitating cancer progression17,18. The T helper 2 cell (Th2) immune deviation hypothesis further suggests that elevated serum IgE levels might redirect Th2 responses, reducing the effectiveness of Th1-mediated tumor clearance19. The lack of a Th1-driven microenvironment mediated by interferon gamma (IFN-γ), IL-2, IL-3, and TNF-α leads to inappropriate cytotoxic activity of T lymphocytes and M1 macrophages, potentially downregulating the processes that promote tumor clearance19. Moreover, chronic inflammation may contribute to the carcinogenic process through increased levels of free radicals, decreased levels of antioxidants, and enhanced DNA damage and mutations15. Given the complex interplay between chronic inflammation and cancer development, it is crucial to further explore specific cancer types in the context of asthma.

Lung cancer is especially pertinent for investigation because of its direct impact on the respiratory system, which is already compromised in individuals with asthma20. Numerous studies have investigated the relationship between asthma and the risk of lung cancer, with some indicating that asthma might heighten this risk20–25. For instance, the population-based cohort study in Sweden, which included 92,986 asthma hospitalized patients, showed that patients with asthma had an elevated risk of developing lung cancer21. It was reported that lung cancer incidence was increased in adults with both partially controlled asthma and active asthma compared with those without asthma, after adjusting for potential confounders, in the HUNT study20. A study involving 64,170 participants indicated that individuals with a history of asthma had a 25% increased risk of developing lung cancer. This positive association was consistent across different racial and ethnic groups22. Furthermore, in a recent study, researchers conducted a retrospective cohort study using electronic health records and claims data from 2012 to 2020 in the OneFlorida + clinical research network. The results showed that, compared to patients without asthma, asthma patients had an increased cancer risk regardless of whether they used inhaled steroids (without inhaled steroids: HR = 1.74; 99% CI: 1.44–2.11; with inhaled steroids: HR = 1.39; 99% CI: 1.14–1.70)24.

However, some studies have reached different conclusions, with reports of, no association22,25,26and negative associations27,28. For example, Rahman et al. reported that a cross-sectional study based on the US population showed no evidence of a correlation between asthma and lung cancer26. A meta-analysis that included 16 studies with 585,444 participants showed similar findings4. Observational studies presented contradictory results as results could be affected by confounding factors, such as air quality and smoking4. Thus, we need an effective approach to elucidate the causal relationship between them.

MR analysis was considered a promising epidemiological method for accurately assessing potential causal relationships between exposures and outcomes29. Due to the relatively stable genetic effects of genetic variants that are less susceptible to environmental influences, and genotypes being determined during the prenatal period30, this inherent characteristic enables MR to bypass the issues of confounding and reverse causation commonly found in traditional observational studies31. However, reverse causation can still impact its analysis results, researchers can adopt several strategies to mitigate or identify biases caused by reverse causation32: (1) Select Appropriate Genetic Variants: Choose variants with a direct and well-understood association mechanism with the risk factor to minimize bias. (2) Utilize MR-Steiger Method: This method helps identify variants that explain more variance in the outcome than the risk factor, indicating potential unsuitability for analysis. (3) Conduct Simulation Studies: Simulations can assess the extent of bias from feedback mechanisms, aiding in understanding bias likelihood and severity. (4) Analyze Cross-Generational Data: When available, use statistical methods to examine how parental outcomes might influence those in offspring, identifying potential reverse causation. (5) Interpret Results Cautiously: Be mindful of reverse causation, particularly across generations, and focus on testing causal null hypotheses rather than estimating specific effects. These strategies enhance the accuracy and reliability of MR studies by mitigating potential biases from reverse causation. Our aim was to utilize GWAS statistics for MR analysis for exploring the possible causal link between asthma and lung cancer in a more feasible way.

Methods

Data sources

This study aimed to assess the causal effects of asthma on lung cancer using two-sample MR analysis.

The data sources consisted of publicly available GWAS summary data obtained from the IEU-OpenGWAS project, involving 462,933 male and female participants from European, to identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with asthma. Summary data for lung cancer were obtained from the GWAS which included 401,624 males and females of European descent33. In the MR study, summary data of 53,598 asthma patients and 409,335 controls, and 37,443 patients with lung cancer and 364,181 controls were obtained, respectively.

Selection of instrumental variables

A series of quality control processes were conducted for the selection of appropriate instrumental variables (IVs). We abstracted SNPs linked to asthma at the genome-wide significant threshold (p < 5 × 10 − 8). A clumping process was conducted to ensure the independence of IVs, excluding SNPs in linkage disequilibrium (LD) with r^2 < 0.001 and SNPs with physical distances in the 10,000 kb range. In addition, to assess potential bias for weak instrumental variable, the F statistic was evaluated with the following formula: F = (N-2) * R^2 / (1-R^2), in which N denoted the sample size, and R2 indicated the extent of exposure explained by IV. The specific calculation method for R2 is as follows: R2= 2*(1-MAF)*MAF*β, where MAF denoted the minor allele frequency, and β represented the effect size of the SNP on the exposure. To minimize weak instrument bias, subsequent analyses were conducted only on SNPs with an F statistic > 1034.

Statistical analyses

Three MR methods were employed to evaluate the causal association between asthma and lung cancer risk: IVW, weighted median, and MR-Egger method. The use of multiple methods was intended to ensure robustness in causal inference, as each has different assumptions and strengths in handling horizontal pleiotropy and other potential biases. Specifically, the IVW method assumes that the IVs influence the outcomes solely through the exposure of interest, rather than through alternative pathways35. This is a widely used method for causal estimation, providing efficient estimates of causal effects. To address the possibility of invalid instrumental variables, we employed the Weighted Median method, which can provide consistent causal effect estimates even when some instrumental variables are invalid. This method calculates the causal effect by taking the median of the valid instrumental variables, ensuring robustness even if more than 50% of the instruments are invalid36. Additionally, the MR-Egger regression allows some instrumental variables to have direct effects on the outcome, thus addressing the issue of horizontal pleiotropy. Through this method, we can assess small-sample bias and detect horizontal pleiotropy among the instrumental variables, enhancing the credibility of our causal inference37. IVW method, which is the most commonly used method of causality estimation in MR analysis35, was the main method for estimating the causal relationship between asthma and lung cancer in the present study. Integrating results from other methods allows for the verification of the consistency of the IVW results, thereby enhancing confidence in the findings. In cases where the IVW method produces a significant result (p< 0.05) and no pleiotropy or heterogeneity is detected, the outcome can be interpreted as a positive finding, provided that other methods also show consistent effect directions, even if they are not statistically significant38,39.

To evaluate the robustness of the observed causal relationship, a series of sensitivity analyses were conducted. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q statistic40. MR Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) was conducted to eliminate the possible outlier SNPs. MR-PRESSO is a statistical tool that identifies outlier SNPs caused by pleiotropy and assesses their impact on causal effect estimates41. The method involves the following steps: (1) calculating causal effect estimates for all SNPs; (2) detecting outliers and evaluating their influence on the overall causal effect; (3) correcting the impact of outliers if necessary. MR-Egger intercept analysis and funnel plots were assessed horizontal pleiotropy, whether the instruments affect the exposures and outcomes through non-causal pathways37. We further performed leave-one-out analysis, which assessed the impact of each SNP on the findings by analyzing the data with one SNP removed at a time.

All analyses were performed with the ‘TwoSampleMR’ R package in the R software (v4.2.0). Regarding MR analyses, a p-value of less than 0.05 for IVW and consistency in the orientation of the three MR methods were essential for demonstrating causality. All p-values were two-sided, and the significance level was defined as 0.05.

Causal association of asthma on lung cancer

The impact of each SNP on asthma susceptibility was illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2. Each point in Fig. 1 represented a SNP and its corresponding effect size on asthma risk. The trend line in the scatter plot reflected an overall positive correlation, indicating that an increase in asthma risk (as indicated by the SNP effect sizes) was associated with an increased risk of lung cancer. In Fig. 2, we focused on two SNPs that were identified as potentially contributing to lung cancer risk: rs58521088 and rs9270911. Both SNPs appeared to show strong effect sizes in the figure, suggesting that they may play important roles in susceptibility to lung cancer risk.

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis supported the robustness of the causal association between genetic predictors of asthma and lung cancer risk. The result from heterogeneity test indicated no evidence of heterogeneity in the analysis (Cochran’s Q statistic, Q = 17.00, P = 0.06). The funnel plot analysis indicated asymmetry in the effect estimates, with two points significantly deviating from the main trend (Fig. 4). However, we further conducted the MR-PRESSO method, and the results indicated no significant outliers (p-value > 0.05). The results of MR-Egger regression revealed no signs of directional pleiotropy (p-value > 0.05). Furthermore, leave-one-out analysis was performed for the purpose of assessing the effect of individual SNPs on the observed causality. Figure 5 illustrateed that no significant differences were observed when excluding individual SNPs, suggesting that the causal impact is not driven by any single SNP, particularly highlighting the major influence of rs9270911.

Discussion

This research represented the first attempt to systematically investigate the causality between asthma and lung cancer utilizing MR analysis. Our findings revealed that genetically predicted asthma related to enhanced risk of lung cancer in individuals of European ancestry. Sensitivity analysis further confirmed the reliability of the estimated positive correlation.

This finding was consistent with previously observational researches that have demonstrated the positive relationship between asthma and lung cancer risk20,21,28. However, the causal nature of this relationship has been uncertain due to confounding factors and reverse causation. By utilizing genetic variants as instrumental variables, MR analysis can overcome these limitations and provide a more reliable estimate of causality42. In our research, several MR methods were utilized to explore the causal link between asthma and lung cancer. The main analysis conducted with the IVW method showed statistically significant results (p < 0.05), suggesting a potential causal effect. Notably, while other MR methods such as MR-Egger and the weighted median did not achieve statistical significance, the consistency in the direction of the effect estimates across all methods reinforces the plausibility of a causal relationship.

The statistical significance observed with the IVW method can be attributed to its higher statistical power, particularly when assumptions about the validity of instrumental variables are met35. The consistency in effect direction across different methods suggests that the observed association is unlikely to be a result of random chance or method-specific biases. This directional consistency, coupled with the absence of evidence for pleiotropy or heterogeneity, strengthens our confidence in the IVW findings. The lack of statistical significance in the MR-Egger and weighted median methods could be due to their conservative nature, which is designed to account for potential pleiotropy and violations of instrumental variable assumptions36. These methods generally have lower power compared to IVW, especially in scenarios with limited sample size or when only a few instrumental variables are available39.

Importantly, sensitivity analyses showed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy, as indicated by the MR-Egger intercept, and no significant heterogeneity was detected across SNPs, as evidenced by Cochran’s Q statistics. Although the funnel plot demonstrated asymmetry and certain outlying points, this may be due to random error, the limited number of available genetic instruments, or other unrecognized systematic biases43. However, the results from MR-PRESSO and MR-Egger regression suggest that these factors may have a limited impact on the overall conclusions.

According to our research results, rs58521088 and rs9270911 showed a significant positive influence on the causal effect of asthma and lung cancer risk in MR analyses (see Figs. 2 and 5). SNP rs58521088 is located in the BACH2 gene, while rs9270911 is situated near the HLA-DRB1 gene and is considered a regulatory region variant that influences immune responses by modulating the expression or function of the HLA-DRB1 gene44. The HLA-DRB1 gene is primarily responsible for presenting exogenous antigens (such as those from bacteria, viruses, or tumor antigens) to CD4 + T cells45. Activated CD4 + T cells secrete various cytokines that regulate the functions of other immune cells, which helps enhance the immune response of the body to infections and conditions including tumors45. Studies suggested that HLA-DRB1 alleles are connected to an elevated risk of asthma and various cancers. For example, it was reported that HLADRB1*11:01 and HLA-DRB1*10:01 alleles are associated with increased breast cancer risk46. Research has indicated that the HLA-DRB1*0901 allele was associated with an increased risk of lung cancer47. In addition, previous study also found that increased HLA-DRB1*03 allele frequency was positively associated with risk of asthma.

The implications of our findings are significant for both clinical practice and public health. Firstly, our results highlighted the importance of increased surveillance and targeted screening for lung cancer in individuals with asthma. Early detection of lung cancer in this population may lead to improved outcomes and survival rates. Secondly, personalized prevention strategies should be considered for individuals with asthma to mitigate their risk of developing lung cancer. This may include lifestyle modifications, such as smoking cessation or reducing exposure to environmental carcinogens, as well as close monitoring of lung health in asthma management.

It was important to acknowledge the limitations of our study. First, this study focused on people of European descent, and therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating the findings to non-European ancestry. Second, our findings provide preliminary evidence supporting a causal link between asthma and lung cancer, future research should increase sample sizes, use additional instrumental variables, and incorporate other study designs to verify and expand these results. Moreover, other potential confounding factors and biological mechanisms may play a role in the relationship between asthma and lung cancer. These factors need to be further explored in future research to fully understand the complex relationship between the two.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study utilized MR analysis to provide preliminary evidence for the causal link between asthma and an increased lung cancer risk. These findings emphasized the importance of targeted screening, early detection, and personalized prevention strategies for individuals with asthma to decrease their chances of experiencing lung cancer. Further research is warranted to elucidate the potential mechanisms and explore possible interventions to reduce the burden of lung cancer in this population.
