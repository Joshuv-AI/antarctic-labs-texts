The World Health Organization (WHO) Enhanced Gonococcal Antimicrobial Surveillance Programme (EGASP) reports continuously high levels of ceftriaxone resistance across Viet Nam, 2024
Authors: Pham Thi Lan, Thuy Thi Phan Nguyen, Vu Tuan Anh, Daniel Golparian, Nguyen Thi Thuy Van, Ismael Maatouk, Magnus Unemo, the WHO EGASP-Viet Nam WGS study group
Published: 2025

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in Neisseria gonorrhoeae is a global public health concern, and the last option for gonorrhoea treatment, ceftriaxone, is threatened.1 Most ceftriaxone-resistant strains have links to Asia.1,2 World Health Organization (WHO) has expanded and improved its global AMR surveillance, especially in Asia. The WHO Enhanced Gonococcal Antimicrobial Surveillance Programme (EGASP) uses standardised and quality-assured protocols in sentinel countries worldwide and has also implemented whole-genome sequencing (WGS).3 In 2023, the WHO EGASP-Viet Nam reported 26.9% resistance to ceftriaxone in Northern (Hanoi) and Southern Viet Nam (Ho Chi Minh City, HCMC).4 Some of this ceftriaxone resistance was caused by the mosaic penA-60.001 allele (11.9%), encoding a mosaic penicillin-binding protein 2 (PBP2), which has caused most ceftriaxone resistance globally. However, the majority of the ceftriaxone resistance was caused by non-penA-60.001 mosaic alleles (88.1%), especially mosaic penA-237.001 (70.1%).4 Recently, many penA-237.001-containing isolates were also described in another study in Hanoi, Viet Nam,5 and occasional penA-237.001-containing isolates have been found in France6 (linked to Viet Nam) and the UK.7

In 2024, WHO EGASP-Viet Nam expanded to include a sentinel site also in Central Viet Nam (Quy Nhơn Nam, Gia Lai province (previously belonging to Binh Dinh province)). Gonococcal isolates (n = 436) were cultured from urethral specimens from males in Hanoi (n = 202), HCMC (n = 201), and Gia Lai (n = 33). Gonococcal confirmation, AMR testing, and WGS were performed on 429 (98.4%) isolates (Appendix pp2-3).

The resistance to ceftriaxone, cefixime, and azithromycin was 32.4% (139/429), 37.3% (160/429), and 7.9% (34/429), respectively (26.9%, 30.9% and 4.8%, respectively, in 20234). The ceftriaxone resistance had increased in both Hanoi (35.5% (71/200); 31.1% in 20234) and HCMC (24.2% (48/198); 20.8% in 20234), and was very high in Gia Lai (64.5% (20/31); not included in 20234) (Fig. 1). The cefixime resistance had also increased in Hanoi (43.0% (86/200); 23.0% in 20234) but decreased in HCMC (28.3% (56/198); 36.2% in 20234). In 2024, only 30.2% (42/139) of the ceftriaxone-resistant isolates contained mosaic penA-60.001. Most (69.8%, 97/139) ceftriaxone-resistant isolates instead harboured mosaic non-penA-60.001 alleles, mostly mosaic penA-237.001 (n = 77). All these mosaic penA alleles contain the ceftriaxone-resistance-associated PBP2 alteration A311V, and penA-60.001 (n = 42) and penA-237.001 (n = 77) were widespread in the gonococcal population across Viet Nam. As in 2023,4 the majority of penA-237.001-containing isolates clustered with the first penA-237.001-containing strain identified (F92, cultured in France but linked to Viet Nam, 2022).6 However, some penA-237.001-containing isolates were also located in other parts of the phylogenomic tree. Similarly, most penA-60.001-containing isolates clustered with the internationally spreading ceftriaxone-resistant WHO R (FC428),2,4,8 but penA-60.001-containing isolates were detected also across the Vietnamese gonococcal genomic population (Fig. 1). Evidently, the main mosaic penA-237.001 and penA-60.001 ceftriaxone-resistant strains are continuously clonally expanding, but these mosaic penA alleles are additionally found in several different genomic backgrounds. This illustrates that the ceftriaxone resistance is not only caused by clonal expansion of ceftriaxone-resistant strains but also due to the spread of ceftriaxone resistance-mediating penA alleles to previously ceftriaxone-susceptible strains. Azithromycin-resistant isolates (n = 34) contained resistance-associated semi-mosaic MtrD variants (n = 31), mosaic MtrD variant (n = 1), and/or mtrR promoter A-deletion (n = 2) (Fig. 1). It is most concerning that 1.4% (6/429) of isolates were extensively drug-resistant (XDR), i.e., resistant to ceftriaxone, cefixime, azithromycin, and ciprofloxacin. These six XDR strains belonged to multilocus sequence typing (MLST) sequence type (ST) 9362 (n = 5) or ST17523 (n = 1) and harboured penA-60.001 (n = 5) or penA-237.003 (n = 1), and a semi-mosaic MtrD variant. However, no isolates of the XDR strain spreading in the neighbouring country Cambodia (penA-60.001, 23S rRNA A2059G, MLST ST16406, NG-STAR CC5793, see AT159 in Fig. 1)9,10 were found in Viet Nam.

The high ceftriaxone-resistance level (32.4%, 139/429) was mainly caused by widespread disseminations of penA-237.001-containing (in Hanoi and HCMC) and penA-60.001-containing (especially in HCMC and Gia Lai) strains. Importantly, 69.8% of the ceftriaxone-resistant isolates contained non-penA-60.001 alleles, which can be missed in the developed real-time PCRs targeting penA-60.001.11, 12, 13 This shows the essential need of implementing WGS in the global gonococcal AMR surveillance. The level of resistance to ceftriaxone (32.4%, 139/429; 26.9% in 20234) across Viet Nam remains exceedingly high (Fig. 1), and XDR strains have emerged and started to clonally expand. It is a major concern that the most common gonorrhoea treatment in 2024 was ceftriaxone 250 mg plus azithromycin 1 g (75.3%, 323/429), and cefixime 400 mg in monotherapy or combined with azithromycin 1–2 g remained to be used (4.0%, 17/429). Only 17.5% (75/429) of participants returned for test-of-cure, and six remained N. gonorrhoeae culture positive. These six participants were initially treated with cefixime 400 mg monotherapy (n = 2), cefixime 400 mg plus azithromycin 1 g (n = 2), ceftriaxone 250 mg monotherapy (n = 1), or ceftriaxone 250 mg plus azithromycin 1 g (n = 1). After positive test-of-cure, they were all cured with ceftriaxone 1 g monotherapy. Notably, the six participants with XDR gonococcal strains were treated with ceftriaxone 250 mg plus azithromycin 1 g (n = 4) or ceftriaxone 500 mg plus azithromycin 1 g (n = 2). Only one of these six participants returned for test-of-cure, which was N. gonorrhoeae negative. The national gonorrhoea treatment guidelines in Viet Nam are essential to promptly revise, which is also in progress, based on the results of the WHO EGASP-Viet Nam 2023–2024 surveillance. Hopefully, first-line treatment will become ceftriaxone 1 g monotherapy (ideally combined with azithromycin 2 g when ceftriaxone 1 g fails to cure the gonococcal infection) for all cases of urogenital, anorectal and oropharyngeal gonorrhoea, which would be in agreement with the recently published WHO gonorrhoea treatment guideline.14 A mandatory test-of-cure for all gonorrhoea cases, at a minimum when patient remains symptomatic and for oropharyngeal infections, would also be valuable. However, it should be acknowledged that a mandatory test-of-cure can add substantial cost to already challenged health-care systems. Encouragingly, based on treatment of occasional cases2,7 and pharmacodynamics studies,15 ceftriaxone 1 g can cure most urogenital infections caused by ceftriaxone-resistant strains with MICs of 0.25–0.5 mg/L (97.1% (135/139) of ceftriaxone-resistant isolates in Viet Nam in 2024 (Appendix p5)).

Resistance in N. gonorrhoeae to ceftriaxone, which is the last option for empiric first-line gonorrhoea treatment, is a major global public health concern. In Viet Nam, 26.9% and 32.4% of isolates were ceftriaxone resistant in 20234 and 2024, and XDR strains are clonally expanding. Expanded quality-assured N. gonorrhoeae global AMR surveillance, including WGS, is imperative, and this is a main focus for WHO EGASP. Strengthened prevention and early diagnosis and treatment (including test-of-cure) are also crucial. For this, continuous national and especially international commitment, including political, and funding are essential. Encouragingly, two novel antimicrobials, i.e., gepotidacin16 and zoliflodacin,17 will hopefully soon become licensed for the treatment of gonorrhoea. Notably, no known resistance determinant for zoliflodacin (in GyrB D429, K450)1,18 or gepotidacin (GyrA A92T)1,16,19 was observed. Nevertheless, mutations that predispose for development of resistance to zoliflodacin (GyrB S467N1,18) and gepotidacin (ParC D86N1,16,19) were found in 4.9% (21/429) and 17.5% (75/429) of isolates, respectively.
