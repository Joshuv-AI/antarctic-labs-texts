The impact of perceived stress on self-management behaviors among men who have sex with men living with HIV in China: the mediating role of self-regulatory fatigue
Authors: Yuhan Wu, Wenwen Yang, Rui Huang, Mengya Wang, Yan Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs a chronic disease, effective HIV self-management is crucial for men who have sex with men (MSM) living with HIV. Psychosocial factors, including perceived stress and self-regulatory fatigue, have been shown to negatively impact self-management behaviors. However, research on the relationships among perceived stress, self-regulatory fatigue, and self-management behaviors remains limited. This study aimed to explore the mediating effect of self-regulatory fatigue in the relationship between perceived stress and self-management behaviors among MSM living with HIV in China.MethodsFrom December 2024 to March 2025, cross-sectional data were collected from 328 MSM living with HIV at an HIV-designated hospital in Jiangsu Province using convenience sampling. Data were collected using the Perceived Stress Scale, Self-Regulatory Fatigue Scale, and HIV Self-Management Scale. Descriptive statistics, univariate analysis, and Pearson correlation analysis were performed in this study. The mediating effect of self-regulatory fatigue in the relationship between perceived stress and self-management behaviors was assessed using Model 4 of the PROCESS macro for SPSS.ResultsAmong 328 MSM living with HIV, the average score of perceived stress was 30.67 ± 7.87, the average self-regulatory fatigue score was 43.99 ± 8.76, and the average HIV self-management score was 36.65 ± 7.88. Perceived stress was positively related to self-regulatory fatigue (r = 0.544, p<0.05), whereas self-management behaviors were proven to be negatively associated with perceived stress (r = -0.486, p<0.05) and self-regulatory fatigue (r = -0.504, p<0.05). Self-regulatory fatigue partially mediated the association between perceived stress and self-management behaviors, accounting for 34.39% of the total effect.ConclusionThis study demonstrated that self-regulatory fatigue partially mediated the relationship between perceived stress and self-management behaviors and underscored the importance of designing targeted interventions aimed at reducing perceived stress and self-regulatory fatigue to improve self-management behaviors among MSM living with HIV in China. Multidimensional strategies, achieved through collaborative efforts among patients, healthcare providers, government, and community, should incorporate personalized mental care and stress management, available social support, trained peer navigation, and consistent health education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12981-026-00869-2.Keywords: HIV, MSM, Perceived stress, Self-regulatory fatigue, Self-management behaviors

Introduction

Since the first documented HIV case linked to unexplained infection among men who have sex with men (MSM) was identified in the 1980 s, the HIV epidemic within this population has continued to grow [1]. In China, the proportion of newly reported HIV cases attributed to homosexual transmission has increased from 2.5% in 2006 to 25.2% in 2024 [2, 3], positioning MSM as a key population in the HIV epidemic. Although effective antiretroviral therapy (ART) suppresses viral replication and extends life expectancy [4], patients continue to live with the virus, facing ongoing and significant challenges in managing their condition. HIV infection may constitute a profoundly stressful and traumatic experience, with significant negative implications for mental health, particularly among MSM who already contend with pressures related to their sexual orientation [5]. As a vulnerable population, MSM living with HIV are more susceptible to external stigma and social alienation [6, 7], facing multiple pressures in areas including education, employment, and marriage, and tend to internalize homophobia and self-concealment [8]. Under the alternating influence of internal and external pressures, MSM living with HIV may experience elevated levels of psychological burdens [9], which are associated with impaired self-management behaviors [10, 11].

Self-management refers to the daily self-care and lifelong tasks that individuals undertake to reduce the impact of the disease on themselves, including maintaining medication adherence, regulating their emotions, and monitoring their illness symptoms [12]. Several studies conducted in China suggest that self-management performance among MSM living with HIV remains suboptimal [13, 14]. A higher proportion of MSM have been reported to engage in behaviors such as having multiple sexual partners, non-stable partners, and condomless sex [15, 16], which may contribute to significantly lower levels of engagement in health management compared to other populations [14]. Like any other chronic condition, effective self-management behaviors play a crucial role in preventing disease progression, reducing the burden of comorbidities, and improving health status and quality of life [11]. Therefore, it is essential to probe deeply into the factors influencing self-management behaviors among MSM living with HIV and to clarify their mechanisms, which will facilitate the development of intervention programs to enhance patients’ control over the disease.

Among the factors influencing self-management behaviors, perceived stress warrants special attention. Perceived stress is defined as an individual’s subjective perception of stress, reflecting how they sense and evaluate internal and external environmental stimuli in their lives [17]. Most MSM living with HIV are navigating critical life stages, including education, career advancement, marriage, and social engagement, and thus bear multiple social and family pressures. Previous research has shown that higher levels of perceived stress are strongly associated with adverse biological outcomes [18] and an increased risk of psychological distress [19], which may subsequently impair their ability to adhere to self-management behaviors. For example, a structural equation modeling analysis of 302 patients with multiple chronic conditions revealed that higher perceived stress negatively affected an individual’s self-management behaviors [20]. Additionally, a cross-sectional study among adolescents with type 1 diabetes demonstrated that lower perceived stress was associated with greater engagement in diabetes care activities [21]. However, the negative effects of perceived stress on self-management behaviors among MSM living with HIV and its underlying mechanisms remain insufficiently explored. Therefore, this study included perceived stress as a predictor variable and reasonably hypothesized that reducing perceived stress could play a critical role in improving self-management behaviors among MSM living with HIV.

Self-regulatory fatigue is indeed a common issue among individuals managing chronic conditions [22]. Individuals possess psychological resources that enable them to regulate and cope with stress in a positive manner [23]. However, in the prolonged process of disease management, patients continuously expend limited psychological resources; as psychological resources become depleted, patients’ ability or willingness to self-regulate declines, leading to the onset of self-regulatory fatigue [24]. Existing studies have shown that self-regulatory fatigue is negatively associated with self-management behaviors [25, 26]. Therefore, alleviating self-regulatory fatigue is also regarded as an effective strategy for improving self-management behaviors. Moreover, a cross-sectional study found that self-regulatory fatigue mediates the relationship between perceived stress and sleep quality among police officers, indicating a significant association between perceived stress and self-regulatory fatigue [27].

In conclusion, previous studies have independently examined the associations among perceived stress, self-regulatory fatigue, and self-management behaviors. However, these relationships have not been confirmed among MSM living with HIV, and whether self-regulatory fatigue mediates these relationships remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the relationship between perceived stress, self-regulatory fatigue, and self-management behaviors among MSM living with HIV in China, as well as whether self-regulatory fatigue mediates these relationships. The following hypotheses are proposed in this study: (H1) perceived stress may negatively predict self-management behaviors; (H2) perceived stress may positively predict self-regulatory fatigue; (H3) self-regulatory fatigue may negatively predict self-management behaviors; (H4) self-regulatory fatigue may mediate the relationship between perceived stress and self-management behaviors.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was carried out in the outpatient infectious disease department at the Second Hospital in Nanjing, Jiangsu Province, which specializes in the prevention and treatment of infectious diseases and provides ART follow-up services for people living with HIV (PLWH). This study was conducted from December 2024 to March 2025. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were followed to report this study (see Appendix S1).

Participants

In this study, a convenience sampling method was used to select eligible participants. Considering the mobility of outpatients, the sensitivity of HIV, and the concealed nature of MSM identity, convenience sampling increased the feasibility of establishing connections with hard-to-reach populations at the designated hospital authorized to admit these patients. The recruitment of MSM living with HIV was conducted under the guidance of outpatient doctors and nurses. Inclusion criteria included: (1) aged 18 and older; (2) biological sex is male; (3) diagnosed with HIV and self-reported HIV infection through sexual contact with men; (4) having normal verbal communication and reading comprehension ability; (5) providing informed consent and voluntarily participating in the study. Exclusion criteria: individuals with a history of mental illness or severe physical diseases that prevent participation.

The assessment of sample size and power in mediation models was performed using the online application Monte Carlo power analysis developed by Schoemann and Boulton [28]. According to the existing literature, we set the correlation coefficients as follows: 0.20 for the a path, −0.37 for the b path, and − 0.17 for the c’ path [20, 25]. α is set to 0.05. When the sample size is 275, the test power (1-β) can reach 0.9. Meanwhile, this study included 17 variables. Based on the principle of at least 5–10 times the number of variables, and considering a 15% invalid response rate, the total sample size should be no less than 324. Ultimately, 350 questionnaires were distributed, and 328 valid responses were collected.

Instruments

General Information Questionnaire The questionnaire was independently designed by the research team and primarily included demographic and disease-related characteristics such as age, household per capita monthly income, level of education, duration of HIV diagnosis, medical expense payment method, HIV status disclosure, and complications.

The Chinese Perceived Stress Scale The Perceived Stress Scale was first designed by Cohen et al. [17] and locally revised by the Chinese scholar Yang and colleagues [29]. This scale consists of 14 items and can be divided into two dimensions: loss of control and tension. A 5-point Likert scale was used (“Never” to “Always”, scored from 0 to 4), yielding a score range of 0 to 56. A higher total score indicates greater perceived stress. This scale has been validated in various populations and has demonstrated good reliability and validity. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the scale and its dimensions ranged from 0.883 to 0.887.

Self-Regulatory Fatigue Scale The Self-Regulatory Fatigue Scale, developed by Nes in 2013 [24], was revised by the Chinese scholar Wang [30]. This scale comprises 16 items across three dimensions: cognition (6 items), emotion (5 items), and behavioral control (5 items). The scale uses a 5-point Likert scoring system, with options ranging from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree” assigned values from 1 to 5. The score range is 16 to 80 points, with higher scores indicating more severe self-regulatory fatigue. The scale has good internal consistency and test-retest reliability and has been used with patients with chronic disease. In this study, the overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was 0.860, and the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for each dimension were all above 0.8.

HIV Self-Management Scale The HIV Self-Management Scale was developed by Webel et al. [31]. The Chinese version of the HIV Self-Management Scale, translated by scholar Han [32], consists of 20 items and three dimensions: daily self-management health practices, social support for self-management, and long-term self-management. The scale uses a 4-point Likert scoring system, with options ranging from “Not Applicable” to “Always” assigned values from 0 to 3. A higher total score indicates better self-management. The scale has an internal consistency coefficient of 0.918, a test-retest reliability of 0.892–0.960, and content validity of 0.957. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the scale and its dimensions ranged from 0.798 to 0.868.

Data collection procedures

Before the formal investigation, the first author conducted a preliminary study with 40 participants to ensure the feasibility of the selected scales. All data were collected through paper questionnaires. Two researchers had received training in the standardized data collection process and recruited eligible participants with assistance from nurse case managers from the infectious disease department. Prior to the research, participants were informed about the study’s purpose, the questionnaire content, and the research procedure. Then, those who expressed willingness to participate completed the informed consent form and anonymously completed paper questionnaires in a quiet, private space. If any questions arose during the questionnaire completion process, the researchers provided one-on-one instruction. All participants in this study volunteered and had the right to refuse or withdraw at any time. Given that no material incentives were offered to participants, this study optimized the questionnaire design to improve the overall response experience. The completion time was kept to approximately 10 min. Additionally, we highlighted the research’s significance and social meaning to activate participants’ intrinsic motivation. The researchers collected and checked the questionnaires on the spot, requesting participants to supply any missing or unclear information. The final effective response rate reached 93.7%, which is considered acceptable for such studies.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software. First, descriptive statistics were used to describe demographic and disease-related information, perceived stress, self-regulatory fatigue, and self-management. Continuous variables were reported using the mean (M) and standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies (N) and percentages (%). Second, normality tests were conducted for continuous variables. Independent-sample t-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare differences in variables across demographic and disease-related characteristics. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships between the studied variables. Finally, the mediation effect was tested using model 4 from the Process 4.1 plugin. The bootstrap method was used to refine the mediation effect with 5000 bootstrap resamples. If the 95% confidence interval did not contain 0, the mediation effect was considered significant. The significance level was set at alpha = 0.05.

Due to the use of a subjective questionnaire survey method to collect data, the common methods bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test in this study. The results showed that 11 factors had eigenvalues greater than 1, and the variance explained by the first factor was 16.07%, which did not exceed the 40% threshold. Therefore, no significant common method bias was present.

The ethical considerations

The researchers followed the Declaration of Helsinki, and ethics approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Nanjing, Affiliated to Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine(2024-LS-ky-093). Considering the sensitivity of the research topic, this study implemented rigorous confidentiality measures and data anonymization procedures to minimize social desirability bias. A secure, private research setting was selected for this study. Data were collected anonymously, with no disclosure of participants’ identities. Hard disk encryption was used to achieve secure data storage. Access to the data was restricted to authorized research personnel. In all public reports of the study findings, sensitive information that could potentially lead to participant identification was either omitted or anonymized.

Factors associated with the research variables (n = 328)

Participants of different ages and duration of HIV diagnosis showed significant differences in perceived stress, self-regulatory fatigue, and self-management behaviors. Further post hoc multiple comparisons revealed that, in terms of age, compared to those in the “25–35 years old” and “36–49 years old” groups, participants in the “aged 50 and above” group showed significantly higher levels of perceived stress and self-regulatory fatigue, and lower levels of self-management. In terms of the duration of HIV diagnosis, compared with the groups diagnosed for “more than 1 year” and “more than 1 year but less than 3 years”, the group diagnosed for “more than 10 years” demonstrated significantly lower perceived stress and self-regulatory fatigue, as well as higher levels of self-management.

Mediating effect analysis

A mediation model was constructed with perceived stress as the independent variable, self-regulatory fatigue as the mediating variable, and self-management behavior as the dependent variable (see Fig. 1). The variables were standardized. Age, household per capita monthly income, complication, and duration of HIV diagnosis were included as control variables. Mediation effects were analyzed using Model 4 in Process 4.1. Model 1 showed that perceived stress negatively predicted self-management behavior (β=−0.471); Model 2 showed that perceived stress positively predicted self-regulatory fatigue (β = 0.493); Model 3 showed that, after including self-regulatory fatigue, perceived stress still negatively predicted self-management behavior (β=−0.309), and self-regulatory fatigue had a negative predictive effect on self-management behavior (β=−0.328), as shown in Table 4. The Bootstrap method was used to test the mediating effect using 5000 simulated samples. The results showed that self-regulatory fatigue mediated the relationship between perceived stress and self-management behavior, accounting for 34.39% of the total effect (see Table 5). This data supports the findings that, when confronted with various psychosocial stressors, the ability of self-regulation among MSM living with HIV plays a crucial role in alleviating internal and external pressure, thereby enhancing self-management behaviors. Additionally, reducing self-regulatory fatigue facilitates more effective stress coping, thereby improving self-management outcomes.

Discussion

Key findings

This study proposed a hypothesized model to examine the impact of perceived stress on self-management behaviors and the mediating role of self-regulatory fatigue in this relationship. The key findings contribute to a better understanding of self-management behaviors among MSM living with HIV. Specifically, perceived stress and self-regulatory fatigue, as critical psychosocial factors, could negatively predict self-management behaviors. Perceived stress is positively associated with self-regulatory fatigue. Furthermore, mediation analysis revealed that self-regulatory fatigue mediates the relationship between perceived stress and self-management behaviors.

Comparisons with existing literature

In this study, perceived stress, self-regulatory fatigue, and self-management behavior in MSM living with HIV were influenced by age and duration of HIV diagnosis. MSM living with HIV aged 50 and above had higher levels of perceived stress and self-regulatory fatigue compared to young and middle-aged patients, possibly due to the dual impact of aging and HIV infection. Cognitive control and emotional processing abilities may be impaired [33], which could weaken the ability to cope with stressors. Additionally, influenced by multiple pressures, including symptom distress, HIV status disclosure concerns, and social discrimination, newly diagnosed MSM living with HIV experienced significant negative emotions [34], which could cause self-regulatory fatigue. Fortunately, with prolonged treatment duration, patients gain greater disease knowledge and confidence and achieve better psychological adaptation, which helps enhance self-management behaviors [14].

Self-management refers to the daily care individuals implement to manage their own disease and promote health. Our findings indicated that the self-management behavior of MSM living with HIV is at a moderate level, which is consistent with the findings from other studies [13, 35]. The probable explanation is that, in the past few decades, governments, society, and individuals have invested in the work of disease control and prevention; patients themselves have acquired HIV-related knowledge, improved awareness of self-protection and self-management, actively engaged in ART treatment and regular follow-up visits, and thereby enhanced their levels of self-management behaviors. However, the overall level of self-management is lower than in other countries [31, 36], suggesting that improving self-management behavior cannot be divorced from continuous efforts and that multidimensional interventions remain to be developed.

Perceived stress was negatively associated with self-management behaviors, suggesting that higher perceived stress among chronic disease patients is associated with lower self-management behaviors, which aligns with previous research findings [20]. The possible reason may be that most of the study population were young and middle-aged patients. They are in the peak period of education, career advancement, and marriage, and shoulder various social responsibilities [9]. In addition, the minority sexual identity and HIV-related stigma further increase psychological pressure for MSM living with HIV [5, 35]. These prolonged and complex stressors could cause behaviour changes, diminish treatment motivation, and further hinder the development of positive healthcare awareness and behaviors.

Perceived stress was positively correlated with self-regulatory fatigue, consistent with the findings of Song et al. [27], who demonstrated that perceived stress was associated with psychological resource depletion among police officers. Perceived stress could trigger psychological responses, such as symptoms of tension, anxiety, and depression [37]. According to the ego depletion theory, individuals need psychological resources to cope with stress and regulate negative emotions [38]. However, psychological resources are limited [39]. When these psychological resources are continuously depleted, an individual’s self-regulatory capacity may decline, potentially leading to a state of “fatigue”.

Self-regulatory fatigue was also negatively related to self-management behavior. A qualitative study revealed that haemodialysis patients with self-regulatory fatigue have difficulties in emotional regulation and tend to adopt passive and avoidance coping strategies, making it difficult to adhere to long-term self-management [26]. As indicated by this study, MSM living with HIV experienced relatively high levels of self-regulatory fatigue. Specifically, in a predominantly heterosexual socio-cultural environment, the dual stigma associated with HIV infection and sexual minority status always implicates moral and sexual issues [40]. Moreover, influenced by traditional cultural norms, Chinese men are often subjected to social expectations—such as serving as the “primary pillar of the family” and adhering to the stoic ideal that “boys don’t cry“ [41]. These ingrained stereotypes compel them to suppress and internalize psychological stress, thereby exacerbating psychological resource depletion. Due to the loss of psychological resources, patients could exhibit fatigue in managing their condition, find it hard to adhere to medication, and exhibit adverse self-management behaviors [25].

Mechanisms explaining the mediation

The mediation analysis indicated that perceived stress could negatively predict self-management behavior, and self-regulatory fatigue partially mediates the negative impact of perceived stress on self-management behaviors among MSM living with HIV, supporting the hypothesis of this study. The above correlations help clarify the mechanisms underlying perceived stress, self-regulatory fatigue, and self-management behavior. Based on the stress-coping theory, individuals adopt different coping strategies in response to perceived stress, which subsequently affect health-related behaviors [42]. Self-regulatory fatigue, as a psychological stress response, may influence stress perception and coping strategies. Specifically, individuals who perceived increased stress could consume more psychological resources to alleviate pressure [27]. According to the ego depletion theory, continuous depletion of psychological resources could weaken patients’ motivation for self-regulation and trigger self-regulatory fatigue [38]. Then, individuals with elevated self-regulatory fatigue may fail to address negative emotions and tend to adopt passive and avoidance strategies in disease coping [43]. Consequently, self-regulatory fatigue may help explain the negative effects on perceived stress and self-management behaviors.

The mediation effect size of 34.39% observed in this study is similar to findings from other populations with chronic diseases, suggesting that the mediating role of self-regulatory fatigue may be applicable in similar contexts [44, 45]. Simultaneously, current results were higher than those reported in diabetic patients, with the proportion of the mediation effect ranging from 23.21% to 31.83% [25, 44]. This could be attributed to the special nature of the disease and sexual orientation. As a chronic infectious disease, the social effects of panic and moral crisis surrounding HIV may persist in many areas. In addition to stress related to chronic disease, some MSM living with HIV may encounter social stigma and tend to conceal their HIV status and sexual orientation in certain social contexts [46], easily giving rise to the accumulation of stress and negative emotions. Thus, they continuously deplete their psychological resources and become more vulnerable to self-regulatory fatigue.

Clinical implications

The mediating mechanism in this study elucidated the relationships among perceived stress, self-regulatory fatigue, and self-management behaviors, offering novel insights for the development and implementation of clinical interventions to improve self-management behaviors. The findings indicated that effective clinical practice requires collaborative efforts among patients, healthcare professionals, government and social organizations.

Firstly, as the primary agent of self-management, patients should proactively address stress by activating the social support system [47]. Patients can mobilize available coping resources, such as emotional support from family and friends, peer education, and professional care from medical workers, to ease concerns about the disease. Also, they may attempt to open their hearts and share their inner thoughts through personalized mental counseling and self-disclosure intervention programs, which have been proven to alleviate psychological burden and provide external support [48].

Secondly, detailed care and humanistic support provided by healthcare providers could effectively reduce perceived stress and enhance self-management efficacy [49]. Healthcare providers should employ effective psychological care strategies, including mindfulness-based stress reduction [50] and cognitive-behavioral therapy. Relaxation training, problem-solving therapy, and acceptance and commitment therapy, as components of cognitive-behavioral intervention, can help patients accept reality, change negative mental state, and enhance adaptive behaviors and treatment adherence [51].

Thirdly, social media has been extensively used to deliver HIV interventions. Weibo and Blued online platforms enable MSM living with HIV to communicate HIV-related information and support each other [52]. Hence, government and society can utilize such platforms to disseminate supportive messages and health education content, and to reduce stigma and disease panic among the public, thereby alleviating patients’ psychological pressure. Besides, people who have similar experiences are more likely to establish effective connections, and HIV peer volunteers have skills to offer genuine empathy and sincere support, and improve HIV care retention [53]. Therefore, social organizations should focus on the advantage of peer support, and effective peer support programs should be implemented to fully mobilize active self-management behaviors of MSM living with HIV.

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first to focus on self-regulatory fatigue among MSM living with HIV in China and provides innovative confirmation of the mediating role of self-regulatory fatigue. Current mediation analyses also demonstrate how self-regulatory fatigue influences perceived stress and self-management behaviors, offering a new perspective that contributes to the development of scientific interventions to enhance self-management behaviors.

There are several limitations in this study. First, the sample in this study was recruited from a single tertiary hospital in Jiangsu Province, a region with higher economic development than other regions in China. This single sample source may limit the representativeness of the findings due to differences in social background. More samples from different regions and hospitals should be included in the future. Secondly, convenience sampling was chosen because it could efficiently obtain data from hard-to-reach populations under resource constraints. However, this non-probability sampling method has selection bias and limits the generalisability of the conclusion. Future studies should conduct multi-center, large-sample research and prioritize probability sampling, such as stratified random sampling, to reduce bias. Thirdly, when using self-report questionnaires for data collection, validity may be influenced by potential social desirability bias and recall bias. Under the premise of ensuring privacy and security, future research could adopt a longitudinal design with repeated measurements at multiple time points and incorporate qualitative interviews to better understand the underlying reasons behind the quantitative findings and to identify potential biases. Finally, the cross-sectional design cannot reflect causal relationships between variables. Future longitudinal studies are necessary to explore the dynamics of these relationships, and targeted interventions should be used in clinical practice to evaluate the practical effect.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that perceived stress and self-regulatory fatigue are risk factors influencing self-management behaviors among MSM living with HIV in China. Empirical data support the hypothesized model, indicating a negative association between perceived stress and self-management behaviors in this population. Moreover, self-regulatory fatigue partially mediates the relationship between perceived stress and self-management behaviors. These findings provide a foundation for designing and implementing effective interventions and highlight the importance of effective joint interventions that target both perceived stress and self-regulatory fatigue, thereby improving self-management behaviors among MSM living with HIV.
