Necrotic Features in Post‐Radiation Nasopharyngeal Biopsies Associated With Carotid Blowout
Authors: Shan‐Chi Yu, Chun‐Nan Chen, Mei‐Chun Lin, Tseng‐Cheng Chen, Cheng‐Ping Wang, Tsung‐Lin Yang, Pei‐Jen Lou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesPost‐radiation nasopharyngeal biopsies in patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) are primarily performed to exclude recurrence, yet their utility in assessing radiotoxicity remains underexplored. This study characterizes two distinct histological patterns of necrosis: coagulative stromal necrosis (Type A) and microscopic osteoradionecrosis (ORN; Type B). We aim to analyze the association of these patterns with radiotherapy (RT)‐related complications.MethodsData from a cohort of 483 patients were reviewed. Study groups included Type A (n = 30), Type B (n = 12), and clinically diagnosed skull base osteoradionecrosis (cORN, n = 25). Controls were categorized by histological findings: ulcer (n = 24), fibrosis (n = 43), and viable lymphoid tissue (n = 21). A comparison cohort of patients without posttreatment biopsy (n = 63) was also included.ResultsCarotid blowout syndrome (CBS) occurred exclusively in the study groups: Type A (13.3%), Type B (8.3%), and cORN (12.0%). No CBS events were observed in the histological control groups or the comparison cohort. Notably, Type A biopsies were temporally clustered closely with CBS events, specifically occurring from 3 months before to 1 month after the episode.ConclusionType A and Type B histological patterns are associated with an increased risk of carotid blowout, comparable to cORN. Type A may represent a “two‐hit” necrotic transition from a vulnerable fibrotic substrate. Beyond diagnosing recurrence, post‐RT biopsies may offer valuable insights into the status of deep tissues. Identifying these patterns can aid in risk stratification and potentially facilitate early intervention to mitigate severe complications.Level of Evidence4.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is particularly prevalent in East and Southeast Asia [1, 2, 3]. While concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) is the standard of care for locoregionally advanced disease [4], the high‐dose radiotherapy (RT) component frequently results in late toxicities, including osteoradionecrosis (ORN) [4, 5]. Long‐term surveillance is critical, as both disease recurrence and severe treatment‐related complications significantly compromise survival [6, 7, 8]. However, while nasopharyngeal biopsies are performed primarily to exclude recurrence, their potential to assess these life‐threatening radiotoxicities remains underexplored.

A critical late complication is carotid blowout syndrome (CBS), a rupture of the internal carotid artery associated with high mortality [9, 10, 11]. The primary risk factor is skull base ORN, which exposes the artery encased within the bone [12, 13, 14, 15]. Closely related is post‐radiation nasopharyngeal necrosis, a condition involving soft tissue necrosis that frequently overlaps with ORN and similarly predisposes patients to arterial rupture [16, 17, 18].

Despite their clinical importance, the histopathological features of these conditions are poorly characterized. Descriptions of post‐radiation nasopharyngeal necrosis remain vague, often failing to distinguish high‐risk necrosis from common benign ulcers [16, 17, 19, 20, 21]. Furthermore, detailed histological documentation of skull base ORN (or radiation‐associated osteomyelitis) remains scarce. Existing reports are largely limited to nonspecific inflammatory findings, lacking a comprehensive characterization of the necrotic tissue patterns [22, 23].

This study defines two specific histological patterns in benign post‐radiation nasopharyngeal biopsies. We hypothesize that these patterns correspond to high‐risk tissue states. By analyzing their association with carotid blowout, we aim to extend the utility of biopsy beyond cancer detection to the identification of life‐threatening radiotoxicity.

Materials and Methods

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (REC No. 202408156RINB) with a waiver of informed consent. We searched the pathology database for nasopharyngeal biopsies collected between 2005 and 2023. Biopsies confirming malignancy—specifically recurrent NPC and radiation‐induced sarcomas [24, 25]—were excluded. Consequently, only benign specimens from patients with a history of irradiated NPC were included for histological review (by S‐C. Y.; Figure 1). Specimens were categorized into three study groups using a hierarchical classification: Type A (defined by characteristic coagulative necrosis; excluding post‐photodynamic therapy [PDT] cases), Type B (presence of nonvital bone/skeletal muscle in the absence of Type A morphology; excluding post‐nasopharyngectomy cases), and clinically diagnosed skull base ORN (cORN, defined as patients with clinical ORN features but lacking Type A or Type B histology). Detailed morphological descriptions of Type A and Type B lesions are presented in the (Section 3). While these histological features are routinely encountered in daily practice, the simplified nomenclature of Type A and Type B patterns was retrospectively defined during this study review to facilitate systematic analysis.

Study flowchart and group definitions. Patients were stratified into a Biopsy Cohort (post‐radiation nasopharyngeal biopsies, 2005–2023) and a Comparison Cohort (no rebiopsy, treated in 2015). The Biopsy Cohort was further categorized into necrotic groups (Type A, Type B), clinically diagnosed skull base ORN (cORN; defined by clinical features despite negative Type A/B histology), and non‐ORN histological controls (N‐Ulcer, N‐Fibrosis, N‐Lymphoid). Exclusion criteria and specific histological definitions are detailed in the corresponding boxes.

Histological controls included N‐Ulcer (microscopic lesion > 5 mm), N‐Fibrosis, and N‐Lymphoid. To ensure statistical power and balanced cohorts, N‐Fibrosis cases were strategically sampled from 2009 to 2015. A “No Rebiopsy” comparison cohort (consecutive patients treated in 2015 without posttreatment biopsy) served as a clinical baseline.

Clinical data, including demographics, tumor stage, treatment, and complications, were analyzed. Given the long study period, detailed records of specific radiation modalities were incomplete for earlier cohorts. Thus, the year of RT initiation served as a surrogate to reflect the evolution of treatment paradigms. “Biopsy time” was defined as the interval from RT initiation to biopsy. Statistical analyses utilized Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables. Because multiple clinical characteristics and complications were compared across groups without formal post hoc corrections for multiple comparisons, these statistical analyses should be interpreted as exploratory in nature. Proportions and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the Clopper–Pearson exact method.

Results

Histological Patterns of Type A and Type B Necrosis

Among 635 post‐radiation biopsies from 483 patients, Type A and Type B patterns were identified in 30 (6%) and 12 (2%) patients, respectively.

Type A is characterized by coagulative stromal necrosis with abundant bacteria and occasional yeasts, yet minimal inflammatory cell infiltration (Figure 2). Masson trichrome staining confirmed the necrotic tissue was primarily collagenous stroma. Warthin–Starry and Periodic acid‐Schiff stains highlighted the heavy burden of bacteria and fungal elements, respectively. Notably, this absence of inflammatory reaction was not attributable to systemic neutropenia, as patients maintained normal neutrophil counts (range: 3485–12,716/μL).

Type A necrosis. (A, C) Hematoxylin and eosin stain showing coagulative necrosis with minimal inflammation. (B, D) Masson trichrome stain confirming the collagenous nature of the necrotic stroma. (E) Warthin–Starry stain highlighting abundant bacteria. (F) Periodic acid‐Schiff stain demonstrating yeast‐like fungal structures.

Type B is defined by the presence of nonvital bone (n = 8) or necrotic skeletal muscle (n = 4), as shown in Figure 3. Associated findings were nonspecific, including fibrinoid necrosis, purulent exudate, blood clots, fungal colonization, and keratinous debris. While resembling superficial ulceration, the identification of deep tissue elements serves as the defining feature.

Type B necrosis (Hematoxylin and eosin stain). (A, B) Nonvital bone trabeculae with empty lacunae. (C, D) Nonvital skeletal muscle fibers showing loss of nuclei and fragmentation; panel D additionally demonstrates inflammatory exudate with fungal agents (black arrows).

Prior transarterial embolization (TAE), a known cause of ischemic necrosis, was rare, occurring in 2 of the 30 Type A patients (6.7%) and 1 of the 12 Type B patients (8.3%), suggesting that these morphologies typically develop independently of TAE. Serial biopsy data for these patients are detailed in Table S1.

Comparison With cORN

From the overall study population of 483 patients, we identified 25 (5%) with cORN for comparison. Baseline demographics, tumor stage, and treatment characteristics (including primary modality and year of initiation) were generally comparable across the three groups. While the cORN group presented with a lower mean age at diagnosis and a longer follow‐up duration, reirradiation rates appeared higher in the Type B and cORN groups (Table 1), these differences were not statistically significant.

Regarding biopsy timing (Figure S1), although the overall median interval did not differ significantly among groups (p = 0.090), Type A exhibited a distinct early‐onset pattern. Specifically, 30% of Type A cases occurred within the first 2 years post‐RT, a proportion significantly higher than that observed in the Type B and cORN groups (Table 1; p = 0.032).

The risk of CBS was comparably high across all groups: 13.3% (4/30; 95% CI: 3.8%–30.7%) in Type A, 8.3% (1/12; 95% CI: 0.2%–38.5%) in Type B, and 12.0% (3/25; 95% CI: 2.5%–31.2%) in cORN (Figure 5). Because our algorithm (Figure 1) prioritized morphology over clinical diagnosis, the Type A cohort naturally included some established ORN cases. However, even after excluding these, the CBS risk in the remaining Type A lesions remained substantial (10.5%, 2/19; 95% CI: 1.3%–33.1%), confirming the independent prognostic value of this specific morphology.

Regarding imaging, radiological ORN was present in 50.0% of Type A and 72.7% of Type B patients (Table S2). Notably, radiological bony defects were identified in 36.4% of Type B cases, compared to only 5.0% in Type A and 13.6% in cORN, a finding consistent with the histological presence of nonvital bone characteristic of Type B lesions. However, radiological findings did not always correlate with histology; specifically, four cases of histologically confirmed nonvital bone lacked definitive radiological bony defects, suggesting that microscopic necrosis may precede gross radiological changes.

When correlating imaging with clinical complications in the Type A group, two of the four CBS events occurred in patients who were negative for radiological ORN (Table 2). This observation indicates that radiological criteria alone may not fully reflect the clinical risk associated with Type A morphology.

Comparison With Non‐ORN Cases

We compared the study groups with three histological control groups: N‐Ulcer (n = 24), N‐Fibrosis (n = 43), and N‐Lymphoid (n = 21). Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, and NPC stage, were generally comparable across groups (Table 1). However, treatment profiles differed significantly. The N‐Lymphoid group consisted predominantly of patients treated in the modern era (2000–Present) and consequently had the highest rate of primary CCRT (95%). In comparison, the study groups (Type A, Type B, and cORN) and the N‐Ulcer group had lower rates of CCRT (50%–75%; p < 0.001) and included a higher proportion of patients treated in earlier eras (p = 0.009). Additionally, the N‐Lymphoid group had the lowest rate of re‐irradiation (0%), a difference that trended toward statistical significance (p = 0.054). These distinct profiles suggest that histological manifestations may be associated with the evolution of treatment strategies.

Endoscopically, N‐Ulcer resembled cORN but lacked bone exposure (Figure 4D), whereas N‐Fibrosis and N‐Lymphoid groups typically presented with intact mucosa without gross necrosis (Figure 4E,F).

Biopsies in these control groups were performed significantly earlier than in the study groups (Type A/B/cORN; Figure S1; p < 0.001). Notably, the N‐Fibrosis group exhibited a prominent early‐onset pattern, with 40% of cases occurring within the first 2 years post‐RT (Table 1; Figure S1), mirroring the early distribution observed in Type A.

Crucially, the risk of CBS was exclusively associated with the study groups. No events occurred in any non‐ORN control groups—including N‐Ulcer (0/24, 0%; 95% CI: 0.0%–14.2%), N‐Fibrosis (0/43, 0%; 95% CI: 0.0%–8.2%), and N‐Lymphoid (0/21, 0%; 95% CI: 0.0%–16.1%)—standing in sharp contrast to the high rates of 13.3% in Type A, 8.3% in Type B, and 12.0% in cORN (p = 0.001; Figure 5). This identifies Type A and Type B morphologies as specific indicators of vascular risk.

Beyond vascular events, other complications showed distinct patterns (Figure 5): cervical spine ORN [26, 27, 28] was exclusive to Type A and Type B, while carotid stenosis was most prevalent in N‐Fibrosis. The N‐Lymphoid group exhibited the lowest overall complication rate, likely reflecting preserved mucosal integrity.

Comparison With Patients Without Posttreatment Biopsy

To address potential selection bias inherent in the biopsied groups, we also analyzed a control cohort of 63 NPC patients who underwent RT but did not require posttreatment nasopharyngeal biopsy (designated as the “No Rebiopsy Group” in tables and figures). Baseline demographics and tumor characteristics were generally comparable to the study groups (Table 1), with a median follow‐up of 108.6 months. However, unlike the biopsied groups that spanned a wide treatment era, all patients without posttreatment biopsy were treated in the modern era (2010–Present; Table 1). During this period, imaging findings suggestive of skull base ORN were identified in only one case (1.7%; Table S2), and crucially, no instances of carotid blowout were reported (0/63, 0%; 95% CI: 0.0%–5.7%; Figure 5).

These findings highlight that carotid blowout is a rare complication in our institution, consistent with the existing literature reports [29, 30]. This underscores the notably high incidence of carotid blowout observed in Type A and Type B cases.

Temporal Relationship With Carotid Blowout

Details of patients with carotid blowout are summarized in Table 2. In the Type A group, four patients underwent biopsy in close proximity to the blowout event: one patient (Case A28) underwent biopsy 3 months prior to the rupture, while three patients (Cases A18, A30, and A29) underwent biopsy within 1 month after the event. This distinct pattern of temporal clustering was unique to the Type A group; in contrast, CBS events in Type B and cORN cases occurred remotely from the biopsy time points, with intervals ranging from 2 to 6 years, with the exception of one cORN case (Case C15) that occurred 9 days after the biopsy.

Notably, Case A18 demonstrated persistent Type A morphology: in addition to the index biopsy performed 8 days after the rupture (Table 2), two subsequent biopsies at 32 and 108 days post‐event consistently revealed Type A features (Table S1). Importantly, these Type A‐related CBS events were not limited to older treatment eras, with RT initiation years ranging from 1996 to as recently as 2022 and 2023.

Differential Diagnosis of Type A and Type B Necrosis

Given the significant risk of carotid blowout associated with Type A and Type B necrosis, accurate histological identification is paramount. To assist pathologists in accurate identification, we highlight several key differential diagnoses for these necrotic patterns.

For Type A necrosis, a particularly challenging mimic is histological change following PDT. In our review of post‐radiation cases, we identified two patients who had undergone PDT 2 and 6 months prior to biopsy, respectively; both biopsies exhibited necrotic stromal morphology identical to Type A (Figure 6A,B). Notably, neither patient experienced carotid blowout.

(C, D) Invasive fungal infection characterized by infarction‐like necrosis with preserved “ghost” outlines of vessels (C) and glands (D). Prominent hyaline septated fungal hyphae are identified in panel C. (E, F) Extranodal NK/T‐cell lymphoma showing extensive coagulative necrosis with faint “ghost” outlines of round tumor cells, distinct from the fibrous stroma seen in panels A and B.

Given that PDT is a rare modality primarily restricted to clinical trials [31, 32], the identification of two such cases suggests that PDT may directly contribute to Type A morphology. We hypothesize that this results from PDT‐mediated vascular shutdown [33], leading to ischemia in the fibrotic post‐radiation tissue. However, as PDT has a relatively shallow depth of penetration, the deeper peri‐carotid tissue may not necessarily undergo similar morphological changes.

Invasive fungal infection is another critical differential diagnosis. Although clinical presentations are typically distinct [34], careful differentiation is required, particularly in immunocompromised patients—such as those developing therapy‐related myeloid neoplasms with associated neutropenia. Histologically, invasive fungal infections manifest as infarction‐like necrosis with preserved “ghost” outlines of glands and vessels (Figure 6C,D). This contrasts with Type A, which consists of acellular, necrotic stromal tissue. Furthermore, the pathogens involved are primarily molds (e.g., Aspergillus spp.) [34], which are rarely observed in the mixed bacterial flora of Type A necrosis.

For Type B necrosis, the primary differential consideration is post‐surgical change resulting from recent nasopharyngectomy [35, 36, 37]. As both conditions are characterized by nonvital bone or skeletal muscle, distinguishing radiation‐induced Type B necrosis from iatrogenic surgical sequelae relies on correlation with clinical history.

Finally, extranodal NK/T‐cell lymphoma is a well‐known cause of necrosis [38], sharing geographic endemicity with NPC [39]. However, the incidence of post‐radiation nasopharyngeal necrosis significantly exceeds that of NK/T‐cell lymphoma. In our 19‐year archive (2005–2023), we identified only two instances where initial biopsies of NK/T‐cell lymphoma showed extensive necrosis without viable tumor cells, confirming this as a rare diagnostic pitfall. Crucially, the morphology in these cases was coagulative necrosis characterized by “ghost” outlines of round tumor cells (Figure 6E,F), which are distinct from the acellular, collagenous necrosis of Type A.

Discussion

Traditionally, the primary role of post‐radiation nasopharyngeal biopsies is to rule out residual or recurrent malignancy [40, 41]. While radiation‐induced atypia is well‐documented as a potential mimic of malignancy [41], the clinical significance of non‐neoplastic histological patterns has been largely overlooked. In this study, we identified two distinct histological patterns—Type A (necrotic stromal tissue) and Type B (microscopic ORN)—that are strongly associated with severe complications. Our findings suggest that these histological markers are not merely incidental, but serve as critical indicators of underlying tissue vulnerability, extending the utility of biopsy beyond simple cancer detection.

The unique morphology of Type A necrosis—acellular, collagenous ghosts—suggests a “two‐hit” pathogenic mechanism. We propose that initial radiation injury induces extensive fibrosis (first hit), creating a collagen‐rich matrix [42, 43] that subsequently undergoes coagulative necrosis due to progressive ischemia from radiation vasculopathy [44] (second hit). This hypothesis is consistent with the temporal synchronization observed in our data, where the early onset of Type A lesions (30% within 2 years) parallels the peak distribution of the N‐Fibrosis group (40% within 2 years). Clinically, this suggests that radiation‐induced fibrosis may not be a stable scar but a vulnerable precursor state. Consequently, patients with biopsy‐proven fibrosis may benefit from closer monitoring, as the subsequent development of mucosal breakdown could potentially reflect the transition to Type A necrosis and a heightened risk of carotid blowout.

Furthermore, the paucity of inflammatory cells within Type A tissue implies a localized immune failure. The avascular, necrotic stroma likely acts as a protected sanctuary for microorganisms, where poor perfusion prevents both immune cell infiltration and antibiotic delivery. This provides a histopathological rationale for why conservative management often fails and supports the clinical consensus that aggressive endoscopic debridement is essential for source control [45, 46, 47]. Regarding Type B necrosis, the presence of nonvital bone fragments indicates microscopic ORN, which may not yet be detectable via imaging or endoscopy but carries similar clinical risks.

The most clinically significant finding is the strong correlation between these histological patterns and CBS. While our control groups had a 0% CBS rate—consistent with the low baseline incidence (< 1%) reported in contemporary cohorts [29]—the Type A and Type B groups showed rates (13.3% and 8.3%, respectively) comparable to those seen in frank skull base ORN (5%–28%) [16, 17, 46, 48, 49]. Traditionally, skull base ORN is considered the primary risk factor for CBS [12, 13, 14]. Our study adds a critical histological dimension, identifying Type A and Type B necrosis as the first microscopic features directly linked to this catastrophe.

It is well established that the risk of CBS is strongly influenced by treatment‐related factors [12, 13, 50]. While advancements in RT techniques have reduced overall complication rates [29], our data show that 33% of Type A and 17% of Type B lesions developed after 2010. This indicates that these histological patterns are not exclusively a consequence of older treatment techniques, and their continued occurrence necessitates ongoing clinical vigilance. However, detailed treatment records were incomplete or unavailable in this retrospective study. These histologic patterns are not intended to substitute for RT dose‐risk information, but rather likely reflect the cumulative biological impact of these unmeasured treatment‐related factors.

Crucially, the distinction between Type A and Type B is most evident in their temporal relationship with CBS. While Type B represents a chronic structural vulnerability (microscopic ORN), Type A lesions were tightly clustered around the blowout event (ranging from 3 months prior to 1 month post‐event). This synchronization suggests that Type A necrosis is not merely a bystander but represents the “tip of the iceberg,” reflecting the state of the deeper peri‐carotid tissues. We propose that this superficial ischemic necrosis (Type A) may serve as a clinical surrogate reflecting severe regional ischemia that compromises the blood supply to the adjacent arterial wall via the vasa vasorum. Therefore, the detection of Type A necrosis highlights the presence of underlying fragility, suggesting that close clinical and vascular follow‐up may be beneficial.

Consequently, our results support the concept of using nasopharyngeal biopsies for the indirect assessment of deep tissue integrity. Just as N‐Lymphoid tissue implies a healthy, well‐vascularized mucosal bed, Type A necrosis signals severe ischemic compromise. We propose that nasopharyngeal histology offers critical insights into deep tissue viability. Rather than indiscriminate sampling, we advocate for a lower threshold for biopsy in patients with suspicious endoscopic findings, such as persistent crusting or minor mucosal breakdown. Ultimately, recognition of Type A or Type B morphology may justify heightened clinical awareness and consideration of vascular imaging in appropriate clinical contexts [9, 11, 51].

Several limitations of our study warrant acknowledgment. First, the study is limited by its retrospective nature and the relatively small number of CBS events. Second, biopsy‐selection bias cannot be fully excluded, as posttreatment biopsies were clinically indicated (e.g., triggered by suspicious endoscopic findings or symptoms) rather than performed randomly. While we introduced a comparison cohort to address this, this analysis is constrained by treatment‐era imbalance, as the biopsy cohort spans a wider period (2005–2023) than the comparison cohort. This imbalance introduces potential confounding factors, including evolving radiation techniques, surveillance protocols, and follow‐up durations.

Furthermore, because the histological slides were reviewed by a single pathologist, interobserver reproducibility was not assessed, which represents a limitation of this study. Finally, while we hypothesize that nasopharyngeal histology reflects peri‐carotid tissue status, direct histological confirmation of the arterial wall is practically challenging. Future prospective studies with standardized protocol biopsies are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusion

This retrospective clinicopathologic analysis delineates two post‐radiation nasopharyngeal biopsy patterns—Type A stromal necrosis and Type B microscopic ORN—that appear to be associated with CBS in patients with previously irradiated NPC. Notably, Type A necrosis demonstrated temporal proximity to carotid blowout in several cases, which may be indicative of an ischemic high‐risk tissue state, although causality cannot be established in this context. While these observations may warrant increased clinical vigilance when such histopathologic features are identified, it should be emphasized that prospective validation—including standardized biopsy criteria, RT dosing parameters, imaging correlation, and larger event cohorts—is necessary before these patterns can be considered reliable predictors or incorporated into clinical risk stratification or management algorithms.
