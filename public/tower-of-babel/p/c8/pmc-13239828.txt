A Rare Vascular Ring Malformation Formed by Right Aortic Arch With Mirror Image Branching Causing Dysphagia: Case Report
Authors: Md. Deluwar Hussen, Lamia Rashid, Maliha Zahir, Nurjahan Dilshad, Md. Atique Faisal Tanim, Md Mahfuzur Rahman Zeehad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA right‐sided aortic arch, a congenital anomaly of the aortic arch, was initially described by Fioratti and Aglietti in 1963. This condition, associated with formation of a vascular ring, can constrict mediastinal organs such as the trachea and esophagus. We present a case of a 42 year old male with the history of intermittent dysphagia and mild retrosternal discomfort for 6 months. The contrast‐enhanced CT scan revealed a mirror‐image branching pattern of the aortic arch on the right side. A vascular ring was formed and a 1.20 cm Kommerell diverticulum was found behind the esophagus. The diverticulum was small and, therefore, no surgery was carried out. The patient was managed in a conservative manner and at 3 months of follow‐up, he reported an improvement in the symptoms. In this case, the diagnostic approach has played a crucial role in identifying the cause of the difficulty in swallowing in this patient. The initial examination, a mere esophagram, gives a hint of early diagnosis; that is, a smooth depression on the posterior wall of the esophagus and a significant constriction of the lumen caliber. CT scan and endoscopy confirm the presence of a pulsating external compression and the absence of primary esophageal disease. Since this patient does not have severe and progressive symptoms, there is minimal or no risk of aneurysm or dissection caused by Kommerell diverticulum. This case highlights the value of clinical suspicion of rare congenital diseases and comprehensive imaging and conservative management to achieve favorable outcomes in patients with dysphagia due to vascular ring malformation in resource‐limited countries.

Introduction

A right‐sided aortic arch is a rare congenital vascular defect that can cause difficulties in swallowing and breathing. The root cause of this defect is an aberrant origination of a right aortic arch that encircles and compresses the esophagus and trachea before merging with the left aortic arch [1]. Incidence of right aortic arch is 0.05%–0.1% in the normal population [2]. Edwards classifies right‐sided aortic arch anomalies with mirror‐image branching as Type I, aberrant left subclavian artery and Kommerell diverticulum as Type II, and isolated left subclavian artery as Type III [3]. These problems occur when the right fourth aortic arch does not regress in days 36–38 of embryonic growth, resulting in both right and left arches of the aorta persisting [4]. Here we are reporting an adult Bangladeshi man presenting with intermittent dysphagia and retrosternal discomfort initially induced by solid foods suggestive of right‐sided aortic arch. This report describes how to diagnose this defect in a resource‐limited setting by using clinical judgment and available imaging.

Case History/ Examination

A 42 year‐old man came to the clinic with a 6‐month history of intermittent dysphagia. He also reported mild retrosternal discomfort, exacerbated by solid foods. He has no associated weight loss, aspiration, respiratory symptoms, or any previous gastrointestinal disorder.

On physical examination, his body temperature was normal and blood pressure was 125/75 mmHg with a stable heart rate; there was no murmur or abnormal heart sound found on cardiac auscultation and an esophagram revealed a smooth posterior indentation of the proximal esophagus extending 3.20 cm with approximately 55% luminal narrowing.

Contrast‐enhanced CT of the neck and thorax revealed a right‐sided aortic arch with mirror‐image branching giving rise sequentially to the left brachiocephalic trunk, the right common carotid artery, and the right subclavian artery. (Figure 1A,B), (Figure 2A,B).

Upper endoscopy confirmed an extrinsic pulsatile impression without mucosal involvement. Final diagnosis is right‐sided aortic arch with mirror‐image branching and Kommerell diverticulum. Due to the small diverticular size, surgical intervention was not needed. The patient was managed conservatively with proton‐pump‐inhibitors and soft diet. The patient reported substantial improvement of symptoms at 3‐months follow‐up.

Discussion

A right‐sided aortic arch (RAA) is a rare congenital variation where the aorta arches to the right of the trachea and esophagus, instead of the normal left side occurring in approximately 0.1% of the population (range 0.05%–0.2%) [2]. This anatomical variation does not in itself result in dysphagia. In the presence of a ligamentum arteriosum and a Kommerell diverticulum, esophageal compression may take place [5]. This manifestation is known in the literature as dysphagia lusoria (a condition where the esophagus or trachea is compressed by an abnormally located right subclavian artery) [6]. In this case, the diagnostic approach has played a crucial role in identifying the cause of the difficulty in swallowing in the patient. The initial examination, a mere esophagram, gives a hint of early diagnosis, that is, a smooth depression on the back wall and a significant constriction of the lumen. These observations indicate extrinsic compression of the esophagus. A CT scan of the chest with contrast, following esophagram, has confirmed the vascular abnormality. It is found that the patient has a right‐sided aortic arch with mirror image branching and a Kommerell diverticulum which creates a vascular ring that surrounds the esophagus describing the symptoms in the patient. Endoscopic examination has provided further confirmation of the presence of a pulsating external compression and the absence of primary esophageal disease. Using active radiography, cross‐sectional imaging, and endoscopy has proved useful to diagnose the unexplained dysphagia [6].

Nevertheless, there is also a limitation with this approach that the patient will need to be adherent to recurrent CT scans to assess the Kommerell diverticulum and avoid late complications, including aortic rupture or dissection.

Long term follow‐up of the patient could not be conducted. As this is a rare case, there is limited evidence on symptom recurrence. Future research may further explore this area.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The right‐sided aortic arch (RAA) is a rare anomaly of the aorta. It may be asymptomatic, but must be considered carefully when it is associated with a vascular ring formation that results in dysphagia lusoria. The majority of patients having mild symptoms need not undergo active surgical interventions; rather, conservative treatment is enough, which is a cost‐efficient measure especially in resource‐deprived areas like Bangladesh. But conservative treatment is accompanied by close continuous monitoring to detect potentially life‐threatening complications such as diverticular rupture or dissection.

This case is also particularly significant to the nations that have limited resources like Bangladesh. It demonstrates that, in adults with chronic unexplained difficulty in swallowing, physicians must be highly suspicious of rare congenital anomalies. The report establishes that the conservative approach is safe and appropriate as routine management of mild and nonprogressive cases. Only in those with severe and progressive symptoms, poor nutrition, or a high risk of aneurysm or dissection caused by Kommerell diverticulum, surgery is most often resorted [7]. Since our patient experienced mild dysphagia, having a small 1.2 cm diverticulum (the minimal size of Kommerell diverticulum recommended for repair is 3 cm) [8], and symptom improvement was reported at a 3‐months follow‐up, the conservative management was deemed medically appropriate and cost‐effective.
