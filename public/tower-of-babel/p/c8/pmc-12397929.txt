Variation in Venous Thromboembolism Outcomes Based on Primary Cancer Site
Authors: Javier Trujillo-Santos, Cihan Ay, Behnood Bikdeli, David Jiménez, Aurora Villalobos, Luciano López-Jiménez, Montserrat Pérez-Pinar, Javier Pagán-Escribano, Maurizio Ciammaichella, Manuel Monreal, RIETE Investigators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with cancer-associated venous thromboembolism (VTE) are at increased risk of both recurrent VTE and bleeding. These risks may vary by cancer site, but the magnitude of this variation remains unclear.ObjectivesThe aim of the study was to compare 90-day risks of recurrent VTE or VTE-related death (composite outcome) and major bleeding across different cancer sites.MethodsWe analyzed 18,660 patients with active cancer and VTE and 88,162 without cancer in the RIETE (Registro Informatizado de la Enfermedad Tromboembólica) registry. Multivariable Cox models and Fine-Gray competing risk analyses were used. Sensitivity analyses included propensity score matching and subgroup stratification by cancer site.ResultsWithin 90 days, 4.0% of cancer-associated thrombosis patients experienced the composite outcome (fatal pulmonary embolism: 1.4%; VTE recurrence: 3.0%), and 3.4% had major bleeding. Compared to noncancer patients, cancer-associated thrombosis patients had higher risks of the composite outcome (adjusted HR [aHR]: 2.54; 95% CI: 2.21 to 2.93) and major bleeding (aHR: 1.40; 95% CI: 1.22-1.61). The highest thrombotic risk was found in lung (aHR: 4.03; 95% CI: 3.38-4.81) and pancreatic cancers (aHR: 3.85; 95% CI: 3.04-4.88), followed by gastrointestinal (aHR: 1.95; 95% CI: 1.53-2.48) and breast (aHR: 1.63; 95% CI: 1.27-2.09) cancers. Major bleeding risk was highest in gastrointestinal (aHR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.37-2.00), genitourinary (aHR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.38-2.00), and pancreatic cancers (aHR: 1.43; 95% CI: 1.05-1.95). Results were consistent in the propensity-matched cohort.ConclusionsVTE and bleeding risks in cancer-associated thrombosis vary substantially by cancer site. These findings support a personalized approach to anticoagulation in cancer patients with VTE.Key words: bleeding; cancer, anticoagulant therapy; mortality; recurrences; venous thromboembolism

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is a major complication and cause of mortality in cancer patients. The risk of VTE in cancer is 4 to 7 times higher than in the general population, due to factors such as hypercoagulability, surgery, prolonged immobility, and prothrombotic effects of treatments, including chemotherapy, hormonal therapy, anti-angiogenic agents, and immunotherapy.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Cancer patients also face an elevated risk of bleeding, especially during anticoagulant therapy.7,8

Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) has long been the preferred therapy, based on randomized trials showing its efficacy and acceptable bleeding risk profile.9, 10, 11 More recently, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have emerged as an alternative to LMWH, showing similar efficacy and an overall comparable bleeding risk, though bleeding outcomes may vary by cancer type.12, 13, 14

The site of cancer may significantly affect VTE-related outcomes, yet current guidelines recommend a largely uniform approach to anticoagulation.15, 16, 17, 18 Emerging data, however, suggest that risks of recurrence and bleeding vary by tumor location. For instance, patients with gastrointestinal (GI) or genitourinary (GU) cancers may have higher bleeding risk, while those with lung or pancreatic cancers are more prone to recurrent VTE.19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24

Using data from the RIETE (Registro Informatizado de la Enfermedad Tromboembólica) registry, this study evaluates 90-day risks of recurrent VTE, VTE-related death, and major bleeding, with a focus on the influence of primary cancer site. Our goal is to guide more individualized approaches to anticoagulation in cancer patients.

Methods

Study design and population

This observational study used data from the RIETE registry (NCT02832245),25 including patients with symptomatic, objectively proven PE (with or without concomitant DVT) or isolated DVT (lower or upper limbs) between March 2001 and June 2024. Active cancer was defined as a diagnosis within 6 months, presence of metastases, or ongoing treatment. Patients with prior cancer in remission >5 years were included in the noncancer group. We excluded patients participating in randomized trials and those with cancer types not among the 6 most prevalent: GI, GU, lung, pancreas, hematologic, and breast. Each category was analyzed separately, using noncancer patients as the reference group, to assess cancer site-specific influences on VTE outcomes.

Demographics, cancer characteristics, treatment, and metastasis status were obtained from the registry. Anticoagulation practices followed local protocols. All patients provided informed consent, and the study was conducted under local ethics committee approvals.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were: 1) the composite of recurrent VTE or VTE-related death (fatal PE); and 2) major bleeding during the first 90 days. Symptomatic recurrent VTE was confirmed using imaging. Fatal PE was defined as death within 10 days of a documented PE (either index or recurrent), in the absence of an alternative identifiable cause. Major bleeding was defined as any overt bleeding requiring transfusion of ≥2 U of blood, involving a critical site (retroperitoneal, spinal, pericardial, or intracranial), or resulting in death. This definition closely resembles that of the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis,26 although the RIETE definition preceded the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis definition.25 Fatal bleeding was defined as death within 10 days of a major bleeding episode, in the absence of an alternative cause of death.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics included mean ± SD, median (IQR), and proportions. Between-group comparisons of baseline variables used standardized differences, with absolute values >0.1 considered relevant.

To account for clustering by hospital, we used multilevel (hierarchical) models, with random effects models for center-level variance. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models estimated HRs for all-cause death, while Fine-Gray competing risk models estimated subdistribution HRs for the composite outcome and major bleeding, with nonevent deaths as competing risks. Follow-up lasted 90 days or until the first of recurrent VTE, major bleeding, or death. Multilevel modeling accounted for clustering at the hospital levels. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals and log-minus-log plots. No significant violations were identified.

Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and prior RIETE studies. Interaction terms between cancer site and anticoagulant type, and cancer site and anemia, were explored but were not significant. Covariates included age, sex, VTE presentation (PE vs isolated DVT), surgery, immobility, recent major bleeding, metastases, hemoglobin, leukocyte and platelet counts, creatinine clearance, cancer therapy, and anticoagulant type and dosing intensity.

Propensity score matching and sensitivity analyses

We conducted 1:1 nearest-neighbor propensity score matching with a 0.1 caliper to match cancer and noncancer cohorts. Postmatching balance was confirmed (all SMDs <0.1). Analyses were repeated in the matched cohort to confirm robustness (Supplemental Tables 3 and 4, Supplemental Figure 1).

Cumulative incidence functions

We estimated cumulative incidence functions (CIFs) for the composite outcome and major bleeding at days 30 and 90, accounting for competing risks. These are shown in Supplemental Table 5. All analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0, IBM Corp) and Stata software (version 15, StataCorp).

Ethical oversight

The RIETE registry has received ethical approval from the institutional review boards or ethics committees of all participating centers, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written or informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legally authorized representatives before enrollment. The study protocol complies with relevant guidelines and regulations for research involving human participants.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Between March 2001 and June 2024, 107,708 patients with acute VTE were enrolled in the RIETE registry. Of these, 19,546 (18%) had active cancer, and 88,162 formed the noncancer cohort. The most common cancer sites were GU (n = 5,270, 28.2%), GI (n = 4,485 patients, 24.0%), lung (n = 3,536, 18.9%), breast (n = 2,782, 14.9%), hematologic (1,474, 7.9%), and pancreas (n = 1,113, 6.0%). Patients with other cancer types (n = 2,215; 2.0%) were excluded from analysis.

Cancer patients were older (mean age: 68 ± 13 vs 64 ± 18 years) and more often presented with upper-limb DVT (9.1% vs 3.1%; standardized difference of means/proportions [STD]: 0.253) and less frequently with isolated lower-limb DVT (32% vs 39%; STD: 0.147) than noncancer patients. Anemia (62% vs 28%; STD: 0.727) and thrombocytopenia (5.4% vs 1.7%; STD: 0.201) were more prevalent in cancer patients (Table 1).

Presentation differed by cancer site: lung cancer was associated with PE in 68% of cases, while only 48% of hematologic cancers presented with PE (Table 1). Treatment patterns

Most cancer patients (91%) received LMWH initially, and 68% continued LMWH beyond 10 days (Table 2). In contrast, vitamin K antagonists and DOACs use were more common in noncancer patients (56% and 20%, respectively). Chemotherapy was more frequent in hematologic cancer (68%) and least common in GU cancer (38%). Radiotherapy was most used in lung and breast cancer (17% each) and least in pancreatic cancer (3.0%).

90-day clinical outcomes

Within 90 days, 750 cancer patients (4.0%) experienced the composite outcome: fatal PE (1.4%) or VTE recurrence (3.0%). Major bleeding occurred in 640 (3.4%), and 4,018 (22%) patients died: 258 died from PE and 120 from major bleeding (Table 3). These rates were significantly higher than in noncancer patients (composite outcome 1.5%, major bleeding 1.6%, and death 3.6%).

Relative risks compared to noncancer patients

Multivariable analysis showed that cancer patients had higher risks for the composite outcome (adjusted HR [aHR]: 2.54; 95% CI: 2.21 to 2.93), major bleeding (aHR: 1.40; 95% CI: 1.22-1.61), VTE recurrence (aHR: 2.79; 95% CI: 2.37-3.28), all-cause death (aHR: 4.55; 95% CI: 4.12-5.02), fatal PE (aHR: 2.07; 95% CI: 1.69-2.54), and fatal bleeding (aHR: 2.27; 95% CI: 1.76-2.93) (Table 4).

The highest risk for the composite outcome was observed in lung (aHR: 4.03; 95% CI: 3.38-4.81) and pancreatic (aHR: 3.85; 95% CI: 3.04-4.88) cancers. Breast (aHR: 1.63; 95% CI: 1.27-2.09) and GI (aHR: 1.95; 95% CI: 1.53-2.48) cancers also had elevated risk but to a lesser extent. Major bleeding risk was highest in GU (aHR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.38-2.00), GI (aHR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.37-2.00), and pancreatic (aHR: 1.43; 95% CI: 1.05-1.95) cancers. Patients with lung, breast, or hematologic cancers had similar bleeding risks to noncancer patients (Table 4).

In cancer patients, stratified analyses comparing the relative risks of the different outcomes showed that the risk of the composite outcome of fatal or nonfatal recurrent VTE (aHR: 2.54; 95% CI: 2.21-2.93) exceeded the risk for major bleeding (aHR: 1.40; 95% CI: 1.22-1.61), as shown in Figure 1. Interestingly, patients with lung, pancreatic, hematologic, or breast cancerhad an over 2-fold higher risk for the composite outcome than for major bleeding (Figure 2). In contrast, those with GU and GI cancers displayed a more balanced risk profile. Subgroup analyses

Among GU cancers, ovarian (aHR: 3.75; 95% CI: 2.76-5.09) and uterine (aHR: 3.34; 95% CI: 2.43-4.59) cancers had particularly high risks for the composite outcome (Figure 3). Notably, ovarian cancer did not show increased risk of major bleeding compared to noncancer patients (aHR: 1.17; 95% CI: 0.71-1.94).

Discussion

Our analysis of the RIETE registry demonstrated that cancer patients with VTE have markedly higher risks of recurrence, major bleeding, and death compared to noncancer patients, consistent with previous research.5,8 Importantly, we found that these risks varied substantially by cancer site, emphasizing the need for more individualized approaches to anticoagulation in this population.

Patients with lung and pancreatic cancers had the highest risk for the composite outcome of fatal PE or VTE recurrence, with aHRs exceeding 3.0 compared to noncancer patients. This aligns with prior studies identifying and highlighting their high thrombotic potential, likely due to aggressive disease, frequent metastasis, and prothrombotic cancer therapies.19,21 While breast and GI cancers were also associated with increased VTE risk, their relative risk was less pronounced. These findings are hypothesis-generating and suggest that certain cancer types may warrant closer monitoring or further evaluation in future studies assessing tailored anticoagulation strategies.

We also found significantly higher risks of major bleeding in patients with GU, GI, and pancreatic cancers, which underscores the need for cautious anticoagulation management in these populations. These higher bleeding risks likely result from anatomic proximity to vascular structures, frequent invasive procedures, and cancer-related coagulopathies. This reinforces current guidelines that prefer DOACs over LMWH in patients with GI or GU cancers due to their more favorable safety profile.15, 16, 17, 18 However, the observed differences in bleeding risks within these cancer sites suggest that even more personalized approaches might be needed, including choice of anticoagulant, dose adjustment or temporary interruptions, enhanced monitoring, and dynamic reassessment.

Subgroup analyses revealed specific nuances. Patients with ovarian or uterine cancer (GU group) had a particularly high risk of the composite outcome, while biliary malignancies (GI group) carried elevated risks for both VTE recurrence and bleeding. These findings suggest that even within broader cancer categories, the risk of the composite outcome and bleeding can vary based on the specific tumor site and location. Therefore, clinicians should consider not only the general cancer site but also the specific tumor characteristics when managing anticoagulation in cancer patients.

In addition to tumor-related factors, systemic patient-level conditions may also influence the risk profile of patients with cancer-associated VTE. Notably, comorbidities such as anemia, renal dysfunction, and thrombocytopenia can affect both thrombotic and bleeding complications during anticoagulant therapy. Recent data suggest that anemia may serve as an independent predictor of adverse outcomes in this setting, underscoring its clinical relevance.27,28 These extracardiac factors represent an additional layer of complexity and highlight the importance of integrating individual patient characteristics into risk stratification tools. Anticoagulation decisions should therefore be tailored not only according to cancer site and treatment but also based on concurrent systemic conditions that may amplify the risks associated with therapy.

Study Limitations

This study has limitations. First, its observational design, while reflective of real-world practice, limits our ability to infer causality. Although we adjusted for a wide range of known confounders, residual confounding by unmeasured variables (particularly from cancer-specific factors such as tumor burden, molecular subtype, progression status, and performance status) may still influence the outcomes. Second, anticoagulant selection and intensity were at physician discretion, introducing potential confounding that could affect the results. Third, the inclusion of patients treated with vitamin K antagonists (particularly in the cancer cohort) may limit the clinical applicability of our findings to contemporary populations managed exclusively with DOACs or LMWHs. Fourth, outcome definitions may have introduced misclassification. Fifth, although we used a Fine-Gray model to account for competing risks (notably death) and a multilevel structure to account for hospital-level clustering, the high number of comparisons across multiple cancer sites raises the potential for type I error. Importantly, the robustness of our findings was supported by a sensitivity analysis using FDR correction, which confirmed the key associations for lung, pancreatic, GI, and GU cancers. Sixth, the 90-day follow-up period focuses on short-term risks and does not capture long-term outcomes, such as late VTE recurrence or delayed bleeding complications. Finally, the exclusion of patients undergoing clinical trials might limit the generalizability to patients receiving novel treatments.

In conclusion, our study underscores the heterogeneity of VTE-related outcomes across cancer sites, emphasizing the need for personalized anticoagulation strategies. Patients with lung and pancreatic cancers were at a particularly high thrombotic risk, while those with GU or GI cancers exhibited a higher bleeding risk, suggesting a cautious approach to anticoagulation is warranted. These findings highlight the need for further research on long-term anticoagulation management in cancer-associated VTE.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: VTE is a frequent and serious complication in cancer patients. This study shows that the risks of recurrent VTE and major bleeding vary substantially by cancer site, with lung and pancreatic cancers conferring the highest thrombotic risk and GI and GU cancers the highest bleeding risk.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Future research should focus on refining risk stratification tools for cancer-associated thrombosis that incorporate both tumor characteristics and patient-specific clinical factors such as anemia or renal function. Randomized trials testing personalized anticoagulation strategies based on tumor site and bleeding risk are warranted.
