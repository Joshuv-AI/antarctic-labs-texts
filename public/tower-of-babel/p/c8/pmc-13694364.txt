Predicting postoperative coronal imbalance in Lenke 1/2 adolescent idiopathic scoliosis: A machine learning model with clinical interpretability
Authors: Tao Li, Chaofeng Guo, Sha Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSelective posterior thoracic fusion (sPTF) for Lenke 1/2 adolescent idiopathic scoliosis (AIS) aims to reconcile multi-planar correction with motion preservation. Nevertheless, postoperative coronal imbalance (CIB) frequently compromises these objectives. This study developed an interpretable machine learning architecture to stratify CIB risk and identify key predictors.MethodsData from 282 patients were analyzed. Following dual-stage dimensionality reduction (Boruta and LASSO) on 24 candidate predictors, ten machine learning architectures were trained and evaluated using split-sample internal validation. Model efficacy was evaluated via area under the curve (AUC), Brier score, and decision curve analysis, using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) framework for algorithmic transparency.ResultsThe LightGBM model demonstrated favorable performance, achieving peak AUC of 0.885 (training) and 0.824 (internal validation). SHAP identified three key predictors: the spatial relationship between the lowest instrumented vertebra and the last substantially touching vertebra (LIV-LSTV), regional lumbar adaptability (Lumbar Modifier), and skeletal maturity (Risser grade). Lower LIV-LSTV values, Lumbar Modifiers C, and lower Risser grades were associated with a higher predicted risk of CIB.ConclusionThis framework incorporates information on distal instrumentation selection, lumbar curve morphology, and skeletal maturity. The model may provide a preliminary basis for future preoperative CIB risk estimation and support a more informed assessment of the trade-off between deformity correction and preservation of lumbar motion segments. Independent external validation is required before the model can be used to guide surgical decision-making.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00264-026-06953-6.Keywords: Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis, Coronal imbalance, Machine learning, Preoperative planning, Surgical decision-making, SHAP analysis

Introduction

The surgical management of Adolescent Idiopathic Scoliosis (AIS), particularly Lenke type 1 and 2 curves treated with selective posterior thoracic fusion (sPTF), requires a delicate balance between multi-planar deformity correction and motion preservation (1–4). However, successful regional curve correction may be compromised by postoperative coronal imbalance (CIB) (5, 6), whose reported incidence varies across different AIS curve patterns and postoperative assessment time points (3, 7). Consequently, coronal balance (CB), alongside sagittal alignment, has emerged as the core evaluative framework for modern surgical efficacy (4). Persistent CIB, defined by an absolute value of Coronal Balance Distance (|CBD|) > 20 mm (3, 8, 9), not only compromises aesthetic outcomes but has been relevant to diminished health-related quality of life (HRQoL)

(10–12) and mechanical hardware failure requiring revision.

Accurately forecasting this complication remains highly challenging due to the intricate, non-linear biomechanics inherent to thoracic-dominant curves. While specific risk factors have been identified, such as type C configurations in degenerative scoliosis (13), these predictors rarely generalize to Lenke 1/2 patients. The unique pelvic compensatory mechanisms of thoracic-major curves may limit the ability of traditional linear models to capture potentially relevant non-linear relationships and interactions. Secondly, the sample size is relatively small. There are few reports on the primary causes and risk factors of CIB in patients with Lenke 1/2 type AIS after corrective surgery, and whether postoperative CIB can be predicted remains unclear.

Recently, machine learning (ML) models have shown potential in predicting postoperative complications by capturing complex relationships that may not be fully represented by conventional statistical approaches (14). To address this evidence gap, the present study developed an interpretable ML-based model for predicting postoperative CIB in patients with Lenke 1/2 AIS, utilizing a single-centre retrospective analysis. By integrating preoperative clinical and radiographic parameters, this approach aims to stratify CIB risk and provide a preliminary data-driven framework for further evaluation in preoperative planning.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

Following the Ethics Committee approval from the Xiangya Hospital, this single-center retrospective study analyzed 282 patients with Lenke type 1 and 2 AIS who underwent selective posterior thoracic fusion between January 2016 and May 2021. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ten to 18 years; (2) Lenke 1 or 2 AIS; (3) surgical treatment with sPTF; and (4) ≥ 24 months of postoperative follow-up. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Incomplete clinical data or loss to follow-up during the follow-up period; (2) concomitant neurological or lower extremity disorders affecting posture; and (3) prior spinal surgery. At the last follow-up, the cohort was dichotomized based on |CBD|: a CB group (|CBD|≤ 20 mm) and a CIB group (|CBD|> 20 mm) (8, 9). CBD was defined as the horizontal distance between the C7 plumb line and the central sacral vertical line (CSVL) on standing coronal radiographs. The 20-mm threshold was selected because it has been commonly used in previous AIS studies to define radiographic coronal imbalance (2). The final follow-up assessment was used to evaluate longer-term coronal imbalance rather than immediate post-operative imbalance, which may improve during postoperative adaptation. The follow-up duration did not differ significantly between the CB group (35.87 ± 6.03 months) and the CIB group (35.74 ± 6.00 months) (t = 0.14, P = 0.888). Surgical technique

All patients received individualized sPTF utilizing pedicle screws or transverse process hooks. Upon placement of pre-contoured rods, 3D correction was executed via apical derotation, convex compression, and concave distraction under continuous alignment monitoring. The lowest instrumented vertebra (LIV) was targeted at or one to two segments adjacent to the last substantially touching vertebra (LSTV), and the upper instrumented vertebra (UIV) was anchored one to two levels proximal to the upper end vertebra (UEV). For quantitative encoding, LIV-LSTV was calculated as the vertebral level difference between the LIV and the LSTV. A value of 0 indicated that the LIV was at the same level as the LSTV. Positive values indicated that the LIV was located distal or caudal to the LSTV, whereas negative values indicated that the LIV was located proximal or cranial to the LSTV. For example, LIV-LSTV = 2 indicated that the LIV was two vertebral levels distal to the LSTV.

Feature engineering and dimensionality reduction

To operationalize the predictive framework, an initial feature space of 24 preoperative candidate predictors was established. This comprehensive dataset integrated demographics (age, sex), skeletal maturity (Risser and triradiate cartilage status), curve morphology (Lenke classification and modifiers), and spatial instrumentation parameters (LIV-LSTV, directionally encoded as described above, UIV-T1). Multi-planar biomechanical characteristics were parameterized via Cobb angles, flexibility indices, and a robust suite of radiographic markers covering shoulder balance (T1 tilt, clavicle angle, radiographic shoulder height), central coronal translation (apical vertebral translation, thoracic trunk shift, LIV-CSVL, baseline Coronal Balance Distance), and sagittal alignment (thoracic kyphosis, lumbar lordosis, sagittal vertical axis). Prior to model development, the cohort was partitioned (training set: internal validation set = 7:3 ratio), and a dual-stage dimensionality reduction pipeline was implemented to isolate high-fidelity predictors. The model was constructed in the training set and then evaluated in the internal validation set. Because both subsets originated from the same institutional cohort, this split-sample validation assessed internal reproducibility rather than external generalizability. The Boruta algorithm was first utilized to determine intrinsic feature significance and mitigate multicollinearity, followed by LASSO regression with tenfold cross-validation (mlr3verse and glmnet) to optimize the feature subset through penalty-based regularization.

Algorithmic development and interpretability framework

The refined feature subset served as the input for ten distinct machine learning architectures, encompassing both traditional linear models and advanced non-linear ensembles: Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), LightGBM, Neural Network, Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), AdaBoost, and Decision Tree. The LR model, based on the same three retained predictors as LightGBM, was included as the conventional reference model to assess whether LightGBM provided incremental predictive value beyond a traditional statistical approach. To rigorously prevent data leakage and ensure optimal generalization, hyperparameter tuning for the non-linear configurations was executed via an exhaustive tenfold cross-validated grid search strictly within the training set. Given the inherent class imbalance between the CIB and CB sets, model efficacy was quantified through a comprehensive and threshold-independent evaluative matrix. Discriminative capacity was primarily assessed using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC) and the F1-score. To translate predictive accuracy into surgical reliability, calibration plots and the Brier score were utilized to evaluate the concordance between predicted probabilities and observed CIB frequencies. Additionally, Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was applied to quantify the standardized net clinical benefit across a continuum of risk thresholds, an essential metric for practical surgical decision-making. Finally, the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) framework was embedded to resolve the "black-box" nature of advanced ensembles. This game-theory-based approach was utilized to quantify both global predictor importance and patient-specific risk contributions, thereby providing transparent and interpretable evidence for the model's decision-making process (15).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2). Continuous variables were assessed for normality and presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (Q₁, Q₃), as appropriate. Categorical data were expressed as counts and percentages. To compare baseline characteristics between the CB and CIB groups, univariate analyses (Chi-square tests, independent-sample t-tests, and Mann–Whitney U tests) were employed. Statistical significance was predefined at a P value < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics and univariate analysis

The study cohort comprised 282 AIS patients, stratified into training (n = 197) and internal validation (n = 85) sets. No statistically significant differences in the recorded baseline characteristics were observed between the training and internal validation sets (all P > 0.05; Table 1). Overall, postoperative CIB occurred in 58 patients. The training cohort included 43 CIB and 154 CB cases, whereas the internal validation cohort included 15 CIB and 70 CB cases. Specifically, patients developing postoperative CIB exhibited significantly lower LC flexibility and fewer LIV-LSTV segments. Furthermore, the CIB group presented with distinct skeletal maturity evidenced by differing Risser grade distributions (P = 0.014), and a markedly higher prevalence of advanced Lumbar Modifiers (types B and C) compared to the CB set (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Dimensionality reduction and feature selection

To isolate robust predictors from the 24 initial candidates, a dual-stage screening pipeline was executed. The Boruta algorithm initially eliminated redundant features, narrowing the pool to ten potential predictors (Fig. 2 A). This subset subsequently underwent LASSO regression with L1 regularization. To prioritize model generalizability and proactively mitigate overfitting, the optimal penalty threshold was defined utilizing the 1-SE criterion (λ1se = 0.041, over λmin = 0.015) via cross-validation (Fig. 2B,C). Ultimately, this feature-selection process retained the dataset into three predictors: LIV-LSTV, Risser grade, and Lumbar Modifier.

Model performance evaluation

With the predictive feature space defined, ten predictive models were comprehensively evaluated (Table 3). Given the potential non-linear biomechanical compensations inherent to AIS, the performance of LightGBM was compared directly with that of the conventional multivariable LR model. Specifically, LightGBM demonstrated the most favourable overall performance within the current dataset, achieving an AUC of 0.885 (95% CI: 0.832–0.938) in the training cohort (Fig. 3B) and 0.824 (95% CI: 0.717–0.930) in the internal validation cohort (Fig. 3A). In the internal validation cohort, LightGBM achieved a numerically higher AUC than multivariable LR (0.824 vs. 0.765) (Fig. 3A), corresponding to an absolute difference of 0.059. LightGBM also showed numerically higher accuracy (0.776 vs. 0.659) and F1 score (0.537 vs. 0.473), together with a lower Brier score (0.118 vs. 0.125) (Table 3). The calibration plots for the internal validation and training cohorts are shown in Fig. 3C and D, respectively. To explore potential net clinical benefit, DCA suggested that LightGBM provided a favorable net benefit across a range of risk thresholds within the current internal validation framework (Fig. 3E,F). These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that use of the model improves surgical decision-making or patient outcomes. Based on its overall discrimination, calibration, and decision-curve performance, LightGBM was selected for subsequent interpretation.

Algorithmic interpretability and risk deconstruction

To demystify the algorithm's decision-making, SHAP analysis delineated three key predictors contributing to predicted CIB risk: LIV-LSTV, Lumbar Modifier, and Risser grade (Fig. 4A, D). Rather than isolated variables, these features reflected intertwined clinical vulnerabilities. The spatial relationship between the lowest instrumented vertebra and the LSTV was associated with distal biomechanical stability, whereas the Lumbar Modifier and skeletal maturity (Risser grade) captured intrinsic structural adaptability. Beyond global feature importance, waterfall and force diagrams illustrated patient-specific contributions of individual predictors to the model output (Fig. 4B, C). In the representative SHAP example, a LIV-LSTV value of 2, a Lumbar Modifier of A, and a Risser grade of 4 all contributed negatively to the predicted CIB risk, shifting the model output from an expected baseline of − 1.28 to − 2.55.

Discussion

By analyzing a single-center retrospective dataset and comparing ten distinct machine learning architectures, this study developed an interpretable LightGBM model for postoperative CIB in Lenke 1/2 AIS. Through the SHAP framework, the model identified three key predictors: LIV-LSTV, Lumbar Modifier, and Risser grade. These findings provide a preliminary basis for further evaluation of individualized preoperative risk stratification.

Previous studies have provided an important foundation for identifying factors associated with postoperative coronal alignment. Li et al. highlighted the potential of ML methods to integrate multidimensional data when predicting AIS progression (16) and surgical outcomes. Beauchamp et al. demonstrated the clinical relevance of the touched vertebra criterion for LIV selection in Lenke type 1 and 2 curves (17). Inappropriate LIV selection relative to the compensatory curve has also been linked to postoperative coronal decompensation in patients with Lenke 5C curves (18). The present study extends this work by focusing specifically on postoperative CIB after selective posterior thoracic fusion and by evaluating the combined contributions of LIV-LSTV, Lumbar Modifier, and Risser grade. Unlike traditional linear models, which generally estimate the independent contribution of each variable, LightGBM can capture non-linear relationships and interactions among surgical planning parameters, lumbar curve characteristics, and skeletal maturity. Because the final model was based on only three predictors, a conventional multivariable LR model remains an important comparator. In the internal validation cohort, LightGBM showed a higher AUC and F1 score and a lower Brier score than LR, showing a modest numerical difference in performance. However, these findings do not establish that a more complex ML architecture is universally preferable to a simpler regression-based model. In addition, SHAP analysis provides both global feature importance and patient-specific explanations, enhancing model transparency and clinical interpretability. These findings suggest that postoperative CIB is not attributable to a single anatomical factor but may reflect the interaction between distal instrumentation selection and the compensatory capacity of the unfused lumbar spine. Whether the observed improvement in predictive performance is clinically meaningful requires confirmation in independent external cohorts.

From a clinical perspective, if externally validated, the LightGBM model may have potential relevance for future preoperative risk stratification in patients with Lenke 1/2 AIS undergoing selective posterior thoracic fusion. The three predictors required by the model, LIV-LSTV, Lumbar Modifier, and Risser grade, are routinely available during preoperative assessment. After an initial fusion strategy has been proposed, the model could be used to estimate the individualized risk of postoperative CIB. A high predicted risk could prompt further review of the planned LIV, the compensatory capacity of the unfused lumbar curve, and the patient’s skeletal maturity. The predicted risk may also facilitate preoperative counselling by helping patients and their families understand the potential trade-off between preserving lumbar motion segments and reducing the likelihood of postoperative coronal imbalance. Nevertheless, the present study did not evaluate whether use of the model alters surgical decision-making, improves fusion-level selection, reduces postoperative CIB, or improves patient outcomes. Accordingly, the model should be regarded as a preliminary risk-prediction framework requiring independent external validation and prospective clinical-utility evaluation. Figure 5 outlines the proposed future validation and implementation pathway, including multicenter external validation, calibration assessment, risk-threshold definition, clinical-utility evaluation, and outcome assessment before the model can be considered for integration into preoperative planning research.

Proposed future validation and implementation pathway for the LightGBM model

The definition of postoperative CIB also warrants consideration. In the present study, CIB was defined as a |CBD|> 20 mm at the final follow-up (9). This threshold has been commonly used in previous AIS studies to identify radiographic coronal imbalance. The final follow-up assessment was selected to capture persistent imbalance, as early postoperative coronal decompensation has been reported to improve over time in patients with Lenke 5C curves (19). Persistent coronal imbalance remains clinically relevant because residual coronal decompensation has been associated with poorer patient-reported outcomes, particularly when the imbalance is severe and persistent (20). However, the 20-mm cutoff should be interpreted as a radiographic classification threshold rather than a definitive patient-reported outcome threshold.

Notwithstanding the potential clinical utility of this framework, certain methodological constraints warrant acknowledgment. Most importantly, the model has not undergone external validation. Although the dataset was divided into training and internal validation cohorts, both subsets originated from the same institution and patient population. Therefore, the current split-sample validation assesses internal reproducibility rather than external generalizability. The retrospective, single-centre design of this study inherently limits the external validity and generalizability of the LightGBM model across diverse geographic populations and varying institutional practices. Furthermore, the strict inclusion of selective posterior thoracic fusions limits the immediate extrapolation of these findings to anterior approaches or extensive long-segment instrumentation. The imbalance between the CIB and CB groups (approximately 1:4) represents an additional limitation. While this distribution may reflect the real-world composition of the cohort, the limited number of postoperative CIB events may have affected model stability and predictive performance. Although Boruta and LASSO feature selection, the 1-SE criterion, and cross-validated hyperparameter tuning were used to reduce model complexity and mitigate overfitting, these procedures cannot fully exclude overfitting in the setting of a relatively small number of outcome events. The limited event frequency may also reduce the stability of predictor selection and increase uncertainty in the estimated model performance, particularly in the internal validation cohort. No class-weighting or resampling strategy was applied in the present analysis. Accordingly, the reported performance should be interpreted as preliminary. Concurrently, although the SHAP framework clarifies complex variable associations, extracting definitive biomechanical causality from retrospective data remains intrinsically challenging. A further limitation stems from the model's reliance on static radiographic parameters, which do not capture dynamic equilibrium shifts during gait, load-bearing transitions, or intra-operative correction (21). Future prospective multicenter studies should validate the model in larger cohorts with sufficient numbers of CIB events and assess whether class-weighted algorithms, resampling strategies, and dynamic parameters, such as three-dimensional motion analysis, gait assessment, and intra-operative alignment data, can further refine risk prediction.

Conclusion

This single-center study developed an interpretable LightGBM model for predicting postoperative CIB in patients with Lenke 1/2 AIS. SHAP analysis identified distal instrumentation selection (LIV-LSTV), lumbar curve morphology (Lumbar Modifier), and skeletal maturity (Risser grade) as the principal predictors. The model demonstrated favorable performance on internal validation using routinely available preoperative variables. If externally validated, it could serve as an adjunct to comprehensive radiographic assessment and surgeon judgement by identifying patients who may warrant closer review of the planned LIV and by supporting counselling regarding the trade-off between coronal stability and preservation of lumbar motion segments. Prospective evaluation in independent multicenter cohorts is required before the model can be used to guide routine clinical decision-making and to establish its generalizability and clinical utility.
