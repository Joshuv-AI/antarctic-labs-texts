Prolonged Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation Without Systemic Anticoagulation in Sheep
Authors: Zahra Mahdieh, Alex Meng, Brian Y Chang, Nicholas X Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeClinically significant bleeding is a common complication of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), with bleeding observed in upwards of 40% of patients. Bleeding is strongly associated with systemic anticoagulation, which is typically necessary to reduce the incidence of thrombosis in the extracorporeal circuit. The InFlo MOBYBOX integrates a multimodal strategy for coagulation mitigation, combining optimized circuit components (pump, oxygenator) design with a biopassive hydrophilic surface coating to address both flow-related and surface-mediated contributors to thrombosis and hemolysis. Background

Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) therapy represents a critical life-support intervention for patients experiencing refractory cardiopulmonary failure, with clinical adoption expanding dramatically as evidence supporting survival benefits continues to accumulate [1, 2]. However, contemporary ECMO systems remain fundamentally constrained by the competing risks of hemorrhage and thrombosis [3]. The non-physiological exposure of blood to synthetic circuit surfaces triggers a cascade of pathophysiological responses including the intrinsic coagulation pathway, complement-mediated inflammatory cascades, and contact-dependent platelet aggregation [4]. Non-uniform flow within the extracorporeal circuit further exacerbates this issue through multiple interconnected mechanisms [5]. Turbulent flow patterns in circuit components create fluctuating shear layers that expose platelets to non-stationary shear rates, promoting aggregate formation either through direct platelet activation or through shear-mediated modulation of von Willebrand factor (vWF) multimers [6]. At shear rates exceeding 10,000 s−1, commonly observed in ECMO pumps and cannulas, vWF undergoes conformational changes that enable binding to platelet GPIb receptors, initiating the vWF–platelet pathway of thrombus formation [7]. Additionally, regions of flow stagnation increase blood residence time and prolong contact with thrombogenic circuit surfaces, while low-flow conditions create localized areas of elevated shear stress that enhance platelet deposition and accelerate thrombus growth [8]. In addition, erythrocyte damage simultaneously triggers the extrinsic coagulation pathway [9]. To mitigate circuit thrombosis, systemic anticoagulation predominantly using unfractionated heparin is mandated. Yet, using anticoagulation precipitates a critical clinical dilemma [10].

Large registry and pooled clinical data underscore the magnitude of this problem. According to the ELSO Registry International Summary Report (2025; data through 2024), clinically significant bleeding complications occur across all adult ECMO modalities. In adult respiratory ECMO, reported bleeding events include surgical site bleeding (7.2%), gastrointestinal hemorrhage (5.8%), peripheral cannulation site bleeding (4.3%), and pulmonary hemorrhage (3.2%). Bleeding rates are higher in adult cardiac ECMO, with surgical site bleeding reported in 15.6% of runs and peripheral cannulation site bleeding in 7.0%, along with gastrointestinal (4.5%) and pulmonary hemorrhage (1.4%). Although less frequent, intracranial hemorrhage occurs in approximately 2.1–3.2% of adult ECMO runs depending on support modality and is associated with disproportionately high mortality [11].

Beyond individual bleeding categories, pooled analyses illustrate the cumulative hemorrhagic burden imposed by anticoagulation-dependent ECMO strategies. In a meta-analysis of 20 adult studies encompassing 1866 patients supported with ECMO for cardiogenic shock or cardiac arrest, Cheng et al. reported a pooled incidence of major or significant bleeding of 40.8%, demonstrating that bleeding is not an isolated complication but a pervasive and systemic consequence of current ECMO practice [12]. Collectively, these data highlight the substantial clinical burden created by the need for systemic anticoagulation in extracorporeal support and underscore the critical need for ECMO platforms and approaches that reduce reliance on anticoagulation while maintaining circuit patency and performance.

Contemporary practice is increasingly exploring heparin-sparing and, in select contexts, no continuous anticoagulation strategies using currently marketed ECMO circuits to reduce bleeding risk [13]. Retrospective clinical series have reported the feasibility of venovenous ECMO without continuous systemic anticoagulation in carefully selected patients, and similar experiences have been described for venoarterial support with periods of anticoagulation avoidance [14, 15]. Systematic reviews indicate that anticoagulation-free ECMO is most commonly pursued in high bleeding-risk scenarios, but circuit thrombosis and oxygenator dysfunction remain important limitations that constrain generalizability [16, 17].

Multiple unimodal bioengineering strategies have been investigated to address blood-circuit biocompatibility limitations and reduce dependence on systemic anticoagulation. Surface modification approaches include heparin immobilization and phosphorylcholine grafting to recapitulate endothelial glycocalyx properties and attenuate platelet activation [18, 19]. Biopassive and biomimetic polymer architectures encompassing poly(ethylene glycol) brushes, zwitterionic copolymers, and tethered perfluorocarbon liquid interfaces have been engineered to minimize hydrophobic interactions and suppress fibrinogen adsorption [20, 21]. Localized drug-eluting platforms delivering immobilized direct thrombin inhibitors or factor Xa antagonists have demonstrated preliminary efficacy in reducing contact activation [22]. Fluid-dynamic optimization strategies, including refined circuit geometry and centrifugal pump designs with minimized shear stress profiles, target mechanically induced activation of the coagulation cascade [6, 23]. Nitric oxide-based interventions through both sweep gas supplementation and NO-releasing polymeric surfaces provide a biomimetic approach to inhibit platelet aggregation and thrombus propagation in preclinical models [24, 25]. However, these unimodal approaches typically address either intrinsic pathway activation (contact-mediated) or extrinsic pathway activation (tissue factor-mediated) but not both simultaneously. Despite this extensive landscape, none have materialized in a device that demonstrates clinically relevant, extended-duration anticoagulation-free extracorporeal support.

Here, we present the InFlo MOBYBOX, a compact and lightweight extracorporeal support system consisting of a portable patient unit and external controller (Fig. 1). The system uniquely combines fluid-dynamic optimization with advanced surface bioengineering to eliminate thrombogenicity without systemic anticoagulation. Computational fluid dynamics-guided circuit architecture optimization eliminates stagnation zones and high-shear stress regions within a novel pneumatic pump and helical oxygenator design. Advanced surface bioengineering is used to coat the circuit with a non-eluting biopassive hydrophilic surface comprising polyethylene glycol and albumin that suppresses early blood–material interactions. The hydrophilic character of the coating limits nonspecific protein adsorption and fibrinogen conformational changes, thereby reducing platelet adhesion and contact pathway activation without reliance on immobilized anticoagulants or drug release. When combined with uniform flow and reduced residence time through optimized system design, this surface strategy limits thrombus nucleation and propagation under anticoagulant-free conditions, including coagulopathic low-flow states. Thus, InFlo MOBYBOX represents a novel multimodal antithrombotic strategy that integrates fluid-dynamic optimization and surface bioengineering without reliance on systemic anticoagulation.

The InFlo MOBYBOX extracorporeal support system, consisting of a compact portable patient unit and external controller. The design is lightweight and compact, while integrating fluid-dynamic optimization and biocompatible surface engineering to minimize thrombosis and hemolysis

We hypothesized that anticoagulant-free performance could be achieved through a synergistic combination of minimized hemolysis through optimized flow dynamics, enhanced blood flow uniformity that prevents stagnation-induced activation, and reduced platelet and protein deposition at the blood–biomaterial interface. To evaluate this hypothesis, we conducted a preliminary seven-day large-animal study operating the InFlo MOBYBOX at low-flow rate continuously without any systemic anticoagulants. This study was designed to assess circuit patency, gas-exchange performance, hematologic stability, and device-related safety during prolonged extracorporeal support while progressively reducing systemic anticoagulation to evaluate the feasibility of sustained anticoagulant-free operation.

Methods

Study Design and Ethical Oversight

A prospective, non-randomized, stepwise interventional study was conducted to evaluate the hemocompatibility and anticoagulation requirements of the MOBYBOX ECMO system in a chronic ovine model. The study was designed as a range-finding investigation, in which systemic anticoagulation was progressively reduced across sequential animal cohorts to identify the lowest anticoagulation strategy capable of sustaining stable ECMO support without clinically significant thrombotic or hemorrhagic complications.

This preliminary investigation was structured as a range-finding study to systematically establish acceptable anticoagulation parameters for device operation. Three sequential experimental groups were studied, each with predefined anticoagulation targets. The first group was designed to operate at ACT levels just below the ELSO-recommended range of 180–200 s. Group 1 was assigned a target ACT range of 150–170 s. To ensure distinct separation between experimental conditions, Group 2 was subsequently assigned a target range of 130–150 s. Findings from these initial cohorts, including observed circuit performance, thrombus burden, and animal stability, informed the final cohort (Group 3), which evaluated prolonged ECMO support following a single peri-procedural heparin bolus with no subsequent systemic anticoagulation. This stepwise approach was adopted to maximize animal welfare by minimizing unnecessary harm while establishing feasibility prior to larger comparative investigations. Because the primary objective of this preliminary study was to assess feasibility and define anticoagulation boundaries rather than to perform formal treatment comparisons, no parallel control group was included. Further comparative studies with appropriate statistical power will be necessary to validate these findings.

The present manuscript focuses on outcomes from Group 3, which represents the culmination of the anticoagulation titration strategy and the primary experimental objective of anticoagulant-free ECMO operation. Data from Groups 1 and 2 are provided as supplementary material to document the stepwise anticoagulation reduction pathway and associated decision framework.

All procedures were conducted in compliance with the Animal Welfare Act and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition) and were approved by the Veranex Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) Administration. The study was performed at an AAALAC-accredited facility (Veranex Inc., GA, US) under BARDA Contract No. 75A50124C00019.

Sample Size and Statistical Considerations

Under these assumptions, a design consisting of three sequential groups of four animals each was determined to be sufficient to support the objectives of this preliminary range-finding study.

Accordingly, four animals were allocated to each anticoagulation cohort. As the present manuscript reports outcomes exclusively from Group 3, statistical analyses were structured to evaluate feasibility, device performance stability, and safety outcomes. These findings should be considered preliminary, and further comparative studies with larger sample sizes will be necessary to confirm these observations and establish statistical significance.

Animal Model and Experimental Subjects

Suffolk crossbred female sheep (n = 4), aged 2–4 years and weighing 45–65 kg, served as the experimental model for this investigation. Animals were sourced from Veranex approved commercial vendor and subjected to a mandatory quarantine period with progressive acclimation to stanchion housing to minimize procedural stress. Pre-enrollment health screening included comprehensive physical examination, complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, and coagulation studies to ensure baseline physiological parameters were within normal limits. Animals demonstrating any evidence of cardiovascular, pulmonary, hematologic, or infectious disease were excluded from the study. This work was performed at Veranex, Inc. (Atlanta, GA).

ECMO System Configuration

The InFlo MOBYBOX system consisted of a tip-to-tip biocompatible hydrophilic surface coating across the entire extracorporeal circuit (Supplementary Fig. 1) incorporating a biventricular pulsatile pump, hollow-fiber membrane oxygenator, and connecting tubing optimized through computational fluid dynamics modeling. The circuit was primed with a standardized lactated Ringer’s solution and heparin (initial concentration 5 unit/mL), with a total priming volume of approximately 650 mL, to achieve physiological oncotic pressure and minimize hemodilution effects. The system was configured for venovenous (VV) ECMO support with target blood flow rates maintained between 1.8 and 2.2 L/min throughout the study period. Gas flow rate was maintained at 0.3 L/min using compressed room air except for 5 min at the beginning and end of the experiment to test the oxygen and carbon dioxide transfer rates.

Surgical Procedures and Vascular Access

Vascular cannulation was performed under general anesthesia using a modified Seldinger technique. Following induction with propofol and maintenance with isoflurane, animals were positioned supine with neck extension to optimize procedural exposure. Vascular access was achieved through percutaneous placement of a single 28 French MC3 Crescent™ dual-lumen cannula into the right internal jugular vein under fluoroscopic guidance. Cannula positioning was confirmed using fluoroscopy to ensure appropriate inflow and outflow port placement within the right atrium and superior vena cava, respectively. Surgical sites were prepared using sterile technique with povidone–iodine antisepsis and maintained under occlusive dressings throughout the study period. The cannula was secured and dressed. An InFlo MOBYBOX ECMO circuit was prepared and primed according to the manufacturer’s instructions for use. Following priming, the circuit was maintained at a flow rate of 2.5 ± 0.5 L/min until connection. Prior to connection, flow was stopped, and tubing was clamped. The withdrawal and return lines were cut and connected to the appropriate cannula ports using a wet-wet connection technique. After verifying secure connections, the tubing was unclamped, and flow was initiated with gradual escalation to 2.0 ± 0.2 L/min. Animals received Cefazolin (22–25 mg/kg) for post-procedure prophylaxis.

The sheep were then transferred to a gurney and moved to a stanchion where they were recovered from anesthesia. Transient shivering was occasionally observed immediately after surgery, consistent with known effects of anesthetic agents. In these cases, animals were externally warmed using a heat lamp for approximately 30 min until shivering resolved. No supplemental heating was required during the study period, as the MOBYBOX system incorporates an insulating housing enclosing the pump and oxygenator, which limits heat loss from the circulating blood. During this post-procedure period, animals received Butorphanol (0.1–0.4 mg/kg IV) and Flunixin (1 mg/kg, IV) approximately every 6–12 h to minimize pain during the first few days post-operation. Each implanted animal was maintained in an approximately 2.5 × 6 feet pen/stanchion that allowed limited forward and backward movement but prevented the animal from turning or activity that could pull out lines or cannula.

The animals were observed 24 h a day during the ECMO phase of the study, during which time pump performance and physiological assessments were made and recorded in each animal’s individual medical record. While on pump, animals received gastrointestinal prophylaxis (Omeprazole, 3–4 mg/kg daily, PO) to minimize discomfort. If an animal became overly stressed or agitated while in the stanchion during the on-pump period, they were permitted to receive an anxiolytic (Midazolam 0.2–0.3 mg/kg, IV). Animals were provided ad libitum access to food and water throughout the study period.

Anticoagulation Protocol

Anticoagulation was only administered as a single bolus of 120 units/kg of unfractionated heparin in the intraprocedural period during cannulation. No further heparin products or other anticoagulants were administered for the remainder of the study. ACT measurements were obtained using the iStat 1 using an ACT test cartridge in at least 4 h intervals during the initial 24 h, followed by 24 h intervals for the remainder of the study period (see supplemental methods).

Physiological Monitoring and Data Collection

Animals were continuously monitored using standard anesthesia equipment which included measures for heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, and oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry. Circuit-specific parameters included blood flow rate, and sweep gas flow rate. Blood sampling was performed at predetermined intervals (baseline, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, 120, 144, and 168 h) for assessment of complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, arterial blood gas analysis, plasma-free hemoglobin, D-dimer, fibrinogen, and coagulation studies through the sampling ports in the extracorporeal circuit immediately before and after the gas exchanger (see supplemental methods). Arterial and venous blood gases were analyzed immediately at the time of collection using a point-of-care blood gas analyzer (i-STAT system, Abbott Laboratories); samples were obtained via pre- and post-oxygenator sampling ports, as appropriate; plasma-free hemoglobin was measured using a UV spectrophotometric assay, and hematologic, biochemical, and coagulation analyses were performed using validated clinical laboratory analyzers (Antech Diagnostics, GLP-certified) (see supplemental methods).

Primary and Secondary Endpoints

The primary endpoint was thrombus formation within the oxygenator, defined as percent of the oxygenator fiber mat layer covered in clot, assessed at study termination. Secondary endpoints included preservation of gas-exchange performance as measured by oxygen transfer rate within ± 10% of initial measurements, and absence of sustained hematologic deterioration (> 50% decrease in hematocrit, red blood cell count, or platelet count) during the study period. In addition, secondary safety endpoints encompassed maintenance of plasma-free hemoglobin levels < 50 mg/dL, absence of mechanical device failure or unplanned circuit interruption, and absence of macroscopic thrombosis in the vena cava or end organs upon necropsy examination.

Post-Mortem Analysis and Circuit Evaluation

Following completion of the 168 h study period, animals were euthanized using pentobarbital and subjected to comprehensive necropsy examination. Major organs (heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, spleen) were harvested and examined for gross evidence of thromboembolism, infarction, or hemorrhage. The entire extracorporeal circuit was systematically flushed with normal saline, disassembled, and subjected to detailed macroscopic examination for evidence of thrombus formation, material degradation, or mechanical failure.

Oxygenator Coagulation Coverage Scoring

Quantitative clot coverage was assessed through systematic image analysis of each oxygenator layer. Digital photographs of explanted oxygenator membranes were acquired under standardized lighting conditions. The analysis workflow involved importing raw images into ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD), followed by conversion to 8-bit grayscale format. A consistent color threshold was applied across all images to isolate red regions indicative of blood clot formation and protein deposition on the membrane surfaces (Supplementary Fig. 2). The threshold parameters were established using representative samples and maintained constant throughout the analysis to ensure consistency. The percent area covered by clotting was calculated as the ratio of pixels above the red threshold to the total membrane surface area visible in each image. Although the oxygenator contains more than 20 fiber layers, clot coverage analysis was restricted to the first 20 inlet-proximal layers, where thrombus formation was consistently greatest and decreased in downstream layers, in order to avoid artificially diluting localized coagulation by averaging across the entire fiber bundle. Semi-quantitative scoring was performed using a predefined 5-point scale (0 = no visible thrombus, 1 = minimal deposition ≤ 10% surface coverage, 2 = moderate 11–25%, 3 = significant 26–50%, 4 = extensive > 50% coverage). For each oxygenator, individual fiber mat layers were analyzed separately, and the results were aggregated to determine overall clot burden. All measurements were performed by a single blinded operator to minimize inter-observer variability.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Hematologic and biochemical parameters were analyzed using repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Circuit performance metrics were evaluated using paired t-tests comparing baseline and terminal values. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Results

All animals were successfully cannulated using the Seldinger technique and cannula positioning was verified through fluoroscopy before ECMO circuit initiation. No animals experienced intraoperative or immediate post-operative complications following initiation of VV ECMO.

During cannulation, a single heparin bolus was administered, which increased ACT from baseline physiological value of 136 ± 7 s to an intraprocedural peak of 316 ± 75 s (Fig. 2). Following circuit initiation, animals were transferred to a stanchion where they remained for the duration of the test. Animals were maintained in an awake and ambulatory state with a VV ECMO circuit configuration during the study. After the single intraoperative heparin bolus, the animals remained heparin-free for the remainder of the test. ACT values returned to baseline physiological levels within 6 h and remained stable throughout the 7-day study period (134 ± 7 s) and were not statistically different from the baseline values (Fig. 2). All coagulation parameters (aPTT, PT, and ACT) followed a similar trend, returning to baseline shortly after cannulation and circuit initiation (Table 1). No additional anticoagulant administration was required throughout the remainder of the test. Post-operative recovery was otherwise uneventful, and all animals tolerated the complete withdrawal of systemic anticoagulant without observable adverse events during the perioperative phase. Pump speed was maintained at 2.0 ± 0.2 L/min throughout the duration of the test and no device malfunctions or mechanical failures occurred during cannulation or initiation of support (Supplementary Fig. 3). All animals reached the planned 7-day endpoint except Sheep 8354, which was electively terminated on day 6 due to fever unrelated to circuit thrombotic performance; data for this animal are therefore absent at day 7. Sheep 8354 remained clinically stable throughout the study, with no symptoms of distress, low-flow state, respiratory illness, alarms, or other abnormalities observed during the preceding days. On the morning of Day 6, the attending veterinarian noted an acute onset of fever (41.7 °C) accompanied by mild coughing. There were no changes to circuit performance or settings. The animal was treated with Baytril (aka enrofloxacin, 300 mg, IV) and Naxcel (aka ceftiofur sodium, 200 mg, IV), and its condition was monitored for approximately 4 h; however, the elevated temperature persisted. To prevent undue suffering and in accordance with humane endpoints, the veterinarian elected to euthanize the animal that day.

Oxygen transfer rate (OTR) remained stable across all animals during the 7-day support period at 403.7 ± 40.1 mL O2/min on day 1 and 406.5 ± 42.0 mL O2/min at term (p = 0.929) (Fig. 3A). Individual values clustered around the group mean, with no directional trends between day 1 and term. pO2 was measured from the oxygenator inlet (pre) and outlet (post) sampling ports (Fig. 3B). Outlet pO2 remained stable throughout support 186.9 ± 8.9 mmHg, with no evidence of decline in oxygenator performance and inlet values remained relatively consistent at 36.0 ± 3.5 mmHg, indicating stable respiratory drive and overall lung health. Absolute outlet pO2 values were lower compared with other high-sweep protocols [26], as sweep gas flow was intentionally minimized (0.35 L/min) to allow preservation of spontaneous breathing. Similarly, CO2 transfer rate (CTR) remained consistent from day 1 at 110.3 ± 9.3 mL CO2/min to term at 110.8 ± 4.8 mL CO2/min (p = 0.875, Fig. 3C), and outlet pCO2 values (Fig. 3D) showed stable clearance, confirming preserved CO2 removal capacity. Day 1 and day 7 pCO2 values appear lower due to momentarily (5 min) increased sweep gas flow rate to maximize CO2 clearance, providing a more sensitive assessment of membrane function since CO2 removal is more susceptible to degradation from thrombus formation than oxygenation capacity [27]. Together, these findings confirm that InFlo MOBYBOX maintains robust oxygenation and CO2 removal performance under anticoagulant-free conditions without changes in key performance characteristics during the duration of the study.

After term, the InFlo MOBYBOX was disassembled, and each circuit element was individually examined for damage or obstruction. Visual inspection of the pump and oxygenator components revealed no occlusion and only minimal thrombus formation on oxygenator fiber mats (Fig. 4A and B). Quantitative clot coverage analysis across the first 20 oxygenator fiber layers demonstrated low overall deposition with an average coverage of 2.3 ± 3.1 % (Fig. 4C and D). All animals showed clot coverage well below levels expected to compromise gas-exchange performance, and no oxygenator required exchange during the 7-day run. No device-related adverse events or complications occurred at any point during the study. In addition to the absence of device-related thrombosis, gross pathology at necropsy revealed no thromboembolic injury in end organs (heart, lungs, spleen, kidneys, and liver), supporting the hemocompatibility of the InFlo MOBYBOX. Examination of the cannulae similarly showed no evidence of device-related adverse events, further confirming preserved end-organ integrity during prolonged extracorporeal circulation. Since no macroscopic signs of organ injury, thrombosis, or coagulopathy were observed in any animal, histological assessment was not performed.

Hematology and hemolysis markers further corroborated these findings (Table 2). Hematocrit (Hct) and red blood cell (RBC) counts remained stable across the 7-day support period. Mean Hct was 25 ± 3% with no statistically significant change during the duration of ECMO performance, and RBC counts followed the same stable trend, remaining within the physiological ovine range (9.1 ± 0.4 106/µL) without evidence of progressive decline. Plasma-free hemoglobin concentrations (Fig. 5A) remained at 3.5 ± 3.9 mg/dL across the duration of the test, well below established hemolysis thresholds of 50 mg/dL [28, 29] and showed no upward trend over time, confirming the absence of clinically relevant erythrocyte trauma. Together, these findings indicate preserved oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood and exclude significant hemolysis attributable to the ECMO circuit. When considered alongside stable OTR and outlet pO2, these results confirm that both blood integrity and oxygenator gas-exchange performance were maintained under anticoagulant-free conditions.

Platelet counts remained within expected limits and did not show a sustained decline (Fig. 5B and Table 2). Although baseline platelet counts were toward the lower end of reported ovine reference ranges, this finding is consistent with known physiological variability in sheep [30]. Ovine platelet counts are influenced by age, season, physiologic state, and perioperative stress and may vary within a broad normal range independent of consumptive coagulopathy [31, 32]. Importantly, platelet counts remained stable throughout the 7-day support period, supporting the absence of ongoing platelet consumption or platelet-driven thrombotic activation under the tested conditions. D-dimer concentrations remained stable across the study period (Fig. 5B), further indicating the absence of ongoing thrombus formation or fibrinolysis. White blood cell (WBC) counts remained within physiological limits, with no sustained leukocytosis or leukopenia attributable to device use (Fig. 5C). These results confirm that InFlo MOBYBOX provided anticoagulant-free ECMO support without inducing hematologic complications or device-related blood trauma, highlighting the potential clinical relevance of device-level solutions to reduce reliance on systemic anticoagulants.

Fibrinogen values are reported in Table 2. A transient decrease in fibrinogen below the device’s measurable threshold was observed on day 2 in all animals; however, levels subsequently normalized and stabilized within physiologic ranges, consistent with other ovine ECMO models [26]. Importantly, data from the earlier anticoagulation titration groups within this study, conducted at higher systemic anticoagulation targets (ACT 157 ± 27 s (group 1) and ACT 142 ± 17 s (group 2)), demonstrated similar fibrinogen levels, along with stable coagulation and hemolysis parameters (Supplemental Figs. 4, 5). In those cohorts, quantitative clot analysis revealed minimal fibrin deposition, with an average coagulation coverage of 0.52 ± 1.61% in group 1 and 0.18 ± 0.36 in group 2 across the first 20 oxygenator fiber layers (Supplemental Fig. 6). Taken together with stable platelet counts, D-dimer profiles, and minimal clot surface coverage, fibrinogen measurements were stable across the range of anticoagulation conditions evaluated in this stepwise titration study and do not support evidence of acute inflammatory or consumptive coagulopathy when interpreted in context rather than as an isolated parameter.

In addition to these hematologic and hemolysis indices, a full panel of coagulation, biochemical, and organ function parameters was monitored throughout the 7-day support period (Table 2). Renal function markers including creatinine (0.60–0.97 mg/dL) and BUN (8.23–19.78 mg/dL) remained within physiologic ranges, with transient early elevations consistent with post-cannulation adaptation rather than progressive dysfunction. Hepatic parameters demonstrated preserved organ integrity, with AST (73.94–127.10 U/L), ALT (11.17–17.50 U/L), and total bilirubin (0.10–0.16 mg/dL) remaining stable throughout the study period without clinically significant elevation. Total protein (5.04–7.34 g/dL) and albumin (2.33–3.18 g/dL) showed expected hemodilution following circuit priming but stabilized within normal ranges, confirming preserved hepatic synthetic capacity. Electrolyte homeostasis was maintained, with sodium, potassium, and calcium levels remaining within acceptable physiologic limits. Hematological parameters demonstrated initial hemodilution followed by stabilization, while WBC counts (1.37–8.75 × 103/μL) remained normal, suggesting absence of significant systemic inflammatory response. These data demonstrate that prolonged anticoagulant-free ECMO support was achieved without progressive organ dysfunction, metabolic derangement, or significant decline in InFlo MOBYBOX performance.

Discussion

Clinical Need for Anticoagulation-Free ECMO

Both thrombosis and bleeding represent major and interrelated complications of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), with their relative incidence and clinical impact varying by support modality, indication, and duration of therapy. Thrombotic events remain a central concern due to blood–material interactions and non-physiologic flow conditions within the extracorporeal circuit, necessitating systemic anticoagulation in most contemporary ECMO strategies [33]. However, systemic anticoagulation itself is strongly associated with bleeding complications, which contribute substantially to morbidity, limit procedural options, and often persist until ECMO discontinuation, particularly with prolonged support and higher anticoagulation targets. In addition, ECMO circuits alter drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, complicating the maintenance of stable therapeutic coagulation times and creating persistent risk of under- or overdosing anticoagulant [34–36]. Supporting this, several clinical studies have reported an association between therapeutic systemic anticoagulation and increased rates of oxygenator dysfunction, even in the presence of heparin-coated oxygenators. This observation has been hypothesized to relate to variability in anticoagulation intensity and the presence of anticoagulation-related coagulopathies, including disseminated intravascular coagulation and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, rather than a direct protective effect of anticoagulation on circuit patency [37–39]. Within earlier anticoagulated cohorts (group 1 and 2) of the present study, we observed that when systemic anticoagulant was administered (Supplementary Fig. 7), ACT values fluctuated over the 7-day period, whereas in the no-anticoagulant cohort (group 3), ACT returned to physiologic levels after cannulation and remained stable thereafter (Fig. 2). This underscores the fundamental challenge: despite increasing bleeding and transfusion requirements, anticoagulation therapy still does not reliably prevent oxygenator failure, even with heparin-coated surfaces, and may lead to other forms of coagulopathy given inherent dysregulation of the coagulation cascade.

Clinically, the feasibility of no-anticoagulant ECMO has often depended on maintaining sufficiently high flows, typically ≥ 3 L/min to reduce stasis and residence time within the extracorporeal circuit [40, 41]. In addition, reported anticoagulant-free strategies using currently available circuits frequently rely on limited support duration and careful patient selection, most commonly in acute, high bleeding-risk scenarios [14, 15]. However, these compensatory approaches are not always feasible, particularly in pediatric patients, flow-limited physiology, or cases requiring prolonged extracorporeal support, thereby constraining the generalizability of anticoagulant-free ECMO using conventional platforms [42–44]. These limitations show that incremental adjustments in anticoagulation practice are insufficient [16]. Device-level solutions are capable of addressing this need by preventing clot formation and maintaining gas-exchange efficiency without systemic anticoagulation. Prior efforts have been largely reactionary, using drug-eluting coatings, systemic anticoagulants, locally delivered agents such as nitric oxide, or high flow rates to manage the consequences of blood damage, yet these strategies remain incomplete and too downstream of the inherent insult [45–47].

Integrated Device-Level Solutions: Design Philosophy and Performance

Our system presented in this study represents a unique multimodal approach that addresses the source of thrombosis formation in ECMO circuits at its origin. We uniquely combine integrated improvements in flow dynamics, surface area reduction through the elimination of the heater–cooler fibers in the gas exchanger, and surface hemocompatibility [26, 27, 48]. In this study, we deliberately operated the InFlo MOBYBOX at lower flow rates (1.8–2.2 L/min) and with no systemic anticoagulant to demonstrate sustained, safe performance under stress conditions that are considered prothrombotic. This extreme highlights the antithrombogenic potential of the InFlo MOBYBOX with a broad clinical safety margin.

The InFlo MOBYBOX incorporates a pump and oxygenator geometry optimized through computational fluid dynamics to maintain uniform flow, minimize shear, and eliminate stagnation zones, thereby reducing hemolysis and platelet activation [26, 27, 48]. The compact, simplified circuit architecture minimizes total blood-foreign surface area, reducing opportunities for protein adsorption and contact activation. In the present study, all blood-contacting components were treated with a biopassive hydrophilic surface coating that enhances surface compatibility without relying on heparin or drug elution, thereby limiting early protein adsorption and platelet activation at the biomaterial interface. These design elements are consistent with the observed absence of clinically relevant hemolysis, and minimal thrombus formation during 7 days of anticoagulant-free support.

These preliminary results suggest that the InFlo MOBYBOX can sustain extracorporeal support in healthy animals under conditions typically considered to induce thrombosis, namely low-flow rates (1.8–2.2 L/min) without systemic anticoagulant, while maintaining circuit patency with no device-related adverse event and no organ injury. Other strategies, such as the addition of nitric oxide to the circuit, have likewise demonstrated success in reducing coagulation in extracorporeal systems operated without anticoagulation over the course of 72 h in an injured porcine model [49]. Residual thrombotic burden and end-organ pathology were reported by the authors, which could potentially be explained by the injured animal model. Given the differences between the study duration, extracorporeal circuit, and model selection, it is impossible to directly compare these results; however, these outcomes highlight the biochemical interventions as a highly promising means of mitigating thrombosis, but they do not fully address the hemodynamic and hemocompatibility stresses imposed by extracorporeal circulation. Further comparative research could elucidate the impact of these different approaches toward the elimination of thrombosis or downstream tissue effects in extracorporeal support systems. A combined approach incorporating all of these approaches may be able to further reduce the likelihood of coagulation [50].

Hemocompatibility and Cellular Preservation: Mechanisms of Protection

Other studies have shown that end-organ injury can also arise from immune-mediated pathways, initiated when circulating blood first contacts artificial circuit surfaces [51]. This blood–surface interaction activates neutrophils, complement, and platelets, leading to the release of reactive oxygen species, proteases, and neutrophil extracellular traps, which amplify coagulation, drive microvascular dysfunction, and propagate organ damage. Importantly, the present study evaluated the InFlo MOBYBOX under sustained low-flow conditions, a regime that is typically associated with increased hemolysis and blood trauma in conventional ECMO systems due to elevated localized shear stress, flow recirculation, and prolonged blood residence time [6, 8]. The InFlo MOBYBOX operates using a distinct pumping mechanism, positive-displacement membrane-based pump, compared to the centripetal pumps used in other ECMO circuits. This design reduces recirculation at low-flow rates [48] and sustains greater pumping efficiency compared to traditional pumps, which see significant efficiency loss at these low-flow rates [52]. Our findings confirmed previous results that demonstrate no sustained or progressive deviations across measured hematologic parameters, including WBC counts, platelet levels, and fibrinogen and D-dimer profiles at low-flow rates [26]. This indicates that the InFlo MOBYBOX prevented shear- and coagulation-driven injury and minimized surface-triggered immune activation with optimized flow dynamic design reducing shear and stasis, and with the hemocompatible coating that suppresses the immunogenic response normally triggered by blood-surface interaction.

Despite the stringent conditions of our study with sustained low-flow rates and no systemic anticoagulation, we observed only 2.3 ± 3.1% clot coverage across the first 20 layers of oxygenator fiber mats, which is among the lowest levels reported across ECMO investigations [53, 54]. Systematic reviews emphasize that while evolving anticoagulation strategies and coated circuits have improved ECMO management, thrombotic complications remain common [17]. Olson et al. in their systematic review reported a total incidence of thrombosis of 22.9% in 201 patients supported with ECMO without systemic anticoagulation for a median of 4.75 days. Thrombotic events were circuit-related in 13.4% of cases (mainly oxygenator thrombosis requiring exchange) and patient-related in 9.5%, with a predominance of arterial thrombosis in VA-ECMO [15].

Importantly, in our study, coagulation within the oxygenator was restricted to the central zone of the helical blood pathway, a region of the InFlo MOBYBOX geometry with inherently higher residence time. This observation underscores the critical role of both surface coating and flow pathway dynamics in thrombosis prevention and validates the design intent of the helical inlet and cylindrical fiber bundle, which homogenize velocity and shear profiles while minimizing stagnation zones. Despite this localized deposition, overall clot burden remained low and did not compromise circuit patency. As this was a preliminary range-finding study, a control was not performed to separate the impact of the coating and the flow pathway geometry. Further testing is necessary to elucidate the individual contributions of this combined method. Oxygenator gas-exchange performance did not degrade over the 7-day support period. In conventional ECMO systems, progressive fibrin deposition and pseudomembranous layer formation on fibers are well-documented drivers of declining gas-exchange performance, rising transmembrane pressure, and frequent need for urgent oxygenator exchange [55, 56]. These observations align with MOBYBOX’s design objective of mitigating oxygenator fouling and preserving gas-exchange performance over time. Given the procedural risks associated with circuit takedown and component exchange, as well as the cost of disposable oxygenators, strategies aimed at reducing oxygenator degradation may offer meaningful safety and health-economic benefits.

Equally significant was the absence of blood element damage. Hematocrit, red blood cell counts, and plasma-free hemoglobin levels all remained stable, confirming that the InFlo MOBYBOX does not induce hemolysis even under prolonged low-flow rate support. In contrast, centrifugal pumps routinely demonstrate measurable hemolysis, particularly at low-flow rates or high afterload conditions [57]. The positive-displacement pneumatic pump used in the InFlo MOBYBOX avoids these high-shear environments, while the streamlined helical flow path reduces turbulence and mechanical stress on blood cells. Collectively, these features enable extracorporeal support with minimized trauma to cellular elements while maintaining robust gas-exchange performance.

Complementing these intrinsic flow-based mechanisms, the proprietary biopassive hydrophilic coating provides an extrinsic layer of protection by minimizing protein adsorption, platelet adhesion, and inflammatory activation at the blood-biomaterial interface. The reinforced hemocompatibility is shown by the stability of platelet counts, fibrinogen, D-dimer, and white blood cell levels throughout the study. These indices, commonly perturbed during extracorporeal circulation due to surface activation and inflammatory signaling, remained within physiological ranges. Conventional ECMO circuits often show early and substantial platelet count reductions within the first 24–48 h, along with progressive fibrinogen consumption, and elevation in D-dimer or fibrin degradation products, reflecting ongoing activation of coagulation and fibrinolysis [58, 59]. The suppression of these responses in the InFlo MOBYBOX suggests that this extrinsic coating strategy, working in synergy with intrinsic flow optimization, may contribute to improved hemocompatibility, though further investigation is needed to confirm these observations.

Taken together, these preliminary findings suggest that the InFlo MOBYBOX may offer advantages over existing ECMO systems by expanding the therapeutic operating envelope. In this study, the device maintained patency at low-flow rates without systemic anticoagulation, preserved cellular and molecular blood integrity, and operated without device-related complications. While these results are encouraging with respect to device-level hemocompatibility, thrombosis risk, and immunogenicity, validation in clinical settings with larger sample sizes and diverse patient populations will be necessary to confirm these observations. If confirmed, this approach may represent a pathway toward safer, more accessible ECMO for high-risk patient populations, particularly those in whom bleeding complications currently limit the benefits of extracorporeal support.

Study Limitations

This study was designed as a preliminary feasibility and range-finding investigation and has several limitations. The sample size was limited (n = 4), and experiments were performed in a healthy female ovine model, which does not fully recapitulate the inflammatory, coagulopathic, potential sex differences, and hemodynamic complexity of critically ill human ECMO patients; species-specific differences in coagulation biology further limit direct clinical extrapolation. Device performance was evaluated under a single operating condition—prolonged low-flow support—without assessment across a broader range of flow regimes. In addition, no parallel control group using other ECMO circuits was included, as the study objective was to evaluate within-device feasibility rather than comparative performance. Additional comparative performance testing could provide a direct comparison to alternative approaches for coagulation reduction. Animals were euthanized at the study endpoint without post-decannulation survival or histologic assessment, limiting conclusions regarding delayed or subclinical effects. Finally, while the integrated system incorporates both optimized flow dynamics and a biopassive surface coating, the relative contribution of individual design elements cannot be isolated in the present study and warrants further investigation.
