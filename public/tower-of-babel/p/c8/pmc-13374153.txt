Ultra-processed food and risk of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Peiyi Zheng, Feifan Wang, Kaiyang Zhang, Jiayi Qian, Qiaoqiao Fang, Jing Guo, Jiayu Li, Ding Ye, Yingying Mao, Xiaohui Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsThe global rise in ultra-processed food (UPF) consumption has been linked to several chronic diseases. However, its association with cholecystitis and cholelithiasis remains underexplored.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 154,376 UK Biobank participants. UPF consumption (assessed as percentage of total food weight, g/day) was categorized by the NOVA classification using Oxford WebQ-based 24-hour dietary assessments. Cox proportional hazards models were performed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident cholecystitis and cholelithiasis risk with the adjustments for demographics, lifestyle factors, nutrients, and comorbidities. Restricted cubic splines analyses were used to evaluate dose-response relationships.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 13.2 years, higher UPF intake was associated with increased risks of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis. Participants in the highest quartile of UPF consumption (Q4) had higher risks of cholecystitis (HR:1.20, 95% CI: 1.07–1.34, P = 0.001) and cholelithiasis (HR:1.15, 95% CI: 1.05–1.25, P = 0.002), compared with the lowest quartile. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings. Nonlinear relationships were observed for both diseases, with risk increasing beyond specific UPF consumption thresholds (27% of total food weight for cholecystitis and 16% for cholelithiasis). Additionally, subgroup analyses revealed inverse associations of breakfast cereals with both cholelithiasis and cholecystitis, and of ultra-processed breads with cholelithiasis, whereas snacks and desserts were positively associated with cholelithiasis risk.ConclusionsHigher total UPF consumption was independently associated with elevated risks of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis, although inverse associations were observed for certain subgroups such as breakfast cereals and ultra-processed breads. However, reducing snacks and desserts may help lower biliary disease risk, but further studies are needed to clarify the role of specific UPF subgroups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01390-y.

Introduction

Cholelithiasis and cholecystitis are prevalent hepatobiliary disorders that pose a substantial burden on public health systems worldwide [1, 2]. In developed regions, cholelithiasis affects approximately 10–15% of the population [3], representing one of the most common gastrointestinal conditions. Beyond its high prevalence, cholelithiasis frequently leads to serious complications including cholecystitis, cholangitis, and gallbladder cancer [4]. Among these, cholecystitis is a leading cause of hospitalization in general surgery departments [5], highlighting the substantial clinical and economic burden of these interconnected hepatobiliary disorders.

Accumulating evidence has shown that several modifiable risk factors, such as smoking, alcohol drinking, and coffee consumption, contributed to an increased risk of cholelithiasis and cholecystitis [1, 6, 7]. Except for these factors, diets were also found to play a particularly critical role in disease pathogenesis [8–10]. Diets characterized by high intakes of refined sugars, confectionery, high-fructose corn syrup, and saturated fats, in conjunction with low dietary fiber and insufficient vitamin C intake, have been linked to a substantially elevated risk of cholelithiasis and cholecystitis [8–10]. These modifiable dietary determinants underscore potential avenues for targeted prevention strategies [8].

Food processing can profoundly influence diet quality. There is a broad spectrum of processing, ranging from minimal processing to ultra-processing [11]. According to the NOVA classification, UPF are typically produced using ingredients rarely used in home kitchens, contain additives, and are designed for extended shelf life [12]. These highly processed products are often rich in added sugars, fats, and additives, while lacking essential nutrients [13–15]. Recently, the consumption of UPF has significantly increased worldwide, prompting widespread concern regarding their potential health implications [16, 17]. Moreover, strong associations between UPF consumption and several adverse health outcomes have been identified [18]. However, the evidence on UPF consumption in relation to cholelithiasis and cholecystitis remains limited. To the best of our knowledge, only one prospective cohort study of 206,266 US adults reported that each additional daily serving of UPF raised risk of cholelithiasis by 2.8%, primarily driven by sugar-sweetened and artificially sweetened beverages, whereas whole-grain UPFs showed a protective effect [19]. However, this study has several limitations, including its relatively homogeneous sample of healthcare professionals and the limited generalizability of its findings [19]. Considering these limitations and the paucity of evidence on UPF consumption and cholecystitis, further research is warranted.

Leveraging the rich data in UK Biobank, we aimed to examine the associations of total UPF consumption and its subgroups with the incidence risks of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis. Understanding these relationships may inform dietary intervention to reduce the burden of biliary diseases.

Methods

Study population

We used data from UK Biobank (Project ID: 113769), which is one of the largest prospective cohort databases globally, designed to investigate genetic, environmental, and lifestyle determinants of common middle-aged and elderly diseases. The project recruited 500,000 UK volunteers aged 40–69 from 2006 to 2010, with a planned follow-up exceeding 30 years [20, 21]. The UK Biobank cohort profile has been thoroughly described in an earlier research paper [22]. In the present study, we collected data on personal demographics and dietary intake from UK Biobank and excluded individuals who withdrew (N = 233); implausible total energy intake (< 500 or > 5000 kcal/day; N = 291,761); participants at baseline if they had missing data on UPF intakes (N = 51,989); a diagnosis of cholecystitis or cholelithiasis (N = 2,808), or missing covariate data (N = 1,188). After exclusion, a total of 154,376 participants remained in the analytical cohort (Fig. 1). To evaluate the representiveness of our participants, we compared the baseline characteristics of participants included in our analysis with those excluded (Supplemental Table 1).

Assessment of ultra-processed food intakes

The UK Biobank’s Oxford WebQ included 206 food and 32 beverage items to assess 24-hour dietary intake [23]. We grouped foods into UPF using NOVA criteria [12]. As the Oxford WebQ did not include brand names or ingredient lists, foods were identified as UPF following the protocol of Chen et al. (2023) [24], which provided a validated list of UPF items and categorized them into six mutually exclusive subgroups following the classification framework of Rauber et al. (2021) [25]. In the present study, borderline items were classified based on the nature and intended purpose of processing as described in the NOVA system. The complete list of UPF items and their subgroup assignments is provided in Supplemental Table 2. We collected portion counts from participants, following standard size instructions, and averaged values across multiple dietary recalls. Participants completed up to five 24-hour dietary recalls (mean 2.18, range 1–5), and intakes of UPF were averaged across all available recalls to approximate habitual intake. Food weights were calculated by multiplying the portion counts by standard gram weights. UPF intake per item was averaged across all valid recalls, and total consumption was obtained by the summing intake across all items [24]. Consumption was expressed as the percentage of total food weight derived from UPF.

Determination of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis

The outcome of this study was the occurrence of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis, which was determined by linking to hospital inpatient records using ICD-10 codes K81, K83.0 [26, 27]. The follow-up period was defined as the time from the last dietary assessment to the earliest occurrence of cholecystitis or cholelithiasis diagnosis, loss to follow-up, death, or the end of the study period.

Assessment of covariates

Based on prior literature, potential confounding variables selected in the present study included age (years, continuous), sex (male, female), ethnicity (white, non-white, unknown), education level (college and above, high school and below, unknown), average total household income before tax (< £18,000, £18,000−£30,999, £31,000−£51,999, £52,000−£100,000, > £100,000 and unknown), body mass index (BMI, kg/m2, continuous), drinking status (never, current, previous), smoking status (never, current, previous), physical activity (regular and not regular), total intake of fiber (g/day, continuous), total intake of protein (g/day, continuous), total intake of fat (g/day, continuous), total intake of carbohydrate (g/day, continuous), total intake of sugars (g/day, continuous), total intake of cholesterol (g/day, continuous), total energy intake (kj/day, continuous) and baseline hypertension/ diabetes/ hyperlipidemia status (yes, no) [19, 28, 29]. Further information concerning the covariates is displayed in the Supplemental Table 3.

Statistical analysis

We calculated cumulative averages of UPF as primary exposure and used Cox proportional hazards regression model to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) according to quartiles of UPF consumption. Three models were utilized in this study for different covariate sets. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex, while Model 2 was further adjusted for ethnicity, education level, average total household income before tax, BMI, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity and total energy intake; Model 3 built upon Model 2 by additionally adjusting for hypertension/ diabetes/ hyperlipidemia status at baseline. To evaluate the exposure-response relationships of UPF consumption with cholecystitis and cholelithiasis risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with four knots were employed in the primary analysis. In secondary analysis, we categorized UPF into mutually exclusive subgroups to investigate whether the associations were driven by specific food subgroup.

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the robustness of the findings: (1) excluding the participants diagnosed with cholecystitis, or cholelithiasis within two years after enrollment; (2) replacing the exposure measurement of UPF consumption from weight% to energy percentage; (3) excluding the participants with a prior diagnosis of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or liver diseases at baseline [30–32]; (4) additionally adjusting for total dietary fiber intake in to assess whether dietary fiber confounded the observed associations; (5) adding all macronutrient as covariates (fiber, protein, fat, carbohydrates, and sugar) in the adjusted model.

Furthermore, we performed the stratified analysis by major risk factors, including age (< 55, ≥ 55 years old), sex (male, female), ethnicity (white, non-white), education level (college and above, high school and below/unknown), household income before tax (< £31000, ≥ £31000), BMI (< 28 kg/m2, ≥ 28 kg/m2), drinking status (current, never/previous), smoking status (current, never/previous) and physical activity (regular, not regular).

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1. All P values were two-sided, and a P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

Table 1 displays baseline characteristics of participants according to quartiles of UPF consumption by weight proportion (Q1 to Q4). The participants had a mean (± SD) age of 56.25 ± 7.95 years; 54.6% were female, and 95.1% were White. We observed that participants with higher UPF consumption tended to be younger, better educated, and had a lower BMI. They were more likely to drink alcohol and less likely to smoke, with 57.3% in Q4 of UPF consumption being never smokers. Regular physical activity was reported by 20.6% of participants in Q4, with the highest proportion observed in Q3 (21.0%). Average total household income before tax was also higher in Q4, with 29.6% of participants earning over £52,000 annually. Energy intake was higher in Q3 (8800.08 ± 2545.97 kJ/day) and Q4 (8779.91 ± 2257.45 kJ/day), accompanied by greater carbohydrate consumption.

UPF and risks of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis

During a median of 13.2 years of follow-up, we documented 2,917 cholecystitis cases and 4,875 cholelithiasis cases. Based on multivariable Cox regression, we observed elevated UPF consumption was correlated to higher risks of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis (Table 2). For cholecystitis, in the age and sex adjusted analysis, a positive association was observed with the weight proportion of UPF (treated as a continuous variable) (HR: 2.51, 95% CI: 2.00–3.16, P = 4.52 × 10− 15). After adjusting for additional variables in Models 2 and 3, this association remained significant (HR: 1.51, 95% CI: 1.20–1.90, P = 4.95 × 10− 4 in Model 2; HR: 1.50, 95% CI: 1.19–1.89, P = 5.34 × 10− 4 in Model 3). In the categorical analysis, participants in Q4 had higher risk of cholecystitis (HR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.07–1.34, P = 0.001, P trend = 0.009), compared with the participants in Q1 in the fully adjusted model (Table 2). A similar pattern was observed for the association between UPF and cholelithiasis. In the continuous analysis, a significant association was found in Model 1 (HR: 2.34, 95% CI: 1.96–2.81, P = 1.32 × 10− 20). This association decreased but remained statistically significant after adjustments in Models 2 (HR: 1.38, 95% CI: 1.16–1.66, P = 4.00 × 10− 4) and Model 3 (HR: 1.37, 95% CI: 1.14–1.64, P = 6.28 × 10− 4). In the categorical analysis, participants in Q4 had the highest risk of cholelithiasis (HR: 1.46, 95% CI: 1.34–1.59, P = 1.01 × 10− 17, P trend = 4.09 × 10− 17 in Model 1), with the association persisting in Model 2 (HR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.06–1.26, P = 0.001, P trend = 0.001) and 3 (HR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.05–1.25, P = 0.002, P trend = 0.002).

Sensitivity analyses showed the robustness of our findings. After we excluded the participants diagnosed with cholecystitis or cholelithiasis within two years after entering the study, the HRs for both conditions were consistent across all models (Supplemental Table 4). The association between UPF consumption and cholecystitis risk remained statistically significant across all models, with a comparable pattern observed for cholelithiasis. After excluding participants with a baseline diagnosis of IBD or liver disease, the HR estimates for cholecystitis and cholelithiasis remained nearly the same (Supplemental Table 5). Moreover, significant estimations remained when replacing weight-based consumption with energy consumption proportion (Supplemental Table 6). After additional adjustment for dietary fiber, and after further adjustment for all macronutrient covariates (fiber, protein, fat, carbohydrates, and sugar), as well as after removing total energy intake, the associations of total UPF with cholecystitis and cholelithiasis remained consistent (Supplemental Table 7).

Additionally, results of stratified analyses by age, sex, BMI, ethnicity, education level, household income, smoking status, drinking status and physical activity are summarized in Supplemental Tables 8–9. The positive association between UPF consumption and cholecystitis risk was more pronounced in participants aged ≥ 55 years, males, white participants, current drinkers, and physically inactive individuals, whereas no significant association was observed in their counterparts. For cholelithiasis, stronger associations were noted in males, White participants, current drinkers, and physically inactive individuals.

We further utilized RCS to investigate potential nonlinear relationships between UPF consumption and risk of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis. In the fully adjusted model, for cholecystitis, the RCS analysis indicated an initial flat-risk phase, with HRs remaining around 1(Fig. 2). A threshold effect was observed at approximately 27% (Fig. 2a), beyond which a positive correlation emerged (P non-linearity = 2.00 × 10− 4). Similarly, the risk for cholelithiasis remained neutral at low UPF consumption until reaching the turning point of approximately 16% (Fig. 2b). Post-threshold, a positive association manifested, supported by a P-value for non-linearity test of 3.00 × 10− 4.

UPF subgroups and risks of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis

In the analyses of UPF and its associations with the risk of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis, various UPF subgroups were examined across continuous and categorical variables. As shown in the Fig. 3, breakfast cereals exhibited inverse associations with risks of both cholecystitis (HR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.96–0.99, P = 0.002) and cholelithiasis (HR: 0.96, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99, P = 2.42 × 10− 4) in the fully adjusted models. Similarly, ultra-processed breads were found to be inversely associated with cholelithiasis risk (HR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–1.00, P = 0.005), but showed no significant association with cholecystitis risk (HR: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–1.01, P = 0.246). When analyzed by categories, similar trend for cholelithiasis was observed (P trend = 8.82 × 10− 4). In contrast, intake of snacks and desserts was associated with increased risks of cholelithiasis (HR: 1.01, 95% CI: 1.00-1.03, P = 0.007), while no significant association with cholecystitis was observed.

Discussion

This prospective cohort study provides compelling evidence that higher consumption of UPF is independently associated with increased risks of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis. In the subgroup analyses of UPF and their associations with cholecystitis and cholelithiasis risk, breakfast cereals and ultra-processed breads were inversely associated with the risk of both conditions, while snacks and desserts were linked to an increased risk of cholelithiasis.

Our findings were consistent with those reported by Uche-Anya et al., who demonstrated a positive association between UPF consumption and cholelithiasis risk in three large U.S. cohorts [19]. We observed a similar association between UPF consumption and cholelithiasis, and a further positive association with cholecystitis. The observed HRs were modest in magnitude, consistent with prior studies [19]; however, given that UPF consumption is highly prevalent in the population, even a small relative risk increase may contribute to a considerable attributable disease burden at the population level. Moreover, our study employed restricted RCS models to further evaluate nonlinear exposure–response relationships. Our RCS analysis showed that risks of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis increased after UPF consumption exceeded approximately 27% and 16% of total dietary weight, respectively. This suggested that the adverse effects of UPF on biliary health may not manifest until a certain level of consumption was reached. Notably, in stratified analyses, the positive associations between UPF consumption and risks of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis were present in both lower and higher BMI groups, with stronger effect estimates observed among participants with BMI < 28 kg/m². This finding suggests that UPF may act as a risk factor for biliary disease independent of obesity, underscoring the importance of dietary quality assessment even in populations traditionally perceived as low-risk. Potential mechanisms that have been proposed to link UPF consumption to adverse health outcomes included gut microbiota dysbiosis, metabolic disorders, and inflammation, which may be triggered by components such as emulsifiers, artificial sweeteners, preservatives, and nanoparticles [19, 32, 33]. These disrupted beneficial bacteria may further lead to oxidative stress, NLRP3 activation [13, 32], and metabolic endotoxemia [34], ultimately causing cholecystitis and cholelithiasis.

For the subgroups of UPF, we and Uche-Anya et al. both found a positive association of snacks and desserts with cholelithiasis risk [19]. Snacks and desserts, typically laden with refined sugars, trans fats, and additives like high-fructose corn syrup [35], may promote cholesterol crystallization and gallbladder hypomotility [19, 36, 37]. Furthermore, our study found a significant protective effect of breakfast cereals and ultra-processed breads on cholelithiasis risk. Similarly, Uche-Anya et al. also reported a protective effect specifically of dark/whole-grain UPF breads [19]. This highlighted that fortified breakfast cereals may be a particularly important beneficial subgroup of UPF. Breakfast cereals, enriched with fiber, vitamins, and minerals, may counteract detrimental effects through enhancing bile acid binding and improving lipid metabolism [38, 39].

Current research on the relationship between cholecystitis and UPF remains limited. Prior studies indicated that dietary habits played a critical role in the development and progression of cholecystitis [40]. It is well-documented that high-fat diet and spicy foods can trigger gallbladder contraction, potentially leading to symptomatic episodes of cholecystitis [41–43]. Moreover, high-cholesterol foods have been identified as contributors to cholesterol crystal formation, which can exacerbate gallbladder burden and promote gallstone formation, a common cause of cholecystitis [44, 45]. In addition, ingredients commonly found in UPF, such as high levels of sodium, preservatives, and artificial additives may disturb the gut-liver axis and alter bile acid metabolism [46, 47]. However, further studies are warranted to validate our observational studies.

Our study benefits from the UK Biobank’s prospective design and extensive covariate adjustment. However, there are several limitations. First, dietary intake was assessed using the Oxford WebQ, a structured online 24-hour dietary questionnaire. Nevertheless, previous studies have investigated the reliability and internal consistency of the Oxford WebQ questionnaire and reported that nutrient intakes calculated from this questionnaire correlate well with nutrient biomarkers measured from 24-hour urine samples [48]. Additionally, due to limited food processing information, misclassification of a few food items may occur, and we have assigned them to the most probable food group based on published findings of common foods and drinks in the UK. Moreover, the exclusion of 291,761 participants with missing dietary data may have introduced selection bias limiting the generalizability to more diverse or less healthy populations. Third, there may be residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as detailed dietary patterns. Fourth, causality cannot be inferred from this observational design, and experimental studies are needed to validate the proposed mechanisms. Future research should include longitudinal studies that track interventions aimed at reducing ultra-processed food consumption. Biomarker-based investigations, including bile composition and inflammatory cytokines, are also necessary to clarify the causal pathways.

Conclusion

In summary, increased consumption of UPF is associated with an increased risk of cholecystitis and cholelithiasis. Reducing consumption of snacks and desserts may help lower the incidence of these biliary diseases. However, the inverse associations observed for breakfast cereals and ultra-processed breads were not robust after controlling for dietary fiber, suggesting that these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Coupled with existing population-based evidence, we suggested that public health policies should prioritize subtype-specific recommendations over broad prohibitions against processed foods, leveraging fortification as a pragmatic strategy to attenuate processing-related harms.
