Telehealth Use During the COVID-19 Pandemic: A Case Study at Dialysis Centers in Washington, United States
Authors: Anawin Sanguankeo, Charlene Desir, Phoom Narongkiatikhun, Kamilah Thomas-Purcell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationale & ObjectiveTelehealth has notably surged since the emergence of the coronavirus 2019 pandemic. In nephrology, it has become crucial in delivering care to patients treated with dialysis, especially after the removal of previous restrictions that limited its application in dialysis settings. The objective of this study is to examine the perceptions of telehealth held by patients treated with dialysis and nephrologists.Study DesignA qualitative case study.Setting & ParticipantsThe case in this study is the telehealth utilization process for dialysis care within dialysis centers in Washington State, USA. Participants were patients treated with in-center hemodialysis and home dialysis and nephrologists who have experienced telehealth in dialysis care.ExposureThe use of telehealth.OutcomesPerceptions, decision-making process, challenges, and the adaptation strategies employed when using telehealth in a dialysis setting.Analytical ApproachData collection involved in-depth interviews with nephrologists and patients treated with dialysis, direct observation of telehealth usage during dialysis care, and analysis of documents from dialysis companies. Data analysis and interpretation were conducted through coding, categorization, and thematic analysis.ResultsThe participants included 12 patients treated with dialysis and 6 nephrologists from 2 dialysis centers. Six themes with subthemes emerged from the analysis, including telehealth acceptance (the ability to see more patients, comparison to in-person visits), telehealth rejection (the lack of personal connection and privacy, the indispensable role of nephrologists), the nephrologist’s role in decision-making, challenges with telehealth connectivity affecting dialysis visits, the unsupportive role of dialysis companies, and adaptation to technological challenges.LimitationsPatients from only one dialysis center participated in this study.ConclusionsTelehealth shows potential to complement traditional dialysis care by enhancing accessibility and patient monitoring. More research is needed to assess the broader impact of telehealth on patient outcomes and health care efficiency in dialysis management.Index Words: Telehealth, Telemedicine, Dialysis, End-stage Kidney Disease, Case Study Research, Qualitative Research

Telehealth is defined as the use of telecommunications and information technology to provide access to health assessment, diagnosis, intervention, consultation, supervision, and information across distance.1 Over the past decade, telehealth has seen increasing use by physicians and patients.2 Many medical fields use telehealth to communicate with patients in rural areas and monitor patient status from a distance.3 The coronavirus 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has further amplified the use of telehealth to avoid close contact and the need to visit the office.4 The application of telehealth allows patients to receive care without having to leave their homes.

Patients treated with dialysis face a higher risk of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 infection exposure due to their need for regular dialysis treatments. A viable strategy to mitigate COVID-19 transmission among patients treated with dialysis is to permit nephrologists to use telehealth for outpatient dialysis visits.5 In the United States, telehealth use for in-center hemodialysis peaked early in the pandemic but later declined.6 The benefits of telehealth for patients treated with dialysis include a reduced risk of exposure, saved transportation time and costs, and increased use of remote patient monitoring.5 However, the use of telehealth for patients treated with dialysis also presents challenges, including limitations in physical examinations, health technology literacy, and concerns related to privacy and security.7

The purpose of this case study research is to explore the perceptions and the process of how nephrologists and patients receiving dialysis use telehealth in both in-center hemodialysis and home dialysis settings during the COVID-19 pandemic. Given that dialysis involves monitoring, examining, and assessing the patient’s status by a multidisciplinary team, it is beneficial to investigate how telehealth can be effectively employed in such settings to enhance the telehealth experience.

Methods

Theoretical Framework

This study used a case study research design.8 Case study research is the most suitable qualitative approach for this study because the research questions in this study revolve around the perceptions, decision-making processes, and challenges of nephrologists and patients in dialysis settings. For this study, the case was telehealth use in dialysis care at dialysis centers in Washington State during the period after the Blanket Waivers guidance was issued by the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services on March 17, 2020.9

Participant Selection

The study used criterion-based case selection, a purposeful sampling method, to recruit participants. In this study, participants needed to have experience with telehealth for dialysis care at least once during the COVID-19 pandemic. To recruit participants, the investigator (AS) sent signed invitation letters to patients receiving dialysis via clinic managers, who distributed them at dialysis treatment stations. AS is a male nephrologist with clinical privileges at dialysis centers affiliated with DaVita and Fresenius Kidney Care.10,11 Nephrologists were recruited by obtaining a list from medical directors, followed by sending invitation emails to potential participants. Informed consent was obtained from all who expressed interest. Exclusion criteria included individuals who had not used telehealth during the pandemic or had other logistical barriers to participation. The sample size was deemed sufficient to generate meaningful themes for the study.

The protocol of this study was reviewed and exempted by the institutional review board of Nova Southeastern University and PeaceHealth System. The researcher was also permitted to access and conduct the research study at the selected dialysis centers.

Setting

Participant recruitment and data collection took place at 2 anonymous dialysis centers, where patients either received at-home or in-center dialysis. Interviews were conducted in private rooms at the dialysis centers. Audio recordings of the interviews were transcribed by a HIPAA-compliant service, and participants were asked to review the transcripts for accuracy within 2 weeks. For participant observation, the investigator coordinated with nephrologists to be informed of telehealth appointments and sought permission to observe. The researcher took a nonparticipant role, recording field notes following an observation protocol without engaging in the telehealth interactions. Document analysis focused on publicly available materials related to telehealth in dialysis care from the dialysis companies’ websites and other sources.

Data Collection

Interview Questions

The interview guide was developed to address the research questions concerning the perceptions, processes, and challenges of telehealth use during the COVID-19 pandemic among participating patients receiving dialysis and nephrologists. The investigator used open-ended interview questions separately for patients treated with dialysis and nephrologists (Item S1-S4). Pilot testing of the interview questions was conducted before the actual interviews with the participants.

Observations and Field Notes

For participant observation, the researcher instructed the participating nephrologist to inform them of any telehealth appointments between the nephrologist and patients treated with dialysis at the dialysis center. The researcher sought permission from the dialysis center to access the center, obtained consent from both the nephrologist and patients treated with dialysis, and directly observed the use of telehealth for dialysis care at the dialysis center. Field notes were diligently recorded by the researcher during participant observation while the nephrologist and patients treated with dialysis were engaged in telehealth for dialysis care. An observational protocol was employed for recording both descriptive and reflective notes (Item S5). Audio or video recording was not used during the observation.

Document Analysis

Publicly available documents and policies regarding telehealth use in dialysis care were obtained. Subsequently, the researcher meticulously reviewed the retrieved documents, searching for information pertinent to the research questions. Following this, the researcher summarized the key findings of the document and included any crucial missing information.

Data Analysis

The analysis of the case study was employed to understand the complexity of the case.12 The investigator thoroughly reviewed the interview transcripts, field notes, and notes from document analysis while making personal notes using analytic memos. Pseudonyms were used to protect participant identities.

Coding

After reviewing the transcripts and notes, the investigator conducted coding of the data using an inductive method to develop a pattern of meaning from the data.12 After applying coding to the initial 5 interview transcripts, the investigator began using a combination of inductive and deductive methods by applying existing codes to the remaining transcripts and the field notes. The step of data analysis started once the interview transcripts and field notes were available, without waiting for the completion of data collection.

Category Scheme and Thematic Analysis

After the generation of several codes, categorization was carried out. Categories were then compared with each other, identifying similarities and contrasting ideas within them. After categorizing the data, themes were developed based on the research questions, using codes, categories, memos, and incorporating exemplar quotes from the transcripts. To facilitate the data management, coding, categorization, and pattern development process, the investigator employed MAXQDA 2022,13 a qualitative computer software program. To establish credibility and improve rigor, this research study employed triangulation of data sources14 by collecting data from multiple sources, including participant interviews, document reviews, and direct observations. In addition, member checking was used by presenting data and interpretations to the participants to confirm the accuracy of raw data and interpretations of categories and themes. Ultimately, the investigator incorporated the participants’ comments into the final findings.14 Externally trained transcriptionists were used to ensure the accuracy of the interview transcripts.

Results

Participants included 6 nephrologists and 12 patients receiving dialysis. Eight patients and 3 nephrologists from Center A, located in a rural area, and 3 nephrologists from Center B, located in a suburban area (Table S1-S2), responded to the research study invitation and were deemed eligible to participate in the interviews. The interviews were conducted from August 2023 and November 2023 with no specific order between nephrologists and patients. Document analysis was conducted in December 2023 using documents obtained from publicly available sources related to telehealth use during the COVID-19 pandemic at 2 dialysis companies (Table S3).

Direct observation occurred on November 6, 2023, at Center A. Four patients met the criteria for observation, consisting of 2 men and 2 women aged 37, 57, 58, and 67 years old. Two patients were excluded from the observation because the investigator served was their primary nephrologist. Center A had 22 dialysis chairs, but it was not at full treatment capacity during the observation period. The chairs were positioned approximately 4 feet apart. Many patients wore headphones and watched personal screens attached to the chairs. Dialysis nurse, equipped with a laptop computer and a speaker on a movable desk, conducted telehealth rounds. The nurse had already logged into Microsoft Teams, the platform used for telehealth with patients treated with dialysis at Center A, using the invitation link sent to Dr Cindy’s email. When the scheduled time arrived, Dr Cindy joined the meeting, appearing on the laptop screen. The nurse moved the computer and speaker to each patient designated for telehealth visits, positioning them beside the patient. The duration of each telehealth visit varied between 2 and 5 minutes.

Factors related to telehealth use at dialysis centers.

Theme 1: Telehealth Acceptance

Sub-Theme 1: The Ability to See More Patients

Nephrologists perceived telehealth as an acceptable tool for use with patients treated with dialysis. They believed that telehealth allowed them to see more patients treated with dialysis compared to not using telehealth. By using telehealth, nephrologists could see patients treated with in-center hemodialysis (ICHD) without the need to travel to dialysis centers. The perceived benefits of telehealth in accommodating additional patients receiving dialysis were further substantiated by patients treated with dialysis.

Sub-Theme 2: Comparison to In-Person Visits

Some nephrologists and patients treated with dialysis believed that telehealth visits were comparable to or better than in-person visits. Among nephrologists who accepted the use of telehealth for dialysis care, they did not see much difference in the quality of care provided to their patients receiving dialysis. The nephrologists could still visibly see their patients, talk to them, and address their concerns via telehealth, whereas patients treated with dialysis continued to receive assessments by dialysis nurses.

Theme 2: Telehealth Rejection

Sub-Theme 3: The Lack of Personal Connection and Privacy

Both nephrologists and patients treated with ICHD felt a loss in the close connection that they usually experienced during in-person visits. Dr Jose explained one of the reasons why the connection between nephrologists and patients was weaker during telehealth visits. Some patients only disclosed their concerns to their primary nephrologists in person and not to other nephrologists or during telehealth encounters. For instance, during an in-person visit, one of his patients shared her depressive symptoms, allowing Dr Jose to comprehend the complete picture of her situation. He doubted that the patient would discuss her depression during a telehealth session. Dr Jose emphasized the need to cultivate the nephrologist-patient relationship, expressing concern that this connection may be lost in telehealth visits.

Lack of patient privacy emerged as another major concern shared by patients treated with ICHD and nephrologists, as evident through direct observation and document review. The communication dynamics between patients treated with dialysis and nephrologists differed in telehealth, as patients needed to speak louder during these sessions. Through direct observation, the investigator noticed that speaker volumes were often so high that nearby patients could overhear conversations between the nephrologists and patients. There are sensitive issues that patients prefer to discuss in person rather than via telehealth.

Sub-Theme 4: The Indispensable Role of Nephrologists

With telehealth visits, there was a lack of real-time input from the multidisciplinary team, including patient care technicians, social workers, and dietitians, which typically provides essential information to nephrologists under conventional dialysis care. Nephrologists were also dissatisfied with the inability to physically assess patients treated with dialysis, as they could during in-person visits. As a result, some nephrologists believed that telehealth visits were inappropriate for emergency situations or in situations where an in-person visit could not be conducted.

Theme 3: The Nephrologist’s Role in Decision-Making

The complexity of patients treated with dialysis was a major factor that affected how nephrologists decided regarding the use of telehealth. Nephrologists were aware that patients receiving dialysis were complex, and their conditions could be stable or unstable during dialysis. Stable patients, such as patients treated with peritoneal dialysis who had all the necessary data, like laboratory results or blood pressure readings, for nephrologists, were considered appropriate cases that could be seen using telehealth. If issues arose during telehealth visits, such as when the patient had shortness of breath or spoke uncomfortably, the patient would be brought in for an in-person examination. The lack of physical examination in stable patients treated with dialysis did not prevent nephrologists from using telehealth.

For patients treated with ICHD whose cases could be complex and emergency situations could arise during dialysis, most nephrologists preferred in-person visits at least once a month. Even when COVID-19 cases were high and telehealth was available for dialysis visits, nephrologists still preferred to see patients at least once a month to gain a comprehensive understanding of their patients. This included aspects not observed by dialysis nurses or aspects that patients did not convey through telehealth. This preference extended to patients treated with dialysis who were comfortable with telehealth but still desired monthly in-person visits with their nephrologists.

Theme 4: Challenges with Telehealth Connectivity Affecting Dialysis Visits

One of the challenges that nephrologists and patients treated with dialysis reported facing while using telehealth was the interruption of video and audio functions, preventing a smooth telehealth visit. The problems associated with telehealth included logging into the telehealth platform, connection interruptions, and audiovisual challenges. The logging-in issue occurred for some elderly patients treated with home dialysis who lacked technological experience and did not know how to log in for telehealth visits. Nephrologists also faced logging-in issues when they did not receive a link for telehealth visits from dialysis nurses.

Theme 5: The Unsupportive Role of Dialysis Companies

Dialysis companies needed to provide better support for telehealth use between nephrologists and patients receiving dialysis. In the document review, dialysis centers supported the use of telehealth for both ICHD and home dialysis by preparing and training the staff at dialysis centers, and providing technological support when issues arose. However, nephrologists did not perceive much support from dialysis companies. For instance, there was a lack of communication between the dialysis company and the nephrologists during the early period of the pandemic. One nephrologist was unaware that she was supposed to use only telehealth visits for dialysis rounds until she arrived at the dialysis center and was told by its staff that in-person visits were not allowed due to the pandemic. In addition, nephrologists complained about the need to improve technology during telehealth visits, the inadequate experience of dialysis staff in conducting telehealth rounds, the lack of technician support, and the inconsistency of telehealth policies among dialysis centers.

Theme 6: Adaptation to Technological Challenges

Nephrologists had to adapt to technological challenges while using telehealth. From document analysis, the dialysis company detailed the availability of live support agents and informative technology experts. However, these resources were not accessible to the nephrologist participants in this case study. In instances of connection interruptions or video/audio failures, nephrologists commonly addressed the issue by switching from telehealth to audio calls and engaging with patients over the telephone.

Discussion

This study explores the use of telehealth in dialysis care, focusing on the perspectives of nephrologists and patients treated with dialysis during the COVID-19 pandemic. The research sought to understand how telehealth was perceived, the decision-making processes involved in the use of telehealth, the challenges faced, and the strategies used to overcome these challenges. Key perceptions included both acceptance and rejection of telehealth, with some seeing it as a valuable alternative to in-person visits during the pandemic, while others highlighted concerns about the lack of physical assessment and patient privacy. Challenges encountered included technological issues with telehealth platforms, communication breakdowns with dialysis companies, and concerns about the appropriateness of telehealth for complex cases. Nephrologists were generally the primary decision-makers regarding telehealth use, influenced by factors like company policies and patient complexity. Although telehealth was often viewed as beneficial, especially during COVID-19 restrictions, some nephrologists and patients preferred in-person care for certain cases. Adaptations to telehealth challenges included shifting to phone consultations, though these were viewed as less effective for dialysis care.

The findings from this study align with existing literature on telehealth use by nephrologists for patients with kidney disease and provide additional context not addressed in the literature. Common themes shared between this case study and existing literature include the support for telehealth use,15 comparison with in-person visits,15,16 the role of telehealth as complementary to in-person visits,16,17 patient privacy concerns,18,19 and concerns that nephrologists are unable to conduct hands-on examinations while using telehealth.19, 20, 21

This case study introduces telehealth acceptance, illustrating how nephrologists can see more patients receiving dialysis treatment at dialysis centers. One motivation for nephrologists to see in-center patients treated with dialysis is the monthly capitation payment model, where the payment amount is based on the number of visits provided to patients treated with dialysis each month.22 While cost savings from transportation is a theme found in existing literature,15 nephrologists in this case study believe that using telehealth saves them time on commuting and eliminates the need to drive to dialysis centers.

Some of the themes uncovered in this study differ from those developed in existing literature. Our findings highlight privacy concerns and technological challenges faced by both nephrologists and patients treated with dialysis. In contrast, a survey of patients treated with hemodialysis reported that most did not express concerns about privacy or technical issues.23 Although other qualitative research focused on themes such as an increase in availability of the physician, safeguards against infection, confidence in the dialysis procedure, and a decrease in concerns about patients treated with dialysis,16,19 the primary concerns among nephrologists in this case study revolve around the irreplaceable role of nephrologists. They emphasize that dialysis nurses have different assessments of patients treated with dialysis, and these differences may lead to adverse events when telehealth is used. Furthermore, nephrologists emerge as the main decision-makers regarding the use of telehealth for dialysis care, considering several factors before choosing telehealth over in-person visits.

The findings of this study highlight the need for additional education and training in telehealth for nephrologists, dialysis nurses, and patients. Nephrologists should be trained on how to effectively integrate telehealth into patient care, while dialysis nurses need to be equipped to assess patients in the absence of nephrologists. Similarly, patients should be educated on the telehealth process, including troubleshooting technical issues. In practice, these findings can guide the development of telehealth protocols to standardize care, addressing challenges such as technical issues, privacy concerns, and lack of communication. For research, future studies could assess the effectiveness of telehealth compared to in-person care while exploring perceptions of all stakeholders involved. In addition, policymakers may consider maintaining telehealth as a complement to in-person visits after pandemic, regulating its use to prevent system abuse while leveraging its benefits in the dialysis setting.

The study has limitations, primarily in participant recruitment, direct observation, and data analysis. All participants were individuals that responded to the researcher's invitation to participate in the study. This may potentially introduce selection bias, limiting insights to participants with certain characteristics, experiences, or views. Moreover, the sample size was small, and only patients receiving dialysis from one center participated, limiting diversity in perspectives and experiences. For future studies, the investigator suggests recruiting a broader range of participants from different settings. In the direct observation, only one nephrologist participated, restricting insights into how telehealth was used by others during dialysis care. For future research, it would be beneficial to observe various nephrologists using telehealth with different types of patients receiving dialysis at both originating and distant sites. Finally, the qualitative data analysis was conducted solely by one researcher, potentially introducing bias due to their experience as a nephrologist. The investigator recommends involving individuals from diverse backgrounds in the analysis process to enhance the interpretation of data in future studies.

Conclusion

This study offers insights into the perspectives of nephrologists and patients treated with dialysis. Telehealth is viewed as a valuable tool, enabling nephrologists to extend care beyond traditional, in-person dialysis services for medically stable patients. However, the use of telehealth presents challenges for both nephrologists and patients receiving dialysis, necessitating adaptation and support from dialysis nurses and companies.
