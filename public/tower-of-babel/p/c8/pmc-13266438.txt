Statin Therapy for Primary Prevention and Clinical Outcomes in Adults Aged 80 and Older: A Retrospective Comparative Cohort Study
Authors: Ophir Lavon, Wafaa Hamodi, Sameer Kassem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEvidence supporting the use of statins for primary prevention of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in individuals aged ≥ 80 years remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the long‐term clinical benefits and safety of statins for primary prevention in patients aged 80 years and older.MethodsWe conducted a population‐based retrospective cohort study using electronic medical records and pharmacy dispensing data from Clalit Health Services in Israel, covering the period from January 2015 to December 2020. Patients aged ≥ 80 years without prior CVD who were persistent statin users were compared with similar patients not receiving statins. Exclusions included prior CVD, dialysis, or death within 1 year of follow‐up. Outcomes included all‐cause mortality, new coronary events, myopathy, dementia, and diabetes mellitus. Cox proportional hazards models, adjusted for potential confounders, were used to assess the association between statin use and clinical outcomes.ResultsAmong 15,745 patients (mean age 84.5 years; 66% female), 8413 were statin users. Over a 4‐year mean follow‐up, statin use was associated with a 31% reduction in mortality (HR 0.69; 95% CI: 0.34–0.74; p < 0.001) and a 20% reduction in new coronary events (HR 0.80; 95% CI: 0.68–0.94; p = 0.008). No significant differences were observed in the incidence of myopathy, diabetes, or dementia. Benefits were not observed in patients who discontinued statins before age 80.ConclusionsIn patients aged ≥ 80 years, statin therapy for primary prevention was associated with reduced all‐cause mortality and coronary morbidity, without increased risk of adverse events. Early discontinuation diminished these benefits.

Introduction

Statin therapy is recommended by the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) for the primary prevention of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in adults aged 40–75 years who have at least one CVD risk factor and an elevated 10‐year risk of a cardiovascular event [1]. For individuals older than 75 years, the USPSTF has concluded that current evidence is insufficient to assess the balance of benefits and harms of statin therapy for primary prevention. Although recent studies suggest potential benefits of statins in older adults, the evidence remains inconclusive [2, 3, 4, 5]. Data regarding the effectiveness and safety of statins for primary prevention in individuals over the age of 75—and particularly those aged 80 and older—are especially limited. The present study aimed to evaluate the clinical benefits of statin therapy for primary prevention in patients aged 80 years and above.

Methods

This was a population‐based, historical cohort study using data from the electronic medical records and the medication dispensing database of the Haifa and Western Galilee District and The Carmel Medical Center, both affiliated to CLALIT, the largest health maintenance organization in Israel (providing medical care to 4.6 million people), from the period of January 1, 2015 to December 31, 2020. The Institutional Review Board of The Carmel Medical Center approved the study. Due to the retrospective nature of the study and anonymization of the collected data, informed consent was waived.

Elderly individuals aged 80 and above who were continuing users of statins were compared to similar elderly patients not currently using statins. Nonstatin control group (never users) included patients who had no record of any dispensing of statin medications in the CLALIT databases. Those who died within 1 year after the study began had a history of cardiovascular events or were undergoing dialysis were excluded. All patients entered the study at the age of 80 as unexposed. A participant became “exposed” after 3 months of statin coverage (per dispensing record). This time‐varying exposure approach was used to mitigate the immortal time bias. Outcomes included all‐cause mortality and new diagnoses of coronary events, myopathy, dementia, or diabetes mellitus. Outcome data were retrieved based on ICD‐10 codes. For each patient, a comprehensive dataset of variables was collected, including age, sex, socio‐economic status, smoking status, chronic medications, and certain comorbidities.

Comprehensive statistical analysis was performed. Continuous variables were reported as means ± SDs, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Population characteristics were described across two groups: statin‐treated patients and those not treated. The associations between statin use and the collected variables were assessed using a t‐test or Mann–Whitney test for continuous variables, and a Chi‐square test for categorical variables. The incidence rate of the evaluated outcomes per 10,000 patient‐years was calculated. The medication possession ratio (MPR) assessed compliance. The continuing users of statins were divided into two groups of high (≥ 80% PMR) and low (< 80% PMR). A univariate Cox proportional hazards model evaluated the safety and effectiveness of statins, and multivariate models were applied to adjust for the independent effects of statins. The variables used for the multivariable analysis are presented in the Supporting Information. All tests were two‐sided. The value p < 0.05 was considered significant. E‐value sensitivity analysis was completed. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

The study included 15,745 patients, of whom 8413 were regularly treated with statins. The average age was 83.7 and 85.5 years for statin‐treated and nontreated patients, respectively. Approximately two‐thirds were female. Table 1 presents the patients' characteristics. 39.5% of the control group had been treated with statins before turning 80. Most statin‐treated patients started treatment before the age of 80 (95.2%). The follow‐up period averaged 4 years per patient.

Mortality among statin‐treated patients was significantly lower, with a 31% relative reduction: 1683 deaths (20%) occurred in the statin group compared to 2739 deaths (37%) in the control group (unadjusted HR = 0.47, unadjusted HR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.34–0.74; p < 0.001). The corresponding survival curve is shown in Figure 1. Among patients receiving statins for primary prevention, the risk of new coronary events was reduced by 20% (HR = 0.80; 95% CI: 0.68–0.94; p = 0.008). The incidence of new coronary events was 167 per 10,000 patient‐years in the statin group, compared to 174 per 10,000 patient‐years in the nonstatin group. The E‐value for the observed HR is 2.26 and for the CI limit closest to the null is 2.04.

In a separate analysis comparing patients who discontinued statin use before age 80 (n = 2898) with those who continued or started treatment beyond age 80, ongoing statin therapy was associated with a 25% reduction in coronary event incidence (HR = 0.72; 95% CI: 0.64–0.89; p < 0.001).

The association was examined across adherence strata based on MPR and found directionally consistent: compared with nonusers, adjusted mortality HRs were 0.58 among statin users with MPR ≥ 80% and 0.74 among statin users with MPR < 80%.

There were no statistically significant differences between groups in the incidence of new‐onset myopathy (HR = 1.10; 95% CI: 0.94–1.13; p = 0.12), diabetes mellitus (HR = 0.89; 95% CI: 0.73–1.09; p = 0.26), or dementia (HR = 1.12; 95% CI: 0.98–1.27; p = 0.09). Table 2 presents the outcomes.

Discussion

In the present study, statin therapy in patients aged ≥ 80 years was associated with a significant improvement in survival, with approximately a 30% relative reduction in all‐cause mortality over a mean follow‐up of 4 years. A reduction in cardiovascular disease (CVD) incidence was also observed in statin‐treated patients. These benefits were not maintained in patients who had discontinued statin therapy before the age of 80. Importantly, no statistically significant increase was found in the incidence of adverse outcomes such as myopathy, diabetes mellitus, or dementia.

Evidence supporting the use of statins for primary prevention in very elderly populations remains limited. A literature review by Ruscica et al. concluded that statin therapy for primary prevention may be inappropriate in the very old, citing frailty, risk of adverse reactions, and limited benefit from non‐HDL cholesterol reduction [2].

A French cohort study evaluated the effect of statins for primary prevention in patients over 75 years of age over a follow‐up period of 2.4 years. A 33% increase in cardiovascular events was found among those who discontinued statins early. However, the authors could not confirm a benefit for patients over the age of 80 years [3].

Ramos et al. found no benefit from primary prevention with statins regarding mortality or CVD incidence in patients over 75 years without diabetes, and a waning benefit in diabetic patients over 85 [4].

Conversely, a meta‐analysis by Gencer et al. showed reduced CVD incidence in patients > 75 without an increase in hemorrhagic stroke, diabetes, or cognitive decline [5]. Similarly and more recently, Xu et al. reported CVD risk reduction in adults older than 75 years who were treated with statins for primary prevention, without increased myopathy or liver dysfunction [6].

In contrast, Zhou et al., analyzing data from the ASPREE (Aspirin in Reducing Events in the Elderly) trial, found no improvement in disability‐free survival in statin users aged ≥ 70 years [7].

Mesidor and colleagues conducted an emulation of a hypothetical randomized trial involving adults over 65 years of age with no prior history of CVD and no statin use in the preceding year, using Quebec's population‐wide administrative data. They reported reduced mortality but increased coronary event rates with statins, limiting definitive conclusions [8].

The present study provides support for the argument that statin treatment improves clinical outcomes even in patients older than 80 years of age.

This study has limitations. Its retrospective design precluded independent data validation, and all data were derived from a single geographic region. Residual confounding, including confounding by indication, cannot be excluded. Immortal time bias and selection bias, while partially mitigated using the time‐varying exposure approach, are also relevant. Cardiovascular mortality outcomes could not be validated and were not included. Formal residual‐based diagnostics were not available. Dementia outcomes in very old adults are particularly sensitive to competing mortality: participants who die cannot later receive a dementia diagnosis, and differential survival can bias dementia incidence comparisons. Nonetheless, the study's strengths include a large cohort, extended follow‐up, and high‐quality data from integrated medical records and pharmacy dispensation, supporting the robustness of the findings. The relatively high mortality rate (28% in the whole cohort) makes the multivariable hazard ratio estimate unlikely to be driven by a small number of influential observations. In addition, crude mortality rates were directionally consistent with the adjusted results. The survival curve shows separation without marked crossing, which does not suggest strong nonproportionality. To reduce bias from near‐term terminal illness and associated deprescribing, individuals who died within the first year of follow‐up were excluded. The separated analysis of prior statin users and never users demonstrated that associations for continuing statin users remained directionally consistent, while estimates for prior users were attenuated relative to continuing users. This consistency across adherence levels reduces concern that the finding is an artifact of a single exposure definition or driven by extreme observations. The calculated E‐value indicates that only a relatively strong unmeasured confounder would be required to fully explain away the association.

In conclusion, statin therapy for primary prevention in patients over 80 years of age was associated with improved all‐cause mortality and a reduction in coronary morbidity, without a significant increase in the incidence of myopathy, dementia, or diabetes. The observed benefits were attenuated when statin therapy was discontinued before age 80.
