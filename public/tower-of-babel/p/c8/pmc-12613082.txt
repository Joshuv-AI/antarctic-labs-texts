Enhanced extraction of human interstitial fluid using microneedles and laser-induced fluid recruitment
Authors: Juan L. Mena-Lapaix, Chizimuzo Chibuko, Min Htat Kyaw, Mark R. Prausnitz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryDermal interstitial fluid (ISF) contains biomarkers with potential use in medical diagnostics, but current systems extract insufficient ISF for many applications. We tested the hypothesis that increased skin water content can increase ISF extraction due to enlarged dermal pore structure. Increasing skin water content ex vivo increased dermal pore size >5-fold, dermal hydraulic conductivity 1.5-fold, and ISF extraction 7-fold. We next developed an in vivo ISF extraction method involving laser pretreatment of skin to increase water content, microneedle puncture of skin to create pathways for ISF flow, and suction to drive ISF flow. This method yielded ISF volumes of up to 55 μL in human subjects, an 8-fold increase compared to extraction without laser and 4-fold increase in ISF volume per microneedle compared to prior studies. Overall, microneedles with laser-induced fluid recruitment provide a new mechanism to increase ISF sampling from skin, advancing the study of ISF biomarkers and ISF-based diagnostics.

Introduction

Interstitial fluid (ISF) in skin is rich in biomarkers with potential use in diagnostics and monitoring for both dermal and systemic indications.1 ISF offers advantages over blood in that it does not clot, which facilitates continuous or repeated monitoring, and has no cells and low albumin content, which simplifies sample preparation for analysis. ISF also contains unique and unexplored biomarkers not found in blood that can provide tissue-specific information.1,2,3,4,5

The only biomarker routinely monitored in ISF is glucose by diabetic patients, which is measured by a sensor implanted in the subcutaneous space.6,7 To avoid an indwelling sensor, various methods have been used to collect biomarkers from dermal ISF for analysis outside skin, including microdialysis,8,9 open-flow microperfusion,10,11 laser photoporation,12,13,14 reverse iontophoresis,15,16 low-frequency ultrasound,17,18 and microneedles (MNs).6,19 These methods suffer from being complex, invasive, and costly and/or able to collect only small amounts of ISF biomarkers.

MN-based methods have received increasing attention because of their simplicity and low cost. MNs are typically solid,2,20,21,22 hollow,19,23,24,25,26,27 or hydrogel28,29,30,31,32 structures measuring hundreds of microns that pierce skin to access dermal ISF. Fluid can be collected from the skin by diffusion, capillary action, pressure/suction, or other driving forces.21 Metabolomic and proteomic analyses of ISF extracted using MNs have established that most compounds found in blood are also present in ISF and that ISF also contains thousands of compounds not found in blood.1,2,3,32,33,34,35 However, widespread use of ISF extracted using MNs in clinical research and medicine has been limited by the small ISF volumes they collect, usually just a few microliters.1,2

In this study, we sought to increase the amount of ISF collected using MNs, guided by two prior findings. First, a broad analysis of ISF extraction from skin by MN-based methods concluded that the rate-limiting step to ISF extraction is fluid flow through dermis to sites of MN puncture.21 Second, a study in rats showed that pretreating skin with MN puncture prior to collecting ISF can recruit ISF to the local tissue and increase ISF collected.22 These findings suggest the hypothesis that increased water content of the dermis can expand dermal pore size and thereby facilitate ISF extraction from the skin.

We tested this hypothesis by first determining how ISF extraction rates were affected by various physical parameters of the ISF extraction system and then focusing on the role of skin water content and dermal pore structure in ISF extraction from pig skin ex vivo. Building on these results, we developed an ISF extraction method involving laser pretreatment of skin to increase skin water content, MN puncture of skin to provide pathways for ISF flow out of skin, and suction to drive ISF flow by convection. Using this approach, we extracted up to 8-fold more ISF from the skin of human subjects compared to the same method without laser pretreatment. On a per-MN basis, this represents at least a ∼4-fold increase in ISF collection volume compared to previous results.2,3,27,36,37,38

Results

ISF extraction from pig skin by pressure-driven flow

We first assessed the effect of positive or negative pressure applied to skin during ISF extraction after skin puncture by MNs (Figures 1A and 1B). After MN puncture but without applied pressure, only 0.003 ± 0.005 μL/MN was extracted from the skin. Applying suction increased ISF extraction in a roughly linear fashion, with a 5-fold increase in ISF extracted due to a 7.5-fold increase in suction pressure from −10 to −75 kPa (p < 0.004, Figure 1C). In contrast, applying positive pressure up to 400 kPa had no significant effect on ISF extracted (p > 0.97); a pressure of 800 kPa was needed to significantly increase ISF extracted (p < 0.009) (Figure 1D). Effect of physical parameters on ISF extraction from pig skin

Next, we tested the effect of different physical parameters on the volume of ISF extracted via pressure-driven flow. We chose parameters that could be controlled in a clinical ISF extraction system by design features of the system or by selection of the treatment site. We assessed the effects of the presence of MN pores; MN length; suction source; and skin stretching, temperature, and thickness.

We first determined that MN-created pores are important for extracting ISF, as shown by an 11-fold increase in ISF extracted from skin punctured with MNs versus intact skin (p = 0.04, Figure 2A). We next found that the depth of MN pores did not significantly affect volume of ISF extracted by comparing skin puncture using MNs measuring 250 μm versus 750 μm long (p = 0.63, Figure 2B). Instead of the bulky laboratory pump used for most of our studies, we found that a small, hand-held cosmetic pump operated at approximately the same suction pressure yielded similar amounts of ISF extracted (p = 0.44, Figure 2C). This finding enables the use of small, low-cost and convenient devices to facilitate widespread use.

We found that, in the absence of effective positive pressure (≤200 kPa), skin stretching had no effect on ISF extraction (p > 0.06), but, at high pressure (800 kPa), stretching helped (p < 0.0001) (Figure 2D). Increased skin tension due to stretching could open skin pores and more effectively spread the applied pressure gradient across skin to drive ISF flow.

There was a small but significant increase in ISF extracted at 37°C versus room temperature (p = 0.017, Figure 2E), suggesting a role of temperature-induced changes in ISF viscosity, skin hydraulic conductivity, or other properties. Finally, volume of ISF extracted was not correlated to skin thickness (R2 < 0.10, Figure 2F), which indicates that the greater ISF content of thicker skin did not facilitate ISF extraction.

Effect of skin water content on ISF extraction from pig skin ex vivo

We hypothesized that increased skin water content could increase ISF extraction by two possible mechanisms. First, increased absolute water content means the presence of more ISF that can be extracted. However, the total amount of ISF we are extracting (<10 μL/cm2) is only a small fraction of ISF in skin (70 μL/cm2 for skin of 1 mm thickness and 70% hydration21,40,41,42), which suggests that total ISF content may not be limiting.

The second mechanism is based on ISF extraction being limited by resistance to flow through the porous medium of dermis, which is composed of collagen and elastin fibers surrounded by an extracellular matrix of hyaluronic acid and other glycoproteins and proteoglycan fibers.1,43 Increased percent water content means larger pores that have less resistance to flow and possibly a larger number of pores that provide more pathways for flow (Figure 3A).44Figure 3Effect of skin water content on ISF extracted from pig skin ex vivo(A) Illustration of the effect of skin water content on the size of dermal pores.(B) Water content of skin dehydrated for different times. To test this hypothesis, we varied skin water content from 67.0% ± 5.0% to 57.7% ± 4.3% by dehydrating skin for up to 20 min (p < 0.0001, Figure 3B) and found that average ISF volume extracted decreased 7-fold (p < 0.0001, Figure 3C). Examining all 62 individual data points with skin water content ranging from 51% to 74% and ISF volume extracted varying from 0.01 to 4.9 μL, we similarly found that ISF volume increased with increasing skin water content (p < 0.01, Figure 3D). Skin having <60% water content generally produced <0.1 μL of ISF, and skin with >65% water content produced volumes as high as 4.9 μL.

Guided by the hypothesis that ISF extraction is limited by flow through dermis, we modeled the skin as a porous fiber matrix; the model had no fitted parameters, and its structure was based on skin’s fiber content and structure (Note S1, Tables S1 and S2; Figure S2).The modeled hydraulic conductivity of dermis (Figure 3D, secondary axis) increased with increasing skin water content due primarily to expansion of water-filled pores in dermis and exhibited a similar functionality to the empirical fit of ISF extracted. We extended the hydraulic conductivity model to predict ISF volume extracted through MN pores (Note S2), which similarly increased with increasing skin water content, although less steeply than the empirical data fit (Figure 3D). Altogether, these data show that increased skin water content increased ISF extraction by a mechanism that is hypothesized to involve increased pore size in dermis.

Effect of enzyme treatment on ISF extracted from pig skin ex vivo

Based on the expectation that ISF extraction is limited by flow through a porous matrix of dermal fibers,43 we found that degradation of the skin’s most abundant fibers using collagenase and hyaluronidase (Figure 4A) increased ISF extraction in a concentration-dependent manner by as much as 6-fold (p < 0.0001, Figure 4B; Note S3; Figure S3). Combination of the enzymes did not further increase the magnitude of the effect (Figure 4C). As a negative control, heat-denatured collagenase did not increase the volume of ISF extracted (Figure 4D). Other types of collagenases similarly increased ISF volume extracted (Figure 4E). Pressure also increased effective dermal hydraulic conductivity, exhibiting >3-fold increase from 34 to 200 kPa (p < 0.0001, Figure 5B; further discussed in Notes S4 and S5; Figures S4–S7), suggesting possible alteration of pore structures in dermis by applied pressure. Extrapolation of the hydraulic conductivity curve to zero pressure yielded a conductivity of 7 × 10−16 m2/(Pa·s), in good agreement with prior literature.45 Reducing dermal water content (Figure 5C; Figure S8) also decreased dermal hydraulic conductivity (p < 0.0001, Figure 5D; Figure S8), which is consistent with observed effects of water content on ISF extraction (Figure 3). Effect of skin water content on skin pore size

We hypothesized that reduced ISF extraction and dermal hydraulic conductivity due to decreased skin water content can be explained by a reduction of size of dermal pores that provides greater resistance to flow (Figure 3A).43,44,45 To test this hypothesis, we determined pore size distribution in skin samples with different water content using thermoporometry, which is a technique that determines pore size based on melting point depression of water confined in small pores (Note S6; Figures S9 and S10).46,47,48,49

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis (Figure 6A) showed that enthalpy change of water increased monotonically from ∼80 to ∼200 J/g for skin with 48%–74% water content (p < 0.0001, Figures 6B and 6C). Measured enthalpy changes were used to calculate mass of freezable water in skin, which increased from ∼30% to ∼60%, and mass of non-freezable water, which remained relatively constant between 15% and 20% (p = 0.02, Figure 6C). If we interpret non-freezable water as bound to the edges of pores,47,50 the fact that the amount of non-freezable water remained relatively constant may mean that total number of pores likewise did not change (Figure 3A). The shifts in thermogram peaks to higher temperatures as skin water content increased (Figures 6A and 6B) correspond to a shift to larger pore size. Using these data, we found that, as skin water content increased, water content in the largest pores (>1,000 nm) also increased (p < 0.0001), while water content in smaller pores did not change significantly (p > 0.05) (Figure 6D). Median pore size in skin increased ∼5-fold, going from 63 ± 7 nm at the lowest water content to 320 ± 109 nm at the highest (Figure 6E). These data further support the hypothesis that increased skin water content increases skin pore size (Figure 3A), which then increases dermal hydraulic conductivity and ISF extraction from skin.

Recruiting ISF in human subjects by laser-induced increase in skin water content

Guided by the hypothesis that increased skin water content can facilitate ISF extraction, we designed a skin pretreatment to locally increase skin water content for safe use in humans. The pretreatment involved minor skin injury by an over-the-counter fractional cosmetic laser used to reduce the appearance of wrinkles (Figure 7A; Note S7; Figures S11 and S12). Treatment was applied to the volar forearm, a site of easy access, with relatively low hair density, and previously used for ISF collection.2,3,21,36,37 When administered to human subjects, each laser pulse generated 54 microthermal zones of <0.5 mm diameter within a skin area of 55 mm2 (Figure 7B) that produced localized edema that was visible to the naked eye within 3 min, peaked at ∼5 min, and mostly subsided within 20 min, leaving only mild erythema that disappeared within a day (Figure 7C). To further confirm that laser treatment increased skin water content in vivo, we exposed rats to the same laser treatment performed on human subjects. Edema in rat skin was less pronounced than in humans and was accompanied by milder erythema (Note S8 and Figure S14). Measurement in skin biopsies demonstrated a modest but significant increase in skin water content of 3% due to laser treatment (p < 0.001, Figure S15), consistent with our findings in human subjects.

Extracting ISF from human subjects after recruiting ISF

We next extracted ISF from the skin of human subjects using laser, MNs, and suction (Figures S16 and S17). ISF collected with or without laser treatment appeared as a clear yellowish fluid on the skin surface (Figure 8A). Applying 0, 1, 4, or 8 laser pulses before MN application and suction for 5 min progressively increased ISF extracted from 0.7 ± 0.8 μL to 5.3 ± 4.0 μL, which shows that laser treatment increased ISF volume by up to a factor of 8 (p = 0.003, Figure 8C). Similarly, enhanced ISF extraction was the same if MNs and suction were administered immediately after laser treatment or after a 5-min delay (p > 0.05, Figure S18). In contrast to ISF extraction in humans, laser treatment in pig skin ex vivo provided no increase in ISF extraction (p > 0.05, Figure 8B), consistent with the hypothesis that enhanced ISF extraction was due to edema, which only occurs in vivo.

To further increase ISF volumes, we extracted ISF under suction for up to 20 min in another cohort of human subjects. We again found that ISF volume increased with number of laser pulses (ANOVA, p = 0.008) and also increased with duration of suction (p = 0.008), ranging from 11 ± 12 μL of ISF after 5 min to 55 ± 49 μL after 20 min suction coupled with 8 laser pulses, which is a 3.5-fold increase compared to no laser treatment (p = 0.013) (Figure 8E; Figure S19 and Video S1). Additional statistical analysis identified outliers in the no-laser-treatment group; removal of these outliers reduced the ISF collection volume with no laser. Making this correction resulted in a 6.8-fold increase of ISF volume after 8 laser pulses compared to no laser treatment (Figure S20).

The largest ISF extraction from any subject was 130 μL, which is 8-fold greater than the average ISF volume extracted over 20 min without pulses. In one subject, we consecutively extracted at least 15 μL of ISF three times (18.8 ± 0.3 μL average per extraction) with 1.5 min of suction each time (Figure 8A). Applying suction for 20 min continuously or as three sequential suction periods yielded similar ISF volumes (p = 0.55, Figure 8E), indicating that intermittent ISF sampling during extended extraction did not limit ISF extraction. OCT imaging after the ISF extraction process showed fluid-rich regions ∼100–200 μm deep in epidermis and superficial dermis (Figure S21). Subjects again reported only minor discomfort with ISF extraction, which produced pain comparable to that of a lancet puncture (Figure 8F).

Total protein concentration in ISF was ∼60% of that in plasma (p < 0.05 for all pairs, Figure 8G), which is similar to prior studies showing reduced protein concentration in ISF,5,36 and the concentration did not depend on suction time (p > 0.05 for all groups) or number of laser pulses (p > 0.05) (Figures S22 and S23).This suggests that ISF collected after laser treatment may have protein content similar to native ISF, but additional studies are needed to further characterize ISF composition.

Overall, ISF extraction in this study was well tolerated. While edema was not visible after ISF extraction without laser, mild, highly localized edema was present after laser treatment, which subsided in ∼1 h. Mild erythema was seen after ISF extraction with or without laser treatment (Figure 7C; Figures S24 and S25). The edge of the suction device left a ring-shaped mark on skin (Figure 8A), which generally disappeared within hours but sometimes persisted for up to 7 days. While ISF extraction mostly produced clear fluid, sometimes small drops (∼1 μL) of blood appeared, apparently from individual micropores. A faint grid pattern of laser spots was seen after treatment (Figure 7B), which generally disappeared within a day after one laser pulse and in ≤1 week after 4 pulses for the subjects treated with suction (Figures S24 and S25). The grid pattern as well as skin hyperpigmentation were seen for ≤4 weeks after 8 pulses with suction. No other adverse events were noted.

Discussion

This work supports our hypothesis that increased water content of dermis can expand dermal pore size and thereby facilitate ISF extraction from skin. In pig skin ex vivo, extracted ISF increased with increasing skin water content and addition of enzymes to break down dermal fibers. Increased water content also increased skin hydraulic conductivity and dermal pore size. Animal and human studies showed that increased skin water content due to laser-induced edema similarly increased ISF extraction. The damaged cells in the laser-treated skin are believed to release signals (i.e., danger-associated molecular patterns) that recruit immune cells, as well as increased fluid, to the treated site.53

We expect that this effect of increased skin water content on ISF collection was not due to availability of more ISF. Skin with physiological water content (∼70%)21,40,41,42 had >50 mg/cm2 of free (i.e., freezable) water. In humans, we extracted ISF from 2.4 cm2 of skin containing, by extension, >120 μL ISF available for extraction. Without edema, our best conditions collected an average of 15.7 μL ISF, which is ≤ 13% of the free water. With edema, an average of 55 μL extracted ISF represents <46% of the free water. We instead interpret increased dermal water content as expanding dermal pore size, which reduced resistance to flow through pores. Flow resistance decreases with the square of pore radius,54 indicating that expanded pore size can have large effects on fluid flow.

Epidermis (including stratum corneum, viable epidermis, and epidermal basement membrane) was a major barrier to extracting ISF, which was overcome by puncturing MNs across epidermis. Convective flow by suction was also needed to drive ISF flow from skin.

We applied our mechanistic understanding of the need to (1) puncture epidermis; (2) reduce resistance to flow in dermis by increasing skin water content; and (3) drive ISF flow by convective forces to develop an ISF extraction protocol involving (1) pretreating skin with MNs to create micropores, (2) pretreating skin with a laser to induce local edema, and (3) administering suction to pull ISF to the skin surface. In human subjects, this approach extracted an average of 55 μL of ISF at optimal conditions through just 200 MN punctures per site. This represents up to an order of magnitude increase relative to previously reported ISF volumes or extraction rates from humans on a per-MN basis (Note S9).2,3,27,36,37,38 ISF extraction by laser, MNs, and suction was well tolerated, causing pain similar to a lancet.

Treatment was applied to the skin of the volar forearm, but other body sites could be used for ISF extraction, which may yield more (or less) ISF upon laser-based collection. Patient acceptability and tolerability, as well as clinical access, may also influence the skin site selection.

Limitations of the study

A limitation of this study is that it drew many of its mechanistic conclusions from pig skin ex vivo, although translational outcomes were shown in humans. In addition, while the mathematical model of hydraulic conductivity provided reasonable agreement with results for ISF extraction ex vivo, the model could be improved by better capturing the complex arrangement of fibers in the skin. The study contained no sham control (i.e., a control that pressed the laser without emitting pulses). However, the effect of pressing the laser against the skin is expected to be negligible, as the volume of ISF collected after applying a single laser pulse was no different from that for the no-laser control.

Another limitation is that laser treatments left cosmetic marks on skin, especially after application of 8 pulses. Protocol optimization (i.e., to enhance the volume of ISF collected from sites treated with gentler laser pulsing protocols), or possibly other ways to increase skin water content (e.g., by using other devices), should be explored to improve acceptability. Although total protein content was not changed, fluid recruitment by edema could alter biomarker composition relative to native ISF, which requires further study.

Finally, our study involved only 23 human subjects, so additional studies are needed with larger and more-diverse populations, especially to adequately determine acceptability of the skin reaction and variability of the increase in ISF collected for different skin types, including those arising from demographic factors (e.g., sex, age, etc.). These studies should also compare ISF composition with and without laser-induced fluid recruitment, as the laser-mediated inflammation could change the concentration of some biomarkers.

Conclusion

ISF holds promise as a blood-free, non-clotting source of conventional and novel biomarkers but has made limited impact on research advances or clinical practice due largely to lack of accessible methods for extraction. Here, we tested the hypothesis that increased skin water content can increase ISF extraction due to enlarged dermal pore structure enabling increased hydraulic conductivity of skin. Our data supported this hypothesis, which led to the laser-MN-suction system developed here. This system provides a simple method to extract tens of microliters of ISF from the skin of human subjects using a cosmetic laser (intended for anti-aging) and cosmetic suction device (intended for blackhead removal) that are low-cost and commercially available. The system in its current form can be translated into clinical use, and the mechanistic findings of this study offer opportunities to develop other methods to recruit fluid via localized edema.

Although our MNs were custom made, similar cosmetic MNs (intended for anti-aging) are readily available. ISF volumes extracted in this study are similar to blood volumes used for existing point-of-care diagnostic tests like lateral flow assays for detection of infectious diseases, clinical devices that measure electrolytes and small molecules, and microfluidic-based biomarker monitoring devices (e.g., volumes of 5–50 [www.sdbiosensor.com, www.globalpointofcare.abbott], 20,55 and 50 μL,56 respectively).

Given the large number and diversity of biomarkers found in ISF, conditions that could be monitored or investigated using ISF biomarkers include diabetes, vitamin levels, cancer, and antibody-based exposure to pathogens or vaccines (e.g., COVID-19).2,34,36 Additional uses of ISF biomarkers could be identified through metabolomic, proteomic, or exposomic analysis. Systems for extracting ISF biomarkers could be integrated with other devices (e.g., by coupling microfluidics or lateral flow strips to the ISF collection device) to measure biomarkers in situ.32,38

Altogether, ISF extraction using MNs and laser-induced fluid recruitment provides a simple method to collect large volumes of ISF to evaluate ISF biomarkers to advance knowledge of physiology and develop ISF-based diagnostics for clinical medicine. Future work should leverage this ISF extraction mechanism to develop integrated devices for use in clinical practice and home-use settings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animal studies

All procedures were approved by the Georgia Institute of Technology Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Eight male Lewis rats and four female Wistar rats 15-30 weeks-old (Charles River Laboratories, Wilmington, MA) were used in these studies. Rats were housed at room temperature (22°C–25°C) on a cycle of 12-h light and 12-h dark. Rats were housed in pairs within litter boxes and with free access to food and water. Rats received treatment on the lateral flank, with an area of the flank of each rat serving as a control.

Human studies

All procedures were approved by the Georgia Institute of Technology Institutional Review Board (Protocol H18418 revisions of 12/03/2021 and 1/23/2023), and written informed consent was obtained from all subjects before the study, and their privacy was conserved through experimentation, data analysis, and data presentation. A total of 23 healthy study subjects (52.1 females and 47.9 males) were recruited from among students, staff, or alumni of the Georgia Institute of Technology with demographics shown in Table S4. Participation was open to any healthy adult. Exclusion criteria included serious dermatological conditions, pregnancy, immunodeficiency, or recent blood donation. Subjects received treatments in the forearm, and for each subject an area on their forearm served as a control.

Method details

Microneedle array fabrication

MNs were designed in Solidworks (Dassault Systemes, Vélizy-Villacoublay, France) and fabricated from grade 304 stainless steel sheets by photo etching (Tech-Etch, Plymouth, MA; or Great Lakes Engineering, North Maple Grove, MN). MNs were fabricated as 5-linear arrays (i.e., MN arrays) with 1250 μm tip-to-tip spacing, a thickness of 50 μm, a base width of 200 μm at the array backing, and tapering to a sharp tip with radius of 10 μm. For experiments ex vivo, MNs were ∼750 μm long, unless otherwise noted. For experiments in vivo, MN length was measured to be 253 ± 15 μm (n = 10).

Measurement of ISF extracted from skin ex-vivo as a function of pressure

ISF was extracted ex vivo from pig skin (Pel-Freez Biologicals, Rogers, AR) from the inner pinna of the ear at various pressures using suction or positive pressure based on a protocol described before.21 After removing subcutaneous fat, skin samples were cut into 24-mm diameter pieces and incubated for ∼20 h in a Franz cell with the receiver chamber containing 1 mg/mL fluorescein (MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA) in phosphate buffer saline (PBS). In this way, the skin ISF was loaded with fluorescein to facilitate subsequent measurements. The skin was then removed from the Franz cell and immediately stretched on a stage using pins and treated with MNs by pressing and immediately removing an MN array with five 750 μm-long MNs twice into the skin, creating 10 micropores.

For experiments using suction, suction was applied for 5 min using a polyvinyl chloride hose with inner diameter of 10 mm placed on the surface of the skin surrounding the sites of MN puncture. A pressure regulator on the suction line from a vacuum pump (KNF Neuberger, Freiburg, Germany) was used to adjust the suction to the desired pressure of −10, −25, −50 or −75 kPa. In some cases, suction applied with a cosmetic pump (VOYOR BR610 Blackhead Remover, Hymax, London, United Kingdom) coupled to a plastic adapter included by the equipment manufacturer. In all suction experiments, suction pressure was applied uniformly across the skin area under the hose or plastic adapter (Figure S1).

For experiments using positive pressure, a hollow cylinder made of glass with an inner diameter of 9.0 mm and outer diameter of 12.5 mm was placed on top of the skin, which created a contact area of 0.59 cm2 between the cylinder and skin. Pressure was applied for 5 min by placing a mass on top of the cylinder. Masses of 4.8, 2.4, 1.2, 0.6, 0.3, 0.15, and 0.06 kg were used to apply pressures of 800, 400, 200, 100, 50, 25, and 10 kPa, respectively. This pressure was applied only to the skin in contact with the cylinder, forming a ring of pressure surrounding the skin area with MN punctures (Figure S1).

In both sets of experiments (i.e., experiments using suction and positive pressure), the fluid on the surface of the skin was wiped off using a Kimwipe (Kimberly-Clark, Irving, TX) cut into pieces measuring approximately 25 mm by 25 mm. Fluorescein from the fluid extracted from the skin was eluted from the Kimwipe by incubating overnight in DI water at room temperature (20°C–25°C), and the total amount of fluorescein in the ISF was quantified by fluorescence using a Biotek Synergy plate reader (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA).

ISF volume was calculated by multiplying the mass of fluorescein collected by the concentration of fluorescein in which the skin was incubated. This analysis assumes that the ISF in the skin had the same fluorescein concentration as in the receiving chamber of the Franz cell in which it was incubated. This assumption was validated by extracting ISF in a 5 mL capillary, measuring the volume of fluid by the height of the capillary column, and quantifying the fluorescence in the known volume of fluid.

Measurement of ISF extracted from skin ex vivo after enzyme treatment

Collagenase types I (250 U/mg activity) was obtained from MilliporeSigma (Burlington, MA); collagenase type III (≥100 U/mg) was obtained from Fisher Scientific (Pittsburgh, PA); collagenase type V (≥450 U/mg) was obtained from Worthington Biochemical (Lakewood, NJ); and hyaluronidases types I (700 U/mg) and II (300 U/mg) were obtained from MilliporeSigma. In experiments that varied enzyme concentration, only collagenase type I was used.

To test the effect of different enzymes on ISF extraction, the procedure described above was used, except the Franz cell receptor solution contained one or more enzymes at a concentration of 0.5–30 mg/mL in addition to PBS and fluorescein. Formulations of hyaluronidase as low as 100 U/mL have been shown to reduce the pressure necessary for injection 1 mL of fluid into the dermis,57 and therefore enzyme concentrations near or above this threshold were chosen. The volume of ISF extracted from treated samples was compared to the volume extracted from 5 to 9 untreated samples incubated without enzyme in the same set of experiments.

Measurement of ISF extracted from skin ex vivo at different skin hydrations

To extract ISF from skin with different water contents, a modified version of the procedure for ISF extraction at different pressures was used, in which skin water content was modified after the Franz cell incubation and prior to ISF extraction.

After Franz cell incubation to fully hydrate the skin, the skin mass was recorded. To change skin water content, the skin was placed on a piece of Tork paper towel (Essity Professional Hygiene, Philadelphia, PA) dermis-side down, and a mass of 8.9 kg was placed on top of the skin (i.e., stratum corneum side) to drive fluid out of the skin by convection.21 Compressing the skin for different durations (0.5–20 min) resulted in different average levels of skin water content (67.0 ± 3.0% to 57.7 ± 4.3% water). After removing the 8.9-kg mass, the mass of the skin was recorded again, and ISF was extracted from 10 micropores in the skin created by pressing 750 μm-long MNs, as described above.

After extracting ISF (mISF), the active area of the skin (i.e., the area of skin exposed to fluid in the Franz cell) was collected using an 8-mm biopsy (Integra Lifesciences, Princeton, NJ), its mass was recorded (mskin), and then the skin was lyophilized for 24 h to remove over 95% of the water in a Labconco Freezone 6-L freeze dryer (Kansas City, MO) at −50°C and less than 0.1 mbar. Skin mass was recorded (mlyo), and the mass loss during lyophilization (mwater) was assumed to be all water. The water content of the skin (W) was calculated using Equation 1:(Equation 1)W=mskin−mlyomskin,mskin=mlyo+mISF+mwater,where mskin, mlyo, mwater, mwater represent mass of hydrated skin, lyophilized skin, water loss during lyophilization, and ISF extracted from skin, respectively.

Measurement of effective hydraulic conductivity of dermis ex vivo

To measure effective hydraulic conductivity of dermis, we built an apparatus to measure the volume of fluid flowing through the dermis under controlled transdermal pressure at known time intervals (Note S4, Figures S4, and S5), based on prior work58 but designed to operate at larger pressures. Because tissues deform at large pressures and the area, thickness, and microstructure may differ from the undeformed tissue, the hydraulic conductivity we measured may differ from the hydraulic conductivity of the tissue without deformation. Therefore, we refer to these measurements as “effective hydraulic conductivity.”

To measure the effective hydraulic conductivity in the apparatus, PBS was used as the fluid flowing through the tissue. PBS is virtually isotonic with extracellular fluid and ISF, and therefore using PBS as the fluid flowing through the dermis reduced the potential to create osmotic pressure gradients that could alter the net volume of ISF crossing through the dermis (or skin) due to the mechanical pressure differential.

Pig ear skin was cut into circles with diameter of 30 mm and weighed. The epidermis was removed by first loosening the dermoepidermal junction by incubation in a water bath at 55°C for 90 s followed by placement in a room temperature water bath for 5–10 min. Then, the epidermis was scraped off using a metal spatula, leaving the dermis behind, which was weighed and mounted in a hydraulic conductivity measurement apparatus.

In the apparatus, a piece of dermis was sandwiched between two acrylic plates. Between the dermis and the plates were a bottom O-ring (12.5 mm inner diameter, 2.5 mm thickness) and a top O-ring (10 mm inner diameter, 3 mm thickness). The bottom plate had a 9-mm hole to expose the dermis to pressurized PBS, and the top plate had a 9-mm hole that served as a cylindrical sampling chamber exposed to air (i.e., at atmospheric pressure). To reduce strain on the dermis produced by tissue deformation from the transdermal pressure gradient, a 3-D printed polymer mesh was placed on the upper side of the dermis.

To verify dermal integrity, a pressure of 3 kPa was applied to the dermis, and samples which allowed flow rates of more than 50 μL within 1 min were discarded as damaged. After this initial test, PBS was pressurized by pushing a sealed column of air against the PBS at a set pressure. Fluid that flowed across dermis and into the sampling chamber was collected in 30 min intervals by pipetting, and its mass measured. The volume of fluid was determined by converting the mass to volume via the density of PBS (1.01 g/mL).

For experiments measuring fluid flow through dermis with different water contents, dermis samples were dehydrated, fluid flow through them measured, and then dermis samples were rehydrated while mounted on a stage, after which flow across them was remeasured. To dehydrate the samples, each dermis sample of 30-mm diameter was placed on a paper towel (Essity Professional Hygiene, Philadelphia, PA) dermis-side down, and a mass of 8.9 kg was placed on top of the dermis (epidermis-side up) to drive fluid out of the dermis by convection. The dermis samples were weighed before and after water removal, and the mass differential was calculated. The samples were then mounted on the apparatus, and fluid flow through the dermis measured as described above.

To measure hydraulic conductivity of the dermis samples in the hydrated state, the sample was left mounted on the apparatus, but pressure was removed, and 0.5 mL of PBS was added to the fluid sampling chamber of each sample. The apparatus was stored at 4°C by placing in a cold room for 20 h, with fluid in the sampling chamber monitored and replenished as needed. The sample was then removed from the cold room, excess fluid was pipetted out of the sampling chamber, the exposed dermis area was gently patdried with a Kimwipe, and the sample was once again subjected to pressurized PBS to push fluid through the dermis. Fluid flow through the dermis was measured as described above.

Prediction of hydraulic conductivity based on a fiber-matrix model

We modeled the hydraulic conductivity of dermis by considering dermis as a fiber matrix based on a model previously developed for scleral tissue in the eye with similar structure and composition.44 Similar to sclera, dermis is made primarily of a matrix of collagen, proteoglycans, and glycosaminoglycans containing pores through which fluid flows. Skin properties used for the model are listed in Tables S1 and S2, and more information about the model is provided in Note S1).

Net hydraulic conductivity of dermis (Kdermis) was calculated by modeling the dermis as a composite medium in which two distinct domains for fluid flow are available (Figure S2). The first domain (the microscale) consisted of pores between the randomly oriented proteoglycan and glycosaminoglycan fibers in the dermis, i.e., the “ground substance”, and the hydraulic conductivity for this domain gave the hydraulic conductivity for the ground substance, Kgs. Kgs was calculated by first calculating the resistance of a medium made only of proteoglycans, then for a medium made of only glycosaminoglycans, and then adjusting the contribution of each type of fiber by the volume fraction it occupies.44

The second domain (the mesoscale) consisted of a composite domain in which collagen fibers were assumed to be periodically arranged and the pores created by collagen fibers are filled with ground substance. These “lamella” had hydraulic conductivity of Klam. Finally, the net hydraulic conductivity was calculated by computing the hydraulic conductivity of a composite medium made of domains with Kgs and Klam.

Thermoporometry

Pore size distribution in skin samples was estimated from thermograms collected by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) using thermoporometry, a technique based on the melting point depression of water confined in small pores.46,47,48 This method has been previously used to measure pore sizes in hydrogels49,60,61 (Note S6).

Four-millimeter biopsies of pig ear skin with original mass of 14–20 mg were submerged in 25 mL of PBS at 4°C for 20 h. The samples were then removed, gently blotted dry, and their mass recorded again. Next, each sample was placed in an aluminum hermetic DSC pan, sealed, and placed in a TA DSC 250 (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE).

The DSC scanning program consisted of an equilibration step to bring the sample to −30°C, a 5-min isothermal step at −30°C, and a ramp at 0.5°C/min from −30°C to 5°C. After the scan, the skin sample was removed from the pan and partially dehydrated by pressing against a Tork paper towel (Essity) until 2–3 mg of mass was removed from the sample. The weight after dehydration was recorded, and the sample was put again in a sealed DSC pan and rescanned. This process was repeated up to four times, after which the sample was fully dehydrated by lyophilization (see above) and scanned in the DSC again. There was little variation in the curves due to freeze-thaw cycles (see Figure S10). For all samples, peak onset of melting and enthalpy of melting were calculated using TRIOS software (TA instruments), and the thermogram data exported to Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) to calculate the amount of water in pores of different sizes, as done previously48 (Note S6, Table S3; Figures S9 and S10). We found that skin samples with lower water content showed smaller peaks of heat flow (i.e., enthalpy change), which is consistent with the decreased amount of water in the skin. Lyophilized skin showed no peak, which supports the interpretation that the peaks in the thermograms correspond to the melting of water in the samples.

To calculate the fraction of freezable water in skin samples, the total enthalpy calculated from the TRIOS software for each sample was divided by the specific melting enthalpy of water (334 J/g47). The difference between the freezable water in each sample and the total water in each sample (known from lyophilization) was deemed non-freezable water.47,62

To calculate the amount of water in pores of different sizes (i.e., the size distribution of water-filled pores in the skin), the cumulative enthalpy of the water melting peak in the sample was first calculated by integrating the heat flow vs. temperature curve. Next, the temperatures in the DSC scan were converted to pore sizes (Figure S9), as previously described,46,48 but using an empirical offset of −1.1°C that corrected for peak onset of water melting in the equipment used for the experiments. Then, the enthalpy of water in pores in a particular size range was determined by subtracting the cumulative enthalpy for the smaller pore size (which correspond to a lower temperature and therefore a lower cumulative enthalpy) from the cumulative enthalpy for the larger pore size (which correspond to a higher temperature and therefore a higher cumulative enthalpy). This enthalpy difference was then divided by the total cumulative enthalpy, which gave the fraction of freezable water in pores within said size range. Finally, multiplying this fraction by the total mass of freezable water in the sample gave the net amount of water within pores in the size range.

In vivo effect of laser on skin water content in rats

Prior to laser treatment, rats were anesthetized using isoflurane (AErrane, Baxter Healthcare, Deerfield, IL) inhalation, and hair from both lateral flanks was removed by shaving and application of depilatory cream (Nair, Princeton, NJ), which was wiped clean with gauze and deionized water at least 24 h before laser treatment.

The day of laser treatment, rats were anesthetized again using isoflurane, and eight laser pulses were applied within a 2.3 cm2 area of one side of the rats. After 5 min from the first laser pulse application, an 8-mm skin biopsy was taken from the treated area, blood gently removed by blotting, and weighted, and the mass was recorded. A biopsy from the untreated site of the same rat was taken as a control. The order of the control and treated biopsy was alternated between rats to control potential sampling order effects. After the mass of the biopsies were recorded, the rats were euthanized by asphyxiation using carbon dioxide.

The biopsies were frozen at −20°C, and the frozen biopsies were lyophilized for 24 h to remove over 95% of the water in a Labconco Freezone 6-L freeze dryer (Kansas City) at −50°C and less than 0.1 mbar. The dry biopsy mass was recorded, and the water content was calculated by assuming all the mass removed by lyophilization was water.

In vivo extraction of ISF from humans

ISF was extracted from the skin using a modified version of the protocol developed by Samant et al.2 The subject’s forearm skin was cleaned with an alcohol swab, and a Tegaderm film (3M, Saint Paul, MN) with three 12-mm diameter holes cut in it was applied to delineate three treatment sites for sequential and identical application of laser, MN array, and suction; each group of three sites is referred to as a “treatment area”. Up to four treatment areas were used per subject, with one of the areas serving as a control treatment receiving application of MN array and suction (no laser).

Control gauze

Prior to laser or MN array application, the surface of the three skin sites was swabbed for ∼30 s with a sterile medical gauze (Ultrapure nonwoven sponges, Crosstex International, Hauppauge, NY) wetted with 5–10 μL of sterile water. This sample was used to determine background biomarker levels on the skin surface. The sample was stored in a LoBind Eppendorf tube (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) and frozen at −20°C until further use.

Laser application

A Palovia Skin Renewing fractional laser (Palomar Medical Technologies, Burlington, MA) was used to pretreat the skin. This cosmetic laser uses 10-ms micropulses of 1410-nm light that generate micro-sterile inflammation in small areas of the skin. The cells damaged by the laser release signals (i.e., danger associated molecular patterns) that recruit immune cells to the treated site.53

To apply the laser on human subjects, the laser power level was set to “high”, aligned with a skin site delineated by the Tegaderm, and pressed to emit a “pulse” comprised of a series of 54 laser micropulses, each micropulse with energy of 2.0 ± 0.2 mJ (Figures S11 and S12). Each pulse treated an area with diameter of ∼200 μm, and all 54 pulses fit within a total skin area of ∼55 mm2. Depending on the treatment selected for a particular treatment area, we applied to all three sites either zero, one, four, or eight laser pulses. Unless otherwise noted, there was a delay of 5 min between initiation of laser pulses and initiation of suction. The eight-pulse treatment (24 pulses total among three skin sites) took approximately 3 min, whereas the single-pulse treatment took approximately 15 s (for all three skin sites).

Microneedle array application

After applying the laser pulses, a five-MN array with needles 250-μm long was applied 20 times to each skin site to create 100 micropores or 40 times to create 200 micropores in each of the three sites. For all treatment areas in which laser pulse(s) were applied, suction was applied 5 min after the first laser pulse. For treatment areas in which no laser pulse was applied, suction was applied 5 min after MN insertion.

Vacuum application and ISF extraction

Vacuum was applied using a Negative Pressure Cutaneous Suction system (NP-2, Electronic Diversities, Finksburg, MD). The vacuum cup was placed over the skin sites treated with the laser and the MN array. The vacuum cup had three 8-mm-diameter orifices that were aligned with the three holes in the Tegaderm at the application site. Vacuum was applied to the skin by slowly ramping the suction from 0 to −50 kPa over ∼3 min. The suction was stopped after 5 min and the ISF on the skin surface was collected by rubbing the skin surface with a piece of sterile medical gauze (Crosstex International) wetted with 5–10 μL of sterile water for 30 s.

In some cases, this process was repeated at the same skin location once more after another 5 min of suction, after which ISF was extracted, and then after another 10 min of suction, after which ISF was extracted, to enable three ISF extractions over a cumulative suction time of 20 min. In some cases, we applied continuous suction for 20 min and only extracted ISF at the end. For the 20-min ISF extraction studies, the procedure time from laser application to end of suction and ISF extraction for all treated areas was up to 35 min. In some cases, subjects returned for visual examination and imaging of the treated skin sites for up to 4 weeks.

Pain scores

Pain scores for the laser treatment, MN insertion, and suction treatments were determined using a visual analogue scale (VAS) immediately after each procedure was performed. In one study, pain scores were normalized to the insertion of a 21G needle (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ) into the forearm to a depth of 5 mm, which was performed at a random time point during the study. In another study, pain scores were normalized to a lancet puncture into the skin of a fingertip.

Capillary blood collection

In some cases, capillary blood from the subject fingers were collected by lancet puncture (CVS Health, Woonsocket, RI) on the fingertip of the middle or ring finger during the beginning of the 10-min suction interval of the 20-min ISF extraction protocol. Approximately 50–100 μL of blood were collected from each subject in BD Microtainer Capillary Blood Collectors with Gold Color Closure (Becton Dickinson).

Optical coherence tomography imaging

We used OCT imaging to study the effect of the laser on skin. OCT images of the skin were taken using a VivoSight Dx (Michelson Diagnostics, Kent, UK) that used infrared light (1305 nm) to visualize skin at depths up to 500 μm from the skin surface. OCT scan settings were set to produce 250 slices of 2-μm thickness in a 6 mm × 6 mm section of the skin. When assessing blood flow and epidermal thickness analysis, the OCT was set to “dynamic scan”. The companion VivoTools software (Michelson Diagnostics) was used to estimate blood vessel plexus depth and epidermal thickness in some subjects.

Measuring the power of the cosmetic fractional laser

The power output of the Palovia fractional laser was measured using an Ophir Vega laser power meter (Ophir Optronics, Jerusalem, Israel) connected to a PE25-C general purpose pyroelectric sensor (Ophir Optronics). A custom-made adapter was added on top of the sensor to position the laser, and the laser was pressed against the adapter. The path between the laser active area and the sensor active area was air.

Analytical techniques

Blood processing and serum separation

The volume of blood collected during fingerstick sampling of capillary blood was estimated visually from the markings of the vial. Serum was separated from blood by spinning the tubes at 16.1g for 10 min in an Eppendorf centrifuge 5415 RH, and then collected in 0.5 mL Eppendorf Protein LoBind tubes.

Determination of ISF volume

ISF volume was determined by measuring the concentration of sodium in the fluid. The sodium concentration was determined electrochemically using an ion selective electrode (PerfectION comb Na+, Mettler Toledo, Columbus, OH). The concentration of sodium in ISF was assumed to have a constant value in health individuals of 140 mM,63,64,65 enabling determination of ISF volume as the product of the measured sodium concentration in fluid swabbed from the skin surface and the volume of fluid swabbed from the skin surface (5–10 μL) divided by the concentration of sodium in ISF (140 mM).2 The volume measured from the ISF sample was corrected by subtracting the background sodium on the skin surface, which was measured from a skin surface swab prior to application of MN and suction (see above).

Determination of plasma and ISF protein concentration

Protein concentration was determined using a MicroBCA assay (ThermoFisher Scientific) to measure the total protein content of plasma and ISF samples. After the total protein content for each sample was measured, it was divided by the total sample volume of plasma or ISF (as determined using the sodium assay) to obtain the concentration of protein in the plasma or ISF extracted).
