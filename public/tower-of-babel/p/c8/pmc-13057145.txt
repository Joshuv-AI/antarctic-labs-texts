Sustainable polyurethane-based dielectric composites from industrial and E-waste for high-voltage insulation applications
Authors: Vinoth Kumar Selvaraj, Jeyanthi Subramanian, Gokul Selvanathan, Nitesh Verma Kannammaraju, Vijayakumar Rajendran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing demand for sustainable materials in electrical and electronic applications has led to the reuse of industrial and electronic waste in high-performance polymer composites. In this study, rigid polyurethane foam waste (WRPU), recycled tire waste (RTW), and printed circuit board (PCB) scraps were incorporated as fillers into a polyurethane matrix using methylene diphenyl diisocyanate (MDI) as a binder. The composites were fabricated via mechanical stirring followed by hydraulic compression. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) using a Central Composite Design (CCD) was employed to optimize the filler composition for dielectric performance. The optimal formulation—15.94 wt% WRPU, 3.0 wt% RTW, and 10.0 wt% PCB—achieved a dielectric constant of 4.45. Confirmation experiments and simulations using COMSOL Multiphysics yielded values of 4.33 and 4.50, respectively, with minimal error margins. FTIR confirmed functional group integration, HR-SEM revealed uniform filler dispersion and strong interfacial bonding, and TGA indicated improved thermal stability. The results highlight the dielectric potential of recycled polymer composites as environmentally friendly dielectric materials, although further studies are needed on breakdown strength, dielectric loss and resistivity for assessing high-voltage insulation applications, such as battery cell separators and power systems.Keywords: Sustainable polymer composites, Dielectric materials, Polyurethane foam waste, Printed circuit board (PCB) recycling, Response surface methodology (RSM), Electrical insulationSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

In the modern day, sustainability is a primary target in several industries. Quite a lot of them have started to incorporate bio-based or recyclable materials to achieve production sustainability. Many research and advance developments have been made in the materials industry to reduce the usage of conventional materials. One of the better ways to reduce the usage of conventional materials is through waste or discarded materials. Materials such as PCB scraps and RTW can be used to enhance insulation properties1–3. RPUF is obtained from shredded waste PU foam, which becomes an eco-friendly and cost-effective option for composite materials. It offers several advantages, such as improving mechanical and thermal properties, and surface quality. Due to its unique physical and mechanical properties, rigid polyurethane foam (RPUF) is one of the most important materials that find numerous applications in various industries. It has the features of high flexibility, low density, good anti-friction, and sound damping4–7. Yet, there are some disadvantages with using this type of foam such as the processing of recycled rigid PU powder can be complex, requiring specific equipment and techniques. Additionally, its compatibility with other materials may vary, necessitating careful consideration during composite formulation and commercial RPUF show limitations of poor electrical and thermal conductivity, which limits its applications in high-performance dielectric materials​. To improve RPUF’s dielectric strength, researchers use reinforcement with nanomaterials, including multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs), bamboo charcoal (BC), and cupric oxide (CuO)8–10​. Thermal Analysis indicates that nano filled PU foam are stable at higher decomposition temperatures, suggesting improvement in stability. The composition optimization (1.25% MWCNT, 2.5% CuO, and 2.22% BC) yields an electrical conductivity of 0.8199 S/m, making them good candidates for the dielectric constant requirements of insulation in electrical materials8,11–13.​

Tire and PCB scraps are an unexplored source of reinforcements for enhancing insulation properties. In many applications, their qualities help in enhancing mechanical stability, electrical insulation, and general performance. Firstly, since PCBs contain fiberglass and epoxy, two superior dielectric materials that provide high insulation and stability under high voltages, recycling them for such applications is very effective14–18. Rigid PU foams can be combined with this PCB scrap to increase insulation properties and dielectric breakdown voltage19,20. Adding to it, the use of ceramic and fiberglass helps to improve thermal stability21–24. Alongside PCB scrap, tyre rubber scrap has a high electrical resistivity and is an insulator, it can be used as reinforcement to enhance dielectric property. While carbon black is conductive in large quantities, its ability to control dispersion within polymer matrices can improve dielectric property while preserving insulation qualities, in contrast to other materials that have been investigated20,25–27. Tyre rubber scrap particles give mechanical flexibility, which lowers the possibility of failure and cracking under high voltage loads. Recycled rubber keeps moisture out, which over time can weaken a material’s dielectric constant.

Dielectric constant can be defined as a measure of how much a material can store electrical energy in an electric field. It is important in electrical and electronic uses, where they can help insulating materials to avoid unwanted current flow and breakdown at high voltage. Dielectric constant values increase greatly due to the addition of fillers. Dielectric constants of at least 3.0 were achieved by reinforced PU foams in comparison with non-reinforced foams which had values of approximately 2.0 for similar polymer types11,12. Dielectric achieved from9where they use Rigid rapeseed oil polyol polyurethane bio-foams and Petrochemical foams was found to have a value of 3. In11–30re-used tire powder is used as a mixer along with linear low-density polyethylene to achieve a di electric constant of 2.5. Reducing charge accumulation at the filler-matrix interface is one strategy that can be used to augment dielectric constant. The accumulation of electric field charge results in the distortion of the electric field and degradation of the insulation performance31–33. The solution involves the addition of micron sized filler in adhesive composites which has been shown in recent studies to reconstruct a strong dielectric constant without alteration34,35. These improvements render adhesive composites more appropriate for high-voltage applications by preventing electrical breakdown and consequently enhancing their longevity and reliability5,6,36.

To perform the procedure of mixing different proportions of PCB scraps, tyre scraps, and rigid PU foam, one should first understand and apply a concept called RSM with the help of a software called Minitab. Response Surface Methodology (RSM) contains the statistical and mathematical optimization and modelling techniques for how several factors control response. It develops models, examines interactions, and improves processes in the fields of engineering, manufacturing, and research37,38. RSM formulates mathematical models describing the influence of some mentioned independent variables on a dependent variable under study. Using diverse experimental designs like Central Composite Design (CCD), or Box-Behnken Design (BBD), it tests and systematically combines inferences from combinations of variables. It also includes response surface analysis where the graphical view of the changing response regarding the variation in input factors help identify optimal process conditions39–41. The CCD is particularly advantageous in RSM as it permits the assessment of both linear and quadratic effects of factors, as well as the determination of the optimal levels of these factors to achieve the desired response. Integrated with Minitab, one may experimentally construct design-experimentation analyse.

interactions, and optimize processes for better organizational judgments and product quality42–48.

COMSOL Multiphysics is a powerful simulation tool which can be used to design and simulate the dielectric properties and verify and compare the experimentation and simulation dielectric constant values. To find the dielectric constant of the sample from COMSOL, we need to simulate through an electrostatics solution in the software. As standard we must create a geometry, assign the materials to said geometry, mesh and apply boundary conditions and finally simulate and make post processing changes. It can be used for Designing capacitors with ceramic materials or investigate how fillers affect the capacitance or even validate TGA or FTIR data10–50. The novelty here is based in the use of recyclable waste materials, i.e. WRPUF, WPCB and RTW to enhance the electrical insulation properties along with the MDI based adhesive. By integrating these recycled materials into the MDI based mixture, the study dives deep into a sustainable approach to creating an efficient insulation material. consequently, the optimization process using RSM with CCD provides for precise control and prediction of dielectric constant, offering tailored solutions for specific insulation needs. This novel not only addresses environmental concerns by reusing waste materials but also helps develop an efficient and sustainable insulation material for various applications51,52.

Methodology

Materials

Rigid Polyurethane Foam (RPU) waste is procured from ASL, DRDO Hyderabad, India and then it is physically recycled using a shredder and is ground into fine powder with size of 200–300 microns projected in Table 1. Waste Printed Circuit Boards (WPCB) was procured from VIT Chennai Electronics lab, Chennai, India and are then mechanically recycled to an average size of 30–40 microns. Recycled Tire Waste (RTW) was procured from VIT Chennai Centre for Advanced material, Chennai, India where it was readily available shredded into an average size 100–150 microns. The Flexi bond is an MDI-based adhesive purchased from Manali Petrochemicals Limited, Chennai, India and the distilled water was procured from VIT Chennai Chemistry lab, Chennai, India.

Synthesis

Figure 1 represents the fabrication process for the synthesis of the sample. The mixture was synthesised by combining RPUF, WPCBs and RTW with an MDI-based adhesive in an appropriate proportion through RSM. Initially, the fillers are added to a beaker according to their weight% and then stirred for 10 min using a stirrer to create a homogenous mixture. For sustainability purposes, a bio-based MDI adhesive is used and is added into the homogeneous mixture and mixed for 15 min to ensure even distribution. Distilled water is now added to the mixture, acting as a hardener, which is 50% of the mass of the adhesive. The mixture is poured onto a circular silicon mould of dimensions 100 × 20 (mm). The mixture is then compressed in the mould using a pressing tool. The sample is cured for 10 h, and the next day, the sample is removed carefully and then placed in a hot air oven at 100 °C for 6 h to remove any moisture from the sample.

Finally, regarding WRPU, the polymer matrix is worth 40–65 wt%, Residual additives such as surfactants and waxes 5–10 wt % along with additives (thickeners) for 10–30 wt%, Foamable WPU dispersions 3–10 wt %59,60.

Di-electric testing

The di-electric test apparatus consists of a constant apparatus, variable capacitor, a specialized test capacitor for solid materials, and connecting wires. After turning on the setup, the variable capacitor is initially set to approximately 100 Pico farads (pF). Following this, the sensitivity in the scale is adjusted to a range of 85–90 milli amperes (mA) using the dedicated knob2–4. The variable capacitor is then adjusted to achieve resonance, aiming for the same sensitivity range. The first initial reading is considered as C1. For the next step the test capacitor encompassing the sample is connected, and adjustments are made to achieve resonance, this will be C2. Finally, the sample is removed from the test capacitor, and the procedure is reiterated to determine C3. The resonance point reading observed from the variable capacitor is considered as C3. Results and discussion

High-resolution scanning electron microscope

At VIT Chennai ZEISS EVO 18 is used to obtain the images of fillers and composites. It is set up for high-resolution imaging of metals, nanomaterials, polymers, and other such materials. The sample is trimmed to a size of less than 1 inch in length and 5 mm thickness to fit the sample holder. The surface of the sample should be flat for accurate focusing. To prevent vacuum to seep in the sample must be completely dry. For non-conductive materials like the RPUF, a thin conductive coating from gold or platinum is applied to prevent charging under the electron beam. The prepared sample is then securely mounted onto an SEM stub using a conductive adhesive to ensure safe and stable positioning. Figure 2(a) at 200 μm with EHT of 10.00 kV, the rigid polyurethane foam sample under HR-SEM shows brittle, fractured surfaces with sharp-edged, broken flakes. The fragments are irregular and rough-textured, forming non-uniform structures, typically seen in waste PU foam52,61. The remains of a closed-cell structure are still visible, which is a key feature of rigid PU foams. In Fig. 2(b) at 10 μm the SEM image of waste printed circuit boards has fibrous and flaky textures, which is the norm for glass fibre reinforced epoxy resin, commonly used in PCB. The image also shows rough, fractured surfaces, which suggests mechanical degradation.

HR-SEM images for all the major contributing components.

Observing Fig. 2(c) tire scrap images, the surface is irregular, crumpled, rough which represents the presence of vulcanized rubber. The morphology also displays wrinkled and swollen particles, commonly associated with carbon black-filled rubber composites used extensively in automotive tires62,63. In Fig. 2(d) at 3 μm the microstructure of the MDI based adhesive mixed with water reveals granular, rough, and agglomerated structures. These features are followed by bubble-like structures and a non-uniform surface texture, which are indicative of gas-evolving reactions. Also, the presence of micron-sized porous spaces strongly suggests CO₂ during curing process. Such characteristics are typical for polyurethane domains formed through the moisture between MDI and water64,65. Finally in Fig. 2(e) at 50 μm, from the image it can be deduced that with the presence of the powdered dust mixed along with the structures of the RPUF, that the fillers and combined and infused well within the mixture and are providing the necessary features65.

Fourier transform infrared test

Shimadzu IR SPIRIT FTIR spectrometer for this test. We clean the Attenuated Total Reflectance (ATR) crystal with alcohol. Then we place a small amount of the sample directly on the crystal. For solids we apply pressure with the built-in pressure for good contact. The compartment is closed and the measurement is started using the software. In Fig. 3 The FTIR spectra has two samples - one sample containing only rigid PU foam dust and MDI (blue line), and the other containing the addition of the main fillers (red line) which reveal key differences in their chemical composition66,67.

FTIR graph of Pure MDI and MDI with fillers reinforced rigid polyurethane foam.

The peaks in the blue spectrum suggest the interaction between MDI (methylene diphenyl diisocyanate) and PU foam dust, which produces typical functional groups. 4000–3500 cm− 1 shows hydrogen bonding properties with a strong peak at 3300 cm− 1 shows N-H stretching, which suggests urethane formation40. The large band between 3200 and 3600 cm− 1 is associated with O–H stretching in correspondence with surface hydroxyl groups. While the peak at 1724 cm− 1 shows C = O stretching, verifying urethane linkages, the 2270 –2250 cm⁻¹peak relates to unreacted isocyanate (-NCO) groups. Smaller peaks at 1648 and 1381 cm− 1 may be associated with traces of hydroxyl (OH) groups and may indicate a little water content. Typical of MDI’s structure, aromatic C-C and C-O-C vibrations show below 1000 cm− 1. Attributed to phenolic OH groups present in tyre materials, the 1381 cm− 1 band is more pronounced41,68. These fillers also have a little impact on current peaks; for example, extra OH groups’ reaction with the -NCO peak might lower it, and the C-O-C area (1100 –1000 cm− 1) gets more noticeable depending on filler composition, along with 1000–500 cm− 1 range consisting of aromatic rings with C-O-C bonding properties. On the other hand, the red spectrum has more or stronger peaks caused by the fillers. Especially, peaks at 3380 cm− 1 and 550 cm− 1 suggest ZrO2 cm− 1, the absorption in the lower reaches of the graph may be related to metal–oxygen bonding, this cannot evidently confirm ZrO₂ formations, ceramic element found in PCBs69.

Thermogravimetric analysis

TA Instruments SDT Q600 is the machine used for this test, where the sample should be in a small solid form. The sample, which should be clean, dry, and uniform, is placed in a pan with a small mass, which is made of aluminium. The thermogravimetric analyser’s high-precision balance is then used to hold this pan. Up to a predetermined temperature, the temperature is raised gradually in a linear fashion at a rate of 10 °C per minute. Nitrogen or argon can be used to create an inert atmosphere. Figure 4(a) shows the thermal degradation pattern via weight loss. Characteristic of the breakdown of urethane linkages and isocyanate degradation, the pure sample shows a single, sharp decomposition event at about 350–500 °C70–72.

TGA graphs of Pure MDI and MDI with fillers reinforced rigid polyurethane foam.

The filled sample’s final residual mass is greater, which implies that inorganic filler materials stay thermally stable, therefore increasing the char content. This corresponds with the FTIR peaks at 550 cm− 1 and 3380 cm− 1, which relate to ZrO and other stable oxides added by the fillers. This interpretation is further supported by the DTG graph (Fig. 4b). Around 370–400 °C, the pure sample exhibits a single, strong decomposition peak that indicates the quick thermal breakdown of urethane structures. On the other hand, the filled sample shows two weaker peaks, suggesting a multi-step degradation process. The breakdown of the PU matrix is represented by the first peak, whereas the degradation of rubber-based fillers or organics in PCB may be the cause of the second, smaller peak, which occurs at 500–550 °C. These several phases of degradation imply that fillers slow down the process and add new elements with unique thermal properties. In line with improved thermal stability, the filled sample’s lower DTG peak intensity also points to a slower rate of degradation72,73.

Di-electric simulation

The Electrostatics interface of the AC/DC module in COMSOL Multiphysics was used to simulate the electrostatic behaviour of the optimized composite. The composite was modelled as an effective homogeneous dielectric medium and a stationary electrostatic investigation was carried out. To validate the experimentation, simulation analysis is performed based on the composition of the sample. The software used is COMSOL Multiphysics to perform the dielectric test. The simulation presents a model that explores the dielectric behaviour of the confirmation sample from Table 6.A rectangular dielectric specimen, measuring 0.1 m in length and 0.07 m in height, was positioned between two parallel electrodes to represent the experimental dielectric test configuration. With local refinement close to the electrode–dielectric interfaces, a fine physics-controlled mesh was used. The average element size was about 10⁻³ m. Material properties are manually defined for each component, i.e. RPUF has a low density (120 kg/m³), very low electrical conductivity (10⁻¹⁴ S/m), low thermal conductivity (0.02 W/mK), and relative permittivity between 1.1 and 1.5. WPCB includes a density of 1850–2100 kg/m³, moderate conductivity (10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁷ S/m), and a relative permittivity of around 4–6. The bottom electrode was grounded (V = 0), and the top electrode was subjected to a fixed electric potential of 1 V. The remaining boundaries were all classified as electrically insulated. The optimized filler composition was used to determine the effective dielectric properties of the composite material, which was assumed to be homogeneous and isotropic. The sample geometry’s meshed model is displayed in Fig. 10(a). The electric field (or current density, depending on which you plotted) distribution inside the composite under the applied potential difference is depicted in Fig. 10(b). The homogeneous material assumption is validated and stable dielectric behavior is confirmed by the comparatively uniform field distribution. A capacitance-based method was used to calculate the effective dielectric constant (εr), where εr = C / C₀. In this case, C is the capacitance found using the composite material in the electrostatic simulation and C₀ is the capacitance of the same geometry filled with air. The optimal sample achieved a dielectric constant of 4.40 with an error of 1.123%. The percentage error between experimental and simulated dielectric constant values was calculated using Eq. 4.

The simulation assumes perfect interfacial bonding between fillers and matrix, neglects space-charge effects and edge fringing, and does not account for frequency or temperature dependence of dielectric properties.

Conclusion

Using the Response Surface Method and Central Composite Design, this study achieved an actual dielectric constant value of 4.45 with an experimental DC of 4.33 and a simulated DC of 4.4 with an error % of 2.772% and 1.137%, respectively at a mixture composition of 15.94 wt% WRPU, 3.0 wt% RTW, and 10.0 wt% WPCB. Model verification yielded strong accuracy with a statistically validated R² of 93.05% and adjusted R² of 88.09%. FTIR analysis confirmed the presence of atomic and chemical bonds of fillers, while TGA demonstrated enhanced thermal stability due to delayed decomposition and an increase in char residue from thermally resistant fillers. Filler dispersion and bond strength captured in the HR-SEM images indicated uniformly dispersed fillers, which confirmed microstructural reinforcement. By reusing rigid WRPU, RTW, and WPCB, they show a general trend of increasing the insulation properties through dielectric testing. The developed composites are particularly promising for use as electrical insulators in high-voltage applications such as electric vehicles and power electronics due to the increased dielectric strength and thermal resistance of the materials. The composite formulations were confirmed to reliably withstand electrical loading conditions through dielectric performance simulated in COMSOL Multiphysics simulations, which validated experimental results.

Although the dielectric constant of the materials was evaluated, some relevant parameters, such as, breakdown voltage strength and dielectric loss, were not investigated. Future work will focus on complete detailed electrical characterization to fully formulate the suitability of the material for high-voltage insulation applications.
