Genetic diversity, population structure and haplotype analysis of Babesia gibsoni based on the thrombospondin-related adhesive protein (TRAP) gene
Authors: Ansu Kumari, Divya Agnihotri, Anil Kumar Nehra, Aman Dev Moudgil, Yudhbir Singh, Prem Sagar Maurya, Devendra Prasad Pateer, Rajat Garg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the genetic diversity, population structure, and evolutionary history of Babesia gibsoni thrombospondin-related adhesive protein (BgTRAP) gene. Phylogenetic analysis revealed three distinct genotypes (BgG1-BgG3) among 47 sequences. The newly generated Indian sequences (n = 12) grouped into BgG1 and BgG3, with BgG3 showing a broad geographical distribution across Japan, India, and Taiwan. The sequences exhibited 83.3–100% nucleotide and 76.3–100% amino acid similarities. A median-joining haplotype network analysis identified 27 haplotypes, with most being unique to one country. India had the highest haplotype diversity (h = 17), followed by Japan (h = 9) and Tunisia (h = 2). The haplotype diversity of the Indian population and the combined dataset exceeded 0.8, suggesting a very high level of genetic diversity. The overall dataset showed low nucleotide diversity (0.05519 ± 0.00292) but high haplotype diversity (0.926 ± 0.029). Significant genetic differentiation was observed between India and other countries, whereas negligible differentiation was found between Taiwan and Japan. Neutrality tests suggested a constant population size. This study provides the first comprehensive insight of B. gibsoni population genetics based on the BgTRAP gene, highlighting its potential as a marker for genetic diversity studies. The high genetic diversity and distinct genotypes suggest a complex evolutionary history of the parasite.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46188-4.

Introduction

Babesia gibsoni, a tick-borne protozoan parasite, causes piroplasmosis in dogs worldwide1. It is mainly distributed across South, East, and Southeast Asia, with prevalence varying widely between countries. In South Asia, Bangladesh reports prevalence rates ranging from 4.61 to 30%2, while India shows considerable heterogeneity, with estimates spanning 0.66–84.9%3–8. In East Asia, reported prevalence ranges from 0.1–64.2% in China9–12, 1.9–30.4% in Japan13,14, 1.8–38% in South Korea15, and 10.6–15.7% in Taiwan16,17. In Southeast Asia, Malaysia and Thailand have reported prevalence rates of 0–17.7%18 and 6.3–9.4%19, respectively. Given India’s vast geographical diversity and notably high prevalence, the present study focuses on elucidating the genetic diversity and population structure of B. gibsoni to support improved disease management strategies in this region.

The primary mode of transmission for B. gibsoni is through the bite of infected tick vectors, specifically Haemaphysalis longicornis, H. bispinosa, and Rhipicephalus sanguineus, as reported in multiple studies20–24. Additionally, non-vector associated transmission has been documented, occurring via direct contact during canine agonistic behavior25, blood transfusions, and transplacental transmission26. Interestingly, canine bites have been identified as the predominant transmission route27,28. The non-vector transmission pathways enable clonal expansion, leading to reduced genetic diversity within the parasite population.

Genetic characterization of B. gibsoni of canine origin has been previously conducted on the basis of 18S rRNA19,9,10,6,2,12, B. gibsoni thrombospondin related adhesive protein (BgTRAP)4,9,16,8, cytochrome b (cytb)9, cytochrome oxidase subunit I (COXI), cytochrome oxidase subunit III (COXIII)3, apical membrane antigen 1 (BgAMA1)5, 50 kDa surface antigen (BgP50)5, and 70 kDa heat shock protein (BgHSP70)5 genes, and Internal Transcribed Spacer region 1 (ITS 1)9,10,6. The TRAP gene is crucial for parasite survival, mediating host cell recognition, adhesion, and invasion29,2. As a conserved member of the adhesive protein family, it provides a reliable molecular marker for assessing genetic diversity and population structure4,16. Its functional importance not only aids in understanding parasite transmission and host–parasite interactions but also highlights potential targets for diagnostics and interventions, making it an ideal choice for elucidating parasite biology9,8.

The 18S rRNA gene is commonly used to differentiate canine piroplasms, and nested PCR-RFLP analysis can distinguish B. vogeli, B. canis, and B. gibsoni25. However, sequence conservation of the 18S rRNA gene and ITS1 region suggests clonal expansion30,1,6. The COXI gene, a widely used barcode marker, offers robust species identification and phylogenetic insights, making it suitable for studying B. gibsoni genetic diversity due to its moderate evolutionary rate and maternal inheritance pattern31. In contrast, the BgTRAP gene has been found to exhibit genetic diversity29,16,2. This gene encodes the BgTRAP protein, a type-I transmembrane protein expressed in sporozoites and merozoites, playing a crucial role in parasite motility and invasion32. Studies have investigated the genetic diversity of BgTRAP in Asian isolates4,29,16,8,2, and it has been identified as a promising candidate for serodiagnosis and vaccine development33,34.

Effective control of canine babesiosis relies on precise identification of the parasite; however, the genetic diversity and clinical impact of B. gibsoni infections in India remain poorly understood. Although previous studies4,8 have explored molecular characterization of the BgTRAP gene in dogs from northern (Uttarakhand and Uttar Pradesh), north-eastern (Assam and West Bengal), and southern (Kerala) states, the principal knowledge gap—the lack of comprehensive haplotype analysis and neutrality testing—persists. This study addresses this gap through molecular analysis of the BgTRAP gene in north Indian dogs to elucidate the genetic diversity, population structure, and evolutionary dynamics of B. gibsoni.

Materials and methods

Blood sample collection and genomic deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) extraction

Between May 2022 and May 2023, approximately 2 mL of peripheral blood was collected from 12 dogs across three Indian states (Haryana, Rajasthan, and Uttar Pradesh) and one union territory (New Delhi) using EDTA-coated tubes. As described previously31, these privately owned dogs were presented to the Department of Veterinary Clinical Complex, College of Veterinary Sciences, Lala Lajpat Rai University of Veterinary and Animal Sciences, Hisar. The animals exhibited clinical signs including fever, anaemia, lymphadenopathy, pale mucous membranes, jaundice, tachypnea, weakness, inappetence to anorexia, and tick infestation or a history of tick infestation, and were microscopically confirmed positive for small Babesia spp. Since co-infections are common in dogs from tropical and subtropical regions, all 12 samples were screened for Ehrlichia canis35, Anaplasma platys36, Hepatozoon canis35, Babesia vogeli37,38, and Trypanosoma evansi39,40 using molecular tests. None of the samples tested positive for these pathogens. The sample details are provided in Supplementary Table 1. This research received approval from the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) at Lala Lajpat Rai University of Veterinary and Animal Sciences (LUVAS), Hisar, Haryana, India (Approval No. VCC/IAEC/2022/1679–1705, dated May 17, 2022; Agenda No. 24) and all the experiments were conducted following relevant guidelines and regulations, in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Genomic DNA was extracted from 200 µL of whole anticoagulated blood using the QIAamp DNA mini kit (Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted DNA was then evaluated for purity, concentration, and quality (260/280 ratio) using optical spectrophotometry (Nanodrop, Thermo Scientific), as described previously (Kumari et al.,39. The DNA was stored at -20 °C until further use. No-template, and negative and positive controls were included in each Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR), consisting of nuclease-free water, and DNA from a known negative and an earlier confirmed B. gibsoni-infected adult dog31, respectively.

Molecular confirmation of B. gibsoni infection using conventional PCR assay targeting the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COXI) gene

Genomic DNA from 12 blood samples was subjected to COXI gene-based PCR assay for confirmation of B. gibsoni infection. The partial mitochondrial COXI gene (649 bp) was amplified using published primers (Bgib649F 5′-ATGACTGGTTTATTTGGAGGTATT-3′ and Bgib649R 5′-GAGCCCATACTAAACATCCTAAT-3′) in a T100 Thermal Cycler31. PCR amplification was performed in a 25 µL reaction volume with 12.5 µL GoTaq Green Master Mix (Promega, USA), 2.0 µL template DNA, 0.5 µL each forward and reverse primers (10 pmol/µL) and 9.5 µL nuclease-free water. The PCR conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of amplification (98 °C for 30 s, 50 °C for 40 s, and 72 °C for 40 s), and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplified product was visualized by agarose gel electrophoresis (1.5%) stained with ethidium bromide under UV transillumination (Biorad, USA; Supplementary Fig. 1), as described previously41,42.

Optimization of the B. gibsoni thrombospondin related adhesive protein (BgTRAP) gene-based PCR assay and sequencing of B. gibsoni

A previously published primer set (Bgib855F 5’- ACCCAGCGAATACAATGCACCACA-3’ and Bgib855R 5’- AGCCCTGCAA AACCGGCAAGTATG-3’) was utilized to amplify an 855 bp region of the B. gibsoni genome containing variable nucleotide repeats8. The PCR reaction was set up in a 25 µL volume, comprising 12.5 µL of Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, USA), 15–25 ng of template DNA, 0.75 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 pmol/µL), and nuclease-free water to make up the remaining volume. The PCR amplification was performed using a T100 Thermal Cycler (Biorad) with the following conditions: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 35 cycles of amplification (98 °C for 10 s, 69 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 1 min), and a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. The amplified products were then analyzed using 1.2% Tris-acetate-EDTA agarose gel electrophoresis, as described previously (Supplementary Fig. 2), and sized using the DNAmark 100 bp ladder (G-Biosciences, USA).

Following PCR amplification, the products from all 12 isolates were purified using the MinElute Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany). The purity and concentration of the purified products were then determined using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Three separate amplicons (~ 855 bp) of each isolate were subsequently sent to AgriGenome Labs Pvt. Ltd. (Cochin, Kerala, India) for custom DNA sequencing to minimize sequencing errors. Bidirectional Sanger sequencing was performed using the Bgib855F and Bgib855R primers. The raw nucleotide sequences were analyzed using BioEdit version 7.0.5.343, as described previously44, to obtain a consensus sequence of each isolate. Each sequence was then subjected to pairwise alignment with reference sequences from the GenBank using the NCBI Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLASTn; https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) for identification purposes.

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis of B. gibsoni based on the BgTRAP gene

The BLAST program of NCBI (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) was used to acquire the homologous sequences of the BgTRAP gene of B. gibsoni available in the GenBank using the default matrix corresponding to positions one and 764 of the gene sequence of Hisar isolate (OR577262, Haryana, India) of B. gibsoni generated in the present study. Smaller, truncated, and degenerate sequences were not included in the analysis; only sequences having nearly complete sequence length of BgTRAP gene (> 749 bp) were used for further study. A total of 47 sequences of B. gibsoni originating from diverse geographical locations, including 12 novel sequences generated in this study, were included in the data set (Supplementary Table 1).

A multiple sequence alignment was performed using 47 sequences with the ClustalW program in MEGA-X version 10.1.745, with default parameters as described by Nehra et al.,46. Phylogenetic inference was conducted using the maximum likelihood method, employing the Hasegawa-Kishino-Yano (HKY;47 and Jones-Taylor-Thornton (JTT + F;48 models, with 1000 bootstrap replicates for nucleotide and amino acid sequences, respectively. The analysis encompassed 47 sequences, comprising 821 aligned nucleotide positions and 273 aligned amino acid positions (Fig. 1a, b).

Genetic diversity and haplotype network analysis

The BLASTn tool was utilized to evaluate identity, query coverage, and e-values using default parameters and the non-redundant (nr) database of the NCBI GenBank49. Comparative analyses of the nucleotide (Supplementary File 1, 2) and amino acid sequences (Fig. 2; Supplementary File 3) of the BgTRAP gene of B. gibsoni isolates were conducted to identify sequence variations. This involved comparing the newly generated sequences with each other and with existing sequences in the GenBank. Nucleotide and amino acid identity percentages (Tables 1 and 2) were calculated using the MegAlign program within the DNASTAR Lasergene 6.0 package50. Supplementary Table 1 lists the BgTRAP gene sequences of Indian and foreign isolates/strains, along with their accession numbers.

Population genetic structure and demographic dynamics

Genetic differences were assessed using various statistics, including haplotype-based (Hs) and nucleotide sequence-based (Ks) metrics, as well as parameters indicative of gene flow (Nm), such as pairwise nucleotide differences (Kxy), haplotype frequency-based genetic differentiation (Gst), nucleotide-based statistics (Nst), nucleotide substitutions per site (Dxy), and net nucleotide substitutions per site (Da), using DnaSP version 6.12.03 (Supplementary Table 2)52,54. To investigate genetic differentiation and population structure, gene flow (Nm) and pairwise genetic distance (FST) between populations (Fig. 6) were calculated using Arlequin 3.5.2 (Supplementary Table 3)55. Additionally, Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA) was performed using the Tajima-Nei substitution model with 1000 permutations to test the significance of FST values (Table 4).

Results

Molecular detection of B. gibsoni by conventional PCR targeting the mitochondrial COXI gene

PCR amplification yielded a ~ 649 bp amplicon (Supplementary Fig. 1), confirmed by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis with ethidium bromide staining. All samples tested positive for B. gibsoni infection.

PCR amplification and sequencing of the nuclear BgTRAP gene of B. gibsoni

PCR amplification yielded an ~ 855 bp amplicon (Supplementary Fig. 2), confirmed by 1.2% agarose gel electrophoresis with ethidium bromide staining. Consistent with the COXI gene results, all 12 samples tested positive for B. gibsoni infection, which was further validated by sequencing. The sequences of all isolates were submitted to GenBank, and the accession numbers (OR577262-OR577273) are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis of B. gibsoni based on the BgTRAP gene

The phylogenetic trees were constructed using nucleotide (Fig. 1a) and amino acid (Fig. 1b) sequences of the BgTRAP gene from Indian isolates (including 12 new sequences) and foreign isolates available in GenBank. The Hasegawa-Kishino-Yano47 and Jones-Taylor-Thornton48 models were identified as the best-fit models for nucleotide and amino acid sequences, respectively. Both phylograms revealed three distinct clades, designated as B. gibsoni Genotypes 1–3 (BgG1-G3). The 47 sequences analyzed were distributed among these genotypes as follows: BgG1 (n = 27), BgG2 (n = 2), and BgG3 (n = 18), with BgG1 being the most prevalent. The 12 new Indian sequences fell into two clades: BgG1 and BgG3. Notably, BgG1 and BgG2 were predominantly represented by Indian and Japanese isolates, respectively. BgG2, comprising only two Japanese sequences, showed a close association with BgG3 and may represent a novel genotype. In contrast, BgG3 exhibited a broader geographical distribution, including Japan, India, and Taiwan. Furthermore, sequences from the same country tended to cluster together within each genotype (Fig. 1).

Analyses of sequences, haplotype network and genetic diversity

The BgTRAP gene sequences of B. gibsoni (n = 47) showed 83.3–100% nucleotide (nt) and 76.3–100% amino acid (aa) identity (Table 1). The 12 new Indian sequences from New Delhi, Haryana, and Uttar Pradesh exhibited 86.1–100% nt and 78.7–100% aa identity among themselves. Within genotypes, BgG1, BgG2, and BgG3 showed high sequence identity: 93.9–100% (nt) and 96.4–100% (aa) for BgG1, 100% for BgG2, and 96.3–100% (nt) and 96.5–100% (aa) for BgG3 (Table 2). In contrast, low sequence identity was observed between BgG1 and BgG2 (85.1–88.5% nt, 77.5–80.1% aa) and between BgG1 and BgG3 (83.3–88.4% nt, 76.3–81.1% aa). The multiple nucleotide and amino acid sequence alignment of the BgTRAP gene of BgG1, BgG2 and BgG3 genotypes indicated the presence of sequence variations at 87, 0 and 42, and 37, 0 and 23 places, respectively. Similarly, nucleotide variations were observed at 167, 196 and 51, and 87, 104 and 29 places between BgG1 and BgG2, BgG1 and BgG3, and BgG2 and BgG3 genotypes, respectively.

Further analysis of sequence alignments revealed extensive variations throughout the consensus sequences, with notable hotspots between nucleotide positions 178–628 (Supplementary File 1) and amino acid positions 59–193(Fig. 2). Fifty-one single amino acid substitutions and nine single amino acid deletions (positions 122–125 and 189–193) were observed. Additionally, 60 molecular signatures were identified in the BgTRAP gene (Fig. 2). These findings highlight substantial genetic heterogeneity, with three distinct genotypes emerging from the analysis.

In total, 47 sequences of the nuclear BgTRAP gene were analyzed to investigate the relationship between B. gibsoni haplotypes and their country of origin. The median-joining haplotype network revealed 27 haplotypes (Fig. 3), with six haplotypes consisting of multiple sequences (2–12 sequences). Haplotypes from the same country tended to cluster together, exhibiting geographical clustering (Fig. 4). Only one haplotype (Hap_3; 3.70%) was shared between two countries, while 26 haplotypes (96.30%) were unique to one country. The major haplotypes, Hap_2, Hap_3, Hap_1, and Hap_4, were represented by 12, four, and three sequences, respectively. Furthermore, Hap_10 and Hap_12 were represented by two sequences each, while the remaining haplotypes were represented by only one sequence (Supplementary Table 2). The network’s star-like shape, centered around Hap_2, suggests rapid population expansion in India. The interior location of Hap_2, Hap_3, and Hap_4 indicates that these are older haplotypes (Figs. 4 and 5). India had the highest number of haplotypes (h = 17), followed by Japan (h = 9) and Tunisia (h = 2; Fig. 3; Table 3). Both location-wise (Fig. 4) and genotype-wise (Fig. 5) median-joining haplotype networks showed three distinct clusters corresponding to the BgG1, BgG2, and BgG3 genotypes. Each genotype comprised a distinct set of haplotypes, with BgG1 having the highest number of haplotypes (16), followed by BgG3 (10), and BgG2 (1). These findings supported the phylogenetic analysis results.

Table 3 summarizes the genetic diversity indices of B. gibsoni populations based on the BgTRAP gene. Across the 821 bp alignment, 116 nucleotide mutations were detected at 114 sites. Nucleotide diversity (π) was low, ranging from 0.00065 ± 0.00035 to 0.02208 ± 0.00918, while haplotype diversity (Hd) was high, ranging from 0.500 ± 0.265 to 0.936 ± 0.051. Nucleotide diversity was highest in India, followed by Japan and Taiwan, whereas haplotype diversity was highest in Japan, followed by India and lowest in Taiwan. The Indian population and the combined dataset showed haplotype diversity > 0.8, indicating high haplotype diversity. The average number of nucleotide differences (k) between sequences within populations ranged from 0.5 (Taiwan) to 16.27 (India).

Within B. gibsoni genotypes, nucleotide diversity (π) was low (0.00000 ± 0.00000 to 0.00459 ± 0.00106), while haplotype diversity (Hd) was high (0.000 ± 0.000 to 0.915 ± 0.041). BgG3 had the highest nucleotide and haplotype diversity, followed by BgG1 and BgG2. Both BgG1 and BgG3 genotypes showed haplotype diversity > 0.8, indicating high haplotype diversity. The average number of nucleotide differences (k) between sequences within genotypes was low (0.00000 to 3.50980), suggesting sequence homogeneity (Table 3). Overall, the 47 B. gibsoni sequences showed low nucleotide diversity (0.05519 ± 0.00292) but high haplotype diversity (0.926 ± 0.029).

Population-genetic structure and demographic history

Gene flow and genetic differentiation indices between B. gibsoni populations and genotypes based on the BgTRAP gene are presented in Supplementary Table 3. The proportion of nucleotide differences (Kxy) and nucleotide substitutions per site (Dxy) between populations ranged from 4.01923 (Taiwan and Japan) to 71.14872 (India and Japan) and 0.00545 (Taiwan and Japan) to 0.09654 (India and Japan), respectively. Significant genetic differentiation was observed between India and Taiwan (FST > 0.25, P < 0.05) and India and Japan, indicating limited gene flow (Fig. 6a). In contrast, Taiwan and Japan showed negligible genetic differentiation (FST = 0.02986) and high gene flow (Nm = 16.24587). Gene flow (Fig. 6c) was low between India and Japan (Nm = 0.11156) and India and Taiwan (Nm = 0.12274).

Between B. gibsoni genotypes, the proportion of nucleotide differences (Kxy) and nucleotide substitutions per site (Dxy) ranged from 9.94445 (BgG2 and BgG3) to 78.89301 (BgG1 and BgG3) and 0.01349 (BgG2 and BgG3) to 0.10705 (BgG1 and BgG3), respectively. Pairwise FST values (Fig. 6b) between all genotypes were statistically significant (P < 0.05), indicating substantial genetic differentiation (> 0.25). In contrast, gene flow (Nm) between genotypes (Fig. 6d) was low, ranging from 0.01293 (BgG1 and BgG2) to 0.22500 (BgG2 and BgG3).

AMOVA analysis of B. gibsoni sequences (Table 4) showed that genetic variation between populations (81.35%) exceeded variation within populations (18.65%). Similarly, genetic variation between genotypes (96.60%) was higher than variation within genotypes (3.40%).

Table 3 summarizes neutrality tests for B. gibsoni populations and genotypes based on the BgTRAP gene. For Indian, Japanese, and Taiwanese populations, non-significant negative Tajima’s D and Fu-Li’s F values (P > 0.10), small positive Ramos-Onsins and Rozas’ R2 values, and mismatch distributions (Fig. 7) indicated a constant population size. Similarly, the overall dataset showed non-significant positive values of Tajima’s D and Fu and Li’s F (P > 0.10), small positive value of Ramos-Onsins and Rozas’ R2, and a bimodal mismatch distribution (Fig. 7), suggesting a constant population size. In contrast, the BgG1 genotype showed significant negative Tajima’s D and Fu-Li’s F values (P < 0.02), a small positive R2 value, and a skewed unimodal mismatch distribution (Fig. 7), indicating population expansion. The BgG3 genotype had non-significant negative values of Tajima’s D and Fu-Li’s F (P > 0.10), a small positive R2 value (Table 3), and a bimodal mismatch distribution (Fig. 7), suggesting a constant population size.

Discussion

Diagnosing and treating B. gibsoni infections, particularly in chronic carriers and subclinical cases, poses a significant challenge for clinicians31. Understanding the genetic diversity of the parasite is crucial for developing effective control measures37,38,6. Although the 18S ribosomal RNA gene has been commonly used in molecular epidemiology studies of B. gibsoni19,9,10,6,2,12, research by Lee et al.,16 and Chao et al.,58 suggests its limitations due to high conservation and limited strain diversity information. In contrast, the BgTRAP gene is hypothesized to be a more informative marker because it encodes a thrombospondin-related adhesive protein involved in parasite invasion processes and is known to exhibit higher levels of sequence variability. Previous studies in apicomplexan parasites have demonstrated that TRAP family genes evolve more rapidly than ribosomal genes8, making them suitable for distinguishing closely related genotypes and for population-level studies. Given these constraints, this study employed the BgTRAP gene to investigate the genetic diversity, haplotype distribution, and population structure of B. gibsoni, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of the parasite’s genetic landscape. All samples analyzed were confirmed negative for concurrent infections with other parasites, including Ehrlichia canis, Anaplasma platys, Hepatozoon canis, Babesia vogeli, and Trypanosoma evansi, ensuring that the TRAP gene-based diversity and population structure analyses presented here remain uncompromised by co-infections.

Phylogenetic analysis of the BgTRAP gene revealed three distinct clades (BgG1-G3) among B. gibsoni sequences, suggesting the existence of multiple genotypes. As more genetic characterizations are reported from diverse regions and new sequences are submitted to databases, additional genotypes may be identified46,12. The BgTRAP gene-based phylogeny showed geographical sub-structuring in B. gibsoni populations, with isolates from each country clustering together, similar to the COXI gene31. The consistency between nucleotide and amino acid-based phylogenies supported this finding. Furthermore, the genetic divergence between these genotypes raises the possibility that they may represent distinct species.

The significant divergence observed in the BgTRAP gene of B. gibsoni indicates high genetic variability, likely due to vector-mediated transmission. Similar variations have been reported in other parasites, such as in the Tams1 gene of Theileria annulata59, and 18S rRNA gene of Babesia caballi46 and Theileria equi60. This sequence heterogeneity emphasizes the need to understand intraspecies variations for developing diagnostic tests that can detect all genotypes. Identifying gene segments with nucleotide or amino acid variations is crucial for designing specific oligonucleotides and probes to detect genotype-specific infections60.

Previous studies using 18S rRNA and ITS-1 markers reported low genetic diversity in B. gibsoni, suggesting clonal expansion of a single strain1,6,12. In contrast, the BgTRAP gene has been shown to be highly diverse4,29,16,8, with variations in the TSR and CTD regions29,16. Our analysis revealed high haplotype diversity (~ 0.93) and low nucleotide diversity, indicating significant genetic diversity between B. gibsoni populations due to unique sequence variations. This finding is consistent with previous reports from India4,8. Notably, haplotypes from the Indian population (BgG1) were distinct from those of foreign populations, with a difference of at least 70 nucleotides. The Indian population exhibited high haplotype diversity (~ 0.84), driven by multiple single haplotypes around the major haplotype, Hap_2. Similarly, the BgG1 genotype showed high genetic diversity (~ 0.81). Compared to COXI31, BgTRAP provided finer resolution of intraspecies diversity, revealing distinct genotype-specific clusters and greater sequence divergence among B. gibsoni populations.

The neutral theory of molecular evolution, proposed by Motoo Kimura, argues that most molecular variation results from the random fixation of neutral mutations, predicting a steady, linear accumulation of differences over time as a molecular clock. Yet it has clear limitations: it underestimates selection, overlooks non-neutral mutations, and fails to account for variation shaped by linkage, epistasis, and epigenetic factors61. While polymorphism levels depend on effective population size and mutation rate62, they also vary across genes and regions due to functional constraints, background selection, and recombination effects63. In contrast, the maximum genetic diversity theory suggests that as organisms evolve greater complexity, genetic diversity reaches a plateau constrained by functional and epigenetic requirements. Most variation is seen as functional and under selective pressures, with sexual reproduction reducing excess diversity. Thus, neutral theory emphasizes neutrality and gradual accumulation, whereas maximum genetic diversity theory highlights functional constraints, optimum diversity, and punctuated evolutionary patterns, pointing to the need for a broader framework to explain genetic diversity.

The haplotype network analysis of B. gibsoni based on the BgTRAP gene revealed geographical clustering of haplotypes, with most haplotypes unique to one country and only one shared between two countries. This suggests that dogs in each country are infected with novel haplotypes37,38,31. The Indian haplotype network showed a stellate shape, indicating rapid population expansion due to low sequence disparity and high unique mutations57,64. Hap_2, Hap_3, and Hap_4 appeared to be ancestral, suggesting a long history. Similarly, the genotype-wise haplotype network showed clustering of haplotypes by genotype, with BgG1 being the most prevalent, followed by BgG3. The BgG1 genotype also exhibited a stellate shape, indicating rapid expansion. The haplotype analysis clearly separated the three novel genotypes, corroborating sequence and phylogenetic analyses, similar to findings in the Tams1 gene of T. annulata59. The co-existence of diverse haplotypes in a geographical location may facilitate interbreeding and genetic recombination in tick vectors during sexual reproduction, potentially increasing genetic diversity65,66. Furthermore, variations in haplotype transmission capabilities may influence haplotype distribution and composition in specific regions67.

Pairwise FST analysis revealed significant genetic differentiation between B. gibsoni populations from India and Taiwan, and India and Japan, with limited gene flow. In contrast, Taiwan and Japan populations showed negligible genetic differentiation, indicating substantial gene flow. The three B. gibsoni genotypes also exhibited significant genetic differentiation, likely due to restricted gene flow and mutations68,64. Horizontal gene transfer may occur through introduction of exotic breeds, migration of infected dogs and ticks, and expansion of tick vectors to new regions. In addition, an increase in the fighting dog population and their transport to other countries can also be accountable. Vertical transmission also occurs through transplacental transmission from parents to offspring26,69. Neutrality indices and mismatch distributions suggest a stable population size across Indian, Japanese, and Taiwanese populations.

Genetic diversity in B. gibsoni differs between acute and chronic infections. Acute cases with high parasitemia may exhibit a broad range of haplotypes or, in instances of direct transmission, clonal expansion with reduced diversity. In contrast, chronic infections characterized by low, persistent parasitemia often favor specific haplotypes under immune pressure, resulting in altered population structures6. Regional differences in diversity are further influenced by limited gene flow, demographic constraints, and transmission mode, with non-tick routes typically reducing variation compared to tick-borne sexual reproduction31,12. Clinically, disease severity ranges from acute hemolysis to chronic, asymptomatic carriage, reflecting both host immunity and parasite genetics. This aspect was not evaluated in this study and should be considered a limitation.

Conclusions

This study revealed genetic diversity among B. gibsoni isolates from various regions and provided novel insights into haplotype and population structure analyses of the parasite. Phylogenetic analysis based on the B. gibsoni thrombospondin-related adhesive protein (BgTRAP) gene suggested the existence of three genotypes, with the presence of two genotypes in Haryana. A relatively low percent identity was noticed within (93.9–100%) and between (83.3–88.4%) B. gibsoni genotypes based on the BgTRAP gene. A low nucleotide and high haplotype diversities were recorded in B. gibsoni isolates. Dogs, in general, were infected with the novel haplotypes of B. gibsoni in each country, in addition to few haplotypes found elsewhere. The three B. gibsoni genotypes based on the BgTRAP gene displayed huge genetic differentiation with a very low level of gene flow between them. Neutrality tests and mismatch distributions for the Indian B. gibsoni population suggested a constant population size.
