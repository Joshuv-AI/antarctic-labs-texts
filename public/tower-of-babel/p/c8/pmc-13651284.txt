Evaluating the informational accuracy of large language models in patient‑directed orthodontic retainer guidance: a cross‑sectional comparison of Chat GPT‑4.1, Gemini 2.5, Microsoft Copilot GPT-4.1 and DeepSeek‑V3
Authors: Muhammad Mughni, Muhammad Raif Ilyas, Syed Muhammad Ali Gilani, Mubassar Fida, Rashna Hoshang Sukhia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe use of artificial intelligence (AI) in orthodontic practice is increasing rapidly; however, there is a notable lack of research evaluating the accuracy of large language models (LLMs) in educating patients about orthodontic retainers and related guidelines.Materials and methodsThis study utilized a cross-sectional, repeated‑measures comparative evaluation design after receiving exemption from the institutional ethics committee. A set of 110 questions related to orthodontic retainers was compiled from previous articles addressing concerns about retainers and approved by a panel of three orthodontists. These questions were submitted to large language models (LLMs), including ChatGPT, Copilot, DeepSeek and Google Gemini. The responses were then reviewed by six independent orthodontists, who rated them using a modified five-point Likert scale.ResultsThe overall accuracy revealed that 68.6% of responses scored 4, while 15.3% achieved a perfect score of 5. Among the LLMs, Gemini ranked first with 96.8%, closely followed by ChatGPT at 95.6%, indicating comparable high‑level performance between these models, while DeepSeek (76.9%) and Copilot (66.2%) demonstrated comparatively lower accuracy. Gemini produced a higher proportion of perfect scores, whereas ChatGPT consistently achieved strong ratings. The mean ratings across six raters demonstrated strong reliability (ICC = 0.81), reflecting expert agreement.ConclusionsFindings suggest that AI models such as ChatGPT and Gemini can generate patient‑directed orthodontic retainer information with high informational accuracy under controlled evaluation conditions. However, specialist oversight remains essential to ensure clinical applicability. Future research using larger and more diverse datasets is needed to assess broader educational and communication‑related outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08960-w.

Introduction

The revolution of artificial intelligence has transformed healthcare by changing how professionals gather information, deliver treatment, and communicate with patients [1]. AI tools in orthodontics are beginning to play a significant role in supporting clinical tasks and educating patients, particularly after treatment [2]. Once orthodontic treatment is completed one of the main concern arises in patients mind is about retainers: how to use, clean and care for them, and for how long they need to wear them [3, 4]. The post‑treatment retention phase is clinically critical, as inadequate retainer use or poor adherence is strongly associated with relapse and long‑term instability of orthodontic outcomes, highlighting the need for accurate and consistent patient education during this period [5]. This information is highly significant at this stage and can influence long-term treatment success, making effective communication essential [6].

Advancements in large language models (LLMs), including ChatGPT-4.1, DeepSeek-V3, Gemini 2.5, and Microsoft Copilot GPT-4.1, have enabled these models to deliver highly personalized and interactive responses across multiple domains [7]. These advance models replicate human-like communication by utilizing extensive linguistic datasets and sophisticated natural language processing frameworks [8]. However, despite their ability to appear confident and fluent, their outputs may occasionally lack clinical accuracy or contextual depth, which is particularly concerning in healthcare-related interactions [9, 10].

Recent studies have demonstrated expanding applications of large language models in dentistry, spanning text‑based tasks such as clinical knowledge support and patient education, and extending to multimodal diagnostic evaluations in which LLMs synthesize radiographic and pathological inputs, including panoramic and cone‑beam computed tomography images as well as intraoral and extraoral photographic data for clinical decision support [11]. Prior work has assessed LLM performance across diagnostic and treatment‑related domains using panoramic radiographs, lateral cephalometric radiographs, hand-wrist radiographs, highlighting the increasing role of AI systems across diverse aspects of dental and orthodontic care [12].

Orthodontic care requires accurate and comprehensive responses, even for seemingly basic questions about retainers [13]. Misleading information can lead to patient confusion, poor compliance, and compromised treatment outcomes [14]. Although AI tools are becoming more advanced and more accessible, their role in patient education on clinical and post-treatment topics remains insufficiently explored [15]. Additionally, variations in regional orthodontic practices can influence how patients interpret this information, emphasizing the need for expert oversight [16].

The rise of artificial intelligence in healthcare raises critical concerns including responsibility, accountability, and ethics. Errors or misleading outputs from AI platforms can lead to patient anxiety, harm and compromised orthodontic outcomes [17]. Recognizing both the strengths and limitation of these systems is essential to ensure their safe and effective integration into clinical practice.

Despite these advances, the informational accuracy of LLM‑generated content intended specifically for patient education during the post‑orthodontic retention phase remains insufficiently studied. Existing research has largely focused on image‑based diagnostic tasks or clinician‑oriented queries, leaving uncertainty regarding how reliably contemporary LLMs address common patient questions related to retainer use, maintenance, and compliance [11, 12]. The present study addresses this gap by providing a systematic, expert‑based comparative evaluation of widely used LLMs focused on patient‑directed orthodontic retainer guidance.

To bridge this gap, this study evaluated the performance of widely used AI chatbots in orthodontic education. Four platforms were assessed: ChatGPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5, Microsoft Copilot GPT-4.1 and new emerging chatbot DeepSeek‑V3. The findings offer a clearer understanding of AI’s potential and limitations in post-orthodontic treatment care, identifying opportunities and areas for improvement. By analyzing responses to frequently asked questions about retainers, this study provided critical insights into their reliability and scope.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval for this cross-sectional study was granted by the Ethical Review Committee (ERC), protocol number 2025-11965-36354. This study utilized a cross‑sectional, repeated‑measures comparative evaluation design, in which a standardized set of orthodontic retainer‑related questions was submitted to multiple AI platforms and the resulting responses were independently evaluated by the expert panel of six orthodontists with three years of post-graduate orthodontic clinical and teaching experience. A comprehensive 110-item questionnaire was developed by the primary investigator to capture common patient concerns and compliance issues related to orthodontic retainers. For each AI platform, ratings from the six expert evaluators were aggregated at the question level, and the question–model pair constituted the primary unit of analysis. The questionnaire was developed as an expert‑derived evaluative tool intended to assess the informational accuracy and clinical appropriateness of AI‑generated responses to orthodontic retainer‑related questions. The questionnaire was intentionally designed to capture a broad range of patient‑directed concerns, including clinical, behavioral, and psychosocial aspects of orthodontic retainer use. While the items span multiple domains to reflect real‑world patient variability, they were collectively evaluated for informational accuracy within a unified analytical framework. While the items were informed by existing literature and reviewed by experienced orthodontists for relevance and completeness, no formal validation procedures such as content validity indexing, pilot testing, or psychometric validation were undertaken. Therefore, the questionnaire should be interpreted as an expert‑derived evaluative instrument rather than a formally validated psychometric tool. Questions were selected through a structured review of relevant literature addressing frequently reported patient concerns related to orthodontic retainers. The selection process aimed to ensure broad domain coverage, including aspects of retainer use, maintenance, compliance, lifestyle considerations, and patient‑perceived challenges. Although a formal systematic review methodology was not employed, the approach was guided by clinical relevance and comprehensiveness of patient‑directed content [18–20]. The final questionnaire was reviewed and approved by a panel of three independent orthodontic experts.

The 110-item questionnaire addressed common patient concerns about orthodontic retainers, including different retainer designs, lifestyle-related issues, recommended duration of wear, compliance challenges, and protocols for cleaning, storage, and transportation. The complete 110-item orthodontic retainer questions used in this study is provided as Supplementary File 1 to allow independent evaluation of item content, domain coverage, and reproducibility.

The goal was to capture the full spectrum of issues patients may encounter during the post-treatment phase, which could lead to anxiety or confusion about their orthodontic treatment.

Following questionnaire development, the 110-question set on orthodontic retainers was submitted to four AI platforms: ChatGPT-4.1, DeepSeek-V3, Gemini 2.5 and Microsoft Copilot GPT-4.1. All questions were submitted to the AI platforms as direct, stand‑alone natural‑language queries without the use of prompt engineering, system instructions, or role‑based prompting. This approach was adopted to reflect typical patient information‑seeking behavior. All queries were submitted in English using identical wording and were administered contemporaneously on a single date (15 July 2025) to reduce temporal variability related to iterative AI model updates. The specific AI model versions used at the time of access are reported for transparency. All interactions with the AI platforms were conducted using their default system configurations, without user‑level control of generation parameters such as temperature, randomness, or response regeneration. Each question was submitted once per platform, and responses were not regenerated, edited, or refined through follow‑up prompting. No system‑level instructions or prompt‑engineering techniques were applied. This approach was adopted to reflect typical patient information‑seeking behavior and to ensure consistency across platforms while preserving ecological validity. Responses generated by each chatbot were evaluated by a panel of six orthodontic experts, each with a minimum of three years of postgraduate and clinical experience, using a modified five-point Likert scale (Fig. 1). Ratings ranged from “very inaccurate” (1) to “very accurate” (5), and only responses rated 4 or 5 were classified as accurate for statistical evaluation, reflecting a clinically meaningful threshold for acceptable patient‑directed information. This dichotomization was used solely for descriptive classification of accuracy (i.e., reporting proportions of acceptable responses), while all statistical analyses were performed using the full ordinal Likert scale to preserve the underlying data structure and variability.

All AI-generated responses were anonymized and presented in a standardized format. A single-blinded (assessor-blinded) evaluation design was employed, in which expert raters were blinded to the identity of the AI platforms during the assessment process to minimize potential evaluator bias. The panel assessed each answer for accuracy and practical applicability. Intra- and inter-rater reliability were calculated using intra-class correlation (ICC) for single and average measures to determine consistency and agreement among reviewers. The expert panel consisted of six orthodontists with a minimum of three years of postgraduate clinical experience. All panelists were actively involved in orthodontic clinical practice, with routine management of post‑treatment retention protocols, and held academic or teaching roles within postgraduate orthodontic training programs in different dental teaching institutes. Several panelists also had prior experience in academic research and peer‑reviewed publications related to orthodontic outcomes and retention‑phase care.

Statistical analysis

SPSS software version 27.0 was used for all the data analysis. The criteria for rejecting null hypothesis across all the tests was set at p ≤ 0.05 for all statistical analyses. To assess the normality of the data, Shapiro-Wilk test was used which indicated non-normal distribution. For comparison, descriptive statistics were used to calculate the median and frequency distributions of scores across the four chatbots. To compare differences in scores Friedman Two-Way Analysis of Variance by Ranks (ANOVA) was employed. Upon observing a significant difference, post-hoc pairwise comparison between chatbots was done using Wilcoxon signed rank test. For Wilcoxon signed‑rank pairwise comparisons, effect sizes were conceptualized using the standardized statistic (r), calculated as the Z statistic divided by the square root of the total number of observations. Inter‑rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on a two‑way random‑effects model with absolute agreement. Both single‑measure and average‑measure ICC values were calculated, corresponding to ICC (2,1) and ICC (2,6), respectively. Cronbach’s alpha was additionally calculated to assess internal consistency among raters. Accuracy estimates are presented with 95% confidence intervals, calculated using the Wilson binomial method based on the total number of questions evaluated per model.

Results

The inter‑rater reliability analysis demonstrated moderate agreement for single‑measure ratings (ICC = 0.41) and excellent agreement for average‑measure ratings (ICC = 0.81). These findings indicate that, although variability exists at the individual rater level, overall agreement was strong when expert ratings were considered in aggregate. This supports the consistency of scoring across evaluators despite the large number of responses assessed.

The median scores for the four AI models, evaluated across 110 questions, are presented in Table 1. Most models scored a median of 4.0, indicating a generally accurate performance across the models. However, Gemini 2.5 demonstrated a higher median score of 4.5 compared to the other models, reflecting its stronger performance and greater consistency in responses.

The overall accuracy revealed that 68.6% of responses scored 4, while 15.3% achieved a perfect score of 5. Among the LLMs, Gemini ranked first with an accuracy of 96.8% (95% CI: 91.6–98.8), followed by ChatGPT at 95.6% (95% CI: 90.0–98.1). DeepSeek demonstrated an accuracy of 76.9% (95% CI: 68.2–83.8), while Microsoft Copilot AI showed the lowest accuracy at 66.2% (95% CI: 56.9–74.3). Both ChatGPT and Gemini AI achieved more than 90% accuracy, but Gemini 2.5 delivered a higher proportion of perfect scores (5), while ChatGPT tended to score more consistently at a slightly lower level (4) as shown in Fig. 3.

Discussion

This study assessed the accuracy of four LLMs: ChatGPT-4.1, DeepSeek-V3, Gemini AI 2.5 and Microsoft Copilot GPT-4.1, in addressing questions related to orthodontic retainers. The results demonstrated significant performance differences across the models, with Gemini demonstrating marginally higher accuracy. These findings suggest that newer-generation LLMs can provide highly proportion of clinically acceptable orthodontic information, thereby rejecting the null hypothesis that no significant differences exist among the models. While a smaller proportion of responses achieved a perfect score, the majority were rated as “accurate,” indicating generally reliable informational quality rather than uniformly optimal performance. Within the scope of expert‑evaluated informational accuracy, Gemini 2.5 demonstrated slightly higher performance relative to the other models under the conditions of this study; however, the absolute difference compared with ChatGPT‑4.1 was small (approximately 1.2%), indicating comparable high‑level performance between these models rather than a clinically.

The reliability of the expert evaluations was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients. While the average‑measure ICC demonstrated excellent agreement among reviewers, the moderate single‑measure ICC reflects variability at the level of individual raters. Accordingly, the findings of this study are most robust when interpreted at the level of aggregated expert consensus rather than individual evaluator agreement.

Abu Arqub et al. [21] previously assessed the accuracy of ChatGPT in relation to clear aligner therapy, finding a mean accuracy of 58%. In contrast, our study reported a significantly higher accuracy of 95.6% for ChatGPT in the context of orthodontic retainers. The observed performance differences may reflect variation in response generation strategies across models; however, the present study was not designed to evaluate the underlying causes of these differences.

Compared to Kılınç et al. [22], who used a smaller dataset of 34 questions for evaluation of accuracy of ChatGPT responses to questions about orthodontics and reported a mean accuracy of 72%, our study employed a larger dataset of 110 questions related to orthodontic retainers, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of AI models. This increase in sample size may, in part, have contributed to improved accuracy and inter-rater reliability. While Alkhamees et al. [23] found inconsistencies in ChatGPT responses, our study, particularly with newer models like Gemini, demonstrated statistical higher accuracy, potentially reflecting more recent advancements in model training and updates. However, these differences should be interpreted in the context of potential methodological variations between studies, including differences in question complexity, evaluation frameworks, prompting approaches, and domain specificity, which may have also influenced model performance. Dursun D and Gecer R [24] also evaluated several AI chatbots, including ChatGPT, Gemini, and Copilot, for clear aligner education and found that while the chatbots provided generally acceptable answers, readability and clarity were significant concerns. In contrast, our study focused on 110 retainer-specific questions and found that ChatGPT and Gemini delivered enhanced clarity and accuracy, offering a more reliable tool for orthodontic patient education. The larger dataset, combined with advancements in AI architecture, led to more consistent and reliable responses in orthodontic patient education.

Salmanpour et al. [25] compared ChatGPT and Microsoft Copilot AI in terms of their ability to answer common orthodontic questions. While they found both models delivered satisfactory answers, expert orthodontists consistently rated the chatbot responses lower than human-provided answers, indicating that AI alone cannot replace professional judgment. Similar to our findings, where although models like Gemini and ChatGPT performed well with high accuracy in retainer-related queries, expert evaluation revealed that certain subtleties in patient education, particularly concerning long-term treatment adherence, require professional oversight to ensure optimal patient care and understanding.

In a recent study by Arslan et al. [26] ChatGPT and Google Bard were evaluated for their ability to provide orthodontic information, with a focus on answer quality and accuracy. The authors reported that while both models delivered generally acceptable responses, their performance showed variability, and they struggled with more complex aspects of orthodontic care. In our investigation, a more extensive and specific questionnaire was used revealing that Gemini produced notably clearer and more contextually accurate responses in the post‑treatment retainer domain.

Similarly, Wu et al. [27] observed that Gemini‑2.0 outperformed other LLMs such as DeepSeek in a dental‑implant scenario, reinforcing our finding that Gemini demonstrated highest accuracy in our retainer-related domain. The consistency across different sub‐fields of dentistry suggests that model selection matters, and that newer versions of Gemini may be particularly well‑suited for patient‑education tasks in orthodontics. While statistically significant differences were observed between models, the absolute differences in accuracy between the highest‑performing LLMs were relatively small. Consequently, the clinical meaningfulness of these differences remains uncertain and should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in the absence of patient‑level outcome measures.

Finally, this study highlights the potential of advanced AI models, such as Gemini and ChatGPT, in improving patient education regarding orthodontic retainers. The significant improvements in accuracy and inter-rater reliability demonstrate the value of utilizing larger datasets and updated AI versions for more consistent and reliable information delivery. These findings suggest that AI chatbots, when used alongside expert oversight, can be a valuable tool in orthodontic patient education, offering a promising approach for enhancing treatment adherence and patient understanding in clinical practice.

From a conceptual standpoint, the findings align with established frameworks of human–AI collaboration in healthcare. As described by Topol [28], AI systems are most effective when functioning as supportive tools that augment, rather than replace, clinician expertise. Similarly, accountability frameworks for AI in health systems emphasize that ultimate responsibility for information use, interpretation, and patient impact must remain with qualified healthcare professionals. Inappropriate or unsupervised reliance on AI tools for orthodontic guidance may pose medico‑legal risks, particularly if inaccurate or context‑inappropriate information influences patient decision‑making. These findings support the use of AI as an adjunctive informational tool, with clinical interpretation and patient guidance remaining the responsibility of qualified orthodontic professionals.

Recommendations

Based on the findings of this study, large language model–based chatbots may be considered as adjunctive informational resources in orthodontic practice, particularly for addressing common patient questions related to retainer use and maintenance. Their outputs should be reviewed, contextualized, and delivered under the supervision of qualified orthodontic professionals, and they should not be regarded as a substitute for clinician‑provided education or clinical decision‑making.

As this study focused on comparative informational accuracy, patient‑centered outcomes such as comprehension, satisfaction, adherence, and behavioral impact were not evaluated, and caution is warranted before extrapolating these findings to routine clinical use. Future research should prioritize prospective and longitudinal designs that assess the impact of AI‑assisted education on patient understanding, compliance, and treatment stability.

Further investigations are also recommended to explore interactive, multi‑turn patient–AI communication, incorporate multilingual and culturally contextualized prompts, and evaluate performance across evolving AI model versions. Integration of AI‑generated educational content into mobile health platforms and electronic health record systems, with appropriate governance and medico‑legal safeguards, may support personalized patient education when implemented within a human‑in‑the‑loop framework.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The use of a 110‑question dataset may have contributed to rater fatigue; however, aggregation of expert evaluations and the observed excellent average‑measure inter‑rater reliability suggest that overall scoring consistency was maintained. The static, single‑turn question format was necessary for standardized comparison but does not reflect the interactive nature of real‑world patient–AI conversations, warranting future multi‑turn evaluations.

The study focused on comparative informational accuracy and did not assess patient‑directed outcomes such as comprehension, adherence, satisfaction, or behavioral impact, which are critical components of patient education; therefore, clinical implications should be interpreted cautiously. Although the questionnaire was developed through literature review and expert input, it functioned as an expert‑derived evaluative tool without formal psychometric validation. In addition, results reflect AI performance at a specific time point, and ongoing model updates may influence future accuracy profiles.

From a statistical perspective, aggregation of expert ratings enabled comparative analysis but may not fully capture rater‑ or model‑level clustering, and dichotomization of Likert scores, although pragmatically useful, reduced the granularity of ordinal data. Finally, potential cultural, linguistic, and demographic biases in AI‑generated responses were not evaluated, limiting generalizability to diverse populations. Future studies incorporating multilevel analytical frameworks, interactive prompts, and broader contextual evaluation are recommended.

Conclusions

This study provides a comparative, expert‑based evaluation of contemporary large language models in the context of patient‑directed orthodontic retainer guidance. Under controlled evaluation conditions, newer AI models—particularly Gemini 2.5 and Chat GPT‑4.1—demonstrated higher median informational accuracy than other evaluated models.The findings highlight that relative differences in informational performance exist among LLMs, although these differences were generally of small‑to‑moderate magnitude and should be interpreted within the constraints of non‑parametric, rank‑based analysis.Importantly, the results underscore that AI‑generated content should function only as an adjunct to clinician‑delivered patient education, with expert oversight remaining essential to ensure contextual accuracy, clinical appropriateness, and medico‑legal accountability.By focusing specifically on the post‑treatment retention phase, this study addresses a clinically relevant yet underexplored area in the AI‑dentistry literature and contributes evidence regarding the reliability of LLM‑generated responses to common patient concerns about retainer use and maintenance.Ongoing evaluation of AI systems remains necessary as models evolve, and future research should extend beyond informational accuracy to assess interactive performance, patient‑centered outcomes, and real‑world educational impact.
