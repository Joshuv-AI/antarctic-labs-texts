A multisectoral effort against Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever: Towards One Health approach implementation in the Balkans and Black Sea region
Authors: Alessia Milano, Claudia Robbiati, Silvia Declich, Aykut Ozkul, Mitra Drakulovic, Tengiz Chaligava, Lela Urushadze, Gvantsa Chanturia, Ana Gulbani, Roena Sukhiashvili, Paata Imdaze, Maria Grazia Dente
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrimean-Congo Hemorrhagic Fever (CCHF) is a notifiable zoonotic disease found also in the Balkans and adjacent regions, transmitted primarily through Hyalomma marginatum ticks and contact with infected animals and humans. This study investigates the implementation of the One Health (OH) approach in addressing CCHF in the Balkans and Black Sea area, highlighting the collaboration among human, animal, and environmental health sectors. We assessed the integration of OH principles in CCHF surveillance and response by comparing the information collected during the exercises implemented in the context of the European Project MediLabsecure in 2016 and 2023, and interviews with representatives from Serbia, Turkiye, and Georgia. Key findings reveal advancements in surveillance systems, with most countries now employing active or passive surveillance strategies. Prevention efforts include robust data collection, multisectoral governance, cross-border collaborations, and capacity building. Response strategies focus on developing standard operating procedures, public awareness campaigns, and seroprevalence studies. However, challenges persist, including insufficient community engagement, lack of integrated electronic data-sharing systems, inadequate funding, and limited cross-border cooperation. To enhance OH implementation, we recommend collaborative funding initiatives, the establishment of governmental policies for data sharing, interdisciplinary training programs, standardized evaluation frameworks, and sustainable tick control strategies. This study underscores the necessity of a comprehensive OH approach to effectively manage CCHF and other zoonotic diseases, highlighting the importance of integrated strategies that address human, animal, and environmental health.Keywords: Crimean-Congo Hemorrhagic fever, One health, Surveillance, Prevention, Response, Balkans, Black Sea region

Background

Crimean-Congo Hemorrhagic Fever (CCHF) is a significant viral zoonotic disease in the Balkans and surrounding regions, primarily transmitted through the bites of infected ticks, particularly Hyalomma marginatum, and through contact with the blood and tissues of infected animals and humans [1]. This vector prefers warm and dry climates, making regions with Mediterranean or semi-arid conditions particularly susceptible to outbreaks [2,3]. Various studies have reported the presence of CCHF virus infection through seroprevalence in cattle, sheep, and goats across Euro-Asia and the Balkans (Fig. 1) [4]. In humans, CCHF virus infection manifests with severe symptoms and a high mortality rate, particularly affecting those in agriculture and animal husbandry due to their increased risk of tick exposure [1].

The emergence of recent pandemics like COVID-19, MERS, Ebola, swine flu, and SARS has revealed the complex web of factors contributing to disease outbreaks, highlighting the necessity for holistic approaches like One Health (OH) [5]. This interdisciplinary model, which encourages collaboration across various sectors and stakeholders, is essential for pinpointing shared priorities and addressing the multifaceted interactions among pathogens, animals, humans, and the environment [6,7]. International bodies support the adoption of OH principles within national frameworks, with research confirming its success in managing zoonotic and other infectious diseases [8]. However, integrating OH into prevention and response strategies faces challenges due to the compartmentalized nature of different sectors [9]. True integration requires substantial investment across all sectors, the promotion of multisectoral governance, and capacity building through targeted activities such as assessments and training exercises [10].

The Balkans and surrounding regions face significant challenges in managing CCHF due to the prevalent tick vectors and the close interactions between humans and livestock. This paper addresses the application of the OH approach to combat CCHF in the Balkans and Black Sea region within the framework of the European Project MediLabSecure [11]. This project, encompassing a network of 22 countries across the Sahel, Middle East, Balkans, and Black Sea regions, aims to strengthen OH prevention and response capacities for vector-borne diseases, with a particular focus on arboviral diseases.

The nine countries considered in this analysis (Fig. 1) — Albania, Armenia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Georgia, Kosovo, Montenegro, North Macedonia, Serbia, and Turkiye — represent the Balkans and Black Sea region. This area encompasses diverse climatic and ecological conditions, ranging from Mediterranean to semi-arid environments, which favor the presence of Hyalomma marginatum ticks, the main vector of CCHF. While some countries, such as Türkiye and Georgia, report recurrent human cases, others, including Montenegro and Bosnia and Herzegovina, have only sporadic detections, primarily in livestock or ticks. Surveillance capacities and data-sharing mechanisms vary across the region, reflecting differences in the presence of the virus and local monitoring efforts.

Since 2002, CCHF virus infection has been documented in Turkiye. Previous research indicated a seroprevalence of 57 % [12], and a recent study, analyzing serum samples from cattle, sheep, and goats in the Adana province, reported a seroprevalence of 31.8 % [13]. The country's diverse climate, ranging from Mediterranean to semi-arid, provides an ideal habitat for H. marginatum ticks [14].

In Armenia, there is limited documentation on the epidemiology of CCHF, although sporadic cases have been reported, particularly in rural areas where agricultural activities are prevalent [15]. Bosnia and Herzegovina have also reported sporadic cases of CCHF, often associated with occupational exposures related to agriculture and animal husbandry [16]. Kosovo has observed a steady presence of CCHF in humans and serological studies have identified CCHF antibodies in livestock, indicating the virus' circulation within the animal population [17]. In Albania, the incidence of CCHF has been relatively low but remains a concern due to the presence of the primary tick vector, H. marginatum [18]. North Macedonia has reported sporadic cases of CCHF, often associated with tick exposure in rural and agricultural areas [19]. Georgia has experienced cases of CCHF, particularly in regions with suitable climates for H. marginatum ticks [20,21].

This study aims to describe the current integration of the OH approach in the surveillance of CCHF within the Balkans and Black Sea region. To achieve this, we utilized information gathered during an exercise conducted in Montenegro in 2023 in the context of MLS. During this exercise, representatives from nine countries were asked to describe their national prevention and response systems and explain how the OH approach is integrated into them. Additionally, we interviewed representatives from Serbia, Turkiye, and Georgia to investigate in greater detail how CCHF is being managed in these countries. Furthermore, we drew on observational studies conducted under the MLS project in Armenia and Georgia to provide additional context and information [[22], [23], [24]]. By assessing the existing frameworks and practices, we aim to understand how OH principles can support the control of CCHF. Secondly, the study seeks to identify the challenges encountered in implementing the OH approach and to propose practical solutions. This objective was achieved through the analysis of recent findings and insights gained from expert interviews, helping in the identification of actionable recommendations for enhancing OH initiatives in the region.

Advancements in CCHF surveillance and response since 2016

Significant improvements have been observed in CCHF surveillance and response since 2016. That year, a regional meeting for the Balkans and Black Sea region under the MLS network included a risk assessment exercise, which provided an opportunity to collect information on the sectors involved in CCHF surveillance and response within the countries. Integrated prevention and response strategies for CCHF in the Balkans and Black Sea region

Based on the results from the exercises and interviews, we have identified prevention and response strategies implemented in the Balkans and Black Sea region essential for addressing CCHF with an integrated approach that involves multiple sectors.

Countries such as Armenia, Serbia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, and Montenegro, which have not reported human cases of CCHF, prioritize prevention measures. Governance structures are key to these efforts. Montenegro has implemented a comprehensive notification system for promptly reporting suspected cases, ensuring rapid communication among health authorities. Serbia has established a multisectoral committee for vector-borne diseases, involving human health, animal health, entomology, and ecology, which is activated at the first human case to coordinate responses and formulate strategies.

Data collection initiatives enhance understanding and monitoring of CCHF risks. Bosnia-Herzegovina conducts seroprevalence testing on ticks, sheep, and cattle to monitor potential vectors and hosts. Armenia has developed detailed maps of Ixodes tick distributions to predict CCHF spread. Capacity building further supports prevention, as seen in Serbia's annual OH training sessions before the transmission season, which involves professionals from various sectors. Cross-border collaborations also bolster preventive efforts. Armenia has formalized partnerships with neighboring countries to share resources and strategies for CCHF control. Serbia collaborates with North Macedonia to exchange information, review guidelines, and provide updates on the current situation. Additionally, measures such as using acaricides on livestock in Serbia help reduce tick populations and minimize transmission risks.

Countries such as Georgia, Turkiye, Albania, Kosovo, and North Macedonia, which have reported human cases of CCHF, focus on response measures to manage outbreaks and their aftermath. Standard operating procedures (SOPs) form the backbone of these strategies, ensuring coordinated actions across sectors. For example, Turkiye and Albania's integration of human and veterinary surveillance within the same institution facilitates unified responses. Its rapid response teams, composed of epidemiologists, virologists, entomologists, and veterinarians, provide a multidisciplinary approach to outbreak management.

Public awareness campaigns are another crucial component of response strategies. In Turkiye, annual public meetings include distribution of free tick removal cards in villages in the endemic area and enhancing community preparedness. Data collection for response planning is emphasized in Georgia, where an integrated electronic data-sharing system enhances disease surveillance and facilitates timely responses. Albania and Georgia also conduct an active surveillance in ticks using PCR and seroprevalence studies in livestock to monitor disease prevalence and transmission dynamics.

Preventive measures aim to create resilience before any human cases emerge, focusing on strengthening governance frameworks, conducting proactive surveillance, and building technical capacities. These efforts are often characterized by investments in long-term infrastructure, such as notification systems, tick distribution mapping, and cross-sectoral training programs.

Conversely, response strategies are designed for immediate action in the face of outbreaks. They emphasize rapid mobilization through structured SOPs, multidisciplinary teams, and community-focused interventions. Countries with reported cases invest heavily in preparedness drills, public awareness, and the establishment of rapid response mechanisms that can quickly adapt to evolving situations. Preventive strategies rely on a predictive and precautionary mindset, leveraging data to anticipate risks, whereas response strategies focus on minimizing impact through reactive and adaptive measures. Together, these approaches, underpinned by the OH framework, form a dynamic and complementary system that addresses the complex challenges of CCHF across the region.

A common governance issue is insufficient community engagement in strategies aimed at preventing outbreaks. Enhancing community participation and increasing awareness about CCHF, particularly in rural areas, is critical for effective prevention. Many countries also face financial constraints that limit resources for prevention and response efforts. Additionally, there is a lack of evaluation systems to assess the implementation and effectiveness of OH strategies, which complicates efforts to refine and improve these approaches.

The absence of integrated electronic systems for sharing data across sectors is a significant obstacle. This deficiency impedes timely information exchange, a cornerstone of effective disease surveillance and response. While some countries, such as Georgia, have developed electronic data-sharing systems, this remains an area for improvement across the region. The main barrier is twofold: on one hand, the absence of formal agreements at the governmental level to harmonize data-sharing policies across human, veterinary, and environmental sectors; on the other, limited digital infrastructure and interoperability among existing national databases. Developing a coordinated regional network would therefore require both political commitment and technological investment. In this context, the use of artificial intelligence (AI) and digital surveillance tools could represent a complementary, lower-cost approach. For instance, AI-based systems could monitor online searches, social media trends, or veterinary health reports to detect unusual patterns indicative of tick activity or disease symptoms. Integrating such tools with conventional epidemiological data could enhance early warning capacity, particularly in settings where laboratory confirmation or electronic health records are limited.

Inadequate training programs that fail to integrate OH principles represent a widespread challenge. Comprehensive training initiatives encompassing human, animal, and environmental health are essential for managing zoonotic diseases like CCHF effectively. Despite existing efforts, these programs often lack the depth and cross-sectoral integration required to address the interconnected nature of CCHF.

Cross-border collaboration among neighboring countries remains underdeveloped. Strengthening regional cooperation is essential for improving surveillance, response capabilities, and resource sharing to mitigate the cross-border spread of CCHF. While formalized partnerships exist in some countries, such as Armenia, these collaborations need further expansion and operationalization.

In both endemic and non-endemic countries, community awareness and understanding of CCHF risks and response strategies are inadequate. Public education initiatives need to be expanded to ensure that communities, particularly in rural areas, are well-informed and prepared to participate in prevention and response efforts.

Inadequate monitoring of animals, which serve as hosts for CCHF vectors, poses a significant challenge. Effective monitoring is critical for early detection and mitigation of outbreaks. Tick control remains another area of concern. Countries like Georgia and Turkiye use repellents and/or anti-tick treatments in endemic areas and on livestock, respectively. However, the toxicity of these products limits their use in broader environmental applications, as they can harm flora and wildlife.

By addressing these interconnected challenges, countries in the region can enhance their capacity to prevent and respond to CCHF effectively. Strengthening governance, fostering regional cooperation, investing in integrated data systems, and building robust OH training programs are essential steps toward mitigating the impact of CCHF and advancing public health security.

Actionable Steps for Enhancing OH Against CCHF

Addressing the multifaceted challenges CCHF surveillance poses in the Balkans and Black Sea region requires a comprehensive and integrated approach that spans various sectors. One of the primary hurdles lies in the scarcity of funds allocated to support such an integrated strategy. To overcome this challenge, collaboration among governmental bodies, international organizations, and private stakeholders is crucial [25].

Moreover, the oversight of environmental factors in surveillance systems presents a critical challenge. Environmental variables significantly influence the transmission dynamics of CCHF, yet they are frequently overlooked in surveillance strategies. By integrating comprehensive environmental monitoring and analysis, surveillance systems can more effectively identify and target high-risk areas, thereby implementing tailored interventions to mitigate disease spread [26,27].

The absence of an integrated electronic system for data sharing among sectors in most countries hampers coordination and collaboration in surveillance activities [28]. One key issue is the reluctance of sector representatives to share data with those from other sectors. Stakeholders must engage in dialogue to foster trust and mutual understanding. The establishment of governmental policies or regulations mandating the sharing of relevant data for disease surveillance and management is well seen. Additionally, specific authorities or agencies could be established to monitor and enforce these policies, ensuring effective and uniform implementation nationwide. There should also be concerted efforts to mobilize resources and organize collaborative meetings to promote the OH approach [29].

Training programs with an integrated perspective for personnel represent another critical component in addressing surveillance challenges. By equipping professionals with interdisciplinary skills and knowledge, training initiatives can enhance the capacity of surveillance teams to detect, respond to, and manage CCHF cases effectively. By implementing joint training workshops and cross-sectoral exchanges, stakeholders can bridge knowledge gaps and promote a shared understanding of CCHF surveillance and control measures [30].

The absence of comprehensive evaluation systems inhibits the assessment of surveillance effectiveness and impedes the identification of areas for improvement. To overcome this, stakeholders must develop standardized evaluation frameworks that encompass key performance indicators and metrics [31].

Addressing community perceptions and behaviors regarding CCHF management is critical for fostering effective disease control measures [32]. Engaging with farmers and raising awareness about the importance of disease prevention and control is essential for promoting behavioral change and fostering community participation in surveillance activities [33]. By offering compensation or subsidies, stakeholders can alleviate the economic burden on farmers and encourage them to report cases promptly, contributing to enhanced surveillance and control measures [34].

Lastly, the challenge of tick control without impacting wildlife underscores the need for integrated approaches that balance disease control efforts with environmental conservation objectives. Implementing sustainable tick control strategies that minimize ecological impact while effectively reducing tick populations is essential. By integrating disease control measures with wildlife conservation initiatives, stakeholders can mitigate disease risks while preserving biodiversity and ecosystem integrity. Collaborative research and monitoring programs can facilitate the development and implementation of ecologically sound tick control strategies that are compatible with conservation goals [35].

Addressing the complex challenges of CCHF surveillance requires a concerted effort to implement integrated strategies that encompass human, animal, and environmental dimensions. By mobilizing resources, fostering collaboration, strengthening training initiatives, implementing evaluation mechanisms, engaging communities, and adopting sustainable disease control measures, stakeholders can enhance the effectiveness and sustainability of CCHF surveillance efforts, ultimately reducing the burden of this disease on human and animal populations (Table 2).

Conclusions

This article describes the current state of OH prevention and response strategies against CCHF in the Balkans and Black Sea region. It also discusses the challenges these countries face in implementing an OH approach. The findings presented also suggest possible solutions to address these challenges.

Several governance structures, such as multisectoral committee for vector-borne diseases and notification system, have been established to improve CCHF surveillance and response. Data collection initiatives like seroprevalence testing and tick distribution mapping complement these efforts. Despite these advances, challenges persist, including insufficient community engagement, a lack of awareness, inadequate funding, and the absence of an integrated electronic data-sharing system. Capacity-building initiatives and cross-border collaborations show promise but require further development and comprehensive evaluation. Tick control measures are in place, but environmental considerations limit their application. Addressing these gaps is crucial for enhancing the effectiveness of CCHF prevention and response strategies.

As shown in the article, the OH approach is crucial in managing CCHF. This comprehensive strategy ensures that surveillance, prevention, and response measures are more effective, timely, and sustainable. The interconnectedness of the sectors is vital for early detection, rapid response, and control of zoonotic diseases, which ultimately protects public health.

Future research and action should focus on improving OH integration by developing comprehensive training programs that include environmental health, establishing integrated electronic data exchange systems, and creating robust evaluation mechanisms to assess the effectiveness of OH strategies. In addition, increasing community engagement and awareness, securing adequate funding, and strengthening regional and cross-border collaboration are critical steps toward more effective CCHF prevention and response.
