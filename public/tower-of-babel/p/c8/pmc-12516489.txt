10,000 Years of Agriculture-driven Human Evolution Through the Lens of a Single Gene
Authors: Pedro Andrade
Published: 2025

The Neolithic agricultural revolution profoundly changed human lifestyles. As our ancestors transitioned from nomadic hunters and gatherers to food producers in thriving permanent settlements, rising population densities promoted a higher circulation of infectious pathogens. Many human pathogens that are common nowadays likely became widespread as a direct consequence of this shift in social organization, initiating an evolutionary “arms race” between pathogens and our species, which continues to this day (Pearce-Duvet 2006).

But how have these ancient interactions shaped our own evolutionary history? A new article published in Molecular Biology and Evolution (Nordgren et al. 2025) delves into this question, examining 10,000 years of human–pathogen co-evolution through the lens of a single human gene. Led by Hugo Zeberg, Senior Lecturer at the Karolinska Institute (Sweden), the article focuses on the co-evolution of enteric viruses and the human gene FUT2 (fucosyltransferase 2).

Enteric viruses are human pathogens that are a frequent cause of severe gastroenteritis and that lead to as many as 400,000 annual deaths worldwide (Lockhart et al. 2022). This makes them a major global health concern. To infect intestinal cells, these viruses exploit a vulnerability in the host's defenses: antigens that coat epithelial cells from the intestine. These epithelial cell-surface antigens are important for mediating the cell's interactions with the gut microbiota. However, the antigens also have a strong molecular affinity to enteric viruses, functioning as an entry point for the viruses during infection (Fig. 1). Importantly, FUT2 is required for the synthesis of these cell-surface antigens (Nordgren et al. 2016; Bánya et al. 2018).

Agriculture has shaped our species in many ways. Nordgren et al. (2025) explore the evolutionary dynamics of the human gene FUT2 since the dawn of agriculture, ca. 10,000 years ago, using data from thousands of ancient genomes. FUT2 encodes an enzyme that is required for the production of antigens that coat the membranes of intestinal epithelial cells. These antigens facilitate the entry of enteric viruses, common viruses that likely became more abundant since the Neolithic agricultural revolution. Although FUT2 loss-of-function mutations have reduced diversity in epithelial cell antigens, they are protected from enteric virus infections. This evolutionary trade-off means that FUT2 loss-of-function mutations remain frequent in many human populations. Image by Hugo Zeberg.

Enteric viral infections are primarily driven by contact with untreated wastewater, a significant health risk in large human settlements. Thus, Nordgren et al. (2025) hypothesized that the adoption of agriculture and the consequent shift to sedentism would be associated with evolutionary dynamics at the FUT2 locus, driven by host–pathogen interactions between humans and enteric viruses.

Leveraging genotype data from 7,389 ancient genomes spanning the period from 10,000 years ago to the present, the authors focused on a FUT2 variant that introduces a premature stop codon (i.e. a truncation variant) that leads to loss-of-function, and that is common in present-day European populations. Their results show how the allele rose in frequency with the introduction of agriculture to the continent. Zeberg explains: “In ancient DNA, the truncation allele is essentially absent before 8,000 years ago in Europe, then rises sharply after farming arrives to a frequency of nearly 40%, with a signal of positive selection in the time window of between 8,500 and 5,000 years before present, precisely when sedentism takes hold.” Although migration and gene exchange with early farmers from Eastern Europe played a role in this rise in frequency across the continent, the authors found evidence that neither gene flow nor drift alone could explain the initial rise and the subsequent maintenance of the allele at such high frequencies. These findings suggest that selection has played a role in the evolutionary dynamics at this locus.

To demonstrate a beneficial effect of this allele, Zeberg's team followed up the genome analyses by performing experimental infections of intestinal organoids. Their results confirmed that norovirus and rotavirus (two common groups of enteric viruses) cannot infect cells that are homozygous for the truncation mutation. According to Zeberg, “these results support a model in which norovirus and rotavirus require FUT2-dependent carbohydrates for efficient entry and replication in intestinal cells.”

But if losing FUT2 function has a clear selective advantage, then why hasn’t this mutation risen to fixation in human populations? In other words, why hasn’t this gene evolved toward complete loss-of-function? After analyzing phenotype and genotype databases, the authors discovered that individuals who lose FUT2 function are at an increased risk of developing gallstones, duodenal ulcers, and liver disease, likely consequences of an impoverished antigen coating in the epithelial cells of multiple organs. The wild-type and truncation alleles are thus under balancing selection, as each carries costs and benefits. After the initial rise in frequency of the new adaptive variant, the locus shifted to a state in which two alternative variants were maintained at stable frequencies for millennia due to a trade-off between pathogen defenses and epithelial cell function.

Humans have a long history of migrations, and agricultural practices quickly spread across the world. As the truncation allele has spread across Europe, a reasonable expectation would be that it would have spread to other populations as well—but that is not the case. In East Asia specifically, it is seemingly absent, despite agriculture having been introduced to the region almost 8,000 years ago. Could these populations harbor any natural protection against enteric viruses that is independent of FUT2? The answer seems to be: no! Zeberg explains that “East-Asian populations lack the European truncation allele but harbor an East-Asian–specific missense variant that rose in frequency over the last 2,000 years to 40% and recapitulates the same phenotypes.” Zeberg adds that “different mutations and the same direction of effects suggest convergent selection on FUT2's function, likely from similar enteric pathogen pressures.”

Nordgren et al. (2025) provide a detailed example of how a novel selective pressure, coupled with selective trade-offs, can act as a potent force that gives rise to balanced polymorphisms—even when independent mutations explain similar phenotypes across populations. The advent of agriculture has trapped the human gene FUT2 in a tug-of-war between promoting viral resistance and mediating cross-talk between endothelial cells and the bacteria living in our bodies. Ultimately, agriculture has influenced humans beyond our history and societal organization, shaping our evolutionary trajectory with consequences for health and disease today.
