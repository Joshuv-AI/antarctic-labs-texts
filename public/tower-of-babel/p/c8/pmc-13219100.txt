Molecular phylogeny of Pseudoglaridacris (Cestoda: Caryophyllidea) and redescription of its type species, P. laruei, the most common cestode parasite of the white sucker (Catostomus commersonii) in North America
Authors: Tomáš Scholz, Olena Kudlai, Emma Nielsen, Anindo Choudhury, Florian Reyda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCaryophyllidean tapeworms (Cestoda) are widespread parasites of suckers (Catostomidae) in North America, but their phylogenetic relationships remain poorly understood. This study presents molecular data on species of the recently established genus Pseudoglaridacris Oros, Uhrovič et Scholz, 2018. The type species, P. laruei (Lamont, 1921), a very common parasite of the white sucker Catostomus commersonii (Catostomidae) in North America, is characterised both molecularly and morphologically based on recently collected, properly fixed specimens from the type host (C. commersonii) and four species of redhorse (Moxostoma spp.), including new host and geographical records from Canada and the United States. Pseudoglaridacris laruei differs from other species in the genus by its size, being the largest species (body length up to 12.7 mm), a high number of testes (62–119) and very few (0–6) postovarian vitelline follicles. Mackiewiczus etowanum from Hypentelium etowanum and Mackiewiczus rarus from Moxostoma poecilurum in Alabama are nomina nuda. The taxonomic status of P. confusa (Hunter, 1929), which occurs in ictiobines, and of P. oligorchis (Haderlie, 1953), which is most probably a junior synonym of P. laruei with a reduced number of testes, is also discussed, based on molecular data indicating the presence of a putative new species from ictiobines that has not yet been described.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00436-026-08648-8.Keywords: Tapeworms, Caryophyllidea, Catostomidae, Morphology, lsrDNA, Nearctic region

Introduction

Suckers (Cypriniformes: Catostomidae) are among dominant components of fish communities in the freshwaters of North America (Page and Burr 2011). These fish serve as hosts for numerous helminth parasites, including tapeworms (Cestoda) of the order Caryophyllidea Beneden in Carus, 1863 (see Hoffman 1999). The highest species diversity of caryophyllidean tapeworms has been reported in the white sucker, Catostomus commersonii (Lacepède), one of the most common and widespread catostomids. Hoffman (1999) recorded up to 14 species of caryophyllidean tapeworms in this fish host. However, most of these species are rare, except for Pseudoglaridacris laruei (Lamont, 1921) and, to a lesser extent, Glaridacris catostomi Cooper, 1920.

Pseudoglaridacris laruei was described by Lamont (1921) as Caryophyllaeus laruei from C. commersonii in Lake Mendota, Wisconsin, but was later placed in Glaridacris Cooper, 1920 by Hunter (1927). Recently, Oros et al. (2018) established Pseudoglaridacris Oros, Uhrovič et Scholz, 2018, with P. laruei as the type species. The genus currently comprises three species: P. laruei, P. confusa (Hunter, 1929) and P. oligorchis (Haderlie, 1953) (Scholz and Oros 2017). Pseudoglaridacris laruei is a widespread parasite of the white sucker but has also been reported from redhorse (Moxostoma spp.) (Oros et al. 2018).

Pseudoglaridacris confusa was originally described as Glaridacris confusus by Hunter (1929) from Ictiobus bubalus (Rafinesque) in Illinois. It is a very common parasite of other ictiobine suckers (Carpiodes and Ictiobus species). However, P. confusa has also been reported from C. commersonii in Iowa, New York, North Dakota and Wisconsin, and from Catostomus macrocheilus Girard in Idaho (see Hoffman 1999). Pseudoglaridacris oligorchis was described from Catostomus tahoensis Gill et Jordan in California, but has also been reported from C. commersonii and Moxostoma spp. (Oros et al. 2018).

All records of Pseudoglaridacris tapeworms from ‘atypical’ catostomid hosts should be checked for possible misidentification, such as P. confusa (as Glaridacris confusa) reported by Van Cleave and Mueller (1934) from C. commersonii in Oneida Lake, New York, which was in fact P. laruei (see Mackiewicz 1965). The occurrence of Pseudoglaridacris species in suckers of different subfamilies (Catostominae and Ictiobinae) is doubtful, as most Nearctic caryophyllideans are strictly specific and occur in a single host species (strict specialists) or in a few species of the same genus (congeneric specialists – Kuchta et al. 2020; Scholz and Kuchta 2025). Therefore, the range of definitive hosts of Pseudoglaridacris spp. should be critically reviewed using molecular data on tapeworms from different hosts in North America.

Confusion among Pseudoglaridacris species is possible due to their morphological similarity and the presumed overlap of the diagnostic features used by Mackiewicz (1965) to distinguish G. laruei and G. confusa. Orcutt et al. (2017) analysed these features but were unable to confirm their taxonomic relevance, concluding that the tapeworms they erroneously identified as G. laruei could not be reliably distinguished from G. confusa. However, the results of the study by Orcutt et al. (2017) raise several questions: (i) the tapeworms identified as G. laruei (= P. laruei) were found in the river carpsucker, Carpiodes carpio (Rafinesque) (subfamily Ictiobinae), which has never been reported as a host of P. laruei (see Hoffman 1999); the type and most typical host of P. laruei is C. commersonii (subfamily Catostominae) (Oros et al. 2018); (ii) the specimens of ‘G. laruei’ originated from Texas, i.e., from a disjunct water system (Mississippi Basin/Great Lakes Basin for the type of P. laruei in a Wisconsin lake versus Gulf Drainage through the Sabine River outlet for Texas), where P. laruei has never been found (Hoffman 1999; Oros et al. 2018); (iii) the ‘G. laruei’ specimens studied by Orcutt et al. (2017) differed morphologically from the true P. laruei described by Lamont (1921) and illustrated by Oros et al. (2018).

Therefore, it is very likely that Orcutt et al. (2017) studied a population of P. confusa from the river carpsucker, not the true P. laruei. Unfortunately, Orcutt et al. (2017) did not deposit any of the specimens they studied in a museum to verify their species identification.

Although Oros et al. (2018) revised the genus Pseudoglaridacris, questions about the actual species diversity of the genus, the host range of the individual taxa, and their geographical distribution in the principal hydrological regions of North America remain insufficiently clarified. Therefore, the type species of the genus, P. laruei, a very common parasite of the white sucker C. commersonii (Catostomidae) in North America, is characterised both molecularly and morphologically based on recently collected, properly fixed specimens from the type host (C. commersonii) and four species of redhorse (Moxostoma spp.).

As this species has sometimes been confused with P. confusa, it is redescribed based on a study of the holotype and extensive new material, including new host and geographical records from Canada and the United States, and is differentiated from congeners. The taxonomic status of P. confusa, which occurs exclusively in ictiobines, and P. oligorchis, which closely resembles P. laruei, but has very few or no testes, is also discussed.

Methods and materials

This study is based on a molecular and morphological evaluation of a large data set of specimens collected mainly by the third author (F. Reyda) and his students (specimens with host code Nos. FRXX [year]_YYY[fish number]). In addition, tapeworms from the private collection of the late John S. Mackiewicz (Albany, NY), kindly provided to the first author in 2017–2019, and some specimens collected by the authors’ collaborators, namely Thomas J. Fayton, Chris T. McAllister (both USA), Roman Kuchta (Czech Republic), and Mikuláš Oros (Slovakia), were included.

The list of Pseudoglaridacris specimens studied is provided in a Supplementary file S1 and in Oros et al. (2018). In total, 132 specimens of P. laruei from Catostomus commersonii, 16 specimens of P. laruei from four Moxostoma species, 26 specimens of P. oligorchis from two Catostomus species, and several dozen specimens of P. confusa sensu lato (see text) from six ictiobine hosts (Carpiodes and Ictiobus species) were studied.

The newly collected parasites were obtained by parasitological dissection of freshly euthanised fish. Fieldwork (collection of fish samples) was conducted with the approval of the official local authorities (granted to the US partners listed in the acknowledgements). The fish were identified on site by staff familiar with the local fish fauna. Newly collected tapeworms were obtained from the intestines of freshly killed fish; they were rinsed with saline and fixed in hot, near-boiling saline (0.9% NaCl) or 4% formaldehyde solution (formalin) for morphological studies. The anterior and posterior parts of some specimens (hologenophores) were fixed in hot 4% formalin, and their middle part was placed in 96% molecular ethanol for DNA sequencing.

For morphological examination, specimens were stained with Mayer’s carmine, dehydrated in an ethanol series, cleared with clove oil (eugenol) and mounted in Canada balsam. Line drawings were made with an Olympus BX 51 light microscope (Olympus Inc. Tokyo, Japan). For scanning electron microscopy, scoleces were dehydrated in an ethanol and amyl acetate series and dried in hexamethyldisilazane (HMDS). The samples were then coated with a 20–25 nm thick layer of gold and examined using a JEOL JSM-740 1 F scanning electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

The phylogenetic relationships of the tapeworms analysed were assessed on the basis of partial (D1–D3 region) nuclear ribosomal large subunit rRNA gene sequences (lsrDNA). COI sequences were not generated as Brabec et al. (2012) demonstrated the presence of nuclear paralogues (numts), i.e., multiple trnK + nad3 + trnS + trnW + cox1 haplotypes within individuals, indicating a history of gene exchange between the mitochondrial and nuclear genomes.

The methodology used for DNA extraction and generation of sequences is identical to that described in detail in an earlier study of three new species of Biacetabulum Hunter 1927 (see Uhrovič et al. 2022). Following the tree topology of the Caryophyllidea described by Scholz et al. (2021), new lsrDNA sequences were aligned with representative sequences available in GenBank as well as new sequences of Janiszewskiella fortobothria Mackiewicz et Deutsch, 1976 from Carpiodes cyprinus (Lesueur) and Ictiobus niger (Rafinesque), and new and published sequences of Hypocaryophyllaeus paratarius Hunter 1927 from C. cyprinus using ClustalW, implemented in Geneious ver. 11 (Biomatters, Auckland, New Zealand). The sequence of Isoglaridacris floriani Uhrovič, Pantoja, Kudlai et Scholz, 2025 from Moxostoma macrolepidotum (Lesueur) was used as an outgroup. The length of the final alignment was 1,357 nucleotides.

Bayesian inference (BI) and maximum likelihood (ML) methods were used to assess the phylogenetic relationships within the dataset. The best-fitting model for the analyses, GTR + I + G, was estimated using jModelTest 2.1.2 (Darriba et al. 2012). The MrBayes software (ver. 3.2.3) (Ronquist et al. 2012) was used to perform the BI analysis. Markov chain Monte Carlo chains were run for 10,000,000 generations, log-likelihood scores were plotted, and only the last 75% of trees were used to create the consensus tree. ML analysis was performed using PhyML version 3.0 (Guindon et al. 2010) on the ATGC bioinformatics platform (http://www.atgc-montpellier.fr/phyml/) with a nonparametric bootstrap validation based on 100 pseudoreplicates. The software FigTree ver. 1.4 (Rambaut 2012) was used to visualise the trees. Genetic distances (uncorrected p-distance and number of differences) were calculated in MEGA ver. 11 (Tamura et al. 2021) and presented in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Results

Molecular phylogeny

A total of 32 new lsrDNA sequences were generated in the present study: 23 sequences from Pseudoglaridacris spp. as well as seven sequences from Hypocaryophyllaeus paratarius and two from Janiszewskella fortobothria. BI and ML analyses of the data included in the alignment revealed trees with similar topologies (Fig. 1) demonstrating the presence of three Pseudoglaridacris species in our material, two of which are known (P. laruei and P. confusa) and one putative new species (Pseudoglaridacris sp.). Isolates of P. laruei formed a well-supported clade with two subclades, one of which consisted of isolates from C. commersonii and the second of isolates from Moxostoma spp. An isolate previously identified as P. oligorchis from Moxostoma anisurum in Wisconsin (PBI-409 – see https://tapewormdb.uconn.edu/index.php/parasites/molecular_search/; MW027529) belonged to this clade.

The intraspecific divergence between morphologically indistinguishable tapeworms from C. commersonii and Moxostoma spp. in the ‘P. laruei’ clade was between 0 and 0.3% (0–4 nucleotides) (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Isolates of Pseudoglaridacris sp. obtained from ictiobine hosts (Carpiodes velifer, Ictiobus bubalus, and I. niger) formed a well-supported clade with an intraspecific divergence of 0 to 0.1% (1–2 nucleotides). The third well-supported clade was formed by isolates of P. confusa from different ictiobine hosts (Carpiodes cyprinus, Ictiobus bubalus and I. cyprinellus). The intraspecific divergence within this group was 0–0.4% (1–5 nucleotides). Interspecific divergence between clades of Pseudoglaridacris spp. ranged from 0.7 to 3.1% (10–42 nucleotides) with P. laruei and Pseudoglaridacris sp. having the closest relationships. The sequences of J. fortobothria and H. paratarius formed sister clades to Pseudoglaridacris spp.

The intensity of infection can reach several dozen specimens in a host.

Representative DNA sequences and phylogenetic relationships: Isolates PBI-54 (MW027527), PBI-404 (MW027528) and PBI-409 (MW027529) (see https://tapewormdb.uconn.edu/index.php/parasites/molecular_search/) and ten new lsrDNA sequences (PX787765–PX787774). Thirteen isolates from two species of fish hosts in Canada and two species in US states (Connecticut, New York, and Wisconsin), considered conspecific with P. laruei, form two groups corresponding to the fish hosts (Fig. 1).

The first group comprises nine isolates from C. commersonii, including two samples sequenced by Scholz et al. (2021; PBI-54 and PBI-404). The second group consists of four isolates from M. anisurum and M. erythrurum, including sample PBI-409 from Scholz et al. (2021), which was identified as P. oligorchis. Isolates from C. commersonii differ from those from Moxostoma spp. by 2–4 nucleotides (0.1–0.3%), and there are no clear morphological differences between the isolates (Figs. 2 and 4; see Remarks below).

The fish hosts, C. commersonii and Moxostoma spp., belong to the same subfamily, Catostominae, and both C. commersonii and M. anisurum were infected with the isolates in the same region of Wisconsin. Based on these criteria, the isolates from C. commersonii and Moxostoma spp. are considered conspecific. Future research, which should include new molecular markers (mitochondrial genes are not suitable for taxonomic and phylogenetic studies in the Caryophyllidea – see Brabec et al. 2012), will confirm or refute the above assumption about the conspecificity of tapeworms from these two genera of suckers.

Redescription (based on 20 specimens from Catostomus commersonii; measurements in micrometres unless otherwise stated): Caryophyllidea, Capingentidae (sensu Scholz et al. 2021). Body elongate, rod-shaped (of similar width throughout total body length – Fig. 2A; and 4.0–12.7 mm long, with maximum width 539–1,019 in middle third or in preovarian region; width at level of cirrus sac and ovarian isthmus 339–958 and 291–956, respectively.

Scolex trapezoidal, always wider than neck, 488–1,153 wide, with pair of larger median loculi and two pairs of shallow lateral loculi, and slightly convex, often indistinct apical disc (Figs. 3 and 4A–D). Neck short and wide, only slightly narrower than scolex (Fig. 3A, B), 312–932 wide at level of first vitelline follicles. Internal and external longitudinal muscles well-developed. Osmoregulatory canals narrow, in cortex, forming usually 8 pairs external to vitelline follicles. Excretory bladder narrow near excretory pore, enlarged in distal part, 148–204 long and 44–46 wide.

Testes medullary, widely oval to spherical, 64–216 long and 62–201 wide, usually in two longitudinal rows on dorsal and ventral sides, 62–119 in number (mean 89; n = 20) (Fig. 2A). Testes usually begin posterior (at distance up to 275) to anteriormost vitelline follicles (in 68% of specimens; n = 100; Fig. 4B, D), at same level (17%; Fig. 4C) or slightly anterior to vitelline follicles (15%; Fig. 4A), 500–1,371 from anterior end. Testes arranged in two regular longitudinal rows posteriorly not reaching cirrus sac, slightly anterior to posteriormost preovarian vitelline follicles (Fig. 2A); rarely, testes in more than two longitudinal rows. Testicular area up to 9.3 mm long, its length representing about 64–73% of length of body. Cirrus sac thick-walled, spherical to subspherical, slightly wide than long, 85–142 × 92–148, its width representing 15–28% of width of body at same level (Fig. 4E–H). External seminal vesicle ovoid to slightly curved (bean-shaped; Fig. 4E–H), thick-walled, 74–222 × 44–112. Male genital pore slightly anterior to female genital pore, opening outside separately (Fig. 4E–H).

Ovary compact (non-follicular), H-shaped (Figs. 2A and 4E–H), with lateral wings tapering towards their ends; total width of ovary 253–855 (at anterior end, more rarely at level of ovarian isthmus), ovarian arms 465–1,320 long and 68–282 wide (near ovarian isthmus). Length of ovary representing 94–99% of length of uterine region. Ovarian isthmus slightly pre-equatorial to slightly postequatorial, situated at 47–60% of length of ovary. Vagina narrow, tubular, slightly sinuous, without seminal receptacle, joins uterus to form short uterovaginal canal opening separately (slightly posterior) from male gonopore, slightly posterior to distinct genital atrium (corresponding to Fig. 5.22 of Mackiewicz 1994). Female gonopore at level of anterior ends of ovarian wings or slightly (up to 78) anterior to ovary, 158–585 anterior to ovarian isthmus (Fig. 4E–H).

Vitelline follicles medullary; preovarian vitelline follicles numerous, highly variable in shape and size, 37–219 long and 33–164 wide. Vitelline follicles begin 503–1,262 posterior to anterior extremity, usually (in > 2/3 specimens, n = 100; also in holotype USNM 1355986) anterior to anteriormost testes (Fig. 4B, D); less commonly (17% and 15%) at same level or slightly posterior (Fig. 4A, C). Posteriorly, preovarian vitelline follicles reach to ovary or to close distance (up to 113) from anterior ovarian wings (Fig. 4E–H). Postovarian vitelline follicles mostly present (in 85% of specimens; n = 100), few in number (at maximum 6) (Fig. 4E, G, H); rarely, postovarian vitelline follicles absent (15%) (Fig. 4F).

Uterus forms few loops, extending with one loop at level of cirrus sac or slightly anterior (Figs. 2A and 4E–H); preovarian uterine loops filling most of space anterior to ovarian isthmus (Fig. 4E–H); uterine glands well-developed, absent only in distal and proximal portions of uterus (Fig. 4E–H). Uterine area 409–1,336 long, representing 10–16% of total body length. Eggs operculate, without fully formed oncosphere; intrauterine eggs in permanent preparations (only uncollapsed eggs measured) 48–53 × 28–30.

Remarks

The present study has confirmed that P. laruei is the most common caryophyllidean of the white sucker (Catostomus commersonii) and has expanded its range to include one Canadian province and three US states. In addition, P. laruei has also been found in four species of redhorse (Moxostoma spp.) and lake chubsucker (Erimyzon sucetta) (Oros et al. 2018; present study). Tapeworms of Moxostoma spp. are genetically almost identical to those of C. commersonii and are considered conspecific. They are almost indistinguishable in morphology (Figs. 2 and 4), including scolex morphology, low numbers of postovarian vitelline follicles, and numerous testes in two longitudinal rows (62–119 [mean 89] testes in tapeworms of C. commersonii, 102–112 testes in specimens from M. anisurum, 68–89 in those from M. erythrurum, 85–110 testes in those from M. macrolepidotum and 72–82 testes in those from M. pisolabrum – see Fig. 2). They are therefore considered to be conspecific (Fig. 1).

Although Orcutt et al. (2017) questioned the distinguishing features between P. laruei and P. confusa, we were herein successfully able to distinguish between the species. Pseudoglaridacris laruei has a larger body size (up to 13 mm compared to up to 7 mm in P. confusa), more testes (62–119 [average 89 in 20 specimens measured] compared to 25–45 in P. confusa) and postovarian vitelline follicles (0–6 follicles in P. laruei compared to dozens, forming a V-shaped structure that extends to the ovary in the latter species).

Although both species have a bothrioloculodiscate scolex with a hexagonal terminal disc at the tip of the scolex and a median, bothrium-like loculus and two lateral loculi on either side, the scolex of P. laruei also differs from that of P. confusa (compare Figs. 1C, 3C and 4B with Figs. 1E, 3A and 4D in Oros et al. 2018). As mentioned in the introduction, Orcutt et al. (2017) actually compared specimens of P. confusa (or Pseudoglaridacris sp.) from different ictiobine hosts, not those of P. laruei.

In March 1971, E. H. Williams found caryophyllidean tapeworms in Hypentelium etowanum from Warmouth tributary of Loblockee Creek and E. H. Williams, R. P. Phelps and J. L. Gaines in Moxostoma poecilurum from a small stream south of Hallawakee Creek near Lake Harding, both in Lee County, Alabama, USA. The tapeworms were deposited in the USNM under the names Mackiewiczus etowanum (USNM 1368114 – “holotype”, USNM 1368115 – “paratype”) and M. rarus (USNM 1368116 – “holotype”, USNM 1368116 – “paratype”). However, neither species has been formally described, so both species are nomina nuda. Furthermore, the Mackiewiczus tapeworms are indistinguishable from P. laruei in their morphology.

Comments on other species of Pseudoglaridacris

Pseudoglaridacris confusa (Hunter 1929) Oros, Uhrovič et Scholz, 2018.

Pseudoglaridacris confusa was described by Hunter (1929) as Glaridacris confusus from Ictiobus bubalus (type host), Ictiobus sp. and Dorosoma cepedianum (apparently incidental host) in Illinois, Iowa and Mississippi (type locality Tallahatchie River). It was subsequently reported from suckers of the subfamilies Ictiobinae and Catostominae in North America. However, records from fishes of the latter subfamily are probably erroneous (misidentification of P. laruei), as suspected by Mackiewicz (1965) and confirmed in some specimens from Connecticut and North Dakota donated to the first author by J. S. Mackiewicz (see Supplementary file S1).

Oros et al. (2018), who reviewed the species of Pseudoglaridacris, reported the following suckers as definitive hosts of P. confusa: quillback, Carpiodes cyprinus (LeSueur, 1817); white sucker, Catostomus commersonii; largescale sucker, C. macrocheilus Girard, 1856 (both hosts require confirmation); bigmouth buffalo, Ictiobus cyprinellus; Usumacinta buffalo, Ictiobus meridionalis; black buffalo, Ictiobus niger (Rafinesque, 1819); greater redhorse, Moxostoma valenciennesi Jordan, 1885 (this host requires confirmation).

The species has been reported from Canada (Ontario, Quebec), Mexico (Oaxaca) and the USA (Arizona, Connecticut, Idaho, Iowa, Mississippi, New York, North Dakota, Oklahoma, Washington, Wisconsin) (Hoffman 1999; Oros et al. 2018), but the identifications of some hosts and some parasites need to be reviewed, as P. laruei may have been misidentified as P. confusa.

In the present study, two different, not closely related clades from the same fish hosts and localities were distinguished, i.e., the clades ‘Pseudoglaridacris sp.’ and ‘P. confusa’. Interestingly, tapeworms of these clades are morphologically almost indistinguishable from each other and in one smallmouth buffalo (Ictiobus bubalus) from Mississippi (host code US 827 – see Table 1), specimens of P. confusa and the putative new species (Pseudoglaridacris sp.) occurred simultaneously. Unfortunately, only few molecular vouchers (hologenophores) are available and they are of poor quality. Therefore, further research is necessary to characterise morphologically specimens of these two species, which occur only in ictiobine suckers (species of Carpiodes and Ictiobus).

Pseudoglaridacris oligorchis (Haderlie, 1953) Oros, Uhrovič et Scholz, 2018 – possible synonym of P. laruei.

This species was described as Glaridacris oligorchis by Haderlie (1953) on the basis of numerous specimens (but only a few adults) found in seven of 17 Tahoe sucker Catostomus tahoensis Gill et Jordan, from Donner Lake and Boca Lake in northeastern California, USA. Meyer (1958) found two specimens of P. oligorchis in two of 18 white sucker C. commersonii in Iowa and Siddiqi (1981) also found P. oligorchis in C. commersonii from Colorado. Oros et al. (2018) reported P. oligorchis in C. commersonii, the northern hogsucker Hypentelium nigricans (new host) and the silver redhorse, Moxostoma anisurum (Rafinesque) (new host) from the Wolf River in Wisconsin. The last sample was sequenced (as PBI-409; https://tapewormdb.uconn.edu/index.php/parasites/molecular_search/) by Scholz et al. (2021) and was closely related to isolates of P. laruei from C. commersonii in Connecticut and Iowa.

In the present study, this isolate (PBI-409) was genetically identical or nearly identical to the isolates of P. laruei, i.e., tapeworms with dozens of testes, from M. anisurum and M. erythrurum in Manitoba (Canada) (divergence 0–0.1%, i.e., 0–1 nucleotides), and differed from the isolates of P. laruei from C. commersonii by only 0.1–0.2% (2–3 nucleotides). All these isolates are considered conspecific, which casts doubt on the validity of P. oligorchis or the correct identification of this sample (PBI-409).

Pseudoglaridacris oligorchis can hardly be distinguished from P. laruei as they have the same shape of the body, ovary and scolex as well as few postovarian vitelline follicles (see Oros et al. 2018). The only difference between the two species is the number of testes: 0–8 testes in P. oligorchis compared to 62–119 testes in P. laruei. However, the number of testes can be greatly reduced in parthenogenetic specimens, as was observed in Atractolytocestus huronensis Anthony, 1958. This species was originally described from the common carp Cyprinus carpio Linnaeus, from the Huron River, Michigan, USA (Anthony 1958). Cytogenetic analysis of the mitotic and meiotic processes of an American population of this tapeworm with a small number of testes (6–20 according to Anthony 1958 and Oros et al. 2004) revealed the triploid character of the species, abnormal spermatogenesis and a subsequent lack of functional spermatozoa (Jones and Mackiewicz 1969; Králová-Hromadová et al. 2010).

The validity of P. oligorchis is questionable, and it is probably only a variant form of P. laruei with a reduced number of testes. However, a formal synonymy of P. oligorchis with P. laruei is not proposed in this study because no tapeworms from the type host C. tahoensis, which is endemic to southeastern Oregon, Nevada and northeastern California (Froese and Pauly 2025), were available for genetic and morphological analyses.

Discussion

The genus Pseudoglaridacris is small in terms of species number, with only three species currently recognised as valid (Scholz and Oros 2017), but two of its species, P. laruei and P. confusa, are among the most common tapeworms found in freshwater fish in North America (Hoffman 1999; Gibson et al., 2005). These species have often been misidentified (e.g., Van Cleave and Mueller 1934; Meyer 1958; Orcutt et al. 2017), although their distinction is simple and clear. The two species also differ with respect to the distribution range of their final hosts, i.e., P. laruei is only found in suckers of the subfamily Catostominae, while P. confusa is found in fish that belong to the Ictiobinae.

However, the molecular data analyses of the present study have shown that there are two tapeworm lineages that parasitise ictiobines: One lineage, which is the most basal and sister lineage to the other lineages, comprises tapeworms corresponding to the isolates of P. confusa from Mississippi sequenced by Scholz et al. (2021). The other lineage, sister to the P. laruei group, includes morphologically similar tapeworms from the same hosts (Ictiobinae) and regions (Mississippi, Oklahoma and West Virginia). A detailed characterisation of these tapeworms from ictiobine suckers will be carried out in a separate study.

Recent research on the caryophyllidean tapeworms of freshwater fish in North America has shown that the diversity of species is much greater than previously thought (Mackiewicz 1981; Schmidt, 1986; Hoffman 1999). To date, 16 new species from the following genera have been described by the authors and their co-workers (the number of new species and the total number of species recognised as valid for a particular genus are given in parentheses): Archigetes Leuckart, 1878 (3/5), Biacetabulum (5/14), Isoglaridacris Mackiewicz 1965 (6/18), Promonobothrium Mackiewicz, 1968 (2/9) (Oros et al. 2016; Uhrovič et al. 2021a, b, 2022). In addition, a new genus, Megancestus Uhrovič, Scholz, Kudlai et Oros, 2021, was proposed by Uhrovič et al. (2021b), to accommodate Biacetabulum carpiodi Mackiewicz, 1969.

Until the last decade of the 20th century, the taxonomy of caryophyllidean tapeworms was based on morphology, but genetic data made it possible to recognise morphologically similar or almost indistinguishable species (Uhrovič et al. 2021a, b, 2022). An integrative approach to the taxonomy of these parasites also allowed a critical assessment of the actual host specificity of the Caryophyllidea and their distribution range. Overall, it shows that the host range of the Nearctic species is narrower than previously assumed (see Table 2 in Uhrovič et al. 2021b). The species of Pseudoglaridacris are to some extent the exception to the strict (oioxenous) host specificity, as individual species occur in suckers of different genera or even different tribes. Pseudoglaridacris laruei is found in C. commersonii (tribe Catostomini) and four species of redhorse (Moxostomatini), P. oligorchis in two species of Catostomus (Catostomini), northern hogsucker (Thoburnini) and redhorses (Moxostomatini), and P. confusa in five species of two genera, Carpiodes (2) and Ictiobus (3) (Ictiobinae).

Further progress in understanding the biodiversity and host associations of the Caryophyllidea and other cestodes in North America will only be possible if properly prepared specimens are studied using molecular and morphological methods (Chervy 2024). As the caryophyllidean tapeworms are monozoic, the preparation of hologenophores (see Pleijel et al. 2008) can be laborious and lead to deformations and unnatural contractions of the live specimens cut into three parts. Therefore, it is recommended to fix the live worms with hot, almost boiled saline solution and then cut the dead, stretched worms into three parts and fix the anterior and posterior part with 4% formaldehyde solution for morphological studies, while the middle part is fixed with 99% ethanol (molecular-grade) for DNA sequencing.

However, this procedure is not ideal for Pseudoglaridacris tapeworms, as the total number of testes is one of the principal distinguishing characteristics. Therefore, a modified procedure, proposed by one of the reviewers of this manuscript, should be tested for Pseudoglaridacris tapeworms to preserve the complete testicular field for accurate identification: a notch should be taken from one side at a specific region that does not interfere with the testis count or vitelline range. A pie-shaped slice can be removed with a scalpel after heat fixation of the worm with hot saline, thus keeping the specimen mostly intact. The slice is placed in ethanol, and the remainder is fixed in formalin for morphological observation.

Catostomids are generally not the most popular fish among anglers and fishermen, but they are integral parts of ecosystems and represent one of the most interesting host groups from a parasitological point of view. Based on molecular phylogenetic data on the Caryophyllidea and the evolution of their final hosts, the otophysan lineages Cypriniformes and Siluriformes, it has been hypothesised that the radiation of the North American Caryophyllidea (family Capingentidae) began in the early Eocene. The radiation of these tapeworms in the catostomids was accompanied by the development of a wide range of scolex types not found in other caryophyllidean lineages, as well as the appearance of an external seminal vesicle, a structure not present in any of the earlier diverging groups of caryophyllideans (Scholz et al. 2021).

Currently, 73 species of the Caryophyllidea are recognised as valid in North America (Scholz and Kuchta 2025), representing a 30% increase compared to the end of the 20th century, when Hoffman (1999) compiled data on parasites of freshwater fish in North America. Since 2000, 17 new species have been described, most of which were found in the southern USA (Scholz and Kuchta 2025). The vast majority of North American caryophyllideans (almost two thirds, 64%) exhibit narrow host specificity as strict specialists (in a single host species) and congeneric specialists (in a single host genus). As only a relatively small proportion of catostomids has been examined for parasites, with a limited subset of well-studied hosts such as Catostomus commersonii or Ictiobus spp. (Kudlai et al. 2026), it is likely that our knowledge of the species diversity of caryophyllideans is incomplete.
