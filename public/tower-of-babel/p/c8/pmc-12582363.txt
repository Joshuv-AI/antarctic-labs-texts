Adverse gestational environment configures a subpopulation of ventral dentate granule cells for recruitment to drive innate anxiety
Authors: Anika Nabila, Rose Sciortino, Nicole Politowska, Elizabeth Brindley, Peter U. Hámor, Lia Zallar, Judit Gal Toth, Kristen Pleil, Miklos Toth
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYAn adverse gestational environment is a risk factor for the development of psychiatric disorders. Although studies have implicated modifications in neuronal DNA and chromatin, how these changes come about and lead to abnormal behaviors is not known. We sought to identify persistent DNA/chromatin and transcriptomic signatures induced by a proinflammatory gestational environment in the ventral dentate gyrus (vDG), a hippocampal region linked to anxiety. A proinflammatory environment shifted DNA methylation of enhancers and promoters and altered synapse-related gene expression, resulting in transcriptional heterogeneity in the vDG. In animals with prior adversity, exposure to a threatening environment recruited vDG neurons with the greatest transcriptional changes, notably in synapse-relevant genes that also tended to be differentially methylated. Finally, vDG activity was increased during transition from a safe to a threatening environment in animals with prior adversity but not in controls, suggesting their enhanced perception of a potential threat. Our data outline a proinflammatory gestational environment-induced neurobiological sequence that leads to anxiety.

INTRODUCTION

Anxiety disorders are some of the most common mental illnesses with a lifetime incidence of around 30%.1,2 While avoidance of aversive and threatening environments is an adaptive behavior and promotes survival, exaggerated reactivity to real or perceived threats is maladaptive, manifested as high arousal, vigilance, avoidance, and overt fear-like reactions. Innate anxiety is an intrinsic condition that can develop to generalized anxiety and other forms of anxiety disorders.

Adversity during prenatal and early postnatal life is a significant risk factor for the development of anxiety disorders because of the sensitivity of the fetal and early postnatal development to disruptions.3,4 Although research has been dominated by childhood adversity and trauma studies, gestational adversity, including maternal stress,5 maternal infection/inflammation,6 and maternal diet,7 represents a major risk factor for anxiety disorders later in life.8

In line with human data, gestational adversity results in anxiety-like behaviors in rodents. Maternal immune activation, induced by the viral/bacterial mimetic polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (poly(I:C)) or lipopolysaccharide (LPS), respectively, has been reported to cause offspring anxiety-like behaviors, such as avoidance of the unprotected open arm of the elevated plus maze (EPM) and the center of the open field (OF) and preference for the relative safety of the closed arm of the EPM and the periphery of the OF.9,10 Rodent studies that assessed effects of maternal stress during pregnancy reported similar findings.11 Together, human and rodent studies highlight the significant role of gestational adversity in shaping future anxiety-related behaviors.

An increasing body of evidence supports the notion that adversity-induced maladaptive behaviors are driven by modifications in neuronal DNA and chromatin (referred to as epigenetic alterations) and associated gene expression changes in the brain.12 DNA methylation undergoes dynamic changes during sensitive periods of brain development that can be disrupted by environmental insults.13 Changes in DNA methylation of NR3C1, a gene encoding the glucocorticoid receptor involved in stress response, have been reported in cord blood cells in association with maternal psychosocial stress and anxiety during pregnancy.14,15 DNA methylation changes were also found in the promoter of NR3C1 and CRH (corticotropin-releasing hormone, also involved in stress response) in the hypothalamus of offspring following maternal stress during pregnancy in mice.16 An epigenome-wide DNA methylation study identified sex-dependent methylation changes in genes and pathways related to brain development and immune function in the prefrontal cortex.17 Although these single-locus and genome-wide DNA methylation studies revealed the powerful and long-lasting effect of an adverse gestational environment on the offspring’s neuronal epigenome, little is known about how these epigenomic changes come about and lead to abnormal behaviors, including anxiety, later in life.

Here, we sought to specifically investigate gestational adversity-induced changes in DNA methylation and gene expression that can be directly linked to the behavioral manifestation of innate anxiety. We employed a mouse model of prenatal adversity that is timed to the midgestational period. We reported previously that a partial serotonin 1A receptor (5HT1AR) deficit (i.e., heterozygote knockout [KO]) in females on the outbred Swiss Webster (SW) background causes peripheral immune abnormality and immune activation.18 This maternal condition, via the placenta, propagates to the fetal brain and results in increased anxiety-like behavior and heightened stress responsiveness in the genetically wild-type (WT) adult offspring.19,20 Specifically, we reported that offspring anxiety was caused by a midgestational deficit in a chemokine (XCL1) in the maternal circulation, with a concomitant increase in the levels of the proinflammatory molecules tumor necrosis factor, interleukin-17a, soluble receptor for advanced glycation end products, and lipocalin-2 (a proxy for tissue injury) in the fetal placenta21 and transmigration of immune cells to the neonatal brain.20

Anxiety is a complex assemblage of maladaptive behaviors emerging from highly interconnected prefrontal and limbic regions.22 A critical contributor is the ventral hippocampus (vHPC), a brain region essential for identifying contexts and attributing their associated threat/aversion levels. There is ample evidence linking high vHPC activity to the manifestation of avoidance of real or perceived threats in an environment. Lesioning the vHPC23–25 or inhibiting vHPC projections to the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC)26–28 decreases avoidance of the unprotected open arm of the EPM. Further, increased glutamatergic activity from the basolateral amygdala onto ventral CA1 pyramidal neurons increases and reduced glutamatergic activity decreases EPM avoidance behavior.29 Finally, direct silencing of the ventral dentate gyrus (vDG) of the vHPC confers resilience to chronic stress-induced anxiety-like behavior (avoidance of the center of the OF), whereas excitation of the vDG promotes susceptibility to anxiety.30

The main goal of our study was to identify gestational adversity-induced changes in DNA methylation in vDG granule cells (vDGCs) of the WT offspring of 5HT1AR heterozygote KO mothers that persist into adulthood and, via changes in the neuronal transcriptome and activity, promote the development of innate anxiety-like behavior. We found that genomic regions with an intermediate level of CG methylation and enrichment in annotated transcriptional enhancers and promoters are preferential epigenomic targets of gestational adversity. Gestational adversity resulted in a variable impact on the epigenome and transcriptome of vDGCs, with the most affected cells recruited to an EPM-induced neuronal ensemble. This ensemble exhibited higher activity in the anxiogenic open than in the safe closed arm, likely contributing to the avoidance of the open arm and to the overall anxiety-like behavior of mice with prior gestational adversity.

RESULTS

Anxiety-like behavior induced by an adverse gestational environment is accompanied by reduced synaptic inhibition and intrinsic excitability in vDGCs

We previously reported that the WT offspring of 5HT1AR-deficient mothers exhibit anxiety-like behaviors that are moderately sex biased, with males displaying more pronounced EPM anxiety and increased escape-directed behavior in the forced swim test (interpreted as increased stress responsiveness) than females19–21 (Figure 1A). Embryo and postnatal cross-fostering experiments showed that these behaviors were caused by the preand not the postnatal maternal environment.19 Further, we showed that the offspring behavioral phenotype was due to immune activation in the 5HT1AR-deficient female as well as in the placenta.21 Therefore, the 5HT1AR-deficient maternal model is similar to poly(I:C)- and LPS-induced maternal immune activation (MIA) models of prenatal adversity.10,31–38 We refer to the 5HT1AR gestational adversity model as 5HT1AR-MIA (hereafter called MIA).

We first replicated the anxiety phenotype of the 5HT1AR-MIA male offspring.19–21 MIA mice spent less time in, made fewer entries into, and traveled a shorter distance in the open arms of the EPM compared to the WT offspring of WT SW mothers, hereafter referred to as control (CON) mice (Figure 1B), indicative of increased anxiety-like behavior. There was no change in overall distance traveled (Figure 1C), indicating that the increased avoidance behavior of the MIA offspring was not due to altered locomotor activity.

Since expression of innate anxiety has been linked to enhanced activity in the vDG,23–30 we examined the synaptic transmission and intrinsic excitability of vDGCs using whole-cell slice electrophysiology recordings. We found no effect of MIA on glutamatergic synaptic transmission in vDGCs, as there were no differences in the frequency, amplitude, or overall excitatory synaptic drive (frequency × amplitude) of miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) in vDGCs between 5HT1AR-MIA and CON mice (Figures 1D and 1E). In contrast, inhibitory synaptic transmission was decreased by MIA, as the frequency and amplitude of miniature inhibitory postsynaptic currents (mIPSCs) was lower in MIA than in CON vDCGs, leading to reduced inhibitory synaptic drive (Figures 1F and 1G). Together, these results suggest that there is an overall disinhibition of vDGCs in MIA mice that may confer an aberrant ability for vDGCs to become activated by excitatory synaptic inputs conveying information about context-specific and behaviorally relevant stimuli associated with anxiety-like behavior.39,40

We also examined the effects of MIA on the intrinsic excitability of vDGCs in adulthood using current-clamp recordings. The resting membrane potential (RMP), rheobase, and threshold for firing did not differ between MIA and CON vDGCs (Figures S1A, S1C, and S1D). However, MIA vDGCs displayed a reduction in the number of action potentials fired across increasing step injections of depolarizing current when measured at both RMP and from a common holding potential of −70 mV (Figures 1H–1K and S1B). These results suggest that vDGC excitation may be less sensitive to excitatory synaptic input, perhaps due to activity-dependent plasticity to maintain homeostatic balance in excitability41 to compensate for the loss of tonic inhibition by the local interneuron network, indicating a perturbation in ventral hippocampal function.

An adverse gestational environment alters CG methylation at intermediate methylated regions in vDGCs

We previously reported MIA-induced DNA methylation changes in vDGCs collected from adult MIA and CON male offspring.20 Here, we repeated this experiment by profiling vDGC methylation from individual adult MIA and CON males to assess both MIA-induced changes and individual variability in DNA methylation. The DGC layer was microdissected from coronal cryostat slices of the vHPC with minimal contamination from other cell types20,42 (Figure 2A). We isolated vDGC nuclei and used the enhanced version of reduced representative bisulfite sequencing (eRRBS)43,44 that expanded the number of CGs profiled for DNA methylation to ∼3 million at 10× coverage. The four biological replicates (from individual animals) in the MIA and CON groups showed high concordance in methylation, and hierarchical clustering separated the two groups, indicating that MIA resulted in changes in the vDGC methylome that persisted to adulthood and were consistent across individual mice (Figure S2A).

Of the 2,904,392 CG sites mapped in all replicates at >10× coverage, 2.75% of CGs underwent methylation change (at false discovery rate [FDR] q < 0.01) by MIA, with a slight bias toward hypermethylation over hypomethylation (Figure 2B). For differential methylation (DM) between MIA and CON DGCs, we set the minimum to 10% (Figures 2B and S2B) because this threshold has been reported to balance sensitivity and specificity in DM detection across RRBS analyses methods.45 Further, we clustered 2 or more CGs within 1 kb of each other, as coordinated changes in CG methylation in a region may have greater regulatory potential46 (Figure 2C). Overall, 23,676 (0.8%) of CGs had a methylation difference of >10% (at q < 0.01) and clustered regionally (<1 kb between CGs), specifying 7,901 DM regions (DMRs; 2.67 CGs on average).

Notably, DM CGs within DMRs, both in CON and MIA DGCs, displayed mostly intermediate methylation (IM; 10%–90% methylation) (Figure 2D). IM at a given IM CG, or a cluster of coordinately regulated CGs, corresponds to the percentage of the methylated epiallele in a cell population (Figure 2C). Since this value also represents the percentage of cells carrying the methylated epiallele, DGCs exhibit DNA methylation heterogeneity, especially given the combinatorial effect of the methylated and unmethylated epialleles at thousands of DMRs. In contrast, CGs outside of DMRs showed a genome-typical bimodal methylation distribution, as they were either uniformly unmethylated or methylated in DGCs (Figure 2F). Within DMRs, DM CGs were interspersed with “non-DM” CGs at a 1:2.2 ratio. Non-DM CGs showed partial IM distribution, indicating that many of them have methylation bistability like DM CGs in the vDGC population (Figure 2E). Of note, the wide range of IM of DM CGs in CON mice indicated that methylation bistability was not due to the mixture of the DGC and non-DGC cell populations, such as the small and steady population of progenitors or young (<3 weeks old) neurons present in adult DGs.47

Analyzing hyper- and hypomethylated DMR DM CGs separately showed that their native, generally IM state (in CON DGCs; Figures 2G and 2H, light purple curves) typically remained in the IM range after MIA (Figures 2G and 2H, dark purple curves). Further, neighboring DM CGs tended to show a similar directionality of change, as ∼60% of DMRs had either all hypo- or all hypermethylated DM CGs (26.76% and 34.57%, respectively) (Figures 2I and S3). DMRs with mixed hypo- and hypermethylated DM CGs had twice the size of hypo/hypermethylated DMRs (300.1, 160.0, and 158.3 bp, respectively) but had a similar average number of DM CGs, indicating larger distances between CGs that may disrupt their coordinated response to MIA (Figure S3). Indeed, methylation of CGs in partially methylated regions has been reported to be coordinated only when in close proximity (<15 bp).48

The average MIA-induced methylation change was ∼22% for both hyper- and hypomethylated DM CGs but varied from the 10% threshold to 30%, with few demonstrating larger changes (Figures 2B and 2J). Given the coordinated methylation changes at hypo/hypermethylated DMRs, MIA decreased and increased the fraction of the methylated DMR epiallele at hypo- and hypermethylated DMRs, respectively. Consequently, the proportion of vDGCs that carry the methylated epiallele of an individual DMR was also altered. For example, a DM CG or a cluster of DM CGs with a 50% baseline methylation (in CON) and 20% following MIA-induced hypomethylation implies that the fraction of DGCs harboring the methylated epiallele was reduced from 50% to 20% by MIA.

We reported that IM sites gradually attain methylation from an unmethylated state in progenitors during postnatal DGC maturation.13 The proinflammatory environment in the fetal and early postnatal brain of MIA offspring may influence this dynamic by setting a lower or higher IM set point than the baseline methylation state in CON vDGCs, thus generating the hypo- and hypermethylated DM CGs/DMRs. Interestingly, less than 60%–70% baseline methylation was associated with a higher IM set point and, thus, producing hypermethylated DM CGs/DMRs in MIA DGCs, while higher baseline methylation with a lower IM set point produced hypomethylated DMRs (Figure 2J). Overall, these data indicate that MIA switches the methylation state of individual DMRs from methylated to unmethylated or vice versa in some cells. It is not clear whether all or most DMRs undergo epiallelic switching in the same subpopulation of DGCs or different but overlapping subpopulations of DGCs. Nevertheless, the combinatorial effect of MIA-induced methylation switching at thousands of DMRs is expected to be variable within the population of DGCs, with some cells affected more than others.

Regions hypomethylated by an adverse gestational environment are enriched in cis-regulatory chromatin states

Approximately two-thirds of MIA DMRs were located within the gene body (Figure 3A, inner circle), a significant enrichment due to the overrepresentation of exons, compared to the distribution of randomized control regions (Figure 3A, outer circle; exon odds ratio [OR] = 1.28, p = 7.32E−22; intron OR = 0.94, p = 1.38E−02). Although around one-third of DMRs were intergenic, they were relatively depleted in this genomic feature (OR = 0.84, 1.20E−12), suggesting that MIA DMRs tend to be located in protein-coding regions of the mouse genome rather than in intergenic regions.

IM is often associated with enhancers51–55; therefore we assigned ChromHMM49 chromatin states, as determined previously in mouse DGCs,50 to MIA-DMRs. ChromHMM categorizes chromatin into distinct states (enhancer, promoter, heterochromatin, etc.) based on the combinatorial presence and absence of histone modifications. Of the 7,901 MIA DMRs, ∼35% were in an enhancer-specific chromatin state based on the distinct combinations of the histone marks H3K4me1 (histone H3 lysin4 monomethyl), H3K4me3 (H3K4 trimethyl), and H3K27ac (H3K27 acetyl) in CON DGCs (Figure 3B). Because the basal methylation state of DMRs (in CON DGCs) may influence their chromatin association and the directionality of MIA-induced methylation change, we analyzed hypomethylated, hypermethylated, and mixed DMRs separately. Hypomethylated and mixed DMRs were enriched in enhancer chromatin states, and hypomethylated DMRs were overrepresented in active and weak promoters (Figure 3C). Hypermethylated DMRs were not enriched in either enhancers or promoters. Further, although a substantial fraction of DMRs was associated with facultative and constitutive heterochromatin signatures (HK27me3 and H3K9me3, respectively; Figure 3B), DMRs were depleted in heterochromatin (Figure 3C). This is consistent with the wide distribution of heterochromatin across the genome and the reduced accessibility and, thus, epigenetic malleability of these regions to MIA-induced perturbations. Overall, a substantial fraction of DMRs (45%–50%) was associated with promoters and enhancers, reaching significant enrichment with hypomethylated DMRs and, to a lesser extent, with mixed DMRs, indicating the potential gene-regulatory role of MIA-DMRs.

Distinct sets of transcription factor binding sites in hypo- and hypermethylated DMRs

The different baseline methylations of hypo- and hypermethylated DMRs (in CON animals) and their different directionalities of change by MIA suggested distinct sequence contexts and binding sites for sequence-specific transcription factors (TFs).56 We used MEME Suite57 to discover putative TF binding motifs within these DMRs. We found that, although hypo- and hypermethylated DMRs shared a number of putative TF binding motifs, they had an overall dissimilar motif pattern (Figure 3D). First, although they shared C2-H2 zinc-finger (ZF) TF binding motifs,58 such as SP5 and MAZ, binding sites for additional SP family members were present in hypomethylated DMRs. Further, the two types of DMRs contained sites for different KLF family members. SP/KLF/MAZ bind to similar but distinct GC-rich targets and function as repressors or activators depending on their interacting co-regulators.59 MAZ has been reported to interact with CTCF to control cohesin positioning and genome organization and may act as an insulator protein.60 The binding site for WT1, a ZF TF, was present in hypomethylated DMRs only. Binding of KLF4,61 SP1,62,63 and WT164 is DNA methylation sensitive; thus, MIA induced switching in a given cell from the unmethylated to the methylated epiallele or vice versa can alter TF binding and possibly DMR-associated promoter and enhancer activity. Second, the cognate binding motif for YY1 (TYY1), still another ZF TF that is linked to stress sensitivity,65 was uniquely enriched in hypermethylated DMRs. Similarly, binding sites for four forkhead box TFs that can act as pioneer factors to initiate gene transcription in condensed chromatin during large-scale chromatin remodeling66 were found exclusively in hypermethylated DMRs. Finally, mixed DMRs contained many of the same TFs but also unique binding sites. This included, most prominently, unique sites for two members of the RXR nuclear receptor family (RXRA and RXRG) that are part of the RAR/RXR heterodimers that transduce retinoid signaling during development and in neuronal stress and neuroinflammatory processes in the adult brain.

An adverse gestational environment configures the transcriptome of a subpopulation of vDGCs for activation in a threatening environment

MIA-induced epigenomic changes are expected to lead to transcriptomic changes that may explain the increased anxiety phenotype of MIA mice in the EPM test. Because of ensemble coding in the DG,67 gene expression in cells that are recruited and activated in an anxiogenic environment is the most relevant to the anxiety-like behavior of MIA mice. Therefore, we transcriptionally profiled DGCs that were activated in the EPM separately from the rest of the DGCs of CON and MIA males. We used the expression of the immediate-early gene product FOS, a proxy for neuronal activation,68 to differentiate activated from non-activated cells for fluorescence-activated nucleus sorting. Adult MIA and CON mice were exposed to the EPM for 10 min, and the vHPC was isolated 1 h later for nucleus isolation. Then, FOS+ and FOS− vDGC nuclei (also labeled by the DGC marker PROX169) were sorted for single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) (Figure 4A). A small and comparable fraction of nuclei was FOS+ in CON (0.85%) and MIA (0.98%) mice (average from two experiments), consistent with sparse coding and the tight regulation of ensemble size by inhibitory interneurons in the DG.70–72 After filtering, a total of 16,456 MIA FOS−, 10,576 CON FOS−, 3,797 MIA FOS+, and 1,575 CON FOS+ nuclei were obtained from two replicates of 6 MIA and 6 CON mice each and used, after filtering, for clustering and expression analyses.

Nuclei from all conditions were annotated to 17 cell clusters that were separated based on cell type marker expression and neuronal activation (Figures 4B and S4; Table S1). Clusters DGC-1 to DGC-5 and DGC-10 nuclei formed a large assortment of closely related clusters that contained nuclei primarily from FOS− samples, while nuclei in the smaller DGC-6 to DGC-9 clusters were derived from FOS+ samples (Figures 4B and 4C). In addition, smaller clusters of neuroblasts and oligodendrocytes were mostly derived from FOS− samples, while non-DGC excitatory cells and various interneurons (Cck, Lamp5, Vip, and medial ganglionic eminence [MGE] interneurons) were derived largely from FOS+ cells. Although PROX1 is considered a marker for DGCs, its expression in interneurons and oligodendrocytes (Figure 4D; Prox1) is consistent with previous reports.73 All DGC clusters expressed the excitatory neuronal marker Slc17a7, and all interneurons expressed the inhibitory neuronal marker Gad2 as expected (Figure 4D; Slc17a7 and Gad2). Additionally, clusters that were composed of FOS+ DGCs expressed the immediate-early genes Arc, Homer1, and Bdnf (Figure 4D). As documented previously, FOS+ interneurons did not strongly express these immediate-early genes.74 Finally, all clusters expressed a similar number of genes and transcripts and were composed of similar proportions of nuclei in CON and MIA samples, including the DGC clusters, indicating that MIA does not lead to overt changes in cell identity and in global transcription in the adult DG.

Next, we combined individual DGC clusters into FOS− and FOS+ DGC pseudobulk samples to assess overall transcriptomic differences between MIA and CON DGCs. Although most variation in the dataset was related to whether MIA and CON cells were activated or not in EPM (Tables S2 and S3), likely representing preexisting differences in neuronal eligibility for recruitment to an ensemble,75 principal-component analysis (PCA) of pseudobulk samples also showed separation between CON and MIA mice for both FOS− and FOS+ DGCs, indicating underlying differences in gene expression due to MIA (Figure 4E, PC1 and PC2, respectively). However, both replicates of FOS+ samples displayed a more pronounced separation (e.g., in PC2) between MIA and CON compared to FOS− samples, indicating that EPM-activated vDGCs have greater differences in MIA-induced gene expression. Indeed, we observed 135 upregulated and 102 downregulated genes (i.e., FOS+ differentially expressed genes [DEGs]) in MIA compared to CON FOS+ DGC nuclei (Figure 4F; Table S4). In contrast, only 31 upregulated and 29 downregulated genes (FOS− DEGs) were identified (Figure S5A; Table S5) in MIA compared to CON FOS− DGC nuclei, 34 of which were also FOS+ DEGs (Figure S5B).

Differential expression between MIA and CON mice within individual clusters yielded the same DEGs as those generated with all DGC clusters combined, although the numbers of statistically significant DEGs were reduced because of the smaller cluster sizes, particularly in FOS+ samples. Of the 60 FOS− DEGs identified in the combined DGC clusters, 31 were also cluster-specific DEGs in individual (often multiple) clusters (Figure S4E). Conversely, cluster-specific DEGs were typically among the combined-cluster FOS− DEG set. For example, DGC-2, the largest FOS− DGC cluster (Figure 4B), had 44 DEGs, 28 of which overlapped with FOS− combined-cluster DEGs. Likewise, 17 of the 19 DGC-1, 20 of the 26 DGC-3, and 7 of the 8 DGC-5 DEGs were also shared. Due to the small size of FOS+ clusters, only 5 cluster-specific DEGs were detected, 4 of which overlapped with FOS+ combined-cluster DEGs. Finally, to control for sample size differences, we assessed differential expression after downsampling FOS− and FOS+ replicates to 2,478 and 381 nuclei each, respectively. Most DEGs remained consistent, indicating that DEG discovery was not driven by total nuclei number per sample (Figure S4F). Differential expression in non-DGC nuclei that included various interneurons, neuroblasts, and oligodendrocytes (Figure 4B) could not be assessed due to small population sizes. Overall, these findings show that individual DGC clusters contribute similarly to the overall transcriptomic effects of MIA.

We next investigated the functional relevance of DEGs through overrepresentation analysis. FOS+ DEGs were enriched in synapse-related genes (Gene Ontology [GO] cellular components) relevant to both the presynaptic active zone (at mossy terminals) and postsynaptic density (at the entorhinal input) (Figures 4G, left, and S6) implicating altered synaptic functions at multiple levels in MIA DGCs. FOS− DEGs, on the other hand, showed enrichment in only one category, neuron projection terminus (adjusted p = 0.037) (Figure S5C).

To explore whether these functional gene expression changes were associated with underlying epigenetic regulation, we next examined the relationship between DEGs and MIA-DMRs. Nearly half of the 237 FOS+ DEGs (109 genes, both up- and downregulated) were associated with differentially methylated genes (DMGs) assigned to DMRs via GREAT76 (Figure 4H, top). However, most of the 7,530 DMGs showed no corresponding expression changes (Figures 4H and S7). Nonetheless, the 109 overlapping FOS+ DEG × DMGs were enriched in several of the same synapse-related GO cellular component categories as the full FOS+ DEG set (e.g., synapse, synaptic membrane, and presynaptic zone; Figure 4G, right). DEGs in these synapse-related categories were also more likely to be associated with DMRs than genes not enriched in GO terms (Yates-corrected χ2 (1, n = 237) = 4.37, p = 0.0366). Further analysis showed that both the up- and downregulated FOS+ DEGs × DMGs (Figure 4H, bottom) contained various combinations of hypomethylated, hypermethylated, and mixed DMRs (Figure S7), although more upregulated genes were associated with only hyper- rather than hypomethylated DMRs (14 vs. 6), while downregulated genes had equal numbers of each (9 each). Many FOS+ DEG × DMGs were associated with mixed DMRs, suggesting a complex relationship between methylation and gene expression. In contrast, FOS− DEGs × DMGs were represented by only 34 genes, 18 of which were also in the group of the 109 FOS+ DEGs × DMGs, highlighting again the more robust transcriptional response in FOS+ than in FOS− cells by MIA.

Adverse gestational environment-induced DEGs are associated with neuropsychiatric disease risk genes

Because anxiety is a common feature across psychiatric disorders and often co-occurs with conditions such as depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and panic disorder, we investigated whether FOS+ DEGs were associated with known neuropsychiatric disease risk genes. Using the PsyGeNET database (v.2.0, 1,549 genes and 117 psychiatric disease concepts),77 we found that 21 FOS+ DEGs (9%) were previously implicated in neuropsychiatric disease (Figure 4I). Furthermore, 11 of these 21 (∼52%) genes were also DMGs, showing concurrent perturbations in methylation and transcription by MIA in this set of genes. Some of these genes were common to multiple diseases (e.g., Ntrk2 in alcohol use disorders, bipolar and affective disorders, depressive disorders, and schizophrenia and psychotic disorders) consistent with the transdiagnostic nature of many psychiatric disease risk genes.78 Interestingly, a majority (14 genes, ∼67%) of DEGs were represented specifically in schizophrenia and psychotic disorders, which is consistent with maternal gestational infection/immune activation being a risk factor for schizophrenia79,80 and with maternal poly(I:C)-induced MIA causing a schizophrenia-like phenotype in mouse offspring.81,82 Overall, the data reveal a relationship between DNA methylation and transcriptional regulation of synaptic genes and neuropsychiatric disease, specifically in FOS+ vDGCs following MIA.

vDGCs of MIA relative to CON mice exhibit increased calcium activity in a threatening relative to a safe environment

While DGCs in general have low firing rates due to low input resistance and hyperpolarized RMP,83 the firing rates of the small population of DGCs activated during a novel experience are increased.84–86 To monitor neuronal activity, especially of DGCs that are activated during exploration of the threatening open and safe closed arms of the EPM, we delivered an adeno-associated virus (AAV) expressing the Ca2+ biosensor GCaMP6 into the vDG and measured Ca2+ activity by fiber photometry87 (Figures 5A and 5B). We first assessed whether the activity of vDGCs differed depending on whether mice were entering the safe vs. threatening zones of the maze. We found that vDGC calcium activity in MIA mice, but not CON mice, was higher from the baseline during behavioral sequences in which they transitioned from safe to threatening environments (closed arm-center-open arm sequences) than from safe to safe environments (closed arm-center-closed arm sequences), particularly in the center zone of the maze (Figure 5C). We further broke down these behavioral sequences into their transition components to investigate whether calcium activity changes were specifically time locked to closed-arm exits and/or subsequent open- or closed-arm entries and whether that contributed to differences in the pattern of GCaMP activity changes between CON and MIA mice (Figures 5D–5K). We found that vDGC GCaMP activity was greater in the center zone in MIA than CON mice during closed arm-center transitions that preceded open-arm entries, but there were no differences between groups or changes across the subsequent open-arm entries themselves (Figures 5D and 5E). The amplitude of the transient GCaMP calcium events was higher in MIA than CON mice in the center zone during closed to open-arm transitions, while there were no differences across subsequent open-arm entries in either group or between groups (Figure 5F). Transient event frequency did not differ between groups or across zone transitions (Figure 5G). The change in event amplitude (but not frequency) across closed arm-to-center transitions was positively correlated with the change in GCaMP Z score in MIA (Pearson’s R = 0.812, p = 0.008) but not CON mice (Pearson’s R = −0.233, p = 0.767), suggesting that it contributed to the observed elevated overall GCaMP activity in MIA mice shown in Figures 5C–5E. In contrast, neither overall GCaMP activity nor the amplitude or frequency of calcium transients differed between groups or changed across transitions during safe-to-safe behavioral sequences (Figures 5H–5K). Overall, these data show that vDGC activity in MIA mice ramps up during closed-arm exits prior entering the open arm, which may reflect their enhanced perception of the potential threat associated with entering to the unprotected open arm.

DISCUSSION

The main finding of our study is that an adverse gestational environment leads to DNA methylation changes at enhancers and promoters and alters expression of synapse-related genes in adult vDGCs. Notably, vDGCs with the most pronounced transcriptomic changes are preferentially recruited into FOS+ ensembles during exposure to the stressful EPM environment. Ensemble activity was also elevated during transitions from safe to threatening conditions, consistent with the increased avoidance behavior observed in MIA mice. Together, these data reveal, for the first time, an epigenetic and neurobiological sequence linking an adverse gestational environment to later-life anxiety-like behavior. This mechanism may help explain the persistent stress sensitivity and avoidance seen in individuals with innate anxiety.

An adverse gestational environment preferentially targets intermediately methylated CGs

The intrauterine environment of 5HT1AR heterozygote mothers resulted in methylation changes in thousands of small genomic regions with an intermediate level of CG methylation in vDGCs. We have previously reported similar IM regions in CA1 neurons (dorsal and ventral combined) that represent the output of the hippocampus13 as well as in CA3 neurons, indicating that this DNA methylation pattern is a general feature of hippocampal subregions and that CA neurons may also undergo MIA-induced DNA methylation changes. IM levels at individual differentially methylated CGs (DM CGs) and DMRs are likely influenced by local DNA sequence and chromatin context. While specific sequence features help establish methylation in partially methylated domains (PMDs),48 PMDs are much larger (∼153 kb) than MIA-DMRs (150–200 bp) and are limited to certain cell types, such as cultured fibroblasts and cancer cells. This suggests that sequence alone is not sufficient to produce IM. Since active and bound TFs can dynamically modulate methylation at cis-regulatory elements,52,88,89 chromatin and associated TFs56 are likely involved in maintaining the IM state at MIA-DMRs.

An adverse gestational environment induces methylation switching at DMRs

Coexistence of the methylated and unmethylated epialleles in the same population of cells suggests relatively unobstructed epiallelic switching that could be sensitive to environmental influences like the proinflammatory environment in the developing brain.20 We reported that not only the maternal peripheral immune system and the placenta but also the brain of newborn pups exhibits signs of inflammation in the MIA offspring20 (see also introduction).

The 10%–30% (mean 22%) difference at most DMRs in bulk methylation between MIA and CON DGCs indicates epiallelic switching at a given DM CG/DMR in a similar proportion of vDGCs by the adverse experience. Since the fetal hippocampus at the time of MIA (during the midgestational period21) consists of proliferating progenitors,90,91 and because IM in young DGCs is established by preferential methylation after the first week of life,13 MIA-induced epiallelic shifts likely occur during DGC maturation in the MIA brain. Further, DMRs with methylation higher and lower than the 60%–70% baseline methylation in CON DGCs become hypo- and hypermethylated in MIA brains as a result of lowered and increased IM set points, respectively.

Epigenetic malleability of cis-regulatory elements by an adverse gestational environment

IM regions are often in an enhancer-specific chromatin state,51–55 and ∼35% of DMRs were associated with enhancer-specific chromatin in CON DGCs. An additional 10%–15% of DMRs were in promoter-specific chromatin states. Overall, these data suggest that up to 50% of the epigenomic targets in the 5HT1AR MIA model are IM regions with enhancer- or promoter-specific chromatin associations and thus can have regulatory activity.

The most MIA-affected cells are allocated to a FOS+ ensemble in an anxiogenic environment

Consistent with sparse coding in the DG,71,72 only a small fraction of vDGCs was activated and expressed the activity marker FOS during exploration in the EPM. Remarkably, FOS+ DGCs exhibited four times more MIA-induced DEGs than FOS− DGCs, indicating that cells with the greatest transcriptomic alterations are preferentially recruited during the EPM task. This difference is unlikely to result from EPM exposure itself, as none of the DEGs (between MIA and CON) were immediate-early genes. Instead, MIA-induced differential expression likely preceded the EPM and contributed to the preferential allocation of highly affected cells to the FOS+ ensemble in MIA mice.

FOS+ DEGs were exclusively enriched in synapse-related GO categories, and DEGs within these categories were more likely to be associated with DMRs than unassigned DEGs. This suggests that DNA methylation may contribute to MIA-induced differential expression of a subset of synapse-related genes. However, most DMR-associated genes were not differentially expressed, consistent with the idea that DNA methylation is only one of several epigenetic mechanisms regulating gene expression.

Adverse gestational environment-induced epigenomic and transcriptomic maladaptation in the vDG governs adult anxiety behavior

Why are the most adversity-affected DGCs allocated to the EPM-activated ensemble? Since relative excitability has been shown to be a major determinant of neuronal activation and allocation to a coding ensemble in the DG,92 as well as in the CA1 region and amygdala,93,94 cells that are most affected by MIA in the epigenetically and transcriptionally heterogeneous population of MIA vDGCs may have increased excitability. However, expression of FOS and other immediate-early genes was comparable in MIA and CON FOS+ cells (e.g., were not DEGs), suggesting no apparent difference in neuronal activity between the MIA and CON FOS+ DGC populations. Another factor in determining neuronal allocation is functional connectivity. It has been shown that preexisting connectivity promotes allocation of CA1 neurons to a coding ensemble.94 Similarly, CA3 neurons with a similar birth date are functionally connected, as neurons born earlier and later during embryonic development are recruited to different ensembles during fear conditioning in adults.95 Because MIA resulted in expression changes in pre- and postsynaptic genes, it is reasonable to hypothesize that the most transcriptionally altered MIA DGCs have increased connectivity and are preferentially activated in the EPM. Further, given the correlation between vDG activity and innate anxiety, the enhanced in vivo activity of the EPM-activated ensemble during the transition from the safe closed arm to the threatening open arm in MIA mice is consistent with their increased avoidance behavior. We propose that MIA exerts a variable impact on the epigenome and synaptic transcriptome of vDGCs. The most affected vDGCs, which display changes primarily in synaptic genes, are preferentially recruited (i.e., express FOS) in an anxiogenic environment, and their heightened activity contributes to avoidance and anxiety-like behaviors.

Limitations of the study

We show that MIA results in methylation switching at DMRs only in a fraction of cells, estimated to be between 10% and 30%. Similarly, the impact of MIA on the transcriptome was variable, with FOS+ cells recruited during the EPM exhibiting 4 times more DEGs than FOS− cells. Because FOS+ DEGs that encode synaptic genes were more likely than unassigned DEGs to be associated with DMRs, we predict that cells with methylation switching at more DMRs have more transcriptional changes. However, demonstrating a direct association between methylation and transcription changes will require future studies using simultaneous single-cell methylome and transcriptome profiling. Further, defining chromatin states in both CON and MIA mice would help clarify how MIA-induced methylation may regulate gene expression. Although the vDG is closely linked to anxiety-like behavior, the hippocampal output region CA1, which connects to anxiety-related areas like the amygdala and mPFC, likely also plays a role. We have shown previously that CA113 and CA3 neurons harbor IM regions, suggesting that this methylation pattern is a general feature of hippocampal subregions. However, the specific effects of MIA on the CA1 methylome and transcriptome remain to be investigated.

STAR★METHODS

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

Experimental mice were either Swiss Webster wild type (WT) control (CON) or 5HT1A–R+/+ (MIA) males and ≥12 weeks of age. Males were used throughout because the behavioral phenotype is sex biased, with males displaying more pronounced anxiety and increased escape directed behavior than females.19–21 5HT1A–R deficient mice were generated as previously reported19,20.. All animal experiments were carried out in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines under protocols approved by the Weill Cornell Medical College Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. All mice were group housed under a 12–hour light/dark cycle with lights on at 6 a.m. Food and water were available ad libitum.

METHOD DETAILS

Behavioral testing

Mice were habituated to the behavior room for 1 h (2 h for snRNA-seq experiments) prior to testing. Briefly, mice were tested at 13 lux (dim overhead lighting) on a cross EPM maze with 19.5 × 2.5 inch arms. Animals were placed on the middle portion of the maze facing an open arm and allowed to freely explore for 10 min. Time spent in, number of entries into, and distance traveled in the open and closed arms were measured by the video tracking system EthoVision 15 (Noldus Information Technology). Differences between groups were assessed using unpaired t-tests and adjusted for multiple testing corrections using the Holm-Sidak method in GraphPad Prism.

Slice electrophysiology

Slice electrophysiology experiments were performed as previously described2. Mice were rapidly decapitated under isoflurane anesthesia before brain collection. Brains were blocked on the coronal plane and acute 300 μm coronal slices prepared and incubated in carbogenated solutions with a pH of 7.35 and osmolarity of 305 as previously described3. Sections including the ventral dentate gyrus were sliced in N-methyl-D-glucamine (NMDG) artificial cerebrospinal fluid (NMDG-aCSF) at room temperature (RT) containing (in mM): 92 NMDG, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 20 HEPES, 25 glucose, 2 thiourea, 5 Na-ascorbate, 3 Na-pyruvate, 0.5 CaCl2⋅2H2O, and 10 MgSO4⋅7H2O. Slices were transferred to NMDG-aCSF at 32°C for 12–14 min, and then incubated for at least 1 h at RT in HEPES-aCSF containing (in mM): 92 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 30 NaHCO3, 20 HEPES, 25 glucose, 2 thiourea, 5 Na-ascorbate, 3 Na-pyruvate, 2 CaCl2⋅2H2O, and 2 MgSO4⋅7H2O. Slices were placed in the recording chamber and perfused at a rate of 2 mL/min with 30°C normal aCSF containing (in mM): 124 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 24 NaHCO3, 12.5 glucose, 5 HEPES, 2 CaCl2⋅2H2O, and 2 MgSO4⋅7H2O, for at least 20 min prior to electrophysiological recordings. Miniature postsynaptic currents were measured in a voltage clamp configuration using a cesium-gluconate-based intracellular recording solution containing (in mM): 117 D-gluconic acid, 20 HEPES, 0.4 EGTA, 5 TEA, 2 MgCl2–6H2O, 4 Na-ATP and 0.4 Na-GTP (pH 7.3 and 290 mOsm), and a holding potential of −70 mV. Miniature postsynaptic currents were measured in the presence of tetrodotoxin (TTX, 1 μM) in the aCSF bath. Signals were acquired using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices), digitized, and analyzed via pClamp 10.4 or 11 software (Molecular Devices). Excitability experiments were performed in current-clamp configuration using a potassium gluconate-based intracellular recording solution containing (in mM): 135 KGluc, 5 NaCl, 2 MgCl2−6H2O, 10 HEPES, 0.6 EGTA, 4 Na-ATP and 0.4 NaGTP (pH 7.3 and 290 mOsm) at −70 mV and resting membrane potential. Input resistance and access resistance were continuously monitored throughout experiments, and cells in which properties changed by more than 20% were not included in data analysis.

Statistical analyses showed postsynaptic currents data were normally distributed in log space (with no outliers), but not always in raw space, as is common for physiological data including electrophysiology measures. Therefore, we analyzed these data in log space with parametric statistical analyses (for which they meet all assumptions), and we represent the raw data points on a log scale to accurately represent the way they were analyzed.

Single nucleus isolation

Following cervical dislocation, brains were extracted and cut coronally into 1mm sections on a brain block. The ventral hippocampus was dissected from the appropriate sections and collected into D-PBS (without Ca2+ or Mg2+) with 1X protease inhibitor on ice. Before nuclei isolation, the tissue was spun down and the buffer was removed. Nuclei were isolated using the Minute Single Nucleus Isolation Kit for Neuronal Tissues/Cells (Invent Biotechnologies, Inc.) with slight modifications to manufacturer protocol. After adding 200 μl of Buffer A, tissue was homogenized with a pestle using 30–50 twists. An additional 400 μl of Buffer A was added, and tissue was homogenized with another 10 twists and then incubated at −20°C for 10 min. Homogenate was first filtered through a 70 μM filter, and then transferred to a filter column provided in the kit. The homogenate was spun at 800g for 5 min at 4°C. The pellet was resuspended with 500 μl of 1% BSA in DPBS with 0.2U/ul of RNase inhibitor and spun again at 600 g for 5 min at 4C. Nuclei were resuspended in the same buffer to a concentration of 1000–5000 nuclei/ul.

Fluorescence activated nuclei sorting

Nuclei were stained for sorting as previously described4. Briefly, first, nuclei were first incubated with Fc Block ((101302, Biolegend, 1:1000) on ice for 10 min to reduce non-specific antibody binding on ice. Nuclei were then stained with anti-PROX1 Alexa Flour 647 (NBP-1–30045AF647, NovusBio, 1:5000) and/or anti-c-FOS Alexa Flour 488 (NBP2–50037AF488, NovusBio, 1:5000), and incubated on ice for 30 min. Nuclei were washed with an equal volume of buffer (1% BSA in D-PBS with 0.2 U/ul of RNase inhibitor) and spun at 600 g for 5 min at 4°C before resuspension to a concentration of 1000–5000 nuclei/ul for sorting on a BD Influx Cell Sorter.

RRBS

Brains were extracted after cervical dislocation, frozen on dry ice and sectioned (200 μM) on the cryostat. DNA was isolated from microdissected ventral dentate gyrus tissue using the QIAamp DNA Micro Kit (Qiagen) according to manufacturer instructions. RRBS libraries were prepared by the Epigenomics Core at Weill Cornell Medicine. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 in a 100 bp paired end mode with a target of 100 million reads per sample (n = 4 animals per group).

Alignment to the mm10 reference genome and methylation calling was done using Bismark v0.22.3.96 Differential methylation analysis was performed with methylKit v1.26 in R 4.3.1.97 Briefly, CGs with at least 10 coverage reads but not more than the 99.9 percentile of coverage reads were kept. Samples were merged, and only CGs covered by all samples were tested for differential methylation using default settings. CGs were considered differentially methylated with 1) a significance threshold of q < 0.01 and 2) at least 10% difference in methylation between the groups of interest. Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were defined as regions containing at least two differentially methylated CpGs with ≤1 kb distance between the sites. DMRs were classified as hypomethylated, hypermethylated or mixed based on whether all differentially methylated CpGs in the DMR were hypomethylated, hypermethylated or a combination of both. Genes were then assigned to DMRs using rGREAT76 2.2.0 in the “basalPlusExt” mode.

Background block regions for statistical tests were determined by clustering all tested CG sites with the same criteria used to cluster DMRs.

DMR annotation

Mm10 genomic coordinates for exons and introns were downloaded from the University of California Santa Cruz (UCSC) genome browser. Promoters were defined as regions ±500 bp from the transcription start site (TSS). Intergenic regions were found through exclusion of genic regions from chromosome coordinates. ChromHMM chromatin state coordinates were extracted from previously published data8 from mouse dentate gyrus. DMRs were then annotated to genomic or chromatin features using bedtools intersect. Enrichment of DMRs in chromatin states, including odds ratios, was calculated using a Fisher’s exact test comparing against background block regions. P-values were corrected using false discovery rate (FDR <0.05). Motif enrichment analysis was carried out using SEA from MEME Suite tools57,101 with default parameters.

SnRNA–seq and analysis

Final single nuclei suspensions (n = 2 per sample group each pooled from 6 animals) were submitted to the Weill Cornell Epigenomic Core for library preparation and sequencing. Sample libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell Reagent V3 Kit from 10X Genomics according to manufacturer instructions. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 to a depth of approximately 25,000–30,000 target reads per nucleus.

Raw sequencing data were processed using the CellRanger v6.0.0 pipeline and aligned to the mm10 reference transcriptome with introns included. Gene–expression matrices of samples were loaded into R 4.3.1, assessed for quality, and analyzed using Seurat 5.1.0.98 Nuclei with fewer than 500 or greater than 6000 genes, greater than 0.1% mitochondrial reads gene reads, and fewer than 1000 UMIs were excluded from analysis. Genes expressed in fewer than 10 nuclei were also filtered out. After normalization with Sctransform v2, samples were integrated using 3,000 variable features. Nuclei were clustered using 40 PCs with a resolution of 0.5. 2 low quality clusters were removed after which nuclei were re-clustered using 40 PCs and a resolution of 0.6. One FOS+ cluster was subclustered using a resolution of 0.1. Cluster markers were found with the FindAllMarkers function (min.pct = 0.25, logfc. threshold = 0.25) using the default Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Bonferroni correction, and cell types were annotated using known cell markers.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by aggregating counts for each replicate per group (WT FOS−, WT FOS +, MIA FOS−, MIA FOS+) across all DGC clusters, with replicate included as a covariate in the DESeq299 design model. Genes with at least 50 aggregated counts in at least two samples were tested for differential expression (DE). Pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wald test, and log2 fold changes were shrunk using the lfcShrink function with the “ashr” method. DEG lists (adjusted p < 0.1) were further filtered to retain only genes expressed in at least 5% of nuclei in one of the sample groups.

DE for downsampled data was assessed using the same approach, following random subsampling of 2,478 nuclei per FOS− replicate and 381 per FOS+ replicate to account for differences in nuclei numbers.

Cluster-specific DEGs were identified with slight modifications to the pseudobulk approach. For DGC clusters 1–5 and 10, FOS− nuclei were subset per cluster for count aggregation per MIA and CON replicate. For DGC clusters 6–9, FOS+ nuclei were similarly subset. DE was then conducted as described above.

Overrepresentation analysis for enrichment in gene ontology categories was done using ClusterProfiler 4.8.2100 with all tested genes set as the background universe. Significant categories (p-adjusted <0.05) were then filtered for redundancy based on p-value and genes represented in each category. Differentially expressed genes that are also neuropsychiatric disease risk genes were identified with psygenet2r.77

GCaMP fiber photometry surgery and behavior

Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and placed onto a stereotaxic frame to perform craniotomy. AAV9-CamKII-GCaMP6s-WPRE.SV40 (275 nL) was site-delivered to the vDG (−3.65 mm A-P, −2.7 mm M-L, −3.0 mm D-V relative to bregma) at a rate of 40 nL per minute using a 1 μL Hamilton 7000 series syringe with a 32-gauge blunt needle attached to a microsyringe pump and controller. The syringe was withdrawn slowly 7 min following injection completion to avoid spreading of the AAV outside of the DG. A 400 μm diameter optical fiber was then implanted above the vDG with the tip at 0.2 mm dorsal to the injection site using Metabond to secure it to the skull. Mice performed a single 10 min EPM trial 3–4 weeks following surgery. Optical patch cables were photobleached through the Tucker-Davis Technologies fiber photometry workstation for at least 12 h prior to being tethered to the mouse’s head stage prior to EPM performance. Raw GCaMP and isosbestic point control signals were collected in the 465 nm and 405 nm channels, respectively, at a sampling rate of 1017.25 Hz. Following EPM performance, mice were perfused with 4% PFA and their brain collected, post-fixed, and sliced into 45 μm coronal sections that were mounted for confirmation of viral expression and correct optical fiber placement. Mice lacking robust enough signal for acquisition or with optical fiber placement outside of the vDG were excluded from analysis.

GCaMP fiber photometry data analysis

Fiber photometry signals acquired during behavior were processed via a custom Python script in Jupyter Notebook v7.2.2 (Project Jupyter, USA). Electrical artifacts were removed using a median filter with a kernel size of 101 and high-frequency noise reduced using a 2 Hz frequency lowpass filter, followed by downsampling by factor of 100. Motion and photobleaching artifacts were corrected by employing a linear fit of the GCaMP signal to the isosbestic control signal, scaling the isosbestic control by the resulting coefficients of the linear fit, and subtracting the scaled control signal from the GCaMP signal. Final signal values were calculated by computing a Z score by subtracting the mean of the whole trace from each datapoint and dividing it by the standard deviation of the trace. Files containing the final processed data were converted into Axon Binary File (.abf) format using Clampfit v10.7.0.3 (Molecular Devices, USA) and Mini Analysis v6.0.3 (Synaptosoft, USA) was used to detect transient calcium events within the GCaMP signal. Ethovision 15 was used to track mouse behavior within the EPM, and temporal alignment of behavior tracking, and GCaMP signal was used to time-lock GCaMP activity to specific behavioral epochs within the maze.

Statistical analyses of the GCaMP signal data were performed in GraphPad Prism v10.4.2 (GraphPad Software, USA) using two-tailed analyses with an alpha level of 0.05. Two-way and three-way repeated measures ANOVAs (RM-ANOVAs) were used to examine the effects of MIA, EPM zone, and transition sequences on GCaMP measures of Z score and the frequency and amplitude of calcium transient events. For all analyses in Figure 5, at least 2.5 s duration in the EPM zone was required for a GCaMP data point to be included, as analysis of variance showed that dependent GCaMP measures were not reliable with <2.5 s representation within the sample; mixed effects models were used when one or more repeated measure data point was unavailable. For analyses specifically examining GCaMP pre vs. post transition as a repeated measure (Figures 5D–5K), both pre- and post-transition stay duration had to meet the 2.5 s minimum to be included, e.g., both closed arm and center time had to be at least 2.5 s for both data points to be included for analysis of closed arm-center transition. Following significant effects in the analyses, post hoc t-tests with Holm-Šídák corrections for multiple comparisons were used (adjusted p-values are reported). Correlation analyses were performed using linear regressions. Data in the figure are presented as mean ± SEM.
