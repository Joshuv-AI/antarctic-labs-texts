Advanced website fingerprinting for detecting VPN-based censorship evasion: a transformer-based approach
Authors: Hadeel Alazzam, Orieb Abualganam, Wesam Almobaideen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe widespread use of Virtual Private Networks (VPNs) and encrypted tunnels has enabled users to bypass state-level censorship. However, it has also created significant challenges for legitimate network monitoring and content governance. Website Fingerprinting (WF) offers a privacy-preserving alternative to deep packet inspection by identifying encrypted traffic based on metadata patterns such as packet direction, timing, and burst structure. Nevertheless, existing deep learning models, primarily Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are highly vulnerable to obfuscation techniques such as Website Traffic Fingerprinting Protection with Adaptive Defense (WTF-PAD), Walkie-Talkie, and Front, which disrupt local burst signatures and degrade classification accuracy. To overcome these limitations, we propose Temporal Patch Attention with Self-Supervised Traffic Masking (TPA-SSTM), a novel hybrid CNN-Transformer architecture designed for robust website fingerprinting for traffic that is protected by encryption measures. TPA-SSTM leverages a 1D CNN backbone to extract hierarchical local features, then applies semantic patching to segment the feature sequence into high-level behavioral phases which are initial load, resource fetching, and idle/user interaction treating each as a structured token for sequence modeling. These patches are embedded and processed by a Transformer encoder with multi-head self-attention, enabling the model to capture long-range temporal dependencies across traffic phases. The framework is further enhanced with self-supervised traffic masking as on-the-fly data augmentation, along with class-balanced learning to improve generalization under noisy and imbalanced data conditions. The proposed TPA-SSTM has been evaluated across four datasets: a non-defended Closed World (CW) and three defended scenarios (WTF-PAD, Walkie-Talkie, and Front). Experimental results demonstrate that TPA-SSTM achieves 97.4% accuracy on CW, 91.9% accuracy on WTF-PAD, 95.25% accuracy on Walkie-Talkie, and 85.91% accuracy on Front, significantly outperforming state-of-the-art baselines.Keywords: Censorship Evasion, Deep Learning, Encrypted Traffic Analysis, CNN-Transformer, VPN Detection, Website FingerprintingSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Network-level control of web access has become a routine component of Internet governance. Governments and Internet Service Providers (ISPs) deploy mechanisms such as website blocking, traffic filtering, and domain-based restrictions to enforce political, social, or legal policies1. In practice, these mechanisms are often implemented at the network layer, where decisions must be made without direct visibility into application-level content. As a result, access restrictions can affect a wide range of online services, including educational platforms, news outlets, and communication tools2.

In response to such controls, users increasingly rely on privacy-enhancing technologies, including Virtual Private Networks (VPNs) and anonymization systems, to conceal destination information and evade filtering3. By encrypting traffic and tunneling connections through remote endpoints, these tools make it difficult for network operators to determine which websites are being accessed. While this behavior supports user privacy and circumvention of blocking, it also complicates network management tasks related to policy enforcement, traffic analysis, and regulatory compliance4.

Website fingerprinting (WF) addresses this challenge by attempting to infer visited websites from observable traffic characteristics rather than payload content. Existing WF techniques analyze side-channel information such as packet direction, timing patterns, and burst structure, enabling classification even when traffic is protected by TLS, VPNs, or Tor5. Unlike deep packet inspection, which relies on payload visibility and raises legal and privacy concerns, WF operates entirely on encrypted traffic metadata. This paper builds on prior work in this area by examining the effectiveness of modern WF defenses and analyzing their implications for practical deployment in encrypted network environments6.

However, applying WF in real-world censorship scenarios introduces significant challenges. Modern censorship evasion tools employ traffic padding, packet reordering, and multiplexing (e.g., Walkie-Talkie, BuFLO) to obfuscate traffic patterns and defeat traditional classifiers. Moreover, the use of long-lived encrypted tunnels and cross-traffic mixing further reduces the signal-to-noise ratio, degrading the performance of conventional machine learning models7.

Prior work has demonstrated that deep learning models, such as Deep Fingerprinting (DF)8 and Var-CNN9, can achieve high accuracy (e.g., ) in closed-world website fingerprinting. However, these models rely heavily on local burst patterns captured through 1D convolutions, making them vulnerable to traffic obfuscation techniques such as padding, packet reordering, and interactive defenses. Furthermore, most existing evaluations are conducted on non-defended or lightly defended datasets (e.g., CW), limiting their validity in real-world censorship environments where tools like WTF-PAD and Walkie-Talkie are commonly deployed10,11. This gap underscores the need for models that can capture long-range temporal dependencies and generalize across diverse obfuscation strategies, motivating our use of a Transformer-based architecture.

This work addresses these limitations by introducing Temporal Patch Attention with Self-Supervised Traffic Masking (TPA-SSTM), a novel hybrid CNN-Transformer architecture for robust website fingerprinting under encrypted and obfuscated conditions. TPA-SSTM integrates the following components:

A CNN backbone for extracting fine-grained local burst features.

We evaluate TPA-SSTM across four diverse datasets: CW, WTF-PAD, Walkie-Talkie, and Front, demonstrating state-of-the-art performance in both non-defended and strongly defended scenarios. Our results show that TPA-SSTM achieves 97.4% accuracy in the non-defended closed world and 95.25% macro F1 on Walkie-Talkie, outperforming DF and other baselines.

Background and related work

This section reviews the foundational concepts and prior advances in encrypted traffic analysis, website fingerprinting (WF), and deep learning for network traces. It establishes the evolution from traditional classifiers to modern deep models, highlights the limitations of existing approaches under traffic obfuscation, and introduces key ideas such as semantic modeling, attention mechanisms, and hybrid architectures that motivate the design of TPA-SSTM.

Website fingerprinting: from metadata to machine learning

WF is a side-channel attack that enables an observer to infer which website a user is visiting by analyzing encrypted network traffic without decrypting payloads12,13. Since protocols like TLS and HTTPS encrypt content, attackers instead exploit metadata leakage, including:

Early WF systems used handcrafted features (e.g., number of bursts, total upload/download volume) combined with machine learning classifiers such as SVM or k-NN14. While effective in controlled environments, these methods required domain expertise and lacked generalization across diverse network conditions.

The shift toward end-to-end deep learning eliminated the need for manual feature engineering. Models can now learn hierarchical representations directly from raw packet sequences, significantly improving accuracy and robustness8,15.

Deep learning models for encrypted traffic

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs): Convolutional architectures have become standard in WF due to their ability to capture local temporal patterns such as request-response cycles and burst structures. DeepFingerprinting (DF)8 demonstrated that a 1D CNN trained on direction-only sequences achieves over 90% accuracy in closed-world settings. Similarly, Var-CNN9 extends this idea with variable-length convolutions for improved scalability.

However, CNNs are inherently limited in modeling long-range dependencies. They excel at detecting short-term patterns (e.g., initial handshake) but struggle to link them with later phases (e.g., JavaScript loading), making them vulnerable to obfuscation techniques that alter local structure while preserving global behavior.

Recurrent Networks (RNNs/LSTMs): To model sequential dynamics, RNNs and LSTMs were explored15. These models process traffic step-by-step and maintain hidden states across time, offering better memory than CNNs. However, they suffer from vanishing gradients, slow training, and difficulty scaling to long sequences common in real-world browsing sessions.

Moreover, neither CNNs nor RNNs explicitly model inter-phase relationships, such as how the timing of resource fetching affects user interaction patterns.

Attention mechanisms and transformers in network analysis

Transformer architectures have attracted increasing attention beyond their original use in natural language processing and computer vision16,17. Their defining feature, self-attention, allows relationships between distant elements in a sequence to be modeled directly, without relying on strictly local receptive fields. This property makes Transformers a natural candidate for analyzing sequential data in which long-range dependencies carry semantic meaning. Previous work in website fingerprinting has relied heavily on convolutional neural networks. Approaches such as Deep Fingerprinting18 show that 1D CNNs can successfully capture burst-level patterns from encrypted traffic. These models achieve strong results when no defenses are present. However, because they mainly focus on short, localized temporal features, they struggle to model relationships that extend across longer portions of a traffic trace. This weakness becomes more apparent when defenses like Walkie-Talkie or WTF-PAD are used, as these mechanisms are specifically designed to distort local timing and burst behavior. To address this issue, more recent studies have considered attention-based models for traffic analysis. This direction is motivated by the sequential nature of network traffic and the need to account for long-range temporal structure. At the same time, applying Transformer models to encrypted traffic is not straightforward. Traffic data can be represented in several ways, such as raw packet sequences, aggregated statistics, or segmented traces, and each representation emphasizes different aspects of behavior. The usefulness of attention mechanisms therefore depends strongly on how traffic is encoded and whether the representation preserves meaningful behavioral information. If this structure is not retained, attention may focus on incidental variation rather than informative patterns. These considerations highlight the importance of introducing structure into traffic representations before applying sequence-level modeling. In particular, approaches that connect low-level packet features with higher-level, interpretable traffic behavior offer a more reliable foundation for learning robust temporal relationships

Semantic traffic phases and behavioral modeling

Empirical analyses of web traffic consistently show that page loading and interaction follow recurring temporal phases8,14. These phases typically include an initial exchange associated with connection setup and page retrieval, a subsequent period dominated by resource downloads, and later intervals characterized by user-driven interactions or idle behavior. Even when payloads are encrypted, these stages give rise to distinct traffic dynamics that can be observed through timing and packet direction.

Although many existing fingerprinting systems implicitly benefit from this structure, it is rarely encoded explicitly during representation learning. As a result, models often operate on long, unstructured sequences in which semantically different portions of traffic are treated uniformly.

In this work, traffic structure is incorporated directly through a semantic patching strategy inspired by ideas from Vision Transformers17. Instead of processing packet-level features as a single continuous stream, CNN-extracted representations are grouped into a small number of behaviorally meaningful segments. Each segment corresponds to a coarse traffic phase and is represented as a token for subsequent Transformer-based modeling. This design allows the model to reason over transitions between behavioral stages rather than relying solely on fine-grained burst patterns.

Toward robust and generalizable models

Robustness under realistic network conditions requires more than architectural changes alone. In practice, generalization is influenced by how variability and uncertainty are handled during training. To this end, several complementary strategies are incorporated into the learning process.

First, data augmentation is used to expose the model to variations in traffic structure that arise from network jitter and partial observations. Techniques such as random cropping and packet reordering have been shown to reduce sensitivity to fixed observation windows19. Second, to mitigate the effects of class imbalance and overly confident predictions, label smoothing with a smoothing factor of 0.1 is applied alongside a class-balanced loss weighted by inverse class frequencies20,21. Finally, self-supervised learning is integrated through traffic masking and augmentation, following recent findings that such objectives encourage more stable representations in encrypted traffic analysis22.

Together, these design choices aim to improve robustness across defended and non-defended settings by reducing reliance on brittle, defense-specific cues.

Despite these advances, no prior WF model combines semantic patching, hybrid CNN-Transformer design, and self-supervised pretraining within a unified framework. TPA-SSTM fills this gap by integrating local feature extraction, structured sequence modeling, and robust training into a single end-to-end pipeline.

Related work

Over the past decade, website fingerprinting (WF) research has evolved from simple statistical methods to sophisticated deep learning architectures, each aiming to infer user activity from encrypted network traffic. To contextualize our work, we review six representative and widely cited WF approaches that span different methodological paradigms: feature-based, distance-based, and deep learning-based models. These methods serve as important reference points in the literature and have been frequently used as baselines in recent evaluations. We also include a discussion of the most recent advances in robust WF modeling.

WF3A presented in23 introduces an N-shot website fingerprinting approach for Tor traffic analysis under few-shot conditions with limited training data. Unlike traditional methods that rely on single features and large datasets, WF3A employs a fusion feature combining packet direction and length, capturing richer and more distinctive traffic patterns. To enhance discriminative capability, the method integrates an Enhanced Channel Attention (ECA) mechanism within the feature extractor, improving the representation of website-specific characteristics. Experimental evaluations show that WF3A consistently surpasses state-of-the-art WF techniques in both closed-world and open-world scenarios, achieving higher accuracy and stable precision even with minimal training samples.

CUMUL in12, has been proposed by Panchenko et al., and is a feature-based method that constructs cumulative, direction-aware representations of packet sequences. By aggregating packet counts and timing information into handcrafted features, CUMUL demonstrated strong performance on early Tor datasets and remains a popular baseline due to its interpretability and efficiency. However, its reliance on manually engineered features limits its adaptability to traffic obfuscation techniques that alter timing or inject dummy packets.

k-FP in24, was introduced by Cai et al., and is a burst-template matching approach specifically designed with defenses in mind. It constructs multiple “fingerprints” per website based on observed burst patterns and matches incoming traces against them. While conceptually aligned with countering burst-oriented padding defenses, its rigid matching mechanism struggles to generalize to stochastic or non-burst-aligned traffic modifications.

k-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) is a classical distance-based classifier that has been applied to WF using raw packet sequences or basic feature vectors. Its simplicity and lack of training requirements make it a useful baseline, but its performance is highly sensitive to trace representation and vulnerable to any form of traffic perturbation, such as padding or timing noise25,26.

SDAE (Stacked Denoising Autoencoder) in27, has been proposed by Lotfollahi et al., and was among the first deep learning models applied to WF. It leverages unsupervised pretraining to learn noise-robust representations of encrypted traffic. While pioneering in its use of deep learning for WF, its relatively shallow architecture and limited capacity constrain its ability to capture complex temporal dynamics present in modern, high-resolution traces.

Deep Fingerprinting (DF)18, introduced by Sirinam et al., represents a major leap forward in WF performance. Using a convolutional neural network (CNN) trained end-to-end on raw packet direction sequences, DF achieves near-perfect classification accuracy on undefended Tor traffic. Its success stems from its ability to automatically learn discriminative burst-level patterns without manual feature engineering. However, its effectiveness under advanced defenses, particularly those that disrupt precise timing or burst structure, remains an open question and an active area of investigation.

AWF (Automated Website Fingerprinting) in15, was proposed by Rimmer et al., and it employs a CNN architecture similar to DF but was trained and evaluated on a large-scale, real-world dataset collected under realistic browsing conditions. AWF was designed to handle the variability and noise inherent in practical Tor usage, making it a strong benchmark for evaluating generalization. Nevertheless, like DF, its robustness to sophisticated, adaptive defenses has not been fully characterized.

Most recently, Pan et al. in28 introduced a defense-agnostic framework that learns robust, invariant traffic representations through contrastive learning and traffic augmentation. Their approach, designed to generalize across multiple defense types without retraining, represents a significant evolution in WF attack methodology, shifting focus from defense-specific adaptation to representation-level robustness. This work highlights the growing emphasis on generalizable, semantically meaningful feature learning in the face of increasingly sophisticated traffic obfuscation.

Collectively, these methods represent the evolution of WF from statistical modeling to data-driven deep learning, and now toward robust, defense-invariant representation learning. While each has contributed significantly to the field, most were not explicitly designed for cross-defense generalization or resilience against adaptive stochastic obfuscation, a gap that motivates the design of our proposed model, i.e. TPA-SSTM. As shown, while deep learning methods like DF and RTF have pushed the boundaries of performance and robustness, most still exhibit vulnerabilities under adaptive or stochastic defenses, motivating the need for architectures that model temporal structure and defense semantics explicitly.

The proposed temporal patch attention with self-supervised traffic masking (TPA-SSTM)

This section presents the proposed TPA-SSTM, which stands for Temporal Patch Attention with Self-Supervised Traffic Masking. TPA-SSTM is a hybrid deep learning architecture designed for website fingerprinting in encrypted network environments. It integrates a 1D convolutional backbone with a Transformer encoder to capture both local burst patterns and long-range structural dependencies in packet-level traces. The model is trained end-to-end using supervised learning, enhanced with techniques that improve robustness and generalization.

Note that raw Tor traffic traces often exceed 5000 packets; prior work has shown that limiting analysis to the first 5000 packets maximizes classification accuracy in closed-world website fingerprinting8,15. This is because the most discriminative traffic patterns, such as initial HTML retrieval, script loading, and API calls, occur early in the session. Later packets often correspond to background activity (e.g. polling, analytics, keep-alive) that adds noise without improving classification. Therefore, we truncate all traces to the first 5000 packets, following the standard practice in the field. This also enables direct comparison with baseline models like DF and Var-CNN.

Data augmentation

Training data are augmented using a set of stochastic transformations that introduce controlled variability into encrypted traffic traces. Together, these transformations form the Self-Supervised Traffic Masking (SSTM) strategy, which is integrated directly into the supervised training process. The goal is to expose the model to variations that resemble noise and perturbations observed in defended or unstable network environments.

Two augmentations are applied during training. The first operates at the temporal level. For each trace, a contiguous subsequence of length packets is selected at random and then zero-padded to restore a fixed length of 5000. This procedure reflects scenarios in which only partial traffic observations are available and introduces variability similar to delay-based defenses such as WTF-PAD14. It also reduces reliance on fixed trace boundaries.

The second augmentation is applied at the packet level. Individual packets within a trace are zeroed independently with probability . This operation approximates the effects of packet loss and transient congestion, as well as defensive strategies that intentionally disrupt packet sequences25. By removing isolated packets, the model is discouraged from depending on specific packet positions and is instead encouraged to learn more distributed representations.

Both augmentations are applied on-the-fly during training using GPU-based operations. This allows variability to be introduced without a measurable increase in training overhead.

CW

The Closed-World (CW) dataset18 is commonly used in website fingerprinting research as a baseline dataset. It provides a controlled setting that is well known in the literature. The dataset contains encrypted traffic traces collected over the Tor network from 95 monitored websites, which represent popular websites that users usually visit. In total, the dataset includes 105,730 traffic traces.

There are no traffic shaping or padding defenses applied in this dataset. All traffic is encrypted using Tor and TLS, so payload information is not accessible. Because of this, the analysis depends only on side-channel features such as packet direction, timing behavior, and trace length. For this reason, the CW dataset is mainly used to measure fingerprinting performance in encrypted traffic without additional obfuscation.

WTF-PAD

The WTF-PAD dataset builds upon the CW setting by incorporating an active traffic obfuscation mechanism. It contains traffic traces from the same 95 monitored websites, but each trace is processed using the WTF-PAD defense30. This defense introduces adaptive padding with the goal of obscuring traffic patterns while keeping latency and bandwidth overhead relatively low.

The dataset maintains the same scale as CW, with 105,730 instances, which allows for direct and fair comparisons between defended and non-defended scenarios. The introduction of adaptive padding significantly modifies local timing behavior and burst structures, making traffic classification more challenging.

As such, the WTF-PAD dataset is primarily used to assess how well a fingerprinting model performs under realistic, lightweight defense conditions. Strong performance on this dataset indicates that the model relies on features that remain informative even when traffic patterns are intentionally perturbed.

Front

The FRONT dataset, evaluated in this work, employs the FRONT defense mechanism introduced by Cherubin et al.31, a state-of-the-art, low-latency traffic analysis countermeasure designed to protect Tor users against website fingerprinting (WF) attacks. Unlike structural defenses such as Walkie-Talkie or WTF-PAD, FRONT operates by injecting dummy packets at both the client and proxy sides, scheduled according to a Rayleigh distribution to obfuscate timing patterns, particularly in the early, most discriminative portion of each trace (the “front”).

The defense is parameterized and consists of three key steps:

Sample a number of dummy packets: Two parameters, Nc (client padding budget) and Ns (proxy padding budget), control the maximum number of dummy packets each side may inject per trace. For each trace, the client samples the actual number of dummy packets nc from a discretized uniform distribution U(1,Nc), and the proxy samples ns from U(1,Ns). This stochastic budgeting ensures bounded overhead while introducing variability that complicates classifier learning.

The lower bound ensures the window is sufficiently large to avoid requiring unrealistically high bandwidth for dummy packet injection.

The dataset includes 95,000 encrypted Tor traffic traces, collected from 1,000 visits to each of 95 monitored websites. All traces are protected using the FRONT defense mechanism. Compared to deterministic padding methods such as Walkie-Talkie, FRONT adds randomness in packet timing and placement, which makes the traffic patterns less stable. This behavior increases the difficulty for website fingerprinting models, especially deep learning models that depend on accurate temporal alignment. As seen in our experimental results, the classification accuracy drops under the FRONT defense, showing that this dataset is challenging for evaluating model robustness.

Walkie-Talkie

The Walkie-Talkie dataset contains encrypted traffic traces collected under the Walkie-Talkie defense mechanism and includes 100 monitored websites with a total of 90,000 instances. Each trace is generated using the Walkie-Talkie protocol, which applies traffic shaping techniques to introduce ambiguity into packet sequences with the goal of reducing the effectiveness of website fingerprinting attacks32.

This dataset is commonly used to study the interaction between fingerprinting models and defense mechanisms that explicitly alter traffic patterns. The structured pairing of monitored websites and defended traces allows for controlled evaluation of how well classification models adapt when local burst information is deliberately obscured.

Exploratory analysis via visual fingerprints

To examine the structure of encrypted traffic traces and obtain a qualitative view of class separability, we perform an exploratory analysis based on low-dimensional visual representations of traffic data. For this purpose, packet-level traces from the WC dataset are transformed into two-dimensional density maps, referred to here as visual fingerprints. Each fingerprint corresponds to a single website and summarizes the distribution of its traffic instances in a learned embedding space.

The analysis begins by loading the preprocessed dataset from WC.npz, which provides a feature matrix and associated labels , where N denotes the number of traces and D the feature dimension. The feature representations are projected into a two-dimensional space using t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE)33. The projection is computed with two output dimensions, a perplexity of 30, and a maximum of 1000 optimization iterations. An adaptive learning rate is used, and random initialization with a fixed seed ensures reproducibility across runs.

Following dimensionality reduction, a kernel density estimate is computed separately for each website class. Density estimation is performed using Seaborn’s kdeplot function with a reduced bandwidth adjustment of 0.5 in order to capture fine-grained structure. Filled contour plots are generated with a high contour resolution and without thresholding, allowing low-density regions to remain visible. For consistency, all plots are rendered without axes or borders and saved using a tight layout, producing one image per website class.

Figure 3 presents the resulting set of visual fingerprints for the 95 websites in the WC dataset. In many cases, traffic instances associated with a single website form compact regions in the embedded space, while separation between different websites remains visible. At the same time, certain classes exhibit elongated or multi-modal distributions, which may reflect variations in page structure, dynamic content loading, or interaction patterns. Limited overlap is observed between a small number of website pairs, indicating potential sources of ambiguity during classification.

Rather than serving as a performance metric, this analysis provides contextual insight into the structure of the data and the variability present within and across website classes. These observations help explain differences in classification difficulty across websites and offer intuition for the design and evaluation of fingerprinting models under defended conditions.

Our evaluation adopts a closed-world setting, which reflects realistic state-level censorship scenarios where adversaries maintain curated blocklists of high-value targets28,31,32.

Experimental setup

We evaluated TPA-SSTM on two standard datasets: WTF-PAD (95 websites) and CW (95 websites). Both use the first 5000 packets of each trace in a closed-world setting. We report mean performance over 5 independent runs with random seeds (42, 123, 456, 789, 999) to ensure statistical reliability. We report macro F1, accuracy, precision, and recall. Our model is trained for 300 epochs using AdamW with learning rates of for the backbone and for the head. Ablation study

As shown in Table 3, our ablation study reveals a critical insight: removing core components like data augmentation and label smoothing increases closed-world F1 performance. Specifically, disabling these techniques improved the CW F1 score from 97.2% to 98.4%. This counterintuitive result confirms that these components act as strong regularizers. Their function is not to maximize accuracy on a clean, independent and identically distributed test set, but to prevent overfitting by learning smoother, more generalizable feature representations. We intentionally accept this marginal decrease in closed-world performance to achieve a model that is significantly more robust to the distribution shifts, noise, and adversarial patterns encountered in real-world VPN traffic. This confirms that our choices trade slight accuracy for robustness, which is a favorable trade-off, one can argue, in real-world settings.

Discussion

The results presented in Table 4 reveal several critical insights into the current landscape of website fingerprinting attacks and defenses, particularly regarding the relative effectiveness of modern countermeasures and the robustness of our proposed model, TPA-SSTM. It should be noted that there is no single standard evaluation protocol for website fingerprinting in the literature. Different works use different dataset splits, preprocessing steps, and sometimes data is collected under slightly different conditions. Because of this, direct comparison between methods is often not fully fair. To reduce this problem, we reimplemented all baseline methods (SDAE, DF, AWF, k-NN, CUMUL, and k-FP) using their original architectures and reported hyperparameters. All models were evaluated using the same 80%â€“20% trainâ€“test split across all datasets. This setup helps to limit external factors and makes the comparison more focused on model design and robustness, rather than data leakage or evaluation bias.

When no defense is applied, as in the CW dataset, all evaluated methods show strong performance. Deep Fingerprinting (DF) achieves an accuracy of 98.3%, which is the highest among the baseline models, while TPA-SSTM reaches 97.38%. Although the proposed model does not exceed DF in this case, this result is expected. The goal of TPA-SSTM is not to maximize performance on clean traffic only, but to remain stable when traffic conditions change due to defenses. This behavior becomes clearer when defended datasets are considered.

Under the WTF-PAD defense, the performance of most baseline methods drops noticeably. For example, SDAE decreases to 35.8% accuracy and k-NN to 15.7%. DF remains relatively strong with an accuracy of 89.9%, which is consistent with previous observations reported in18. In comparison, TPA-SSTM achieves 91.92% accuracy under the same defense. This result indicates that the proposed model can still extract useful information even when local traffic patterns are disturbed by adaptive padding.

A further decrease in performance is observed for all baseline models when the FRONT defense is applied, compared to Walkie-Talkie. For instance, DF drops from 48.9% under Walkie-Talkie to 41.2% under FRONT, while CUMUL decreases from 37.6% to 31.5%. A similar trend appears for k-FP, which was designed to handle burst-based defenses, yet still drops from 7.5% to 6.8%. This behavior suggests that stochastic padding focused on the early part of a trace introduces more uncertainty than deterministic burst shaping. By adding dummy packets based on a Rayleigh distribution and concentrating them near the beginning of the trace, FRONT disrupts temporal patterns that many learning-based models depend on.

Across all defended datasets, TPA-SSTM shows consistently higher performance than the baseline methods. On the FRONT dataset, it maintains an accuracy of 85.91%, while the other approaches remain below 45%. This large gap indicates that the proposed model is better able to cope with noise and distribution changes introduced by modern defenses. The results suggest that TPA-SSTM relies less on precise local bursts and more on broader temporal structure.

Recent work such as RTF28 has shown that contrastive representation learning can improve generalization across defenses. The approach used in TPA-SSTM follows a different direction by explicitly modeling temporal structure and state transitions in defended traffic. Instead of depending mainly on synthetic data augmentation, the model learns how perturbations affect traffic behavior. This allows TPA-SSTM to keep high accuracy, even under the stochastic padding strategy used by the FRONT defense.

Conclusion

This study examines the problem of identifying website access patterns within encrypted tunnels, such as VPN connections, where payload inspection is not feasible. Although website fingerprinting techniques can infer visited websites from traffic metadata, many existing deep learning approaches, particularly those based on convolutional neural networks, show limited robustness against traffic obfuscation defenses. Techniques such as WTF-PAD, Walkie-Talkie, and Front intentionally alter local traffic burst characteristics, which are commonly used as the primary discriminative features in prior models.

To mitigate this limitation, this work introduces Temporal Patch Attention with Self-Supervised Traffic Masking (TPA-SSTM), a hybrid architecture that combines convolutional feature extraction with Transformer-based sequence modeling. The proposed framework employs a semantic patching mechanism that divides encrypted traffic traces into coarse temporal phases, including initial page loading, resource retrieval, and idle or interaction periods. A Transformer encoder is then used to capture long-range dependencies across these phases, enabling the model to learn global behavioral patterns that remain informative under obfuscation. In addition, self-supervised traffic masking during training, together with data augmentation strategies, improves the model’s ability to generalize across defended and non-defended scenarios.

Extensive experiments were conducted on four evaluation settings, including a non-defended Closed World dataset and three defended environments using WTF-PAD, Walkie-Talkie, and Front. The proposed approach achieves 97.4% accuracy in the Closed World setting, 91.9% accuracy under WTF-PAD, and macro F1-scores of 95.25% and 85.91% under Walkie-Talkie and Front, respectively. These findings demonstrate that temporally structured, phase-aware modeling provides substantial gains in robustness when analyzing encrypted traffic protected by obfuscation mechanisms.

The evaluation in this work is performed under a closed-world assumption, where the set of monitored websites is known in advance and remains fixed. This assumption reflects practical censorship and traffic regulation scenarios, in which network operators aim to detect access to a predefined set of restricted domains rather than identify arbitrary or previously unseen websites. Within this context, the proposed framework supports targeted detection of censored content over encrypted communication channels.

Future work will focus on extending the proposed method to open-world settings, where unknown or adversarial websites must be distinguished from known classes. Additional directions include investigating model compression and optimization techniques to support real-time deployment in high-throughput networks, as well as evaluating the robustness of the approach across diverse network environments and geographic regions.
