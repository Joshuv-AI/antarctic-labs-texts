Novel multi-metal ion-exchanged zeolite for oxidative-adsorptive desulfurization of model and real diesel fuel: a response surface methodology (RSM) study
Authors: Jafar Shafaghat, Salman Movahedirad, Mohammad Amin Sobati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The efficient removal of refractory organosulfur compounds, such as dibenzothiophene, remains a critical bottleneck in achieving ultra-low sulfur diesel standards. While oxidative-adsorptive desulfurization is promising, there is a significant research gap in rationally designing adsorbents that utilize synergistic multi-metal active sites to enhance the capture of sterically hindered species. To address this, we synthesized a novel multi-metal ion-exchanged zeolite (AgNiCeY) via a sequential ion-exchange process, aiming to strategically incorporate Lewis acid sites (Ag+, Ni2+, Ce3+) within the NaY framework. Using response surface methodology (RSM) on a model fuel system (DBT in n-octane)), the optimal capacity was determined at a contact time of 55 min and an oil/adsorbent ratio of 18.2 ml/g. The resulting AgNiCeY adsorbent demonstrated a remarkable increase in equilibrium adsorption capacity from 12.61 mg/g (NaY) to 33.26 mg/g, marking a 164% enhancement. Mechanistic analysis, supported by FT-IR and electronic structure visualization, confirms that the synergy between the exchanged cations acts as Lewis acids, facilitating superior σ\sigmaσ-bond formation with the sulfur atom. Crucially, when tested under realistic conditions using real diesel fuel containing 2544 ppm sulfur, the modified AgNiCeY maintained high efficiency, achieving a 61.3% sulfur removal, significantly outperforming the parent NaY (57.8%). This study validates a novel design principle for heterogeneous catalysts, demonstrating that precise, sequential multi-metal exchange in zeolites offers a robust and scalable strategy for improving the selective adsorption of refractory sulfur compounds. Regeneration experiments were also conducted to assess the recyclability of the catalysts over multiple cycles.

Introduction

The presence of sulfur compounds in fossil fuels poses significant environmental and industrial challenges. Combustion of these fuels releases sulfur oxides (SOx), contributing to air pollution, acid rain, and respiratory ailments1. Refractory species like dibenzothiophene (DBT) present a major hurdle as regulations demand sulfur content below 10 mg/ L2–4.

Hydrodesulfurization (HDS) is the established method, involving catalytic hydrogenation5,6. However, HDS is limited in deep desulfurization of refractory compounds due to its requirement for severe conditions (high temperature/pressure) and substantial hydrogen consumption, increasing operational costs7,8.

To overcome these HDS limitations, alternative methods like ADS, EDS, BDS, ODS, OEDS, and OADS have been developed6,9,10.

ODS advancements include ultrasound-assisted ultrasound-assisted ODS11, mixing-assisted ODS12 and specialized POM catalysts13 or sulfone adsorbents14, showing continuous innovation15,16.

OADS is favored for refractory removal without hydrogen, under milder conditions3,17–20, by oxidizing sulfur to polar molecules for adsorption21.

Zeolites are key OADS adsorbents22,23 due to their porous structure, stability, and surface tunability via ion-exchange23, providing ideal channels for selective adsorption.

Recent research has increasingly focused on functionalizing zeolites with transition metals or metal oxides to improve their performance in desulfurization processes. This functionalization, achieved through ion-exchange or deposition methods, increases the number and dispersion of active sites, which play a crucial role in the oxidation and adsorption of sulfur compounds9,24. Specifically, incorporating multiple metal ions (e.g., Ag, Ni, Ce) into the zeolite framework can create synergistic effects, significantly enhancing the adsorbent’s activity and selectivity in the OADS process6,18,20. While V(V) removal often relies on electrostatic surface adsorption onto metal oxides, this work focuses on the deep removal of neutral, sterically hindered organosulfur compounds via electronic structure modification within a porous zeolite framework, representing a distinct chemical challenge.

Numerous researchers have employed oxidation and adsorption methods for desulfurization3,18,20,24,25. For oxidation, various oxidants such as formic acid, often in the presence of a strong acid like sulfuric acid to adjust the pH have been used alongside catalysts25. Similarly, various zeolites, including Y zeolite, have been extensively studied for adsorptive desulfurization26–31.

Despite significant advancements, notable research gaps persist in optimizing desulfurization processes using novel adsorbents. Specifically, few studies have comprehensively investigated and optimized the OADS process using multi-metal ion-exchanged zeolites in conjunction with advanced statistical methods such as Design of Experiments (DOE) and Response Surface Methodology (RSM). This study aims to address this gap by investigating and optimizing the oxidative-adsorptive desulfurization of model fuel using zeolite Y ion-exchanged with silver (Ag), nickel (Ni), and cerium (Ce) ions (AgNiCeY). The primary novelties of this research are as follows:

Utilization of a multi-metal ion-exchanged zeolite adsorbent (Ag-Ni-Ce): To leverage potential synergistic effects among these metals for enhancing the adsorption and oxidation of sulfur compounds.

Application of Response Surface Methodology (RSM): To identify optimal process conditions and understand the interplay of key parameters on desulfurization efficiency.

Comprehensive investigation of OADS performance: With a specific focus on removing refractory sulfur compounds like DBT from model fuel.

The findings of this study are expected to contribute significantly to the advancement of knowledge and the development of more efficient solutions for deep fuel desulfurization.

In this extended part of the research, the optimized oxidative–adsorptive desulfurization (OADS) parameters obtained from the model fuel system were applied to real diesel samples to assess the practical efficiency of NaY and AgNiCeY zeolites. The procedure was modified to directly test the zeolites under real fuel conditions while maintaining similar oxidation and adsorption sequences. This approach enables direct comparison between model and real fuel performance and validates the industrial feasibility of the proposed adsorbents.

Materials and methods

Materials

NaY zeolite prepared from Hutong global company and other materials were supplied from Merck Company. The materials required for each section are as follows:

Preparation of model fuel: n-octane) 99.9% as model fuel solvent and DBT served as the model sulfur compound with several initial concentration: 150, 500, 850, 1200, 1550 ppm S.

The selection of n-octane) was an intentional scientific choice designed to facilitate the precise evaluation of the intrinsic, fundamental adsorption performance of the synthesized zeolite. As a simple non-polar alkane, n-octane) serves as a non-polar proxy that minimizes the competitive adsorption effects expected from the complex mixture of polar aromatics and other heteroatom-containing species found in real diesel fuel. By isolating the intrinsic desulfurization capacity in this controlled system, a necessary baseline performance benchmark was established before the zeolite’s efficacy is subsequently assessed under realistic conditions.

Oxidation-extraction section: sulfuric acid 98% was used to adjust pH in the desired range. formic acid 99% and hydrogen peroxide (30% aqueous solution) were implemented as oxidants.

Adsorption section: NaY zeolite was used as adsorbent. Ag(NO3), Ni(NO3)2·6H2O and Ce(NO3)3.6H2O were used for ion exchanging with zeolite.

Zeolite synthesis

The synthesise of NiAgCeY caried out in 2 steps:

Step 1: Removal of possible impurities from primary NaY zeolite

According to the method of Zhang et al., to remove impurities from the NaY zeolite structure, the zeolite was first washed with deionized water. The washed NaY was then washed with a 3 mol/L NaCl aqueous solution at 60 °C. This process was repeated six times. Subsequently, the zeolite was washed with deionized water and dried in an air oven at 100 °C. The product obtained from this process is purified NaY zeolite30.

Step 2: Ion exchanging step with purified NaY zeolite

Different metal salts were used for the ion exchange with purified NaY zeolite. The ion-exchanged Y zeolites used in this research were prepared using the liquid-phase ion-exchange method at 70 °C for 7 h.

In the synthesis of bimetallic ion-exchanged zeolites, researchers have adopted two distinct approaches regarding the order of metal exchange, each dependent on the preferential exchange affinity of one specific metal over the other. One approach is to use both metal salts simultaneously; that is, all ions are exchanged in one step30,32. he second method is to first perform an ion exchange with one salt and then treat the ion-exchanged zeolite with the second salt, exchanging one ion in each step33–35.

In this research, the simultaneous ion exchange method was not used. Instead, ions were exchanged one at a time to synthesize the multi-metal ion-exchanged zeolite (MMIEZ). Furthermore, considering that Wang et al. first exchanged nickel and then cerium35, Song et al. first exchanged silver and then cerium34, another study by Song et al. first exchanged cerium and then copper33 and Zhang et al. exchanged copper and zinc simultaneously, the ion exchange sequence for this study was determined to be Ag, then Ni, and then Ce. An aqueous solution of nitrate salts of Ag (0.1 mol/L), Ni (0.25 mol/L), and Ce (0.5 mol/L) was used for the ion exchange.

The stages of AgNiCeY zeolite synthesis are shown schematically in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1Schematic stages of AgNiCeY zeolite synthesise.

Schematic stages of AgNiCeY zeolite synthesise.

As is clear from Fig. 1, for the synthesis of AgNiCeY zeolite, 5 g of NaY zeolite was first added to a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 0.1 M Ag(NO₃)₃ aqueous solution and mixed for 7 h at 70 °C. After ion exchange, the AgY zeolite was separated using filter paper, washed with deionized water, and dried in an oven at 100 °C for 12 h.

In the following step, Ni2+ ion loading onto the AgY zeolite was carried out in the same manner. Specifically, 5 g of AgY zeolite was placed in contact with 250 mL of a 0.2 M Ni(NO₃)₂·6 H₂O aqueous solution for 7 h at 70 °C. After filtering, washing, and drying, AgNiY zeolite was obtained.

In the final step, to load Ce3+ ions, 5 g of AgNiY zeolite was placed in contact with 250 mL of a 0.5 M Ce(NO₃)3·6 H₂O aqueous solution. After this final exchange, the product was filtered, washed, dried, and calcined to form the AgNiCeY zeolite.

The specific sequence of ion exchange (Ag+ → Ni2+ → Ce3+) was strategically designed to maximize the total loading of active metal sites and minimize phenomena such as competitive exchange and site-blocking, which are common issues in multi-metal exchange processes on the NaY framework. Cation exchange in zeolites is generally governed by the charge density, hydration energy, and ionic radius of the exchanging species.

Initial Exchange (Ag+): The monovalent Ag+ ion (radius 1.08 Å) was introduced first. Ag+ is known to exchange readily for Na+ and plays a critical role in the π\piπ-complexation mechanism due to its 4d10 electronic configuration. This sequence leverages the differential access of the ions based on their size to optimize site utilization.Final Exchange (Ce3+): Ce3+ was deliberately exchanged last. Trivalent ions possess the highest affinity for the NaY framework. Due to its high charge density, Ce3+ (radius 1.15 Å) can coordinate with multiple framework oxygens, potentially leading to significant structural changes or premature blocking of several adjacent exchange sites if introduced earlier in the process. By performing the Ce3+ exchange last, we utilize its strong driving force to replace the remaining Na+ ions, ensuring maximum overall exchange while preventing it from dominating and blocking the large active sites crucial for Ag+ and Ni2+ functionality.

Experimental methods

Oxidation process

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and formic acid were used to oxidize dibenzothiophene. The following oxidation parameters, based on a literature review25:

In this research, an Erlenmeyer flask was used as a reactor. This reactor was equipped with a magnetic stirrer and a thermometer inserted into the solution through a cork placed over the flask’s opening.

The procedure was as follows: First, fuel with an initial concentration of 150, 500, 850, 1200, or 1550 mg/L was poured into the reactor and heated to 60 °C. The solution was then stirred for 5 min at 750 rpm. Next, the appropriate amount of formic acid (with an A/S ratio of 30) was introduced into the reactor. Simultaneously, the corresponding amount of 30% hydrogen peroxide (with an O/S ratio of 10) was immediately added to the reaction mixture. The reaction commenced upon the addition of hydrogen peroxide. The mixture was stirred at 750 rpm with a magnetic stirrer for the duration of the oxidation reaction (60 min).

After the reaction, the temperature of the oxidized fuel was rapidly reduced to 25 °C using a cold water flow around the flask. This temperature is optimal for the subsequent adsorption process. Once cooled, the adsorbent was added to the oxidized fuel at the appropriate weight as specified by the experimental design.

Adsorption process

Immediately after the oxidation was completed and the fuel temperature was lowered to 25 °C, zeolite of the desired weight was added to the fuel. A schematic of the oxidation and subsequent adsorption process is shown in Fig. 2. As shown in Fig. 2 The adsorption process was then carried out at room temperature in an Erlenmeyer flask equipped with a magnetic stirrer operating at 500 rpm. The desulfurized fuel was separated from the adsorbent after a specific period using filter paper (No. 40). The concentration of DBT in the model fuel was analyzed with a UV–Vis spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 296 nm.

Schematic of oxidation and adsorption process.

For real diesel fuel, total sulfur content was measured using X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectroscopy to assess overall desulfurization. In contrast, for the well-defined model fuel system (DBT in n-octane), UV–Vis spectrophotometry was employed to directly monitor DBT concentration based on the Beer–Lambert law, with its characteristic absorption at 296 nm. To eliminate interference from the solvent, oxidant (H₂O₂), catalyst, and formic acid, a differential two-cell method was used, subtracting the spectrum of a DBT-free reference mixture to isolate the net DBT signal. The method was validated with a linear calibration curve (R² = 0.9989) and demonstrated LOD and LOQ values of 1.5 ppm and 5.0 ppm, respectively. This approach ensures accurate DBT quantification despite the increasing polarity of sulfone products, aligning with established analytical principles and prior studies using UV–Vis in catalytic and oxidative-adsorptive DBT removal contexts36–40.

Experimental procedure for OADS of real diesel fuel

The experimental procedure was specifically tailored for OADS of real diesel fuel. The oxidation stage commenced by transferring 20 mL of real diesel fuel. The fuel was heated to 60 °C before oxidation. The required amounts of oxidants were then added: Formic Acid to achieve an A/S = 30, and H2O2 (30%) to achieve an O/S ratio of 10. This addition initiated the reaction for a total reaction time of 60 min. Following the oxidation, the temperature of the mixture was rapidly reduced to 25 °C. Immediately after cooling, the NaY or AgNiCeY adsorbent was introduced into the system. The adsorbent dosage was selected to establish an Oil/Ads. ratio of 18.2. The adsorption process then proceeded 55 min to reach equilibrium. After the specified time, the desulfurized fuel product was separated from the solid adsorbent with filter paper. The final determination of the remaining sulfur content in the treated diesel fuel was conducted using a XRF-Sulfur Analyzer model SLFA-60 manufactured by HORIBA, Japan.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to determine whether bonds formed between the adsorbent and the sulfur compounds. For this purpose, the FTIR spectrum of each adsorbent (NaY and AgNiCeY) was studied before and after the adsorption process. The spectra of NaY and AgNiCeY zeolite before and after adsorption are shown in Fig. 4.

Fig. 4FT-IR spectra for NaY (a) and AgNiCeY (b) before and after adsorption.

FT-IR spectra for NaY (a) and AgNiCeY (b) before and after adsorption.

The central focus of this FTIR spectroscopic investigation is monitoring frequency shifts in key regions following the adsorption of DBTO2​ onto both NaY and AgNiCeY zeolites, as well as comparing the effect of ion exchange (from Na+ to active metal cations) on these interactions.

Observations in the region of Zeolite Framework Vibrations around 1050–1075 cm− 1 indicate the structural stability of the silicate/aluminate framework. In the parent NaY zeolite, the dominant peak in this range is attributed to the symmetric stretching vibration of Si–O–T (T = Al or O). After adsorption, this peak (or its center) experiences only a negligible shift, suggesting minimal disruption to the primary framework bonds by the adsorbed molecule. Conversely, in the AgNiCeY zeolite, changes in this region, although subtle, may reflect the influence of the presence of trivalent metal ions or long-range interactions with the adsorbed molecule, affecting the stiffness of the framework bonds. This relative framework stability suggests that adsorption occurs primarily in the vicinity of the metal ions and acidic sites (and not within the main structure).

Regarding the Sulfone functional group, the asymmetric S=O stretching vibration serves as a strong probe. In the parent NaY zeolite, this vibration peak is observed at 1320 cm− 1. Following DBTO2​ adsorption, this peak shifts to 1325 cm− 1. This positive shift (increase in frequency) indicates an environmental effect (possibly weak electrostatic forces or lack of direct involvement of the sulfonyl oxygen with the Na+ ion), leading to a slight increase in the S=O bond order. Conversely, in the modified AgNiCeY zeolite, this peak also shifts from its base position (which should be at 1320 cm− 1 pre-adsorption) to 1325 cm− 1. This stability in the post-adsorption shift frequency (relative to the base position) in both systems is considered a favorable finding, indicating that although adsorption occurred, direct interaction between the sulfone oxygen and strong metal cations sufficient to cause significant S=O bond weakening (associated with a decrease in frequency) did not take place. The dominant interaction is likely through the aromatic or hydrogen bonding rather than the sulfone oxygen.

It is known that when DBT and DBTO2 adsorb onto zeolite, a peak appears in the 1400 to 1490 cm− 1 range17,34,42. In this region, a significant difference is observed between the two systems post-adsorption, highlighting the distinct role of the metal ions. In the NaY zeolite, only a single peak appears in this range (at 1415 cm− 1) after adsorption, which can be attributed to weak electrostatic interaction between the Na+ ion and the aromatic electrons, or vibrations from residual groups. However, the appearance of three distinct peaks in this frequency range in the AgNiCeY zeolite provides conclusive evidence for the diversity of adsorption mechanisms. These three peaks may indicate: the first peak (at a lower frequency), a strong π-complex interaction with Ce4+ and Ni2+ sites; the second peak (intermediate), weaker interactions with Ag+ sites or other metal centers; and the third peak (at a higher frequency), possibly related to electronically excited interactions induced by the presence of trivalent ions. This multiplicity of peaks in the trivalent metal system strongly confirms the high efficiency of this structure for stabilizing larger molecules through varied Lewis acid interactions.

Finally, the presence of molecular peaks at 3070 cm− 1 (C–H aromatic) and 1600 cm− 1 (C=C aromatic) in both samples after adsorption proves the successful presence of DBTO2 ​ without degradation of its core structure. The success in observing these peaks in both zeolites, whether anionic (NaY) or containing active metal sites (AgNiCeY), confirms the adsorption capability of both supports for this specific molecule, although the diversity and intensity of interactions are clearly transformed by the cation modification.

Furthermore, the existence of a strong, broad peak in the 3200 to 3550 cm− 1 region in the zeolites is due to the adsorbed moisture within the zeolites.

Microscopic analysis of the adsorption process

Adsorption mechanisms

The adsorption mechanisms of sulfur-containing compounds can generally be divided into two categories: chemical mechanisms and physical mechanisms.

One chemical mechanism is adsorption through covalent bond formation, which involves three types of bonds. The first is a sigma (σ) bond, where orbitals overlap with a single lobe. Orbitals from the s, p, d, and f sublayers can form this bond; the sulfur–metal (S–M) bond falls into this category. The second is a pi (π) bond, which involves the sharing of π orbitals by both atoms in the bond (π-complexation). In π-complexation, two lobes overlap; consequently, s orbitals cannot form this bond. The third type is a dative or coordination bond. In a coordination bond, one atom (a Lewis acid) has an empty orbital and accepts a pair of electrons, while the other atom has a non-bonding electron pair and donates it. In the classification of adsorption mechanisms, some articles refer to π-complexation35,43–48, sigma bond mechanism49 as well as the Lewis’s acid and base mechanism43. These are all part of the broader mechanism of covalent bond formation between the adsorbent and dibenzothiophene (DBT).

The second type of chemical adsorption mechanism on zeolites is reactive adsorption. Although chemical reactions also occur in the previous mechanism, reactive adsorption is considered a separate category because new materials are produced.

Physical mechanisms are also divided into two categories: sieve adsorption and polar adsorption. Sieve adsorption is based on the kinetic diameter of sulfur-containing compounds and the size of the zeolite pores. It enables the removal of smaller sulfur-containing compounds that can enter the zeolite windows. In polar adsorption, an intermolecular force is created between the polar molecules of sulfur-containing compounds and the metal cations in the zeolite, which facilitates desulfurization.

Final quadratic models for the NaY and AgNiCeY adsorption capacity (Eqs. 3, 4) is built upon an integrated approach, by which statistical significance (P values) is combined with a deep understanding of the underlying physical and chemical phenomena. The retention and exclusion of terms were not solely based on statistical thresholds; rather, they were validated by principles governing the adsorption process. Terms exceeding a P value of 0.1 were excluded because significant statistical impact within the experimental domain was lacked by them, by which their contribution is rendered negligible compared to inherent noise.

The main factors, namely time (A) and initial concentration (B), along with their quadratic terms (A², B²), were retained in the model. A fundamental role in the kinetics of the adsorption process is played by Time (A), by which the rate at which the system approaches equilibrium is represented. From a kinetic perspective, the duration of interaction between the adsorbent and adsorbate is dictated by time. Its substantial influence on the adsorption process is confirmed by the low P value for this variable (< 0.0001). The driving force for mass transfer is acted as by Initial concentration (B). A steeper concentration gradient is established by a higher adsorbate concentration in the solution phase, by which the transfer of adsorbate molecules to the adsorbent surface is accelerated. As the concentration increases, the probability of collisions between molecules and the adsorbent surface, and consequently, their adsorption, is enhanced. This effect is also supported by a very low P value (< 0.0001). The quadratic terms (A², B²) were retained to account for non-linear effects, as a simple linear relationship may be deviated from by the system’s behavior at very high concentrations and times.

The retention of the interaction terms AC (time × initial concentration) and BC (initial concentration × adsorbent weight) is justified by their statistical significance (P = 0.0032 and P = 0.0320, respectively). From a physicochemical perspective, it is indicated by term AC that the effect of time on the extent of adsorption is dependent on the initial concentration. At high concentrations, the adsorption rate may initially be high but is decreased as the adsorbent surface becomes saturated. At low concentrations, the adsorption rate may be lower but is increased gradually and steadily. It is shown by Term BC that the overall adsorption performance is a function of the interaction between the initial concentration and the amount of adsorbent. It is implied by this that a greater impact is had by increasing the adsorbent weight at higher concentrations because a larger number of active adsorption sites are required to adsorb the adsorbate molecules, and this capacity is provided by increasing the adsorbent weight.

Conversely, the linear effect of adsorbent weight (C: adsorbent weight) was removed due to its high P value (P = 0.5421). By normalizing the data based on the unit weight of the adsorbent, the direct and linear effect of adsorbent weight on adsorption capacity is eliminated, by which the interaction effects with other variables are highlighted (especially the initial concentration in term BC). In other words, the primary influence of the adsorbent quantity is removed by normalization, by which only effects related to the intrinsic properties of the adsorbent are left. Similarly, the interaction term AB (time × initial concentration) was removed (P = 0.3465). From a physicochemical standpoint, it is implied by this that the adsorption process is influenced independently by time and initial concentration, and their interactive effect is negligible compared to the main effects. This could be stemmed from the fact that the rate and extent of adsorption are affected by both variables in different and independent ways.

In conclusion, the final model is not only statistically accurate and reliable but also consistent with our understanding of the physical and chemical phenomena by which the adsorption process is governed, by which a strong and defensible justification for variable selection is provided. The effects of different variables can be accurately predicted by this model, while a solid basis for the interpretation and understanding of the adsorption process is provided.

Validation of models

After obtaining the mathematical model, it must be validated. This is accomplished in two ways: analysis of variance (ANOVA) and residual analysis, which are discussed below.

ANOVA

The equilibrium adsorption capacity models were validated through Analysis of Variance (ANOVA), with the full results summarized in Table 9. The models derived for both NaY (F = 76.30) and AgNiCeY (F = 96.27) demonstrate high statistical significance (P < 0.0001). Residuals

Residual analysis was conducted to verify model assumptions. Figure 8 (Normal Probability vs. Externally Studentized Residuals) confirms that the errors for both NaY and AgNiCeY are normally distributed, as the residuals closely follow a straight line. The analysis of residuals versus the predicted capacity (Fig. 9) confirms that the assumptions of error independence and constant variance were not violated.

Influence diagnostics, employing Cook’s Distance (Fig. 10), Leverage Plots (Fig. 11), and DFFITS plots (Fig. 12), were utilized to check for influential data points (following the methodology outlined in51). The Cook’s Distance analysis showed that all observations were distributed between 0 and 1, indicating no single point exerted a detrimental effect on the overall fitted capacity. The DFFITS plot identified two influential points for NaY and three for AgNiCeY; however, their limited influence did not necessitate model exclusion.

Investigation of variables

Importance of variables

A perturbation plot for NaY and AgNiCeY zeolite was used to evaluate the relative importance of each of the three factors, as shown in Fig. 13. According to this Fig., adsorption time is the least important factor, while the Oil/Ads. (C) has the greatest effect on the adsorption capacity for both NaY and AgNiCeY. The sulfur content of the model fuel (i.e., DBT in n-octane)) is less influential than the Oil/Ads. but still has a significant effect on adsorption capacity.

Fig. 13Perturbation plot of NaY and AgNiCeY zeolite for investigation of variables importance.

Perturbation plot of NaY and AgNiCeY zeolite for investigation of variables importance.

The paramount importance of Oil/Ads. is a direct consequence of the definition of the response variable, Q and the underlying thermodynamic constraints of the process:

Normalization Effect and Stoichiometry. Since Q is fundamentally normalized by the mass of the adsorbent (g Ads.), any change in C directly influences the denominator of the response equation. In a system operating under conditions where the active sites are not fully saturated across all experimental runs, the mass of the adsorbent dictates the total potential adsorption capacity available.

Model Structure Confirmation. The statistical modeling reinforces this physical observation. Referring to the fitted quadratic model for AgNiCeY (Eq. 4), the coefficient associated with the squared term of the Oil/Ads. (3.30C2) is the largest among all squared terms and positive, signifying a strong quadratic contribution and high sensitivity (high slope) of the predicted capacity to changes in C across the tested range. This indicates that C is a key parameter driving the curvature of the response surface.

Contrast with Kinetic/Concentration Factors: The relative insignificance of time (A) suggests that the experiments were conducted long enough to approach or reach pseudo-equilibrium conditions, diminishing kinetic limitations. While Initial Concentration (B) drives the chemical potential gradient, the mass availability (C) ultimately dictates how much can be adsorbed per unit mass, making it the overriding factor within the defined Design Space.

Interactions of variables

Given that Oil/Ads. and initial DBT concentration are the most important parameters, and adsorption time has almost no effect on adsorption capacity, the interaction between Oil/Ads. and initial DBT concentration was investigated using the response surface method; the results are shown in Fig. 14. According to this Fig., adsorption capacity increases synergistically with decreasing Oil/Ads. and increasing initial DBT concentration. It is also clear that at higher concentrations, the effect of Oil/Ads. is greater, while at lower Oil/Ads., increasing the initial concentration has a more pronounced effect on the adsorption capacity for both NaY and AgNiCeY adsorbents.

Correlation coefficient analysis (R2) indicated that the Langmuir model provided a better fit to the experimental data for both adsorbents (NaY and AgNiCeY, with R2 values of 0.9943 and 0.9968, respectively). This excellent fit suggests that DBT adsorption primarily occurs through a homogeneous monolayer adsorption mechanism on the active sites of the adsorbent.

The most significant finding is the remarkable increase in the theoretical maximum adsorption capacity (Qmax) in the AgNiCeY adsorbent (equivalent to 58.49 mg/g) compared to the initial NaY adsorbent (equivalent to 21.36 mg/g). This increase in capacity clearly validates the effectiveness of the multi-metallic ion exchange (MMIEZ) process. This improvement in capacity is attributed to two main factors, derived from structural data:

The presence of Ag+, Ni2+, and Ce3+ cations, which possess vacant orbitals (as shown in the orbital analysis in Fig. 6), strengthens the S-M bond (sigma bond between sulfur and metal) and, according to the covalent adsorption mechanism, creates a greater affinity for Lewis base molecules like DBT35,43–48. As mentioned in the mechanisms section, the cations activate the polar adsorbent for sulfur-containing molecules (polar adsorption), and the replacement of Na+ with heavier metals induces a stronger electrostatic interaction.

Desulfurization results of real diesel fuel

In the process of desulfurization of real fuels such as diesel, developing adsorbents that can sustain high performance under practical conditions is of critical importance. The sulfur removal efficiency of NaY and AgNiCeY is shown in Fig. 15. According to this Fig., the AgNiCeY sample achieved a sulfur removal efficiency of approximately 61%, compared to about 57% for NaY, demonstrating a distinct enhancement in adsorption performance under real fuel conditions.

Fig. 15Sulfur removal from diesel using two zeolitic adsorbents, NaY and AgNiCeY.

Sulfur removal from diesel using two zeolitic adsorbents, NaY and AgNiCeY.

This improvement can be attributed to the synergistic interaction between the incorporated silver (Ag+), nickel (Ni2+), and cerium (Ce3+) ions. Silver ions, known for their “soft” character, exhibit strong affinity toward sulfur atoms in aromatic sulfur compounds, thereby increasing sulfur adsorption capacity. Nickel ions possess high coordination ability with sulfur-containing molecules, further stabilizing adsorbed species within the zeolite framework. Meanwhile, cerium ions, with their multivalence and electron-donating properties, enhance surface redox functionality and facilitate stronger adsorptive interactions with sulfur species.

The simultaneous presence of these metal ions in the Y-zeolite lattice generates multiple active centers with tunable polarity and stronger interaction strength toward sulfur compounds. As a result, the AgNiCeY zeolite displays superior desulfurization performance not only under laboratory conditions but also in real diesel systems, indicating its effective ion-exchange functionality. This key enhancement highlights the pivotal role of multi-metal ion-exchange in improving both the selective adsorption and the practical applicability of zeolitic materials for deep fuel desulfurization.

This improvement in regeneration correlates with the increased number of exchangeable ions. The underlying reasons for this enhanced performance are rooted in the structural and thermodynamic differences stemming from the augmented ion count. In the AgNiCeY system, the simultaneous presence of transition metals (Ag, Ni, Ce) facilitates electronic decoupling, which ensures the stability of active sites against physical and chemical damage during repeated cycles. From a thermodynamic perspective, the increase in ion count leads to enhanced disorder and entropy. Conclusions

This research successfully validated a new design principle for advanced desulfurization materials by developing the novel Multi-Metal Ion-Exchanged AgNiCeY Zeolite (MMIEZ) for oxidative-adsorptive desulfurization (OADS) of refractory sulfur compounds. The MMIEZ synthesis strategy, which involves the sequential introduction of specifically chosen cations (Ag+, Ni2+ and Ce3+) to tailor synergistic active sites, has proven significantly more effective than traditional single-metal loading approaches.
