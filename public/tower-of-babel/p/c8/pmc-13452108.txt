Single-cell analysis reveals an endothelial TP53-CXCL14 axis in breast cancer progression
Authors: Wen Chen, Xinliang Liu, Simeng Li, Zhigang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe TME of BRCA plays a core role in disease progression and treatment resistance. As a pleiotropic chemokine, CXCL14 exhibits context-dependent roles in cancer. While its potential prognostic value in BRCA has been noted, its precise role and regulatory mechanisms remain elusive.MethodThe expression of CXCL14, its prognostic significance, and its association with the immune microenvironment were systematically analyzed using public databases, such as TCGA, GEO, and cBioPortal. In vitro experiments were performed to generate HUVEC cells with stable TP53 silencing or overexpression. ChIP-qPCR and dual-luciferase reporter assays were used to assess TP53’s effect on CXCL14 transcriptional regulation. In vivo tumor growth was evaluated in a xenograft mouse model co-injected with HUVECs and MDA-231 cells.ResultsHigh expression of CXCL14 was significantly associated with rounding down for 4 and rounding up for 5 indicators, such as favorable OS (HR = 0.64, P = 0.006), and PFS (HR = 0.65, P = 0.002) in BRCA patients, and indicated a better response to immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment. Its expression is associated with a remodeled immune microenvironment, characterized by an increase in the infiltration of anti-tumor immune cells (e.g., CD8⁺T cells) and a decrease in immunosuppressive cells (e.g., M2 macrophages). ScRNA-seq analysis identified ECs as the main cellular source of CXCL14 in the TME for the first time. Mechanistic analyses indicated that mutant TP53 binds the CXCL14 promoter, supporting transcriptional repression. Functional experiments confirmed that the secretion of CXCL14 by TP53-mutated ECs decreased, thereby promoting the migration and invasion of BRCA cells. In vivo, co-injection of TP53-mutant (R175H) HUVECs with BRCA cells resulted in significantly larger and heavier tumors, accompanied by increased Ki-67 staining and decreased CXCL14 expression, compared to the wild-type TP53 group.ConclusionOur findings indicate that CXCL14 serves as a promising independent prognostic factor associated with an anti-tumor immune microenvironment in BRCA, with ECs identified as a major source. Introduction

Breast cancer is a serious health challenge worldwide, and its occurrence, development, and treatment response are deeply regulated by the tumor microenvironment (TME) [1]. In the TME, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), as important matrix components, participate in multiple key processes, including tumorigenesis, metastasis, and immune regulation through dynamic cellular interactions and cytokine secretion [2]. Recent studies have shown that CAFs exhibit prominent functional heterogeneity, with both pro-tumor and anti-tumor populations [3]. In addition to participating in angiogenesis, ECs are increasingly acknowledged as critical regulators of TME immune homeostasis, and extracellular matrix remodeling [4].

Chemokines play a core role in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment [5]. Among them, CXCL14 (also known as BRAK), as a pleiotropic chemokine, participates in processes such as immune cell chemotaxis and matrix activation [5]. Previous studies have shown that CXCL14 may play completely opposite roles in different tumors: it may act as a tumor suppressor in some contexts, while in others it may promote tumor development [6]. In BRCA, although there is some evidence suggesting that high CXCL14 expression is associated with a good prognosis, its exact cellular origin, functional mechanism, and upstream regulatory pathways still need to be systematically clarified.

TP53, the most important tumor suppressor gene, has a mutation rate of 30–50% in BRCA. In triple-negative BRCA cases, the mutation rate can exceed 80% [7]. TP53 mutation leads to the loss of p53 protein function and may acquire new oncogenic functions (gain-of-function), promoting tumor progression and treatment resistance through multiple mechanisms [8]. For example, the mutant TP53 may reprogram the TME [9], alter metabolic interactions or adaptive signaling pathways, thereby promoting tumor development and resistance to treatment. This reprogramming has a significant impact on the functions of key cell components in the TME. In addition, TP53 mutations can affect tumor angiogenesis and immune cell infiltration by regulating cell interactions in the TME [10, 11]. Recent studies have identified an association between TP53 status and CXCL14 and have constructed the “TP53-CXCL14” axis to explain the effects of TP53 and CXCL14, as well as their prognostic significance [12]. However, in the context of BRCA, whether mutant TP53 directly regulates CXCL14 and its downstream signaling mechanisms remains unclear.

This study aims to comprehensively investigate the role of CXCL14 in BRCA. We seek to systematically evaluate its prognostic value and immunomodulatory function, identify its precise cellular origin within the TME using single-cell resolution, and elucidate whether and how TP53 mutations directly regulate CXCL14 expression, thereby uncovering a potential novel mechanism underlying BRCA progression.

Materials and methods

Public database download and analysis

The Cancer Genome Atlas Breast Cancer (TCGA-BRCA) dataset: RNA-seq transcriptome data, clinical information (survival time, tumor grade, stage, race, age, PR status, HER2 status, etc.), somatic mutation data, and copy number variation (CNV) data were downloaded from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) Data Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) [13]. 2) GEO database: download the gene expression matrices and clinical survival data of the several independent BRCA cohorts GSE115577 [14], GSE25055 [15], GSE18229 [16], GSE42568 [17], etc. to verify the prognostic value of CXCL14. 3) cBioPortal: used to obtain the gene mutation profiles and clinical annotation information of 1,712 BRCA patients. 4) CPTAC: it is used to obtain protein-level expression data in BRCA tissues and verify the consistency of mRNA and protein expression. 5) single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data: Download the datasets GSE248288, GSE239706 [18], and GSE306201 [19] from GEO, which contain single-cell transcriptome data of BRCA and adjacent tissues. The single-cell RNA sequencing dataset used in this study is shown in Supplementary Table 1. All R packages were from CRAN, Bioconductor, and GitHub.

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

The GSVA score reflects the changes in gene set activity within the BRCA sample population in an unsupervised manner. The genes were divided into two groups for the GSVA analysis: one “down-regulated gene set” (containing genes whose expression levels significantly decreased as the tumor grade increased, such as CXCL14, CXCL12, and CCL2) and the other “up-regulated gene set” (containing genes whose expression levels increased as the grade increased, such as ACTA2 and CTHRC1). To compare GSVA scores, either a two-sample Wilcoxon test or a Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test with post-hoc Dunn’s pairwise multiple-comparison testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction was performed, with an adjusted p-value < 0.05 considered significant.

Survival analysis

We obtained gene expression data and corresponding clinical information from the TCGA and GEO databases. Patients were stratified into “CXCL14-high” and “CXCL14-low” groups based on the median CXCL14 expression across the entire cohort. Survival analysis was conducted using the survival R package (v3.8-3) to systematically evaluate the association between the expression level of CXCL14 and various clinical outcomes, including overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), progression-free interval (PFI), disease-specific survival (DSS), disease-free survival (DFS), disease-free interval (DFI), and recurrence-free survival (RFS). The log-rank test was used to compare survival differences across CXCL14 expression groups. To further eliminate the influence of potential confounding factors, a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model was used to correct covariates such as tumor stage, patient age, progesterone receptor (PR) status, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status, thereby more accurately evaluating the independent predictive value of CXCL14 expression for prognosis.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve

To evaluate the discriminatory ability of CXCL14 in diagnosing BRCA, we constructed an ROC curve using RNA-seq data from the TCGA-BRCA cohort. The analysis included 113 normal breast tissues and 1226 primary breast tumor tissues. Samples were dichotomized into ‘normal’ and ‘tumor’ groups. The expression level of CXCL14 was used as the predictor variable. Sensitivity and specificity at different thresholds were calculated using the pROC R package (v1.18.0), and the ROC curve was plotted. The area under the curve (AUC) was used to quantify the diagnostic efficacy of CXCL14 in differentiating BRCA from normal tissues.

Immune cell infiltration and scoring

After downloading the data from the TCGA database, the CIBERSORT algorithm was applied to quantify the proportions of 22 immune cell subsets [20]. This method precisely analyzes the complex composition of immune cells through linear support vector regression. The results were presented as proportions, and only samples with higher deconvolution confidence were retained for subsequent analysis. ssGSEA independently conducted gene set enrichment analysis for each sample by using the GSVA R package (v2.2.0). Calculate the enrichment scores of predefined immune cell types or immune function-related gene sets (such as immune pathways in Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and MSigDB) to reflect the activation level of specific immune cell subpopulations or pathways. In addition, the ESTIMATE tool was used to calculate microenvironment scores for tumor samples, including the immune score (characterizing immune cell infiltration), the StromalScore (reflecting stromal content), and the ESTIMATEScore. Indicate the overall tumor purity.

ScRNA-seq data processing

scRNA-seq data processing was performed using the Seurat R package (v4.2.2) [21]. Low-quality cells were filtered by retaining those with 200–5000 detected genes and excluding those with a mitochondrial gene proportion > 10%. Ambient RNA contamination was corrected using SoupX R package (v1.6.2) [22], and doublets were removed with DoubletFinder R package (v2.0.4) [23]. After LogNormalize normalization, we identified 2000 variable features for downstream analysis.

For cross-dataset batch integration, we first applied the standard Seurat CCA integration workflow: FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData functions were used to integrate multiple scRNA-seq datasets and correct major batch effects. Subsequently, the Harmony algorithm was further applied to the integrated Seurat object to refine residual batch effects and generate corrected low-dimensional embeddings for visualization and clustering [24]. Key Harmony parameters were set as follows: theta = 2, and the top 30 principal components (PCs) were used for correction. After batch correction, PCA was performed using the top 30 PCs, followed by unsupervised clustering via the Louvain algorithm. Cell type annotation was determined based on the expression of canonical marker genes.

Pseudotime trajectory analysis and cell communication

The communication relationships between cell populations were inferred based on ligand-receptor interaction databases (such as CellChatDB.human) using the CellChat R package (v 1.6.0), and the input and output patterns of signal pathways and the flow directions of key signals were analyzed [25]. The Monocle3 R package (v 1.3.4) was used to conduct pseudotime trajectory analysis on endothelial cell (EC) subsets, construct cell differentiation trajectories, and visualize the expression dynamics of genes such as CXCL14.

Cell culture

HUVEC and two BRCA cell lines (MCF-7 and MDA-231) were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The cells were cultured in an incubator at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2 and subcultured according to their growth status using the standard procedure. The experimental results were based on cells in the logarithmic growth phase, with good morphology. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination every three months and authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling.

Gene knockdown and overexpression

Three specific shRNA fragments targeting TP53 (shRNA#1: 5’-GACTCCAGTGGTAATCTAC-3’; shRNA#2: 5’-GCAGTCAGATCCTAGCGTC-3’; shRNA#3: 5’-GACCTCCATCGAGATCACAG-3’) were cloned into the pLKO.1-puro lentiviral vector. For overexpression, wild-type TP53 and four common loss-of-function mutants (R175H: c.524G > A; R248W: c.742 C > T; R273H: c.818G > A; R282W: c.844 C > T) were constructed in the pLVX-EF1a vector with a Flag tag [26].

After infecting HUVEC cells, puromycin selection was used for stable cell line screening, and finally, cell lines with stable downregulation or overexpression of wild-type/mutant TP53 were obtained. The expression changes of TP53 at the protein and mRNA levels were verified by Western Blotting (WB) and qPCR to confirm the successful construction of the stable strain. The specific primer sequences used for RT-qPCR amplification of TP53 were as follows: forward, 5′-CTGGATTGGCAGCCAGACT-3′; reverse, 5′-GCTCGACGCTAGGATCTGAC-3′.

WB

Total cell proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer, and the protein concentration was determined by the BCA method and quantitatively standardized. Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After the transfer, the membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat skim milk at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, TP53 antibody (CST, #2524, 1:1000) and β-actin antibody (Abcam, #ab8226, 1:5000) were added, respectively, incubated overnight at 4 °C. After TBST washing, the corresponding HRP-labeled secondary antibody (Servicebio, #GB25301, 1:5000) was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. After washing again, develop with an ECL substrate and detect signals using a gel imaging system. Semi-quantitative analysis of TP53 protein expression was conducted using β-actin as an internal reference.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Use the human CXCL14 quantitative detection kit (#CB14483-Hu) from Shanghai Coiro Bio and operate strictly in accordance with the product instructions. The brief steps are as follows: Coat the microplate with purified human chemokine (CXCL14) capture antibody to form a solid-phase antibody, and then add the human chemokine to the coated microplate wells in sequence. CXCL14 is then combined with HRP-labeled detection antibody to form an antibody-antigen-enzyme-labeled antibody complex. After thorough washing, substrate TMB is added for color development. TMB is converted to a blue color under the catalysis of the HRP enzyme, then to the final yellow color under the action of acid. The depth of color is positively correlated with CXCL14 expression. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader, and the CXCL14 content in the sample was calculated from the standard curve.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation quantitative real-time PCR (ChIP-qPCR)

The binding of the TP53 protein in the CXCL14 promoter region was detected by ChIP-qPCR technology. The CXCL14 promoter primer pairs includes the following: #1: 5’-CTTGGCACCTGCTCCAAAAC-3’; #2: 5’-CCAGGGACTCCAAGAGCTTG-3’; #3: 5’-TTTGACTCCAGACACCAGCC-3’. The brief steps are as follows: First, the cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde. After the reaction is terminated with glycine, the chromatin is ultrasonicated to produce 200–500 bp fragments. Some chromatin was taken as the Input control, and the remaining samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with p53-specific antibody (CST, #2524, 1:100) and normal rabbit IgG (CST, #2729) as the negative control for non-specific binding. The immune complex was captured using Protein A/G magnetic beads, and DNA fragments were obtained after elution, reverse cross-linking, and purification. The specific region of the CXCL14 promoter was amplified by qPCR. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using the SYBR Green method.

Dual-luciferase reporter assays

To verify TP53’s transcriptional regulation of the CXCL14 promoter, this study constructed a dual-luciferase reporter system. Firstly, the DNA fragment containing the core region of the CXCL14 promoter was cloned into the pGL4.17 firefly luciferase reporter vector. The constructed reporter plasmid was co-transfected with the reference plasmid pRL-SV40 into HEK293T cells. After 24 h of transfection, the cells were seeded in 96-well plates. In specific groups, the wild-type TP53 expression vector, the TP53 mutant (e.g., R175H) expression vector, or an empty control vector was transfected. After 48 h of transfection, the cells were lysed using the dual-luciferase reporter gene assay kit, and the fluorescence signal was detected. The relative transcriptional activity of the CXCL14 promoter was normalized by the ratio of firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity, and used to compare the effects of different TP53 states (wild type, mutant, knockdown) on the transcriptional activity of CXCL14.

Preparation of conditioned medium (CM)

To study TP53 function, culture supernatants from TP53-overexpressing, TP53-knockdown, and control HUVEC cells were collected. The supernatant was centrifuged at 4 ° C and 5000 × g for 10 min to remove suspended cells. Then, it was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane to thoroughly remove cell debris and large particles. The resulting CM was aliquoted and stored at -80 °C until use.

Cell migration and invasion

Cell motility was evaluated using transwell chambers (8 μm pore size, Corning). For the migration experiment, cells were seeded into the upper chamber. For the invasion experiment, the small chamber membrane was pre-coated with Matrigel (diluted 1:8) to simulate the basement membrane barrier. For both detection methods, 2 × 10 ^5 Cells (in 200 µl of serum-free culture medium) were inoculated into the upper petri dish. The above-mentioned conditioned media were respectively added to the lower chambers as chemotactic stimulants. To verify the specific role of CXCL14 in the TP53-mediated effect, in some experimental groups, CXCL14-neutralizing antibodies (R&D Systems, #AF866) were used to pretreat the CM for 1 h to block its biological activity. After inoculation, the cells were cultured for 24 h. Then, the small chambers were removed, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The number of transmembrane cells was counted under a microscope to evaluate the effects of different treatments on cell migration and invasion.

Mouse xenograft assays

Stable overexpressed HUVEC cells with wild-type TP53 (HUVEC-TP53-WT) or R175H mutant (HUVEC-TP53#R175H) were mixed with MDA-231 cells at a ratio of 1:1 (2 × 106 cells each) in 150µL of sterile matrix gel. Then, they were subcutaneously inoculated into the right abdominal region of 4-6-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (n = 6 per group). From the 7th day after inoculation, the tumor’s long and short diameters were measured using a digital caliper to calculate the tumor volume. On the 28th day after inoculation, the nude mice were euthanized, and the tumor was completely dissected, weighed, and photographed. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the animal ethics committee.

Histological and immunohistochemical

After fixation, dehydration, and paraffin embedding, the sections were prepared. Subsequent staining was carried out according to the standard procedure. The brief steps are as follows: After the sections were dehydrated, hydrated, and antigen-repaired, 3% H2O2 was used to block endogenous peroxidase, and 5% BSA was used to block nonspecific binding. Then, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-Ki67 (Proteintech, #27309-1-AP, 1:10000), anti-p53 (Proteintech, #10442-1-AP, 1:500), and anti-CXCL14 (Proteintech, #10468-1-AP, 1:200). The next day, the corresponding HRP-labeled secondary antibodies and a DAB staining kit were used for staining. Hematoxylin was used to restain the cell nuclei; the sections were then dehydrated, counterstained, and sealed. All sections were observed under an upright optical microscope, and images were collected.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times, and the data were expressed as mean ± standard error (SE) or standard deviation (SD). Select the appropriate statistical method based on the data distribution: When the comparison between the two groups conforms to the normal distribution, use Student’s t-test; otherwise, use the Mann-Whitney U test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare multiple groups, with post hoc tests (e.g., the Tukey method) used when necessary. The correlation between variables was evaluated by Pearson (normal distribution) or Spearman (non-parametric) correlation coefficients. The differences in survival curves were analyzed using the Log-rank test. The statistical significance threshold was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses and chart drawing were accomplished using RStudio (v4.5.0) and GraphPad Prism (v9.0.0).

Results

CAF signature correlates with tumor grade in BRCA

In the TME, CAFs, as important stromal cell components, play a key role in tumorigenesis, development, and metastasis [27]. To further explore the expression of CAF-related gene sets across pan-cancer, we first analyzed these marker genes. The results showed that multiple CAF marker genes were significantly downregulated in BRCA tissues compared to normal controls (Figs. 1A-L). Furthermore, the expression of six genes (CCL2, C7, CXCL12, CXCL14, OGN, and RGS5) significantly decreased with higher tumor grade, whereas others, such as ACTA2 and CTHRC1, showed an increasing trend (Supplementary Table 2, Fig. 1M).

Expression patterns of CAFs marker genes in BRCA. (A-L) Bubble plots (or box plots) show the mRNA expression levels of individual CAFs marker genes (ACTA2, CTHRC1, CCL2, C7, CXCL12, CXCL14, OGN, RGS5, etc.) in BRCA. (M) A comprehensive chart showing the correlation between the expression trends of all analyzed CAFs genes and the increase in tumor grade. (N, O) GSVA was used to up-regulate and down-regulate the enrichment scores of CAFs gene sets in different tumor grades

To comprehensively evaluate the functional changes in CAF-related gene sets, we employed GSVA to calculate enrichment scores for the corresponding gene sets (Figs. 1N, O). This analytical method can help us better understand the overall expression behavior of the gene set in the sample and avoid potential deviations caused by individual genes. The analysis results show that the GSVA score of the gene set with decreased expression gradually decreases with increasing tumor grade, whereas the score of the gene set with increased expression does not show a significant change. In summary, these findings reveal that CAF marker genes exhibit a distinct, graded-dependent expression pattern in BRCA, suggesting that these genes or distinct functional subtypes of CAFs may play an important role in the malignant evolution of BRCA.

CXCL14 is a novel prognostic and diagnostic biomarker in BRCA

Next, we further explored the association between the above-mentioned marker genes and the prognosis of BRCA. The results indicated that the high expression of CXCL14 was significantly associated with better survival outcomes in patients, specifically reflected in the prolonged OS (HR = 0.64, P = 0.006), PFS (HR = 0.65, P = 0.002), DSS (HR = 0.53, P = 0.005), DFS (HR = 0.64, P = 0.045), PFI (HR = 0.61, P = 0.003), and RFS (HR = 0.70, P = 0.000) (Figs. 2A-E) Supplementary Table 3). To verify the findings in the TCGA database, we further analyzed the expression of CXCL14 and the survival status of patients in several independent datasets in GEO (Table 1), and the results were consistent with the aforementioned conclusion. To further integrate clinical prognostic factors, we constructed a prognostic nomogram that included tumor stage, race, age, PR status, and HER2 status (Fig. 2F; Table 2). This result once again supports the protective role of CXCL14. Given the established prognostic value of CXCL14, we sought to additionally evaluate its potential as a diagnostic biomarker for distinguishing BRCA tissues from normal breast tissues. The ROC curve analysis indicated that CXCL14 expression showed some diagnostic discrimination (Supplementary Fig. 1). In two independent datasets, the AUC was: 0.726 (95% CI: 0.699–0.754) for the TCGA dataset and 0.800 (95% CI: 0.575–0.975) for the GSE267442 dataset (Fig. 2G). To explore its expression regulatory mechanism, we analyzed the correlation between CXCL14 CNV and expression levels (Fig. 2H) and found a weak positive correlation (r = 0.21, P < 0.001). This indicates that CNV may account for only a small portion of the variation in CXCL14 expression, suggesting that other regulatory mechanisms are likely to play a more dominant role and warrant further investigation.

Association of CXCL14 expression with immune cell infiltration, immunogenic phenotype, and clinical outcomes in BRCA

To deeply explore the role of CXCL14 in immune regulation of BRCA, we analyzed the expression patterns of immune-regulation-related genes and immune checkpoint genes. The results showed that CXCL14 was significantly positively correlated with the expression of multiple immune-related genes, including CXCL12, CXCL19, CCR10, and CCR2 (Fig. 3A). Infiltration levels of 22 immune cell types were compared using the CIBERSORT algorithm. It was found that the proportions of naive B cells, memory B cells, plasma cells, CD8⁺T cells, and CD4⁺memory T cells in the CXCL14 high-expression group were significantly higher than those in the low-expression group. In the low-expression group, the infiltration ratios of resting NK cells, M0-type macrophages, and M2-type macrophages were higher (Fig. 3B, Supplementary Fig. 3). Further analysis using the ssGSEA algorithm indicated that the infiltration levels of mast cells, NK cells, and immature dendritic cells (iDC) in the CXCL14-high expression group were significantly higher, whereas the proportion of Th2 cells was significantly negatively correlated with CXCL14 expression (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, as shown in Fig. 3D, there were significant differences in CXCL14 expression among the six immune subtypes of BRCA (P = 4.1 × 10 − 6), and the immune score, matrix score, and ESTIMATE score of the high-expression group were all significantly increased (Fig. 3E). To verify the relationship between CXCL14 and the tumor immune microenvironment from different perspectives, we further analyzed the correlation between CXCL14 expression and the infiltration levels of six major immune cells using the TIMER algorithm. The results showed that CXCL14 expression was positively correlated with the infiltration of various anti-tumor immune cells (Supplementary Table 4). Among all 1086 BRCA samples, the high expression of CXCL14 showed a statistically significant positive correlation with the infiltration of CD8⁺T cells (rho = 0.125, adj p = 0.001). In the molecular subtype analysis, the correlation was strongest in the BRCA-Basal subtype, where CXCL14 expression was significantly positively correlated with CD4⁺T cell infiltration (rho = 0.250, adj p = 0.008). In the BRCA-LumA subtype, the expression of CXCL14 was also significantly positively correlated with the infiltration of CD4⁺T cells (rho = 0.125, adj.p = 0.028), and was positively correlated with the infiltration of myeloid dendritic cells (rho = 0.135, adj p = 0.015).

(B) Comparison of immune cell infiltration abundances estimated by CIBERSORT between CXCL14-high and CXCL14-low expression groups. (C) Immune cell infiltration levels assessed by ssGSEA, showing significant associations with CXCL14 expression. (D) Differential expression of CXCL14 across six immune subtypes of BRCA. (E) Comparison of Immunescore, Stromalscore, and ESTIMATEscore between CXCL14-high and CXCL14-low groups. (F, G) Analysis of cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) levels and predicted response to anti-PD-1 (F) and anti-CTLA4 (G) immunotherapy in patients with high or low CXCL14 expression, suggesting enhanced efficacy in the high-expression group

To investigate the prognostic value of T-cell characteristics in immunotherapy, we analyzed the relationship between the level of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTL) and the OS of patients, and stratified them according to the expression level of CXCL14 (Fig. 3F). In the CXCL14 high-expression group (n = 214), the survival curves for patients with high CTL levels were overall higher than those for patients with low CTL levels, suggesting a longer survival trend, but this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.156). In the CXCL14 low-expression group (n = 19), although the sample size was small, a similar trend of separation in the survival curves was observed. Further analysis indicated that tumors with high CXCL14 expression scored significantly higher on immune therapy sensitivity signatures (e.g., ips_ctla4_neg_pd1_pos and ips_ctla4_pos_pd1_neg; P < 0.001). Collectively, these data suggest that a CXCL14-enriched tumor immune microenvironment may be more responsive to immune checkpoint blockade.

CXCL14 is highly expressed in endothelial subpopulations and modulates cell-cell communication in the BRCA microenvironment

As shown in Fig. 4A, we conducted single-cell RNA analysis on two single-cell datasets. First, seven major cell subtypes were identified through clustering: B cells, T cells, macrophages, fibroblasts, mast cells, epithelial cells, and ECs (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Figs. 4A-D). Subsequently, based on the ligand-receptor interaction database, an intercellular communication network was constructed to infer the interaction relationships mediated by chemical signals (Figs. 4C, D). The analysis results indicate that, in the BRCA microenvironment, macrophages, ECs, epithelial cells, and other cells interact extensively with one another. Further visualization of the top seven signaling pathways revealed that the MK signaling pathway stood out significantly (Fig. 4E). Next, we examined CXCL14 expression levels across different cell types and found that it was most highly expressed in ECs (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, intercellular communication reveals the central hub role of CXCL14 + ECs in the TME (Fig. 4G). In terms of the number of interactions, the ligand-receptor interactions between CXCL14 + ECs and epithelial cells are the most frequent (a total of 51 pairs). At the same time, CXCL14 + ECs also interact extensively with T cells (31 pairs) and mast cells (22 pairs). In terms of interaction intensity, the interaction weights between CXCL14 + ECs and T cells, fibroblasts, and B cells are significantly higher than those of other cell pairs. It is worth noting that although the number of interactions between CXCL14 + ECs and epithelial cells is the highest (51 pairs), their interaction weight (0.2) is relatively low, suggesting a large amount of, but weak, signal exchange between the two.

The expression dynamics of CXCL14 in ECs were tracked using pseudotime trajectory analysis, revealing that its expression gradually decreased over cell evolution, suggesting that this gene may be associated with early differentiation or a specific functional state of ECs (Fig. 4H). Subsequently, through subcluster clustering, a total of 7 EC subpopulations were identified (Figs. 4I, J). Among them, CXCL14 was most expressed in Activated_ECs, stalk_cells, and blood ECs (BECs)_general. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted on the EC population with high CXCL14 expression (Figs. 4K, L). The GO entries indicated that these cells were significantly enriched in processes such as extracellular structural organization, extracellular matrix assembly, and external encapsulation structural organization. KEGG analysis indicated that the main enrichment pathways included proteoglycans in cancer, endoplasmic reticulum protein processing, adhesion plaques, actin cytoskeleton regulation, and the Hippo signaling pathway. These results suggest that ECs with high CXCL14 expression may play an important role in the TME by regulating the extracellular matrix and multiple signaling pathways.

TP53 mutation modulates CXCL14 expression in ECs to promote BRCA progression

Genomic mutations represent the key intrinsic driving force behind the genetic and morphological heterogeneity of tumors [28, 29]. Based on the cBioPortal database, we constructed a gene mutation map of 1705 BRCA patients stratified by CXCL14 expression level and found that TP53 mutations were the most significant (Figs. 5A, B, Supplementary Table 5). To further evaluate its clinical significance, we used the TCGA database to plot survival curves for TP53 wild status and CXCL14 expression level. The results showed that within the first 6 months after diagnosis, the survival rate of patients with TP53 mutations and low CXCL14 expression dropped sharply (Fig. 5C; Supplementary Fig. 5). Furthermore, analysis based on the CPTAC database indicates that the expression of CXCL14 is significantly downregulated after TP53 mutation. However, at the single-cell level, TP53 pathway activity did not show a significant correlation with CXCL14 expression (Supplementary Fig. 6). Previous studies have found p53 accumulation was not a result of increased TP53 mRNA translation but rather a consequence of enhanced protein stability [30]. To deeply explore the potential mechanism by which TP53 regulates CXCL14 expression, we conducted cell clustering analysis of scRNA-seq data from the GSE239706 dataset and identified seven cell subpopulations (Figs. 5E, F).

By analyzing the cell ratio and CXCL14 expression (Figs. 5G, H), we found that TP53 expression was higher in ECs from the mutant group, suggesting that ECs may play an important role in regulating CXCL14 expression through TP53 mutations. Further analysis of cell communication revealed that TP53 mutations have restructured the communication between ECs and other cells. Specifically, TP53 mutations significantly increase the number of interactions with B cells and macrophages, while reducing their intensity. In addition, GSVA enrichment analysis indicated that the hypoxia response and TNFA signaling pathways were significantly downregulated in TP53 mutant samples. Subsequently, we subdivided ECs into two subtypes: activated_ECs and BEC_Arterial. Consistent with the previous results, in the TP53 mutant type, the activated_ECs subgroup showed higher TP53 expression and lower CXCL14 expression (Figs. 5K, L). In conclusion, this study reveals that TP53 mutations may be involved in the regulatory mechanism of BRCA progression by influencing CXCL14 expression in ECs.

TP53 transcriptionally represses CXCL14 to promote tumor progression in BRCA

Firstly, by constructing four common TP53 mutants, we found that the R175H mutation could produce the highest level of TP53 mRNA (Fig. 6A). Indeed, the TP53 R175H mutation is highly prevalent in human BRCA [31]. Based on this result, we constructed stable HUVEC cell lines with TP53 overexpression and knockdown, and verified their knockdown and overexpression efficiencies by WB (Figs. 6B-D). In addition, ELISA results showed that after TP53 overexpression, HUVEC CXCL14 secretion decreased, whereas after TP53 knockdown, HUVEC CXCL14 secretion increased (Fig. 6E). To further explore the regulatory mechanism of TP53 on CXCL14, we used JASPAR to predict TP53 binding sites in the CXCL14 promoter region and confirmed, through ChIP-qPCR, that TP53 directly regulates CXCL14 expression through transcriptional regulation (Figs. 6F, G). Subsequently, to more realistically simulate the impact of ECs on tumors, we conducted co-culture experiments. As shown in Fig. 6H, we collected CM for TP53 overexpression and knockdown stabilization transfer, respectively. To verify the specific role of CXCL14, we pre-treated the conditioned medium with CXCL14 neutralizing antibody to block its activity. The results indicated that after TP53 knockdown, CXCL14 expression increased, thereby inhibiting the proliferation, migration, and invasion of BRCA cells (Figs. 6I-K, Supplementary Fig. 7). Conversely, a decrease in CXCL14 levels promotes these malignant phenotypes.

To verify the tumor-promoting function of the TP53 R175H mutation in the in vivo environment, we constructed a mouse model of co-transplantation of HUVEC and MDA-231 cells. As shown in Fig. 6L, compared with the group of HUVEC cells co-transplanted with the stable wild-type TP53 overexpression, the group of HUVEC cells co-transplanted with TP53 R175H formed significantly larger tumors at the experimental endpoint (Fig. 6M). Additionally, the average tumor weight of the TP53 R175H mutation group was also significantly higher than that of the wild-type control group (Fig. 6N). To explore the potential molecular mechanism, we conducted immunohistochemical analysis on the tumor tissues. The results showed that, compared with the wild-type control group, the Ki-67-positive rate in tumor tissues from the TP53 R175H mutation group was significantly higher (Figs. 6O, P), indicating that tumor cells were proliferating more actively. At the same time, the accumulation of p53 protein was significantly enhanced, and the protein expression level of CXCL14 was significantly downregulated. In conclusion, these in vivo experimental results indicate that ECs carrying the TP53 R175H mutation can significantly enhance the in vivo tumor-forming ability, growth rate, and final tumor weight of the co-transplanted BRCA cells.

Discussion

This study systematically elucidates the multi-level role of CXCL14 in BRCA, ranging from its prognostic value and immunomodulatory function to its precise cellular origin and complex regulatory mechanisms. Our findings not only position CXCL14 as a core player in the TME of BRCA but also reveal a novel, therapeutically relevant regulatory axis: TP53 mutations promote tumor progression by transcriptionally inhibiting CXCL14 expression in ECs.

Our analysis revealed the evolution of CAF marker genes in BRCA with respect to grade, characterized by a gradual increase in fibroblast markers (ACTA2, CTHRC1) and a corresponding decrease in immunomodulatory genes (C7, CXCL12, CXCL14, OGN) [32]. This pattern indicates a dynamic shift in the CAF phenotype towards a pro-fibrotic direction. At the same time, the reduction of immunomodulatory factors may reflect the decrease in the subset of immunomodulatory CAF, which may affect the ability of the TME to monitor anti-tumor immunity [33]. Notably, CXCL14, as a key member of CAF features, shows robust association with favorable survival outcomes across multiple cohorts and survival endpoints. We found that the expression of CXCL14 was positively correlated with a favorable immune environment - characterized by enhanced infiltration of cytotoxic T cells, memory B cells, and dendritic cells, while a reduction in immunosuppressive M2-type macrophages and Th2 cells - which provided a convincing mechanism explanation for its protective effect. This positions CXCL14 as a key mediator of CAFs-immune interactions. Contrary to the traditional perception that ECs usually promote tumors, our data suggest that EC subpopulations with high expression of CXCL14 may help establish an immunological “hot” TME, thereby enhancing the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade therapy [34]. This discovery challenges the traditional single view of ECs as immunosuppressive entities and adds a new dimension to our understanding of the functional diversity of ECs [35].

A key advancement of this study was the determination, via single-cell analysis, that ECs, especially specific subsets such as activated ECs and BECs, are the main source of CXCL14 in the BRCA TME. This discovery shifts the paradigm by attributing CXCL14 expression solely to CAFs or epithelial cells and highlights the profound functional contribution of vascular niches. The enrichment of ECs with high CXCL14 expression in extracellular matrix tissues and related pathways, such as the Hippo signaling pathway, indicates that CXCL14 is not only a soluble chemokine but is also intrinsically associated with EC activation and matrix remodeling, which are crucial for angiogenesis and tissue structure [36].

TP53 mutation is not merely a concurrent genetic event but actively inhibits the expression of CXCL14 in ECs [37]. This inhibition is not a universal indirect consequence of TP53 deletion, but is mediated by direct binding to the CXCL14 promoter, which has been strictly confirmed by ChIP-qPCR [38]. Mutated TP53 not only promotes intracellular carcinogenicity but also forms an inhibitory TME externally by silencing key immune-stimulating chemokines from ECs [39]. This provides a unified mechanistic explanation for the poor prognosis of patients with TP53 mutations and low CXCL14 expression. Notably, patients with TP53 mutations and low CXCL14 expression had a sharp decline in overall survival within 6 months after diagnosis, which may be related to the enhanced aggressiveness of high-grade tumors and impaired immune surveillance. Notably, the TP53 pathway activity score, calculated from classical p53 target genes, was not negatively correlated with CXCL14 expression, but this does not contradict our mechanistic conclusions. Firstly, the traditional pathway characteristics reflect the transcriptional activity of wild-type p53 [40], which has been lost in mutant cells and cannot capture the functional gain-of-function regulation of mutant p53; secondly, in single-cell data, wild-type and mutant cells coexist, and the pathway score integrates both signals, weakening the mutation-specific effect. Therefore, computational inference cannot replace direct molecular detection.

Multiple studies have shown that CXCL14 can recruit and activate immune effector cells, including dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cells, and cytotoxic T cells, thereby shaping an immune microenvironment with anti-tumor activity [41, 42]. For BRCA patients carrying TP53 mutations (typically associated with treatment resistance and poor prognosis), our work indicates that restoring CXCL14 signaling may be a promising therapeutic strategy. This can be achieved through gene therapy, CXCL14-Mimetic peptides, or preparations that disrupt the inhibitory complex formed by mutant TP53 at the CXCL14 promoter. In addition, the combination of CXCL14 supplementation strategies and immune checkpoint inhibitors holds great potential in reversing immunotherapy resistance in these invasive tumors. This work redefines CXCL14 as a key EC-derived factor and reveals that its inhibition is a new mechanism by which TP53 mutations drive the malignant progression of BRCA, providing a new approach for prognostic stratification and targeted intervention.

Although this study provides new insights into the mechanism of action of CXCL14 in BRCA, several limitations remain. Firstly, the dual luciferase experiment in this study did not introduce a binding-site mutation in the CXCL14 promoter; therefore, it cannot verify TP53 mutations in combination with CXCL14 promoter specificity. Secondly, HUVECs cannot fully replicate the complex characteristics of ECs in BRCA tumors in vivo, and there are limitations to extrapolating mechanisms. Thirdly, because nude mice lack complete immune components, our xenograft model can only assess tumor proliferation phenotypically but cannot directly verify the immunomodulatory function of CXCL14 in vivo. Fourthly, high CXCL14 expression is associated with a better response to anti-PD-1/CTLA-4 therapy, but these data are derived from computational prediction models in public databases. Therefore, using CXCL14 as a biomarker to predict the efficacy of immunotherapy must undergo rigorous clinical validation in a retrospective or prospective cohort of BRCA patients who have received checkpoint inhibitor immunotherapy. Finally, there are multiple TP53 mutant types. This study mainly focuses on the R175H mutant. The regulatory effects of other common TP53 mutants (such as R248W and R273H) may require further research.

In conclusion, to our knowledge, this study provides the first description of a novel mechanism by which TP53 mutations in ECs promote the malignant progression of BRCA by transcriptionally inhibiting CXCL14 expression.
