MYB Factors: Hubs of Plant Stress and Hormone Crosstalk
Authors: Aye Aye Htun, Ya Wang, Jiahao Liu, Zhiqiang Guo, Yi Zheng, Wenjie Wang, Shuting Ma, Aolin Jia, Yan Ren, Feng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMYB transcription factors function as main regulatory hubs that integrate environmental signals with multi‐hormonal pathways to synchronize plant growth, metabolism and stress responses. This review delineates the regulatory roles of MYB in the signalling pathway of salicylic acid, jasmonic acid, ethylene and abscisic acid signalling. These are responsible for regulating hormone biosynthesis and facilitating interaction between biotic and abiotic stress responses. The post‐translational regulation of MYB activity by kinase cascades and the direct regulation of secondary metabolic pathways, such as the biosynthesis of phenylpropanol and lignin, by MYBs through the MYB‐bHLH‐WD40 (MBW) complex were investigated. MYB transcription factors located at the intersection of hormone signalling, metabolic regulation, and developmental regulation play a unique role in coordinating plant adaptation from the cellular level to the whole plant.Keywords: hormone crosstalk, metabolic regulation, MYB transcription factors, post‐translational modification, stress signalling

Introduction

Modern crops are subjected to a number of biotic and abiotic stresses, such as drought, salinity, nutrient deficiency, elevated carbon dioxide levels and disease. These stresses jointly constrain plant productivity and yield consistency. Plants respond by mounting an orchestrated defence mechanism to overcome these obstacles. This defence system is activated by sensory mechanisms, conveyed through an integrated signalling network that leads to the transcriptional reprogramming of stress‐responsive genes, thereby facilitating additional physiological and metabolic adaptations (Jones and Dangl 2006; He et al. 2022; Ortega et al. 2024).

The perception of signals underpins this regulatory cascade. Biotic stresses are predominantly identified by cell‐surface pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) resulting in the initiation of the pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)‐triggered immunity (PTI) (Pruitt et al. 2021). They are also identified by intracellular NLR receptors, which mediate effector‐triggered immune responses (ETI) (Wang, Li, et al. 2023). Abiotic stress factors cause disturbances of cellular homeostasis, such as osmotic balance, membrane integrity and redox status. These perturbations are rapidly transformed into molecular signals, often through the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by NADPH oxidases (RBOHs). The initial signals are subsequently transmitted through interconnected signalling pathways, encompassing ROS waves, calcium ion flux, Mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascades, and dynamic interactions of plant hormones including salicylic acid (SA), jasmonic acid (JA), ethylene (ET) and abscisic acid (ABA) (Pruitt et al. 2021; Mittler et al. 2022; Myers et al. 2023).

Diverse signalling pathways finally converge at transcriptional regulation, where transcription factors (TFs) serve as master regulators, activating various genes to facilitate plant stress adaptation (Meraj et al. 2020; Liu, Xi, et al. 2024). Transcription factors (TFs) are important engineering targets for climate‐resilient crops, as they regulate gene expression to integrate abiotic and biotic stress signalling networks. Transcription factors possess DNA‐binding domains, transcriptional regulation modules, oligomerization sites and nuclear localization signals. They modulate gene expression by interacting with other transcription factors or binding to the downstream genes' promoter region to affect transcriptional activity (Bhoite et al. 2025). This regulatory mechanism is important to enhance plant tolerance to abiotic stresses and to coordinate physiological and biochemical activities during the plant life cycle. Some TF members function as central hubs to integrate hormone signalling, secondary metabolism and broad‐spectrum disease resistance. Among the principal transcription factor families (NAC, WRKY, bZIP, AP2/ERF and MYB) implicated in stress reactions, the MYB superfamily is characterized by its significant evolutionary diversity and functional adaptability (Table 1).

The MYB family comprises a large and diverse group of proteins found in eukaryotes. Most MYB proteins function as transcription factors, generally distinguished by a unique and conserved MYB repeat sequence (R) at the N‐terminus, and are involved in DNA binding and protein–protein interactions. Numerous members of this family have been recognized in Arabidopsis, rice, maize and soybean, and are implicated in the regulation of diverse cellular processes, including cell cycle and morphogenesis, as well as responses to biotic and abiotic stresses (Roy 2016). Many MYBs have been characterized under particular stress conditions. Nonetheless, a comprehensive understanding of how MYB transcription factors encode and orchestrate simultaneous, frequently conflicting hormonal signals under combined stress circumstances remains insufficient. This review elucidates the role of MYB transcription factors in particular defensive signalling pathways regulated by hormones (SA, JA, ET and ABA). The hub‐like roles of MYB‐mediated hormonal crosstalk and signal integration under multifaceted and concurrent stress situations are emphasized as prospective research path for developing climate‐resilient crops.

Structural and Functional Diversity of MYB Transcription Factor

DNA Recognition and Binding to Target Genes

The plant MYB family is categorized into four primary groups (1R‐MYB/MYB‐related, R2R3‐MYB, 3R‐MYB and 4R‐MYB) based on the number and repeats (Stracke et al. 2001). Each of the repeats possesses a helix‐turn‐helix structure and generally consists of 50 to 53 amino acids (Figure 1A) (Dias et al. 2003; Feller et al. 2011). Among them, the R2R3‐MYB family is the largest and most abundant MYB transcription factors in higher plants (Dubos et al. 2010). These proteins are characterized by their N‐terminal conserved domain and a more variable C‐terminal transcription domain (TAD). The N‐terminal domain is called the DNA‐binding domain (DBD) and also acts as an important platform for protein–protein interactions (PPIs) with transcriptional co‐regulators. R2R3‐MYB proteins bind to specific cis‐regulatory sequences known as MYB recognition elements (MREs), typically contain a core MYB‐binding motif (YAACKG), and the AC‐rich elements AC‐I (ACCTACC), AC‐II (ACCAACC) and AC‐III (ACCTAAC) (Kim et al. 2012; Nakano et al. 2015). This cis‐regulatory composite architecture enables MYB transcription factors to bind target genes through their DBD and recruit co‐regulators to activate or repress transcription, thereby acting as master regulators of diverse plant processes, including development, stress adaptation and secondary metabolism, such as flavonoids, anthocyanin and lignin biosynthesis (Figure 1B) (Cao et al. 2020; Yan et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2025). In maize seedlings, ZmMYB104 directly activates the ZmCAT2 gene encoding catalase to improve the ability of the plant to eliminate hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). This improved scavenging ability restricts the accumulation of ROS and oxidative damage, thus improving heat‐stress tolerance (Zhang et al. 2025). The R2R3‐TaMYB30 directly activates the expression of PRX and GST genes, and overexpressing lines exhibit significantly enhanced tolerance to oxidative stress with mitigated H2O2 accumulation during post‐infection. It enhances resistance to wheat sharp eyespot caused by Rhizoctonia cerealis by scavenging to reduce ROS accumulation. The transgenic overexpression of TaMYB30 promotes tolerance to oxidative stress, while knockdown of wheat compromises the response to stress (Li et al. 2025). The MYB transcription factor also plays a role in how plants interact with pathogens by managing sugar transportation. In wheat, TaMYB50 binds to the promoter region of the TaSWEET14d gene, thereby enhancing the transport of sugar. This binding regulates the activity of TaSWEET14d during stripe rust infection induced by Puccinia striiformis f. sp. tritici. This interaction takes place at the TA box, where TaMYB50 negatively regulates the function of TaSWEET14d and increases susceptibility to disease. Conversely, genetic alteration of TaSWEET14d leads to improved resistance against stripe rust in wheat (Zheng et al. 2025). MYB transcription factors are also involved in phytoalexin production. MYB15 also directly activates the scopoletin biosynthesis genes PAL, C4H, 4CL, COMT and F6′H1 during infection with Pseudomonas syringae . The loss‐of‐function mutants, myb15‐1 and myb15‐2, have reduced scopoletin levels in the presence of flg22 and are more susceptible to P. syringae (Chezem et al. 2017). Some MYB factors are negative regulators of defence. OsMYB48 directly regulates the transcriptional activation of HSP71.1 and reduces rice blast resistance. By contrast, CRISPR/Cas9‐generated myb48 knockout lines show decreased lesion numbers and sizes with no alteration in plant height or panicle number. Thus, MYB48‐HSP71.1 is a promising dual target for breeding rice resistant to blast (Song et al. 2025).

Transcriptional and Post‐Translational Regulation Mechanisms

Besides binding to DNA, MYB proteins use different structural motifs to interact with partner proteins and integrate signals and crosstalk between pathways. The C‐terminal region of MYB repressors is structurally and functionally distinct from other MYB transcription factors (Xu et al. 2014; Ranade and García‐gil 2024; Ma, Hu, and Zhong 2025). The specific C‐terminal repression domains allow different mechanisms of gene silencing. The C1 motif (LlsrGIDPxT/sHRxI/L) is a conserved sequence present in MYB repressors but has also an apparent activator activity (Aharoni et al. 2001; Stracke et al. 2001; Matsui et al. 2008). The C2 motif (pdLNLD/ELxiG/S), also called the ethylene response factor‐associated amphiphilic repression (EAR) motif, contains an LxLxL or DLNxxP sequence and is found in various repressors from different transcription factor families (Kagale et al. 2010). The EAR motif interacts with TPL via the TPD domain that binds histone deacetylases (HDACs). These enzymes strip acetyl groups from histones, a change that makes the chromatin structure more compact, limiting the ability of DNA to be transcribed and effectively turning off gene expression (Ma et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2018). Protein–protein interaction assays showed that CmHSFA4 interacts with a transcriptional repressor, CmMYBS3, which recruits the corepressor CmTPL by its C‐terminal EAR motif. This complex inhibits H3 and H4 histone acetylation at the CmMYB121 locus, resulting in the repression of its transcription under salt stress in chrysanthemum (Wang et al. 2024). Some of these proteins contain the C3 zinc‐finger motif (CX1‐2CX7‐12CX2C) as an auxiliary domain element, but it is not the main effector of repression (Deluc et al. 2008). The motif C4 (FLGLx4‐7 V/LLD/GF/YR/Sx1LEMK) is found in R2R3‐MYB repressors such as chrysanthemum CmMYB1, petunia PhMYB4, switchgrass PvMYB4 and maize ZmMYB31 and ZmMYB42 and is a regulatory element involved in negative regulation of early phenylpropanoid and lignin biosynthesis pathways (Matsui et al. 2008; Zhu et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2018). The C5 motif (TLLLFR) in the C‐terminal region contributes to transcriptional repression in various ways (Chen et al. 2019; Li, Shan, et al. 2020; Xie et al. 2022). Grapevine VvMYBC2‐L1 and VvMYBC2‐L3, with EAR and TLLLFR motifs, inhibit flavonoid and anthocyanin pathways. Without TLLLFR, VvMYB4a and VvMYB4b repress the general phenylpropanoid pathway. Therefore, the TLLLFR motif functions as the repressor to target the flavonoid/anthocyanin branch instead of the general pathway (Cavallini et al. 2015). FtMYB8, containing a C2 repression motif and a TLLLFR motif, together with TT8, suppresses flavonoid metabolism and trichome development (Huang et al. 2019). These R3‐MYB repressors can participate in the regulation of the MBW complex, but they function by competing with activating R2R3‐MYB proteins for binding to bHLH partners (Li, Shan, et al. 2020).

Post‐translational protein modification has been reported to regulate the function of R2R3‐MYB transcription factors in plant development and secondary metabolic pathways (Li et al. 2006). Generally, alternative splicing and PTMs occur in intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), thereby leading to a modification of the associated interactome (Romero et al. 2006; Buljan et al. 2013; Niklas et al. 2015). In Pinus taeda , PtMYB4 is phosphorylated by PtMAPK6 on serine 236, located in the C‐terminal activation domain. Modification of this target serine did not alter DNA binding in vitro but did alter the ability of PtMYB4 to promote transcriptional activation in yeast (Morse et al. 2009). In tea plants, the mitogen‐activated protein kinase CsMPK3‐2 was shown to phosphorylate Ser 181, occurring within the C2 repression domain of CsMYB4a, and rescues the transcriptional inhibitory activity of CsMYB4a by decreasing its ability to inhibit key phenylpropanoid pathway genes, including PAL, C4H and 4CL (Ma, Han, et al. 2025).

MYB–bHLH–WD40 (MBW) Complex and Higher‐Order Regulation

MYB proteins frequently require the support of co‐factors, such as basic helix–loop–helix (bHLH) and WD40 repeat (WDR), to fully activate pathway genes (Figure 1C). The MBW transcriptional complex has traditionally been viewed as a static ternary structure in which an R2R3‐MYB, a bHLH factor such as GLABRA3 (GL3), ENHANCER OF GLABRA3 (EGL3), or TT8, and a WD40‐repeat protein TRANSPARENT TESTA GLABRA 1 (TTG1) are present. The MBW complex is highly dynamic with respect to phosphorylation‐mediated modulation, alternative dimer formation, and reversible assembly despite the high conservation and structural definition of the MYB‐bHLH binding motif [D/E]Lx2[R/K]x3Lx6Lx3R (Zimmermann et al. 2004; Lloyd et al. 2017). The formation and function of the MBW complex are regulated by environmental factors, hormones, metabolic status, post‐translational modifications and protein competition (Hill 2015; Sakr et al. 2018; Gil‐muñoz et al. 2020).

Under normal conditions, PRODUCTION OF ANTHOCYANIN PIGMENT 1 (PAP1, MYB75), and TT2 can form complexes with bHLH partners to regulate the biosynthetic pathways of anthocyanins or proanthocyanidins in Arabidopsis (Gonzalez et al. 2008; Lim et al. 2022). However, stress‐responsive proteins are often inhibitors that mimic MBW subunits to inhibit stable complex formation. During abiotic stress, ZmSRO1e interacts with ZmPL1 (MYB), instead of ZmR1 (bHLH), preventing the formation of the MBW complex and repressing genes for anthocyanin biosynthesis such as ZmA4, thereby reducing the capacity for ROS scavenging leading to increased stress sensitivity (Qin et al. 2021). Besides, transcription factors such as APETALA2 (AP2) also elevate the competition level by forming a repressor complex with MYBL2 and TT8, which interfere with the correct assembling of MBW and inhibit the expression of proanthocyanidin (PA) genes, ANR and TT12, in seed endothelium (Jiang et al. 2024). MYBL2 also acts as a competitive inhibitor by replacing activating MYBs such as PAP1, MYB11, GL1 and TT2, to bind bHLH partners in a similar manner (Dubos et al. 2008; Matsui et al. 2008). This conserved repressive mechanism is exemplified by BrMYB32 in Chinese cabbage, which regulate anthocyanin accumulation through interaction with BrTT8 and inhibits MBW complex assembly (Lim et al. 2024). The mechanism of such repressors has been further refined. In poplar, MYB182 shows differential regulatory outcomes depending on its interaction with distinct bHLH partners (bHLH079 or bHLH131), indicating partner‐specific effects (Yoshida et al. 2015). Beyond secondary metabolism, MBW‐associated MYB complexes also participate in heat stress signalling. In Arabidopsis, the MBW components TT8 and TTG1 together with MYB5 and TT2 bind the SIIE element of the HSFA2 promoter, thereby activating HSFA2 and downstream heat shock protein (HSP) genes involved in thermotolerance (Jacob et al. 2021). However, hormonal signals (GA, JA and ABA) integrate developmental and stress stimuli to accurately govern the assembly and disassembly of the MBW complex, evolving it from a static compositional model to a dynamic equilibrium.

Hormonal Regulation of MBW Activity

Facilitatory functions are more indirect; however, as they require the hormone‐mediated activation of the MBW complex through the sequestration of repressors. MBW regulation is involved in ethylene signalling, but effects depend on the context. ETHYLENE‐INSENSITIVE 3 (EIN3) inhibits the interaction of TTG1 with GL3 and the activation of GL2, thus inhibiting the formation of the MBW complex (WER‐GL3‐TTG1) required for the determination of non‐hair cell fate in Arabidopsis. EIN3 promotes hair elongation through RSL4, which inhibits GL2 and ectopic root hair formation by ethylene (Qiu et al. 2021). Thus, EIN3/EIL1 remodels epidermal patterning by repression of a transcriptional complex maintaining non‐hair identity. More complex repression by ethylene in pears is mediated by the transcription factor PpERF9. PpPERF9 can repress the anthocyanin activator gene PpMYB114 and its upstream regulator PpRAP2.4 by recruiting the corepressor PpTPL1 and promoting histone deacetylation at their promoters (Ni et al. 2023).

The regulatory accuracy of MYB‐mediated jasmonic acid responses is attained through the dynamic regulation of MBW complex formation and functionality. JAZ family proteins compete with activating MYBs for binding to GL3, EGL3 and TT8; thus, JAZ proteins sequester the bHLH factors, preventing their interaction with MYB partners (Qi et al. 2011; Song et al. 2022). In apple, the JA‐mediated accumulation of proanthocyanidins and anthocyanins is dynamically regulated by the JAZ1‐TRB1‐MYB9 complex, illustrating species‐specific variations in MYB‐JAZ regulatory circuits (An et al. 2021). JAZ repressor proteins, such as JAZ1/8/11, obstruct late biosynthesis genes (LBGs) associated with flavonoid synthesis by competitively hindering MBW complex formation through the sequestration of bHLH partners (Shi et al. 2023) (Figure 2). Ethylene‐responsive ERF transcription factors MdERF1B directly activate the promoters of MYC2 and R2R3‐MYB (MYB1/9/11) to initiate anthocyanin production. MdJAZ5/10 repressors inhibit MdERF1B transcriptional activity in low JA conditions. JA induces ubiquitination and degradation of JAZ proteins, releasing MdERF1B to activate MYC2 and R2R3‐MYB genes and promotes formation of the MBW complex and anthocyanin biosynthesis (Wang, Li, et al. 2022).

Integration of SA, JA, ABA, and ET signalling pathways in plant defence. By using transcription factors and regulatory proteins to activate the expression of PR genes, salicylic acid (SA) plays a crucial part in systemic acquired resistance. When SA level is low, ABA can suppress the expression of NPR1 and reduce lignin accumulation. SA‐associated ROS strengthen pathogen defences, and ABA‐associated ROS control stomatal closure and drought responses. JA‐receptor targets JAZ degradation, and activates downstream of JA‐responsive gene. On the other hand, NPR1 suppresses pathogen virulence by interacting with JA‐mediated MYC2. Through ERF1‐driven PDF1.2, JA and ET work in concert to provide a pathogen defence response. DELLA proteins involved in gibberellin signalling physically interact with and sequester MYBL2, a competitive inhibitor of the MBW complex and jasmonate ZIM‐domain (JAZ) repressors, freeing bHLH and MYB subunits to assemble an active complex and thereby promoting anthocyanin accumulation under abiotic stress conditions (Howe and Jander 2008; Qi et al. 2014; Xie et al. 2016). Under low GA conditions, DELLAs accumulate and function as molecular sinks for repressors, thereby promoting anthocyanin accumulation. Ultimately, jasmonate (JA) signalling degrades JAZ and reverses inhibition of the SCF (COI1) proteasome pathway, thereby releasing bHLH to engage with MYB partner (Pauwels and Goossens 2011). Following jasmonic acid (JA) accumulation, the degradation of JAZ proteins alleviates this inhibition, allowing for MBW complex activity and the subsequent upregulation of LBGs. During wound healing in poplar, the degradation of JAZ1 liberates transcriptional activators such as the MYB118‐TT8 complex, leading to increased expression of anthocyanin biosynthetic genes (ABGs) (Wang et al. 2020). This inhibition is also potentiated by kinase regulation. Stress‐responsive kinases (SnRK1) sense the depletion of carbon/energy and initiate multi‐level repression to inhibit the formation of MBW. SnRK1 directly phosphorylates MYB75/PAP1, TT8 and TTG1, leading to the release of the MBW complex, nuclear export of TTG1, and ubiquitin‐mediated degradation of MYB75, leading to inhibition of the binding of the structural genes such as DFR and UFGT to promoters (Figure 3A) (Broucke et al. 2023). This results in downregulation of anthocyanins, which are a marker of the cells redirecting resources from secondary metabolism to survival mode under stress.

MYB Transcription Factors as Integrative Nodes in Stress‐Hormone Crosstalk Network

Plant stress responses are inherently non‐linear and result from highly interconnected signalling networks rather than isolated linear pathways (Raffaele et al. 2006; Thaler et al. 2012; Zheng et al. 2019; Xiao, Gong, et al. 2021; Seth et al. 2025). During drought, plants arbitrate between competing demands, as stress does not simply activate ABA signalling but instead triggers coordinated interactions among multiple pathways (Ren et al. 2023; Fang et al. 2024; Das et al. 2025). The molecular basis of this integration lies in transcriptional regulators, particularly MYB factors, that process multi‐hormone input and produce context‐dependent outputs (Park et al. 2010; Yu et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2026). We organize our analysis around a hub‐and‐node hierarchy: MYB96 and MYB30 serve as central hubs integrating multiple stress pathways, while secondary factors and accessory nodes provide specialized inputs.

Major MYB Hubs: Central Integrators of Multi‐Hormone Signalling

The hormones induce a variety of adaptive responses at different levels of interconnectedness, including the closure of stomata to reduce water loss through transpiration, the transcriptional activation of networks of stress‐responsive genes and the metabolic reprogramming that establishes cellular tolerance (Yang and Benning 2017; Lu et al. 2020; Hrmova and Hussain 2021; Aslam et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2022; Zhang, Chen, et al. 2024).

MYB96 is proposed to be a major regulator of ABA signalling under drought stress by accumulating cuticular waxes and modifying root architecture, thereby limiting transpiration and ensuring water uptake (Seo et al. 2011). TaMYB96‐2D controls the biosynthesis of diketone wax components such as TaDMC, which increase glaucousness and decrease water loss under drought stress (Li et al. 2026). AtMYB96 interacts with the histone deacetylase HDA15 to establish a repressor complex that directly binds to the promoters of ROP GTPase genes (ROP6, ROP10 and ROP11), promoting the deacetylation of histone H3 and H4 at the transcriptional level. Thus, repression of negative regulators of ABA signalling leads to a repressive chromatin configuration that improves the ABA response (Lee et al. 2019). In addition, AtMYB96 integrates ABA‐responsive signalling and metabolic reprogramming to regulate carbon and lipid metabolism to maintain cellular functions and enhance drought tolerance (Lee et al. 2019). Triacylglycerols (TAGs), mainly stored in seeds, can also be synthesized in vegetative tissues under stress conditions as an alternative energy source when the primary carbon uptake is inhibited (Yang and Benning 2017; Lu et al. 2020). Such metabolic stability is conducive to the maintenance of cellular homeostasis during stomatal closure and allows developmental modifications for stress adaptation. AtMYB96 directly regulates ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE4 (ABI4) expression, which inhibits lipid breakdown in the embryo and therefore controls seed germination in the presence of ABA, which allows optimal seedling establishment under stress (Lee et al. 2015). MYB96 bridges pathways of abiotic and biotic stress beyond the ABA‐centred responses. In Arabidopsis, AtMYB96 acts as a regulatory hub that integrates ABA signalling and pathogen defence through the up‐regulation of SALICYLIC ACID INDUCTION DEFICIENT2 (SID2/ICS1), which leads to increased accumulation of salicylic acid and downstream pathogenesis‐related gene expression, thus enhancing disease resistance (Seo and Park 2010). Therefore, MYB96 integrates drought stress via ABA signalling, suppresses carbon uptake, regulates development, promotes seed germination under stress and modulates salicylic acid–mediated defence responses.

MYB30 is a master regulator that coordinates major metabolic reprogramming to accommodate stress adaptation. MYB30 is involved in SA‐triggered defence responses that are independent of the Nonexpressor of Pathogenesis‐Related Genes 1 (NPR1) (Raffaele et al. 2006, 2008). MYB30 interacts with the active form of phytochromes, PhyA and PhyB, in light signalling. It also interacts with EIN3 to regulate ROOT HAIR DEFECTIVE SIX‐LIKE4 (RSL4) expression to affect root hair development through the formation of an ethylene‐induced MYB30–EIN3 complex (Xiao, Gong, et al. 2021). MYB30 activity is tightly regulated at the post‐translational level. MYB30‐Interacting E3 Ligase 1 (MIEL1), a RING‐type E3 ligase from Arabidopsis, inhibits the activity of MYB30 by promoting its ubiquitination and degradation to restrict the activity of MYB30 under non‐stressed conditions. Under ABA or stress conditions, MYB30 and ABI5 are stabilized and MIEL1 levels decrease. This stabilization supports ABA signalling, which is required for proper seed germination and seedling establishment (Zheng et al. 2012; Nie et al. 2022). During infection, MIEL1 is downregulated while MYB30 is stabilized to trigger an alternative hypersensitive response (Marino et al. 2013). Nuclear import of MYB30 during infection requires the importin β‐like protein sensitive to ABA and drought2 (SAD2). Functional deficiency of SAD2 reduces nuclear localization and increases susceptibility to Pseudomonas syringae (Shi et al. 2024). In addition to hormonal crosstalk, MYB30 coordinates stress responses across multiple stress contexts. Under the submergence response, MYB30 directly inhibits the expression of 1‐aminocyclopropane‐1‐carboxylic acid synthase 7 (ACS7) to repress ethylene synthesis and avoid excessive accumulation of ethylene. Conversely, loss‐of‐function myb30 mutants exhibit excessive ethylene accumulation and are sensitive to waterlogging and also more susceptible to pathogens (Zhang, Xie, et al. 2023). And studies in diverse plants suggest that the MYB30 function in hormone crosstalk is conserved in species. In tomato, SlMIEL1 associates with a MYB30 homologue to promote its ubiquitination and subsequent degradation. In addition, a NAC transcription factor, SlNAC72, was identified as a negative regulator of JA biosynthesis. SlNAC72 degradation by SlMIEL1 facilitates the expression of JA biosynthesis genes, including SlLOX10, SlAOS1 and SlAOC. Therefore, the accumulation of JA is increased to improve high‐temperature tolerance by reducing the levels of ROS (Meng et al. 2026). In addition, OsMYB30 activates the expression of lignin biosynthesis genes (4CL3 and 4CL5) and salicylic acid biosynthesis genes (OsPAL6 and OsPAL8) in rice, enhancing cell walls and resistance to rice blast disease and brown planthopper infestation (Kishi‐Kaboshi et al. 2018; Li, Wang, et al. 2020; Sun et al. 2024). MYB30 also orchestrates metabolic reprogramming by co‐repressing the anthocyanin pathway and co‐activating the biosynthesis of defence compounds. In yellowhorn, XsMYB30 induces wax biosynthesis genes and lipid transfer proteins in epidermal cells and a large deposition of epicuticular wax. An integrated drought resistance mechanism integrating transcriptional control with metabolic adjustments has implicated MYB30‐mediated deposition of surface wax in controlling stomatal activity to minimize non‐stomatal water loss, linking ABA signalling to drought adaptation (Liu, Ban, et al. 2024). This combination of regulatory and metabolic functions results in different phenotypes.

MYB functions as a single transcription factor and as part of higher‐order complexes, thereby increasing its integrative capacity. MYB4‐type repressors (AtMYB4, AtMYB7 and AtMYB32) compete with activator MYBs for bHLH partners to repress phenylpropanoid pathway genes, allowing for a dynamic adjustment of metabolism under environmental change (Jin et al. 2000). This MBW complex functions as a co‐regulator of secondary metabolism in response to stress. Flavonoid‐mediated stress tolerance, however, is context‐dependent. MYB4 also downregulates 4CL and FLS and blocks the formation of the MBW complex, which results in a reduction of flavonoid‐dependent photoprotection and possibly impacts ROS homeostasis during heat stress (Banerjee et al. 2024). On the contrary, activators like MYB12 positively regulate flavonoid biosynthesis and promote resistance to UV, heat and other stress (Lin et al. 2021). Furthermore, MYB4 is involved in stress tolerance by regulating the ROS‐scavenging enzymes (POD, SOD) and interacting with LpGPX6 to maintain ion homeostasis under saline‐alkaline stress (Zhang, Zhang, et al. 2024). The activity of MYB4 appears to be epigenetically regulated. For example, salt stress up‐regulated MsMYB4, which was associated with low DNA methylation and high levels of activating histone marks (H3K4me3 and H3K9ac), which could enable fast transcriptional reprogramming (Dong and Lin 2021). In Arabidopsis, importin‐β‐like protein (GIR) is necessary for nuclear accumulation of AtMYB4, and modification of AtMYB4 induces growth phenotypes but does not rescue the phenylpropanoid imbalance, suggesting that MYB4‐dependent growth regulation is partially independent of its role in metabolic repression (Panda et al. 2020). Functional divergence of MYB4‐type factors occurs in different species. MbMYB4 enhances the antioxidant capacity and osmotic adjustment to improve the drought tolerance of apple. However, VcMYB4a with EAR repression motif represses stress‐responsive genes and increases sensitivities to drought and salt stresses (Yao et al. 2022). In addition, PvMYB4 negatively regulated the genes involved in lignin biosynthesis in switchgrass, thus decreasing lignin content and enhancing saccharification efficiency without affecting plant growth in switchgrass (Liu et al. 2018). Similarly, TaMYB4 negatively regulates cold tolerance in wheat by binding to the promoters of TaCBF family genes and repressing their expression (Liu, Jiang, et al. 2026). Depending on the context, MYB4 functions as a repressor or activation factor, maintaining a dynamic equilibrium between growth and stress adaptation.

Secondary MYB Integrators: Distributed and Context‐Specific Nodes

MYB‐Mediated Integration of Hormonal Pathways During Stress Responses

The wheat R2R3‐TaMYB29 integrates multiple hormone signals (SA, JA, ABA and ET) to enhance resistance to stripe rust infection. TaMYB29 enhances hypersensitive response (HR) and resistance through promoting H2O2 accumulation, increasing the expression of PR genes and inducing SA‐dependent signalling (Figure 3B). Knockdown of TaMYB29 compromises immunity and decreases oxidative burst, confirming that TaMYB29 functions as a positive regulator of hormone‐mediated immunity (Zhu et al. 2021). SA‐responsive transcription factors directly activate PR genes and control programs of HR for amplification of defence signalling. ROS accumulation triggers the activation of gene networks controlling cellular responses from growth inhibition to pathogen resistance. For instance, pathogen infection and high temperature induce TaMYB73, which upregulates SA‐responsive defence genes, including TaPR1 and TaPR2 and enhances SA‐mediated defence signalling in wheat (Liu et al. 2025). MYB factors regulate whole‐plant physiology. AtMYB68 overexpression enhances ABA sensitivity and reduces water loss by transpiration to contribute to increased yield under combined heat and drought stress (Deng et al. 2020). Poplar MYB94 improves drought tolerance by ROS scavenging and regulation of ABA signalling pathway (Fang et al. 2020). TaMYB33 overexpression confers drought tolerance in wheat through ABA‐mediated signalling pathways of osmotic adjustment and ROS scavenging (Qin et al. 2012). MYBs are involved in structural adaptation under stress conditions. For instance, CgMYB58 is involved in stress‐induced structural modifications, as ABA treatment upregulates the expression of lignin biosynthesis genes (PAL, 4CL and C3H) in pummelo and indole‐3‐acetic acid (IAA) treatment downregulates the expression, suggesting that CgMYB58 is a node of integration for antagonistic hormone signals that regulate cell wall fortification (Shi et al. 2020). Similarly, FvMYB82 modulates multiple abiotic stresses via stress‐inducible expression that induces both drought‐responsive genes (AtNCED3 and AtSnRK2.4/2.6) and cold‐responsive targets (AtCBF1/2, AtCOR15a and AtCOR78) and increases antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD and CAT) expression and proline accumulation (Zhang, Li, Zhang, and Li 2020; Li et al. 2022) (Figure 4). This suggests that MYB controls stress responses conserved among species.

MYB‐Mediated Regulation of Cellular Homeostasis in Abiotic Stress Conditions

MYB transcription factors are also involved in the regulation of ionic homeostasis and redox balance under salt stress. Under drought and salt stress, IbMYB48 co‐activated ROS‐scavenging and NCED3 (ABA biosynthesis) genes. ABA enhanced osmotic adjustment and antioxidant capacity, associating hormone accumulation with redox balance (Zhao, Zhao, et al. 2022). FvMYB24 directly binds to the promoter of AtSOS1 and promotes Na+ extrusion to improve the tolerance of plants to salt stress. Moreover, FvMYB24 enhances the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT), which in turn inhibit the accumulation of ROS and reduce the damage of cells caused by ionic toxicity (Wang, Shi, et al. 2021). MdMYB108L shows similar ion‐centric behaviour, indicating that its N‐terminal transcriptional domain is activated under salt stress and binds directly to the NHX1 promoter to prioritize vacuolar Na+ sequestration. This protects photosynthetic machinery and secondarily reduces ROS by restoring cytoplasmic ionic balance (Du et al. 2022). Similarly, BnaMYB73 is a good strategy for tolerance via broad‐spectrum multi‐module activation. Co‐activation of sodium extrusion (AtSOS1), antioxidant defence (AtCAT1), osmolyte biosynthesis (AtP5CS1) and stress‐responsive proteins (AtRD29B, AtDREB2A and AtRAB18) in parallel, rather than sequentially, to salt and osmotic stress shows no one module is sufficient alone (Wang et al. 2026). Therefore, MYB‐mediated regulation of Na+ extrusion, ROS scavenging and osmolyte production is important for salt tolerance. Conversely, FtMYB22 from Tartary buckwheat interacts with ABA receptor components and represses ABA signalling, stomatal closure and osmotic adjustment. Disruption of these ABA‐dependent pathways leads to decreased expression of genes involved in proline synthesis and antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and POD. Therefore, the plant encountered reduced tolerance to drought and salinity (Zhao, Yao, et al. 2022). These results reveal that the MYB‐mediated stress responses are dependent on the balance of the network rather than the activation of a single gene (Figure 4). Implicitly, MYBs control positive regulation through ROS defence, osmolyte synthesis and ABA signalling, whereas negative regulation occurs through repression of protective genes.

MYB‐Mediated Integration of Developmental and Environmental Signals

A further extension of this notion is that MYBs responding to multiple environmental cues tend to regulate overlapping downstream networks. FvMYB108 may also play a role in developmental processes such as root or leaf development, given its higher expression in young leaves and roots. Under cold stress, FvMYB108 upregulates cold‐tolerance genes such as AtCBF1, AtCOR47, AtERD10 and AtDREB1A through the CBF signalling pathway. Under salt stress, it promotes salt‐responsive genes AtCCA1, AtRD29a, AtSnRK2.4 and AtP5CS1 through the ABA‐dependent pathway. Therefore, FvMYB108 may regulate cold and salt responses through distinct cis‐elements or via different signalling pathways, the CBF pathway for cold and the ABA pathway for salt (Song et al. 2024). Some MYB transcription factors regulate targets for crop improvement by conferring resistance to pathogens and tolerance to nutritional quality or abiotic stress. In Gossypium hirsutum , GhMYB36 upregulates PR1 expression and enhances resistance to Verticillium wilt and drought stress. The silencing of GhMYB36 showed increased malondialdehyde (MDA) content and decreased total antioxidant activity (T‐AOC) in comparison with the control plants. In contrast, plants over‐expressing GhMYB36 showed lower MDA levels and higher T‐AOC activity (Liu et al. 2022). In tomato, SIMYB1 binds to the promoters of lycopene synthesis genes and PR genes, enhancing both lycopene content and resistance to Botrytis cinerea, and improving fruit shape to reduce mechanical damage (Yin et al. 2023). Likewise, BpMYB4 in birch enhances stress tolerance by elevating proline levels and reducing H2O2 and O2 − accumulation, thereby protecting membranes from lipid peroxidation and lowering MDA content (Yu et al. 2021) (Figure 4). This improvement maintained the cell membrane integrity and water balance under drought stress and therefore improved their antioxidant activities.

Flavonoids help plants respond to biotic and abiotic stresses and have a protective role in the maintenance of cellular redox homeostasis (Nakabayashi et al. 2014). The DELLAs (RGA/GAI) proteins were shown to interact with MYB12 in Arabidopsis by facilitating MYB12 binding to promoters of target genes in low gibberellic acid (GA). Flavonol accumulation is inversely correlated with auxin levels in the root tip (Zhao et al. 2023). MYB12‐mediated flavonol biosynthesis inhibits root development. Loss of MYB12 partially rescues the ga1‐3 short‐root phenotype, and exogenous quercetin rescues root meristem size, confirming the developmental importance of this pathway (Tan et al. 2019). MYB12 regulates tobacco metabolic routes by upregulating genes encoding fatty acid‐degrading enzymes, NtLOX6, NtLOX5, NtSFAR4 and NtGDSL2. Sucrose signalling induces NtMYB12 expression, linking the regulation of lipid and flavonoid metabolic pathways to carbohydrate content (Wang, Wang, et al. 2021; Zhang, Zhang, et al. 2023). Thus, MYB12 functions as a dual regulator of lipid and flavonoid metabolism, and it suggests that MYB transcription factors regulate different metabolic pathways.

Anthocyanins are produced by plants in response to stress, such as oxidative stress or high light levels. JA promotes light‐dependent anthocyanin accumulation by the phyA pathway, in which phyA stabilizes AtMYB75, and COP1 is a negative regulator in the dark to form a phyA‐COP1‐MYB75 module (Li et al. 2014). Moreover, ABA enhances anthocyanin biosynthesis by destabilizing the HAT1–MYB75 interaction, which releases the HAT1–TPL‐mediated repression and restores the MBW complex activity (Zheng et al. 2018). Moreover, its activity is well regulated by negative regulators. MYBL2 represses AtMYB75 in high light and establishes a feedback loop to avoid overaccumulation of anthocyanin (Xing et al. 2024). Thus, AtMYB75 acts as an integrative regulatory hub connecting perception of environmental stresses to hormonal signalling and transcriptional control of anthocyanin biosynthesis to enhance ROS scavenging and stress adaptation (Figure 4).

Role of MYB Transcription Factors in Redirecting Resources Toward Defence

The phenylpropanoid‐based defence is under balanced management since the constitutive activation can be detrimental and is therefore under strict control by positive and negative mechanisms to guarantee an appropriate investment in metabolism for pathogen stress. In poplar, MYB115, a part of the MYB‐bHLH‐WD40 complex, directly regulates genes involved in PA biosynthesis. Overexpression of MYB115 increased PA accumulation and resistance to Dothiorella gregaria, while CRISPR/Cas9 mutants showed lower PA levels and susceptibility to pathogen infection (Wang et al. 2017) (Figure 3B). On the contrary, NtMYB308, a transcriptional repressor, represses the biosynthesis of anthocyanin and lignin in tobacco. CRISPR deletion lines of NtMYB308 showed increased levels of phenylpropanoid metabolites and increased resistance to fungal infections such as Alternaria solani, while overexpression lines had decreased levels (Singh et al. 2026).

The increased secondary cell walls enhance the hydrophobicity of lignin and reduce water loss under drought stress and water permeability. It also provides a mechanical barrier against pathogen invasion (Miedes et al. 2014; Yang, Zhong, et al. 2021; Lv et al. 2023). Besides MYB46, MYB85 regulates metabolic flow and activates genes of lignin biosynthesis and phenylalanine precursor pathways. Disruption of MYB85 along with MYB20, MYB42 and MYB43 results in growth defects and reduced lignin accumulation and impaired SCW formation (Geng et al. 2020). This regulatory function is evolutionarily conserved and can be used for metabolic engineering. Expression of NbMYB85 in tobacco functions as an early switch for the activation of lignin biosynthesis and an increase of precursor supply for phenylpropanoid‐derived compounds. The increased deposition of lignin and cellulose upon overexpression of MYB85 in bamboo also supports the role of MYB transcription factors in the strengthening of cell walls (Zhang, Li, Zhang, Ying, et al. 2020). In Arabidopsis, AtMYB46 binds to a cis‐element in the promoter of Ep5C, which encodes a type III cell wall‐bound peroxidase. Knockdown of MYB46 increases disease resistance to Botrytis cinerea, accompanied by transcriptional reprogramming of cell wall protein genes, including extracellular type III peroxidases. MYB46 links cell wall remodelling with defence activation, and its absence increases resistance by modifying cell wall integrity and secondary defence responses (Ramı'rez et al. 2011). However, disease resistance is restricted to specific types of pathogens and certain physiological conditions. BnMYB43 is involved in lignin biosynthesis and vascular development in Brassica napus . Silencing of BnMYB43 by RNA interference led to decreased lignin deposition and increased resistance to Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, indicating a growth‐defence trade‐off (Figure 4) (Jiang et al. 2020). Such findings suggest MYB proteins are essentially a common toolkit that plants use to beef up their cell walls in the face of stress.

MAPK‐Dependent Modulation of MYB Function

Phosphorylation predominantly influences the stability and activation of transcription factors, rather than their specificity for DNA binding, by altering certain phospho‐acceptor sites (Table 2). In PAMP‐triggered immunity, the activation of MPK3 and MPK6 initiates a signalling cascade. This leads to the transient phosphorylation of MYB44 in Arabidopsis, after which phosphorylated MYB44 subsequently activates downstream defence genes and triggers the expression of EIN2 (Wang, Li, et al. 2023) (Figure 3B). In addition, VIP1, a bZIP transcription factor targeted by MPK3, modulates MYB44 activity in a stress‐specific way during osmotic stress in Arabidopsis (Persak and Pitzschke 2013). Similarly, MPK4 phosphorylates MYB75 during light‐induced anthocyanin biosynthesis, thereby increasing MYB75 stability, which enhances anthocyanin accumulation in Arabidopsis (Li et al. 2016). Consistent with this, transient overexpression experiments demonstrated that MdMPK4 is essential for light‐induced anthocyanin accumulation by enabling optimal MdMYB1 function (Yang, Ma, et al. 2021). Taken together, these findings indicate that MAPK‐mediated MYB phosphorylation is predominantly stress‐induced rather than being constitutive, with distinct MAPK cascades activated by various stresses to phosphorylate specific subsets of MYB transcription factors. However, MPK6 demonstrates constitutive activity in developing xylem, where it sustains phosphorylation of lignin biosynthesis‐associated MYB46, leading to site‐specific phosphorylation‐dependent degradation of MYB46 by the ubiquitin‐mediated proteasome pathway to regulate secondary cell wall biosynthesis in plants (Wang and Gou 2020; Xiao, Zhang, et al. 2021). This dual functionality enables MAPKs to execute both stress‐response and housekeeping roles.

Phosphorylation also influences MYB involvement in the MBW complex by influencing bHLH and WD40 interactions (Li et al. 2016; Yan et al. 2021; Broucke et al. 2023). Under low brassinosteroid conditions, BIN2, GSK3‐like kinase, phosphorylates TTG1 and bHLH proteins like EGL3, thereby disrupting their interactions with other components of the complex. This phosphorylation impedes the assembly and function of the WER‐GL3/EGL3‐TTG1 (MBW) transcriptional complex, thereby limiting its formation and ultimately fostering root hair cell differentiation (Cheng et al. 2014). Furthermore, phosphorylation affects competitive MYB interactions. In Arabidopsis, AtMYB30 physically interacts with AtMYB75, and it inhibits AtMYB75 incorporation into the MBW complex, thereby anthocyanin biosynthesis is suppressed. Under elevated sucrose conditions, AtMYB75‐mediated expression of RHA2b induces AtMYB30 degradation. This liberates AtMYB75 to enhance the expression and promote anthocyanin biosynthesis (Zhou et al. 2024) (Figure 3A). Individual MYB proteins can also be phosphorylated by various kinases at specific amino acid residues. MYB75 undergoes phosphorylation by MPK4 which is activated by light and cold stress, and stabilizes the protein through phosphorylation of C‐terminal serine residues. Under light, cold, or oxidative stress, MPK4 becomes activated and phosphorylates MYB75 in Arabidopsis. This phosphorylation takes place at C‐terminal serine residues and also at Thr126 and Thr131. Phosphorylation on Thr126 and Thr131 prevented the binding of the COP1 E3 ligase to MYB75 and thus its proteasomal degradation. In contrast, MYB75 that is not phosphorylated interacts strongly with the WD40 domain of COP1 and is degraded as a result. Thus, MPK4‐mediated phosphorylation stabilized the MYB75 protein, which subsequently led to increased anthocyanin accumulation under light stress and contributed to the plant's response to cold and oxidative stress conditions (Li et al. 2016).

Cold stress induces dynamic post‐translational modifications, particularly ubiquitination, which fine‐tune the timing and intensity of cold‐responsive gene expression (Xie et al. 2025). Under cold stress conditions, the U‐box E3 ligases PUB25 and PUB26 modify key components of the cold signalling pathway to precisely regulate CBF pathway activation. For example, they catalyse the addition of both K48‐ and K63‐linked ubiquitin chains to ICE1, thereby modulating its stability and abundance at different stages of the cold response. In addition, PUB25 and PUB26 also ubiquitinate AtMYB15, a transcriptional repressor of CBF genes, thereby influencing its regulatory function during cold stress (Wang, Lv, et al. 2022; Wang, Zhang, et al. 2023). The function of MYB15 is modulated by MPK6‐mediated phosphorylation at Ser168. Phosphorylation induced by MPK6 diminished the binding affinity of MYB15 to the CBF3 promoter, while the mutation of its phosphorylation site (MYB15S168A) augments the transcriptional repression of CBF3 by MYB15 in Arabidopsis (Figure 3A) (Kim et al. 2017). It shows that phosphorylation can influence both protein stability and DNA‐binding capability.

AtMYB30 is controlled by a dynamic switch in post‐translational modification of lysine 283 (K283) that is required for both SUMOylation and ubiquitination. AtMYB30 is stabilized by SIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation, but RHA2b, an E3 ligase, mediates its ubiquitination for proteasomal degradation, thus forming a dynamic post‐translational switch to regulate ABA responses (Zheng et al. 2018). This modification increases the activity of AtMYB30 on AOX1a, which is involved in alternative respiration and cellular redox homeostasis, and therefore promotes the adaptation of plants to salt stress conditions (Gong et al. 2020). In addition, AaMYB31 directly activates AaKCS2 under cold stress, improving kiwifruit's ability to withstand freezing and produce wax. By interacting with AaMYB31, AaSIZ1 facilitates its SUMOylation, which enhances protein stability and promotes the deposition of cuticular wax in cold environments (Liu, Cao, et al. 2026). Similarly, MdMYB30 is stabilized by MdSIZ1‐mediated SUMOylation, which inhibits its proteasomal breakdown. The resulting MdMYB30 accumulation promotes MdKCS1 expression, increasing cuticular wax buildup under stress (Zhang, Tian, et al. 2023). Additionally, MYB‐mediated anthocyanin accumulation during cold adaptation is supported by SUMOylation. The SUMO E3 ligase MdSIZ1 SUMOylates MdMYB1 in apples, shielding it from ubiquitin‐mediated degradation and maintaining its role in cold tolerance and anthocyanin biosynthesis (Jiang et al. 2022). MdMYB2 directly activates the MdSIZ1 promoter at low temperatures, boosting the expression of SUMO E3 ligase and strengthening the SUMOylation of downstream targets like MdMYB1 (Zhou et al. 2017; Jiang et al. 2022). These mechanisms improve the signalling networks that respond to cold at multiple layers to control the activity of other regulatory proteins. Additionally linking SUMOylation to anthocyanin‐related stress adaptation, analogous positive regulation has been observed for MYB75, whose stability and functionality are enhanced by SIZ1‐dependent SUMOylation (Zheng et al. 2020). Collectively, these findings indicate that SUMOylation enhances abiotic stress tolerance through various regulatory mechanisms.

MYB proteins transition between repressor and activator states through phosphorylation‐dependent functional switches, signifying heightened regulatory complexity. The transcription factor LTF1 functions as a repressor of lignin biosynthesis. Under non‐stress conditions, non‐phosphorylated LTF1 binds to the 4CL promoter and inhibits transcription. In response to wounding, PdMPK6 phosphorylates LTF1, facilitating its degradation through the proteasome. The elimination of this repressor initiates the lignin biosynthetic pathway (Gui et al. 2019). Collectively, the phosphorylation of MYB transcription factors mediated by MAPK occurs under specific stress conditions, affecting their protein stability and transcriptional activity. Phosphorylation frequently modifies MYB interactions with bHLH and WD40 partners, consequently influencing MBW complex functionality and subsequent gene expression.

Future Perspectives and Research Directions

In conclusion, MYB transcription factors have emerged as key mediators of metabolic reprogramming and the integration of multiple hormonal stress signals. We developed a conceptual model based on our study and the literature review, wherein MYB transcription factors serve as central integrators, precisely regulating metabolic flow through the reception of inputs from MAPK cascades and hormone signalling (Figure 5). Furthermore, their regulatory impact is amplified through protein–protein interactions with bHLH partners, post‐translational modifications, and epigenetic recruitment of chromatin modifiers. Despite decades of studies describing the functions of MYB in secondary metabolism, the molecular processes by which they function as key hubs for integrating different hormonal and environmental signals are still evolving. We also propose significant questions for future exploration.

Although part of the same extensive gene family, various MYBs demonstrate significantly divergent regulatory functions, encompassing both general stress tolerance and specialized, pathway‐oriented responses. The fundamental reasons for this diversity of function remain poorly understood. Future study ought to employ comparative structural and functional genomics among various plant species to comprehensively identify conserved and variable residues, protein domains, or interaction interfaces that govern target gene selection. Deciphering these molecular ‘codes’ would significantly enhance the capacity to anticipate MYB function in the program and to engineer transcriptional regulators for targeted crop development.

For many stress‐responsive MYBs, the precise upstream sensors and signalling cascades that lead to their activation (e.g., post‐translational modifications like SUMOylation or phosphorylation) remain opaque. Identifying the kinases, phosphatases, or E3 ligases that directly modify MYB proteins in response to drought, cold, or pathogen attack will move our understanding from the process level to the mechanistic level and reveal the precise wiring of stress signal transduction.

How do stress‐responsive post‐translational modifications (PTMs) of MYB transcription factors regulate the assembly dynamics of the MBW complex and subsequent binding to target promoters? While static analyses have shown that MYB proteins are phosphorylated and undergo other PTMs, the real‐time effects of these on complex formation and DNA binding are not clear. Future studies should employ live‐cell imaging and dynamic tracking of protein–protein interactions using phospho‐mimetic and phospho‐dead mutants of key MYBs. Such strategies would allow defining the impacts of individual PTMs on MBW complex assembly, nuclear localization and promoter occupancy under stress conditions.

Recent studies have elucidated the action of MYB in response to specific stresses such as drought, cold, infections and salinity, but their roles in concurrent conditions are not well known. To better understand this complexity, it would be beneficial to perform factorial stress experiments (e.g., drought + pathogen, heat + salinity) coupled with high‐resolution single‐cell RNA sequencing to delineate MYB‐driven transcriptional reprogramming across different cell types to engineer crops with improved resilience to multifaceted stress conditions.

Conceptual model: MYB‐mediated coordination of stress responses and metabolism. External stimuli induce biotic/abiotic stress responses that activate two parallel pathways: Hormone signalling pathway modulates metabolic status and MAPK cascades (mediating phosphorylation). Both pathways converge on MYB transcription factors. MYBs regulate downstream genes either independently or through MBW complex formation, directing metabolic flux in secondary metabolism.
