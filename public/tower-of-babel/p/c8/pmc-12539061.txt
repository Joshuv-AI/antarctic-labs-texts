Metabolic dynamics and stage-specific biomarkers in chronic HBV infection: a metabolomics study
Authors: Lizhi Liu, Zifei Zhu, Wenwen Jin, Xi Su, Zhonghua Deng, Cunyan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study was aimed to identify and investigate changes in serum metabolites across the natural progression of chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients, discover differential metabolites and metabolic pathways in different disease phases, and provide an experimental foundation for clinical diagnosis, staging, treatment, and prognosis of CHB.MethodsA total of 279 CHB patients hospitalized at Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital from June 2020 to March 2021 were enrolled, including 47 in the immune-tolerant (IT) group, 44 in the immune-clearance (IC) group, 73 in the low-replication (LR) group, 57 in the enhanced-replication (ENH) group, 32 in the liver cirrhosis (LC) group, and 26 in the hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) group, along with 44 healthy controls. Serum metabolites were analyzed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC–MS). Preprocessed metabolic data were subjected to univariate and multivariate statistical analyses via MetaboAnalyst 6.0. The metabolites were mapped to the KEGG database to identify key differential metabolic pathways (Impact value > 0.02).ResultsThe results of principal component analysis (PCA) and Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) pattern recognition showed clustered distribution patterns of metabolites between CHB patients at different natural history phases and healthy controls. Venn diagrams of differential metabolites (DMs) across the six phases of CHB patients revealed that there were phase-specific DMs in each phase: retinyl beta-glucuronide and APGPR Enterostatin in the IT group; creatinine, deoxycorticosterone, O-phosphoethanolamine, and tetradecanedioic acid in the IC group; 4-Pyridoxic acid, sulfasalazine, calcium, and dodecanoylcarnitine in the LR group; oxoglutaric acid, creatine, and 8-Hydroxy-7-methylguanine in the ENH group; umbelliferone, leukotriene D4, malic acid, 3-methylcrotonylglycine, and sulfathiazole in the LC group; 4'-O-methyl-(-)-epicatechin-3'-O-β-glucuronide and DG(16:0/18:1(9Z)/0:0) in the HCC group. All these DMs exhibited AUC values > 0.7, indicating their potential as phase-specific biomarkers for CHB staging. Additionally, compared to healthy controls, there were 6 differential metabolic pathways in the IT group, 8 differential metabolic pathways in the IC group, 6 differential metabolic pathways in the LR group, 7 differential metabolic pathways in the ENH group, 9 differential metabolic pathways in the LC group, and 8 differential metabolic pathways in the HCC group. These differential metabolic pathways were primarily enriched in amino acid metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, hormone metabolism, and energy metabolism.ConclusionSerum DMs and metabolic pathways existed in CHB patients across natural progression phases, demonstrating dynamic variations and potential as staging biomarkers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04332-w.Keywords: Chronic hepatitis B, Metabolomics, Serum biomarkers, Differential metabolites

Introduction

Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) is defined as hepatitis B virus (HBV)-positive individuals with a disease course exceeding six months or those with an unclear onset date but exhibiting clinical manifestations of chronic hepatitis [1]. HBV infection represents a significant global health burden, with over 296 million individuals chronically infected worldwide [2]. The progression of HBV-mediated liver disease can culminate in hepatic fibrosis, cirrhosis, and ultimately hepatocellular carcinoma [3]. According to the Guidelines for the Prevention, Diagnosis, Care and Treatment for People with Chronic Hepatitis B Infection (2024) issued by the World Health Organization, current laboratory assessments for CHB and its associated cirrhosis primarily include serological tests, biochemical profiling, and histopathological examinations [2].

The natural history of CHB infection is conventionally categorized into four phases based on virological, biochemical, and histopathological characteristics: the immune-tolerance phase (IT), immune-clearance phase (IC), low-replicative phase (LR), and enhanced-replication phase (ENH) [4, 5], corresponding to HBeAg-positive infection, HBeAg-positive disease, HBeAg-negative infection, and HBeAg-negative disease, respectively. However, emerging evidence revealed significant diagnostic limitations, with 28–55% of untreated HBV-infected patients failing to be accurately classified into these phases during one-year follow-up periods when assessed by conventional HBV DNA levels, alanine transaminase (ALT) activity, or histopathology [6–8]. This clinically challenging population, termed "indeterminate-phase" CHB carriers, primarily comprised cases with ambiguous differentiation between HBeAg-positive chronic carriers and active CHB patients, or between HBeAg-negative carriers and active disease. Notably, these indeterminate-phase patients demonstrated substantially higher risks of disease progression compared to true IT phase or LR phase carriers, potentially necessitating earlier therapeutic intervention compared to true HBeAg-positive carriers (i.e., IT phase) or HBeAg-negative carriers (i.e., LR phase), potentially necessitating earlier therapeutic intervention [6]. These findings underscore the critical inadequacies of current diagnostic modalities in precisely staging untreated HBV infections, with consequent implications for clinical management decisions.

The liver, the body's primary metabolic regulator, orchestrates the synthesis, catabolism, transformation, and storage of diverse biomolecules including carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and hormones [9]. While HBV itself is non-cytopathic, the ensuing host immune response mediates hepatocyte injury and inflammatory necrosis [10], inevitably disrupting hepatic metabolic networks and altering the profiles of numerous small-molecule metabolites [11].

Metabolomics has emerged as a powerful tool in hepatology research [12, 13]. Particularly, untargeted metabolomics offers an unbiased, comprehensive approach to characterize global metabolic perturbations, can holistically reveal metabolic alterations and underlying mechanisms during disease progression, facilitating the discovery of novel biomarkers for early diagnosis, disease monitoring, and prognosis [14]. Therefore, this study employed untargeted metabolomics to delineate differential metabolites (DMs) and perturbed metabolic pathways across the four canonical CHB phases, as well as during progression to HBV-related cirrhosis and HCC. Our findings were anticipated to provide crucial insights into the metabolic rewiring underlying CHB progression, potentially informing improved diagnostic stratification and therapeutic strategies for CHB patients.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

This study included 221 untreated chronic HBV-infected patients (stratified into immune-tolerance [IT, n = 47], immune-clearance [IC, n = 44], low-replicative [LR, n = 73], and enhanced-replication [ENH, n = 57] phases), 32 cirrhosis patients, 26 HCC patients, and 44 healthy controls from Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital (June 2020–March 2021). Classifications followed established HBV phase guidelines [2], cirrhosis criteria [10], and HCC diagnostic standards [15]. Exclusion criteria encompassed concurrent liver diseases, other infections, malignancies, or metabolic disorders. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Declaration of Istanbul, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Provincial People's Hospital, which granted a waiver of informed consent.

Sample collection and processing

Fasting venous blood was centrifuged (4,000 rpm, 10 min), with serum stored at −80 °C. Clinical parameters (age, sex, alanine transaminase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST], total bilirubin [TBIL], direct bilirubin [DBIL]) were recorded. For LC–MS, 100 μL serum was mixed with internal standard, precipitated (methanol:acetonitrile = 2:1, v/v), freeze-thawed, centrifuged (13,000 rpm, 15 min), and analyzed. Quality control (QC) samples were interleaved every six runs.

LC–MS analysis

LC–MS analysis was performed using a Thermo Scientific™ system (United States). Chromatography used an Acclaim™ C18 column (40 °C, 0.2 mL/min) with 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile (B). Mass spectrometry utilized an electrospray ionization (ESI) source in positive ion mode. Data were acquired across an m/z range of 20–1000.

Data processing

Clinical data were analyzed using SPSS 20.0. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean ± SD (Student’s t-test), non-normal data as median (interquartile range) (Mann–Whitney U test), and categorical variables using chi-square tests, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Raw metabolomic data were processed using MetaboScape 3.0 for peak extraction, noise reduction, and normalization. Metabolites were annotated using the Bruker Spectral Library, HMDB, and online databases. Multivariate analyses (Principal component analysis [PCA], Partial least squares discriminant analysis [PLS-DA]) and univariate analyses (t-test, fold change [FC]) were performed in MetaboAnalyst 6.0. DMs were identified using thresholds of FC > 2 or < 0.5, P < 0.05, and VIP > 1.5. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was conducted with an impact value threshold of > 0.02.

Stage-specific signatures analysis in CHB progression by supervised PLS-DA

We first applied the PCA model to cluster serum metabolomics data from CHB patients at different natural history phases and healthy controls. The results showed that, except for the LC group and healthy controls, other groups could not be well clustered from healthy controls, with intra-group differences exceeding inter-group differences (Fig. 1 A, D, G, J, M, and P). Subsequently, the supervised PLS-DA algorithm based on partial least squares regression was employed to cluster serum metabolites between each group and healthy controls. All groups and healthy controls exhibited clear separation, with samples clustering into two distinct groups (Fig. 1 B, E, H, K, N, and Q). For the PLS-DA models of the six groups, after 100 permutation tests, the R2X(cum) values were relatively high (0.627—0.724), the R2Y(cum) values were very high (0.875—0.953), and the Q2(cum) values were also very high (0.787—0.893). The Q2 values were generally slightly lower but close to the corresponding R2Y values, and all p-values were less than 0.05. These results collectively demonstrated that the outcomes of the PLS-DA models were highly reliable (Fig. 1 C, F, I, L, O, R and Supplementary Table 1).

Differential metabolites in each period of the natural course of chronic Hepatitis B

Analysis of final differential metabolites of CHB by KEGG pathway analysis

KEGG pathway analysis of final differential metabolites revealed phase-specific metabolic alterations (Fig. 2 C, F, I, L, O, and R). Pathways with impact > 0.02 and p < 0.05 were considered significant. Interestingly, the IT and LR groups exhibited nearly identical differential metabolic pathways. Glutathione metabolism, D-glutamine/D-glutamate metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, alanine/aspartate/glutamate metabolism, and arginine/proline metabolism were enriched across all CHB phases. Folate biosynthesis was observed in the IC, ENH, LC, and HCC groups; the Tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle appeared in the ENH, LC, and HCC groups; while glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism was enriched only in the LC and HCC groups.

The distribution of differential metabolites across CHB phases

A Venn diagram illustrated the distribution of differential metabolites across CHB phases (Fig. 3A). 11 metabolites were consistently present across all phases, with unique metabolites identified for each phase: 2 in the IT group, 4 in the IC group, 4 in the LR group, 3 in the ENH group, 5 in the LC group, and 2 in the HCC group. ROC analysis of these unique metabolites demonstrated AUC values > 0.7 (Fig. 3B-U), confirming their diagnostic potential as phase-specific biomarkers.

Discussion

The clinical management of CHB is critically dependent on accurate disease staging, as patients in different phases exhibit distinct prognostic outcomes and therapeutic requirements. Evidences highlighted the diagnostic challenges posed by indeterminate-phase CHB patients—a substantial subgroup that fails to meet the classic criteria for the four conventional phases [2, 6–8]. To address this gap, we employed untargeted LC–MS to systematically profile serum metabolites across all CHB phases. Multivariate analyses (PCA, PLS-DA) combined with univariate statistical approaches revealed distinct metabolic signatures and pathway perturbations associated with disease progression. Importantly, ROC analysis identified potential diagnostic biomarkers for CHB staging, offering new insights into the metabolic rewiring underlying HBV pathogenesis.

Our findings demonstrated that DMs across CHB phases effectively mirrored the dynamic inflammatory and oxidative stress processes during disease progression. Notably, metabolic disturbances were relatively mild in the IT and LR phases but became progressively exacerbated in the IC, ENH, LC, and HCC phases. Throughout CHB progression, accumulations of ascorbic acid (vitamin C), quinolinic acid, cysteinylglycine, and L-glutamic acid were observed, alongside reduced alpha-CEHC levels. Quinolinic acid, a tryptophan metabolite produced by activated macrophages, was a known inducer of oxidative stress via reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation [16, 17]. Similarly, cysteinylglycine and L-glutamic acid—byproducts of glutathione (GSH) catabolism—reflect dysregulated redox homeostasis [18, 19]. Gamma-glutamyltranspeptidase (GGT)-mediated GSH cleavage generated ROS, which activated NF-κB, further amplifying inflammatory signaling [20, 21]. Additionally, cysteinylglycine, a pro-oxidant generated during GSH catabolism, induces oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and carcinogenesis [22, 23]. Accordingly, CHB patients across all phases, including those progressing to LC and HCC, exhibited abnormal GSH metabolism compared to healthy controls. Ascorbic acid, a potent antioxidant, scavenges free radicals and protects against oxidative damage [24, 25]. Alpha-CEHC, a cytochrome P450-mediated metabolite of vitamin E in the liver [26], exhibits anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [27, 28]. These DMs and pathways suggest sustained oxidative stress in CHB patients.

Moreover, CHB patients in the IC, ENH, LC, and HCC phases exhibited significant dihydrobiopterin (BH2) accumulation, indicative of tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) oxidation, due to sustained oxidative stress. BH2 induces nitric oxide synthase (NOS) uncoupling, markedly increasing superoxide production [29, 30], exacerbating oxidative damage.

In ENH phase patients, accumulation of 8-Hydroxy-7-methylguanine had been observed. In the study cohort comparing ENH with healthy controls, the AUC for 8-Hydroxy-7-methylguanine was 0.843. This modified nucleoside inhibits human poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) [31, 32], a critical NAD⁺-dependent enzymes in DNA damage repair [33]. PARP activation under oxidative stress had been linked to post-ischemic liver injury via inducing programmed necrosis (Parthanatos and necroptosis) [34], suggesting that 8-hydroxy-7-methylguanine-mediated PARP suppression may exacerbate genomic instability in CHB. Given the established role of PARP inhibitors in oncology [35–37], this pathway warrants further investigation as a potential therapeutic target in HBV-related hepatocarcinogenesis.

Leukotriene D4 accumulation was observed only in CHB patients progressing to LC, reflecting severe hepatic inflammation via its potent pro-inflammatory effects. Intriguingly, vitamin B6 metabolism exhibited phase-dependent dynamics: LR-phase patients showed elevated 4-pyridoxic acid (PLP, active vitamin B6), whereas LC/HCC patients accumulated pyrrole-2-carboxylic acid (PCA, a vitamin B6 metabolite). Vitamin B6 modulates immune responses and mitigates oxidative stress [38, 39]; its depletion in advanced disease may thus reflect exhaustion of anti-inflammatory reserves. Clinical studies corroborated that higher vitamin B6 levels correlated with improved cancer outcomes, supporting its protective role in CHB progression [40, 41].

Our study also identified differential metabolic pathways in CHB patients. Beyond GSH metabolism, pathways consistently dysregulated throughout CHB included D-glutamine/D-glutamate metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, alanine/aspartate/glutamate metabolism, and arginine/proline metabolism. Glycerophospholipid metabolism alterations were observed in IT, IC, and LC phases. Glucose metabolism progressively dysregulated during CHB progression, with Tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle disturbances emerging in ENH, LC, and HCC phases. Specifically: Oxoglutaric acid (α-ketoglutaric acid) accumulation in ENH phase, reduced malic acid and elevated citric acid in LC phase, and increased citric acid in HCC phase. These findings likely reflect HBV-induced mitochondrial dysfunction [42] and metabolic reprogramming. In cirrhosis, insulin resistance upregulates pyruvate dehydrogenase activity, enhancing glycolysis and acetyl-CoA flux into the TCA cycle [43–45]. In HCC, the Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis) and hypoxic tumor microenvironments (via hypoxia-inducible factor) further distort mitochondrial metabolism [46–50], driving citrate accumulation. TCA cycle disturbances only emerged in ENH, LC, and HCC phases, suggesting glucose metabolism abnormalities worsen with CHB progression. Oxoglutaric acid in ENH phase patients showed an AUC of 0.731 (95% CI [confidence interval], 0.635–0.822), indicating diagnostic potential for glucose dysregulation and disease progression.

Beyond hepatic injury, our data implicated extrahepatic complications. IC-phase patients exhibited elevated creatinine (renal dysfunction) and deoxycorticosterone (an aldosterone precursor), while LC/HCC patients showed tetrahydroaldosterone-3-glucuronide accumulation—changes that may exacerbate fluid retention, hypertension, and renal fibrosis.

Prior metabolomic studies identified CHB vs. LC/HCC biomarkers [51–55]. Ye et al. identifies dysregulation of lipid metabolism in LC of CHB patients by metabolomic analysis, which compared the serum metabolic profiles of patients in IT phase and patients in IC phase of CHB infected patients. Detection of metabolic signatures implicated in the progression from hepatitis to cirrhosis to hepatocellular carcinoma, which characterized the metabolic profiles of the different stages of the hepatitis B-cirrhosis-HCC transition [54]. Yu et al. characterized the evolving metabolic landscape across hepatitis-cirrhosis-HCC progression by identifying progression-associated differential metabolites [55]. Our study was the first to systematically delineate differential metabolites and pathways across four CHB phases and progression to LC/HCC, identifying metabolic signatures of poor prognosis and phase-transition biomarkers: creatinine and deoxycorticosterone (IC progression), Oxoglutaric acid and 8-hydroxy-7-methylguanine (ENH progression), and 4-pyridoxic acid (LR phase stability). This study also had certain limitations. The ROC analysis identified biomarkers with potential diagnostic value based on internal validation in the discovery cohort. In the future, we will conduct external validation in an independent cohort.

In conclusion, mapping metabolic perturbations across CHB progression revealed that disease progression-associated differential metabolic pathways were primarily linked to amino acid and energy metabolism, predominantly mediated by intrahepatic oxidative stress. Our findings demonstrated that oxidative stress remains relatively mild during IT and LR phases but escalates progressively through IC, ENH, LC, and HCC stages. We further identified stage specific biomarkers that contribute to diagnostic and therapeutic potential.
