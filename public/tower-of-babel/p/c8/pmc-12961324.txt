How can ultrasonic scouring of sheep’s wool chemically modify its surface?

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Ultrasonic cleaning is most commonly applied in industrial processes. It is based on the generation of shock waves and the formation of currents accompanying the collapse of acoustic cavitation [1]. Ultrasonic cleaning first appeared as a technology in the 1950s and continues to be used and improved to this day.

A special advantage of ultrasonic cleaning is that, thanks to its ability to penetrate, it can reach even hard-to-reach crevices that are inaccessible to other conventional cleaning methods. Therefore, various objects with highly intricate surfaces are cleaned using this method. The effectiveness of the cleaning process is influenced by the working conditions, including the type of cleaning liquid, temperature, ultrasound intensity, and the presence of an acoustic standing wave. This is created when an ultrasonic wave with a single frequency emanates from the bottom of the bath, hits the surface of the liquid, and reflects back into the tank. Setting a set of optimal parameters for a given type of cleaned material, including the geometry of the tank, can significantly affect the efficiency of the process.

While hazardous organic solvents such as carbon tetrachloride, trichloroethylene, and tetrachloroethylene were traditionally used for degreasing components, ultrasound generally works effectively with water-based solvents. This enables a broader range of temperatures for the cleaning medium.

With regard to ultrasonic frequency, a value of approximately 40 kHz is now used almost exclusively. This is because younger individuals can still perceive 20 kHz, whereas 40 kHz is inaudible to all operating personnel—although associated vibrations from metal enclosures and other components may still be present. McQueen (1986) found that the removal of certain submicroscopic contaminants was more effective at a frequency of 220 kHz [2]. This indicates that applying uniform ultrasonic cleaning parameters to all products may not yield optimal results.

The effects of ultrasound are not limited to physical phenomena. As reported by Ashokkumar and Mason [3], chemical processes also occur in water. During the expansion phase of the bubbles, molecules of the solvent and dissolved gases diffuse into the bubble. When the bubble collapses, these molecules experience extreme temperature conditions and undergo decomposition reactions that generate H-radicals and OH-radicals (reaction (1). These radicals can undergo recombination reactions that generate molecular products (reactions (2), (3), (4)).

Since H* is a strong reducing agent and OH* is a strong oxidizing agent, these radicals can participate in various redox reactions. If the water is saturated with air/oxygen, secondary oxidizing radicals can be regenerated as (reaction 5).

Strong oxidizing agents (*OH, HO2*, H2O2) generated could be used for the degradation of organic pollutants. It is also possible to convert all primary radicals into reducing secondary radicals (R*) in the presence of solutes such as alcohols ROH (equation 6).

It has been observed that the amount of generated radicals strongly depends on ultrasound power and frequency. Stirring can promote the course of potential chemical reactions by improving mass transfer. Thus, the above events can fundamentally affect the material in the water and be subjected to ultrasound. If there are more substances in the water environment, their potential interactions must also be considered. The resulting situation can only be estimated with difficulty, because the ultrasound parameters used can also significantly determine the result. Therefore, each event variant must be tested individually.

Despite the widespread use of ultrasound for cleaning the surfaces of various materials in industry, the scouring of sheep wool in an ultrasonic bath has not seen broader practical application, even though a standard scouring represents the most demanding stage of wool processing. This process generates large volumes of wastewater containing detergents and other auxiliary agents, so that the water treatment is associated with high chemical and biological oxygen demand.

Available sources provide only limited data on this topic, mostly from laboratory-scale studies. Slightly more attention is focused on ultrasound-assisted wool dyeing. Regardless of the textile use of wool, it is important that neither the physical nor the chemical effects of ultrasound damage the wool fiber to an undesirable extent.

The physical changes in wool fibers after exposure to ultrasound were investigated by Hurren et al. [4] and Li et al. [5]. Using electron microscopy they reported that ultrasonic treatment caused scale cracking/peeling in cuticle on some wool fibres. On the other hand, Kadam et al. [6] did not observe any cracks after ultrasonic scouring however, ultrasound was applied not longer than 15 min. Similarly, Goud et al. [7] did not find any topographic changes or damage when wool was ultrasonically scoured for 3 min. Anyway, when the demand for wool purity is not too high, the total ultrasonic scouring time can be reduced to avoid some damage to the fibre surface.

Chemical changes in the fiber induced by the ultrasonic effect on water according to reactions (1–6) may have some consequences, but in relation to the operating parameters of ultrasound, they have not yet been described. Our previous research was focused on the influence of temperature, intensity, and duration of the ultrasonic bath on some physical changes of the wool surface scoured in tap water without chemicals [8]. The wool mass loss, residual grease content and microscopic appearance of the fiber surface were measured. The following study aims to test the influence of some ultrasonic bath parameters on the change in chemical functional groups of keratin and the sorption potential of ultrasonically treated wool towards a model metal cation as an indicator. Analogous data have not yet been published.

Experimental

Materials

The wool came from the spring shearing of the meat-dairy breed Valaška bred in the region of western Slovakia. Samples for the experiments were taken from several parts of the fleece randomly, manually cleaned of coarse dirt (residues of feed, plants, excrement), and manually loosened.

Hydrochloric acid, sodium hydroxide, and zinc chloride were supplied by Centralchem C​o. (Slovakia).

Instruments

An ultrasonic bath Kraintek K5LE (Podhájska, Slovakia) with tap water was used for scouring wool according to a set experimental plan. The technical parameters of the bath were as follows: volume 5 L, power 350 VA with adjustable range (10–90) %, high-frequency output 150 W, frequency 38 kHz, power density 30 W/L, temperature control in the range (20–95) °C, timer 0–99 min.

A laboratory horizontal shaker (Witeg SHR–2D, Labortechnik GmbH, Wertheim, Germany) was used to ensure thorough contact of the wool with a solute in water.

An atomic absorption spectrometer (Spectrometer 240 FS AAS, Agilent Technologies, USA) was used to determine the content of residual Zn(II) in the filtrate.

Scouring

All wool samples were processed in triplicate. Each parallel sample with an individual weight of 5 g was placed in a netted bag and scoured together in triplicate in 5 L of tap water without any detergent. Temperature of the bath was set to a desired value, controlled with an alcohol thermometer and carefully maintained at ± 1-2 °C adding ice. The wool to water ratio in the bath of 1:333 was kept in all subsequent experiments. After each scouring cycle, the water in the bath was exchanged.

Effect of ultrasonic wool scouring conditions on the content of basic and acidic groups

According to Brønsted theory, basic functional groups (BG) are considered to be groups capable of binding H+, and conversely, acidic groups (AG) are those that release H+. The Boehm titration is one of the few methods that enables a quantification of specific oxygen-containing surface groups on carbon materials [13].

The strength of the corresponding acids can be distinguished by titration with a titrant of graded alkalinity [14]. In our experiment, only the total acid content was determined, without distinguishing acid strength.

The Boehm method was developed to determine the surface functional groups of carbon materials and is solution-based. According to the author, it only examines the part of the surface accessible to the basic/acidic solution, so the sample under investigation must be sufficiently hydrophilic. It is applied mostly to biochar (activated carbon). If it is of plant origin, its functional groups will probably be oxygenic such as carboxyls, aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, quinones, etc. However, keratin functional groups do not differ fundamentally from these, only in that they also include nitrogenous and sulphuric groups. Therefore, we are convinced that the Boehm titration is also well applicable to wool and can provide useful information.

Since sheep wool is known for its high moisture absorption, up to 30 % of its own mass, it is questionable to speak only of surface functional groups in the case of wool. Under such circumstances, moisture must also be present in the internal structures of the wool, in the vacuoles. Ultrasound during scouring must also act on this water fraction and influence the development of basic groups (BG) and acidic groups (AG). Since we cannot differentiate between the groups affected in the bulk of the fiber and on its surface, the term “surface groups” is replaced by the term “BG, AG groups.” However imprecise the difference in their understanding may be, under the same conditions for determining BG and AG in our experiments, the data obtained will provide relevant information about trends.

The constituent amino acids of keratin contain both acidic and basic groups in their side chains. If the carboxyl and amino groups already bound in peptide bonds –CO–NH– are not considered, the acidic side groups are represented by carboxyl groups (e.g., from aspartic acid, glutamic acid, cystine), alcoholic (e.g., from serine, threonine), and phenolic (e.g., from tyrosine). Arginine, lysine, and cystine contribute mainly to the basic amino side groups.

In addition to the original acidic and basic groups, their content in wool is undoubtedly influenced by the series of chemical reactions (1)–(6) occurring in the water itself under the influence of ultrasound [3].

Scouring of wool at 60 °C

Increased temperature can lead to the denaturation of proteins, including keratin. Although the effects of simultaneous exposure to temperature and ultrasound on wool have not yet been described, it is assumed that the probability of denaturation increases with rising bath temperature (Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9). Our expectations are based on the findings of Haly and Snaith [15], who showed that the denaturation of α-helical fibrous proteins depends on the surrounding environment. The denaturation temperature of α-keratin in the dry state was found to be approximately 240 °C, [16], whereas for wet keratin, it was measured between 120 °C and 150 °C [17]. These different denaturation temperatures also correspond to different activation energies: 416 kJ/mol for dry keratin and 263 kJ/mol for wet keratin [18].

The development of BG and AG in wool scoured at 60 °C is evident from Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9.

Within the group of experiments conducted at 60 °C, the results for BG and AG are consistent in our opinion, too.

Testing results using ANOVA

We compared our subjective evaluation of the results with the conclusions provided by the ANOVA program. A three-factor analysis of variance was used to assess the influence of bath temperature, ultrasound intensity, and scouring time on the BG and AG development, including the evaluation of interactions between these factors.

The number of basic groups confirmed a significant effect of temperature (F = 88.495; p < 0.001), while scouring time (F = 0.460; p = 0.634) and ultrasound intensity (F = 2.434; p = 0.097) did not reach statistical significance on their own. In contrast to the acidic groups, however, the interactions of temperature and time (F = 3.712; p = 0.010) and temperature and intensity (F = 3.175; p = 0.020) were statistically significant, while the interaction of time and intensity was marginally significant (F = 2.256; p = 0.075) and the triple interaction did not reach significance (F = 1.078; p = 0.392).

The dominant effect of temperature was confirmed also for the amount of acidic groups (F = 73.101; p < 0.001), while scouring time (F = 2.168; p = 0.124) and ultrasound intensity (F = 2.577; p = 0.085) were not statistically significant, although a tendency towards threshold limit of significance was observed for intensity. Of the interactions, only the interaction between temperature and time (F = 2.371; p = 0.064) appeared to be threshold limit significant, while the other interaction effects did not reach significance (p > 0.05).

Overall, the results confirm the dominant role of temperature in all monitored parameters, with the significance of interaction effects being more pronounced especially for basic groups. The effectiveness of ultrasonic scouring is conditioned by cavitation phenomena, the intensity of which is primarily dependent on the temperature and the supplied ultrasonic power. Higher temperature of the liquid medium reduces the solubility of gases in water, thereby affecting the formation of cavitation nuclei and the dynamics of bubble collapse [19], [20].

However, the ANOVA analysis should be viewed with caution because it analyzes the dispersion of partial results. In this respect, wool is a special case. Sample to sample may differ in the degree of contamination, breed of sheep, place of collection from the sheep's body, fiber thickness and even the season of shearing. The rate of the result dispersion may partially overlap weaker interaction effects between the monitored parameters. Although statistical analysis in the given experiment did not show significant interactions between temperature and intensity, the physical nature of the process suggests that these factors are not independent, but cooperatively affect the intensity of cavitation phenomena and thus the efficiency of the scouring process.

Chemical effect of ultrasound on wool

Based on reactions (1), (2), (3), (4), (5), (6) [3] we believe that the trends in BG and AG development are also related to the redox effect of water-derived products generated by US, especially given that the amount of radicals produced strongly depends on US power and frequency.

Although it is difficult to identify a determining reaction from the set (1)–(6), partial support can be found in an earlier study by Smith and Harris [21], which investigated the oxidizing effect of hydrogen peroxide on wool. Attention was given to disulfide groups as they have the lowest dissociation energy. At the same time, increasing H2O2 concentration reduced the cystine content in wool while maintaining the total sulfur content, thereby demonstrating that peroxide attacks the disulfide bridges of keratin. Since, according to reactions (1), (2), (3), (4), (5), (6), the action of ultrasound on H2O produces both reducing and oxidizing radicals [3], disulfide bonds may undergo cleavage, reaction (7).

and subsequent reduction as one of the options, reaction (8).

However, upon ultrasonic exposure of H2O, oxidative cleavage predominates due to the formation of multiple oxidizing species. Since the dissociation energy of the O–H bond into radicals is practically the same (428.0 ± 2.1 kJ/mol) as that of the S–S bond (428.9 ± 6.3 kJ/mol), [22], simultaneous cleavage of both types of bonds should occur even upon ultrasonic exposure. If so, cleavage of the O–H bond also plays a role in the background of reactions (1), (2), (3), (4), (5), (6).

It is a well-established fact that exposing wool to elevated temperatures in solution, such as during dyeing, reduces fiber strength, since disulfide bridges are an important part of its structure. Their destruction impairs the physical properties of wool. A decrease in breaking strength when heating wool to 50 °C in a hydrogen peroxide solution was also reported by the above-mentioned Smith and Harris [21], which was accompanied by a decrease in cystine content. So it can be assumed that even under ultrasonic scouring, cystine and its oxidation products are primarily responsible for the development of BG and AG. However, the resulting reduction products R-SH (7) cannot be neglected either.

If the BG content increases, it should be associated with an increase in the amount of the R–SO3− anion. Although the role of the (intermediate) products in schemes (7)–(10) is difficult to quantify due to their variability, their involvement in the formation of AG is inevitable. The amount of AG at 60 °C is practically identical to that at 40 °C (Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6) for all scouring times and US intensities, but lower than at 20 °C.

From this, it follows that the expected reactions (a slight increase in BG from 20 min of scouring and a reduction in AG at 40 °C and 60 °C) were activated at 40 °C at the latest, under 90 % intensity. If this is the case, then scouring at 20 °C is the most suitable for preserving the original properties of the wool.

Effect of ultrasonic scouring conditions on Zn(II) adsorption

The adsorption experiments aimed to investigate whether and how ultrasonic scouring affects the sorptivity of wool. The adsorption capacity of wool can be a certain indicator of the effect of ultrasound and temperature on the chemical properties of wool. A solution of zinc chloride was chosen as a model adsorbate since it will not form complex forms in a given environment [23]. But, Zn(II) can be bound substitutionally as carboxylate, alcoholate, phenolate, or cysteinate. Then it can be expected that a change in the representation of basic and acidic groups could also affect the adsorption of Zn(II). The instantaneous ratio of oxidized and reduced S-forms should be determined by the dynamics of the above processes and the transformation of intermediate products according to the reactions (7–9).

The cleavage of keratin disulfide bonds, associated with the (partial) separation of the double-helix and the formation of subsequent reduction and oxidation products according to reaction (8,9), leads to keratin amorphization. Similar substitution reactions can also occur with anions of other acids from keratin side chains, provided that spatial conditions allow it. From this point of view, the –SO3− anions have the greatest potential. In the case of cations of elements with a stronger tendency to coordinate than Zn (e.g., Cr, Co, Ni, Cu) and multiple possible conformations of the complex salt, the spatial requirements would be higher. As it was found [24], [25], [26], cations of some of the mentioned metals showed adsorption isotherms with fluctuations when in contact with wool. Therefore, they are not suitable as a reference adsorbate in this case.

To facilitate the interpretation of the relationship between the BG and AG content and the sorption capacity of wool scoured in an ultrasonic bath under the applied conditions for Zn(II), an alternative representation of the dependencies was deliberately adopted in Fig. 10, Fig. 11, Fig. 12. However, it must be kept in mind that the sorption capacity of wool is also influenced by the physical sorption of Zn(II) onto the wool, which is related to changes in the surface topography induced by the conditions of ultrasonic scouring [8]. This component may contribute to the variability observed in the measurement data.

From the dependencies shown in Fig. 10, Fig. 11, Fig. 12, it is evident that the measured parameters influence each other. While the deviations for BG and AG are generally within ± 0.07 mmol/g, those for Zn(II) sorption are significantly higher.

Differences in sorptivity are evident for individual temperatures as well as scouring times.

The basic premise used is the connection of BG from the conjugate base of cysteic acid with the sorption of Zn(II) as described in reaction (11).

If the study Smith and Harris [21] showed that the oxidation of wool with hydrogen peroxide decreased the cystine content while keeping the sulfur content and was accompanied by a loss of breaking strength, the cause must have been the weakening of the chains due to a greater or lesser separation of the double helix chains. Analogously, the effect of ultrasound also causes the disulfide bonds originating from cystine to be broken and their final transformation into cysteine ​​acid (reaction 9). The subsequently generated conjugate bases expressed as BG (reaction (10) react with Zn(II) according to reaction (11). If so, then changes in BG content and Zn(II) sorption may be an indicator of changes in the strength of wool as a measure of damage/preservation of the physical properties of wool scoured in an ultrasonic bath without chemicals; the higher the BG content or Zn(II) sorption at the same initial concentration of dissolved sorbate, the greater the disruption to the wool.

ANOVA analysis of dispersion showed that sorptivity was statistically significantly affected by temperature (F = 17.652; p < 0.001) and scouring time (F = 4.913; p = 0.011), while the effect of ultrasound intensity was at the threshold limit of significance (F = 2.979; p = 0.059). Of the interaction effects, only the interaction of temperature and time appeared to be threshold limit significant (F = 2.424; p = 0.059), while the other two-way and three-way interactions did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05).

Summarizing all the findings leads to the next consideration. If temperature has proven to be the dominant factor in all monitored properties, it moderates all changes in the indicators. The aim is to ensure that the wool is sufficiently scoured with minimal change in its original utility properties. This requirement is best met by scouring at 20 °C. At this temperature, BG, AG, and zinc removal show the smallest changes. Since the scouring time is not a statistically significant parameter, or is on significance threshold, it is rational to choose the lowest applied time of 10 min. The ultrasound intensity was evaluated as insignificant. For the selection of the optimal intensity, microscopic images of the fiber surface of the same samples [8] scoured at 20 °C, time 10 min, and different intensities served as a supporting fact. The samples scoured at the highest intensity of 90 % appeared to be optimal. So, taking into account the energy-economic criterion, it follows that scouring wool at 20 °C and an intensity of 90 % for 10 min could be sufficient.

A complete picture of the impact of ultrasonic wool scouring on product quality could be supplemented with data on changes in fiber strength, yellowing, damage rate, felting, long-term stability, etc., in next work.

Conclusions

The development of basic and acidic groups (BG and AG) on sheep wool scoured in an ultrasonic bath without chemicals was used to evaluate the effect of the bath parameters as temperature, intensity, and scouring time on chemical changes induced by ultrasound at a frequency of 38 kHz.

The results for basic and acidic group development confirmed the dominant role of temperature in all monitored parameters, with the significance of interaction effects being more pronounced for basic groups, especially.

The Zn(II) sorption on the scoured wool was statistically significantly affected by temperature and scouring time, while the effect of ultrasound intensity was at the threshold limit of significance. The interaction of temperature and time appeared to be a threshold limit significant, while the other two-way and three-way interactions did not reach statistical significance.

The evaluation of the impact of ultrasonic bath parameters on chemical changes in wool fiber may be essentially affected by the heterogeneity of the scoured fibers, modifying the significance of the interactions.

Evaluation of the development of the monitored indicators for all applied bath parameters leads to the conclusion that scouring wool at 20 °C with the highest ultrasound intensity of 90 % for just 10 min provides sufficiently favorable results.
