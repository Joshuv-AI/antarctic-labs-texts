Unpacking contextual changes underlying self-injurious thoughts and behaviors among bullying-involved adolescents and young adults in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Sijia Li, Alvin Junus, Paul S. F. Yip
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-pandemic societal contexts and mental health landscape plausibly differ from those in peri-pandemic times. Peri-to-post-pandemic changes in self-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITB) prevalence may be associated with changes in prevalence of SITB determinants (endowment effect) and their associations with SITB outcomes (coefficient effect). These patterns may vary across at-risk subgroups, particularly bullying perpetrators and victims. However, evidence remains limited. This study investigated how these effects were related to peri-to-post-pandemic changes in SITB prevalence.MethodsUsing population-representative surveys from peri-pandemic (2021, 2022; N = 2,986) and post-pandemic (2024; N = 1,500) periods, multivariate logistic regressions identified associations between bullying involvement and SITB. We then conducted a threefold non-linear Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition with SITB psychosocial determinants as covariates and differences in suicidal ideation (SI), self-harm (SH), and suicide attempt (SA) prevalence as separate outcome variables. Analyses were independently conducted for the total sample and subsamples stratified by bullying involvement, i.e., perpetrators, victims, and those uninvolved.ResultsPrevalence of SI, SH, and SA in the young population increased post-pandemic. SI and SH increases were associated with endowment and coefficient effects of depressive and anxiety symptomatology. Bullying involvement was strongly associated with SI, SH, and SA. Perpetrators’ SITB prevalence decreased post-pandemic and was related to determinants’ endowment and coefficient effects. Victims’ SITB prevalence increased, with the increase for SI mainly linked to determinants’ coefficient effect.ConclusionsEfforts in safeguarding adolescents and young adults’ mental wellbeing need to be improved and sustained. Tailored strategies to better care for bullying victims need to be explored.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-04998-1.

Introduction

Suicide has posed an increasingly serious public health concern for young populations worldwide in the past few years [1, 2], consistently being the leading cause of death among adolescents and young adults (AYAs, collectively referring to persons aged 11–35 years here) in eastern and western societies [3, 4]. Rising rates of self-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITB) – suicidal ideation (SI), self-harm (SH), and suicide attempt (SA) – have been further noted in the aftermath of the COVID-19 pandemic among the general young population in diverse settings, from Asia Pacific [5], Finland [6], to Spain [7]. These upward trends underscore an urgent need to (re)formulate more effective suicide prevention efforts and strategies for AYAs specifically. Yet, societal contexts and the mental health landscape in today’s post-pandemic era are markedly different from those in peri- and pre-pandemic times. These contextual shifts may be reflected by changes in both prevalence and their observed associations with SITB outcomes. Thus, we propose that a foundational step towards this cause is to first understand these changes.

Post-pandemic societal changes may have influenced multiple established determinants of SITB. Worsened economic outlooks may adversely affect financial well-being, meaning in life, and hopelessness [8–11], while greater reliance on remote work, learning, and social interaction may alter social connectedness and belongingness [12–14]. Evidence also suggested that psychological distress and some mental health problems remained elevated following the pandemic, although patterns varied across conditions and population groups [6, 15–18].

Higher suicide cases post-pandemic would also signify AYAs’ more frequent exposure to SITB. Prior research suggests that exposure to SI, SH, and SA may influence subsequent risk [19, 20], particularly when the exposed individual has a close social relationship with the self-injurious person [21]. Of note, researchers have noted that AYAs might be more vulnerable to contributing to SITB clusters once exposed [22, 23], possibly due to amplified effects of social mechanisms such as socialization and normalization of SITB in this age group [21].

Importantly, transitions in social, educational, and work practices would entail changes in patterns of bullying behaviors. Recent studies have shown decreased overall bullying involvement among AYAs during pandemic restrictions [24], but this prevalence rebounded when the restrictions were lifted [24, 25], and in some settings exceeded pre-pandemic levels [24, 26]. Cyberbullying may also have become more prevalent than before [27] with accelerated digitalization over the pandemic [28]. Involvement in online and offline modes of bullying may altogether have compounded adverse effects on AYAs [29]. Given that bullying is a well-established risk factor for SITB and a form of targeted interpersonal rejection [30, 31], understanding how post-pandemic changes relate to bullying-involved young people is particularly important. Recent work further suggests that public health crises may reshape social norms and social integration processes, potentially influencing belongingness and interpersonal functioning [32]. From this perspective, bully victims and perpetrators may experience post-pandemic social environments differently. Particular attention to AYA bully victims and perpetrators in the post-pandemic context is thus warranted.

Along with bullying involvement, the factors outlined above, i.e., financial well-being, meaning in life, sense of hopelessness, belongingness, social isolation, psychological distress, mental disorders, and exposure to suicidality, are theoretically and empirically established psychosocial determinants of SITB [33–35]. Nevertheless, observed peri-to-post-pandemic changes in SITB prevalence among AYAs may be may reflect two broad sources of variation. First, a shift in population characteristics (termed “endowment effect”), such as a growing proportion of individuals being socially isolated, would likely increase SITB prevalence. Second, the observed associations between SITB determinants and outcomes (termed “coefficient effect”) might have changed following the pandemic; for example, social isolation might exhibit a stronger association SITB in a less socially cohesive post-pandemic society. Decomposition analysis [36, 37] enables these two components to be quantified separately and may therefore help identify determinants most relevant to post-pandemic suicide prevention efforts. Importantly, although decomposition analyses can identify whether differences are associated with changes in the distribution of determinants or changes in their observed associations with outcomes, they cannot directly identify the specific contextual processes responsible for such differences.

Using representative surveys on AYAs in Hong Kong conducted during peri- and post-pandemic periods, this study examined the extent to which changes in psychosocial determinants and their observed associations with outcomes were associated with changes in SI, SH, and SA prevalence. Analyses were conducted for the overall sample and separately for bully perpetrators and bully victims.

Methods

Participants and procedures

The present study employed a repeated cross-sectional survey design and was conducted in Hong Kong as part of a cross-institutional suicide prevention initiative. Participants for each survey wave were randomly sampled using mobile phone numbers published by the Office of the Communications Authority (OFCA), ensuring an age distribution that closely reflected that of the young population in Hong Kong. Individuals aged 11 to 35 years who resided in Hong Kong and were proficient in Cantonese were eligible to participate in the study.

Three waves of survey were conducted. Data collection periods were 2 August to 17 December 2021, 29 August to 4 November 2022, and 24 September to 30 November 2024 for the first, second, and third waves, respectively. 1,501, 1,502, and 1,500 complete responses were collected in 2021, 2022, and 2024, respectively, with satisfactory response rates of 68.9%, 61.49%, and 62.6% [38]. The 2021 and 2022 survey waves encompassed the peri-pandemic period, while the 2024 wave represented the post-pandemic period.

Participants were briefed on the survey’s objectives (to gain an in-depth understanding of the general well-being of the general young population in Hong Kong), estimated duration (approximately ten minutes), strict confidentiality measures for their data, and their right to withdraw from the study at any time. Special attention was given to crafting survey questions that posed no risk and minimized potential stress for participants. Informed consent was obtained from all participants in each survey before their involvement. Upon survey completion, contact information for emotional support hotlines and services was provided to encourage any distressed respondents to seek immediate support.

All procedures and protocols implemented in this study adhered to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [39] and were approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee for Non-Clinical Faculties at the University of Hong Kong, reference number EA1709039. The committee determined that consent from parents or legal guardians for underage participants was not required, as the approved study was assessed to pose minimal potential harm to these individuals. Previous research has consistently shown that suicide-related screening and survey questions do not induce or exacerbate suicidal thoughts and behaviors among participants [40, 41]. Accordingly, parental or legal guardian consent was waived and was not obtained for participants younger than 18 years.

Measures

Self-injurious thoughts and behaviors (SITBs) Three binary-response questions assessed whether participants had considered suicide (SI), injured themselves intentionally (SH), or attempted suicide (SA) in the recent 12 months, respectively.

Bullying involvement Participants’ involvement in bully perpetration and bully victimization in the past 12 months – either physical or online – were each assessed with a binary question.

Other psychosocial determinants in this study include (1) Sociodemographics: gender, age, occupational status, education level, living arrangements, and financial well-being which was measured using two items from the validated Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) Financial Well-Being Scale [42, 43]; (2) Psychosocial outlook: meaning in life assessed using the Presence subscale of the short form of the Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ-SF) [44], and pessimism toward society measured by items developed in accordance with Steenvoorden’s empirical framework on societal unease [45]; (3) Online time for work or study; (4) Social proximity to SI, SH, and SA; (5) Psychopathology: mental illness diagnosis, social withdrawal evaluated using Hikikomori Questionnaire [46], and symptomatology of depression and anxiety measured using the 4-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ) [47]. Detailed information on the measurement of each variable, variable reclassification, and handling of missing values is provided in the supplementary material entitled Measurement of Variables.

Analysis

Following the lifting of pandemic measures in March 2023 in Hong Kong [48], as well as the World Health Organization’s declaration of the end of the COVID-19 public health emergency of international concern on May 5, 2023 [49], we combined data from 2021 to 2022 to represent the peri-pandemic period, while data from 2024 represented the post-pandemic period. This classification was guided by official policy milestones and was intended to provide an operational framework for examining changes across broadly distinct pandemic contexts rather than to imply a definitive transition between pandemic and post-pandemic conditions. Participants with missing data (0.38% of the study population) were excluded from analyses.

First, we calculated descriptive statistics for the study population. Independent t-tests and chi-squared tests were used to examine variances in SITB prevalence and determinants between the peri- and post-pandemic periods. This was done for the total sample and each bullying behavior subgroup. We additionally conducted multivariable logistic regressions with SI, SH, and SA as outcome variables and SITB psychosocial determinants as covariates. This was also done for the total sample and each bullying behavior subgroup. Separate models for peri- and post-pandemic periods were estimated. To evaluate the linearity-in-the-logit assumption for PHQ-4, we conducted Box-Tidwell tests for each logistic regression model. Following the approach proposed by Box and Tidwell [50], an additional term representing the product of PHQ-4 and its natural logarithm was included in the model. All Box-Tidwell models included the same covariates as the corresponding primary logistic regression models. As the majority of models did not indicate substantial departures from linearity, PHQ-4 was retained as a continuous predictor in subsequent regression and decomposition analyses, consistent with widespread practice in psychiatric epidemiology and population health research [51]. This approach was adopted to ensure analytical consistency across outcomes, subgroups, and study periods. Results are reported in Supplementary Table S1.

We then conducted the threefold non-linear Blinder-Oaxaca (B-O) decomposition to unpack peri-to-post-pandemic differences in SITB prevalence. The B-O decomposition was initially developed to explain mean differences in outcome variables between groups [36, 37], such as gender wage disparities. It has since been extended to non-linear models, including logit and probit, enabling the decomposition of observed differences in proportions and counts [52]. This method has been increasingly adopted to better understand prevalence changes and disparities in mental health outcomes [53–55]. Here, the B-O decomposition allowed us to examine the extent to which peri-to-post-pandemic changes in the prevalence of SI, SH, and SA associated with (i) shifts in the distribution of determinants in the population (i.e., an “endowment effect”), and (ii) changes in the observed associations between these determinants and SITB outcomes across periods (i.e., a “coefficient effect”). Although coefficient effects are often interpreted as reflecting changes in the social or contextual environment, they may also capture influences of unmeasured factors, changes in sample composition, measurement differences, model specification, or other sources of unobserved heterogeneity [52, 56]. Therefore, coefficient effects should not be interpreted as direct evidence of specific causal mechanisms.

The threefold non-linear B-O decomposition model disaggregates differences in the prevalence of the outcome variable between two cross-sections into three components (Eqs. 1–3): the endowment effect (E), the coefficient effect (C), and the interaction effect (I) of explanatory factors [57]. The interaction effect lacks intuitive sociological meaning and is thereby less amenable to interpretation [58], but captures covariation between endowment and coefficient differences, facilitating unbiased estimates of the endowment and coefficient effects and thus reliable insights into key drivers of intergroup differences [59, 60]. We thus did not interpret the interaction effects here.

Overall endowment and coefficient effects could each be further decomposed to assess contributions of specific covariates. In our models, contributions of each covariate’s endowment and coefficient effects to SI, SH, and SA differences were assessed from a post-pandemic perspective, using the peri-pandemic period as the reference group. Directionality of covariates’ effects in the B-O decomposition models was first triangulated with difference tests and logistic regression coefficients to ensure consistency before interpreting endowment and coefficient effects.

To evaluate potential temporal heterogeneity during the peri-pandemic period and assess the robustness of the wave-aggregation strategy, we conducted additional sensitivity analyses. First, key outcomes and psychosocial determinants were compared across the 2021, 2022, and 2024 survey waves using analysis of variance (ANOVA) and chi-square tests, with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons where appropriate. Second, to examine whether combining the 2021 and 2022 waves influenced the decomposition findings, separate threefold non-linear Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition analyses were conducted comparing 2021 versus 2024 and 2022 versus 2024 across the total sample and each bullying-involvement subgroup (i.e., perpetrators, victims, and uninvolved individuals).

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2) and STATA (version 18.0), employing the STATA package oaxaca [57, 61] for the threefold non-linear Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition. Significance threshold for all analyses was p < 0.05.

Results

Descriptive statistics

There was a total of 4,486 participants – 2,986 during the peri-pandemic period and 1,500 post-pandemic – who were further categorized into bully perpetrators, bully victims, and individuals uninvolved in bullying. Across subgroups (Table 1), age and the proportions of males and females were relatively similar peri- and post-pandemic, with most participants in full-time study or employment (75.90% to 88.90%), and having post-secondary education or above (59.85% to 74.40%). Post-pandemic participants in all groups had fewer individuals living alone, reported lower meaning in life and reduced pessimism toward society, spent less time online for work or study, and demonstrated lower social proximity to suicidality.

In the post-pandemic period, both bully perpetrators and victims had fewer individuals living alone and reported reduced pessimism toward society, lower social proximity to suicidality, and lower depressive and anxiety severity. Among perpetrators, additionally, financial well-being increased, online time for work or study decreased, and fewer perpetrators reported having a mental illness diagnosis, social withdrawal, and being victimized.

Figure 1 illustrated the proportions of individuals exhibiting SITB in the total sample and the three subgroups during the peri- and post-pandemic periods. Prevalence of SI, SH, and SA in the general young population all increased post-pandemic, albeit by varying degrees. However, these overall increases masked underlying distinct patterns across different bullying-related subgroups: Prevalence of SI, SH, and SA rose among bully victims and those uninvolved in bullying; in contrast, prevalence of SI, SH, and SA all decreased among bully perpetrators post-pandemic.

Significant peri-to-post-pandemic changes were detected for all outcomes and examined subgroups, except for SA among bully victims and individuals uninvolved in bullying. After controlling for interaction effects, i.e., covariation amongst covariates, population-level rise in SA prevalence was mainly attributed to the examined determinants’ endowment effect (β=-0.019, 95% CI [-0.029, -0.009], contribution = 51.28% of prevalence change). Statistical evidence for specific drivers was insufficient. Determinants’ coefficient effect (marginally significant, β=-0.015, 95% CI [-0.030, 0.000], contribution = 39.85% of prevalence change) might potentially explain changes in SA, with the perpetrator’s influence (β = 0.003, 95% CI [0.000, 0.007], contribution=-9.26%) identified as a critical factor. Likewise, there was no statistical evidence supporting the examined determinants’ endowment and coefficient effects as drivers of population-level increases in SI and SH prevalence. Yet, such effects were evident at the subgroup level, reflecting heterogeneity in determinants’ effects for different population segments.

Among bully perpetrators, both endowment and coefficient effects of determinants drove decreases in all of SI, SH, and SA prevalence after controlling for interaction effects. Perpetrators’ lower depressive and anxiety symptom severity in the post-pandemic context was linked to SI and SH decreases (β = 0.092, 95% CI [0.050, 0.134], contribution = 20.72% and β = 0.066, 95% CI [0.022, 0.109], contribution = 15.44%, respectively), although these decreases were somewhat counteracted by increases in the symptoms’ conferred association for SI and SH (β=-0.335, 95% CI [-0.450, -0.220], contribution=-75.61% and β=-0.247, 95% CI [-0.343, -0.151], contribution=-58.18%, respectively). A decreased proportion of perpetrators who were facing victimization in the post-pandemic context was associated with the SI, SH, and SA prevalence decrease (β = 0.049 to 0.061). Greater financial well-being among perpetrators also was related to SA prevalence decrease. Other notable factors were increased SI risk linked to bully victimization, decreased SH risk related to the female gender, and a loss of protective effect against SH for meaning in life.

Among bully victims, coefficient effects of determinants were linked to the post-pandemic increase in SI prevalence after controlling for interaction effects (β=-0.153, 95% CI [-0.228, -0.078], contribution = 112.70%). Possible candidates were reduced protective effect of meaning in life (marginally significant, contribution = 185.11%) that could be partially buffered by bully perpetration (β = 0.087, 95% CI [0.000, 0.174], contribution=-63.98%).

For those uninvolved in bullying, post-pandemic increases in SI and SH prevalence could be attributed to both endowment and coefficient effects after interaction effects had been controlled. Lower depressive and anxiety symptom severity exerted a downward contribution to SI and SH prevalence, but there was insufficient statistical evidence to pinpoint which determinants these effects were counteracted by. Higher risk associated with older age drove the rise in SH prevalence post-pandemic, while there was again insufficient statistical evidence on which specific determinants’ coefficient effects were the main drivers for SI prevalence rise.

Sensitivity tests

Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine temporal heterogeneity across survey waves and the robustness of the peri-pandemic wave aggregation strategy. Comparisons of key outcomes and psychosocial determinants across the 2021, 2022, and 2024 waves indicated significant differences for several variables, although effect sizes were generally small. While some indicators in 2022 appeared closer to 2024 than to 2021, differences between 2022 and 2024 remained evident for multiple outcomes and determinants, suggesting a gradual transition over time. Separate decomposition analyses comparing 2021 versus 2024 and 2022 versus 2024 yielded patterns broadly consistent with the primary findings. Across subgroups, year-specific analyses either identified determinants contributing in the same direction as the combined-wave analyses or showed non-significant peri-to-post differences that limited decomposition feasibility. Notably, the strengthening association between bullying victimization and SITB, alongside the weakening association observed for bullying perpetration in the post-pandemic period, remained evident across the sensitivity analyses. The combined 2021–2022 analyses provided greater statistical stability and feasibility for rare outcomes and smaller subgroups while yielding patterns that were largely consistent with those observed in the separate wave comparisons. Full results are reported in Supplementary Tables S16-18.

Discussion

This study examined psychosocial factors associated with peri-to-post-pandemic changes in SI, SH, and SA prevalence among young population. Using decomposition analyses, we distinguished changes attributable to shifts in the distribution of determinants from changes in their observed associations with SITB outcomes. We further examined these patterns across bullying-involvement subgroups to identify potential targets for tailored preventive interventions in the post-pandemic context. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation to pinpoint which specific aspects of psychosocial determinants could be associated with SITB risks within Asian Pacific post-pandemic societal contexts, particularly among adolescents and young adults (AYAs) involved in bullying.

There are three main findings arising from our study. First, we observed an increase in SI, SH, and SA prevalence in the general young population post-pandemic. This trend extends earlier evidence based on observations up to 2023 [6, 7, 62] that AYAs’ mental health was still worse off than pre-pandemic levels well into 2024, thus suggesting a longer-term trend and an imperative for the international community’s sustained, stepped-up efforts to more effectively safeguard AYAs’ mental wellbeing.

Second, results here highlighted a consistently strong association between bullying involvement and SITB among the young population, even after accounting for effects of various psychosocial determinants. Bullying involvement’s associations here were stronger than those of mental disorders and gender, corroborating recent findings by Scott et al. [30] that targeted interpersonal rejection functioned as a particularly strong proximal risk factor of SITB. Moreover, we further revealed distinct patterns by which SI, SH, and SA risks diverged between perpetrators and victims of bullying. While risks in the post-pandemic period decreased for perpetrators, they increased for victims, suggesting that more resources should be devoted to caring for bully victims in today’s post-pandemic societal context.

The above contrast also indicates that as society transitioned out of the pandemic, changes in risk and protective factors of SITB might have varied across distinct at-risk segments of the young population [63]. These findings are broadly consistent with interpersonal models of suicide [64], which emphasize belongingness and interpersonal relationships in shaping suicide risk. The divergent post-pandemic trajectories observed among perpetrators and victims suggest that social reconnection following the pandemic may not confer uniform benefits across young people. Although the present study cannot directly test these mechanisms, the findings raise the possibility that changes in social connectedness may alleviate interpersonal vulnerabilities for some groups while exacerbating them for others.

For example, bully perpetrators (especially girls [65]) might benefit more from a return to face-to-face interactions and the reestablishment of social ties and support networks, thereby restoring their sense of belongingness [66] and reducing their distress. This interpretation is consistent with the observed post-pandemic decline in perpetrators’ SITB prevalence despite the continued importance of depressive and anxiety symptomatology. In contrast, bully victims might have become more exposed to face-to-face targeted interpersonal rejection following the resumption of in-person activities, potentially increasing their SITB risks. Post-pandemic changes in social norms and patterns of social integration may be relevant to understanding young people’s experiences of belongingness and interpersonal relationships [32]. Alternative explanations, including differences in unmeasured characteristics such as bullying severity, social support, coping resources, or help-seeking behaviors across survey periods, may also contribute to the observed patterns. Accordingly, these interpretations should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive explanations. Other determinants, such as pessimism toward society, meaning in life, and close social proximity to others who have attempted suicide, were found to influence either SI or SH risks individually, but did not exhibit consistent or universal effects. Findings here could be extended with further empirical verification into perpetrators and especially victims’ vulnerabilities to build a more robust evidence base for tailored prevention strategies.

Third, we found insufficient statistical evidence for various determinants’ association with peri-to-post-pandemic changes of SITB prevalence, despite significant changes in the determinants’ prevalence and coefficients. Statistically insignificant associations of endowment and coefficient effects for determinants such as living alone, work/study hours, social withdrawal, mental illness diagnosis, and pessimism towards society suggest limited evidence that the observed associations between these determinants and SITB differed across periods. On the other hand, the absence of specific drivers for group- and subgroup-level increases in SA prevalence might be attributed to two reasons. One possibility is that we had not considered determinants that were pertinent to specifically SA, such as access to means [67], episodes of acute crisis [68], or specific traits of impulsivity [69]. Additionally, given the naturally low 12-month prevalence of SA in the general population, statistical power to detect significant effects may have been inherently limited in several subgroup analyses. Consequently, coefficient estimates for SA should be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the rarity of outcome events among individuals not involved in bullying. These aspects underscore the need for a future sufficiently powered investigation, e.g., relying on recruitment through social services and clinical settings, to more specifically consider SA-specific determinants.

The following briefly foregrounds the study’s implications. First, the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition has significant implications for future research. The decomposition findings suggest that associations between psychosocial determinants and SITB may differ across social contexts and historical periods. Future research is needed to identify the specific contextual mechanisms underlying these changing associations. Second, bullying involvement should be prioritized as a critical factor in AYAs’ suicide risk screening and assessment models, as it is strongly associated with an increased risk of SITBs compared to internal emotional symptoms. Finally, suicide intervention and prevention efforts should be implemented in a role-specific and stage-specific manner. In light of post-pandemic changes in the effects of SITB risk factors, targeted measures should be adopted for both perpetrators and victims to ensure the efficient allocation of limited resources.

Several limitations in this study must nevertheless be noted when considering the above findings. First, all measures relied on participants’ self-reports, which were liable to recall bias and social desirability effects. Findings here should thus be interpreted with caution, particularly when generalizing to settings beyond the general young population in Asia Pacific. Second, bullying involvement was assessed using broad binary indicators covering the previous 12 months. This approach did not distinguish between traditional and cyberbullying, nor did it capture variation in intensity, frequency, or specific forms of bullying. Given evidence that these forms of bullying may differ in their social dynamics and psychological consequences, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting overall bullying involvement rather than specific bullying experiences. Future research could employ a finer-grained classification of bullying (e.g., cyberbullying versus traditional bullying) to more thoroughly examine the influence of bullying involvement on SITB in the post-pandemic era.

Third, although all decomposition models converged successfully, the relatively low prevalence of suicide attempts resulted in sparse outcome events in some subgroup analyses, particularly among participants uninvolved in bullying. This may have reduced statistical power, increased uncertainty in coefficient estimates, and limited the ability to detect meaningful decomposition effects. Consequently, findings related to SA should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating pending replication in larger or higher-risk samples.

Fourth, coefficient effects identified in the decomposition analyses should be interpreted cautiously. Though the decomposition analyses suggested that observed associations between psychosocial determinants and SITB differed across survey periods, the specific contextual processes underlying these differences could not be directly identified. More broadly, the present decomposition analyses should be viewed as a first step in identifying whether peri-to-post-pandemic differences in SITB were associated with changes in determinants or changes in their observed associations with outcomes. Identifying the specific contextual mechanisms responsible for these patterns remains an important priority for future research.

Finally, sensitivity analyses suggested that psychosocial adaptation might have occurred gradually across the study period rather than at a single discrete time point. Nevertheless, the primary findings remained robust across alternative wave comparisons, supporting the use of WHO and local policy milestones as a transparent framework for distinguishing peri-pandemic and post-pandemic contexts. Future studies incorporating additional intermediate time points may provide a more nuanced understanding of how psychosocial determinants and SITB risks evolved throughout the transition from pandemic to post-pandemic conditions.

Conclusions

Rising SITB prevalence in the 2024 post-pandemic young population exemplifies a renewed urgency to sustain efforts in safeguarding adolescents and young adults’ mental wellbeing, particularly for those involved in bullying. Contextual changes might have benefited perpetrators’ mental health, but victims were now more vulnerable to SITBs and would require special attention in today’s societal context. Further empirical evidence on risk and protective factors for young bully victims and their lived experiences could inform suicide risk stratification and tailored preventive interventions.
