Perceived Self‐Efficacy in Decision‐Making: The Influence of Personality and Cognitive Variables
Authors: Valeria Sebri, Clizia Cincidda, Gabriella Pravettoni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveDecision self‐efficacy reflects individuals' confidence in their ability to make effective health‐related decisions. In oncology, it is essential to foster active engagement throughout the healthcare journey. Despite its relevance for quality of life, limited evidence exists on the factors influencing decision self‐efficacy among cancer survivors. This exploratory study examined the role of personality traits, metacognition, coping strategies, and social support in predicting decision self‐efficacy in breast cancer survivors.Methods187 breast cancer survivors voluntarily completed online questionnaires assessing socio‐demographic variables and standardized measures. Correlational analyses, multiple regressions, and mediation analyses were conducted.ResultsFindings evidenced that conscientiousness and cognitive self‐consciousness positively predicted decision self‐efficacy, whereas agreeableness showed a negative association. Problem‐focused coping was positively related to decision self‐efficacy; however, this effect was not significant when social support was included. Significant‐other support and cognitive self‐consciousness emerged as additional positive predictors. Mediation analysis indicated that agreeableness negatively influenced decision self‐efficacy when mediated by problem‐focused coping strategies.DiscussionKnowing individual and social characteristics that foster decision self‐efficacy can be crucial to promote well‐being in breast cancer survivors, decreasing anxiety and distress about their health.Keywords: breast cancer survivors, coping, decision self‐efficacy, metacognition, personality, psycho‐oncology, social support

Introduction

Breast cancer survivors had to make several health decisions over their cancer journey. Some of them are more complicated and even stressful, being decisions related to treatment options and their short/long‐term implications [1, 2]. Perceiving control, which refers to belief in individuals' ability to bring changes that will result in desirable outcomes, is crucial for the decision‐making process [3, 4]. Literature underlined that perceiving a decision control influences the overall well‐being positively, increasing skills in coping with distress, functional behavioral changes, and cognitive performances [5, 6, 7]. Indeed, individuals' attitudes and personality tendencies strongly influence patients' perceived efficacy, underlining the central role of personal beliefs in shaping health‐related decisions [8]. Conversely, an impaired perception of control over decisions prompt compensatory, unhealthy behaviors (e.g., following superstitions), increasing emotional issues, such as anxiety and depression [9]. In this regard, Bandura and the social‐cognitive theory (1986) affirmed that self‐efficacy involves various models that comprise specific behavioral domains or tasks [10]. Following this multidimensional approach, current literature shows that several individuals' variables can influence health decisions.

Firstly, personality plays a relevant role in decision‐making and decision self‐efficacy [11, 12, 13]. Personality traits are defined as individuals' thinking, feelings, and behaviors that are typically the result of the interaction between biology and the external environment [14]. Except for some pathological conditions, personality traits are intrinsic and relatively stable across time [15]. Big Five traits comprises the basic dimensions of personality and are: neuroticism (that refers to emotional instability and inability to adjustment), extroversion (the tendency to be active, outgoing, energetic, and assertive to experience positive emotions), consciousness (characterized by goal‐directed, careful, and persistent behaviors, resisting impulses and temptations), agreeableness (i.e., tendency to be sympathetic and altruistic toward others, emphasized from compromises and conflict solution approaches into interpersonal relationship), and openness (the cognitive disposition to esthetics and creativity). Particularly, neuroticism, extroversion, and conscientiousness are linked to the decision‐making process, while agreeableness and openness are less consistently associated with it [16]. Specifically, indecisions correlated positively to neuroticism [17] and negatively to extroversion and consciousness [17, 18]. Referring to decision self‐efficacy, a study published by Penn and colleagues (2019) demonstrated that each of the personality traits is related to it [12]. Moreover, in a study by Zulkifli et al. (2021), conscientiousness has a moderate association with decision‐making self‐efficacy [19].

Second, metacognitive abilities and coping skills are other relevant constructs in the field of decision making. Metacognition is defined as how individuals monitor and control thinking, thanks to both introspective and self‐regulatory processes [20]. Metacognitive abilities may lead to increased performance goals focused upon oneself and, sometimes, situations in which one demonstrates abilities by comparing them to other people [21]. Specifically referring to decisions, knowing one's metacognitive process is positively associated with intuitive and spontaneous decision‐making, while skills in cognition regulation are associated with a rational decision‐making style [22]. Being aware and focusing on cognitive processes as a part of self‐consciousness can help in decision‐making to assess each option as much as possible [23]. Otherwise, the regulation process of decision, as a need to control thought, emerged as the strongest predictor of dysfunctional behaviors, such as addictive attitudes [24].

Moreover, coping styles play a relevant role in decision self‐efficacy, affecting quality of life and patient satisfaction with care. In a study by Yildiz et al. (2023), the authors suggested that patients' decision‐making role is a key component of their coping style, considering them as two parts of one process, and not a separate phenomena [25]. More specifically, authors stated that people with problem‐focused coping who show high decision self‐efficacy have better physical outcomes than those who implement fewer active coping strategies. They tend to be more engaged in their healthcare process, being confident in interacting with physicians, and showing proactive behavior to solve issues. In other words, they are more likely to play an active, rather than a passive role in making decisions [26].

Beyond individual characteristics, decisions in the healthcare context are strongly influenced by the perceived social support [27]. The presence of a caregiver, for example, can support patients in better coping with diagnosis and its related therapies and treatments, starting from the exploration of treatment options and through also the support of tailored decision aids [28, 29]. Caregivers tend to participate in medical consultations, interacting with oncologists and providing a relevant contribution in terms of opinions and thoughts to healthcare decisions [30]. Recent evidence further emphasizes the centrality of caregivers in the cancer care pathway, highlighting that patients prefer to share treatment decisions with their caregivers or make decisions after receiving his/her feedback, and that such dyadic involvement may reduce psychological distress and improve quality of life [31, 32]. Psychological interventions specifically targeting patients and caregivers together could enhance alignment in decision‐making and improve treatment adherence [33]. Not only families are involved in decision‐making; social support can be characterized by suggestions and emotional support received from others, such as peers and friends, with implications for emotions [34]. Others can be crucial in making decisions with a more conscious and mindful approach, decreasing anxiety and distress [35]. However, caregivers' involvement is not always free from difficulties, as patient–caregiver dyads may experience moral conflicts or divergent perceptions of illness and caregiving responsibilities, with possible negative implications for the care process [36, 37].

Despite the previous background, there is a lack of current literature regarding the influence of personality traits, metacognition, social support, and coping on decision self‐efficacy in breast cancer survivors. The present study aims to explore the impact of personality traits, metacognition, coping, and social support on decision self‐efficacy, specifically focused on the perception of women who had received a breast cancer diagnosis in the past. Considering the aforementioned background, in this study we expected that: (Hp1) Personality traits could have a significant impact on decision self‐efficacy; in particular, consciousness and extroversion could affect decision self‐efficacy positively, while agreeableness negatively; (Hp2) Social support could positively impact on decision self‐efficacy, with a significant role played by others and family; (Hp3) Metacognition and, in particular, cognitive confidence and cognitive self‐consciousness could influence decision; and (Hp4) Coping skills could influence decision‐self efficacy, with a significant impact of problem‐focused coping on decision self‐efficacy specifically.

Methods

Participants

187 breast cancer survivors participated in the study. All of them have been diagnosed with breast cancer and were out of the primary treatment(s) in the previous 5 years. Participants who were excluded from the study if they: (a) were younger than 18 years old, (b) had cognitive impairments and/or severe mental illness, (c) did not complete all the questionnaires, and (d) were not fluent in Italian.

Procedure

The invitation to participate in this study was posted on online social networks (i.e., Facebook) and sent via a commercial mailing list. Moreover, women were welcomed to share the invitation to participate in the research study providing contacts of researchers (e.g., email address) and following a snowball sampling technique to increase the number of participants. Thus, a self‐selection method was applied to recruit participants interested in the present project. They received an email with a cover letter, explaining the goal and procedure of the study. After agreeing to participate in the study and confirming their eligibility, women received an online link. Clicking on this, women first signed an informed consent statement; then, they were asked to answer the anonymous survey questions to test our hypothesis. The online survey was available in July 2025 and lasted about 20 min. Participation was voluntary, and no reward was given. The current study was approved by the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committee, in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Measures

Sociodemographic information (i.e., age, marital status, highest educational level achieved, employment, place of residence), and if they received psychological treatment were collected at the beginning of the survey. Moreover, a battery of self‐administered, standardized questionnaires was administered, as follows:

The short version of the original Big Five Inventory (BFI‐S) by Rammstedt et al. (2010) [38] is a self‐report questionnaire rated on a 5‐point scale (1 = strongly disagree; 5 = strongly agree) [39] that measures five personality dimensions—extraversion, openness to experience, conscientiousness, emotional instability, and agreeableness—with two items per trait. It has been validated across countries [40], including an Italian version published by Guido et al. (2015) [41]. Internal consistency, assessed via Spearman–Brown coefficients, ranged from low to moderate: Extraversion ρ = 0.42, Agreeableness ρ = 0.11, Conscientiousness ρ = 0.01, Neuroticism ρ = 0.66, and Openness ρ = 0.36.

The brief version of the original COPE Inventory by Carver et al. (1997; 1989) [42, 43] is a 28‐item questionnaire with a 4‐point scale (“I absolutely do not do this” to “I am used to doing this”) to assess coping strategies. Items are grouped into three categories: problem‐focused coping (active coping, planning, instrumental support), emotion‐focused coping (acceptance, humor, positive reframing, emotional support), and dysfunctional coping (denial, behavioral disengagement, self‐distraction, self‐blame, substance use, venting). Participants rate how often they use each strategy to handle stress. The scale's reliability has been confirmed by Numsang and Tantrarungroj (2018) [44] and validated across countries [44, 45]. The short version reduces response time without compromising psychometric quality [46, 47, 48]. Reliability coefficients in this study were: Problem‐Focused Coping α = 0.691, Emotion‐Focused Coping α = 0.705, and Dysfunctional Coping α = 0.675.

The Metacognitions Questionnaire (MCQ‐30) by Scardaccione (2008) [49] is a 30‐item scale based on a 4‐point Likert format (1 = do not agree to 4 = completely agree) to assess metacognitive beliefs and processes related to emotional disorder vulnerability and maintenance. It includes five subscales: Cognitive Confidence (trust in attention and memory), Cognitive Self‐Consciousness (tendency to monitor one's thoughts), Positive Beliefs about Worry (viewing worry as helpful), Negative Beliefs about Worry (perceiving worry as uncontrollable or dangerous), and Need to Control Thoughts (belief that certain thoughts must be suppressed). Higher scores indicate more dysfunctional beliefs. The Italian validation showed good internal consistency (α = 0.87) with subscale alphas ranging from 0.71 to 0.87. In this study, overall reliability was good (α = 0.875), with subscale alphas of: Cognitive Confidence α = 0.694, Positive Beliefs about Worry α = 0.921, Negative Beliefs about Worry α = 0.800, Need to Control Thoughts α = 0.559, and Cognitive Self‐Consciousness α = 0.281.

The Multi‐dimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (MSPSS) by Zimet et al. (1988) [50] is a 12‐item scale rated on a 7‐point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree) that measures perceived social support from three sources: family, friends, and significant others (e.g., colleagues or classmates). Each subscale includes four items, summed to yield total scores for each support type. In this study, reliability was excellent, with Cronbach's alphas of 0.930 for significant others, 0.955 for family, and 0.972 for friends.

Decision Self‐Efficacy (DSES) by O'Connor (1995) [51] is a 11‐item scale based on a 5‐point rating system to assess individuals' self‐confidence and belief in their ability to make decisions and participate in SDM, reflecting their perceived certainty in making informed choices [52]. Total scores are calculated by summing item scores and linearly transforming them to a 0–100 scale, where 0 indicates very low and 100 very high self‐efficacy. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity across populations. In this study, internal consistency was excellent (α = 0.924).

Data Analysis

A descriptive analysis (mean and standard deviation scores) was performed to explore socio‐demographics and psychological scores in breast cancer survivors. Moreover, Pearson's correlation coefficients were executed to identify which variables were related to decision self‐efficacy, considering psychological and personality variables. Then, a hierarchical multiple regression assessed predictors of decision self‐efficacy. Lastly, a parallel mediation model was tested using PROCESS Model 4, with 5000 bootstrap samples.

A power analysis was conducted to ensure meaningful and statistically significant results [53]. Analyses were run with the software G∗Power [54], with power (1—b) set at 0.95, a medium effect size (one‐tailed test), and a 5% level of significance. Data highlighted that we needed a total sample size of 80 participants to detect a large effect size. Our sample estimates are higher than this hypothetical suggestion, ensuring a good level of power for the detection of effects. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic Characteristics

A total of 187 women with a history of breast cancer participated in the study. Participants' ages ranged from 27 to 74 years (M = 54.15, SD = 8.41), with time since diagnosis varying from 1 to 28 years (M = 8.12, SD = 5.20). The majority resided in Northern Italy (69.4%, n = 129), followed by the Center (16.7%, n = 31) and the South/Islands (14.0%, n = 26). Regarding educational attainment, 34.2% (n = 64) had completed upper secondary school, 42.8% (n = 80) held a bachelor's or master's degree, and 23.0% (n = 43) a postgraduate qualification. Employment status indicated that 80% were white‐collar workers, 5.4% blue‐collar workers, and 14.6% retired or homemakers. Marital status distribution was as follows: single (18.9%), married or cohabiting (58.3%), divorced or separated (21.4%), and widowed (0.5%). Most participants had children (67.4%), with 62.6% having one or two children. Engagement in psychotherapy was reported by 27.3% (n = 51), while 72.7% (n = 136) were not receiving psychotherapy at the time of assessment. Decision self‐efficacy was positively associated with age (r = 0.176, p < 0.05), suggesting that older participants perceived themselves as more confident in managing health‐related decisions. Among metacognitive dimensions, cognitive self‐consciousness was positively related to decision self‐efficacy (META_CSC; r = 0.187, p < 0.05), suggesting that individuals who are more inwardly attentive and tend to monitor their own thoughts may experience greater confidence in making health‐related decisions. Regarding personality traits, only conscientiousness (r = 0.220, p < 0.01) was positively related to decision self‐efficacy, indicating that patients, who are characterized by traits such as self‐discipline, organization, goal orientation, and a strong sense of responsibility, tend to feel more confident in their ability to make informed and autonomous health‐related decisions. Moreover, among the coping strategies assessed, only problem‐focused coping was positively associated with decision self‐efficacy (r = 0.227, p < 0.01). This indicates that individuals who tend to engage in active efforts to directly manage or solve stress‐inducing situations also perceive themselves as more capable of making effective decisions regarding their health. Finally, decision self‐efficacy showed positive correlations with perceived social support from significant others (r = 0.241, p < 0.01), family (r = 0.242, p < 0.01), and total support (r = 0.196, p < 0.05), highlighting the importance of close relational bonds in sustaining confidence in managing health‐related decision.

Multiple Regression Analysis

A hierarchical multiple regression was conducted to examine predictors of decision self‐efficacy (see Table 4). In Model 1, sociodemographic variables (age, education, time since diagnosis) were included, yielding a non‐significant model, F (3,154) = 1.81, p = 0.147, R 2 = 0.034. Model 2 added metacognitive beliefs, which did not significantly improve the model (ΔR2 = 0.001, p = 0.681). Model 3, which added personality traits (conscientiousness, agreeableness, extraversion, openness, emotional stability), significantly improved prediction, F (9,148) = 2.47, p = 0.012, R 2 = 0.131. Within this model, conscientiousness (β = 0.235, p = 0.005) positively predicted self‐efficacy, while agreeableness (β = −0.194, p = 0.017) had a negative effect. Model 4 introduced coping strategies (problem‐focused, emotion‐focused, dysfunctional), leading to an increase in variance explained (R 2 = 0.168), although the change was not statistically significant (ΔR2 = 0.037, p = 0.094). Problem‐focused coping was a significant predictor (β = 0.176, p = 0.037). Model 5 included perceived social support, which significantly contributed to the prediction (ΔR2 = 0.025, p = 0.035), with social support being a significant predictor (β = 0.178, p = 0.035). The final model explained 19.4% of the variance in decision self‐efficacy, F (13,144) = 2.66, p = 0.002. In this final model, conscientiousness remained a significant positive predictor (β = 0.214, p = 0.009), agreeableness retained its significant negative association (β = −0.255, p = 0.002), and perceived social support also significantly predicted decision self‐efficacy (β = 0.178, p = 0.035). Although problem‐focused coping was a significant predictor of decision self‐efficacy in the fourth model, its effect became non‐significant in the final model when perceived social support was added. This suggests that the influence of coping strategies may partially overlap with the effects of social support, reducing its unique contribution in the presence of strong interpersonal resources.

Parallel Mediation Analyses

The model tested whether three coping styles (problem‐focused coping, emotion‐focused coping, and dysfunctional coping) mediated the relationship between agreeableness and decision self‐efficacy. The total effect of agreeableness on decision self‐efficacy was not statistically significant (c = −1.746, SE = 1.115, 95% CI [–3.95, 0.45], t = −1.57, p = 0.119). However, when the three coping strategies were included as mediators, the direct effect of agreeableness on self‐efficacy became significant and negative (c’ = −2.792, SE = 1.144, 95% CI [–5.05, −0.533], t = −2.44, p = 0.016), suggesting a suppression effect. Specifically, agreeableness showed a small, non‐significant positive association with problem‐focused coping (a 1 = 0.293, 95% CI [–0.40, 0.63]), a significant positive association with emotion‐focused coping (a 2 = 0.857, 95% CI [0.34, 1.37], p < 0.01), and a significant negative association with dysfunctional coping (a 3 = −0.485, 95% CI [–0.96, −0.002], p < 0.05). Among the mediators, problem‐focused coping significantly and positively predicted decision self‐efficacy (b 1 = 1.592, 95% CI [0.547, 2.637], p < 0.01), while emotion‐focused (b 2 = 0.418, 95% CI [–0.252, 1.088]) and dysfunctional coping (b 3 = −0.455, 95% CI [–1.15, 0.24]) were not significant. The total indirect effect of agreeableness on decision self‐efficacy through the three coping styles was significant (B = 1.046, BootSE = 0.518, 95% CI [0.108, 2.143]), indicating that the coping variables partially mediated the relationship between agreeableness and self‐efficacy. However, individual indirect effects did not reach significance at the 95% confidence level: Problem‐focused coping: B = 0.467, BootCI [–0.014, 1.187]; Emotion‐focused coping: B = 0.359, BootCI [–0.269, 1.217]; Dysfunctional coping: B = 0.221, BootCI [–0.137, 0.794]. In summary, although agreeableness was not directly associated with decision self‐efficacy in the total effect model, its relationship became significant and negative after accounting for coping strategies. This pattern suggests a suppression effect, whereby the positive indirect effect of agreeableness through problem‐focused coping was masked by a negative direct effect. In other words, individuals with higher agreeableness may be more inclined to use adaptive coping strategies, which in turn enhance self‐efficacy, but their interpersonal style may also reflect a tendency to defer decision‐making or prioritize others' needs over their own, thereby diminishing their perceived decision‐making confidence when coping is not accounted for (see Figure 1).

Discussion

The present exploratory study provides evidence regarding the association between breast cancer psychological characteristics and decision‐making. Specifically, we explored whether personality traits, coping strategies, social support, and metacognition abilities impact the perception of self‐efficacy in making decisions in women who received a breast cancer diagnosis.

In line with Hp1, we found an association between consciousness and decision self‐efficacy, as supported by the literature [11]. More specifically, consciousness results as a personality trait that positively impacts decision self‐efficacy. This is in line with the study by Penn et al. (2019), in which the planfulness tendency associated with consciousness, as well as the cognition persistence abilities, influences decision‐making [12]. Similarly, Ngcamu et al. (2023) reported that consciousness characterized people who carefully planned future actions and paid attention to details in making decisions [55]. In the breast cancer field, women show high health consciousness as their perceptual orientation toward health, with engagement in healthy behaviors, motivation, and responsibility, which results in a sense of self‐efficacy [56]. Furthermore, the positive impact of consciousness on decision self‐efficacy may be explained by goal‐directed, careful, and persistent behaviors that characterized this personality trait and lead individuals to need to make decisions, increasing self‐efficacy decision perceptions [7, 57]. In line with our Hp1, agreeableness has a medium, negative impact on decision self‐efficacy. Duru et al. (2021) reported that agreeableness could lead to the insufficient expression of personal ideas aiming at avoiding conflict with others [58]. Thus, this trait may hinder the development of new insights and problem‐solving behaviors, making it a personality trait that could be ineffective in the healthcare context of decisions. However, it is crucial to consider that the low internal consistency of this variable may affect the results, potentially highlighting its negative influence on decision‐making. Interestingly, although previous studies (e.g., [12]) have highlighted the role of extraversion in supporting decision‐making, considering positive affectivity and sociability as some of the main characteristics, our findings did not show a significant association with decision self‐efficacy. This lack of correlation may be due to overlapping influences of social support, which could have diminished the unique contribution of extraversion in our sample.

In this regard, social support significantly impacts decision self‐efficacy (Hp2). Particularly, significant others promote healthcare decisions and reduce conflict, which is in line with the current literature [59]. As proposed by Lazarus and Folkman (1984), social support could be beneficial to cope with distress. In the oncological context specifically, emotional support provided by others can alleviate the complex medical decisions' pressure, offering guidance for decisions, psychological reassurance, and practical support in following medical duties [60]. Within a biopsychosocial overview, patients need to rely on their significant other to guide decision‐making, considering the relevant role played by the external context [61]. On a theoretical background, Rapley and colleagues (2008), who proposed the concept of “distributed decision‐making” [62], and Epstein and Street (2011) with their “shared mind” concept, pointed out how decisions can be the result of social interactions [63]. However, perceived support from friends was not significantly associated with decision self‐efficacy. This suggests that not all types of support may be equally significant in health‐related decision‐making [64]. As known, not all sources of social support are beneficial. Literature defines negative social support as unsupportive interactions wherein others may not have the best‐intended goals [65]. Accordingly, illness disclosure and requests for support are challenging for cancer patients. This is in line with evidence that patients and caregivers may perceive illness and care responsibilities differently [37]. Such discrepancies may complicate the decisional process, highlighting the importance of acknowledging multiple viewpoints within the family system [37]. Indeed, adopting a dyadic perspective in cancer care is, therefore, crucial: caregiver involvement in treatment decision‐making can have a protective role for both patients and caregivers, improving quality of life and reducing distress [31]. Classic theories assert that individuals seek help from friends when family members do not provide adequate support [66]. However, openness to friends about the disease and its implications is distressing due to the fear of causing additional worry in others. Choid and colleagues (2016) highlight the relevant role of confidence in sharing information, where experiencing the lack of intimacy can result in an invaluable perception of support [67]. Moreover, women could conceal their emotions and psychological difficulties about cancer and its consequences to avoid concerns for others, which could sometimes evoke internalized shame [68]. Similarly, moral conflicts within patient–caregiver dyads may undermine the decisional process. As suggested by Cincidda and colleagues (2025), conflicts concerning values, interests, or autonomy represent critical challenges that may weaken patients' confidence in their ability to make decisions, thereby influencing decision self‐efficacy [36].

Referring to Hp3, cognitive self‐consciousness was low‐to‐medium positively associated with decision self‐efficacy. Our study showed that higher age and cognitive self‐consciousness had a medium impact on higher decision self‐efficacy among breast cancer survivors. Eldest breast cancer survivors could indeed tend to be more reflexive and goal‐directed in making decisions in the healthcare context, thanks to their personal experience. However, there was no impact of metacognition on decision self‐efficacy. As reported in the literature, abilities in the metacognitive process could increase the tendency to compare an individual's abilities to other results, increasing anxiety. Negative beliefs about worry may be particularly crucial for anxiety issues [69, 70]. Therefore, it would be possible that people with high metacognition may tend to express their anxiety first, which decreases the involvement in decision‐making and decision self‐efficacy. In this regard, breast cancer survivors experience anxiety and distress issues even some years after treatments, due to the fear of cancer recurrence and difficulties in coming back to their daily life routine before cancer.

Lastly, problem‐focused coping is a direct, significant predictor of decision self‐efficacy in the fourth model of our findings. However, integrating the perceived social support, its effect became non‐significant in the final model (Hp4). Social support could partially overlap with the effects of coping strategies as a key point in the breast cancer survivors' well‐being, as specified above (see Hp2). Additionally, the non‐impact of emotional strategies on decision self‐efficacy could be related to the role of the management of emotional experiences in breast cancer survivors. As is known, breast cancer survivors show relevant difficulties in the regulation of emotions due to cancer‐related issues that damage their well‐being [71, 72]. A study by Benedict et al. (2023) demonstrated that breast cancer survivors showed avoidant coping as a response to their emotional distress [73]. Moreover, authors demonstrated that distress and avoidant coping negatively influenced decisions in the healthcare context, where avoidance is recognized as a common approach to face distressing events. In other words, it is a helpful way to manage negative emotions elicited by health threats to decrease uncertainty [74]. However, when persistent over time, avoidance coping tends to underlie negative emotions, avoiding addressing decision making (e.g., interfering with information seeking) and increasing depression, anxiety, and self‐blame [73]. Similarly, coping resources are predicted by individual differences, such as willingness to adapt oneself to the external environment and cognitive flexibility skills [75, 76]. Women after oncological treatments need to foster their cognitive skills, such as attention and flexibility, due to the chemo brain effect. Studies specifically reported the relevance of implementing tailored interventions focused on cognitions [77], highlighting the importance of improving quality of life and decision‐making.

Furthermore, to further explore which variables strongly impact decision self‐efficacy, we conducted a mediation analysis. Based on the previous significant findings, we assessed whether coping mediates the relationship between personality traits and decision self‐efficacy. The mediation analysis revealed a suppression effect in the relationship between agreeableness and decision self‐efficacy. While the total effect of agreeableness was not significant, the direct effect became significant and negative when coping strategies were included as mediators. This suggests that agreeableness, as an adaptive coping strategy, was masked by an adverse direct effect, which could reflect a tendency to prioritize others' needs and defer their decisions. As supported by the literature, agreeableness could support the expression of problem‐focused strategies [78], as an active coping strategy that favors self‐efficacy in cancer‐related decisions. In the oncological context, the tendency to be active and open to face cancer‐related problems could enhance shared decision making, with positive outcomes on treatment adherence and clinical outcomes [79]. At the same time, being active in their cancer journey can enhance their positive self‐perception in reference to decisions, supporting a patient‐centered approach [80]. However, the negative impact on decision self‐efficacy could be explained by the tendency to be accommodating and passive of agreeable individuals, which is in contrast with having an active role within the cancer journey. Individuals characterized by agreeableness may tend not to make decisions alone, due to their need to be supported by others, which can impair their perception of self‐efficacy, whereas decisions are perceived as more shared. In this regard, a study by Byram and Aydemir (2017) found that agreeableness has a significant and positive impact on dependent decisions, highlighting the relevant role of others [81].

Clinical Implications and Future Research

The present findings could be helpful to design future psychological assessment and interventions even more personalized for breast cancer survivors' needs [82]. In this regard, Artificial Intelligence could be crucial to collect and integrate a huge amount of information [83] to tailor decision aids, with also a specific interest in fostering women's self‐efficacy in making decisions. Providing personalized tools may increase the possibility of being active and engaged in the process of care and future healthcare controls, increasing desirable outcomes. Otherwise, it could decrease fear of cancer recurrence, thanks to a great control perception [72]. For these reasons, professionals should be specifically trained to accept and implement Artificial Intelligence into clinical practice, leading to future interventions that could be even more based on the whole individuals' characteristics, ranging from personality traits to coping skills [84]. Moreover, future studies should include other cancer types to assess potential differences in decision self‐efficacy, while ensuring data homogeneity. Longitudinal research may also be needed to further support these findings and to better examine the causal relationships among the variables. In this regard, future longitudinal studies could investigate other individual and social factors to deepen the understanding of this topic. Additionally, the low internal consistency of certain scales, particularly Agreeableness, should be addressed in future studies to better understand its impact on decision‐making. Finally, integrating a qualitative approach with the quantitative design could provide a more comprehensive exploration of the phenomenon.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present findings highlight the relevant role of individuals and social variables on decision self‐efficacy. In particular, this study suggests that consciousness, significant others, problem‐focused coping, and cognitive self‐consciousness are significant, positive factors associated with decision self‐efficacy, whereas agreeableness and friends are negatively linked to it. Additionally, agreeable women after breast cancer tend to perceive low self‐efficacy, as the role mediated by problem‐focused coping skills. Evaluating these variables in a psychological assessment over the cancer journey could be essential to assess how to support women's decision self‐efficacy and, therefore, foster their well‐being. It would be beneficial to stress the engagement in the process of care, supporting their active role and a shared decision‐making approach. Otherwise, our findings evidence the need to better explore the role of problem‐focused coping in the influence of agreeableness on decision self‐efficacy in particular. Starting from these results, further research is required to shed sufficient light on this point.
