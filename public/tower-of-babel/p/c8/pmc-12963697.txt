Unphysical Structure Collapse in Quantum Mechanics Calculations With Continuum Solvation
Authors: Andreas H Göller, Dieudonné T Tshitenge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe work reports on issues of the polarizable continuum solvent models conductor‐like polarizable continuum model (CPCM) and solvation model density (SMD) to adequately describe the relative conformer energies of drug‐like molecules that fit the quite common general structural pattern of two aromatic segments connected by a flexible linker. Conformer ensembles derived either from a quantum mechanics (QM)‐based CREST workflow or from optimized potentials for liquid simulations (OPLS4) force‐field based macromodel Monte Carlo multiple minimum (MCMM) torsional sampling, after r2SCAN‐3c optimization and r2SCAN‐d3(BJ)/aug‐cc‐pVTZ result exclusively in collapsed π‐stacking conformations. OPLS4 optimizations with generalized‐Born/surface‐area (GB/SA) continuum solvent model result almost exclusively in semistretched and stretched conformations. 1D and 2D nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) experiments, on the other hand, clearly show no hydrogen‐shielding nor any other hints for the occurrence of collapsed conformations under various NOESY conditions. Explicit solvation molecular dynamics simulations are consistent with NMR experiments in that they reveal maximally about 5% population of collapsed conformations in explicit water and 0.2% in DMSO. We conclude that PCM as implemented in all major quantum chemistry codes does not reflect the real nature of solvents consisting of molecules with specific and directed solvent–solvent and solvent–solute interactions and in addition missing entropic contributions and by this continuum solvation contributions result in unphysical conformational ensembles.Keywords: conformers, continuum solvation, DFT, molecular dynamics, NMR

Introduction

Starting point of this study was the observation that drug candidate molecules consisting of aromatic or lipophilic aliphatic moieties connected by flexible linkers with rotatable bonds tend to form folded conformations with strong lipophilic or π‐stacking contacts in ab initio Hartree–Fock or density functional theory (DFT) calculations if a water continuum solvation model was applied. We therefore investigated if those conformations that we felt looked strange were real or an artifact of the calculation.

Thorough literature review revealed no investigations so far on this topic for small molecules. For proteins and peptides, the effect is well known [1]: “Hydrophobic collapse is a proposed process for the production of the 3‐D conformation adopted by polypeptides and other molecules in polar solvents.” A prominent example is cyclosporin A where hydrophobic clustering of the side chains and reorientation of the amide bonds of the backbone [2], also known under the term chameolonic effect [3], cooperatively result in different predominant conformations in polar and nonpolar media and are suspected to result in the peptide being highly membrane permeable. Intramolecular hydrophobic interactions in small drug molecules could increase solubility by reducing the hydrophobic surface, but would also decrease the binding free energy by the conformational change needed to adapt to the binding site. The only work we found on such experimentally observed interactions is on molecules specifically designed to exert hydrophobic π‐stacking interactions by having a highly polar linker region with two carboxylic acids and long aromatic tails, i.e., resembling a soap‐like amphiphilic structure [4, 5].

Andreussi et al. [6] in their work reported on two artifacts of continuum solvation models as there are unphysical cavities in semiconductor materials and unphysical vacuum bubbles too small to be penetrated by solvent molecules. Both artifacts add erroneous energy corrections to gas‐phase energies due to continuum solvation but are not reported to result in the phenomenon we observed.

Another known deficit of continuum solvation models is that for very polar or charged species implicit solvation models are inadequate “mainly because strong directional and local interactions between solute and solvent molecules, that is, noncovalent interactions (NCIs), ionic, and hydrogen bonds, are not treated properly. By neglecting explicit solvent molecules, important interactions are missing or only described poorly” [7]. Poor performance of continuum solvation models due to the estimation of the nonpolar contributions via a simple solvent‐accessible surface area term has been reported for general organic molecules and also protein–protein complexes in case of force‐field implementations [8]. Quantum mechanics (QM) continuum solvation models instead are more refined having different parametrizations for different solvents.

The nonpolar parts of a solute on the other hand cannot suffer from weak representation of directed interactions to the solvent molecules in a continuum solvent. Here, inter‐ and intramolecular London dispersion is attenuated by the solvent, which is not appropriately described by dispersion corrections [9], as shown by Pollice et al. in the case of dissociation reactions. Our case of the equilibrium between collapsed and stretched conformations can be seen as a special case of a dissociation reaction.

Finally, since solvation entropy contributions in implicit solvation models are calculated using gas phase formulas, they were shown to yield too high binding free energies in reactions that change molecularity. Again, the equilibrium between collapsed and stretched conformations can be seen as a special case of binding though the molecularity here does not change [10].

Experimentally, hydrophobic intermolecular interactions that lead to aggregation of solutes can be observed, quantified, and even kinetically understood by combining flow experiments and fluorescence spectroscopy [11]. The identification of the preferred conformation or conformational ensemble is not that easy. Crystallography can reveal conformations in the solid state, but those are influenced by intermolecular interactions with the crystal mates, like for instance π‐stacking. From nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) studies it was realized that the average solution conformer might not be the same as the bioactive conformation, since it is now even possible to perform NMR experiments for conformations of ligands bound to small proteins [12], in addition to bound bioactive conformations from crystal structures. As it can be laborious to reproduce biologically relevant conditions, NMR experiments have often to be done in organic solvents instead of water due to poor compound solubility. Therefore, a concern might be on how far these conformations would reflect the ones in aqueous media. In addition, the protonation states between solvents might change, again causing major deviations in preferred conformations. There are published examples for hydrophobic collapse in peptides [3], but often this is a mixed effect of interacting hydrophobic side chains, backbone orientation, and fluctuating hydrogen bonding patterns. As the reading timeframe of NMR is slower compared to molecular motions, the observed spectrum is averaged over a conformational ensemble. The most biologically relevant conformer might be less populated, thus tricky to probe empirically by solution‐state NMR. Our previous NMR results on small peptides [13] in this respect were therefore also not conclusive. A follow‐up study [14] using extended molecular dynamics (MD) simulations with multiple seed conformations, different solvents, and simulation temperatures revealed that though there were significant changes in hydrogen bond patterns and intramolecular hydrophobic interactions, the overall polar surface area for each molecule stayed about constant and the “amount of hydrophobic collapse” was limited.

In this work, we present computational quantum chemistry, force‐field and MD calculations as well as experimental solution‐state NMR experiments for one example Bayer compound as a representative for many cases from different chemical series and drug discovery projects. The combined results provide strong evidence on a major shortcoming of conductor‐like polarizable continuum model (CPCM) and solvation model density (SMD) continuum solvation models as implemented in common QM codes that result in biased ensembles of collapsed conformers. Use of those biased ensembles for many applications like assessment of strain energies in ligand binding, and any properties derived from the 3D structure like for instance molecular electrostatic potentials, atomic charges, Fukui indices, lipophilicity, HOMO–LUMO (highest occupied molecular orbital, lowest unoccupied molecular orbital) gaps.

DFT single‐point electronic energies reported in this work are obtained with r2SCAN [22] and D4 dispersion correction [23, 24], the aug‐cc‐pVTZ basis set, and SMD solvent model [25]. The functionals r2SCAN‐3c for optimization and r2SCAN for the final energies were selected based on the outcome of our previous benchmark study [26]. Additional single‐point energies were obtained using coupled cluster DLPNO‐CCSD(T) model chemistry [27] with the cc‐pVTZ basis set [28].

The solvation correction ΔG solv as derived from continuum solvation models consists of four terms, namely, the solvation energy E solv, the electrostatic polarization energy ΔG el, the energy to create the cavity ΔG cav, and finally ΔG disp accounting for the changes in dispersion energy when solvating the solute [29].

The latter two, combined to the nonelectrostatic contribution ΔG nel (ORCA output term GCDS), are not added to the final energy reported in the ORCA CPCM implementation. Since the term is expected to be considerably different for collapsed and stretched conformations, we additionally performed geometry optimizations with r2SCAN‐3c with SMD continuum solvation (with GCDS gradients included during optimization) and followed by energy calculations with r2SCAN/ aug‐cc‐pVTZ(SMD). Additional implicit solvation calculations were performed with the open COSMO‐RS implementation [30].

Macromodel [31] MM conformational searches were done in the Schrödinger suite [32] with the optimized potentials for liquid simulations (OPLS4) force‐field [33] in water or CHCl3 continuum solvation (since solvent DMSO is not available), Monte Carlo multiple minimum (MCMM) torsional sampling with automatic ligand setup and intermediate sampling level, 1000 steps maximum, and 100 steps per rotatable bond, maximum atom deviation cutoff of 0.5 Å and 42 kJ/mol (10.05 kcal/mol) energy window. The analytical generalized‐Born/surface‐area (GB/SA) model is used for continuum solvation. For conformer minimization PRCG was used, with gradient convergence threshold of 0.05 kJ mol−1 Å−1 (0.012 kcal/mol) and 2500 iterations. The force‐field conformations were again optimized with r2SCAN‐3c and electronic energies obtained with r2SCAN.

MD simulations were performed for 30 ns each with 1001 recorded snapshots. Systems were set up with either simple point‐charge (SPC) water or DMSO, in an orthorhombic box with starting dimensions of 10 Å per side around the molecule, and buffer box size calculation applied to minimize the volume.

We chose the NPT (constant number of atoms, pressure, temperature) ensemble with 1.01325 bar and either 200, 300, or 400 K, Nose–Hoover chain thermostat, Martyna–Tobias–Klein barostat, 2 ps relaxation time and isotropic coupling, a distance cutoff of 9 Å, and periodic boundary conditions. No replicate calculations were performed, since test runs with only 1.2 ns already gave similar conformer distributions.

All DFT calculations were done with ORCA [34] version 5.0.3, via the workflow software WEASEL [35] by FAccTs [36] on 32 core 3.9 GHz Intel CPU (central processing units) nodes with Redhat Linux 8.10 operating system. All MM and MD calculations were performed in the Schrödinger suite [25], the MD simulations on A100 GPGPUs (general purpose graphics processing units) with the DESMOND [37, 38, 39] package using Maestro for system for setup and analysis.

NMR Spectroscopy

NMR devices Ascend 700 MHz and Ultra‐Shield 500 MHz (Bruker BioSpin), operating with cryo 1.7 mm TCI and cryo 5 mm DCH probeheads were used. The sample was recorded successively in deuterated DMSO‐d6, TFA‐d (trifluoro‐acetic acid), and CDCl3. The solubility in TFA was the best and the one in CDCl3 was the poorest. Complete data acquisition was performed to enable the structure determination using 1D and 2D NMR experiments. TopSpin 4.4.1 and ACD/Lab NMR software were used for full data processing and analysis. Among those experiments, heteronuclear single quantum coherence (HSQC) enables identification of linked hydrogens and carbons, heteronuclear multiple‐bond correlation spectroscopy (HMBC) reveals hydrogens to carbons two bonds away, while correlation spectroscopy (COSY) correlates hydrogen atoms separated by three bonds. To reveal the three‐dimensional arrangement, a series of 2D‐NOESY (nuclear Overhauser effect spectroscopy) was acquired at mixing times ranging from 200 to 800 ms and at two different temperatures, namely, 298 and 333 K. NOESY experiments indicate through space correlations between spatially close hydrogens, and the magnitude of the nuclear Overhauser effect is inversely proportional to the sixth power of the interproton distance in space. Empirically, two hydrogens will show a measurable signal in the NOESY experiment if their interatomic distance is at most 5 Å. To maximize the sensitivity of the observed 2D‐NOESY patterns, a series of 1D‐NOESY experiments using specific irradiation frequencies were done to capture the occurrence of those folded conformers in solution. Through the specific irradiation of a signal belonging to a hydrogen, the intensity of every hydrogen atom within the 5 Å threshold will be enhanced by nuclear spin polarization transfer [40].

CREST as a QM‐based conformational sampling tool promises to yield reliable low energy conformers and likely in most cases the absolute minimum conformer. After extensive sampling and three levels of energy filtering, a low number of 11 and 16 conformers is retrieved for the two continuum solvents water and DMSO (see Figure 2a,b).

Some calculations did not converge.

In case of water solvation, we find that already the initial 29‐member CREST ensemble created even before GFN2‐xTB preoptimization only contains collapsed structures, whereas in case of DMSO solvation 80 of the original 219 conformers are fully stretched and only 36 are fully collapsed. A significant proportion of the stretched conformers is carried through all process steps but finally filtered out in the ωB97X‐V energy calculation. Thus, depending on the solvent selected, there is a risk of unintendedly narrowing the explored conformational space by using our default CREST approach by finally applying a filter on the relative conformer energies with continuum solvation energies included. This is clear from our alternative approach described in the following, i.e., applying the same final energy threshold on the ensemble from the macromodel process, we would have been left with mostly only collapsed conformers.

Macromodel conformer searches, on the other hand, result in about 10 times more starting conformers due to the larger energy window of 10.05 kcal/mol that we selected with the intention to avoid missing relevant conformers in the DFT optimization and energy calculations to be performed as final steps. For sampling in water, only 10 out of 145 conformers show ring–ring distances shorter than 5 Å, and in DMSO only 2 out of 101, i.e., the OPLS4 force‐field in combination with GB/SA prefers semistretched and stretched conformations (see Figure 2c,d). In water, the lowest three conformers are fully collapsed (<5 Å), the first semistretched one (6.1 Å) without π‐stacking is only 0.5 kcal/mol higher in energy than the minimum, and the first stretched one (13.2 Å) is only 1.7 kcal/mol higher in energy. In CHCl3, we find only semistretched and stretched conformers, except for two with relative OPLS4 energies of 3.7 and 4.7 kcal/mol.

Optimization of the OPLS4 conformers with r2SCAN‐3c and energy scoring with r2SCAN again changes the picture, as shown in Figure 2e,f. Note that the conformers are shown in the same order as the OPLS4 conformers that were sorted by increasing OPLS4 energy to allow for easy comparison of the changes due to QM treatment.

After optimization, for water solvation we find 65 conformers with ring–ring distances below and 83 with distances beyond 5 Å, and for CHCl3 we find 29 and 68, respectively, i.e., a quite significant shift toward collapsed conformers. For water solvation, there is an almost perfect separation between the two sets, and the first stretched conformer is 3 kcal/mol higher in energy compared to the collapsed minimum. The same is true for CHCl3 solvation, but with 2.6 kcal/mol difference and 13 collapsed conformers in the same energy range as the stretched ones.

To isolate the effect of continuum solvation, we performed a CREST conformer search in gas‐phase following the same protocol. Starting with 19 raw conformers, three of them semistretched, we ended up with 5 conformers differing only by the relative orientations of the chloro‐thiophene and the 2‐oxazolidone groups, all of them π‐stacked, as expected for gas‐phase calculations.

To ensure that our finding was not obscured by potential limitations of DFT, we performed DLPNO‐CCSD(T)/cc‐pVTZ calculations in gas‐phase and SMD(DMSO) for one collapsed and one stretched minimum conformer each. The collapsed conformation in gas‐phase (r2SCAN/cc‐pvTZ values in brackets) is more stable by −6.3 (−7.09) kcal/mol and in DMSO by −3.86 (−4.63) kcal/mol, i.e., DFT and coupled cluster energy deltas deviate by less than one kcal/mol and the delta in stabilization by implicit solvation are almost identical with −2.44 (−2.46) kcal/mol.

As proposed by a reviewer, we compared the solvation free energies obtained from the SMD ansatz with the ones from the open COSMO‐RS implementation in ORCA, again for one collapsed and one stretched conformer. The DMSO solvation free energies going from gas‐phase to implicitly solvated state for the r2SCAN/cc‐pVTZ/SMD and BP86/def2‐TZVPD/COSMO‐RS models are −26.7 and −30.8 for the collapsed and −29.16 and −35.09 kcal/mol for the stretched conformers. The COSMO‐RS solvation free energies are more negative by about 4–6 kcal/mol compared to the SMD ones, but the trend is the same, with stronger stabilization of the stretched conformer by the implicit solvation model.

Finally, we speculated that the applied Grimme D4 dispersion correction could be the reason for collapsed conformers. The correction accounts solely for solute–solute interactions, but not for solute–solvent interactions, and should favor gas‐phase like conformers. We therefore turned off dispersion for all steps of conformer generation. For this we had to do geometry optimizations with r2SCAN/def2‐SVP since dispersion is one of the three implicit correction factors in r2SCAN‐3c. We got 30 raw conformers after GFN2‐xTB optimization and 10 final conformers from the full workflow. All are collapsed, the only difference is that some are slightly parallel displaced whereas with dispersion they are all almost perfectly sandwich‐shaped. We mention here that this experiment is a hint that solute–solute dispersion is not relevant for the observed behavior but that solvent–solute dispersion that is only implicitly accounted for in the parametrization of the solvent models might be the issue.

Detailed analysis of the QM outputs and discussions with FAccTs scientists (the company providing ORCA for commercial use) made us aware that there is a fundamental difference between the CPCM and the SMD solvation models, in that CPCM does only account for the dielectric solute–solvent interactions whereas geometry optimizations with SMD additionally account for the nonelectrostatic ΔG CDS term.

Though the gradient of ΔG CDS contributes in the geometry optimization, the changes are small and the final conformer distribution is the same. Figure 4 shows the dielectric and the nonelectrostatic ΔG CDS contributions to the final conformer energies for water solvation. The ΔG CDS values have a spread of 2.32 kcal/mol but are clustered with values around 7 kcal/mol for the collapsed conformations and around 9 kcal/mol for stretched conformations. The dielectric terms have a larger spread of 23.3 kcal/mol, but are not clustered. We find that both components are not coupled and that the dielectric interaction terms with the solvent do not outweigh the synergistic strong π–π‐stacking interactions and the slightly lower cavity creation penalty for collapsed conformations. For continuum solvation with DMSO as solvent, the spreads are even smaller with 0.46 kcal/mol for ΔG CDS and 13.8 kcal/mol for dielectric contributions. The observed effect on conformational energies is the same as for water.

Plots of (a) the nonelectrostatic contributions for creating the cavity and change in dispersion energy (dG(CDS)) and (b) the electrostatic polarization energy ΔG el (dielectric), in units of kcal/mol, versus the ring–ring distance in Å, for solvent water and continuum solvation model SMD.

Summarizing the data, we find a clear preference for DFT for collapsed conformations regardless of the continuum solvent used (water or DMSO), whereas OPLS4 shows solvent‐dependent differences and a strong preference for stretched and semistretched conformations. For DFT calculations, the energy gain for hydrophobic interactions respectively π‐stacking is therefore much stronger than the solvation free energy contributions as calculated from continuum solvation models.

NMR Experiments

To elucidate the true nature of the preferred conformation, we next performed NMR experiments.

Unless other structural techniques, NMR can be performed under physiological conditions getting as close as possible empirically to the solution phase conformer ensembles of small molecules. One of the hurdles to overcome in this case of NMR experiments is often the poor water solubility. Knowing that NMR experiments can depict different conformational averages, varying experimental conditions increases the probability to capture one or the other occurring conformer.

Based on the experimental procedure described above, the structure elucidation of the compound was confirmed by full set analysis of NMR data. As summarized in the Figure 5, our investigations of the 3D structural data did not provide hints at the occurrence of such folded conformers from room to elevated temperatures and under various NOESY conditions. The first indication of the nonoccurrence of these folded conformers can be deduced from the lack of the upfield shielding of the two hydrogens in the thiophene moiety (Figure 5a), which should have been observed due to the anisotropic effect induced by the close phenolic ring. The 2D‐NOESY and the 1D‐NOESY specific irradiation experiments did not show any correlation nor intensity enhancement as expected from the collapsed conformers, where the calculated ring–ring distance should be in the visible range of NOESY experiments, i.e., below 5 Å [32].

MD Simulations

The discrepancy between preferred DFT conformers and the NMR results led us to run MD simulations with explicit solvent molecules, based on our earlier observations of pronounced differences between the solvents for small macrocycles [10]. We performed 30 ns simulations with 923 explicit water or, respectively, 191 DMSO molecules in orthorhombic boxes of initially 10 Å in each direction with periodic boundary conditions. Calculations were performed at 300 K as well as at 200 and 400 K (plots shown in Supporting Information).

Figure 6 and Table 2 report remarkable differences between the trajectories in water and in DMSO for MD simulations at 300 K. Whereas in water we find that 5.1% of the snapshots show short distances indicative of at least some π‐stacking interactions and only 40.4% of conformations without any intramolecular interactions, in DMSO the percentages are 0.2% and 79.8%, respectively. The shift to more stretched conformations in going from water with dielectricity constant ε = 78 to DMSO with ε = 48 is in line with expectation. The shift is even more clear when looking at the distance frequency plots in Figure 7. Whereas in water angular conformers with distances of 7.5 ± 2 Å dominate, in DMSO the fully stretched conformers clearly dominate.

In our MD simulations at 400 K (see Supporting Information), in both water and DMSO the highest bin populations are seen for fully stretched conformations. At 200 K (i.e., low molecular motions) almost exclusively the two bins >12 Å and >13 Å are populated.

The low 0.2% π‐stacking conformations in the DMSO MD simulation fits perfectly with the NMR results, showing that those conformations are not relevant in this solvent. Due to the low solubility of the molecule in water, NMR measurements are not possible. MD simulations nevertheless provide a strong indication that the π‐stacking collapsed conformations are neither relevant in water, despite the strong preference of those conformations by DFT calculations with continuum solvent.

Summary and Conclusions

Starting from the observation that drug‐like molecules that fit the quite common general structural pattern of two aromatic or lipophilic segments connected by a flexible linker in quantum chemistry calculations with continuum solvation tend to form collapsed conformers with pronounced hydrophobic contacts, we performed systematic conformer searches with QM‐based CREST and OPLS4 force‐field based macromodel MCMM torsional sampling, followed by DFT geometry optimization and electronic energy calculations. The QM calculations in water and DMSO continuum solvation were complemented by force‐field optimizations in the GB/SA continuum solvent model with water and CHCl3 and by force‐field MD simulations with explicit water and DMSO solvent molecules. The results were compared to 1D and 2D NMR experiments in three different solvents.

We find that during CREST conformer sampling there is already some tendency for collapsed conformations. After DFT geometry optimizations and final DFT single point calculations, no extended conformations remain. Sampling with the OPLS4 force‐field and GB/SA continuum solvation results in a mix that is dominated by extended and semiextended conformations, a low number of collapsed conformations in water and only two energetically high‐lying ones in CHCl3. This mix is mostly reverted after postoptimization with DFT.

In our 1D and 2D NMR experiments, we do not find the expected shielding of the two hydrogens in the chlor‐thiophene moiety nor any hints at the occurrence of such folded conformers under various NOESY conditions, like correlation or intensity enhancement as expected for collapsed conformers.

Explicit solvation MD simulations are consistent with NMR experiments in that they reveal maximally about 5% population of collapsed conformations in explicit water and 0.2% in DMSO.

We conclude that polarizable continuum solvation models as implemented in all major quantum chemistry codes do not correctly account for the electronic interactions between solute and solvent in case of the generic hydrophobic–linker–hydrophobic structural motif. They penalize stretched starting conformers energetically so that they do not survive in the final conformer ensembles.

Not being method developers but users applying the software to practical drug discovery problems, we can just speculate about the reasons for the observed problem. For the type of molecules discussed here the assumption of a polarizable continuum which does not reflect the real nature of a solvent consisting of molecules with specific and directed solvent–solvent and solvent–solute interactions might be problematic. We further observe that the nonelectrostatic term in the SMD solvation model favors collapsed conformations. We expect missing entropic contributions a further source of error. Finally, the molecules used in the parametrization of the solvent models are way too small to expose the problem.

Our results show that there is a clear need for improvement of polarizable continuum solvation models since there is a significant risk that they provide wrong conformer ensembles. An ad hoc solution could be to add an empirical energetic penalty to collapsed conformers to retrieve balanced ensembles.

Most current public repositories of QM structures are not affected by the problem because they contain only small compounds and typically only gas‐phase conformers, like QM9 [41], ANI‐1 [42], PubChemQC [43] or Qmugs [44]. Repositories like AQM [45] that provide solution phase conformers might be affected. QM properties or machine‐learning potentials derived from described artifact conformer ensembles would obviously yield further downstream errors if applied to chemical problems.
