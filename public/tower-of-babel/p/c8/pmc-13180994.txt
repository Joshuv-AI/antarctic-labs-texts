Microwave scattering signatures for distinguishing healthy and infested date palm trees
Authors: Afshin Moradi, Mohammed M Bait-Suwailam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly detection of pest-infested date palms is vital to prevent pest spread and support agricultural sustainability. By analyzing microwave scattering behavior, specifically radar cross-section (RCS), this work assesses the potential of microwave sensing in identifying healthy versus infested date palms. The trunk is represented as a dielectric cylinder illuminated by a uniform plane wave with both TM and TE polarizations (with respect to the z-axis). An assessment of dielectric properties in healthy versus infested trees is conducted, along with an analysis of angle-dependent () scattering behavior. Results reveal significant differences in the RCS between healthy and infested trees, particularly in the angular ranges of for TM polarization, and for TE polarization. The study demonstrates that microwave sensing offers a promising non-invasive tool for early pest detection and effective tree health management, providing a basis for large-scale agricultural monitoring.Keywords: Microwave response, Date palm trees, Plant health monitoring, Scattering, Radar cross-sectionSubject terms: Biophysics, Engineering, Optics and photonics, Physics, Plant sciences

Introduction

In arid and semi-arid regions, date palm trees (Fig. 1) are a vital agricultural asset1. Beyond their nutritional value, they significantly bolster local economies. The date palm industry sustains millions by supplying dates—a dietary staple—as well as by-products like palm fronds and wood, which support traditional crafts and industrial uses. Safeguarding the health of these trees is essential for ensuring long-term agricultural productivity and economic stability.

The date palm has a straight, cylindrical trunk that can reach heights of up to 35 meters. Its trunk maintains a uniform diameter throughout, even below the crown, with no branching. Typically, the trunk measures between 40 and 50 cm in diameter, though it can range from 35 to 70 cm. The photograph was captured in Muscat, Oman, by A. Moradi (first author).

However, date palm trees are vulnerable to numerous pests and diseases, among which the red palm weevil2 is particularly destructive. This pest inflicts severe damage, often resulting in substantial yield reductions and, in extreme cases, tree mortality. Early infestation detection is crucial for effective pest control and damage mitigation. Conventional detection techniques—such as visual inspections and manual sampling—are typically invasive, labor-intensive, and lack scalability. These limitations hinder efficient monitoring in large-scale agriculture, where rapid and precise health assessments are vital for successful pest management.

The development of non-destructive, real-time techniques for assessing tree health has gained significant attention in recent years3–9. These techniques take advantage of advances in remote sensing10, signal processing11, temporal unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) imagery12,13, optimal control analysis14, and the Internet of Things (IoT)15 to provide more efficient and scalable solutions. Among these, microwave sensing has emerged as a promising approach due to its ability to penetrate vegetation and provide detailed information about the internal structure and dielectric properties of plant tissues16,17. The basis of microwave sensing lies in the differential interaction of microwaves with materials, governed by their dielectric properties. Since factors like moisture levels and structural integrity affect these properties, pest-infested trees exhibit detectable changes that microwave technology can identify and assess.

In a recent development, microwave sensing was employed for non-invasive health monitoring of date palm trees, leveraging the scattering-to-absorption width ratio (SAWR) of dielectric cylinders under oblique plane-wave excitation18. This technique treats palm trunks as dielectric cylinders and studies their scattering behavior under 2.45 GHz microwaves (part of the ISM band) for transverse electric (TE) and transverse magnetic (TM) modes. While the study initially focused on oblique incidence, results demonstrated that normal incidence provides superior sensitivity for discriminating between healthy and infested trees, offering a practical advantage for field applications.

However, the SAWR metric relies on integrated scattering and absorption widths, which are not directly measurable in practical field settings. To translate this principle into a viable remote sensing technique, an analysis based on the directly measurable angular distribution of the scattered field is required. Furthermore, a field-deployable system benefits from the simpler geometry of normal incidence. Therefore, building on the finding that normal incidence provides effective discrimination, this work advances the methodology by focusing on this practical case and employing the standard, measurable radar cross-section (RCS). We perform a comprehensive bistatic analysis to identify optimal angular and polarization configurations, thereby establishing a direct framework for the design of operational microwave-based tree health monitors.

Here, we analyze the microwave scattering properties of date palm trunks, particularly the RCS under TE and TM polarizations. This analysis enhances our understanding of the underlying interaction mechanisms and demonstrates that microwave sensing can serve as an effective, non-destructive tool for early pest detection. Its scalability offers a practical solution for sustainable orchard management and precision agriculture.

The present analysis confirms that dielectric property variations between healthy and infested tissues produce distinguishable microwave scattering signatures. Building directly on this foundation, we19 introduced the concept of dielectric properties as intrinsic biomarkers for pest detection, outlining a comprehensive microwave sensing strategy that exploits these biomarkers for the early identification of Red Palm Weevil infestation. This work translates the theoretical scattering differences demonstrated here into a practical and sensitive diagnostic tool.

Materials and methods

In this section, we present the theoretical framework and analytical formulations used to model the interaction of electromagnetic waves with date palm trees. The goal is to derive expressions for the scattering characteristics of the tree trunk, which is modeled as a long circular dielectric cylinder embedded in air. This model allows us to analyze the interaction of microwaves with the tree trunk, providing insights into the health status of the tree based on its dielectric properties.

Modeling the date palm tree trunk

The date palm trunk is modeled as an infinitely long, homogeneous dielectric cylinder of radius a, aligned along the z-axis in a cylindrical coordinate system . The trunk’s electromagnetic properties are defined by a relative permittivity , which represents the dielectric response of the palm tissue. The cylinder is surrounded by air, which has a relative permittivity of 1.

Moisture content (MC) and structural integrity are key factors affecting the dielectric properties of palm tree tissues. When a tree is infested, its tissues exhibit significantly higher moisture content (up to 733% in damaged tissue) compared to healthy tissues (max 186% MC), due to larval activity, fermentation, and loss of structural integrity16. The elevated moisture content in damaged tissues leads to significantly higher values for the real part of across the entire measured frequency range (0.4–18 GHz). However, the imaginary part of exhibits a frequency-dependent crossover. Below 2.9 GHz, healthy tissues actually demonstrate higher values. Above 2.9 GHz, this relationship reverses, with damaged tissues showing greater loss characteristics. Conversely, healthy tissues retain lower moisture and maintain more stable dielectric properties. These differences can be detected using microwave sensing, as the interaction of electromagnetic waves with the tree trunk depends on its dielectric properties.

Model limitations and practical considerations

The analytical model presented above is based on key simplifying assumptions to elucidate the fundamental scattering mechanisms. First, the trunk is modeled as a homogeneous dielectric cylinder, representing either entirely healthy or entirely infested tissue. In reality, an infested state may be localized rather than pervasive, and the trunk structure is layered, comprising a drier, fibrous outer crust (bark) and an inner core of vascular and parenchyma tissues where infestation typically initiates and progresses.

Second, the model assumes a perfectly smooth cylindrical surface. Actual palm trunks exhibit surface roughness from bark texture and internal heterogeneity from tissue structures. These features may introduce additional scattering mechanisms (e.g., diffuse scattering) that could modify the angular distribution and magnitude of the measured RCS, particularly at higher frequencies where the wavelength becomes comparable to surface irregularities.

Using the dielectric properties from Fig. 2 and Table 1, the penetration depth in infested palm tissue at 2.45 GHz is estimated to be on the order of several centimeters. This is sufficient to probe beyond the outer crust and into the inner trunk where pathological changes occur. At 18 GHz, reduces significantly, typically to a few centimeters or less, due to increased dielectric losses. Consequently, the high-frequency (18 GHz) results presented in Section III primarily illustrate the principle that resolution and angular contrast improve with frequency in an idealized scenario. For deep probing of a trunk with a non-uniform infestation profile, lower frequencies are more appropriate due to their greater penetration capability.

The choice of 2.45 GHz in this study is motivated by its location within the ISM band, which offers practical advantages for field-deployable systems. Notably, this frequency lies below the dielectric loss crossover near 2.9 GHz (Fig. 2), meaning that healthy trees already exhibit higher loss than infested trees at this frequency. While operating at even lower frequencies would further increase penetration depth and could enhance the loss-based contrast, several trade-offs must be considered. Reducing the frequency decreases the electrical size () of the trunk, leading to weaker overall scattering and broader angular patterns that may reduce sensitivity to the dielectric contrast. Additionally, lower frequencies offer coarser spatial resolution, making it more difficult to isolate the trunk signature from background clutter. Moreover, the contrast in the real part of the permittivity—the primary driver of the scattering signatures analyzed in this work—remains substantial across the entire frequency range and provides a robust basis for discrimination. The 2.45 GHz frequency thus represents a balanced compromise that maintains adequate penetration depth, preserves scattering contrast, and leverages the practical advantages of ISM-band operation.

Notwithstanding the simplifications of homogeneity, smoothness, and uniform infestation, the core dielectric contrast between healthy and infested tissues—driven by profound differences in moisture content and structure—is expected to dominate the bulk electromagnetic response. The model, therefore, provides insight into the scattering signatures associated with this contrast. Future work incorporating layered dielectric models, surface roughness effects, and more realistic infestation profiles will be essential to quantify these practical effects and optimize system design for field conditions.

Validation of the analytical scattering model

The electromagnetic scattering model used in this study—the analytic Mie-type series solution for a dielectric cylinder illuminated by a plane wave—is a canonical and rigorously validated standard in theoretical and computational electrodynamics. Its accuracy is routinely used as a benchmark for numerical simulation tools. For example, Ansys optics employs this exact analytic solution to validate the results of its Finite-Difference Time-Domain (FDTD) simulations, demonstrating agreement over multiple orders of magnitude30. Therefore, the core scattering formulation applied here rests on a robust and independently verified theoretical foundation. The novelty of the present work lies in the application of this established model to the dielectric contrast between healthy and infested plant tissues for agricultural remote sensing.

Practical Considerations for Field Deployment: The results indicate a measurable contrast of approximately 1.5 dB in RCS between healthy and infested trees under specific conditions (e.g., monostatic TM polarization at 2.45 GHz). A legitimate practical concern is whether such a difference is sufficient for reliable detection in a field environment, where signals may be affected by system noise, wind-induced tree motion, and ground clutter.

While these environmental factors present a signal-to-noise challenge, they are not unique to this application and are routinely addressed in radar and microwave remote sensing. Several strategies can be employed to enhance the detectable contrast:

1. Wind-induced motion and random clutter effects can be mitigated by averaging multiple measurements over time or across a small spatial area of the trunk, significantly improving the signal-to-noise ratio.

2. As shown in this study, the contrast depends strongly on polarization and observation angle. A sensor system can be optimized to operate in the most discriminatory configuration (e.g., TM polarization, ), maximizing the inherent signal difference relative to isotropic clutter.

3. Modern radar systems can utilize coherent processing and reference measurements from known healthy trees or adjacent ground areas to adaptively suppress stationary clutter and highlight the differential scattering from the tree trunk itself.

4. Operating at a lower frequency (e.g., 2.45 GHz) not only improves penetration but also typically results in lower phase noise and more stable propagation characteristics compared to higher frequencies, aiding in consistent measurement.

Therefore, while the 1.5 dB contrast represents the theoretical signal difference, the practical feasibility hinges on intelligent sensor design and signal processing that leverages these strategies to extract the diagnostic signature from the noisy environment. Future experimental work can focus on quantifying the achievable signal-to-noise ratio and validating these mitigation techniques in real orchard conditions.

Conclusion

In this work, we assess the feasibility of microwave sensing in distinguishing healthy and infested date palms. The tree trunk is modeled as a dielectric cylinder, and scattering behavior under TM and TE polarizations is examined. Our results indicate that distinct RCS signatures and scattering intensity patterns enable effective discrimination between healthy and infested specimens. The results revealed significant variations in RCS values, particularly in specific angular ranges, such as for TM polarization, and for TE polarization. These differences, which are influenced by the dielectric properties of the tree tissues, provide a reliable basis for identifying the health status of date palm trees. Furthermore, the study highlighted that higher frequencies enhance the resolution and sensitivity of microwave sensing, making it a promising tool for early pest detection and tree health monitoring. The forward-directed scattering patterns and the semicircular backward scattering observed in both polarizations further support the potential of this technique for large-scale agricultural applications. Overall, this research underscores the effectiveness of microwave sensing as a non-invasive, real-time method for monitoring the health of date palm trees. The findings contribute to the development of sustainable agricultural practices by enabling early detection of pest infestations and improving tree health management, ultimately supporting the economic and ecological stability of regions reliant on date palm cultivation.
