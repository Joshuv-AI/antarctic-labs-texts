Assessing vaccine cold chain storage and transport in Ukraine: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Stanislav Gaievskyi, Yevgenii Grechukha, Oleg Khomenko, Nasrin Musa Widaa Musa, Oleg Benes, Jarno Habicht, Oleksandr Zaika, Ihor Starychenko, Ihor Kizin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to monitor risks of temperature excursions along vaccine distribution routes in Ukraine to identify weaknesses and recommend improvements.MethodsThis cross-sectional study followed the World Health Organization’s temperature monitoring protocol. Vaccine shipments along randomly selected distribution routes were monitored using loggers, recording temperatures at 20 min intervals across all levels of the cold chain. The collected data were analysed to assess compliance with the recommended range and identify instances of heat and freeze exposure.ResultsA total of 72040.1 h were recorded from July 2024 to October 2024 across 48 distribution routes. The recorded temperature ranged from − 13.6 °C to + 28.4 °C. The level of compliance with the recommended temperature range varied by immunisation supply chain level and across facilities at a given level. The highest compliance (~ 100%) was observed at national and oblast stores and during transits between these levels. At the same time, the lowest compliance (88.8%) during storage was observed at primary healthcare centre (PHC) sub-offices, with three of such facilities failing to maintain the recommended range at any point during the study. Additionally, 20 freeze and five heat alarms were recorded at this level, indicative of prolonged exposures. During transits to PHC and sub-offices, compliance was achieved during 54.8% and 60.3% of the recorded time, respectively. Furthermore, eight transits to PHC and 15 to PHC sub-offices failed to maintain temperatures within the required range throughout the transit.ConclusionsWhile Ukraine’s vaccine cold chain system is effective at higher levels, temperature excursions were common at lower levels, particularly in PHC sub-offices and during transport to lower-level facilities. Equipment malfunctions, gaps in knowledge and lack of procedures, compounded by power outages and lack of power supply contingencies, are probable causes. Systematically identifying and documenting temperature excursions and assessing the potential damage to vaccines are recommended immediate actions. Providing further qualified equipment, ensuring resilient power supply, strengthening training, and improving procedures and supervision to prevent and mitigate risks of temperature excursion incidents are longer-term recommended actions. Implementing these actions would strengthen Ukraine’s immunisation supply chain resilience, ensuring safe and effective immunisation services.

Background

Since the Russian Federation’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, Ukraine’s health system has been operating amid the largest conflict in Europe since the Second World War [1]. As of March 2025, the World Health Organization (WHO) has registered over 2 265 attacks on healthcare facilities [2]. Despite these challenges, Ukraine’s health system has shown remarkable resilience, with over 96% of facilities remaining at least partially operational [3], largely sustaining essential health services and even furthering health system reforms [4]. However, access to healthcare in conflict-affected regions is notably reduced, such as in Donetska oblast, with only 53% of facilities being at least partially functional [5], combined with large population displacement, inevitably heightening the risk of infectious disease outbreaks [6].

Vaccination is one of the most effective public health interventions [7] and is a critical tool for protecting populations from vaccine-preventable diseases, particularly in conflict settings [8]. However, the success of immunisation programmes depends on a robust cold chain system, which ensures vaccines are stored and transported within the recommended temperature range to preserve their potency and safety [9]. Russian Federation’s attacks on energy infrastructure, which began in 2022 and intensified in the spring and summer of 2024, have led to approximately half of Ukraine’s electricity generation capacity being either occupied, destroyed or damaged, causing frequent rolling blackouts and unscheduled interruptions [10, 11]. This, compounded by pre-existing challenges in Ukraine’s vaccine cold chain, poses a significant risk to vaccine storage and distribution integrity. These disruptions may threaten immunisation efforts and increase population susceptibility to vaccine-preventable diseases.

In recent years, alongside broader health sector reforms, Ukraine has made significant progress in strengthening its immunisation programme, particularly the cold chain system. The Ukrainian Public Health Centre was designated as the national authority responsible for immunisation programme implementation, supported by newly established oblast Centres for Disease Control and Prevention (oCDCs), each with dedicated immunisation departments overseeing vaccination programmes at the oblast level [12]. In addition to strengthening governance, substantial donor investments, initially driven by the COVID-19 pandemic and later by the response to the full-scale invasion, have enabled Ukraine to upgrade much of its cold chain infrastructure. These investments have supported the installation of WHO-prequalified walk-in cold rooms (WICRs) at oblast storage facilities, procurement of ice-lined refrigerators (ILRs), refrigerated trucks, cold boxes, and backup power supplies [13, 14]. Thanks to these reforms and investments, Ukraine’s immunisation programme demonstrated to be largely resilient, maintaining routine service provision, conducting catch-up vaccination campaigns, and responding effectively to outbreaks [15].

To ensure and further strengthen the integrity of Ukraine’s vaccine cold chain, the Ministry of Health of Ukraine requested WHO support to conduct an evaluation of temperature excursion risks during the storage and transport of vaccines. Following WHO’s standard temperature monitoring study protocol [9], this study aimed to provide a comprehensive assessment of Ukraine’s vaccine supply chain, from national stores to service delivery points. By systematically collecting and analysing temperature data along a sample of vaccine distribution routes, this study seeks to identify vulnerabilities, assess compliance with the recommended temperature range, and inform targeted corrective actions. The findings can be used to support Ukraine in mitigating risks, preventing vaccine wastage, and ensuring the potency of vaccines, thereby enhancing the effectiveness and resilience of its immunisation programme.

Methods

Study settings

Ukraine operates three national vaccine stores responsible for storing and distributing vaccines to 25 oblast-level stores following procurement or donation through a push approach. Oblast-level stores, managed by oCDCs, oversee the storage and distribution of vaccines to service delivery points within their oblasts. Some oblasts also maintain district-level stores to facilitate distribution to remote areas. Service delivery points are primary healthcare centres (PHC), with vaccines either administered on-site or further transported to geographically remote sub-offices. A combination of push and pull approaches is used to transport vaccines from oblast-level stores to service delivery points. Ukraine has approximately 4960 service delivery points [16].

National and oblast vaccine stores primarily use WHO-prequalified WICRs and refrigerated trucks for storage and distribution. At the district level and in PHCs, vaccines are mostly stored in ILRs and distributed using cold boxes and vaccine carriers, with most equipment being WHO-prequalified, though some non-specialised equipment is also in use. PHC sub-offices mainly rely on non-specialised refrigerators for storage and a mix of WHO-prequalified and non-prequalified cold boxes and vaccine carriers for transportation.

Study design

The study was designed to continuously record temperatures in cold chain equipment along 54 randomly selected vaccine distribution routes, from national-level stores to service delivery points. It followed a cross-sectional design guided by the WHO’s Study Protocol for Temperature Monitoring in the Vaccine Cold Chain [9] and supplemented by additional research tools from the Temperature Monitoring Study Handbook [17]. Both the protocol and tools were adapted to align with Ukraine’s vaccine cold chain structure and the resources available for the study.

Site selection

In order to capture the high variability of vaccine delivery routes in our study sample, we selected 54 target service delivery points using a four-stage randomized sampling technique, as follows: (1) selecting all three national stores; (2) nine oblasts (three per each national store), excluding seven oblasts from the sampling frame due to security concerns and four due to an ongoing WICR qualification exercise1; (3) eighteen districts (two per selected oblasts); (4) fifty-four vaccination service delivery points (three per district). The sampling frame for oblasts and districts was stratified by transport route distance to ensure the representation of both long and short transport routes. Additionally, service delivery points were stratified by vaccination volume to capture variations in cold chain equipment and practices between high- and low-volume sites.

Study tools

A total of 54 study packages were prepared to be shipped alongside vaccine deliveries to the selected service delivery points. Each package contained a temperature logger, a printed instruction sheet, a paper-based tracking form and a link to the online reporting form. The WHO-prequalified LogTag TRIX-8 temperature loggers, with ± 0.5 °C recording accuracy, were selected for the study. Using LogTag Analyzer software, all loggers were programmed to record temperature at 20-minute intervals, enabling data collection for up to 110 days without overwriting earlier recordings. All loggers were set to start at 08:00 on 5 July 2024. The paper-based tracking form, adapted from the Temperature Monitoring Study Handbook [17], was used to document storage duration, transport times, and cold chain equipment types at each level. Additionally, an online reporting form enabled near real-time monitoring of study package progress along all distribution routes.

Logistics of study packages

All study packages were prepared at Farmasoft’s national store, which WHO contracted to provide logistical support for the study’s initiation. Farmasoft delivered the study packages designated for the other two national stores on 4 July 2024, one day before the programmed start of logger recordings. Upon arrival at the national stores, the study packages were placed in the cold chain to ensure adequate cooling before temperature data recording began.

From the national stores, the study packages were paired with vaccine shipments designated for selected service delivery points, passing through oblast stores, as well as district stores and PHCs (if used), without altering routine distribution practice. Once study package reached selected service delivery point and the paired vaccine was administered, the return process was initiated. Data collectors from oCDCs were responsible for collecting study packages within their oblasts. Once collected at the oblast level, the packages were sent via postal service to the Farmasoft warehouse, where data from the loggers and the paper tracking forms was extracted.

Training and coordination of data collectors

Representative from each of the three national stores and the oCDCs from the participating oblasts, which oversee oblast- and district-level stores, were engaged in the study as data collectors and participated in a one-day in-person training conducted by the study team. A total of 28 data collectors took part in the training. During the training, data collectors were briefed on the study aim, methodology, and processes. Given the large number of data collectors involved in the study at the service delivery level (one per each PHC and one per each PHC sub-office, totalling 104 health workers that supported data collection), it was decided not to train them centrally. Instead, we asked data collectors from the oCDCs to instruct the health workers in their respective regions during the handover of study packages. However, we were not able to monitor the quality of the instructions provided by the trained data collectors to the health workers supporting data collection at the service delivery level.

A chat group, including all national, oblast, and district-level data collectors and the study team, was created to answer any questions during the study’s implementation.

Data management and analysis

OK collected all paper-based tracking forms, photocopied and archived in cloud storage. SG extracted data from the photocopies of tracking forms into a predefined Microsoft Excel form, including route numbers, serial numbers of loggers, facility names along the study route, entry and exit timestamps for each facility, and the equipment used for storage and transport. During data extraction, tracking forms were checked for missing information and inconsistencies. In cases of discrepancies or missing critical data, SG contacted data collectors from the relevant oCDCs for clarification.

OK extracted data from all temperature loggers and uploaded it to the cloud storage. SG plotted temperature data recorded by loggers and examined it for any anomalies using LogTag Analyzer. The data was then extracted into.csv files and trimmed according to the actual study end date recorded on the tracking forms.

The extracted data from tracking forms and csv files from loggers were analysed using the Microsoft Excel-based Data Analysis Tool for Temperature Monitoring Studies (Version 4.0) developed by the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) [17]. Using this tool, we calculated the duration and proportion of time recorded within and outside the recommended temperature range at each level on each route, as well as predefined heat and freeze alarms. Results

Data collection lasted from 5 July 2024 to 31 October 2024. A total of 72040.1 h of temperature recording during the storage and transport of vaccines across all five levels of the vaccine supply chain were recorded on 48 selected routes out of 54 initially planned (Fig. 1). Six routes planned for Cherkaska oblast had to be excluded from the analysis due to significant deviations from planned routes and incomplete study documentation. Additionally, recordings from one PHC sub-office in Mykolaivska oblast were excluded, as the facility reported not storing the logger in the cold chain for an unknown reason. The remaining routes were largely implemented according to the initial plan, with minor, well-documented deviations that reflected actual vaccine distribution routes. Specifically, out of 18 districts included in the study, only five districts saw study packages passing through district-level stores. In Zakarpatska and Ivano-Frankivska oblasts, selected districts had more than one district-level store, increasing the total number of district stores in these study oblasts to three. On six routes, study packages were delivered directly to PHC sub-offices, bypassing PHCs, while on four routes, the study packages remained at the PHC level without being transported to PHC sub-offices as initially planned.

Storage at the national level

During the study period, a total of 20018.0 h were recorded at three national stores, which accounted for 27.8% of the total time recorded (Table 1). Most recordings remained within the recommended temperature range, with only one instance of a temperature drop to + 0.9 °C, recorded by a single logger at one national store for 0.3 h. No heat or freeze alarms were triggered. Two stores reported using WICRs for vaccine storage, while the third used ILRs (Table 2).

Transit to oblast level

Transits from national to oblast stores were recorded on all routes for 771.0 h. The temperature recorded during these transits ranged from + 3.1 °C to + 8.2 °C, with 99.8% (769.3 h) of the recordings falling within the recommended temperature range. No heat or freeze alarms were recorded. Two stores reported using cold boxes for transport, while another reported using refrigerated truck crates.

Storage at the oblast level

At the eight oblast level stores, 27819.0 h were recorded during the study, accounting for 38,6% of all time. The recorded temperature ranged from + 1.6 °C to + 16.3 °C. The recommended range was maintained for 99.9% (27804.7 h) of recordings. Exposure to temperature below the recommended range lasted for 4.7 h (< 0.0%) and occurred on five routes. Exposure to temperatures above the recommended range lasted for 9.7 h (< 0.0%) and occurred on nine routes. No heat or freeze alarms were recorded. Five of the included oblasts reported using WICRs for vaccine storage, while the remaining oblasts reported using ILRs. No clear association was observed between the type of equipment used and the presence of temperature excursions.

Transit to district-level

Transit to district-level stores occurred on 14 routes, lasting 44.3 h. The recorded temperature ranged from − 1.6 °C to + 12.4 °C, and it remained within the recommended range for 73.6% (32.7 h) of the total time recorded during all such transits. However, compliance with the recommended range varied significantly between routes, with eight routes achieving 100% compliance, while the remaining six achieved only 37.4%. Exposure to temperatures below the recommended range lasted for 13.5% (6.0 h) and occurred on five routes, while exposure to freezing lasted for 9.7% (4.3 h) and occurred on two routes. Two freeze alarms were triggered on one route, indicating two instances where exposure to freezing lasted for more than one hour continuously. Exposure to temperatures above the recommended range lasted 2.9% (1.3 h) of all such transits and occurred on one route. Cold boxes were reportedly used during 57.1% (8 routes) of these transits, with the remaining transits using reused international shipping cartons. All routes with recorded temperature excursions were reported using cold boxes. However, it is worth noting that we did not record whether the equipment used for transits was WHO-prequalified. On all six routes with the lowest temperature compliance, transit times were shorter than the average for this level, suggesting that transit duration was not associated with the occurrence of temperature excursions.

Storage at the district level

Fourteen study routes passed through seven district-level stores, with a total recorded time of 1205.7 h, accounting for 1.7% of the total recorded time. The recorded temperature ranged from − 1.2 °C to + 19.8 °C. Compliance with the recommended temperature range was maintained during 99.4% (1198.3 h) of the recorded time. However, similar to transits to the district level, compliance varied between different stores, with five stores achieving over 99% compliance, while the remaining two had compliance below 75%. Exposure to temperatures below the recommended range was observed during 0.1% (1.0 h) of recorded time and occurred on three routes, while exposure to freezing was observed for 0.3 h (< 0.0%) on one route. Exposure to temperatures above the recommended range accounted for 0.5% (6.0 h) of recorded time and was present on nine routes. No heat or freeze alarms were recorded. All district stores reported using ILRs for vaccine storage.

Transits to primary healthcare centres

Transits to PHCs occurred on 42 routes to 41 PHCs (with two routes passing through one PHC), and recording during such transits lasted for 44.3 h, with temperatures ranging from − 2.0 °C to + 18.2 °C. Compliance with the recommended range was achieved during 54.8% (36.3 h) of all time recorded during these transits. However, high variability in compliance was observed between the routes, with 13 routes achieving 100% compliance, while eight routes did not maintain the recommended range at any point during the study. Exposure to temperatures below the recommended range was recorded during 8.0% (5.3 h) and was observed on four routes, while exposure to freezing lasted for 0.5% (0.3 h) and was recorded on one route. Exposure to temperatures above the recommended range was recorded during 36.7% (24.3 h) of transit time and was observed on 22 routes. No heat or freeze alarms were recorded. The equipment most commonly used for transits at this level was cold boxes and vaccine carriers, with 42.2% (19 routes) and 40.0% (17 routes) of the routes using them, respectively. We did not observe any clear link between the type of equipment used and low performance on the routes.

Storage at primary healthcare centres

A total of 12327.3 h were recorded at 41 PHCs, accounting for 17.1% of the total time recorded. Temperatures ranged from − 13.6 °C to + 26.0 °C. Compliance with the recommended range was maintained during 99.2% of the recording time, with compliance lower than 95% observed only in three PHCs, ranging from 63.5% to 84.8%. Exposure to temperatures below the recommended range accounted for 0.4% (50.7 h) of time recorded at this level and was noted on six routes, while exposure to freezing accounted for only 3.7 h (< 0.0%) and was documented on two routes. However, one freeze alarm was triggered, indicating that the recorded freeze exposure lasted longer than one hour continuously. Exposure to temperatures above the recommended range accounted for 0.4% (46.3 h) and was noted on 25 routes. No heat alarms were recorded. Most PHCs reported using ILRs for vaccine storage (61.9%; 26 routes), while 7.1% (3 routes) reported using domestic refrigerators. At the same time, a sizable portion of the facilities did not report the equipment used (28.6%; 12 routes). Most routes with temperature excursions used ILRs (71.1%; 26 routes). However, the PHC where the recorded freeze alarm occurred (where the recorded temperature dropped to -13.6 °C) was reported using a domestic refrigerator.

Transits to PHC sub-offices

Transits to PHC sub-offices were recorded on 41 routes for 31.0 h. During these transits, recorded temperatures ranged from − 1.9 °C to + 21.2 °C. Compliance with the recommended range was maintained during 60.3% (18.7 h) of the recordings. Significant variation in compliance was observed between different routes, with 18 routes achieving 100% compliance during such transits, while 15 routes achieved 0% compliance. Exposure to temperatures below the recommended range lasted for 6.5% (2.0 h) of the recordings and was observed on three routes, while exposure to freezing was observed during 1.0% (0.3 h) and was noted on one route. Exposure to temperatures above the recommended range lasted for 32.3% of all time and was observed on 16 routes. No freeze or heat alarms were triggered; however, it is important to mention that most transits lasted shorter than the time required to trigger an alarm. Vaccine carriers were the most common equipment used for these transits, accounting for 63.0% (25 routes), while 15.0% (6 routes) used cold boxes, and 22.5% (9 routes) did not report the equipment used. No clear association was observed between the type of equipment used and compliance with the recommended temperature range.

Storage at PHC sub-offices

Storage at PHC sub-offices was recorded on 41 routes. However, as mentioned earlier, data from one PHC sub-office had to be excluded, as it reported that the logger was not stored in the cold chain. This reduced the number of PHC sub-offices to 40. The total recorded time at this level was 9757.7 h, accounting for 13.5% of all recorded time. The temperature recorded at this level ranged from − 4.3 °C to + 28.4 °C. Compliance with the recommended temperature range was maintained during 88.8% (8663.3 h) of the recorded time. However, compliance varied significantly between different routes, with 25 routes achieving over 95% compliance, while three did not adhere to the recommended range at any point during the study. Exposure to temperatures below the recommended range accounted for 6.4% (627.7 h) and was observed on 12 routes, while exposure to freezing accounted for 1.9% (187.7 h) and was noted on eight routes, triggering a total of 20 freeze alarms across four PHC sub-offices. Exposure to temperatures above the recommended range accounted for 2.9% (279.1 h) and was noted on 26 routes, triggering five heat alarms across four PHC sub-offices. ILRs were the most common equipment used at the PHC sub-office level, accounting for 55,0% (22 routes). Domestic refrigerators were reported on 17.5% (7 routes), while 27.5% (11 routes) did not report the equipment used. No clear link was established between the type of equipment used and the occurrence of heat or freeze alarms.

Discussion

The findings of our study highlight both strengths and vulnerabilities in Ukraine’s vaccine cold chain system, providing valuable insights for improving vaccine storage and distribution practices. The recorded data demonstrates strong performance at both national and oblast-level stores, where WICRs and ILRs are predominantly used, as well as during vaccine transit between these levels, with compliance to the recommended temperature range approaching 100%. This strong performance is particularly commendable given the challenges posed by frequent power supply disruptions due to attacks on Ukraine’s energy infrastructure [10, 18].

However, the collected data also demonstrated increased occurrences of temperature excursions during storage at lower levels of the cold chain. This was especially true for storage at the PHC sub-office level, which appeared to be the weakest storage level in the system. At this level, compliance with the recommended range was maintained only during 88.8% of all time recorded, with freezing being a more pronounced issue, triggering a total of 20 freeze alarms compared to only five heat alarms. The recorded alarms are indicative of the prolonged excursion events that risk causing damage to vaccines. Notably, three PHC sub-offices appeared not to maintain the recommended temperature at any point during the study. The fact that excursions were noted in WHO-prequalified and non-prequalified equipment indicates that the issue extends beyond the used equipment.

Another important point identified by the study is the vulnerability to temperature excursions of transits between lower levels. During transits to districts, some routes showed very low performance (37.4%), exposing vaccines to low and high temperatures. Two freeze alarms were recorded during such transits, most likely caused by the inappropriate preparation of coolant packs. High variability in performance was noted during transits to PHC and PHC sub-offices, with almost perfect performance on 13 transits to PHC and 18 PHC sub-offices, while at the same time, eight transits to PHC and 15 transits to PHC sub-offices were not maintained in the recommended range at all. Exposure to temperatures above the recommended range appears to be a prevailing issue during transits to PHC and PHC sub-offices. As our study did not assess the quality of equipment used for transits nor the workers’ skills and knowledge, additional investigation into these issues is warranted to identify the causes of underperformance.

The findings of our study align with similar studies conducted in other settings, all of which observed decreased cold chain performance at lower levels [19–23]. Furthermore, a systematic review conducted by Hanson et al. suggests that vaccine exposure to freezing remains an ongoing issue across both low- and high-income countries [24]. Based on findings from similar studies conducted in other countries, the identified deficiencies may be attributed to equipment malfunctions or inadequate staff training, exacerbated by the absence of SOPs and insufficient supervision [24–26].

Another likely explanation for the observed temperature excursions at lower levels of the cold chain is power supply interruptions. Although our study did not specifically record the frequency of power outages or if any contingencies were employed, widespread power disruptions occurred across Ukraine during the study period [10]. According to a WHO survey conducted among 500 primary care providers in Ukraine, only 28% reported having a generator or other means of uninterrupted power supply before the war began. Power supply interruptions, therefore, may have significantly compromised cold chain integrity, particularly at lower levels [27]. The proportion of providers with backup power supply is likely higher now, as the government and its partners have made substantial efforts to enhance energy security since attacks on Ukraine’s energy infrastructure began in 2022 [28]. However, our study points out that this issue deserves additional investigation and consideration, as a resilient power supply system of the health system during wartime is crucial to maintaining cold chain integrity, sustaining immunisation efforts and protecting the population from vaccine-preventable diseases.

Based on the results of our study, we recommend that the following actions be considered to prevent potential damage to vaccines that may occur during their distribution within Ukraine. As an immediate action, the volume and duration of vaccine storage at PHC sub-offices should be minimized, taking into account the programmatic and financial implications of such a change. Furthermore, at both PHC and PHC sub-office levels, it is recommended to review SOPs for vaccine storage and transport and to provide additional training and supervision to all responsible staff, with particular attention given to temperature monitoring, preparation of the coolant packs, placement of vaccines inside refrigerator units [29], and vaccine storage during power cuts or equipment malfunctions. The cold chain equipment used at those levels, including passive and active equipment, should be reviewed, and non-specialised equipment should be gradually phased out. Availability of generators and other equipment for uninterrupted power supply for the cold chain should be ensured. The findings also indicate that some oblast and district-level staff can benefit from refresher training on vaccine storage and transport procedures. At the cross-cutting system level, developing a cold chain inventory would be beneficial to enable systematic monitoring of equipment performance, inform the need for service and replacement. This system could also support the identification of vaccine shipments exposed to temperature excursions through the system, allowing timely assessment for potential damage to prevent vaccine recipients from receiving potentially damaged vaccines. Furthermore, monitoring of the vaccine wastage levels across the cold chain system can inform further planning and allocation of supply to secure continuous access to vaccines. Regular review of SOPs for vaccine storage and transport at all levels, combined with routine monitoring and supervision, could support improved compliance and system resilience [30]. To ensure the feasibility and sustainability of these recommendations, it is essential to integrate them into national policies and guidelines, along with adequate financial and technical support from national authorities and development partners.

Our study had several limitations. First, we did not evaluate actual vaccine potency at the end of the study routes, primarily due to ethical considerations of discarding over 100 vaccine vials that would be required for such evaluation, aimed at shortages of vaccines in Ukraine. Instead, we relied on assessing the exposure of vaccines to temperatures outside of the recommended range using temperature loggers. However, we acknowledge that exposure to temperatures outside the recommended range does not always mean that vaccines lose potency. Second, we excluded seven frontline oblasts from the sampling frame due to high security risk. The war may, to a greater extent, negatively affect the cold chain performance in those oblasts. Therefore, with those oblasts excluded, the results of our study may overrepresent better-performing oblasts. Last, our study was conducted during the summer season. During winter, the temperature in Ukraine may drop below zero for prolonged periods, potentially increasing the frequency and duration of freeze events that our study could not capture. Despite these limitations, we believe that the study offers valuable insights into the vaccine cold chain performance and that its findings can be used to inform actions necessary for its further strengthening.

Conclusions

Our study observed varied levels of compliance with the recommended temperature range by immunisation supply chain level and across facilities at a given level. The highest compliance was observed at national and oblast stores and during transits between these levels. At the same time, reduced compliance with the recommended temperature range was observed at lower levels, particularly during storage at PHC sub-offices and lower-level transits, where multiple prolonged excursion events (alarms) were documented, indicative of the risk of potential vaccine damage. Equipment malfunctions, staff knowledge and skills gaps, lack of procedures and power supply issues are probable causes of the identified exposures. Minimising the volume and duration of vaccine storage at PHC sub-offices is an immediate recommended measure. In the longer term, cross-cutting system-level actions include the development of a cold chain inventory to enable systematic monitoring of equipment performance and to facilitate need-based servicing and replacement. Furthermore, gradually phasing out the use of non-specialised active and passive cold chain equipment, ensuring a resilient power supply, strengthening training, and improving procedures and supervision are also recommended as longer-term actions. Implementing these actions would further strengthen Ukraine’s immunisation supply chain, ensuring safe and effective immunisation services.
