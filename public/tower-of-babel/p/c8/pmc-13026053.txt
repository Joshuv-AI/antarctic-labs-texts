Regulation and Interaction Among SOCS1 and SOCS3 by MicroRNAs in Multiple Sclerosis: A Review and In Silico Analysis
Authors: Moisés Manuel Gallardo-Pérez, Solón Javier Garcés-Eisele, José Manuel Madrazo-Cabo, Virginia Sedeño-Monge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic demyelinating disease and a leading cause of disability in young adults. Its pathophysiology involves complex genetic and immunological mechanisms, among which the suppressors of cytokine signaling (SOCS) proteins play a critical role. These intracellular regulators control cytokine-mediated JAK/STAT signaling, thereby modulating immune activation. In particular, SOCS1 and SOCS3 inhibit key cytokines such as IL-2, IL-12, IL-6, and IL-23, preventing excessive Th1/Th17 responses and neuroinflammation. Dysregulation of SOCS expression or function can favor persistent immune activation and contribute to central nervous system demyelination. MicroRNAs (miRNAs), small non-coding RNAs that repress gene expression post-transcriptionally, have emerged as important modulators of immune regulation. Several miRNAs target SOCS transcripts, influencing their expression and, consequently, cytokine signaling. Altered miRNA profiles have been reported in autoimmune and neurodegenerative diseases, highlighting their potential relevance in MS pathogenesis and as biomarkers. This review summarizes current evidence on SOCS1 and SOCS3 in MS and examines their regulation by miRNAs. In addition, we present a hypothesis—generating in silico analysis identifying candidate miRNAs with predicted interactions with SOCS genes. These computational findings are intended to support and prioritize potential regulatory relationships rather than provide direct experimental validation. Collectively, this integrative approach may offer new insights into disease biology and help guide future experimental studies aimed at identifying biomarkers or therapeutic targets in MS.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a demyelinating disease that continues being one of the most common causes of disability in young adults around the world [1]. Different factors are implicated in physiopathology which is still under study. Different proteins and genes have been related to MS [2-4], among them the suppressors of cytokine signaling (SOCS), constitute a family of intracellular proteins that act as key negative regulators of cytokine-mediated signaling, especially in the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway, which is critical for signal transduction of many cytokines and growth factors [5,6]. The SOCS family includes eight members: CIS (Cytokine-Inducible SH2-containing protein) and SOCS1 to SOCS7 [7].

These proteins are induced in response to cytokine receptor activation and exert their inhibitory function through several mechanisms: direct inhibition of Janus kinase (JAK) activity, competition for binding sites on cytokine receptors, and promotion of proteasomal degradation of signaling pathway components [8]. For example, SOCS1 and SOCS3 are particularly relevant for their ability to inhibit JAK activity and modulate the immune response, thus preventing excessive activation and associated tissue damage [5,6]. In MS, excessive activation of Th1/Th17 cells and macrophages leads to CNS demyelination. By negatively regulating key cytokine signals (IL-2 and IL-12 by SOCS1; IL-6 and IL-23 by SOCS3), these SOCS proteins act as critical brakes on neuroinflammation. Thus, loss or inhibition of SOCS function (for instance by certain miRNAs) can exacerbate immune-mediated damage, whereas enhancing SOCS activity could be protective.

The function of SOCS is essential for maintaining immune homeostasis and preventing uncontrolled inflammatory responses. Alterations in the expression or function of these proteins have been associated with autoimmune diseases, allergies, cancer, and inflammatory disorders [9,10]. In addition, SOCS regulate the differentiation and function of immune cells such as T lymphocytes, NK cells, and macrophages, and are involved in immune tolerance.

In recent years, microRNAs (miRNAs) have played a central role in different physiological processes. miRNAs are small, non-coding RNA molecules, typically composed of 20 to 25 nucleotides; their primary role is to regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level. miRNAs achieve this by binding to the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of target messenger RNAs (mRNAs), leading to either the degradation of the mRNA or inhibition of translation, thus influencing gene expression significantly [11].

One of the critical aspects of miRNA biology is their biogenesis, which involves a series of tightly regulated steps. Initially, miRNAs are transcribed as primary miRNAs (pri-miRNAs) that are processed in the nucleus by the Drosha-DGCR8 complex. This processing results in the formation of precursor miRNAs (pre-miRNAs), which are then transported to the cytoplasm where they undergo further cleavage by the enzyme Dicer to produce functional miRNA duplexes [12]. These duplexes are then unwound, and one strand is incorporated into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), where it can exert its regulatory effects on target mRNAs.

The multifaceted roles of miRNAs extend beyond gene regulation; they are involved in various biological processes, including development, differentiation, cell proliferation, apoptosis, and responses to stress [13]. In the context of diseases, altered miRNA expression has been linked to various conditions, ranging from cancer to neurodegenerative disorders [14].

Despite increasing interest in the role of SOCS proteins in MS, the available evidence regarding SOCS1 and SOCS3 regulation remains fragmented across experimental models, biological compartments, and study designs. In particular, data on miRNA-mediated regulation of SOCS1/3 in MS are dispersed and often derived from heterogeneous sources, limiting their interpretability and translational value. Therefore, a consolidated and MS-focused synthesis of SOCS1/3 regulatory evidence is needed. In this review we focus on the existing evidence on the relationship between SOCS1 and SOCS3 in MS and propose a prioritized shortlist of candidate miRNAs potentially involved in their regulation. By systematically organizing existing knowledge and highlighting high-priority miRNA-SOCS interactions, this work aims to provide a coherent framework to guide future experimental validation and mechanistic studies in MS.

Materials and Methods

Literature Search Strategy and Data Sources

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify relevant studies concerning the relationship between SOCS1 and SOCS3 in MS and their regulation by miRNAs. The search was performed across the following electronic databases: PubMed and EBSCO.

The search strategy employed a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords, including: “Multiple sclerosis,” “SOCS1,” “SOCS3,” and “miRNA.” Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to refine the results (eg, SOCS1 AND SOCS3 AND Multiple sclerosis).

To complement traditional database searches, the Consensus AI academic search engine (Consensus, Boston, MA) was utilized to identify and synthesize evidence-based insights from peer-reviewed articles. Natural language queries were employed to evaluate scientific agreement on the relationship between SOCS1 and SOCS3 in MS and their regulation by miRNAs. Papers from Q1-Q2 journals and highly cited articles were prioritized for further manual review to ensure the inclusion of robust, scientifically grounded data.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

To maintain the focus of this review, specific criteria were established for literature selection:

Inclusion Criteria: Peer-reviewed original research articles; studies focused on MS patients reporting levels of biomarkers including SOCS and miRNAs; articles published in English; experimental validations using animal models or cellular lineages.

Exclusion Criteria: Conference abstracts, editorials, or unpublished manuscripts; studies with redundant data or lack of methodological clarity.

Data Abstraction and Synthesis

The selection process followed a non-systematic approach, prioritizing high-impact studies and recent technological breakthroughs. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance, followed by a full-text analysis of the selected papers. Information was synthesized narratively, categorizing the findings into thematic sections: expression of SOCS1/SOCS3, miRNAs evaluation, effect pathways.

In Silico miRNAs and SOCS Data Collection and Preliminary Processing

A miRNA-gene interaction network was constructed using miRNet 2.0 [15]. The next parameters were employed to construct and analyze the interaction network between SOCS1/SOCS3 and associated miRNAs: The Mixed input types configuration and Multiple query types enabled; Organism was set to Homo sapiens, with no tissue specificity applied; Genes and Diseases modules were selected; Official gene symbols SOCS1 and SOCS3 were used as gene inputs, while MS was selected as the disease of interest.

In silico prediction of miRNAs targets for SOCS1 and SOCS3 was performed using the TargetScanHuman v8.0 algorithm. This is an online tool that comprehensively searches for potential miRNA targets across the entire genome based on small RNA and mRNA sequence information [16]. TargetScanHuman v8.0 predicts biological targets by searching for conserved 8mer, 7mer, and 6mer sites that match the seed region of each miRNA. To prioritize the most effective interactions, candidates were ranked according to their cumulative aggregate PCT that refers to an aggregate percentile score, a metric used by TargetScanHuman v8.0 to predict the likelihood of a gene being a target of a specific miRNA. SOCS1

SOCS1 is an important regulatory protein that plays a critical role in controlling the immune response and maintaining cellular homeostasis, particularly in the context of inflammation and immune signaling. Its primary function is to act as a negative feedback regulator of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, which is crucial in mediating the effects of various cytokines, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and interleukins (IL) such as IL-4 and IL-6 [17,18].

SOCS1 expression has been reported to decrease in leukocytes from MS patients, which may contribute to an exacerbated immune response [19,20]. However, some studies have reported overexpression of SOCS1 in patients with relapsing-remitting MS, suggesting a complex and clinically context-dependent role [21]. The differences between these works could be the result of different variables like the sample size, MS type, treatments and differences between the analyzed populations.

According to the results in animal models with experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), SOCS1 is increased in CNS tissue. Maier et al. (2002), demonstrated that the cerebral expression of SOCS1 and SOCS3 was increased and restricted primarily to the CNS-infiltrating mononuclear cell population during the evolution of type 1 immune responses in mice with EAE [22]. Berard et al. (2010), found SOCS1 levels significantly elevated in the spinal cord in early stages of EAE relapsing-remitting or chronic in mice. Also reported that SOCS1 protein is highly expressed in immune cells obtained from spinal cord lesions in EAE model with expression predominantly localized within macrophages [23].

The results of these studies indicate that the upregulation of SOCS1 allows for decreased levels of proinflammatory cytokines that play a crucial role in the pathophysiology of MS and could be essential in the formation of new lesions in the central nervous system (CNS). In an EAE mouse study, Rapamycin treatment was found to improve neurological function and prevent myelin loss, mainly by modulating the TAM/TLR/SOCS signaling pathway to regulate innate immunity [24].

SOCS3

SOCS3 is primarily known for its involvement in the negative feedback of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, particularly in modulating the effects of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-23 [25,26]. SOCS3 achieves this by competing with signaling molecules and binding to cytokine receptors, preventing the activation of associated JAKs and subsequently inhibiting the phosphorylation and activation of STAT transcription factors. This inhibition is critical in preventing excessive inflammation that could lead to tissue damage, thereby promoting cellular homeostasis during immune responses. SOCS3 is a critical negative regulator of Th17 cell differentiation and function, acting primarily by inhibiting IL-23 and IL-6 signaling pathways in T cells. Loss of SOCS3 in T cells leads to enhanced Th17 responses and worsening of EAE, supporting its role as a brake on pathogenic T cell-mediated autoimmunity [27-30].

On the relationship of SOCS3 with MS, results are variable; some studies on MS patients show an increase of SOCS3 expression [19] or a decrease [31] while others found no significant differences with respect to healthy controls [20].

Yan et al. (2019) mentioned that SOCS3 deficiency in myeloid cells can aggravate inflammation and brain damage in animal models of MS [32]. They demonstrated that neutrophils from the cerebellum of mice with SOCS3 deficiency exhibit a hyperactivated phenotype and increase ROS production, which contributes to the development of atypical EAE.

The evidence from these studies underscores the multifaceted role of SOCS3 as a critical modulator of the immune response in MS and EAE. It serves as a regulator of inflammatory signaling pathways and interacts with other immune mediators, influencing disease outcomes and progression.

miRNAs Interaction with SOCS

There are several reports that have analyzed the relationship between different miRNAs and SOCS1/3. One of the most related miRNAs to SOCS is the miRNA-30-5p family. This family consists of five members namely miR-30a-5p, miR-30b-5p, miR-30c-5p, miR-30d-5p, and miR-30e-5p. One study reported that miR-30e-5p directly downregulates SOCS1 and SOCS3 expression in bacteria-infected macrophages, enhancing innate immunity by inhibiting these negative regulators of immune signaling. The regulation was validated by transcriptomic analysis and functional experiments in cellular models [33]. The resulting depletion of SOCS1/3 proteins removes the steric and biochemical constraints on the JAK/STAT signaling apparatus; specifically, it prevents the SOCS-mediated ubiquitination of JAK kinases and the competitive inhibition of STAT recruitment to cytokine receptors. The resulting constitutive phosphorylation of STAT3/5 facilitates their nuclear translocation and the subsequent transactivation of genes associated with cell cycle progression (eg, Cyclin D1, Myc) and epithelial-mesenchymal transition. There is also a report that observed that silencing miR-30a-5p promotes M2 polarization of macrophages and improves cardiac injury in viral myocarditis patients by targeting SOCS1 [34]. As result of this study, it appears that miR-30a-5p exerts its effects by directly binding to the SOCS1 3’-UTR, inducing its post-transcriptional silencing. The resulting deficiency in SOCS1 protein disrupts the balance of macrophage activation, preferentially driving pro-inflammatory M1 polarization at the expense of the cardioprotective M2 phenotype. The inhibition of miR-30a-5p upregulates SOCS1, may promote M2 polarization of macrophages and improve cardiac injury following viral myocarditis.

In essential thrombocythemia, miR-221 and miR-203 were found to directly bind the 3′UTRs of SOCS1 and SOCS3 respectively, as shown by luciferase assays, with an inverse correlation between miRNA and SOCS levels [35]. These miRNAs function by directly targeting and suppressing SOCS1 and SOCS3, respectively. The resulting post-transcriptional silencing of these negative regulators facilitates the constitutive activation of the JAK/STAT signaling pathway, thereby providing a molecular mechanism for myeloproliferation. Platelets from patients had elevated levels of SOCS1/3 when miR-221/miR-203 were low, consistent with these miRNAs repressive effect on SOCS expression.

In a mouse model of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury was found that the downregulation of miR-122-5p induces SOCS1 expression, this interaction effectively interrupts the JAK2/STAT3 axis and provides significant cardioprotection by improving cardiac function and reducing inflammation [36].

miR-155-5p is one of the miRNAs most analyzed in different pathologies and has been found that is a prominent miRNA that directly binds the SOCS1 3′UTR, repressing SOCS1 expression, as confirmed by luciferase assays and in vivo studies. Accordingly, blocking miR-155-5p leads to increased SOCS1 levels and attenuation of inflammation in various contexts, including atherosclerotic inflammation [37-39] and regulatory T cells [40-42]. Related to the immune regulatory role of miR-155-5p, a study found that under physiologic conditions and during autoimmune inflammation or viral infection, some immunological functions of miR-155-5p were fully or largely attributable to the regulation of SOCS1, whereas others could be accounted only partially, or not at all, by this interaction [40]. In another study conducted in thymic and peripheral Foxp3+ cell subsets in mice, found that Foxp3 drives elevated expression of miR155, leading to translational downregulation of the SOCS1 protein, which in turn leads to a heightened sensitivity of Treg cells to IL-2 stimulation [41]. The interaction between miR-155-5p and SOCS1 has been commonly validated by luciferase assays and in vivo studies, showing that miR-155-5p inhibition increases SOCS1 and reduces inflammation through the JAK/STAT and NF-κB pathway.

In a study conducted in colonic mucosa from ulcerative colitis patients reported an inverse expression level between SOCS1 and miR-142-5p in these patients. Furthermore, luciferase activity assays indicated that miR-142-5p directly targets SOCS1. Their qRT-PCR and western blotting assay verified that miR-142-5p could dramatically decrease SOCS1 expression in HT-29 cells. The upregulation of this miRNA in active disease states of ulcerative colitis leads to a significant reduction in SOCS1 protein expression, thereby removing the negative feedback inhibition on cytokine signaling. As a consequence, there is an aberrant elevation of IL-6 and IL-8 secretion, driving the chronic mucosal inflammation characteristic of ulcerative colitis [43]. Notably, miR-142-5p is also elevated in MS and EAE T cells. The post-transcriptional silencing of SOCS1 by this miRNA facilitates a phenotypic shift toward pro-inflammatory Th1 lineages, exacerbating the inflammatory infiltrate within the CNS [44].

miR-29b has shown a significant role in the regulation of SOCS1 through the STAT3/SOCS1 pathway in aortic valve disease, validated in cellular and animal models [45]. Activated STAT3 promotes the transcription of miR-29b, which subsequently targets the 3’-UTR of SOCS1, leading to its post-transcriptional silencing. The resulting loss of SOCS1-mediated inhibition facilitates a feed-forward loop of STAT3 hyperactivation, which in turn triggers NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis in aortic valve interstitial cells.

In silico studies have identified miR-203a-5p and other miRNAs (miR-6756-5p, miR-6732-5p, miR-1203, miR-6887-5p) as potential regulators of SOCS3 in cancer, although further experimental validation is required [46]. By suppressing SOCS3, the upregulation of this miRNA profile amplifies pro-inflammatory cytokine sensitivity via the JAK/STAT axis. Such molecular dysregulation facilitates the tumor expansion and metastasis by perturbing the homeostatic control of cellular division and motility.

Several other miRNAs (miR-326, miR-146a, miR-21) are elevated in MS immune cells [47], although their identified targets lie in other pathways (eg, IL-1/TLR signaling for miR-146a, or T cell differentiation factors for miR-326), these findings underscore the global dysregulation of miRNAs in MS. It would be interesting to investigate whether some of these MS-associated miRNAs also converge on the JAK/STAT–SOCS regulatory axis.

miRNAs Targeting Conserved Sites in SOCS Genes

The presence of conserved miRNA sites in a gene is of great biological and evolutionary importance, as it indicates that miRNA-mediated regulation of that gene is functionally relevant and has been maintained by natural selection. The conserved sites are usually found in the 3’UTR regions of mRNAs, although they can also be in coding regions (CDS), and their conservation suggests that miRNA-mRNA interaction plays an essential role in the post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression [48,49]. The conservation of miRNA-binding sites has key functional implications, including robust gene regulation, evolutionary constraints, widespread functional roles in protein-coding genes, and utility in predicting biologically relevant miRNA targets.

Using TargetScanHuman v8.0 and mirNet [15,16], we identified conserved miRNA binding sites in the genes SOCS1 and SOCS3 and the miRNAs related to these genes in MS. As seen in Figure 1, a miRNA interaction network was performed for SOCS 1 and 3 genes with MS. The miRNAs showing relationship with both genes and with the term “Multiple Sclerosis” were the following: miR-16-5p, miR-19b-3p, miR-26a-5p, let-7a-5p, miR-196a-5p, miR-29b-3p, and miR-107. For SOCS1 and MS alone it was miR-24-3p and for SOCS3 and MS the miRNAs were miR-484 and miR-92a-3p.

Table 2 shows that the miRNAs most likely to target SOCS1 are miR-19-3p and miR-30-5p, these based on “aggregate PCT” that refers to an aggregate percentile score, a metric used by TargetScanHuman v8.0 to predict the likelihood of a gene being a target of a specific miRNA. This score is based on the degree of conservation of a miRNA binding site across different species, along with other factors like seed sequence complementarity and thermodynamic stability of the binding interaction. The miRNAs most likely to target SOCS3 are miR-19-3p, miR-30-5p, miR-302c-3p.2/520-3p, and miR-124-3p.2/506-3p.

Evidence of the miRNAs Interaction with SOCS in MS

Reports on the relationship between miRNAs and SOCS1/3 in MS are scarce. Experimental evidence demonstrates that increased availability of miR-30b-5p in THP-1 cells, leads to the targeted silencing of SOCS3, which subsequently triggers the constitutive activation of the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. This molecular circuit effectively amplifies the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-1β, identifying the interaction between miR-30b-5p and SOCS3 as a critical determinant of macrophage-driven inflammatory responses [51]. Similarly, miR-155-5p has been shown to inhibit SOCS1 expression in activated CD4+ T cells, implicating this miRNA in the sustained activation of pro-inflammatory signaling pathways by attenuating SOCS1-mediated suppression of JAK/STAT signaling. Reduction in SOCS1 levels lowers the activation threshold for pro-inflammatory cytokines, preferentially driving the differentiation of naive CD4+ T cells into pathogenic Th1 and Th17 lineages [52].

In a study conducted in a murine model of lung cancer, it was discovered that miR-30a regulated the suppressive function of inducible regulatory T cells (iTregs) in vitro. Adoptive cell transfer assays showed that miR-30a abolished the suppressive function of iTregs on CD8+ T cell activity and reported that overexpression of SOCS1, reversed the effect of miR-30a on the suppressive function of iTregs [53]. miR-30a has been shown to promote inflammation not just by targeting SOCS1, but by inhibiting PPAR-gamma coactivator 1-beta, a master regulator of mitochondrial metabolism and anti-inflammatory phenotypes [54].

miR-19b targets the SOCS3 3’-UTR, leading to its post-transcriptional silencing and the subsequent removal of inhibitory constraints on the JAK/STAT signaling axis. The resulting activation of STAT3 facilitates a pro-tumorigenic transcriptional program that enhances cellular proliferation, inhibits apoptosis, and promotes invasive potential. By upregulating SOCS3, inhibition of miR-19b inactivates the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, thereby suppressing cell proliferation and promoting apoptosis and senescence. In some diseases, miR-19b-3p has shown oncogenic and proinflammatory activity by downregulating SOCS3, for example in glioma progression [55], Crohn’s disease [56], and even osteosarcoma cell growth [57].

Further investigations have identified miR-409-3p and miR-1896 as regulators of SOCS3, where their overexpression results in reduced SOCS3 levels, an increased phosphorylation of STAT3 and the subsequent overproduction of pro-inflammatory chemokines, indicating their role in promoting STAT3-dependent inflammatory responses [58]. Another miRNA, miR-384, has been found to exacerbate EAE by disrupting the balance between Th17 and regulatory T cells (Tregs). This effect is mediated through the direct targeting of SOCS3. miR-384 suppresses SOCS3 expression via direct 3’-UTR binding, which subsequently facilitates the constitutive activation of STAT3. This signaling shift preferentially drives the differentiation of naive CD4+ T cells toward a pro-inflammatory Th17 phenotype while simultaneously impeding the development of immunosuppressive Tregs [59].

Additionally, luciferase assays have confirmed SOCS1 as a direct target of miR-142a-5p, with overexpression of this miRNA leading to suppressed SOCS1 transcript levels in lymphocytes [44]. This finding underscores the role of miR-142a-5p in modulating cytokine signaling by relieving SOCS1-mediated inhibition. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that miR-30b-5p, miR-155-5p, miR-409-3p, miR-1896, miR-384, and miR-142a-5p function as key regulators of SOCS1 and SOCS3 expression, with significant implications for immune homeostasis and inflammatory disease progression.

Post-transcriptional regulation of the SOCS family is a key control point in autoimmune neuroinflammation. This stratification highlights the need to distinguish between established pathogenic mechanisms and potential targets that warrant further experimental investigation.

miRNA Profiles and the Transition from RRMS to SPMS

Beyond their role in acute inflammatory activity, miRNAs may also participate in the transition from relapsing-remitting MS (RRMS) to secondary progressive MS (SPMS). Longitudinal biomarker studies suggest that changes in immune-related miRNAs accompany, and may even precede, clinical conversion. In a recent serum and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteo-miRNome study, Tan et al. reported that conversion to SPMS was marked by a transient peak in granzyme B levels that coincided with enrichment of immune cell-associated miRNAs, particularly miR-150, in circulating extracellular vesicles. Of 24 miRNAs showing different trends before versus after conversion, 10 significantly correlated with granzyme B, indicating that specific immune cell-driven processes are tightly linked to the RRMS-SPMS transition [60].

These findings align with earlier work showing that exosomal miRNA signatures can distinguish relapsing from progressive MS forms with high accuracy, for example a three-miRNA panel (miR-223-3p, miR-485-3p, miR-30b-5p) that discriminated RRMS from secondary/primary progressive MS with approximately 95% accuracy. Together, these data raise the possibility that progressive upregulation of pro-inflammatory or anti-SOCS miRNAs (such as miR-155-5p or members of the miR-30 family) in chronic stages could contribute to a functional “failure” of SOCS1/3 to restrain inflammatory signaling, thereby promoting smouldering neurodegeneration. Although direct longitudinal measurements of SOCS1/3 expression and SOCS-targeting miRNAs across RRMS and SPMS are still lacking, the available evidence provides a mechanistic framework in which miRNA-SOCS regulatory circuits could influence disease progression. Future studies should systematically evaluate whether specific miRNA-SOCS1/3 interaction profiles predict conversion risk and whether therapeutic modulation of these circuits can delay or prevent the transition to SPMS [61].

Current Limitations and Future Biomarker Candidates

The evaluation of miRNAs and SOCS1/3 in different samples has revealed the different pathways in which they play an important role. It appears that these could have great potential as biomarkers for different diseases; however, there are some limitations. These limitations primarily stem from issues associated with methodological variations, stability of miRNAs, and the inherent complexity of their biological roles. Understanding these challenges is crucial for leveraging miRNAs and SOCS1/3 effectively in diagnostics and therapeutic contexts. Interestingly, IFN-β therapy has been reported to induce SOCS1 and SOCS3 expression in immune cells, contributing to its anti-inflammatory action [47]. This suggests that therapeutic boosting of SOCS1/3 (whether by cytokines, drugs, or gene therapy) might be beneficial in MS. Notably, a cell-permeable peptide mimicking the kinase-inhibitory region of SOCS1 has been shown to ameliorate relapsing-remitting EAE in mice [62], underscoring the therapeutic potential of augmenting SOCS1 activity. Also, it has been reported that rapamycin administered in EAE mice, can act on the TAM-TLRs-SOCS pathway by inhibiting the secretion of TLR-3, TLR-4, and proinflammatory factors. This inhibition showed an alleviation of inflammatory demyelination and likely has a neuroprotective role in EAE mice [24]. SOCS1/3 mimetic peptides are designed from the KIR region and act as substrates that block JAK kinase activity in a highly specific manner. Compared to systemic JAK inhibitors, which can have significant side effects due to their broad action on multiple cytokines, SOCS mimetics offer more “physiological” and targeted regulation [63]. Therapeutic strategies targeting miRNAs focus on either restoring or inhibiting their expression [64]. Restoration can be achieved through miRNA mimics that replace downregulated miRNAs [65]. Inhibition approaches include antisense oligonucleotides, small-molecule inhibitors, locked nucleic acids, anti-miRNA oligonucleotides, aptamers, and antagomirs, which block miRNA function by preventing target interaction or inducing miRNA degradation [64]. miRNAs that downregulate SOCS1 (such as miR-155-5p or miR-142-5p) are attractive targets for therapeutic inhibition, as blocking these miRNAs might restore SOCS1 levels and immune balance. Also, a combination therapy could emerge, for example, a specific miRNA antagonist to decrease the acute inflammatory phase, followed by a miRNA mimetic to improve the repair and remyelination phase.

One major limitation lies in the variability of miRNA profiles across different individuals or populations, which can hinder their reliability as biomarkers. This variability can arise due to multiple factors, including age, sex, ethnicity, and even sample processing techniques [66,67]. For instance, methodological inconsistencies in sample collection, extraction, and analysis can lead to inconsistent results, highlighting the essential need for standardization in protocols used for miRNA studies. The specificity and clinical relevance of SOCS1 and SOCS3 expression can vary significantly depending on the pathological context. For example, although SOCS1 and SOCS3 expression has been shown to be associated with prognosis and progression in glioblastoma and other neoplasms, as well as with severity and response to treatment in inflammatory diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, these findings are not always consistent across different studies and populations, limiting their universal applicability [68-71].

Another critical issue affecting the viability of miRNAs as biomarkers is their stability in biological fluids. While miRNAs generally exhibit a degree of stability, their levels can be impacted by factors such as hemolysis or enzyme degradation, particularly in plasma or serum samples [72]. Furthermore, the biological function of miRNAs is characterized by their ability to regulate multiple target genes, complicating the establishment of clear correlations between miRNA levels and specific disease conditions or outcomes. For SOCS1, the presence of polymorphisms and mutations can affect both protein expression and function, complicating the interpretation of results and their prognostic or diagnostic value in diseases such as lymphomas and rheumatoid arthritis [68,70]. In addition, post-transcriptional regulation by miRNAs and other epigenetic mechanisms can modify the expression of SOCS1 and SOCS3, generating interindividual variability and hindering the standardization of reference thresholds.

The main challenge in the use of miRNAs or SOCS1/3 from a therapeutic approach is the permeability of the blood-brain barrier, which prevents the passage of most large therapeutic molecules, also the inhibition of proinflammatory miRNAs can have systemic side effects. For example, although silencing miR-155-5p helps diminish the neuroinflammation in MS, its absence can weaken the response to bacterial or parasitic infections. While Table 1 demonstrates differential SOCS1/3 expression across peripheral and central compartments, emerging evidence indicates that miRNA profiles similarly exhibit compartment-specific patterns [47,60,73,74]. Recent exosomal miRNA profiling revealed that 17 of 18 inflammation-related miRNAs displayed distinct patterns between cerebrospinal fluid and serum in RRMS patients, underscoring that peripheral blood miRNAs do not universally mirror CNS processes [74]. This distinction is particularly relevant for miRNAs such as miR-155-5p, which is elevated in both peripheral blood mononuclear cells and active CNS lesions, versus miR-30a, where CNS-specific expression in EAE microglia involves additional targets beyond SOCS1. From a technical standpoint, there are challenges in the detection and accurate quantification of SOCS1 and SOCS3, especially at the protein level. For example, the specificity of antibodies used in immunohistochemistry may be insufficient, as demonstrated with antibodies that exhibit cross-reactivity and lack specificity, limiting their reliable diagnostic use in human tissue [75]. In addition, obtaining adequate samples (tissue, blood, bronchoalveolar fluid, etc.) and standardizing analytical methods remain obstacles to their routine clinical implementation. For miRNAs the main limitation of using them as biomarkers lies in the lack of standardization and reproducibility in detection and quantification methodologies.

Despite all these limitations, miRNAs and SOCS1/SOCS3 show potential as biomarkers in various diseases. Studies are needed to standardize techniques for their detection and to analyze the influence of genetic and environmental factors on the expression of these biomarkers.

Conclusions and Future Directions

In summary, multiple miRNAs (miR-30b-5p, miR-155-5p, miR-142-5p, miR-409-3p, miR-1896, miR-384) have been identified to regulate SOCS1/3 in immune cells relevant to MS. Figure 1 and Tables 1-2 highlight additional miRNAs candidates (eg, miR-19b-3p, miR-30a/e-5p) that emerge from in silico analyses and warrant investigation in the MS context. The reliability of miRNA target prediction tools, such as TargetScanHuman v8.0, must be evaluated in the context of the general limitations of miRNA target prediction algorithms. Prediction algorithms have high false positive rates and low concordance between different tools, meaning that many of the predicted interactions may not be biologically relevant or functional [76]. While the TargetScanHuman v8.0 database presents a valuable approach for predicting miRNA target genes, its reliability is limited by factors such as the complexity of miRNA-target interactions, the necessity for rigorous experimental validation, the influence of contextual modifications on gene expression, and the need for cautious interpretation of findings in relation to broader biological processes. Future studies should validate these interactions in MS patient samples or disease models, which could open avenues for biomarker development or miRNA-based therapies.

Because miRNAs are detectable in blood and CSF and often remain stable even under storage, there is growing interest in them as non-invasive biomarkers for MS. If certain miRNAs (eg, miR-155-5p, miR-142-5p) correlate with disease activity by modulating SOCS1/3, they could potentially indicate an ongoing inflammatory state or response to therapy. However, careful validation is needed, as circulating miRNA levels can be influenced by many factors. Moreover, technical challenges exist in miRNA biomarker studies, such as normalization and assay sensitivity. In MS, where obtaining CNS tissue is invasive, blood-derived miRNAs that reflect CNS processes (like those affecting SOCS pathways) would be especially valuable as biomarkers. Ongoing research is needed to determine if the miRNAs discussed here have clinical utility for monitoring MS. It is worth noting that miRNAs do not act in isolation; they are embedded in a larger regulatory network. Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), such as the recently described BRE-AS1, can modulate SOCS3 levels by sponging miR-30b-5p, and circular RNAs might have similar functions. Such interactions hint at a complex post-transcriptional regulatory hub involving SOCS1/3, where fine-tuning by multiple RNA species determines the outcome of cytokine signaling.
