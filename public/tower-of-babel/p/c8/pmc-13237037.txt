Towards noninvasive blood count using a deep learning pipeline from bulbar conjunctiva videos
Authors: Tamir Denis, Ifat Sher, Emily Praisman, Marian Haiadry, Amir Zag, Ohad Benjamini, Abraham Avigdor, Keren Asraf, Ram Doolman, Lior Wolf, Haim Suchowski, Ygal Rotenstreich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Invasive venous blood draws remain the clinical standard for hematology, yet they are invasive, time-consuming, and costly. We introduce Video-to-Vessels, a computer-vision pipeline that converts high-magnification videos of bulbar conjunctiva capillaries into low-dimensional spatiotemporal vessel representations, reducing video dimensionality by ~200-fold while preserving hemodynamic information. These representations feed VesselNet, a multi-instance regression network that encodes each vessel with a modified ConvNeXt backbone, fuses vessel-specific thickness via cross-attention, and predicts blood biomarkers from concatenated embeddings. On a cohort of 224 participants with paired laboratory counts, VesselNet achieves a hemoglobin-based anemia ROC-AUC of 82.8% and a Spearman’s ρ of 0.47, while attaining a ρ of 0.46 for red-blood-cell (RBC) count regression. Removing local stabilization and segmentation-denoising lowers ρ by 38% for hemoglobin and 19% for RBC, underscoring their contributions. Our results mark a step toward a fully noninvasive complete blood count, coupling representation learning with ocular imaging.

Introduction

Blood tests, among the most extensive and commonly used medical tests, are essential for diagnosing and monitoring a wide range of health conditions, from anemia and infections to immune deficiencies and malignancies. Despite their critical role in healthcare, these tests remain invasive, requiring venous blood draws followed by time-intensive laboratory analysis. This process causes discomfort for patients, relies on skilled personnel and sterile conditions, and incurs significant costs for transportation, instrumentation, and manpower. Additionally, the generation of biomedical waste and delays in obtaining results can hinder timely, lifesaving interventions.

Among the most frequently tested parameters are red and white blood cell (RBC and WBC) counts and hemoglobin (Hb) concentrations, which provide crucial insights into patients’ health. For example, anemia, characterized by low Hb levels, is one of the most prevalent medical conditions, affecting approximately 30% of the global population. Children and women are particularly vulnerable, with 40% of children and 37% of pregnant women suffering from anemia, according to the World Health Organization (WHO)1. This condition is most common in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in rural areas with limited access to healthcare, resulting in the loss of an estimated 50 million healthy life years annually 1,2.

A noninvasive, point-of-care solution to measure these essential blood parameters could revolutionize healthcare by enabling rapid, accessible, and cost-effective diagnostics, particularly in underserved populations. Optical fingertip sensors that estimate Hb from multi-wavelength light absorption reported correlation with laboratory Hb levels ranging from 0.67 to 0.9 and offer a promising but motion-sensitive alternative to venous sampling3–5. Occlusion-spectroscopy rings improve robustness yet show modest sensitivity and specificity in large trials6–8 while portable spectroscopic probes such as “EZECheck” are susceptible to skin integrity, pigmentation, and temperature effects despite encouraging accuracy reports9,10. None of these point-of-care devices measure RBC or WBC counts, and all interrogate peripheral tissue where perfusion can vary strongly with ambient conditions.

Deep-learning models applied to retinal fundus photographs reach high AUCs for anemia detection but depend on costly ophthalmic hardware and patient cooperation11,12. Low-magnification still images of palpebral or bulbar conjunctiva allow cheaper acquisition, yet existing color-feature methods lack spatiotemporal haemodynamic information13–17. Attempts to predict RBC or WBC counts non-invasively, using optical coherence tomography of lip mucosa, fingertip dynamic spectra, or confocal microscopy, remain proof-of-concept with no reproducible accuracy data18–20. Thus, a technology that captures capillary blood flow with consumer-grade optics while exploiting modern spatiotemporal learning could close a critical diagnostic gap.

Here, we present a new approach for noninvasive prediction of Hb levels and RBC counts through bulbar conjunctiva videos taken with an RGB camera. The bulbar conjunctiva was selected as the region of interest because its background color remains relatively consistent across different ethnic groups, unlike skin tissue. This consistency is due to the low density of melanocytes in the sclera and bulbar conjunctiva compared to the skin’s epidermis21. Consequently, optical assessments of the sclera are less susceptible to the absorption interference of melanin, which often complicates hemoglobin estimation in transcutaneous, finger-based, or skin-facing technologies9,10. We introduce Video-to-Vessels, which first performs sub-pixel video stabilization with a Local Feature TRansformer (LoFTR)22 to compensate for ocular micro-movements, then applies a Full-Resolution U-Net (FR-UNET)23 pretrained on retinal vessels to segment the aligned frames, and finally extracts the spatiotemporal vessel information compactly using mid-vessel over time, which is about 200-fold smaller than the raw video patch. From these representations, we built a dataset organized by participant, where each of the 224 participants contributed multiple vessel instances, all labeled with the participant’s same-day laboratory Hb concentrations and RBC counts. We trained VesselNet, an adapted ConvNeXt regression model, on this dataset; VesselNet aggregates predictions across a patient’s vessel instances to produce single patient-level estimates of Hb and RBC. VesselNet achieved a Spearman ρ = 0.47 for Hb and ρ = 0.46 for RBC, and detects anemia with an AUC of 82.8%. Because the entire workflow relies only on consumer-grade video and automated post-processing, it offers a scalable route to self-administered screening and large-scale epidemiological monitoring of blood biomarkers. Results

To evaluate the results of our approach, we collected data from 224 volunteers (Table 5). Ten-second bulbar conjunctiva videos were taken from both eyes using a ×50 (ø4.5) magnification at a resolution of 1296 × 972 pixels (5 μm) with a frame rate of 33 frames per second (fps).

“Video-to-Vessels” pipeline

Each video was preprocessed using the “Video-to-Vessels” pipeline in six steps, as detailed in the “Methods” section. Briefly, 5-s video segments (160 frames) were extracted with the most focused and well-lit section. These frames were globally stabilized, skipping alignment between outlier frames based on the number of matches from the deep transformer-based feature matcher model LoFTR22. The globally stabilized videos were then divided into patches (128 × 128 pixels), which were further locally stabilized using a similar procedure. Blood vessels were segmented using a pre-trained FR-UNET model23, originally trained on retinal vessels. The segmentation output was refined with temporal denoising and a minimum length filter. Finally, each vessel was converted into a mid-vessel representation spanning 80 × 160 pixels, where the first dimension represents the vessel’s midline along its length, and the second dimension tracks temporal changes (Fig. 2). First, we visually demonstrate the contribution of local motion stabilization in multiple examples (Supplementary Fig. 1). Second, we compare vessel-segmentation outputs with and without the subsequent segmentation-denoising step (Supplementary Fig. 2). Finally, an ablation study contrasted models trained on datasets produced by the full Video-to-Vessels pipeline with those generated by an ablated variant lacking local stabilization and denoising (Table 1). The complete pipeline delivered markedly higher Spearman correlations and anemia-detection AUCs for both hemoglobin and RBC, as removing local stabilization and segmentation-denoising lowers ρ by 38% for hemoglobin and 19% for RBC, confirming the critical value of these components.Fig. 2“Video-to-Vessels” pipeline that converts raw conjunctival video into a low-dimensional vessel dataset.Starting from a patient’s scleral video (left), the pipeline executes six stages: (1) automatic selection of a focused, well-lit 5 s segment (160 frames); (2) transformer-based global motion stabilization with LoFTR; (3) patch-level local stabilization to compensate for ocular curvature and micro-motions; (4) vessel segmentation in each stabilized patch using a pretrained FR-UNET; (5) temporal smoothing and minimum-length filtering to denoise the segmentation maps; and (6) construction of an 80 × 160 “mid-vessel-over-time” image that captures the vessel center-line (spatial axis) and its temporal flow pattern (time axis), together with a scalar thickness estimate. Deep regression for hemoglobin or RBC estimation using VesselNet

To keep the data dimensionality low and the amount of patient information high, each sample consists of 16 randomly selected vessels of the patient and was used to train VesselNet, to predict the patient’s Hb levels or RBC counts. The same model architecture was used for both biomarkers. The model input included 16 vessels from the same patient and their thickness attributes. The model architecture and training procedure are detailed in the methods section and graphically depicted in Fig. 3. A cross-validation method was applied to train 20 different regression models on 20 different groups of patients, each time leaving 10% of the patients for evaluation only. For the Hb assessment, a Spearman’s ρ of 0.47 (CI: 0.35–0.58) was obtained (Fig. 4A). The regression analysis for RBC yielded a similar Spearman’s ρ of 0.46 (CI: 0.35–0.56, Fig. 4C). Bland-Altman analysis revealed good agreement between our algorithm prediction and laboratory Hb and RBC, and with no bias (Fig. 4B, D).Fig. 3VesselNet architecture.Multi-instance architecture for blood-biomarker regression (a). Classification results

Next, we tested the ability of VesselNet to differentiate between healthy volunteers and patients with anemia or low RBC levels. All classification metrics presented here are based on the regression results. The ROC AUC for anemic patients (Hb <11 g/dL) was 82.8% (95% CI: 73.9%–90.3%, Fig. 5A). For mild anemia, ROC AUC of 74.2% (95% CI: 64.1%–84.6%, Fig. 5B) and 70.2% (95% CI: 60.8%–79.1%, Fig. 5C) were obtained for women and men, respectively. ROC AUC of 78.4% (95% CI: 64.8–88.3%, Figs. 5D) and 70.4% (95% CI: 60.0%–79.2%, Fig. 5E) were obtained for the detection of low RBC in females and males, respectively. Data ablation-vessel thickness

Next, we analyzed the effect of vessel thickness on the hemoglobin regression results. Although thinner vessels were typically captured with lower focus than wider vessels, we hypothesized that thinner vessels might harbor essential information for Hb prediction. Thin vessels correspond predominantly to small conjunctival microvessels, capillaries, and post-capillary venules, where individual erythrocytes and leukocytes transit in single-file flow, providing clearer hemodynamic contrast and stronger hemoglobin-related optical signals that may enhance predictive accuracy24,25. Ideally, our model may be able to predict Hb levels out of all the vessels captured with a very large bag size; however, incorporating all vessels increases the dimensionality of the problem significantly relative to the limited amount of data collected in this study, potentially leading to overfitting.

To explore the tradeoff of vessel thickness and dataset size, we trained three models with identical architectures, each using a different subset of vessels based on thickness: thin vessels (≤eight pixels wide), thick vessels (>eight pixels wide), and all vessels without filtering by thickness. As shown in Fig. 6A, the correlation between the model predictions and the laboratory results was significantly better for the model trained on thinner vessels, with a Spearman’s ρ of 0.47 compared to a Spearman’s ρ of 0.3 for the model trained on thick vessels. Additionally, as presented in Table 2, the model trained without vessel thickness filtering performed worse in terms of Spearman’s ρ and ROC AUC, despite being trained on approximately twice the number of vessels. This indicates that thicker vessels have a lower signal-to-noise ratio compared to thinner vessels, and model performance is improved when trained exclusively on thin vessels. The threshold of 8 pixel-wide was selected after observing the vessels’ thickness distribution as shown in Fig. 6B. The chosen vessel-width upper barrier of approximately 8 pixels was guided by both empirical distribution and physiological considerations. In our videos, 8 pixels correspond to approximately 28 µm, which is slightly above the average range for bulbar conjunctival capillaries and is likely representative of a small venules or a larger microvessels, rather than capillaries, but it is still within the full reported microvessel diameter range for this tissue26,27. We predicted that the 8-pixel barrier would effectively separate fine microvessels exhibiting near single-file cell flow from larger conjunctival blood vessels that would contribute higher optical variability and motion artifacts24,25. Note that the median vessel thickness is 7.94 pixels. This means the models were trained on approximately the same number of vessels and indicates that the differences in model results are not attributable to the amount of data but rather to the improved signal-to-noise ratio achieved when training the model exclusively on thin vessels. By isolating and emphasizing thin vessels, our approach leverages the most informative features within the bulbar conjunctiva videos. This finding aligns with our overall methodology of efficiently transforming high-dimensional video data into low-dimensional spatiotemporal vessel representations using our “Video-to-Vessels” pipeline. VesselNet multiple instance learning (MIL)

In order to test the advantage of multiple instance learning of VesselNet, which uses multiple vessels in every sample (bag size), we conducted multiple cross-validation experiments, each experiment with different bag sizes (Table 3). We found that 16 vessels in each sample resulted in the highest correlation between VesselNet estimations and the laboratory results and the highest ROC AUC for anemia detection. Thus, Spearman ρ of 0.47 was obtained for a bag size of 16 vessels, compared to 0.42 for a bag size of a single vessel. Additionally, the bag size of 16 vessels yielded a ROC AUC of 82.8% for anemia detection, compared to 78.5% with the bag size of a single vessel. Although a bag size of eight vessels yielded a slightly higher ROC AUC (83.0% for anemia detection) compared to the bag size of 16 vessels, the correlation with the laboratory results was lower (Spearman’s ρ = 0.42). Increasing the bag size beyond 16 vessels did not improve these results (Table 3). VesselNet architecture ablation studies

Additional ablation studies on the VesselNet architecture included changing the stem kernel size, the ConvNext block kernel size, and comparison between using the thickness value with cross attention or not (Table 4). Interestingly, regardless of the block kernel size, using a stem kernel of 2 × 2 with a stride of 2 yields a higher correlation with the laboratory blood test results with a maximum of Spearman’s ρ = 0.47, compared to 0.41 using a 4 × 4 kernel size with a stride 4 convolutional layer. An additional experiment was done to test the benefit of using vessel thickness values with cross attention, as elaborated in the methods section. Vision Transformer (ViT) results

We also evaluated a Vision Transformer (ViT) model configured with a patch size of 8 × 8 pixels, a 512-dimensional embedding space, and 8 attention heads to predict hemoglobin concentration. Despite the flexibility of self-attention, this Transformer-based approach performed worse than our convolution-driven VesselNet, likely due to Transformers’ higher data requirements and the useful inductive bias provided by convolutions. Specifically, the ViT model reached a Spearman’s ρ of only 0.40 (CI: 0.29–0.51) for hemoglobin estimation and an anemia AUC of 80.0% (95% CI: 70.9%–87.7%), whereas VesselNet’s ConvNeXt backbone achieved better predictive metrics.

Analysis of population-level distribution shifts

To explore the generalizability of our cohort, we performed a computational analysis to estimate our model’s performance on populations with different underlying Hb distributions. We re-weighted our test set samples using importance sampling to match the target Hb distributions for Non-Hispanic White and Non-Hispanic Black populations, stratified each by sex, based on datasets derived from public health surveys, as detailed in the methods section. The Non-Hispanic White population was selected as a representative proxy for the study’s primary cohort (Israel), where the Black population constitutes approximately 2% of the total population28. The Non-Hispanic Black population was specifically chosen to evaluate the model’s robustness under two distinct conditions: (i) the known physiological shift toward lower baseline hemoglobin levels observed in individuals of African descent29–31, and (ii) the potential technical challenges that higher skin pigmentation may pose to transcutaneous, finger-based optical assessments9,10. Our training cohort covers a wide dynamic range of hemoglobin values encompassing the target population distributions used for re-weighting (Supplementary Fig. 3). Our model achieved a ROC-AUC of 82.8% (95% CI: 73.9%–90.3%) for anemia (Hb < 11 g/dL) in our cohort dataset (Figs. 5 and 6). After re-weighting, the ROC-AUC was 82.9% (95% CI: 74.3%–90.2%) and 89.1% (95% CI: 82.6%–94.0%) for Non-Hispanic Black females and males respectively, and 84.5% (95% CI: 77.7%–90.9%), 89.3% (95% CI: 81.3%–95.3%) for Non-Hispanic White females and males, respectively.

Discussion

In this work, we present a new approach for predicting Hb levels and RBC counts through bulbar conjunctiva videos with a simple RGB camera by using a DL pipeline for extracting spatiotemporal information of multiple blood vessels, followed by a deep regression model. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use DL techniques on a bulbar conjunctiva blood vessel videos dataset with spatial-temporal information to predict Hb levels and RBC counts. This work opens new avenues for medical research on visible blood vessels of human bulbar conjunctiva using the same pipeline to create a low-dimensional vessel dataset containing spatial-temporal information. Potentially, future work can use this approach to non-invasively predict additional biomarkers. Using this approach, we were able to extract an average of 41.63 blood vessels per video, effectively creating a large and diversified dataset of vessels despite the challenges posed by a small sample size.

The study dataset had numerous challenges, including small size, lack of longitudinal data, high dimensionality, noise-unfocused parts, blinking, different lighting, etc. We designed an architecture that facilitated feeding the neuronal network with information from a large ocular surface while keeping the dimensionality low. In addition to predicting Hb values, we demonstrate the broader applicability of this approach by estimating RBC counts. Predicting multiple blood parameters from bulbar conjunctiva vessel videos can significantly enhance noninvasive diagnostic capabilities. Furthermore, the consistency of our pipeline for several blood biomarkers (i.e., RBC and Hb) underscores the robustness of our approach using the same dataset extracted with our “Video-to-Vessels” pipeline, and opens new avenues for using ocular data to gain critical health information.

Our ablation studies indicate that thin vessels contain more information for predicting Hb than thick vessels. These thin vessels most likely correspond to the narrow capillaries, where, due to their narrow width, individual RBCs typically pass through in a single file, one at a time, enabling direct visualization of the flow of blood cells. Our experiments show that a more compact ConvNext stem, achieved by reducing the initial kernel size, outperforms the larger kernel stem proposed in the original ConvNext paper, which achieved slightly better results on general-vision benchmarks32. This suggests that small details in the blood vessels are crucial for predicting Hb and RBC values, highlighting the necessity of tailoring model architectures to capture these fine-grained features.

Our findings that WBC counts could not be obtained most likely resulted from the small number of videos that were captured with enough in-focus thin blood vessels that enable the detection of individual cells in the blood flow. Designing a camera with high resolution and higher magnification video imaging capacity is predicted to enable the noninvasive determination of additional blood biomarkers, including WBC. Additional limitations of our study include a small dataset size, and a study population that was Caucasian, aged 18 years old and older. In this study, most patients presented with anemia secondary to their hemato-oncologic conditions, leading to an underrepresentation of other hematologic etiologies, notably iron deficiency anemia, which remains the most prevalent form of anemia in clinical practice, especially in outpatient settings33. Future research will involve larger, more diverse cohorts, including patients with iron-deficiency anemia, and repeated testing, to validate and extend our findings.

As the scleral background color is similar across ethnic groups, skin pigmentation is not expected to affect the results. The effect of pathological conditions such as jaundice and hyperemia on biomarker detection accuracy should be assessed in future studies. Moreover, we performed an importance-weighting analysis simulating performance on Non-Hispanic White and Non-Hispanic Black populations. This analysis yielded ROC-AUCs ranging from 82.9% to 89.3% (compared to 82.8% in our cohort), indicating that our model’s discriminatory performance is robust to population-level shifts in anemia prevalence and hemoglobin distribution. While these simulations rely on the assumption of physiological invariance, we note that our training cohort spans a wide dynamic range of hemoglobin values, encompassing the distributions of the target populations (Supplementary Fig. 3). Furthermore, to mitigate overfitting to the specific distribution of our cohort, we employed cluster-based sampling, which balanced the training data between healthy and extreme values. The model’s resulting ability to accurately regress biomarkers across this broad spectrum (Fig. 4) suggests it captures fundamental hemodynamic signals rather than demographic confounders. Confirming our findings across diverse ethnic groups and distinct pathologies remains a critical objective for future prospective studies. We predict that robust imaging protocols and analytical methods will enable the required adjustment and account for inter-individual and demographic variability.

Several prior studies have explored anemia detection using low-magnification still images of the palpebral conjunctiva, employing ML and DL techniques without utilizing spatiotemporal vessel information13–15. For instance, one study with 556 participants achieved Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.48 for females and 0.53 for males in Hb level regression13. Another study involving 150 participants reported correlation coefficients of 0.45 using a convolutional neural network (CNN) and 0.38 without CNN14,15. Their anemia classification using a threshold of 11 g/dL yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 74% with CNN and 82% without CNN. A third study with 435 participants, including a significant number of anemic individuals, demonstrated strong classification results with an AUC of 90% for a threshold of 9 g/dL and 76% for 12 g/dL15. In contrast, our study utilizes higher magnification videos of the bulbar conjunctiva, capturing rich spatiotemporal information of the blood vessels. With a sample size of 224 participants, we achieved a Spearman’s ρ of 0.47 for Hb level regression, which is comparable to the correlation coefficients reported in previous studies with larger cohorts and a comparable ROC AUC of 82.8% using a threshold of 11 g/dL for all participants. Our current ROC AUC and correlation values, while encouraging for this proof-of-concept study, remain below thresholds typically required for routine clinical implementation. For context, recent work evaluating the FDA-approved noninvasive point-of-care device Pronto Pulse CO-Oximeter (Masimo, Irvine, California), which estimates hemoglobin via capillary absorption at the nail bed using a finger sensor, reported higher ROC AUCs for preoperative anemia screening than those achieved in our study. Specifically, Pronto-7 demonstrated ROC AUC values between 80% and 88% for detecting hemoglobin concentration <13.0 g/dL in males, and 84% to 87% for hemoglobin <12.0 g/dL in females34. These values highlight the need for further optimization and validation of our methods. Further studies using a camera with higher magnification, higher resolution, better focus, optimized for selected spectral wavelengths, and with gaze stabilization, along with larger and more diverse cohorts, are predicted to enhance the accuracy of this noninvasive blood test and validate our findings towards clinical application.

We anticipate that this camera-based technology will ultimately be developed into a lightweight, handheld device suitable for point-of-care deployment in outpatient clinics, such as pediatric clinics, and possibly in home care environments. Our approach may not necessarily serve as a direct substitute for laboratory-based RBC measurement or Hb concentration assessment. Instead, it may serve as an initial screening tool to prompt confirmatory laboratory testing or to enable frequent noninvasive longitudinal monitoring of Hb and RBC. The technology could function as an aid for triage and early decision-making, helping to determine when additional medical evaluation or intervention is required, both in the home and in field settings. This advancement in noninvasive blood tests may pave the way for more efficient and accessible healthcare solutions, especially in resource-limited environments.

Methods

Study approval

The study was approved by the Sheba Medical Center Institutional Review Board and the Israeli Ministry of Health (IRB approval 7473-20-SMC). The study was conducted according to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was received from all participants before their inclusion in the study.

Participants

Based on the study of Dimauro et al., who used a data set of 218 still images of human sclera to classify patients with anemia, and obtained an F1 score >80%17, we enrolled a similar number of participants. Adults (≥18 years) who were able to provide a written informed consent and agreed to undergo venipuncture and bulbar conjunctiva imaging were eligible for inclusion. Potentially eligible participants were identified several days before their planned visit to the clinic by OB and AA, based on previous blood test results documented in the Sheba Medical Center (SMC) electronic medical records. Healthy volunteers were recruited from the investigators’ work environment. 224 adults were enrolled in the Sheba Hemato-Oncology Department (Supplementary Fig. 4). The study followed the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy guidelines (STARD) 2015 (Supplementary Table 1)35. Each subject underwent a standard blood test and imaging of the bulbar conjunctiva within four hours of venipuncture. RBC counts and Hb concentration are considered stable within four hours36–38. None of the subjects enrolled received chemotherapy or blood transfusions, nor did any suffer significant blood loss that could have changed their RBC or Hb concentration during the time period between venipuncture and conjunctival imaging.

Blood samples underwent processing at Sheba’s ISO15189-accredited Automated Mega-Laboratory using a DxH900 Hematology Analyzer (Beckman Coulter) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The laboratory complies with all the requirements of the relevant international standards, including ISO15189, and is routinely accredited by the Israel Laboratory Accreditation Authority (ISRAC), which meets European Accreditation (EA) standards. Laboratory results were exported as a CSV file from the patient’s electronic medical records. Anemia classification binning

Three anemia classifications were used for binning: female and male patients with Hb <11.0 g/dL were labeled as anemic39; mild anemia for women was defined as <12 g/dL, and <13.5 g/dL for men40. RBC classifications were based on the DxH900 Hematology Analyzer (Beckman Coulter) manufacturer’s instructions.

Bulbar conjunctiva video capture

Videos of multiple bulbar conjunctiva blood vessels were captured Using LED Slit Lamp MW50D (Righton, TOHOKU RIGHT MFG. CO., LTD. Miyagi Japan, Fig. 1A). Ten-second bulbar conjunctiva videos were taken using a ×50 (ø4.5) magnification at a resolution of 1296 × 972 pixels (1 pixel ≈ 3.5 μm) with a frame rate of 33 frame-per-second (fps). At least two bulbar conjunctival videos were captured for each patient in both eyes. Image capture was performed in a masked manner. The personnel capturing the images were unaware of the patients’ Hb concentration and RBC counts. No adverse events were observed following the ocular imaging.

“Video-to-Vessels” pipeline- low-dimensional representations of vessels over time

The approach undertaken in this study involved several key preprocessing steps designed to create a low-dimensional vessel dataset from the challenging raw videos of each patient. The primary advantage of our Video-to-Vessels pipeline is its ability to isolate and emphasize vessel-specific information, reducing the dimensionality from 128 × 128 × 160 video frames to 80 × 160 spatio-temporal vessel segments, an approximately 200-fold reduction in dimensionality. This reduction is especially important when training deep learning models on small datasets, as it mitigates overfitting and improves model efficiency. The stages of the pipeline are shown in Fig. 2 and are outlined below:

(1) We first identified the most focused continuous 5-second video segment from the entire video, which exhibits minimal instances of dark or flashed frames. To achieve this, we computed two metrics for each frame: (a) the mean gradient, which measures image sharpness by quantifying intensity changes between adjacent pixels, and (b) the mean Value (V) in the Hue-Saturation-Value (HSV) color space. In the HSV model, Hue represents the type of color, Saturation is its intensity, and Value corresponds to brightness. We focused on the Value channel to gauge the brightness and exclude frames that were too dark or overexposed. By combining these criteria, we ensured that the chosen 5-second segment consisted of frames that were both well-focused and appropriately illuminated, providing high-quality input for subsequent preprocessing steps.

(2) A global stabilization process was implemented upon isolating the desired 5-s video segment. The global stabilization was achieved using Local Feature Transformer (LoFTR)22, a deep feature matcher, to ensure consistency and alignment between frames. Using its matches, we estimate an affine transformation matrix limited to four degrees of freedom, allowing only translation, rotation, and uniform scaling combinations. This approach prevented the stabilization process from introducing errors, such as distortion or misalignment of frames, by avoiding unnecessary transformations like shear, non-uniform scaling, or perspective adjustments, which could degrade the video quality and are not useful considering our data collection process. Due to involuntary eye movements and blinking during video capture, some frames were blurry or “blacked”. To handle involuntary eye movements and blinking, we applied a dynamic minimum match threshold, which defined successful alignment based on a comparison with previously stabilized frames. If a frame was deemed an outlier, we reused the transformation matrix from the last successfully aligned frame. Each participant underwent a standardized video acquisition protocol in which at least two bulbar-conjunctiva videos were captured per eye under identical illumination and magnification settings. If motion artifacts, blinking, or loss of focus occurred, additional captures were performed immediately until a stable and well-focused video was obtained. During preprocessing, videos were screened for focus, stabilization quality, and illumination consistency. Of the 936 acquired participant videos, 207 (22.1%) were excluded due to either excessive eye motion, incomplete stabilization, or insufficient focus, leading to unreliable vessel segmentation. Failures were most often caused by micro-saccades and partial blinks during capture. Importantly, because multiple videos were acquired per eye, no participant was excluded from the study. Each participant had at least one video that met the inclusion criteria and was used in the analysis. This protocol ensured comprehensive subject retention while maintaining high-quality vessel data for subsequent modeling.

(3) Given the curvature of the eyeball, the blood vessels are not aligned in a single plane, and global stabilization is insufficient. Therefore, the video was divided into patches of 128 × 128 pixels, and each patch was further subjected to local stabilization. A context window, 64 pixels larger than the patch itself, was used to provide a broader perspective for effectively stabilizing each patch. This step is crucial to track the blood vessels over time, especially for thin blood vessels. The importance of local stabilization is visualized in Supplementary Fig. 1.

(4) Once the patches were stabilized, blood vessels were segmented using the full-resolution UNET (FR-UNET) deep learning model, which had been pre-trained on retinal blood vessel segmentation22.

(5) The retinal vessels dataset has a domain shift compared to the bulbar conjunctival blood vessels. A denoising technique was applied to enhance the segmentation results, using a temporal smoothing window on the output logits. Furthermore, the data was filtered by component length, assuming the blood vessels are generally thinner and longer than noise artifacts. After getting the masked segmentation on each stable patch, the mask was split into components; each component was skeletonized to measure its length, and finally, components that were less than 80 pixels were removed. This approach helped to obtain consistent crops of individual blood vessels over time and avoid noise, as can be seen in Supplementary Fig. 2.

(6) Each blood vessel in the patch videos was converted to a mid-vessel over time image representation spanning 80 × 160 pixels. The first dimension represents the midline of the vessel along its length, while the second dimension captures its temporal variations. To construct this representation, we tracked vessels across consecutive frames by comparing newly segmented components to previously identified ones. Specifically, we matched each new component to the existing vessel track with the highest intersection over union (IoU), provided the IoU exceeded 30%. This threshold was selected to accommodate the slight positional and shape fluctuations that can occur with extremely thin vessels, even after stabilization. A new vessel track was initiated if no previously tracked vessel surpassed the 30% IoU threshold. After completing this matching process, we skeletonized the combined mask of each tracked vessel over all frames. We use the ratio between the number of pixels of the combined mask and the number of pixels of the skeletonized mask as the vessel thickness value. The skeleton pixels from each frame were then used to form the final spatiotemporal image. Note that any vessel pixels associated with unstable frames, as indicated by the local stabilization step, were replaced with zero values across all channels. Additionally, we excluded the corresponding vessel from the dataset if the spatiotemporal image contained black pixels due to the stabilization process or other factors. The final output of this pipeline was a low-dimensional dataset representing the vessels’ spatiotemporal characteristics, which was then paired with the patient’s laboratory blood test data.

VesselNet- estimating blood test biomarker levels with deep learning

We utilized the low-dimensional vessels dataset to predict hemoglobin (Hb) concentration and red blood cell (RBC) count using a deep learning architecture that inputs multiple instances of vessels from the same patient, including their original thickness. The number of vessels included in each sample is referred to as the model’s bag size.

For the vessel encoder, we employed a modified ConvNeXt architecture32 as a feature extractor for each vessel image. We chose (64, 128, 256, and 512) as the number of channels of each stage and adhered to the recommended block ratio of 1:1:3:1, selecting (2, 2, 6, and 2) as the number of blocks in each ConvNeXt stage. We selected a small number of blocks for each stage because, after experimenting with deeper encoders, we noticed no improvement in the results. The vessel normalized thickness value is followed by a linear layer of 32 channels, and then a multi-head cross-attention mechanism between the vessel thickness and the extracted vessel features was implemented to generate the final embeddings for each vessel. These embeddings were then concatenated, and a simple multi-layer perceptron (MLP) was used to predict the blood biomarker levels (Fig. 3). We optimized the model using the L1 loss function.

Despite having data from only 224 patients, the average of 3.05 videos per patient and 41.63 vessels per video effectively resulted in a larger, diversified dataset of blood vessels, which is crucial for deep-learning approaches. Blood vessels from different patches within the Video-to-Vessels pipeline were treated as distinct individual vessels, contributing to the overall effective dataset size.

During training, we employed a cluster sampling strategy by grouping patients into clusters based on predefined value ranges. We randomly selected a cluster, then a patient within that cluster, and finally sampled the bag size of vessels from the patient using in-place sampling (allowing a vessel to be picked multiple times in each sample). This method helps the model better represent patients with rare or extreme biomarker values. For Hb regression, we used bins of 0–10.5 g/dL, 10.5–14 g/dL, and >14 g/dL; for RBC, we used 0–3.8 million cells/μL, 3.8–5.2 million cells/μL, and above 5.2 million cells/μL.

To equalize the brightness between videos, we first calculated the mean ‘V’ (value) component of the HSV color space for each video’s stable frames, excluding pixels with vessel segmentation, serving as a measure of the video’s brightness independent of the number of vessels or patient blinking. The vessel RGB values were then converted to HSV color space, and the ‘V’ channel was multiplied by 128 and divided by the video’s mean ‘V’. Finally, we converted back from HSV to RGB color space and used these values for the vessels during training and inference. We applied standard image normalization so our vessels dataset would have a normal distribution with a mean of 0 and a variance of 1 in each channel. Additionally, we normalized the vessel thickness values to have a mean of 0 and a variance of 1. To ensure a consistent input size, we extracted an 80-pixel-wide segment from the center of each vessel’s spatial dimension and then applied a random 80 × 80 crop augmentation to the vessel’s temporal dimension.

For all experiments, we used a batch size of 32 samples, 50 batches per epoch, and trained each cross-validation model for 30 epochs. During inference, we used 70 samples for each patient and averaged the results to obtain the final prediction.
