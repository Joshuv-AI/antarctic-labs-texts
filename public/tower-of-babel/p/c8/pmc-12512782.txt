Fluconazole-resistant vulvovaginal candidiasis in reproductive-age women: a study from Himalayan country
Authors: Deepshikha Yadav, Purnima Baidya, Prabhakar R. Panday, Pritee Yadav, Kul R. Rai, Prasha Shrestha, Manita Aryal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVulvovaginal candidiasis is the second most common fungal infections among reproductive-age women. These conditions are commonly treated with the azole derivatives administered locally or systemically, most commonly fluconazole (FCZ). This study aimed to determine the prevalence of Candida spp. infection, access antifungal resistance, and detect different enzymes in Nepalese women of reproductive-age.MethodA cross-sectional study was conducted from Narayani Hospital, Parsa, Nepal. Duplet 345 high vaginal swabs and mid-steam urine samples, demographic, comorbidities, and symptoms, were collected from reproductive-age Nepalese women suffering from vaginitis. Bacteria and Candida spp. were isolated by culture, and further identification of Candida spp. was achieved through molecular sequencing of the Internal Transcribed Spacer region using universal primer sets. Antifungal susceptibility tests, followed by Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method, and minimum inhibitory concentration of FCZ were determined according to Clinical Laboratory and Standards Institute guidelines. Enzymatic activity was also determined using a semi-quantitative method.ResultsThe prevalence of vulvovaginal candidiasis was 6.9%, and Candida albicans (44%) was the leading cause of infection, followed by C. metapsilosis (36%) and C. tropicalis (8%). A correlation was established between vulvovaginal candidiasis and douching, spraying, or washing (COR 0.36, 95% CI 0.13, p value = 0.05). All isolated Candida spp. were resistant to FCZ, and 8% of C. albicans and 4% of C. metapsilosis produce hemolysin, esterase and phospholipase activity.ConclusionsVulvovaginal candidiasis is a significant cause of morbidity, and resistance to FCZ, as well as enzyme produced, indicate a potential challenge for standard treatment approaches. Besides C. albicans, non-albicans Candida are also a possible threat to vulvovaginal candidiasis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04391-9.

Introduction

Vulvovaginal Candidiasis (VVC) is characterized by inflammation of the vagina [1], and the second most common vaginal infection, mainly damaging the vulva and vagina, affecting women of reproductive-age [1, 2]. The most common disorder includes menstrual disorder, musculoskeletal disorder as well as alopecia [3]. This condition affects women at various life stages, and the most common causes include stress, disorders, hormonal changes, trauma, and irritants [2]. Colonization of vagina by Candida spp. is common in healthy women, and studies have shown that 20–30% of asymptomatic, non-pregnant women of reproductive-age may carry Candida spp. without any signs or symptoms of infection [4].

Candida albicans is a globally distributed fungal pathogen and a normal inhabitant member of the human microbiota of mouth, throat, gut, skin, and vagina, producing no harms in healthy conditions [5]. Nonetheless, it may overgrow on mucosal sites or disseminate to other tissues, resulting in infection, which most commonly affects critically ill and immunocompromised patients, with an overall mortality rate of invasive candidiasis ranging from 20 to 50% [5, 6]. Over the last 10 years, the prevalence of C. albicans has remained stable; however, hospital-based data suggest that the incidence and species distribution indicate an increase in infections caused by the Non-albicans Candida (NAC) [7–10]. The NAC includes Nakaseomyces glabratus (C. glabrata), C. tropicalis, C. parapsilosis (no longer exists and replaced by C. orthopsilosis, and C. metapsilosis), Pichia kudriavzevii (C. krusei), Debaryomyces hansenii (C. famata), C. dubliniensis, Kluyveromyces marxianus (C. kefyr), Clavispora lusitaniae (C. lusitaniae), and Meyerozyma guilliermondii (C. guilliermondii) [11, 12].

The most common treatments of candidiasis includes a single dose of oral fluconazole (FCZ) and an azole or clotrimazole cream for external use [13]. Azole target CYP51 enzymes in fungi, cleaving the cell membrane [14, 15]. Emerging data indicate a growing trend of azole resistance, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [16–18]. C. albicans is associated with different levels of FCZ resistance depending on the site of infection [19, 20]. The C. albicans isolated from candidemic patients has the lowest resistance, and oropharyngeal candidiasis has higher resistance depending upon pre-treatment [20]. N. glabratus has the highest incidence of azole resistance, exhibiting intrinsic decreased susceptibility to the azole antifungals. C. tropicalis has 0–83% FCZ resistance [20]. C. parapsilosis has 2–5% of FCZ resistance [20]. P. kudriavzevii has intrinsic resistance to FCZ [20]. Surveillance and prevention of colonization are fundamental in managing patients at increased risk of Candida spp. infections [11].

Different virulent factors present in Candida spp. includes adhesion/invasion, phenotypic switch, proteases production, nutrient acquisition, biofilm formation, pH sensing, thigmotropism, stress response, toxin production, and extracellular enzymes, which constitute essential factors in the initiation and maintenance of infections [21, 22]. The most common enzymes produced include proteinases, DNAse, hemolysin, aspartyl protease, phospholipase, and esterase, which enhance its pathogenicity by accentuating adhesion, tissue damage, immune system evasion, and dissemination [23].

In Nepal, vaginitis and VVC are the most common reproductive-age female health issues [24–26], with limited information available on their species distribution. In accordance with this, timely, and precise detection, as well as the antifungal susceptibility pattern of isolated Candida spp., are crucial for proper diagnosis and treatment of VVC, which is essential for the overall health and well-being of reproductive-age women. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the causative agent of VVC and reported C. metapsilosis as a potential cause of VVC for the first time from Nepal.

Materials and methods

All antifungal agents (amphotericin B (100 U), ketoconazole (30 µg), miconazole (50 µg), clotrimazole (10 µg), nystatin (50 µg), and FCZ), culture media (Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA), MacConkey Agar (MA), Blood Agar Base (BA), Cystine Lactose Electrolyte Deficient (CLED), Mueller Hinton Agar (MHA)) and chemicals (KOH, glucose and methylene blue dye) were purchased from Hi Media company, India.

Study design, setting, and population

A hospital based cross-sectional study was conducted from December 2023 to May 2024 among reproductive-age women presenting with clinical symptoms and visiting the Department of Gynaecology and Obstetrics at Narayani Hospital, Birjung, Parsa, Nepal. Duplet 345 high vaginal swabs (HVS) and mid-stream urine (MSU) samples were collected by professional healthcare workers. The patient’s history and underlying comorbidities are mentioned in Supplementary Methods 1 and 2.

Processing of specimen

Of the two collected HVS, the first was used for the determining pH, and the Whiff test. The detailed method of the pH determination and Whiff test are mentioned in Supplementary Method 3. The second HVS was used for culture in SDA for isolation of Candida spp. [27] and in MA, BA, and chocolate agar for isolation of bacteria, followed by wet mount, and Gram staining. The MSU sample was cultured in CLED agar using a semi-quantitative culture technique with a standard loop (0.01 mL) for the detection of urinary tract infection (UTI) [28]. The detailed method for urine culture is described in Supplementary Method 4.

Wet mount microscopy of HVS

Wet mount microscopy was used for observation of yeast cells and clue cells (Vaginal epithelial cells coated with coccobacillary organisms with indistinct or stippled border) and Trichomonas vaginalis (TV) [29]. Briefly, a thin and homogenous suspension of HVS was prepared using sterile normal saline and examined under the light microscope with x10, x40 and x100 objectives (Primo star, Zeiss, Germany).

Screening of bacterial vaginitis

The screening for bacterial vaginitis (BV) was based on Amsel’s criteria (including vaginal discharge, pH of the vagina > 4.5, the presence of clue cells in wet mount, and a positive Whiff test). If three criteria were positive, then the patient was diagnosed with the BV [25].

Detection and identification of Candida spp

Genomic DNA from Candida spp. was extracted [30] and identified by PCR (T100 Thermal Cycler, Bio-Rad) amplification of internal transcribed spacer regions (ITS1, 5.8s rDNA and ITS2 regions) using universal primers amplifying ITS1 and ITS4 regions [31], and then sequenced by Sanger sequencing. The obtained sequences were blasted against the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database, submitted to GenBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/), and their corresponding gene accession numbers were obtained. The Phylogenetic tree was constructed using Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (MEGA) 12 by the maximum likelihood method and Tamura-Nei (1993) model of nucleotide substitutions. The analytical procedure encompassed 51 nucleotide sequences with 943 positions in the final dataset [32]. The genes used for the analysis are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Antifungal susceptibility test

Kirby-Bauer disc diffusion technique was performed to determine the antifungal drug susceptibility profiles of Candida spp. on MHA containing glucose (2%) and methylene blue dye (0.5 µg/mL) according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [33]. Briefly, a suspension of each Candida spp. from overnight culture was prepared in normal saline and turbidity was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard. The broth was carpet cultured on sterilized, dried media, and then antifungal discs, namely amphotericin B, ketoconazole, miconazole, clotrimazole and nystatin were inoculated. The plates were incubated for 24 h at 35 ℃. Diameter of the zone of inhibition was measured in millimeters (mm), and the interpretation was carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions [33, 34].

Similarly, the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of FCZ against Candida spp. was determined using the broth microdilution method following CLSI guidelines with some modifications. Briefly, the stock solution of FCZ (1280 µg/mL) was prepared and 0.05 µg/mL– 256 µg/mL range of antifungal activity was tested against Candida spp. Briefly, 24 h fresh culture from SDA was transferred to normal saline and turbidity was adjusted with 0.5 McFarland solution. Using microtiter plate, 100 µL of FCZ of different dilutions were added in respective wells followed by the addition of 100 µL of Candida spp. and incubated at 35 ℃ for 48 h. The concentration of FCZ with the ∼50% inhibition of growth compared with a drug-free control was determined as MIC. The MIC (µg/mL) interpretations follow the CLSI breakpoints for FCZ (MIC ≤ 2 µg/mL, Susceptible; MIC = 4 µg/mL, Susceptible-dose dependent; MIC ≥ 8 µg/mL, Resistant) [33, 35].

Detection of enzymes

Enzymatic activity of Candida spp., such as hemolysin, phospholipase, and esterase activity, was determined via a semi-quantitative measurement derived from colony appearance and precipitation zone measurement [36, 37]. Initially, the plate method was used for screening the extracellular enzymatic activities of Candida spp., where 10 µL of the inoculum suspension, at a concentration of 10⁷ Colony Forming Unit (CFU)/mL, derived from overnight growth on SDA media, was applied to each test medium. For hemolysin activity (sheep blood agar plates; 48 h), phospholipase (egg yolk agar plates; 48 h), and esterase (bovine serum albumin plates; 10 days) were incubated at 37 °C and evaluated on different days for enzymatic activity. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using Graph pad version 8. The chi-square test was used to establish the association between VVC and other factors, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant. All experiments were performed thrice.

Discussion

Vulvovaginal candidiasis is estimated to affect 75% of women at some point in their lifetime [4]. Most studies in Nepal typically report STIs and molecular identification of Candida spp. using ITS regions has not been previously reported [40–42]. Since species identification is not typically performed, the problem of recurrent vaginitis has been on the rise. This study provides a comprehensive analysis of microbial aetiology of VVC; including species distribution and antifungal susceptibility patterns among Candida spp. in Nepalese women.

Studies from Asian countries report a wide range of Candida spp. prevalence in women from HVS. In North East India, the prevalence of VVC was 35% [43], 29.7% in Bangladesh [44], 24% in Pakistan [45], 51.3% in Vietnam [27], 14.6% in Malaysia, 19% in Thailand, and 39.5% in Laos [46]. Similarly, in Nepal, prevalence of VVC was 30.95% [47], which was higher as compared to 6.9% (Table 2). The difference in the prevalence of VVC might be attributed to study methodologies, geographical region, environmental, behavioral, and socioeconomic factors [48].

During this cross-sectional study, no significant associations were observed with age, pregnancy, TV, BV, or UTI (Table 2). Similarly, no any significant association was established with underlying comorbidities, history of vaginal infection, sanitary products used during menstrual periods, discharge, foul smell, pH > 4.5, presence of clue cells, itching as well as lower abdominal pain (Fig. 3). However, low association was observed for douching, spray or vaginal washes used by women (p < 0.05). As the douching imbalances vaginal flora and vaginal spray or washing imbalances pH which increases chances of vaginal infection [49]. Although, pregnancy, presence of TV, BV, UTI as well as comorbidities, curdy white discharge and itching should have positive association [2, 50, 51], but negative association with VVC might be due to lower prevalence of Candida spp. due to over the counter drug used and dependency on only culture based diagnosis.

Trichomonas vaginalis is considered a sexually transmitted disease and affects women of reproductive-age group. The prevalence of TV in this study was 0.58% (Table 2) and surprisingly, one of the women suffering from TV had coinfection with BV, while the other was pregnant (third trimester) and suffering from UTI. The prevalence of TV was lower compared to other studies, at 5.4% [42] which may be due to the wet mount technique used for the detection, which has 100% specificity, 33.3% sensitivity, and an accuracy of 66.7% [52].

Among 200 species of Candida spp., only 17 species have been isolated from clinical specimens which includes, C. albicans, C. parapsilosis, C. tropicalis, N. glabratus, P. kudriavzevii, C. lusitaniae, M. guilliermondii, C. dubliniensis, D. hansenii, Cyberlindnera jadinii (Candida utilis), Yarrowia lipolytica (C. lipolytica), Pichia norvegensis (C. norvegensis), Pichia alaskaensis (C. inconspicua), K. marxianus, Diutina rugosa (C. rugosa), C. haemulonii (Torulopsis haemulonii), C. viswanathii and C. albicans var. stellatoidea [12, 53, 54]. C. metapsilosis was proposed in 2005 to replace the existing designations of C. parapsilosis groups II and III and was considered the least virulent [55]. C. tropicalis was isolated from a patient with fungal bronchitis in 1910 and considered the second most virulent [56]. During this study, C. albicans, C. metapsilosis and C. tropicalis (Fig. 6) were isolated, which reflects an increase in infection by NAC [10, 11, 57]. In case of women infected with C. metapsilosis, four women have clue cells and were positive for Amsel’s criteria, reflecting co-infection with BV. The remaining five women do not have co-infection with BV, but symptoms like lower abdominal pain and itching have been observed. In case of women infected with C. tropicalis, both women were co-infected with BV, and both had symptoms like discharge, and one of the patients had a UTI. Although C. albicans is the leading cause of VVC, the prevalence of NAC has been increasing worldwide [58]. Of 63 vaginal C. parapsilosis specimens analyzed by molecular method, 14.3% of C. parapsilosis isolates were identified as C. metapsilosis [58]. Similarly, in another study from Spain, 12 C. metapsilosis were identified from the C. parapsilosis complex [59]. In a study from Nepal, of 71 Candida spp. isolates from clinical specimens were examined, and the results showed that 48 (67.6%) were C. albicans; 11 (15.5%) were C. tropicalis; 9 (12.7%) were N. glabratus, and 3 (4.2%) were P. kudriavzevii [26].

Of the isolated Candida spp., all were resistant to FCZ (Table 3), that threatens the effectiveness of azole-based treatments. FCZ is the most widely used antifungal agent for treating Candida spp. infections in many low- and middle-income countries [60]. In Nepal, previous studies on Candida spp. have shown that 48% of isolated C. albicans and 54.6% of C. tropicalis were resistant to FCZ [26].

The detection of hemolysin, esterase and phospholipase enzymes (Fig. 8) by FCZ -resistant Candida isolates suggests a potential link between antifungal resistance and enhanced virulence. These enzymes contribute to the organism’s ability to invade host tissues, evade immune responses, and cause persistent infections. The concurrent expression of these enzymes in FCZ-resistant strains suggest that drug resistance may be accompanied with increased pathogenic traits, rendering infections more severe and difficult to treat. This highlights the clinical importance of monitoring both antifungal resistance and virulence enzyme activity in VVC.

Conclusion

This study emphasizes the diverse clinical profiles among reproductive-age women undergoing vaginal infection in Nepal. Women with symptoms of vaginitis without any visible discharge, underscores the importance of systematic clinical evaluation beyond symptoms reporting. A weak correlation observed between VVC and the vaginal hygiene practices such as douching, sprays, or washing, suggesting a potential influence of these behaviors on VVC occurrence that warrants further investigation. Antifungal susceptibility testing revealed that C. albicans isolates were susceptible to amphotericin B and resistance to FCZ. NAC species exhibited resistance to FCZ but retained sensitivity to amphotericin B and other agents to varying degrees. Enzymatic profiling indicated that hemolysin and esterase were the most commonly produced virulence factors, with a smaller percentage of isolates expressing all three enzymes: hemolysin, esterase, and phospholipase. This finding highlights the pathogenic potential of both C. albicans and C. metapsilosis strains. This study highlights the clinical significance of NAC in women of reproductive-age. However, the sample was collected from a single regional hospital, which does not represent the whole country scenario. Secondly, this study includes clinical-based recruitment missing women who do not seek care; differing in the community prevalence. So, the future study should be conducted from more regional hospitals and community level, and the antifungal susceptibility pattern should be determined for better treatment outcomes. Since, all isolates were FCZ resistance, exploring the resistance mechanism will be an interesting topic for the future study. Finally, FCZ-resistant Candida spp. with increased virulence capacity have been detected, making infections more severe and difficult to treat.
