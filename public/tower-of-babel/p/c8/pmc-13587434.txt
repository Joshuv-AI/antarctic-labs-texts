Orthodontic loading modulates epithelial progenitor dynamics and enamel formation in the mouse mandibular incisor
Authors: Idan Bar On, Yehuda Klein, Elias Shahin, Jihan Asmar, Sanako Takano, Jimmy K Hu, Stella Chaushu, Amnon Sharir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdult stem cells (SCs) maintain tissue homeostasis through a balance of self-renewal and differentiation regulated by biochemical and mechanical cues. While biochemical pathways are well studied, the in vivo mechanisms by which external forces influence SCs and their progeny remain poorly understood. The continuously growing mouse incisor offers a tractable model for investigating these mechanisms because dental epithelial stem cells (DESCs) reside in a spatially restricted apical niche, and their progeny undergo stereotyped proliferation and differentiation along the apical-incisal axis. We investigated how intrusive mechanical loading affects dental epithelial cell kinetics and enamel regeneration during injury repair.MethodsA modified orthodontic tooth movement device was adapted to apply controlled intrusive forces (0.05–0.5 N) to the mouse mandibular incisor. To stimulate regeneration, the incisal tip was trimmed, and mice were subjected to force application for three days, followed by recovery after device removal. Dental epithelial responses were evaluated using histology, dual EdU/BrdU labeling, in situ hybridization, and micro-computed tomography. Expression of proliferation (Ccnb1) and differentiation (Igfbpl1) markers, as well as nuclear localization of the mechano-responsive transcription co-factor YAP, were analyzed.ResultsForces ≥ 0.2 N caused adverse health effects, whereas 0.05 N induced consistent epithelial changes without systemic consequences. At 0.05 N loading, incisors showed delayed regeneration and hypomineralized enamel. The apical epithelium exhibited folds, tears, reduced proliferation, and premature amelogenin expression. These effects reversed after load removal, coinciding with restored proliferation and enamel deposition. Gene expression analysis revealed apical–incisal shifts in Ccnb1 and Igfbpl1 domains, consistent with altered cell kinetics. Mechanistically, loading decreased nuclear YAP localization in the central body of the labial cervical loop, suggesting a role for this mechanosensitive pathway in mediating the cellular response to mechanical force.ConclusionsThis study presents an in vivo model for examining mechanical influences on dental epithelial progenitor cells. Intrusive loading suppresses proliferation, promotes precocious differentiation, and alters epithelial architecture. These findings support a role for mechanical cues in modulating tissue regeneration and provide a framework for future mechanistic studies in tooth biology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05138-7.Keywords: Orthodontic tooth movement, Stem cells, Mechanical force, Mouse, Mandibular incisor, Enamel formation

Introduction

Adult stem cells (SCs) are required to replenish cells lost through normal wear and tear. To preserve tissue integrity and organ function while preventing excessive growth, adult SCs must precisely balance self-renewal and differentiation [1]. While biochemical signals are vital for SC regulation, growing evidence highlights the important role of mechanical forces, both internally generated and externally applied [2–4]. Due to the scarcity of suitable in vivo model systems, most studies to date have relied on ex vivo culture techniques to analyze SC response to mechanical stimuli [5, 6]. However, SCs may behave differently in culture, where niche constraints are absent. Our understanding of how external mechanical forces influence SC behavior in living tissues thus remains incomplete. Specifically, the in vivo mechanism by which mechanical signals regulate gene expression and proliferation during regeneration and injury repair is poorly defined [4, 7]. Establishing the in vivo parameters of SC responses to external forces is therefore crucial for guiding future mechanistic studies.

The continuously growing mouse incisor is a well-established model for investigating SC biology and dental regeneration [8–10]. Unlike human teeth, which stop growing 2–3 years after eruption [11], mouse incisors contain active SC populations in the apical region that drive lifelong growth. These SCs can differentiate into various adult tooth cell types: Dental epithelial SCs (DESCs) give rise to ameloblasts that form enamel, whereas dental mesenchymal SCs generate odontoblasts, dental pulp cells, periodontal ligament cells, and cementoblasts [12, 13]. DESCs reside within a relatively restricted SC niche in the apical labial cervical loop, while their immediate progeny proliferate and differentiate as they move along the apical-incisal (proximal-distal) axis of the epithelium. This spatial arrangement permits trackable, quantifiable assessment of SC activity and progeny kinetics during both tissue homeostasis and regeneration [14]. Combined with accessibility to mechanical perturbations, the mouse incisor is thus an ideal model for defining how external forces regulate DESC activity within the SC niche and the proliferation and differentiation dynamics of their descendants in vivo.

Mechanical forces are known to influence incisor eruption rates in several rodent species [15–19]. Intrusive loading, which pushes the incisor into its socket, slows eruption, while subsequent load release triggers a recovery period with accelerated eruption [15, 17]. Prior studies, however, often lacked precise control over force magnitude and primarily examined effects on enamel formation and eruption, leaving the impact on DESC activity and progeny dynamics unresolved. Orthodontic tooth movement (OTM) studies have used controlled mechanical forces to explore how rodent teeth, including those of mice, respond to mechanical loads [20–24]. These studies primarily examined how forces remodel the alveolar bone and periodontal tissues through the actions of osteocytes [25], osteoclasts [26], osteoblasts [27], the periodontal ligament [28], and immune cells [29]. Notably, because these investigations focused on molars, the effects of controlled external forces on incisor DESCs remain unexplored.

Here, we explore how external mechanical forces affect the organization, proliferation, and differentiation of DESCs within the rodent incisor during injury repair. To achieve this, we adapted the standard OTM appliance to apply controlled intrusive forces to the mandibular incisor of mice, combined with trimming the incisal end of the tooth. This approach serves two purposes. First, trimming reduces uncontrolled and variable masticatory forces between animals, minimizing confounding mechanical inputs and isolating the effects of the applied load [17, 30–32]. Second, it allows us to examine how mechanical loading affects the ability of dental epithelium and the incisor as a whole to recover from injury. Using this combined approach, we found that moderate intrusive loads delayed incisor recovery after trimming, caused enamel hypomineralization, and disrupted the organization of the apical epithelium. Histological and molecular analyses showed that intrusive forces inhibited progenitor cell proliferation by suppressing YAP activity and altering gene expression and tissue morphology. These findings demonstrate that externally applied mechanical forces can modulate the proliferative capability of progenitor cells during injury repair, and define the in vivo parameters of cellular responses to forces, informing future mechanistic studies of SC mechanobiology in teeth.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6JRccHsd mice (Envigo laboratories, Jerusalem, Israel) at 9 weeks of age were used for all experiments. Mice were maintained in the Hebrew University of Jerusalem specific pathogen-free animal facility following the guidelines established by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and Laboratory Animal Resource Center. All the experimental procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Resource Center at HUJI (No: MD-16562-3).

Orthodontic force application

To apply an intrusive force to the incisor apical region, the previously described method for OTM [33, 34] was modified. The three left mandibular molars were secured as a stationary anchorage unit using 37% phosphoric acid etchant gel (Super Etch, SDI), light-cured adhesive (SecuraLight, Silmet), and light-cured flowable composite (FlowIT, Pentron). A nickel titanium (Ni-Ti) closed-coil spring (1 mm diameter, TOMY International, Tokyo) was attached between the left lower incisor and the molar anchorage unit to apply forces of 0.05, 0.2–0.5 N (3.2 mm, 2.6 mm–1.8 mm, respectively). These forces were independently confirmed using a force gauge (Correx Tension Gauge, Haag-Streit Diagnostics). The incisor was trimmed 2 mm from the tip using a dental probe and scissors. Because load application can affect the function of the contralateral tooth [35], an untreated mouse was used as a control. The health of the treated mice and appliance position were monitored daily. After 3 days, the orthodontic appliance was removed under general anesthesia, and recovery was assessed as described in the following.

Dual EdU and BrdU pulse-chase labeling

Mice received an intraperitoneal (ip) injection of 1 mg per 25 g body weight of 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) (A10044, ThermoFisher) 24 h before sacrifice and bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) (B5002, Sigma-Aldrich) 30 min before sacrifice, respectively. Left lower hemimandibles of sacrificed mice were extracted and processed for immunostaining as described below. EdU+ and BrdU+ cells were counted for each region, and the distance to the most incisal EdU+ cell was measured.

NIBR-LTSi treatment

Starting at the time of force application, half of the day-3 loaded mice were treated once daily with the selective LATS kinase inhibitor NIBR-LTSi (HY-160769, MedChemExpress) [36] at 60 mg/kg via ip injection, or with an equivalent volume of vehicle (5% DMSO, 40% PEG, and 20% Tween 80).

Tissue preparation, histological analysis, and immunostaining

A ketamine/xylazine solution was delivered intraperitoneally. Deeply anesthetized mice were transcardially perfused using PBS and then 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS. Lower hemimandibles were dissected away from the rest of the cranium and fixed in 4% PFA in PBS overnight at 4 °C. Mandibles were then decalcified, paraffin sectioned in coronal or sagittal planes, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For immunostaining, paraffin sections were rehydrated and antigen retrieval was performed by sub-boiling slides in a microwave for 15 min in a citrate buffer (pH 6.2) containing 10 mM citric acid, 2 mM EDTA and 0.05% Tween-20. For BrdU immunostaining, samples were additionally washed with 2 N HCl for 30 min. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-amelogenin (rabbit, 1:300; Abcam), BrdU (rat, 1:500; Abcam), and YAP (rabbit, 1:100; Cell Signaling 4912 S). For dual EdU and BrdU pulse-chase labeling, mouse anti-BrdU (B-35128, Invitrogen, 1:500) was used. Samples were blocked in 1× animal-free blocker (Vector Laboratories, SP-5030) supplemented with 2.5% heat-inactivated goat serum, 0.02% SDS and 0.1% Triton X-100. Antibodies were diluted in the same blocking solution without serum. For detection of YAP, biotinylated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:1000, Vector Laboratories) was used, followed by sequential amplification using VECTASTAIN Elite ABC HRP Kit (Vector Laboratories) and Tyramide Signal Amplification kit (Akoya). All other antibodies were detected using Alexa fluorophore-tagged secondary antibodies (Thermo). EdU was detected using a Click-iT Plus EdU Alexa Fluor 555 or 488 Assay Kit (Invitrogen, C10638). DAPI (D9542, Sigma-Aldrich) was used for nuclear staining.

In situ hybridization

RNAscope 2.5 HD Reagent Kit - RED (ACD, #322350) was used following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sections were treated with peroxide solution at room temperature for 12 min, washed in water and then boiled in the target retrieval solution at 95 °C for 10 min. Samples were subsequently treated with Protease Plus solution at 40 °C for 18 min. Probes for the following genes in Mus musculus were used: cyclin B1 (Ccnb1; 316241), insulin-like growth factor binding protein-like1 (Igfbpl1; 488851), secreted frizzled-related protein 5 (Sfrp5; 405001).

Imaging

Bright-field images were obtained using an upright Nikon Eclipse Ti2 microscope equipped with a Nikon DS-Ri2 camera. Immunostaining fluorescent images were obtained using an A1R+ Confocal mounted on a Nikon Ti2 Fluorescent Microscope. In situ hybridization images were obtained using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope.

Micro-computed tomography (µCT)

Following euthanasia, the left hemimandible was dissected, cleaned of soft tissue, and fixed overnight in 4% PFA, followed by dehydration to 70% ethanol. Samples were kept at -20 °C until imaging using an ex-vivo µCT machine (Zeiss Xradia, Versa520). µCT source was set at 60 kV and 84 µA. A total of 800 projections with a flat panel detector were taken over 360 degrees with 0.5 s exposure time. Volume was reconstructed by the XRadia software XMReconstructor 8.2.2720, which uses the Feldkamp algorithm for filtered back projection. We obtained the final 3D images with an isotropic voxel size of 6.2 µm3. Hydroxyapatite (HA) calibration phantoms (1.6 and 3.1 g/cm3; Himed, NY, USA) were used to facilitate conversion of the linear attenuation of a given voxel to mg HA/cm3. Incisor and enamel features, including the erupted length, mineralization onset and mean density, were quantified using Dragonfly (ver. 2022.2).

Statistics and reproducibility

The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. All experiments were repeated at least three times using independent biological samples. Representative images are shown in figures. Numerical results are presented as mean values ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using Prism (GraphPad, 9.5.1). Normally distributed data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey–Kramer post hoc test. P-values < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Converting OTM appliance to apply intrusive forces to the mouse mandibular incisor

Most OTM studies in rodents have focused on molars in the upper jaw (maxilla), inducing an anterior (mesial) movement of the first molar [23, 34, 37–41]. To investigate how external forces affect the behavior of DESCs and their progeny, we adapted the standard OTM appliance [33, 34] to apply a controlled intrusive force to the lower (mandibular) incisor. All three molars were secured as a single stationary anchorage unit to prevent molar movement, ensuring that the applied force was directed along the incisor axis rather than producing alveolar bone remodeling in the molar region (Fig. 1a).

To determine the optimal force that induces changes in the apical epithelium while minimizing adverse effects, mice were subjected to forces of 0.05 N, 0.2 N, or 0.5 N for 3 days. The 0.2 N and 0.5 N groups showed severe epithelial folding and tearing, tooth recession, and body weight loss, whereas 0.05 N induced detectable changes in the labial cervical loop (laCL) without compromising overall health (Supplementary Fig. 1a). µCT analysis confirmed that the molar anchorage remained stationary, with no measurable molar displacement or change in intermolar spacing, indicating that the force acted along the incisor axis (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Histological measurements further showed that loading displaced the tooth as intended, shortening the distance between the apical epithelium and the alveolar bone (Supplementary Fig. 1c). We therefore selected 0.05 N as the experimental force. This magnitude represents a physiologically relevant load that elicits a biological response without causing excessive tissue damage. Teeth from mice without orthodontic devices served as controls, because device attachment induced some phenotypic changes even without applied force (Supplementary Fig. 1b), likely due to mechanical loading during eruption.

These results show that a modified OTM appliance allows application of retracting forces to the apical region of the mouse mandibular incisor and that a force of 0.05 N effectively induces changes in the apical epithelium without adversely affecting the mouse’s well-being. This setup provides a reliable model for studying how external forces regulate incisor regeneration and influence the biology of DESCs and their progenitors.

Intrusive load delayed tooth regeneration and resulted in formation of enamel with reduced mineral density

To investigate the impact of external force on the ability of DESCs and their progenitors to repair injured teeth and regenerate enamel, we first trimmed the incisor tip and applied 0.05 N intrusive force for 3 days. After removing the load, we allowed the incisors to recover for an additional 7 days. In a separate group, teeth were trimmed without force application (Fig. 1a, b). Trimming the tooth tip also eliminated potential confounding effects from masticatory forces [42]. Without loading, the tooth fully restored its original length and sharp bevel edge within 7 days (Fig. 1c, d). However, following load application for 3 days, the tip remained blunt even 7 days after load removal (day 10), indicating slower tooth growth (Fig. 1c, d).

Next, we examined the onset of enamel mineralization in untrimmed and unloaded (control), loaded (load) and unloaded (no-load) trimmed teeth. In unloaded trimmed incisors, enamel mineralization was initially delayed and shifted towards the incisal edge 3 days after trimming (day 3), but returned to its original position by day 7 (Fig. 1c, e), consistent with normal incisor regeneration [43]. However, in loaded trimmed teeth, enamel mineralization began more apically compared to controls, suggesting an earlier initiation. During the recovery period, the mineralization front gradually returned to its original position (Fig. 1c, e). By day 7, we observed a region of reduced mineral density within the enamel of loaded teeth. This hypomineralized region advanced towards the tooth tip as growth continued, and mineral density was eventually restored between day 7 and day 10 (Fig. 1c, f; days 7–10).

These findings show that the intrusive load applied by the modified OTM appliance resulted in delayed tooth recovery and the formation of less dense enamel.

Mechanical forces alter cell organization and kinetics in the apical epithelium

To maintain enamel homeostasis, progenitor cells in the incisor apical epithelium proliferate and produce differentiating progeny that migrate toward the incisal edge, where they become ameloblasts and deposit the enamel matrix [44] (Fig. 2a). We next sought to understand how mechanical forces affect dental epithelial structures and differentiation by analyzing histological mid-sagittal sections of the incisor apical region.

Histological analysis revealed that 3 days after trimming, apical epithelium shape and cell organization in the unloaded laCL remained unchanged. Compared to untrimmed controls, enamel matrix formation and amelogenin expression shifted incisally at day 3, but returned to the baseline by day 7 (Fig. 2b-d, f; days 3 and 7; no load). These findings closely match the recovery pattern of enamel mineralization we observed (Fig. 1). In contrast, force application caused significant changes in epithelial morphology, including visible tears, folds, and a narrower, displaced laCL (Fig. 2c; days 3, 4 and 7; load). Enamel matrix deposition and amelogenin expression were also located more apically compared to untrimmed controls (Fig. 2c, d, f). Four days after appliance removal (day 7; load), enamel matrix deposition and amelogenin expression shifted back, but closer to the incisal edge, while epithelial folds began to straighten. By day 10, laCL shape, amelogenin expression, and enamel matrix deposition were similar to untrimmed controls (Fig. 2c, d, f).

Incisor regeneration is typically associated with increased cell proliferation [43]. To determine whether delayed recovery in loaded incisors resulted from altered proliferation patterns, we analyzed cell proliferation using EdU and BrdU labeling. EdU was injected 24 h before sacrifice, and BrdU was administered 30 min before sacrifice. This dual-labeling approach enabled us to assess proliferation at the time of sacrifice (BrdU labeling), and track changes in the distribution of cycling cells over a 24-hour period (the distance to the most incisal EdU+ cell).

Three days after trimming without load, we observed a significant increase in proliferating cells (BrdU+ and EdU+ cells) within the epithelium compared to uncut homeostatic teeth (Fig. 2e, g). The region of displaced cells (leading EdU+ cell) also extended further toward the incisal edge, occupying a larger portion of the epithelium (Fig. 2e, g, h; day 3; no load). By day 7, these alterations in cell proliferation and displacement had resolved (Fig. 2e, g, h; day 7; no load). In loaded teeth, however, we observed a marked reduction in proliferating cells at day 3. These cells occupied a smaller area of the apical epithelium and exhibited reduced displacement (Fig. 2e, g, h; day 3 and 4; load). This effect was reversible, as 4 days after load removal (day 7; load), the number of proliferating cells significantly increased and their displacement toward the incisal edge resembled the pattern observed in trimmed, unloaded teeth on day 3. By day 10, the apical epithelium in loaded incisors had reverted to normal (Fig. 2e, g, h).

Together, these results indicate that alterations in enamel formation and mineralization are closely linked to changes in the organization of apical epithelium and amelogenin expression. Furthermore, the observed changes in cell proliferation and migration highlight the dynamic cellular responses to mechanical loading, which contribute to the impaired enamel formation during load application and support epithelial restoration following load removal.

Mechanical forces alter laCL gene expression patterns associated with proliferation and ameloblast differentiation

Given that cell proliferation and differentiation are typically associated with distinct dental epithelial populations in the incisor, our results above could reflect changes in cell composition in response to external load. To investigate this, we performed in situ hybridization to analyze the expression of marker genes representing major cell classes in the incisor apical epithelium [43]. After trimming without load, we observed a significant incisal shift in the expression of Igfbpl1, a pre-ameloblast marker, and Ccnb1, a marker of cycling cells. However, by day 7, their expression pattern returned to baseline (Fig. 3a, b; no load). In contrast, when trimming was combined with loading, Igfbpl1 expression shifted apically during the initial loading period (Fig. 3a, b; load days 3 and 4), followed by an incisal shift after load removal, similar to that observed with trimming alone (Fig. 3a, b; load day 7). These expression patterns reflect the expansion and reduction of proliferating populations, as tracked using BrdU and EdU labeling in both unloaded and loaded teeth (Fig. 2e, g). Notably, in the loaded teeth, Igfbpl1 and Ccnb1 expression levels returned to baseline 3 days later than in trimmed, unloaded teeth (Fig. 3a, b; load day 10 vs. no-load day 7). Lastly, we examined the expression of Sfrp5, which marks both the outer enamel epithelium and the early progeny of Sox2-positive SCs in the cervical loop under homeostatic conditions (Fig. 3c) [43, 45]. However, Sfrp5 expression remained relatively stable after trimming, regardless of loading (Fig. 3c).

Mechanical loading is associated with alterations in laCL shape and cellular dynamics

As tissues and cells can deform under load [46], we next investigated whether orthodontically applied forces induce changes in laCL shape that could affect gene expression. To assess the effects of loading on laCL morphology and cell kinetics, we analyzed transverse sections of the incisor. In these sections, the laCL resembles the shape of a bird’s wishbone, consisting of mesial and lateral epithelial arms encircling the pulp, along with a centrally located laCL body facing apically (Fig. 4a).

Results showed that unlike the normal laCL shape and architecture in trimmed teeth without loading, 3 days of loading caused significant changes in epithelial morphology. Specifically, the mesial and lateral arms were shorter and farther apart, resulting in a decreased arm length-to-width ratio (Fig. 4b, d; day 3 load). Additionally, the central body exhibited distinct apical-incisal narrowing of the middle segment (Fig. 4b, insets). After load removal, cellular arrangement, epithelial shape, layer structure, and arm length-to-width ratio gradually returned to normal (Fig. 4b, d; days 3–10).

Consistent with our proliferation analysis above, transverse sections also showed a decrease in the number of BrdU+ and EdU+ cells in the central bodies of loaded teeth, while the arms appeared less affected. Following load removal, the number of dividing cells in the central body returned to normal levels (Fig. 4c-e).

These findings indicate that mechanical loading induces significant changes in the shape and cellular dynamics of the laCL, which are restored following load removal. Furthermore, the reduction in proliferative cells in the central body suggests a localized response to mechanical stimuli within the incisor epithelium.

Mechanical loading reduces nuclear Yes-associated protein (YAP) localization in the laCL body center

Because our results showed that cell proliferation and differentiation changed in response to force application and removal, we next examined how laCL cells may translate mechanical signals into transcriptional responses. We focused on the mechanoresponsive transcription co-factor YAP, which translocates between the nucleus and the cytoplasm depending on the cell’s mechanical state [47, 48]. By regulating expression of genes important for proliferation and differentiation, nuclear YAP functions as a key regulator of progenitor cell activity in many SC systems [48–50], including the incisor laCL [51, 52].

Immunostaining results showed that the intensity of nuclear YAP in laCL cells was significantly reduced in loaded teeth on day 3 compared to controls. This reduction occurred in the central body region, where proliferation was halted during loading (Fig. 5b, d). Following load removal, nuclear YAP intensity in the central body returned to normal levels, coinciding with the restoration of cellular proliferation and laCL morphology (Fig. 5b, d). Importantly, trimming alone, without loading, had no notable effect on YAP intensity, underscoring the role of mechanical forces in modulating YAP activity.

Because YAP inactivation and cytoplasmic retention depend on phosphorylation by kinases such as LATS1/2 in the Hippo pathway, we tested whether load-induced nuclear exit of YAP is mediated through this signaling axis. To assess this, we treated half of the day-3 loaded mice with NIBR-LTSi, a selective LATS1/2 inhibitor that prevents YAP phosphorylation and promotes its nuclear localization [36]. In these mice, loading-induced reduction of nuclear YAP in the laCL center was attenuated compared with vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 5b, d). Correspondingly, proliferation in the central body, which was nearly abolished by mechanical loading, was partially rescued in NIBR-LTSi–treated mice (Fig. 5c, e). Together, these results show that mechanical loading impedes proliferation by reducing nuclear YAP localization, in part through LATS1/2-mediated Hippo signaling, thereby linking spatial YAP localization to mechanotransduction and proliferation of progenitor cells in the laCL.

Discussion

The influence of mechanical forces on adult SC activity remains poorly understood, largely due to the lack of suitable in vivo animal models [4, 6]. In this study, we used a modified orthodontic device to investigate how externally applied forces influence the organization, proliferation, and differentiation of the dental epithelial lineage during incisor regeneration. Unlike most rodent OTM studies, which focus on alveolar bone responses [20–22], our study examines cellular responses of incisor DESCs and their epithelial descendants to controlled mechanical load. The mandibular incisor was chosen because of its relatively straight longitudinal axis, which facilitates efficient force transmission to the apical epithelium [53], and its status as a well-established model for adult dental SCs [9, 10, 54].

Using this device, a 3-day load application caused folds and tears in the incisor epithelium, reduced the number of dividing cells, and accelerated ameloblast differentiation and enamel mineralization. Similar epithelial folding was observed in previous studies when eruption was prevented by elastic bands [55], pinning the incisor to its socket [56], or capping the tooth [57]. However, those studies lasted weeks to months and folding was attributed to the accumulation of cells under continuous growth. In the current study, folds occurred within 72 h and likely arose from compressive stress that buckled the epithelium along the apical-incisal direction.

Our findings show that, in the absence of ectopic loads, epithelial progenitor proliferation increases and ameloblast differentiation occurs closer to the incisal end of the injured teeth. While delaying ameloblast differentiation may seem counterintuitive when rapid enamel production is needed to restore the trimmed tooth tip, the delay likely allows progenitor cells to continue proliferating, ultimately generating more ameloblasts. A similar incisal shift in differentiation, accompanied by extensive proliferation, has been observed during periods of rapid postnatal incisor growth and recovery from cytotoxic injury [43]. Comparable mechanisms that enhance progeny production by temporarily favoring proliferation over differentiation have also been demonstrated in other organs during injury repair [58].

Despite these regenerative changes, we did not detect a clear alteration in Sfrp5 expression, which marks the OEE and early descendants of Sox2-positive SCs in the laCL [45]. The absence of a detectable increase in Sfrp5 expression contrasts with our previous findings from a cytotoxic injury model, in which Sfrp5-positive cells expanded near the SC niche following 5-FU treatment, which induces apoptosis in proliferating cells and thus direct injury to the cervical loop [43]. This difference may reflect distinct regenerative programs triggered by mechanical trimming versus severe cytotoxic injury. One possibility is that trimming-induced regeneration is driven primarily through changes in transit-amplifying and differentiating cell populations, leaving the upstream Sfrp5-positive stem or early progenitor compartment relatively unchanged. On the other hand, loss of transit-amplifying cells due to cytotoxic injury would require re-activation of DESCs to repair the injury. Another possibility is that changes in Sfrp5 expression were highly transient following trimming and occurred outside the time window examined in this study. Finally, regeneration-associated alterations within the SC compartment may be reflected by markers other than Sfrp5. Future studies incorporating spatiotemporal single cell transcriptomic and earlier post-injury time points will be necessary to distinguish among these possibilities.

Our data show that once enamel regeneration is complete, the apical region quickly returns to its steady-state kinetics and shape, demonstrating its plasticity. However, applying an intrusive load immediately after trimming delayed tooth growth and recovery, thereby delaying regeneration and restoration of normal tooth function. Mechanical loading is a key regulator of SC proliferation, differentiation, and fate determination, as well as the behavior of their progeny during tissue regeneration. The type, magnitude, and duration of the forces can activate distinct signaling pathways that promote proliferation and direct SCs, including DESCs, to differentiate into specific lineages [7, 59–63]. However, excessive or improperly applied loading can disrupt SC function and the behavior of downstream progenitor populations, resulting in reduced self-renewal, cell death, aberrant differentiation, and niche disruption [4]. Similarly, low-to-moderate orthodontic forces preserve root integrity and may enhance cementum repair by stimulating cementoblast differentiation [64]. Conversely, excessive forces can cause periodontal damage, persistent inflammation, and odontoclast activation, increasing the risk of root resorption and inhibiting cementum repair [65].

In this study, applying 0.05 N of intrusive force to the mouse incisor reduced proliferation within the epithelial progenitor compartment and triggered earlier differentiation. This contrasts with studies of stretched cultured dental pulp SCs [61]. The discrepancy may stem from differences in how mesenchymal and epithelial cells respond to mechanical loading and from differences in loading paradigms and the forces experienced by cells. These findings also underscore the challenge of translating in vitro results to in vivo applications [4]. Notably, even 0.05 N, which is much lower than the force applied to the mouse incisor during biting [66], caused significant changes in the behavior of epithelial progenitor and differentiating cell populations. This may reflect the application of a constant load rather than the brief, intermittent forces experienced during chewing. For instance, short bursts of mechanical stimulation enhance osteogenic differentiation, whereas prolonged exposure leads to negative cellular responses, such as reduced proliferation and abnormal chondrocyte differentiation [67].

In humans, ameloblasts undergo apoptosis after enamel formation, and most DESCs disappear, leaving epithelial cell rests of Malassez (ERM), remnants of Hertwig’s epithelial root sheath, as the primary epithelial population in the periodontal ligament. ERM cells, which persist throughout adult life, help maintain the periodontal ligament space [68], contribute to cementum and periodontal repair [69], and, under certain conditions, produce proteins secreted by ameloblasts [70]. Various types of orthodontic forces modulate ERM activity differentially. Tensile forces increase ERM proliferation, whereas compressive forces reduce it [71–73]. The latter is consistent with our findings, as we examined only compressive forces.

YAP is a mechanoresponsive transcriptional co-factor whose localization depends on the mechanical context [47, 74, 75]. We observed reduced nuclear YAP localization in loaded incisors, which was restored after load removal. Notably, pharmacologic inhibition of LATS partially rescued nuclear YAP localization and cell proliferation during loading, supporting a functional link between Hippo pathway activity and force-associated suppression of epithelial progenitor proliferation. Together, these findings suggest that LATS1/2 mediate part of the response to compressive mechanical cues that regulate YAP localization and progenitor cell behavior. However, additional mechanisms likely operate in parallel, as the rescue was incomplete. Future studies using genetic approaches will be required to establish causality and to define the upstream mechanotransduction pathways involved.

Conclusions

This study describes the application of controlled intrusive forces to the mouse mandibular incisor using a modified OTM device as a useful system for in vivo investigation of how mechanical loading influences dental epithelial lineage and incisor regeneration. Intrusive force was associated with suppression of proliferation, accelerated differentiation, and altered epithelial architecture, effects that were reversible upon load removal. Future studies examining different force types and magnitudes, as well as the molecular mechanisms that mediate their effects, will deepen our understanding of how mechanical forces regulate dental epithelial cell behavior, SC-progenitor dynamics, and enamel regeneration.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. Loading lasted three days and does not capture long-term responses. Our analysis focused on the dental epithelium; we did not examine the mesenchyme, periodontal ligament, or alveolar bone, which may be also influenced by the device. Mechanistic analysis relied on a candidate approach centered on YAP/Hippo signaling; other mechanotransduction pathways may contribute. The applied force was constant and does not reflect the intermittent forces of mastication.
