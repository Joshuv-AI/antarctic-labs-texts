Post-cervical artificial insemination with a low sperm dose in gilts and sows improved reproductive performance in subtropical climates
Authors: Mahak Singh, Rahul Katiyar, J K Chaudhary, Rekha Yadav, P L Bhutia, J Barman, Rakesh Kumar, Jayanta Chamuah, H Kalita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of post-cervical artificial insemination (PCAI) and corresponding sperm dose in gilts is still inconclusive. The present study evaluated the effects of cervical artificial insemination (CAI) and post-cervical artificial insemination (PCAI) using three semen doses (3 × 109 sperm/75 mL, 2 × 109 sperm/50 mL, and 1 × 109 sperm/25 mL) on reproductive performance, procedure efficiency, and adverse events in gilts and sows under subtropical climatic conditions. A total of 716 female Hampshire and Ghungroo crossbred (50%) pigs (284 gilts and 432 sows) were allocated to a 2 × 3 factorial experimental design with AI technique (CAI vs. PCAI) and semen dose as fixed factors. The interaction effect between AI techniques and semen doses on reproductive variables was also analysed. In gilts and sows, non-return and farrowing rates were significantly higher in PCAI than CAI at all semen doses. Similarly, total piglets born, piglets born alive, and litter size at weaning in gilts and sows were significantly influenced by AI technique, semen dose, and their interaction, with PCAI outperforming CAI at lower doses. However, PCAI catheter passage in gilts and sows required significantly more attempts than CAI. PCAI significantly reduced the time to infuse semen doses and total procedure time across doses in sows. Semen backflow was significantly lower in gilts and sows inseminated by PCAI. Our data suggest that, under conditions of successful catheterization (per-protocol), PCAI at lower semen doses was associated with improved reproductive performance in gilts and sows, reduced semen loss, and enhanced procedural efficiency compared to cervical AI; however, these findings may vary with catheterization success.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46814-1.Keywords: Pig, Cervical artificial insemination, Post-cervical artificial insemination, Reproductive efficiency, Semen backflowSubject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Physiology, Zoology

Introduction

Pig production plays a critical role in global food security due to the pig’s exceptional biological efficiency in converting low-value feed resources and agro-industrial by-products into high-quality animal protein in the form of pork. Pork is currently the second most-consumed meat worldwide, and its demand is projected to increase with population growth and changing dietary preferences. Meeting this demand in a sustainable manner requires producing more pork from fewer animals, thereby increasing the efficiency of pig production systems, particularly reproductive efficiency, which is the primary driver of productivity and genetic progress1,2. Already, pig farming has benefitted immensely from reproductive biotechnologies such as artificial insemination (AI), estrus synchronization and fixed time AI3–7. AI is the most important reproductive biotechnology, having tremendously improved the efficiency and genetics of swine worldwide2,4,8. The widespread adoption of AI has enabled specialised boar studs to house genetically superior boars for semen collection and distribution, reducing the need to maintain large numbers of breeding boars while maximising the dissemination of elite genetics. In natural mating, a boar can sire 40–60 pigs in a year; however, the use of AI can increase this number by nearly 20-fold3. Consequently, AI is currently used in more than 90% of breedable sows in major pig-producing countries, primarily with liquid-stored semen4,9. Recently, the adoption of pig AI has also increased in several developing countries, including India, Vietnam, and Thailand, although its efficiency under tropical and sub-tropical conditions remains constrained6,10,11.

Traditionally, AI in pigs involves the use of three billion motile spermatozoa in 80–90 ml of liquid stored semen, and sperm are deposited in the anterior cervix using a disposable catheter2,12. Although this approach generally yields acceptable reproductive performance, with farrowing rates of 70–90% and litter sizes of 10–14 piglets, it requires high sperm numbers and often multiple inseminations per estrus to ensure optimal fertility. As a result, the utilisation efficiency of semen from genetically superior boars remains suboptimal. There is significant sperm waste, as fertilization requires only a small number of spermatozoa4–6,12.

To bridge this gap, several innovations in AI have been proposed, including reductions in sperm dose per AI or per estrus, improvements in boar sperm fertility, intra-uterine or post-cervical artificial insemination, and fixed-time AI3,13. The post-cervical AI consists of depositing the semen in the uterine body through an inner canula inserted into the main catheter14. In recent years, there has been renewed interest in this technology, and consequently, more studies have been reported from different countries11,13,15. The main advantage of PCAI is the use of fewer spermatozoa per AI dose, with outcomes similar to or greater than those of traditional AI16. Therefore, more semen doses can be prepared from a single ejaculate, and more sows can be bred from fewer boars. This has increased the population of siblings on pig farms, leading to more homogeneous pork quality at slaughterhouses11,12,17. Moreover, genetic improvement in PCAI is faster than that in traditional AI because of greater use of higher indexing boars. Additionally, the time required to perform PCAI decreases significantly, with minimal sperm loss through backflow13,17. The sum of these benefits results in significant financial savings12,18. However, there is still no consensus on the number of sperm required for acceptable reproductive outcomes, and some studies have documented the adverse results of PCAI in gilts19–21. On the other hand, Suarez-Usbeck et al.17 suggested the use of PCAI in gilts with 1.5 billion spermatozoa, achieving a 92% farrowing rate and producing 18 piglets per litter.

The need to optimize semen utilization is particularly acute in tropical and sub-tropical production systems, where indigenous and locally adapted boars often produce lower semen volumes, fewer total sperm per ejaculate, and semen of variable quality compared with boars maintained under temperate conditions22,23. Under these conditions, total sperm output per ejaculate commonly ranges from 20 to 30 billion, restricting the number of sows that can be inseminated from a single ejaculate when conventional sperm doses are used22,24. Improving semen-use efficiency through advanced insemination techniques, such as PCAI, therefore represents a biologically and economically important strategy for sustainable pig production in these environments. Additionally, few studies have examined the use of PCAI in subtropical regions; most have focused on sows with few animals, and none have included gilts.

Therefore, the present study was designed to test the hypothesis that post-cervical artificial insemination improves reproductive efficiency by allowing successful fertilization with reduced semen doses compared with cervical insemination in gilts and sows. Hence, the present study was conducted to (i) study the effects of different semen doses in PCAI and cervical AI on the reproductive performance of sows and gilts, and (ii) to study the effect of parity on reproductive performance using PCAI.

Materials and methods

Experiment location

The experiment was conducted at Pig Research Farm of the Indian Council of Agricultural Research, Nagaland Centre, Medziphema, Nagaland, from April 2020 through December 2024. The experimental location has a subtropical climate with hot, humid summers and pleasant winters. The annual rainfall ranges from 1500 to 2000 mm. The procedures involving animal handling, management, care, and experimentation were conducted in accordance with the norms of the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee, as established by the ICAR Research Complex for NEH Region, Umiam, Meghalaya, India, and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The study was approved by the Institute Animal Ethics Committee of ICAR Research Complex for NEH Region, Umiam, Meghalaya, India (2100/GO/RBi/L/20/CPCSEA) under ICAR-All India Coordinated Research Project on Pig (OXX01916) and the Tribal Sub-Plan of the ICAR Research Complex for NEH Region, Umiam, Meghalaya, India. No animals were euthanized, sacrificed, or killed as part of the experimental procedures described in this study. All procedures were conducted on live animals under standard husbandry and handling conditions, in accordance with relevant institutional and ethical guidelines.

Animals

For this study, a total of 716 Hampshire and Ghungroo crossbred (50%) pigs (284 gilts and 432 sows) were used. Gilts were 260–285 days old, weighed 105.24 ± 5.85 kg, and had one previous estrus. Sows were 2–5 parities and weaned at 35 days after farrowing. All the gilts and sows used in this experiment came to estrus naturally, and no estrus synchronization protocols were used. The animals were housed in individual pens (10 m2) throughout the study. There was no artificial environmental management system in the pig shed other than the roof fan. Animals were offered ready-made commercial pig breeder feed twice daily (8.00 am and 16.00 pm). Water was provided ad libitum. Throughout the study, uniform farm management practices were followed for routine activities. For semen collection, 37 boars of the same genetic origin from the boar semen station of ICAR Nagaland Centre were used in this study. Boars were housed in a separate shed away from the gilts and sows’ sheds. All experimental animals used in this study were owned and maintained by the Pig Research Farm of the Indian Council of Agricultural Research (ICAR), Nagaland Centre, Medziphema, Nagaland, India.

Experimental design

The study was conducted on cycling gilts and multiparous sows maintained under standard management conditions at a swine research farm. The experiment was organised separately for gilts and sows, and for each parity group, a 2 × 3 factorial arrangement of treatments was used in a completely randomized design. Gilts and sows were analysed separately to account for physiological differences between parity groups. Within each parity category, animals were assigned to treatment combinations using a randomized block approach as they entered estrus, ensuring balanced distribution across treatment groups and avoiding systematic bias. Sows of different parities were distributed across treatments to prevent imbalance among groups. Two fixed factors were evaluated: AI technique (Factor A; cervical artificial insemination [CAI] and post-cervical artificial insemination [PCA]) and semen dose (Factor B; 3 × 109, 2 × 109, and 1 × 109 sperm per dose). This produced six treatment combinations within each parity category (CAI–3 × 109, CAI–2 × 109, CAI–1 × 109, PCAI–3 × 109, PCAI–2 × 109, and PCAI–1 × 109). The individual gilt or sow per oestrus served as the experimental unit.

Semen collection and processing

Proven fertile boars of the same genetic lines were used in this study. Ejaculates were pooled prior to dose preparation, heterospermic doses were prepared using semen from three boars per dose, and pooling was performed immediately after semen quality evaluation. This approach was used to reduce the influence of individual boar fertility differences. Boar semen was collected by standard double-gloved-hand techniques twice a week in a pre-warmed thermos fitted with a filter to remove the boar semen gel. The semen was transported to the laboratory within five minutes of collection and transferred to a graduated glass cylinder maintained at 37 °C. The semen was evaluated for total motility, progressive motility, concentration (haemoctometer), live-dead spermatozoa (eosin-nigrosin), acrosomal integrity (Giemsa stain) and abnormality using a phase contrast microscope. To prevent human bias, separate technicians collected and evaluated boar semen in the laboratory. Semen ejaculates with a minimum 80% motility, 90% intact acrosomes and less than 20% sperm abnormalities were selected for further use in the AI program. Immediately after evaluation, ejaculates were diluted with commercial PrimxCell extender at 37 °C. The dilution was made so that each semen pouch contains 3 billion sperm in 75 ml, 2 billion in 50 ml and 1 billion in 25 ml extender for CAI and PCAI. According to previous studies, the insemination volume used with PCAI does not affect fertility13,19; however, in the present study, sperm dose and volume were reduced simultaneously, preventing separation of their individual effects. Semen doses were stored at 15–18 °C in a BOD incubator for 72 h. Semen doses were prepared from pooled ejaculates using the same quality thresholds and extender, and the resulting pooled semen batches were distributed across treatment groups without systematic preference, thereby minimizing the risk that a particular boar or batch was associated with a specific treatment.

Estrus detection and AI

Estrus was detected on the farm with the help of a mature boar parading and manual visualization in the morning and evening before feeding. Estrus was determined using the back pressure test in the presence of a boar. The time of onset of estrus was defined as the first time a gilt or sow showed a standing response, standing rigidly to back pressure, with vulval swelling and reddening, vocalisation, frequent urination, and a positive ear reflex. Post-cervical AI was performed via PC Blue, SafeBlue Foamtip with PCAI cannula (17112/2000, Minitube, Germany) and cervical insemination was performed via Golden Gilt (IMV, France) catheter. In gilts, the first AI was performed upon detection of estrus, and the procedure was repeated 24 h later. In sows, the first AI was performed 24 h after estrus detection, and a second AI was performed 12 h later. Two inseminations were performed per animal, in accordance with the farm’s standard operating guidelines. AI (both CAI and PCAI) was performed in the absence of a boar. Six technicians performed AI (both CAI and PCAI) on gilts and sows at random. Technicians conducted both CAI and PCAI across treatment groups, and efforts were made to distribute their work among treatments to minimize operator-related bias. A maximum of three attempts was made to insert PCAI catheters. When the PCAI catheter failed to pass in sows and gilts, CAI was performed, and these animals were excluded from the experiment. The average total time (seconds) to perform the AI procedure was recorded from the beginning of catheter insertion to the end of dose infusion. Similarly, the average time taken to infuse the dose (seconds) was recorded from the start to the end of infusion for all animals. Data were also recorded for unsuccessful passage of PCAI catheter, difficult passage, semen backflow, and bleeding. Semen backflow and bleeding post AI were recorded as qualitative variables only.

Assessment of reproductive performance

The return to estrus was observed by boar parading and manual observation. Accordingly, non-return to estrus was recorded for animals that did not return to estrus after AI within 25 days. Reproductive performance was recorded in terms of farrowing rate (%), total number of piglets born per farrowing, and the number of live-born piglets per farrowing. Stillborn piglets and mummified fetuses for every farrowing were also recorded. Weaning was done at 35 days of age, and pre-weaning piglet mortality was also recorded. Similarly, litter size at weaning was also recorded for every farrowing.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27. All statistical analyses were conducted according to the 2 × 3 factorial design with artificial insemination (AI) technique (cervical AI, CAI; post-cervical AI, PCAI) and semen dose (3 × 109, 2 × 109, and 1 × 109 sperm per dose) as fixed factors, analyzed separately for gilts and sows. The individual animal per estrus served as the experimental unit. Binary outcomes were analyzed using generalized linear models with a binomial distribution and a logit link function. Results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Count outcomes were analyzed using generalized linear models with a Poisson distribution and a log link. Results are expressed as incidence rate ratios (IRR) with 95% CI. Dispersion statistics were examined to assess model fit (Supplementary file). Continuous variables (total time required for insemination and time required for dose infusion) were analyzed using a two-way factorial linear model with fixed effects for AI technique, semen dose, and their interaction. Results are presented as mean ± SEM and model-based mean differences with 95% CI. Model assumptions for continuous outcomes were assessed using residual diagnostics, including the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality and Levene’s test for homogeneity of variances. Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05.

Results

Effect of AI techniques and sperm dose on the gilt’s reproductive performance

In gilts, the AI technique and semen dose had significant (p < 0.05) effects on non-return (Fig. 1) and farrowing rates (Fig. 2), whereas the interaction between AI technique and dose was not significant (Table 1). For the non-return rate, the odds of success were approximately twice as high with PCAI as with CAI (OR = 2.02, 95% CI 1.14–3.59, p < 0.05). Similarly, for farrowing rate, PCAI gilts had about 2.13 times higher odds of farrowing compared with CAI gilts (OR = 2.13, 95% CI 1.24–3.66, p < 0.01). PCAI was associated with significantly higher litter size outcomes than CAI, including total piglets born, piglets born alive, and litter size at weaning (IRR > 1; p < 0.001 for AI effect). The total number of piglets born differed significantly among AI techniques (p < 0.01), semen dose categories (p < 0.01), and their interaction (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3A). The number of piglets born alive also showed significant effects of AI technique (p < 0.01), semen dose (p < 0.01), and their interaction (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3B). Litter size at weaning was significantly influenced by AI technique (p < 0.01), semen dose (p < 0.01), and their interaction (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3C).

Effect of AI techniques and sperm dose on the sow’s reproductive performance

In sows, the non-return (Fig. 4) and farrowing (Fig. 5) rates were significantly (p < 0.05) higher in PCAI groups than in the CAI groups, and there was a significant effect of semen dose (Table 2). For the non-return rate, the odds of success were higher with PCAI than CAI (OR = 2.30, 95% CI 1.41–3.74, p < 0.001). For farrowing rate, PCAI also increased the odds of farrowing (OR = 2.03, 95% CI 1.30–3.15, p = 0.001). Total piglets born differed significantly by AI technique (IRR = 1.38, 95% CI 1.28–1.48; p < 0.01), semen dose, and their interaction (all p < 0.01) (Fig. 6A), with PCAI groups having significantly higher values. Similarly, the number of piglets born alive was significantly affected by AI technique (IRR = 1.40, 95% CI 1.30–1.51; p < 0.01), semen dose, and their interaction (all p < 0.01) (Fig. 6B). Pre-weaning mortality differed significantly between AI techniques (p < 0.01) and semen dose (p = 0.03), although their interaction was not significant. Litter size at weaning was significantly influenced by AI technique (IRR = 1.38, 95% CI 1.28–1.50; P < 0.001), semen dose, and their interaction (all p < 0.01) (Fig. 6C), with PCAI maintaining higher weaned litter sizes across all semen doses compared to CAI.

Effect of AI techniques and sperm dose on adverse events and semen backflow in gilt

PCAI was associated with markedly lower odds of successful catheter passage (Table 3) compared with CAI (OR = 0.01, 95% CI 0.00–0.08; p < 0.01) and substantially higher odds of unsuccessful passage (OR = 127.4, 95% CI 7.7–2099; p < 0.01). The likelihood of successful passage on the first attempt was also significantly lower with PCAI (OR = 0.02, 95% CI 0.01–0.05; p < 0.01), whereas difficult catheter passage occurred more frequently (OR = 4.60, 95% CI 2.29–9.25; p < 0.01). In gilts, PCAI required approximately 47% more catheter insertion attempts than CAI (IRR = 1.47; 95%CI 1.34–1.61; p < 0.01). For time-related variables, total time to perform the AI procedure was affected by AI technique (− 2.66, 95% CI − 5.98 to 0.66; p = 0.005), and there was a significant effect of semen dose and technique × dose interaction (p < 0.01) (Fig. 7A). PCAI significantly reduced infusion time compared with CAI (estimated mean difference = − 266 s; 95% CI − 268.5 to − 263.6; p < 0.01), and also revealed significant effects of dose and interaction (Fig. 7B). The incidence of semen backflow was significantly lower with PCAI than CAI (OR = 0.32, 95% CI 0.20–0.53; p < 0.01).

Successful catheter passage performance was significantly (OR = 0.15, 95% CI 0.04–0.61; p < 0.01) higher in sows inseminated by CAI as compared to PCAI (Table 4). Correspondingly, unsuccessful catheter passage occurred only in PCAI groups, with (OR = 6.61, 95% CI 1.63–26.75; p < 0.01) for technique. Difficult catheter passage (OR = 54.29, 95% CI 20.16–146.24; p < 0.01) and the passage of the catheter in the first attempt (OR = 0.06, 95% CI 0.03–0.10; p < 0.01) were significantly higher in PCAI than in CAI. PCAI required approximately 26% more catheter insertion attempts than CAI (IRR = 1.26; 95% CI 1.18–1.35; p < 0.01). Bleeding occurred only in PCAI-treated animals, resulting in a higher relative risk than in CAI-treated animals (OR = 17.05, 95% CI 2.38–121.86; p > 0.05), although the absolute incidence was low.

Discussion

Artificial insemination (AI) is a key reproductive technology in modern pig production, enabling genetic progress, biosecurity, and the efficient use of boar semen. Over the past two decades, post-cervical artificial insemination has gained widespread adoption because of its ability to reduce sperm doses while maintaining or improving fertility outcomes. The distinctive aspects of the present work include the large sample size, inclusion of both gilts and multiparous sows, and evaluation across multiple semen-dose levels under subtropical production conditions. The results demonstrate that PCAI allows a substantial reduction in semen dose while improving reproductive variables, increasing insemination efficiency, and reducing semen backflow in gilts and sows.

In gilts, PCAI improved the reproductive performance even at lower semen doses. The non-return rate, farrowing rate, total piglets born, piglets born alive, and litter size at weaning were significantly larger in the PCAI groups, while CAI exhibited dose-dependent decreases in these traits. These findings are significant, given that there are very few studies on the use of PCAI in gilts and no study with a 2 × 3 factorial design. Mellagi et al. 7 reported that PCAI is a widely used technique in sows but has limitations in gilts. The findings of the present study clearly reinforce the concept that deeper semen deposition facilitates the use of lower semen doses with improved reproductive outcomes in gilts, which is consistent with recent reports demonstrating that PCAI can maintain or improve fertility compared with CAI even at reduced sperm doses7,12,25,26. Previous studies have reported farrowing rates of 8–90% in gilts using PCAI with doses of 1.5–2.5 billion sperm21,27,28. Sbardella et al.19 reported that in primiparous sows, there was no difference between CAI (3 billion sperm) and PCAI (1.5 billion sperm) in terms of the farrowing rate and litter size, and suggested the use of PCAI in primiparous sows with low sperm doses. Suarez-Usbeck et al.17 reported similar litter sizes in gilts between CAI and PCAI using 3 billion and 1.5 billion sperm, respectively, and recommended PCAI for gilts. In comparing PCAI with or without fixed-time AI in gilts, Suárez-Usbeck et al.25 reported a farrowing rate of 89–91% with 1.5 billion sperm. Hernández-Caravaca et al.21 reported that vetrabutin chlorhydrate, warm extenders, or specific catheters can be useful tools for improving the success rate of the PCAI (1.5 billion sperm) technique in gilts without affecting reproductive variables, compared with CAI (3 billion sperm). Sonderman29 and Ternus et al.27 reported no differences in pregnancy and farrowing rates between the CAI and PCAI groups and recommended the PCAI. In contrast to our finding, Will et al.28 reported that reproductive performance was not affected by AI techniques or sperm dose, suggesting that CAI with 1.5 billion sperm cells/50 mL could be used in gilts due to difficulty with catheter passage. These variations may be because of differences in insemination devices (catheters and cannulas), the number of inseminations, the timing of insemination, training of the inseminator, farm management, breed, age, body size characteristics, and the anatomical characteristics of the cervix of gilts7,12,28. Parity, which is also influenced by ageing, determines important changes in the size, structure, and tissue content of the cervix wall, as well as in the morphology of the cervical canal, which may be responsible for the different levels of performance of PCAI in the gilts and sows12. In the present study, stillbirths, mummified fetuses, and pre-weaning mortality were unaffected by AI technique or semen dose and remained uniformly low.

In the present experiment, PCAI significantly improved reproductive variables in sows at a lower semen dose. In PCAI groups with reduced semen doses, there were significant improvements in the non-return rate, farrowing rate, total number of piglets born, number of piglets born alive, and litter size at weaning. When comparing total born piglets, piglets born alive, and litter size at weaning between PCAI and CAI groups with the lowest semen dose, there was a difference of approximately five piglets. The findings of the present study reveal that even at a substantially reduced dose (1 × 109 sperm/25 mL), PCAI maintained or improved reproductive performance, whereas CAI clearly became suboptimal. Our findings are in agreement with those of previous studies documenting the benefits of PCAI in sows7,11,12,25,26. Singh et al.11 reported that PCAI with 1 billion sperm resulted in a farrowing rate similar to that of cervical insemination with 3 billion sperm. As PCAI reduces sperm transit through the cervix, the number of sperm and dose volume can be reduced without impairing reproductive performance14. Previous studies have documented the use of semen doses containing 1–2 billion sperm cells, extended to a total volume of 40–50 ml, for PCAI3,9, with improved reproductive outcomes.

In weaned sows, most studies evaluating reduced sperm numbers for PCAI did not include parallel CAI treatments with equivalent dose sizes20,28, making it difficult to determine whether any observed effects on reproductive outcomes were due to the technique itself or to the sperm dose. In agreement with our study, Watson and Behan14 reported reduced reproductive performance when 1 billion sperm cells were used with CAI in a 2 × 3 factorial experiment assessing IUAI and CAI with three sperm cell numbers (1, 2, and 3 × 109 in 80 mL). Conversely, Pelland et al.30 also applied a factorial design (IUAI vs. CAI; 1 vs. 3 × 109 in 80 mL), and reported no impairment in reproductive performance with 1 billion sperm via CAI when inseminations were performed within the optimal window (0–24 h before ovulation). Hernández-Caravaca et al.20 reported better reproductive variables in sows inseminated with 1 or 1.5 billion sperm by PCAI than with 3 billion sperm by CAI; however, PCAI with 1.5 billion sperm outperformed PCAI with 1 billion sperm in terms of live-born litters. As highlighted by Rozeboom et al.31, optimal reproductive results depend on both sperm number and appropriate timing of insemination, particularly when low sperm doses are used.

The better reproductive performance observed with PCAI in the present study likely reflects underlying physiological differences in sperm transport and survival rather than procedural factors alone. During CAI, the complex cervical anatomy acts as both a selective barrier and a site of substantial sperm loss due to reflux32 and entrapment within cervical crypts, particularly when insemination volume and sperm dose are reduced. PCAI circumvents this barrier by depositing semen directly into the uterine lumen, thereby increasing the proportion of spermatozoa that reach the uterotubal junction, where a limited population forms the functional sperm reservoir responsible for fertilization12. It has been suggested that the inner cannula stimulates the cervix, facilitating the early release of prostaglandin metabolites, increasing uterine myometrial activity, and facilitating faster, more efficient transport, while reducing backflow33. In addition, uterine deposition may influence post-insemination uterine immune responses, including leukocyte recruitment and phagocytosis, which are known to eliminate large numbers of spermatozoa. Reduced semen volume combined with intra-uterine placement may mitigate these losses and support prolonged sperm survival34. Therefore, PCAI may enhance fertility through a combination of reduced sperm loss, improved transport kinetics, and more effective establishment of the sperm reservoir, rather than simply through deeper placement of semen.

Moreover, it is important to stress that high-quality semen doses (in terms of production, storage, and transport) and rigorous insemination procedures were ensured under our experimental conditions. From a boar-stud and herd-management perspective, this has important implications for increasing the number of sows inseminated per ejaculate, reducing the number of boars, increasing the use of high-indexed boars and reducing semen-production costs7,26. This is more significant in tropical and sub-tropical ecology, where the volume of boar semen is inherently lower than that in temperate climates.

The main constraint to PCAI application in gilts is the difficulty of cannula insertion through the cervix due to anatomical features compared with pluriparous sows12,26. However, regarding PCAI catheter insertion and related adverse events, our study observed a similar trend in gilts and sows compared with CAI. In the present study, successful passage of the PCAI catheter was achieved in 70–77% of gilts across different groups, and this was statistically lower than in the CAI groups. In sows, the PCAI success rate was 95–97%, but it was significantly lower than CAI. Similarly, more attempts were needed to pass the PCAI catheter in gilts and sows (approximately 50% and 30%, respectively, needed more than one attempt). Under field conditions, Ulguim et al.35 reported that the cannula advanced only 10 cm beyond the cervix in 44% of gilts on the first attempt. Another study found that full catheter insertion was achieved in only ~ 20% of cases with a multiparous PCAI device; however, the success rate increased to 60.3% on the first attempt when using a device specifically developed for gilts21. More recently, a 91% success rate of cannula insertion into the uterus has been reported in gilts27, although the authors suggest that this performance may be influenced by genetic line characteristics and the anatomical constraints imposed by cervical size and structure. Will et al.28 reported more attempts and greater total time to inseminate gilts with PCAI than with CAI. However, in the present study, the difficult passage of the catheter and more attempts to pass the PCAI did not affect the reproductive variables and even PCAI in gilts and sows still produced high non-return and farrowing rates and generated larger litters than CAI at reduced semen doses, comparable to or better than previously published PCAI protocols using fixed-time insemination or hormonal control32. The total time to perform the AI procedure was similar between the two techniques in gilts, but it was shorter in sows inseminated with PCAI. Also, the time to infuse the semen dose was much shorter in PCAI groups, and this was affected by the semen dose and its interaction. PCAI substantially reduced the time required to infuse the semen dose in both gilts and sows, reflecting the more direct deposition site. Total procedure time was consistently shorter with PCAI than with CAI, consistent with earlier technical assessments that highlighted PCAI as a time-saving and labour-efficient technique28. Already, artificial insemination in pigs is more economical than natural mating for smallholder pig farms24. The development of PCAI technology will further advance breeding programmes in developing countries.

The gilts and sows exhibiting semen backflow were more in the CAI groups as compared to the PCAI groups, and the bleeding post-AI was low only. Although semen backflow volume and sperm concentration therein were not recorded in the present study, the proportion of gilts and sows exhibiting semen backflow was higher in CAI groups, indicating wasting of sperm and subsequent decreased reproductive outcome. A higher proportion of gilts and sows exhibiting semen backflow with the highest semen dose in the CAI groups may be because of the higher semen dose volume used in this group. Backflow represents a direct loss of inseminated sperm and has been recognized as a key factor influencing fertilization success in CAI32. By depositing semen beyond the cervical canal, PCAI minimizes sperm loss, which likely contributes to its ability to maintain fertility at lower sperm doses7,12. However, Will et al.28 did not record any difference in semen backflow between CAI and PCAI groups in gilts. The difference may be due to the authors’ use of fence-line boars during CAI, whereas fence-line boars were not used during CAI in the present study.

This study has limitations inherent to large-scale field trials. Each insemination dose consisted of pooled semen from multiple boars, and pooling procedures were applied uniformly across all treatment groups to dilute individual boar effects and reflect standard industry practice. Nevertheless, residual batch-to-batch variation cannot be completely excluded in a field setting. In addition, semen dose and insemination volume were reduced simultaneously, precluding full separation of their independent contributions to reproductive outcomes. Previous studies have suggested that insemination volume within typical PCAI ranges may not markedly influence fertility13,19. Moreover, volume effects may differ between cervical and post-cervical insemination due to differences in semen deposition site, uterine distension, and reflux. Consequently, the results should be interpreted as reflecting practical dose–volume combinations rather than isolated sperm-dose effects. Unsuccessful attempts at post-cervical catheter insertion were converted to cervical AI (CAI), and these animals were excluded from the PCAI outcome analysis. Consequently, the reported PCAI results represent a per-protocol analysis of successful procedures rather than an intention-to-treat population.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that post-cervical artificial insemination (PCAI) with 1 × 109 sperm/25 mL dose is an effective and efficient reproductive technique for both gilts and sows under subtropical production conditions when successful catheterization is achieved. Under these conditions, PCAI consistently maintained or enhanced reproductive performance while enabling a substantial reduction in semen dose. In both parity groups, PCAI improved non-return and farrowing rates, increased total and liveborn litter sizes, and reduced semen backflow compared with CAI, even at lower semen doses. Although catheter insertion was more challenging in PCAI—particularly in gilts—these difficulties did not impair reproductive outcomes in successfully catheterized animals. PCAI also reduced insemination time and dose infusion time, supporting its advantage as a labour-efficient technique. Overall, the results reinforce that deeper semen deposition may enhance sperm use efficiency and allow reductions in sperm dose without compromising fertility under controlled conditions. These findings have important implications for AI programs, boar-stud productivity, and cost-effective semen management, particularly in tropical and subtropical environments where ejaculate volume may be inherently limited. PCAI with 1 × 109 sperm/25 mL dose may represent a practical and advantageous alternative to CAI for modern pig breeding systems.
