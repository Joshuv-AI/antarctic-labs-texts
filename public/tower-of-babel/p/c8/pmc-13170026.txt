PER2 knockdown drives immunotherapy resistance in gastric cancer via metabolic reprogramming of myeloid-derived suppressor cells
Authors: Shenggang Zhan, Shixi Lu, Feng Xiong, Haitao Li, Wenbiao Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCircadian rhythm disruption is increasingly linked to tumor progression and therapy resistance, but its role in immune evasion remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that knockdown of the core circadian gene PER2 (PER2-KD) drives resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in gastric cancer (GC) by promoting monocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cell (M-MDSC) accumulation and metabolic reprogramming. RNA-seq and scRNA-seq revealed that PER2-KD upregulates immunosuppressive genes (e.g., PD-L1, IL-10) while suppressing T cell activation signals. Mechanistically, PER2-KD GC cells secrete elevated VEGF and IL-6, inducing STAT3 phosphorylation in M-MDSCs and enhancing their immunosuppressive function. Untargeted metabolomics identified glycolysis and fatty acid metabolism as key pathways dysregulated in PER2-KD M-MDSCs, with lactate accumulation further amplifying CD8 + T cell dysfunction. In vivo, PER2-KD tumors exhibited accelerated growth, reduced PD-1 blockade efficacy, and shorter survival, accompanied by increased M-MDSC infiltration and lactate levels. Neutralizing VEGF/IL-6 or inhibiting lactate metabolism restored CD8+ T cell activity, suggesting actionable therapeutic targets. Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a malignant tumor of the digestive system with high global incidence and mortality rates [1]. Global Cancer Statistics 2020 reported that GC ranks fifth in new cases and fourth in cancer-related deaths globally [2]. Although multidisciplinary treatments, such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and targeted therapy, have progressed in recent years, the survival outcomes for patients with advanced or metastatic GC remain unsatisfactory [3]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), as a novel antitumor therapeutic strategy, have achieved remarkable breakthroughs in several malignancies, such as melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer [4]. However, their clinical efficacy in GC has been inconsistent. Despite exhibiting “sensitive” biomarkers such as high PD-L1 expression, some patients have shown no significant response to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy [5]. Current studies widely suggest that immunosuppressive components and their metabolic characteristics within the tumor microenvironment (TME) are critical factors limiting the therapeutic efficacy of ICIs [6, 7]. Identifying and targeting these key immunosuppressive mechanisms represents a significant challenge in enhancing immunotherapy efficacy and prolonging survival in patients with GC.

In tumor-associated immune evasion mechanisms, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) represent a core immunosuppressive cell population [8]. MDSCs are enriched in various TMEs and impair the host antitumor immune response by inhibiting CD8⁺ T cell proliferation and activity, fostering regulatory T-cell (Treg) differentiation, and facilitating immune tolerance [9–11]. Clinical sample studies have shown that MDSC levels are significantly elevated in both the peripheral blood and tumor tissues of patients with GC [12] and are closely associated with disease stage, lymph node metastasis, and poor prognosis [9, 11]. MDSCs are generally categorized into two subtypes: monocytic MDSCs (M-MDSCs) and polymorphonuclear MDSCs (PMN-MDSCs), with M-MDSCs playing a particularly critical role in promoting immunosuppression [8, 9]. Although earlier research has provided initial insights into the association between MDSCs and immune evasion in GC, the mechanisms underlying their accumulation and the regulatory networks governing their function remain incompletely understood [13]. In addition, MDSCs frequently interact with several key signaling cascades, including STAT3, NF-κB, and mTOR, suggesting they may be influenced by complex upstream regulatory factors [14]. Therefore, elucidating how tumor cells induce MDSC accumulation and activation through the secretion of metabolic products or signaling molecules holds significant research value.

The circadian rhythm system is a core regulatory mechanism for maintaining homeostasis and metabolic rhythms in the body, composed of a series of clock genes, including the PER family members PER1, PER2, and PER3 [15, 16]. In recent years, increasing evidence has suggested that circadian rhythm disruption is not only closely associated with metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular diseases but also plays a critical role in the development, progression, and therapeutic response of various tumors [17]. Notably, the PER2 gene, a key negative regulatory factor in the circadian rhythm system [18], is downregulated in several solid tumors, such as breast cancer [19, 20], lung cancer [21, 22], and colorectal cancer [23]. Dysregulation of PER2 has been implicated in controlling tumor cell proliferation, apoptosis, invasion, and metabolic reprogramming [23, 24]. Circadian rhythm disruption may also influence the tumor immune microenvironment by modulating immune-metabolic networks [25]. For example, studies have shown that PER2 can regulate metabolic processes such as glycolysis and fatty acid oxidation [26]. Given that the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs is highly dependent on metabolic states, whereby the accumulation of metabolic products such as lactate and fatty acids enhances their function [27], we hypothesize that PER2 knockdown (PER2-KD) may indirectly promote MDSC accumulation and activation by modulating metabolic pathways [28]. However, direct evidence supporting a link between PER2 and MDSC-mediated immune regulation in GC is currently lacking, and the underlying mechanisms remain largely unexplored.

Given the above background, this study focused on the circadian gene PER2-KD to systematically investigate its regulatory role in the immune microenvironment of GC, particularly its potential to impair the efficacy of PD-1 immune checkpoint therapy by promoting the accumulation of M-MDSCs and enhancing their immunosuppressive function. The objective was to determine whether PER2-KD induces the immunosuppressive phenotype of M-MDSCs through metabolic reprogramming and activation of the STAT3 signaling pathway, ultimately affecting CD8⁺ T cell function and antitumor immune responses. The scientific significance of this study lies in revealing a novel molecular link between circadian rhythm gene dysregulation in tumors and resistance to immunotherapy, thereby advancing our understanding of immune-metabolic regulatory mechanisms. The findings may provide a basis for more refined immunotherapeutic stratification of GC patients and identify potential targets for combination therapy, such as metabolic modulators in conjunction with ICIs, offering a theoretical and practical framework for precision immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and Gene Ontology/Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (GO/KEGG) functional enrichment analysis

Total RNA was extracted from tumor tissues of three wild-type (WT) PER2 (PER2-wt) and three PER2-KD tumor-bearing mice using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), followed by purification with RNeasy spin columns (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). RNA-seq libraries were constructed from 100 ng of total RNA using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The pooled libraries were subsequently sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform. The HISAT-Cufflinks pipeline was used to splice and align the sequencing reads (FASTQ format) to the reference genome (GRCm38/mm10).

Differential gene expression was analyzed using the edgeR package (v4.0.16) with thresholds of |log₂FC| > 1 and p < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO), covering molecular function (MF), cellular component (CC), and biological process (BP), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed with clusterProfiler (v4.10.1). Results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis

Cells were isolated from tumors of three PER2-wt and three PER2-KD tumor-bearing mice, digested, pooled, and stained with anti-CD45.2 antibody and DAPI. Viable CD45⁺ cells were sorted by flow cytometry, washed twice with PBS containing 0.04% BSA, and combined with the 10x Genomics Chromium single-cell RNA master mix. The suspension was loaded onto a 10x Chromium chip to generate single-cell cDNA, followed by library construction and sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). TRaw reads were demultiplexed and converted to FASTQ format using bcl2fastq, and processed with Cell Ranger (v6.1, 10x Genomics, USA) using default parameters to align reads to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38/mm10). Duplicate and non-uniquely mapped reads were excluded.

Downstream analyses were performed with the Seurat R package (v5.1.0). Cells expressing fewer than 200 genes or with mitochondrial transcripts > 10% were filtered out. Data were normalized using LogNormalize, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were selected for principal component analysis (PCA). The JackStrawPlot and ElbowPlot functions determined significant principal components, and the top 30 PCs were used for Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) clustering analysis. Differential expression between sample groups was assessed using the FindMarkers function. Genes with FDR < 0.05 and |log₂FC| > 0.5 were defined as cluster-specific marker genes. Clusters were manually annotated based on canonical markers: macrophages (Cd68, C1qb, Adgre1), CD8 T cells (Cd8a, Cd8b1), monocytes (Fn1, Csf1r), natural killer (NK) cells (Klrb1c, Ncr1, Gzma), dendritic cells (DCs) (Fscn1, Mgl2), CD4 T cells (Cd4), MDSCs (S100a8, S100a9, Slfn4), and B cells (Cd79a, Cd79b, Ebf1).

Cell line selection and culture

Human GC cell lines MKN-45 (BFN60700329, Qingqi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) and AGS (CRL-1739, ATCC, USA) were cultured in complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; D5030, Sigma, USA) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; F8687, Sigma, USA), 100 µg/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (P7539, Sigma, USA). All cells were maintained in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 95% air. Before experiments, cells were pre-cultured for 24 h. Passaging was performed every 2–3 days, and cells were used for subsequent experiments when they reached 70%-80% confluency [29].

CRISPR target design and human PER2 gene editing

PER2-KD cell lines derived from the human GC cell lines MKN-45 and AGS were custom-generated and provided by Cyagen Biosciences (Guangzhou, China). Using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing technology, small insertion/deletion (indel) mutations were introduced into exon 2 of the PER2 gene to achieve functional gene disruption (resulting in PER2 knockdown). After editing, monoclonal screening was performed, and PER2-deficient stable clones were selected. The PER2-KD and control cell lines were used for subsequent functional validation experiments.

RT-qPCR

Following the completion of PER2 gene editing in MKN-45 and AGS cells via the CRISPR/Cas9 system, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, USA). RNA concentration and purity were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (1011U, NanoDrop, USA). RNA samples meeting the following quality criteria were used for subsequent experiments: RNA integrity number ≥ 7.0 and 28 S:18 S ratio ≥ 1.5. Primers for PER2 were designed as follows: Forward, 5′-GTGTTCCTTCAGCTCCTGCT-3′; Reverse, 5′-GGCATATGCTCAGCTCTGGT-3′. TaKaRa synthesized all primers, and GAPDH was used as the internal control gene. The relative expression levels of each gene were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. All RT-qPCR assays were performed in triplicate.

Western blot (WB)

M-MDSCs were lysed using RIPA buffer (P0039, Beyotime, China), and tumor tissues were lysed with RIPA buffer (89901, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for protein extraction. Protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0010, Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (ISEQ00010, Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (P0216, Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3) (9145 S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, 1:2000), anti-HK2 (2867T, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, 1:1000), anti-PFKFB3 (ab181861, Abcam, USA, 1:1000), anti-LDHA (2012 S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, 1:1000), and anti-β-tubulin (2128 S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA, 1:1000). The next day, membranes were incubated for 1 h with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (ab6721, Abcam, USA, 1:5000). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (32209, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

M-MDSC induction

PER2-KD and PER2-wt GC cells were cultured until 80% confluency, then switched to serum-free RPMI-1640 medium for 48 h. After incubation, the conditioned medium (CM) was collected, filtered through a 0.22 μm filter (Millipore, USA) to remove cellular debris, and immediately stored at -80 °C.

The collected CM was added to cultures of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy donors (Delf-12133, Wanwu Biotechnology, China) at a 50% volume ratio. PBMCs were isolated from fresh peripheral blood using Ficoll-Paque Plus (GE Healthcare, USA). The induction culture was carried out in RPMI-1640 medium enriched with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 72 h [30].

Flow cytometric identification of M-MDSC phenotype

The phenotype of M-MDSCs was analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSCanto II, BD Biosciences, USA). Induced M-MDSCs were stained with antibodies against CD11b-PE (12-0118-42, eBioscience, USA), CD33-APC (47-0338-42, eBioscience, USA), and HLA-DR-FITC (11-9956-42, eBioscience, USA), using 200,000 cells per staining reaction. Cells were incubated with antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark, then washed and analyzed by flow cytometry. Data were processed using FlowJo software (v10.7, BD Biosciences, USA).

Glucose uptake assay using 2-NBDG fluorescent labeling

To evaluate the glucose uptake capacity of M-MDSCs, the fluorescent probe 2-NBDG (HY-116215, MedChemExpress, China) was used. M-MDSCs were incubated in RPMI-1640 medium (C11875500BT, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (A5256701, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15140122, Gibco, USA) for 12 h. The medium was then replaced with serum-free RPMI-1640 containing 100 µM 2-NBDG, and cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 30 min. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS. 2-NBDG fluorescence imaging was performed using a 488 nm laser and visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Lactate concentration assay

Lactate secretion by M-MDSCs was measured using a lactate assay kit (A019-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). M-MDSCs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well and incubated for 24 h. The culture supernatant was then collected, and lactate concentration was determined colorimetrically. Absorbance was recorded at 530 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Seahorse metabolic analysis

The glycolytic flux of M-MDSCs was measured using the Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer (Agilent, USA). Cells were seeded into a Seahorse 96-well plate at a density of 5 × 10⁵ cells per well and incubated for 1 h in phenol red-free DMEM (103335-100, Agilent, USA). The extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and glycolytic proton efflux rate (glycoPER) were assessed using the Seahorse XFe96 Glycolysis Stress Test Kit (103020-100, Agilent, USA) and the Seahorse XF Glycolytic Rate Assay Kit (103344-100, Agilent, USA), respectively. Data were analyzed using the Seahorse XFe96 extracellular flux analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience).

For ECAR measurements, glucose (10 mM; HY-B0389, MedChemExpress, USA), oligomycin (1 µM; HY-N6782, MedChemExpress, USA), and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG, 50 mM; HY-13966, MedChemExpress, USA) were sequentially injected. For glycoPER analysis, rotenone and antimycin A (Rot/AA), followed by 2-DG, were added sequentially to measure the metabolic flux.

Cell co-culture assay

CD8⁺ T cells were co-cultured with M-MDSCs at a 1:1 ratio. Experimental groups included the Con group, the lactate-treated group (10 mM; HY-B2227, MedChemExpress, USA), the fatty acid-treated group (20 µM; HY-L182, MedChemExpress, USA), the lactate + LDH inhibitor group (Lactate + GSK2837808A, 1 µM; HY-100681, MedChemExpress, USA), and the lactate + MCT inhibitor group (Lactate + Syrosingopine, 10 µM; HY-N4115, MedChemExpress, USA). After 48 h of co-culture, CD8⁺ T cells and the culture supernatants were collected to analyze the proliferation rate and IFN-γ secretion by flow cytometry.

Blocking assay

Neutralizing antibodies against interleukin-6 (IL-6, 501103, BioLegend, USA) and Bevacizumab (anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), HY-P9906, MedChemExpress, USA) were added to the co-culture system for blocking experiments. The final concentration of each blocking agent was set at 10 µg/mL. After 72 h of treatment, cells were collected to assess M-MDSC phenotype and their suppressive effect on CD8⁺ T cells.

Flow cytometric analysis of CD8⁺ T cell proliferation

CD8⁺ T cells were labeled using a CFSE Cell Proliferation Kit (C34570, Invitrogen, USA). Cells were seeded at 5 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6-well plates and co-cultured with M-MDSCs from different treatment groups for 48 h. After incubation, cells were collected and stained with APC-conjugated anti-CD3 antibody (300312, BioLegend, USA). Following PBS washes, cell proliferation was analyzed by flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa, BD Biosciences, USA), and data were processed using FlowJo software.

Cytotoxic function assay

To assess CD8⁺ T cell cytotoxic function, the expression levels of Granzyme B, Perforin, and CD107a were analyzed by flow cytometry. Based on the co-culture conditions described above, CD8⁺ T cells were co-cultured with M-MDSCs in 12-well plates for 48 h. After incubation, cells were harvested, resuspended in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, and stimulated with BD Pharmingen™ Leukocyte Activation Cocktail and BD GolgiPlug (550583, BD Biosciences, USA) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 4 h.

Subsequently, cells were incubated with Human FcR Blocking Reagent (420510, BioLegend, USA) at 4 °C for 15 min, followed by surface staining using an anti-CD3 antibody (561806, BD Biosciences, USA, 1:100). After 30 min of incubation at 4 °C, cells were washed once with PBS containing 0.2% BSA (A1933, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and then fixed and permeabilized using intracellular fixation and permeabilization buffer (88-8824-00, Invitrogen, USA).

Intracellular staining was performed using monoclonal antibodies against Granzyme B (561999, BD Biosciences, USA, 1:100), Perforin (570592, BD Biosciences, USA, 1:200), and CD107a (48-1079-42, Invitrogen, USA, 1:200). After staining, cells were washed with PBS containing 0.2% BSA, resuspended and analyzed for fluorescence signal using a flow cytometer (BD LSRFortessa, BD Biosciences, USA).

CD8⁺ T cell-mediated tumor killing assay

CD8⁺ T cells were co-cultured with human GC cells (MKN-45, CL-0292, Procell, China; AGS, CRL-1739, ATCC, USA) at a 5:1 ratio for 24 h. Tumor cell apoptosis was assessed using an Annexin V/PI double staining kit (C1062L, Beyotime, China). After co-culture, cells were collected and stained for Annexin V and PI fluorescence, which was analyzed by flow cytometry. The tumor cell killing capacity mediated by CD8⁺ T cells was quantified using FlowJo software.

Metabolomics

M-MDSCs were first washed once with pre-chilled saline solution to prepare samples for metabolite analysis. Then, 1 mL of 80% methanol was added to each sample, followed by vortexing and rapid freezing in liquid nitrogen. After three freeze-thaw cycles, samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were evaporated to dryness. Dried metabolites were reconstituted in 50 µL of 0.03% formic acid, vortexed, and centrifuged to remove debris. The supernatant was transferred to HPLC vials for analysis using a QTRAP 6500 mass spectrometer (AB-SCIEX, MA, USA). MultiQuant software (Applied Biosystems SCIEX) extracted peak areas and retention times.

Data processing was conducted using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 with the following parameters: Data Type: Peak intensities; Format: Samples in columns (unpaired); Variance filter: Relative standard deviation (RSD = SD/mean); Sample normalization: Normalization by sum; Data transformation: Log transformation (base 10); Data scaling: Auto scaling.

To evaluate metabolic differences among groups and assess model interpretability, we first performed PCA for global pattern recognition and outlier detection. Subsequently, Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) were applied to construct supervised learning models for discriminating between different groups.

PLS-DA is a supervised multivariate statistical method that reveals intergroup differences and reduces dimensionality by maximizing the covariance between predictor variables (metabolite signals) and categorical response variables (group labels). Model robustness was assessed by R² (explained variance) and Q² (predictive accuracy). OPLS-DA, an improved version of PLS-DA, separates group-unrelated orthogonal variation from group-related predictive components, enhancing interpretability and reliability in identifying key variables. The closer the R²Y and Q² values are to 1.0, the stronger the model’s fit and predictive power.

Differential metabolites were identified using Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) values with thresholds of VIP ≥ 1.0, |log₂FoldChange| ≥ 2, and P < 0.05. Significantly altered metabolites were then analyzed for pathway enrichment via MetaboAnalyst 6.0 using the Small Molecule Pathway Database (SMPDB) to reveal associated metabolic pathway changes.

Establishment of murine xenograft tumor model

Male huPBMC-C-NKG mice (4–6 weeks old; C001329, Cyagen, China) were housed under standard conditions with a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 50 ± 10%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. After one week of acclimatization, PER2-KD GC MKN-45 cells and WT GC MKN-45 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right axilla of each mouse at a concentration of 4 × 10⁶ cells in 100 µL sterile PBS (P4474, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Following model establishment, mouse health status was monitored daily [31].

Experimental grouping and treatment

Mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group): (1) Con group (injection of WT GC cells + oral gavage with saline); (2) PER2-KD group (injection of PER2-KD GC cells + oral gavage with saline); (3) Immunotherapy group (injection of WT GC cells + intraperitoneal injection of PD-1 inhibitor [HY-P9902, MCE, USA] at 10 mg/kg every three days for three doses); (4) PER2-KD + immunotherapy group (injection of PER2-KD GC cells + PD-1 inhibitor treatment under the same dosing regimen). Treatments continued until tumors reached the experimental endpoint volume or mice met humane endpoint criteria [32].

Tumor growth monitoring

Tumor volume was measured every three days using a digital caliper (Mitutoyo, Japan). The longest and shortest diameters were recorded, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: Volume = 0.5 × length × (width)². Tumor growth was recorded for each group, and growth curves were plotted to compare tumor progression across groups.

Survival analysis

Survival time was monitored and recorded for all mice in each group. All procedures adhered to humane endpoint criteria. Mice exhibiting signs of severe pain, a body weight loss exceeding 20%, or tumor volume greater than 2000 mm³ were euthanized. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was performed to compare survival curves among groups.

Flow cytometric evaluation of tumor immune microenvironment

Tumor tissues from xenografted mice were harvested, minced, and digested with collagenase IV (C4-BIOC, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 30 min to obtain single-cell suspensions. After filtration and centrifugation, the proportions of M-MDSCs and CD8⁺ T cells were analyzed by flow cytometry. For M-MDSC staining, cells were incubated with CD11b-PE (MABF365, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and Gr-1-APC (53170 S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). For CD8⁺ T cell staining, cells were labeled with CD3-PE (28306 S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) and CD8a-FITC (55397 S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). After staining, cells were washed with PBS and analyzed using a flow cytometer. Data were processed using FlowJo software (v10.7, BD Biosciences, USA).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4-µm-thick slices. For IHC staining, tumor sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD8 (ab217344, Abcam, USA), CD11b (ab209970, Abcam, USA), Gr-1 (ab238132, Abcam, USA), PD-L1 (64988 S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), ARG1 (ab315110, Abcam, USA), and IDO (ab311847, Abcam, USA). After washing three times with cold PBS, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 30 min. Images were captured using a Leica M165 FC microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Student’s t-test was used for two-group comparisons. One-way ANOVA was applied to compare multiple groups, and two-way ANOVA was used for comparisons across multiple time points among groups. Bonferroni post hoc testing was performed where applicable. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

PER2-KD is associated with an immunosuppressive phenotype in GC

To verify PER2 protein expression levels in gastric cancer cell lines MKN-45 and AGS, we performed WB and RT-qPCR analyses, confirming successful establishment of PER2-KD cell lines (PER2-KD) (Fig. 1A–B). Subsequently, a murine tumor xenograft model was constructed (Figure S1A), and tumor volumes were measured (Figure S1B). The results showed that tumors derived from PER2-KD cells were significantly larger than those in the PER2-wt group. To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying PER2 knockdown in GC, RNA-seq analysis was conducted on tumor tissues. Compared with PER2-wt tumors, a total of 1,164 genes exhibited significant differential expression in the PER2-KD group (Fig. 1C-D). Notably, the PER2-KD group exhibited increased expression of several immunosuppression-related genes, including Cd274 (PD-L1), Il1b, Il10, and Arg1, while genes associated with T cell activation, such as Cd28, Icos, and Ifng, were downregulated (Fig. 1E).

Further, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that these differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in BP, including immune and inflammatory responses, cell adhesion, lipid metabolism, glycolysis, and the JAK-STAT signaling pathway (Fig. 1F-G).

These findings suggest that PER2-KD is a key driver of the immunosuppressive phenotype in GC.

scRNA-seq analysis reveals that PER2 -KD promotes M-MDSC infiltration and suppresses CD8⁺ T cell accumulation in GC

To investigate the impact of PER2-KD on the transcriptional landscape of the immune microenvironment in GC, we performed scRNA-seq analysis on tumor-infiltrating immune cells from PER2-wt and PER2-KD tumor-bearing mice. During quality control, we applied stringent filtering criteria (nFeature_RNA > 200 and percent.mt < 10%) to remove low-quality cells, resulting in 27,033 high-quality single cells for downstream analysis (Figure S1C-D). Correlation analysis between sequencing depth metrics indicated a strong association between nCount_RNA and nFeature_RNA after filtering (Figure S1E), confirming the reliability of the dataset. The top 2,000 highly variable genes were identified (Figure S1F), and cell cycle scoring showed no significant interference in subsequent analyses (Figure S1G). Linear dimensionality reduction was conducted using PCA (Figure S1H), and the optimal number of PCs (PC = 30) was determined using JackStrawPlot and ElbowPlot functions to adequately capture the primary biological variation in the data (Figure S1I-J).

Subsequently, we applied UMAP for nonlinear dimensionality reduction and clustering, identifying 14 distinct cell clusters (Fig. 2A-B). Based on known lineage-specific marker gene expression profiles, these clusters were annotated into eight major immune cell types: CD8⁺ T cells, CD4⁺ T cells, B cells, monocytes, macrophages, DCs, MDSCs, and NK cells (Fig. 2C-D, Figure S2A). When comparing immune cell population distributions between WT and PER2-KD tumor-bearing mice, we found that CD8⁺ T cells and monocytes were predominantly derived from the WT group, whereas MDSCs were mainly enriched in the PER2-KD group (Fig. 2E–F). These findings suggest that PER2 knockdown may induce aberrant remodeling of myeloid cell proportions in the TME, thereby impairing CD8⁺ T cell–mediated antitumor immunity.

PER2-KD induces M-MDSC accumulation and suppresses CD8⁺ T cell function

In this study, human GC cell lines MKN-45 and AGS were genetically edited using CRISPR/Cas9 technology to generate PER2-KD and WT control cell lines (Fig. 3A). These cell lines were used for all subsequent M-MDSC–related experiments.

PER2-KD and WT cells were cultured to 80% confluency for CM collection and then incubated in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium for 48 h. The collected conditioned media were subsequently used in M-MDSC induction assays. Flow cytometry analysis showed that compared to WT MKN-45 CM, the CM from PER2-KD cells significantly promoted the induction of M-MDSCs, as indicated by an increased proportion of CD11b⁺ HLA-DR⁻ cells. These results suggest that PER2-KD GC cells enhance M-MDSC differentiation potential through the secretion of soluble factors, thereby potentially contributing to the formation of an immunosuppressive phenotype within the immune microenvironment (Fig. 3B-C).

Furthermore, the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs in the co-culture system was assessed using flow cytometry and ELISA. Under PER2-KD conditions (Fig. 4A), M-MDSCs significantly suppressed CD8⁺ T cell proliferation, as shown by a marked reduction in proliferation rate detected by CFSE staining (Fig. 4B).

In addition, ELISA results showed that the secretion level of IFN-γ by CD8⁺ T cells was significantly decreased in the PER2-KD co-culture condition (Fig. 4C). STAT3, a transcription factor, regulates the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs by modulating target genes such as CXCL1 and Arg1 [33, 34]. Therefore, we examined p-STAT3 expression under PER2-KD conditions. WB analysis further revealed a substantial increase in p-STAT3 levels in M-MDSCs under PER2-KD conditions, indicating activation of immunosuppressive signaling pathways (Fig. 4D).

To determine whether the immunosuppressive effects induced by PER2-KD were related to specific soluble factors, cytokine levels secreted by PER2-KD and WT GC cells were examined. The results showed that PER2-KD cells secreted significantly higher levels of VEGF and IL-6 compared to WT cells, suggesting that these cytokines may play critical roles in M-MDSC accumulation and functional regulation (Fig. 4E).

To further validate the role of these PER2-KD-associated secreted factors in M-MDSC induction and function, neutralizing antibodies against IL-6 and VEGF were added during the M-MDSC induction phase. Blocking these cytokines significantly reduced M-MDSC generation and their suppressive effect on CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 4F), indicating that both factors are key mediators in M-MDSC-driven immunosuppression.

These findings demonstrate that PER2-KD in GC cells significantly promotes M-MDSC accumulation and enhances their immunosuppressive activity against CD8⁺ T cells.

Metabolomics reveals that PER2-KD enhances the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs through metabolic reprogramming

To examine the influence of PER2-KD on the metabolism of M-MDSCs within the TME, we performed an untargeted metabolomic analysis of M-MDSCs isolated from PER2-wt and PER2-KD tumor-bearing mice.

PCA revealed distinct separation of metabolite profiles between the PER2-wt and PER2-KD groups (Fig. 5A), indicating that PER2-KD significantly altered the metabolic state of M-MDSCs. This difference was further confirmed by PLS-DA and OPLS-DA (Fig. 5B-C), and the reliability of the OPLS-DA model was validated through 100 permutation tests (Fig. 5D). Metabolites with |log₂FoldChange| ≥ 2, p < 0.05, and VIP ≥ 1 were considered differentially expressed. 74 metabolites were significantly altered in PER2-KD M-MDSCs compared to the PER2-wt group, with 42 upregulated and 32 downregulated (Fig. 5E-F; Table S1).

Further pathway enrichment analysis of differential metabolites revealed significant enrichment in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, pyruvate metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism pathways (Fig. 6A). Additionally, to visualize the interrelationships among metabolic pathways, we constructed a metabolic pathway interaction network based on metabolite co-enrichment relationships (Fig. 6B). Each node represents a metabolic pathway, where node color indicates enrichment significance, node size reflects the number of metabolites within the pathway, and edges represent potential associations based on shared metabolites or biological functions. The results showed that energy metabolism–related pathways (glycolysis, TCA cycle, and fatty acid β-oxidation) formed a tightly interconnected network, suggesting that PER2 knockdown–induced metabolic reprogramming primarily occurs in processes related to energy production and substrate conversion. In the PER2-KD group, the concentrations of several glycolysis-related metabolites (e.g., glucose 1-phosphate, glucose 6-phosphate, phosphoenolpyruvic acid, glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate, pyruvic acid, and lactic acid) and fatty acid metabolites (e.g., stearic acid, myristoleic acid, palmitoleic acid, acetyl-CoA, docosahexaenoic acid, and glycerophosphocholine) were significantly elevated (Fig. 6C-D).

These findings suggest that PER2-KD may enhance the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs in the TME by promoting metabolic reprogramming toward increased glycolysis and fatty acid metabolism.

PER2-KD promotes glycolysis and weakens CD8⁺ T cell antitumor activity

Based on metabolomic and KEGG enrichment results, lactate and fatty acids were selected as key metabolites to evaluate their specific contributions to the immunosuppressive capacity of M-MDSCs.

We first evaluated glucose uptake in M-MDSCs using the 2-NBDG fluorescence labeling assay. The results showed that the glucose uptake rate of M-MDSCs treated with CM from PER2-KD GC cells was significantly higher than that of M-MDSCs treated with CM from PER2-wt tumor cells (P < 0.01) (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, measurement of lactate secretion revealed that the lactate concentration in PER2-KD M-MDSCs was markedly increased compared with the WT group (Fig. 7B).

In addition, Seahorse metabolic analysis revealed that PER2-KD in tumor cells significantly enhanced glycolytic flux in M-MDSCs, as evidenced by increased glycolytic capacity (ECAR values) and glycolytic reserve (Fig. 7C and Table S2). WB analysis confirmed that key glycolytic enzymes, including HK2, PFKFB3, and LDHA, were significantly upregulated in the PER2-KD group compared with the WT group (Fig. 7D).

To clarify whether the PER2 knockdown–induced immunosuppressive effect of M-MDSCs in GC cells depends on lactate metabolism, we performed functional assays using a co-culture system of M-MDSCs and CD8⁺ T cells treated with CM from PER2-KD GC cells. The experimental groups included Con, Lactate (10 mM lactate added to the co-culture), Fatty Acid (20 µM fatty acid added), Lactate + LDH inhibitor (1 µM LDH inhibitor added to block lactate metabolism), and Lactate + MCT inhibitor (Lactate + MCT Inh). The proliferation of CD8⁺ T cells, IFN-γ secretion levels, and the expression of immunosuppressive markers on M-MDSCs were analyzed (Fig. 7E).

Flow cytometry using CFSE labeling showed that lactate treatment (10 mM) significantly enhanced the suppressive activity of M-MDSCs, as evidenced by a marked reduction in CD8⁺ T cell proliferation. In contrast, fatty acid treatment had no significant effect on M-MDSC immunosuppressive capacity (Fig. 7F).

Furthermore, ELISA results revealed that IFN-γ secretion by CD8⁺ T cells was significantly decreased in the lactate-treated group, while no change was observed in the fatty acid group, indicating lactate’s critical role in regulating M-MDSC immunosuppressive function (Fig. 7G). Notably, inhibition of lactate metabolism using an LDH inhibitor (1 µM) or MCT1/4 inhibitor (Lactate + MCT Inh) significantly reduced the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs, with both CD8⁺ T cell proliferation and IFN-γ secretion levels restored (Fig. 7F-G).

In addition, flow cytometry analysis showed that treatment with the LDH inhibitor and the MCT1/4 inhibitor significantly reduced the expression of immunosuppressive markers (CD11b, ARG1, and p-STAT3) in lactate-treated M-MDSCs (Fig. 7H).

To evaluate whether lactate metabolism in M-MDSCs compromised the antitumor function of CD8⁺ T cells, we further assessed the expression of cytotoxic markers in CD8⁺ T cells. Flow cytometry analysis showed that Granzyme B, Perforin, and CD107a expression levels were significantly reduced in the Lactate group (Fig. 7I), indicating that lactate suppressed the cytotoxic function of CD8⁺ T cells.

Moreover, in the CD8⁺ T cell-tumor cell co-culture system using human GC cell lines (MKN-45/AGS), Annexin V/PI dual-staining results demonstrated that the ability of CD8⁺ T cells to kill tumor cells was reduced in the Lactate-treated group. However, this cytotoxic activity was restored following treatment with either the LDH or MCT inhibitor (Fig. 7J).

These findings support the conclusion that PER2-KD enhances the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs through lactate metabolism, thereby impairing the antitumor activity of CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 7K).

Effect of PER2-KD on tumor growth, immunotherapy efficacy, and mouse survival

In this study, we investigated the effect of PER2-KD on the efficacy of immunotherapy in GC using a murine xenograft model (Fig. 8A). To establish the xenograft model, PER2-KD and WT GC cells were subcutaneously injected into C-NKG mice. The mice were then randomly divided into four groups: Con (injected with WT GC cells + saline), PER2-KD (PER2-KD, injected with PER2-KD GC cells + saline), Immunotherapy (Con + PD-1, injected with WT GC cells + PD-1 inhibitor), and PER2-KD + Immunotherapy (PER2-KD + PD-1, injected with PER2-KD GC cells + PD-1 inhibitor).

Note: (A) Schematic of the animal experiment workflow (Created in BioRender); (B) Tumor volume changes in transplanted tumors measured with calipers; (C) Tumor growth curves showing volume trends across treatment groups; (D) Kaplan-Meier survival analysis comparing survival rates among different groups. **p < 0.01, ****p < 0.0001; number of animals (N) = 6

After successfully establishing the xenograft model, we monitored tumor volume at regular intervals (Fig. 8B) and plotted tumor growth curves for each group (Fig. 8C). Tumor growth remained relatively stable in the Con and Immunotherapy groups. In contrast, the PER2-KD group exhibited significantly accelerated tumor growth, especially within the first 15 days post-inoculation. In the PER2-KD + PD-1 group, PD-1 inhibition delayed tumor progression, but its effect was notably weaker than that in the Immunotherapy group (Fig. 8B-C). These results suggest that PER2-KD may impair immune checkpoint therapy efficacy in GC by promoting M-MDSC accumulation.

We performed a Kaplan-Meier survival analysis to evaluate treatment efficacy among the groups further. The results showed a significantly prolonged median survival in the Immunotherapy group. However, mice in the PER2-KD + PD-1 group exhibited markedly reduced survival rates compared to the Immunotherapy group, with a median survival time similar to that of the Con group. These findings indicate that PER2-KD significantly diminished the therapeutic effect of PD-1 inhibition (Fig. 8D).

Effect of PER2-KD attenuates the efficacy of immunotherapy in GC via M-MDSC accumulation

Flow cytometry was performed on tumor tissues from each group to analyze the proportions of M-MDSCs (CD11b⁺ Gr-1⁺) and CD8⁺ T cells (CD3⁺ CD8⁺), thereby assessing immune cell infiltration under different experimental conditions. Compared to the Con group, the PER2-KD group exhibited a significant increase in M-MDSC infiltration and a marked decrease in CD8⁺ T cell infiltration. Although the PD-1 inhibitor modestly enhanced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in the PER2-KD + immunotherapy group, the level remained significantly lower than that observed in the immunotherapy group (Fig. 9A). These findings support the hypothesis that PER2-KD compromises immunotherapy efficacy by promoting M-MDSC accumulation.

IHC staining was conducted on tumor tissues from each group to examine M-MDSC markers (CD11b and Gr-1), CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and the expression of immunosuppressive factors (PD-L1, ARG1, and IDO). The results showed that CD11b and Gr-1 expression was markedly elevated in both the PER2-KD and the PER2-KD + immunotherapy groups, accompanied by reduced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration. Moreover, immunosuppressive factors PD-L1, ARG1, and IDO were significantly upregulated in the PER2-KD group, suggesting that PER2-KD may further suppress CD8⁺ T cell activity by enhancing the expression of these inhibitory molecules (Fig. 9B).

ELISA was used to measure the expression levels of immunosuppressive cytokines (IL-6, TGF-β, and VEGF) in tumor tissues from each group. IL-6 and VEGF levels were significantly elevated in the PER2-KD and the PER2-KD + immunotherapy groups. Notably, these cytokines were most abundant in the PER2-KD + immunotherapy group, further supporting the role of PER2-KD in enhancing the immunosuppressive TME (Fig. 9C).

WB analysis showed a marked increase in p-STAT3 expression in both the PER2-KD group and the PER2-KD + immunotherapy group, suggesting that activation of the STAT3 signaling pathway may contribute to the enhanced immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs (Fig. 9D). Metabolites were extracted from tumor tissues and analyzed by LC-MS/MS. The results indicated a significant increase in lactate and fatty acid metabolism in the PER2-KD group, with lactate levels notably higher than those in the Con group. These findings suggest that M-MDSCs in the PER2-KD group exhibit a metabolic profile skewed toward glycolysis and are strongly associated with increased lactate production (Fig. 9E).

M-MDSCs were isolated from mouse xenograft tumors and co-cultured with CD8⁺ T cells derived from healthy mice. CD8⁺ T cell proliferation was assessed using CFSE dye, and IFN-γ secretion was measured by ELISA. The results showed that M-MDSCs from the PER2-KD group significantly suppressed CD8⁺ T cell proliferation and markedly reduced IFN-γ secretion (Fig. 9F-G). In the PER2-KD + immunotherapy group, the suppressive effect of M-MDSCs on CD8⁺ T cells remained pronounced, indicating that the efficacy of PD-1 blockade was compromised in the context of PER2-KD.

In summary, PER2-KD promoted the accumulation of M-MDSCs and the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment through activation of the STAT3 signaling pathway and metabolic reprogramming, thereby weakening the antitumor efficacy of PD-1 inhibition.

Discussion

This study systematically elucidated the critical role of the circadian gene PER2-KD in mediating resistance to immunotherapy in GC. It highlights how PER2-KD promotes the accumulation of M-MDSCs through metabolic reprogramming and impairs CD8⁺ T cell function, thereby diminishing the antitumor efficacy of ICIs. This finding aligns with a growing body of evidence suggesting that circadian rhythm disruption is closely associated with tumor immune evasion [25]. As a core regulator of the circadian rhythm [17], PER2 has been implicated not only in the regulation of the cell cycle and apoptosis but also, more recently, in modulating immune responses [21, 25]. Furthermore, the study by Fortin et al. confirmed that the circadian clock is a key regulator of immune function, playing an essential role in host defense and tumor immunosurveillance. In colorectal cancer models, they demonstrated that circadian-dependent antitumor immune activity can guide the optimal timing and efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade [35]. Our study further demonstrated that PER2-KD in the GC model led to significant upregulation of immunosuppression-related genes and a concomitant downregulation of T cell activation signals. This shift in the immune microenvironment may underlie the lack of response to immunotherapy. While previous studies have implicated PER2 in immune regulation in some solid tumors, this is the first comprehensive analysis of its effect on the response to ICIs.

Distinct from previous studies that have primarily focused on mechanisms such as T cell exhaustion and Treg cell expansion, this study shifts attention to tumor-associated myeloid cells, particularly the central role of M-MDSCs in immunosuppression. Through single-cell transcriptomic and immunophenotypic analyses, we found that PER2-KD significantly promoted the infiltration of M-MDSCs within tumor tissues, with a notably increased proportion of M-M-MDSCs. This finding is highly consistent with prior reports that identified M-MDSCs as contributors to tumor immune evasion and diminished CD8⁺ T cell effector function in GC [36, 37]. More importantly, using a CM system and co-culture assays, we demonstrated that PER2-KD GC cells could secrete soluble factors that induce the immunosuppressive phenotype of M-MDSCs, resulting in markedly reduced IFN-γ secretion and proliferation of CD8⁺ T cells. Unlike conventional studies that rely on exogenous stimulation or late-stage tumor models, our research focuses on M-MDSC induction mechanisms under endogenous knockdown of the circadian gene PER2, offering a novel perspective for understanding the myeloid immune regulatory network in GC.

Moreover, we observed that in the context of PER2-KD, the phosphorylation level of STAT3 in M-MDSCs was significantly elevated, accompanied by increased expression of key immunosuppressive factors such as VEGF and IL-6. This finding aligns with previous studies identifying STAT3 as a central pathway for maintaining M-MDSC function [38, 39]. STAT3 promotes the expansion and survival of M-MDSCs and enhances their immunosuppressive effects by regulating metabolic status and releasing immunomodulatory factors [33, 40]. In our study, blockade of the IL-6 and VEGF pathways with neutralizing antibodies partially restored the immune activity of CD8⁺ T cells, further confirming the critical role of this mechanism. Compared to prior studies that primarily analyzed STAT3 activation from the perspective of tumor cells, our work places the STAT3 signaling axis at the core of enhanced M-MDSC function, highlighting the bidirectional regulatory mechanism of tumor-immune cell signaling.

At the metabolic level, metabolomic data indicated that PER2-KD significantly enhanced glycolysis and fatty acid metabolism in tumor cells, leading to a substantial accumulation of metabolic byproducts such as lactate. As a key component of the metabolic microenvironment [41], lactate has been shown to suppress effector T cell function and enhance the immunosuppressive capacity of M-MDSCs [42]. In this study, lactate treatment directly strengthened the inhibitory effect of M-MDSCs on CD8⁺ T cells, suggesting that PER2-KD promotes immune evasion indirectly via metabolic reprogramming. This finding enriches our understanding of tumor metabolism-immune interactions and establishes a link between circadian genes and the metabolism-immunity interface. While previous research has rarely addressed the role of circadian rhythm disruption in tumor metabolic regulation, our study is the first to demonstrate that PER2-KD drives metabolic pathway alterations and directly associates these changes with M-MDSC-mediated immunoregulation, representing a novel and significant contribution to the field. We acknowledge certain limitations of this study. Our results demonstrate that PER2 regulates glycolysis and lactate production, thereby inhibiting CD8⁺ T cell proliferation and function. However, we did not explore the detailed molecular mechanisms underlying this process. Future studies will aim to determine whether this immunosuppressive effect arises from a decrease in extracellular pH caused by lactate accumulation or from lactate-induced protein lactylation modifications.

Animal experiments further confirmed the biological effects of the aforementioned mechanisms. Tumor growth was significantly accelerated in PER2-KD mice, the therapeutic efficacy of PD-1 antibody treatment was markedly reduced, and overall survival was significantly shortened. These findings closely mimic the clinical phenomenon observed in certain GC patients who exhibit resistance to ICIs, suggesting that PER2-KD may serve as an essential molecular marker of ICI resistance. Previous studies on PD-1 resistance mechanisms have primarily focused on the composition of immune cells within the TME, PD-L1 expression levels, or tumor mutational burden. In contrast, our study integrates molecular knockdown, metabolic alterations, and immune regulation, offering a novel perspective for understanding mechanisms underlying immunotherapy resistance.

In summary, this study reveals a novel mechanism by which the circadian gene PER2-KD induces CD8⁺ T cell dysfunction and resistance to PD-1 therapy through metabolic reprogramming, activation of the STAT3 signaling pathway, and enhanced accumulation and function of M-MDSCs. These findings not only expand our understanding of the role of circadian genes in regulating the tumor immune microenvironment but also provide a potential explanation for the variability in ICI efficacy among GC patients. From a scientific perspective, this study establishes a new pathogenic model that integrates circadian rhythm, metabolic regulation, and immune evasion. Clinically, PER2-KD may serve as a molecular biomarker to predict immunotherapy response and holds promise as a potential target for future combination therapies, such as chronotherapeutics, STAT3 inhibitors, or metabolic modulators. Nevertheless, this study has limitations, including the lack of large-scale retrospective clinical validation and insufficient exploration of the differential impact of PER2-KD subtypes on the immune microenvironment. Future studies should further validate the association between PER2-KD and ICI efficacy in clinical cohorts and explore novel immunotherapeutic strategies based on circadian rhythm modulation, thereby providing a theoretical basis and intervention approach for personalized precision therapy in GC. As a circadian regulatory gene, our current study primarily investigated the role of PER2 in modulating immune checkpoints within the circadian framework of GC. Previous research has also demonstrated non-circadian functions of PER2—specifically, PER2 inhibits p53 degradation by binding to MDM2 and preventing its ubiquitination [43]. Whether this non-circadian mechanism contributes to immune resistance in GC remains an important question that warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

This study systematically elucidated how the circadian gene PER2-KD promotes the accumulation of M-MDSCs through metabolic reprogramming, enhances their immunosuppressive function and weakens the antitumor efficacy of ICIs in GC. Transcriptomic and single-cell sequencing results demonstrated that PER2-KD significantly remodeled the immune microenvironment in GC, upregulated immunosuppression-associated factors, and inhibited CD8⁺ T cell activity. Functional experiments further confirmed that PER2-KD activated STAT3 signaling and increased the expression of immunosuppressive markers in M-MDSCs. Metabolomic analysis revealed that PER2-KD led to significant enrichment of glycolysis and fatty acid metabolic pathways, with lactate accumulation playing a key role in enhancing the immunosuppressive function of M-MDSCs. In vivo experiments showed that PER2-KD accelerated tumor growth and conferred resistance to PD-1 immunotherapy, resulting in significantly shortened survival in mice.

This study is the first to reveal the mechanism of PER2-KD-mediated immunotherapy resistance in GC from a multidimensional perspective of “circadian rhythm-metabolism-immunity.” It expands the theoretical scope of circadian genes in tumor immune regulation and suggests that PER2 may be a potential biomarker for predicting immunotherapy resistance. Targeting PER2 or its downstream metabolic pathways may improve the efficacy of immunotherapy and facilitate the optimization of personalized treatment strategies for GC.
