Recurrent spinal subdural hematoma following percutaneous kyphoplasty: A unique case report
Authors: Wen-Hui Liu, Xin-Min Lin, Xin-Yu Lu, Hai Han, Liang-Zhi Zhang, Hai-Xian Lin, Zi-Wen Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Spinal intradural (subdural and subarachnoid) hematoma following percutaneous kyphoplasty is an extremely rare complication. In this report, we described a case of 2 episodes of subarachnoid hemorrhage with delayed paralysis after kyphoplasty. A 73-year-old man underwent percutaneous kyphoplasty in our hospital an osteoporotic vertebral fracture at the T12 level. On the 55 h after kyphoplasty for T12 osteoporotic vertebral fracture, he developed paralysis of the lower limbs. An emergency posterior decompression from T8 to L2 was performed. And the subarachnoid hematomas were removed. Postoperatively, the neurological symptoms improved rapidly. However, 2 weeks after the operation, the patient experienced a setback with severe neurological decline (paraplegia with sensory and autonomic dysfunction). An emergency posterior decompression from T5 to L2 was performed. The subarachnoid hematomas were removed. This case reflects the cause and progression of spinal subdural hematoma. Previous literature has debated the best treatment approach for spinal subarachnoid hemorrhage, but the prognosis of patients is heavily dependent on precise symptom evaluation and localization.

Introduction

Percutaneous kyphoplasty, a common surgical procedure for treating osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures, is widely utilized to provide rapid and effective pain relief. Yet, it also led to many complications.1 One infrequent complication is spinal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SSDH), typically occurring within 12 – 24 h after surgery. Symptoms may manifest either immediately following the procedure or several days later, presenting as severe neurological symptoms that can be effectively managed with timely surgery. Prevention is crucial as complications can have serious consequences. Only a few articles describe this similar complication. Furthermore, there is no clinical report of a patient experiencing spontaneous SSDH or spinal subdural hematoma twice after percutaneous kyphoplasty. In this study, we conducted a retrospective analysis of the treatment process for this case, drawing from previous studies. We discussed the optimal treatment method for SSDH, with an emphasis on symptom assessment and location of affected segments. Additionally, we reflected on the causes and progression of spinal cord subarachnoid hemorrhage and subdural hematoma.

Case report

A 73-year-old male suffered a T12 vertebral compression fracture due to a sprain and underwent T12 vertebroplasty on February 26, 2019. The patient experienced numbness of the skin below the groin on both sides, a partial relief of pain, and a significant decrease in muscle strength in all muscle groups of the bilateral lower limbs 55 h after the operation. After receiving symptomatic treatment with hormones and hemostatic drugs, the muscle strength of most muscle groups of both lower limbs recovered to level II − IV (based on manual muscle test: The manual muscle test was performed according to the Medical Research Council2). Every assessed muscle group was rated from 0 (no visible contraction) to Ⅴ (normal). However, 1 day later, the patient's left lower limb muscle strength again significantly declined, and his neurological function declined severely. However, on the 14th day after the operation, the patient experienced severe neurological decline again (Table 1, Table 2). We assessed that the affected segment was located at T7 − T8, and MRI (Fig. 4) was completed. Therefore, on March 16, 2019, emergency spinal hematoma removal and spinal cord decompression surgery were performed. Intraoperative exploration showed that the dura mater was intact and there was no epidural hematoma. Incision of the dura mater and arachnoid mater revealed rust-colored cerebrospinal fluid gushing out with respiratory fluctuations, and film-like blood clots were observed in the subarachnoid space. The subarachnoid space was repeatedly flushed until no blood clot was present, and the longitudinal incision of the dura mater was sutured continuously once it was unobstructed. Discussion

Anatomy divides the vertebral venous plexus into 2 parts: the internal (intradural) vertebral plexus and the external (or epidural) vertebral plexus. Some authors consider it to be the secondary pressure regulation system of the human body.4,5 The medullary nerve root veins constitute the weakest link between the 2 systems (Fig. 6).6Fig. 6Schematic representation of the vertebral venous system. Before the 2 emergency surgical decompressions, the patients were combined with factors that increased the pressure in the thoracic/abdominal cavity (history of sprains and long-term use of Enema Glycerin to assist defecation), resulting in high pressure in the epidural venous system, venous obstruction, and ultimately vertebral collapse. The secondary pressure regulation system of the venous plexus is out of control, the minimum resistance point of the vertebral venous plexus system ruptures, and blood overflows into the subdural space.7,8 Corresponding to the preoperative MRI on March 2, 2019, the posterior mass image of the spinal cord at the T12 segment and the discovery of a small blood vessel rupture in the spinal cord at the level of the T11 − T12 spinal canal during the operation.

In previously reported cases, another suggested cause of venous congestion is related to Polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) obstruction of the vertebral venous plexus draining the vertebral body veins.9 Venous outflow is obstructed since the PMMA is occluded, then secondary venous congestion and hypoxia caused secondary neurological deficits. However, during the percutaneous kyphoplasty operation, we established the working cannula with only 1 puncture, and there was no dural tear and the CT shows there was no large-scale extravasation of PMMA into the spinal canal, only minor leakage.

On March 16, 2019, the patient again suffered from severe neurological decline and underwent surgery. Surgical exploration revealed that the cerebrospinal fluid was rust-colored, gushing out with respiratory fluctuations, and film-like blood clots were seen in the subarachnoid space. However, most previous case reports of SSDHs did not find mixing between subdural blood and cerebrospinal fluid.10 The presence of suspicious air cleft shadows (air within the subdural space) on preoperative emergency MRI also supports the hypothesis of venous system collapse: Due to the negative pressure in the cavity, a pressure difference occurs with the surrounding cavity, eventually causing air to accumulate in the subdural space.

On March 2, 2019, the patient experienced severe neurological decline and received hormone and hemostatic drug treatment. Partial restoration of motor function in both lower limbs led us to suspect mild hemiplegia caused by brain injury. The delay in diagnosis and treatment of spinal hemorrhage due to incorrect positioning and physical signs.11,12 Therefore, when the patient experienced severe neurological decline again, we promptly evaluated and performed emergency surgery.

The management of SSDH has been controversial in previous literature. Some authors recommend surgical spinal cord decompression and hematoma drainage in the acute phase13, while others believe that opening the SSDH is necessary without radiographic evidence of spinal cord compression. Surgical attempts to remove the meninges and evacuate the hematoma are of questionable value, especially in extensive lesions involving multiple segments.14 Furthermore, neurologically insufficient patients often recover aggressively without spinal decompression or hematoma drainage.15,16 In this case, emergency surgical decompression and hematoma evacuation were performed twice. The patient still had short-term incomplete neurological deficits, but no further surgical treatment was performed. Later, the muscle strength in both lower limbs gradually improved through rehabilitation training, supporting this observation. In the long-term, the physiological flow of cerebrospinal fluid will gradually dilute SSDH, leading to its spontaneous regression, as observed subsequently.17,18

In this article, the authors propose an etiological pathway emphasizing the role of venous congestion leading to the rupture of vulnerable medullary root veins into the subdural space, which could explain the development of SSDHs, at least in a subset of patients. Finally, this case report highlights the need for further anatomical and histological studies to investigate better their behavior in congestive venous hypertension and the relationship between their possible rupture and the development of SSDH.
