Fever and Chimpanzees
Authors: Lise Claessens, Filip Haenen, Inez E. Rodrigus, Bernard P. Paelinck
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Tropic fever can have several causes. It is important to investigate thoroughly and consider less obvious explanations. This paper presents the case of a biologist in close contact with chimpanzees, who developed fever in the tropics. Despite treatment for some tropical diagnosis, the fever persisted. On arrival in Belgium, further diagnostics revealed an unexpected diagnosis: Citrobacter koseri endocarditis of the native aortic valve. He was treated with ceftriaxone and amikacin and underwent aortic valve replacement. C koseri is a commensal in humans and animals. It is likely that the patient was infected with this bacterium through his close contact with chimpanzees. Only a few cases of C koseri endocarditis have been published worldwide, with most patients being immunocompromised. Patients with tropical fever may have unsuspected underlying causes, like endocarditis.

History of Presentation

A 49-year-old man was admitted at the emergency department because of recurrent high fever. He lived and worked as a biologist in the Democratic Republic of Congo. He had been feeling unwell and experiencing episodes of fever for a few weeks. Initially, malaria was suspected and treatment with artemether/lumefantrine was initiated. After a few days of clinical improvement, the fever relapsed. Because he tested positive for typhoid and rickettsial disease, norfloxacin (400 mg/d) and doxycycline (200 mg/d) were started. The antibiotics were switched to cefixime (400 mg twice daily) and doxycycline (100 mg twice daily) due to lack of clinical improvement. Despite a prolonged course of antibiotics, he remained febrile on arrival in Belgium 1 month later. Furthermore, he felt tired and weak. He also has been complaining of transient atypical muscular pains (lower back pain, pain in the left arm, pain in the right thigh) for a few days. He mentioned that he had fostered a baby chimpanzee for nearly 6 months into his home a few months ago. Past Medical History

His medical history was blank.

Differential Diagnosis

Tropical fever including malaria, dengue, typhoid, leptospirosis, and rickettsial disease.

Investigations

His vital signs remained reassuring, except for episodes of fever. Clinical examination was unremarkable. Blood analyses were highly inflammatory with a C-reactive protein of 202 mg/L (normal <10 mg/L) and leukocytes of 8.02 × 109/L (normal: 4.2 × 109/L to 10.3 × 109/L). Thick smear for malaria was negative. Further testing for Coxiella burnetii, Bartonella henselae, human immunodeficiency virus, and hepatitis B were negative. Urine culture was negative.

Management

Piperacillin/tazobactam (4 g, 4 times daily) with azithromycin (1,000 mg the first day, then 500 mg/d) was started empirically. Blood cultures (n = 10) were positive for Citrobacter koseri, the first ones after 9 hours only. Piperacillin/tazobactam and azithromycin were stopped and based on the antibiogram, ceftriaxone (2 g twice daily) and amikacin (1 g/d for 5 days) were started because of persistent fever and multisusceptible C koseri. Chorioretinitis was excluded.

In the search for an entry port and localizations of infection, a positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) scan was performed. Because of hemodynamically significant aortic valve regurgitation, aortic valve replacement was performed after 10 days of ceftriaxone and amikacin. Because of difficult access to medical care in the jungle, a bioprosthesis was chosen in consultation with the patient. Visual Summary

Ceftriaxone was continued for a total of 6 weeks postoperatively. At that time, a control transesophageal ultrasound showed normal function of the bioprosthetic aortic valve without vegetations.

In view of the PET-CT findings, a bone marrow biopsy was performed, which revealed a small monoclonal B-cell population, which was insufficient to confirm the diagnosis of B-cell lymphoma. An additional superficial axillary lymph node biopsy was performed with anatomopathologic confirmation of a reactive lymph node. Two months after discharge, a repeat PET-CT scan was performed, after clinical reinvestigation and suspicion of enlarged axillary lymph nodes, demonstrating similar significant uptake in the axillary glands. An axillary lymph node debridement revealed a small population of B-cell non-Hodgkin cells with a nonspecific phenotype (CD20+/CD5–/CD10–/CD23–), consistent with nodal marginal zone lymphoma. Given the typically slow progression and favorable prognosis of 91% 5-year overall survival, a watch-and-wait strategy with surveillance every 6 months was chosen.1 Further follow-up of his hematologic condition was planned in a peripheral hospital, closer to his hometown.

Discussion

We report a case of tropical fever in which classical pathogens were initially considered. However, because there was no improvement after treatment was started, a more detailed investigation was initiated on arrival in Belgium. This revealed an unexpected pathogen: we cultured C koseri from his blood and discovered that his aortic valve was affected by this bacterium. It is possible that C koseri was transmitted by the baby chimpanzee that this patient was caring for because C koseri is known to be found in chimpanzees.2

C koseri is a gram-negative bacillus found in food, water, and soil, and as a commensal in the gut of humans and animals.3 It may cause infections in neonates, patients who are immunocompromised, and older adults. It mainly causes infections of the urinary, respiratory, and gastrointestinal tract.4 Rarely, C koseri causes cardiovascular infections, usually in patients who are immunocompromised.5, 6, 7

We performed a search of the current literature and found 7 case reports.

There is only one case reporting a 30-year-old man with C koseri endocarditis without any underlying disease. The primary source of infection was not identified. This patient was treated successfully with 4 weeks of ceftriaxone and 5 days of amikacin.4

Another very similar case was reported of a 38-year-old previously healthy woman diagnosed with an infected mass with C koseri extending from the ascending aorta, but without valve involvement.8

Two of the published cases of C koseri endocarditis were intravenous drug users.5,7 We also found a case study of a patient on hemodialysis who developed C koseri endocarditis.6 Another 80-year-old woman developed endocarditis after recurrent urinary tract infections with C koseri.9 Finally, there was a case of a patient with pacemaker lead endocarditis due to C koseri.10

We report a patient with C koseri endocarditis of a native aortic valve. It is possible that this C koseri was transmitted from the baby chimpanzee this patient was caring for, given that C koseri also is known to be found in chimpanzees.2

Initially, an underlying immunocompromising disease was not suspected. However, based on a PET-CT scan after extensive investigation with multiple lymph node biopsies, a diagnosis of nodal marginal zone lymphoma was made. To our knowledge, this underlying disease has never been described in C koseri endocarditis. Our findings indicate that further investigation for underlying diseases is appropriate in patients with C koseri infections.

Follow-Up

One year later, the patient was doing well and his hematologic condition remained stable. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a well-functioning aortic valve bioprosthesis.

Conclusions

We describe a rare case of a patient with tropical fever in whom further investigations revealed an infective endocarditis of the aortic valve caused by C koseri. This uncommon organism for infective endocarditis is a commensal in humans and animals. We suspect that the patient acquired the bacterium when caring for a chimpanzee.
