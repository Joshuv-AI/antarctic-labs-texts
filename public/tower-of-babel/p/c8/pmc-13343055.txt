Neighbourhood environments, structural inequities, and colorectal cancer risk and mortality: analysis from the Southern Community Cohort Study
Authors: Fekede Asefa Kumsa, Jay H Fowke, Soheil Hashtarkhani, Brianna M White, Martha J Shrubsole, Arash Shaban-Nejad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveNeighbourhood socioeconomic status (nSES) and built environment features strongly influence diet, physical activity and cancer screening adherence, potentially affecting colorectal cancer (CRC) incidence and prognosis. The aim of this study is to assess the association between neighbourhood obesogenic environments (eg, nSES, restaurant and retail food indices, recreational facilities, and business district residence) and CRC risk and mortality.DesignA secondary data analysis was performed using prospectively collected data from the Southern Community Cohort Study.Setting12 states in the Southeastern USA.ParticipantsWe analysed data from 70 519 participants enrolled in the Southern Community Cohort Study.OutcomesThe primary outcomes in this study are CRC risk and mortality.Data analysisWe used multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, adjusting for individual-level factors to investigate neighbourhood-level risk factors associated with CRC risk and mortality. We further performed race-stratified analyses (Black/White) to examine potential disparities in CRC risk.ResultsAmong 70 519 participants (69.49% Black, 30.51% White), 927 (1.31%) were diagnosed with CRC (1.37% Black and 1.19% White participants). Of these, 255 (27.5%) died from CRC. Compared with participants residing in the highest (fifth) nSES quintile, those residing in the wealthier (fourth) quintile of nSES exhibited a higher CRC risk (adjusted HR (aHR) 1.27 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.57)). The Retail Food Environment Index was associated with an increased risk of CRC among participants residing in the fifth (wealthiest) nSES quintile (aHR 3.07 (95% CI 1.23 to 7.61) for Tertile 1 vs None; aHR 3.06 (95% CI 1.23 to 7.60) for Tertile 2 vs None). Similar associations were observed among both Black participants (aHR 3.65 (95% CI 1.19 to 11.20) for Tertile 1 vs None) and White participants (aHR 5.20 (95% CI 1.29 to 20.97) for Tertile 2 vs None) living in the same neighbourhoods, although these subgroup estimates should be interpreted cautiously due to wide CIs. Living in a low-walkability neighbourhood was associated with a higher risk of CRC (aHR 2.42 (95% CI 1.09 to 3.56)) among residents in the second-lowest nSES quintile, particularly among Black participants (aHR 3.41 (95% CI 1.29 to 9.02)). Compared with residents of the most walkable neighbourhoods, those in the least walkable (aHR 2.23 (95% CI 1.10 to 4.54)), below-average walkable (aHR 2.10 (95% CI 1.08 to 4.07)) and above-average walkable areas (aHR 2.33 (95% CI 1.24 to 4.39)) had significantly higher CRC mortality risk.ConclusionOur findings suggest that nSES and unhealthy food environments are associated with CRC risk, while less walkable environments were associated with higher CRC mortality. These findings highlight the need for a more detailed assessment of neighbourhood-level deprivation and support enhanced public policies targeting neighbourhood deprivation in low-income populations in the Southeastern USA.Keywords: Mortality, Body Mass Index, EPIDEMIOLOGY, Weight Gain

Introduction

Globally, colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most commonly diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality.1 In the USA, it is the fourth most prevalent cancer. The development of CRC is influenced by a range of factors, including genetic variation, race, social determinants of health, smoking, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity and an unhealthy diet.2,6 Non-genetic risk factors such as diet are modifiable, and an individual’s dietary patterns are often shaped by the type of food available in their surrounding environment.7 Notably, increasing Body Mass Index (BMI) and obese individuals face a significantly elevated risk of CRC compared with the general population.4 7 8 Restricted exposure to healthy food options can lead to a less healthy diet, with an unhealthy food environment being strongly associated with adverse health outcomes, including obesity.9 The incidence or mortality of CRC is linked with obesity, and there are also race differences in CRC risk and mortality.10 11 However, the contribution of the food obesogenic environment to CRC differences associated with race or factors related to social determinants of health is unclear.8

Neighbourhoods are defined by both physical and social characteristics, and neighbourhood obesogenic factors consist of socioeconomic indices and the built environment. The impact of neighbourhood social factors and the built environment on obesity and CRC outcomes may be mediated through multiple mechanisms, including access to healthcare resources, material deprivation, health behaviours and psychosocial factors.12 This constellation of partially modifiable constructs may interact to contribute to a sedentary lifestyle,13 energy imbalance and obesity.14,16 Individuals living in a low socioeconomic environment are more likely to become obese14 and to have a greater CRC incidence and mortality.17 The built neighbourhood environment associated with obesity may include an individual’s proximity to unhealthy restaurants, food deserts, limited walkability, more commuting by car, and a higher perceived or real traffic density.15 16 The intersection of neighbourhood environments, social determinants and individual risk factors is increasingly recognised as important in the broader context of Precision Health and Medicine.18 The goal of this study is to understand the effects of neighbourhood-level obesogenic factors on CRC risk and mortality while also controlling for individual-level BMI. Further understanding the effects of neighbourhood obesogenic environments on cancer risk and outcome among race and ethnic groups may identify structural elements of obesity that contribute to cancer racial disparities.

Methods

Data source

We used data from the Southern Community Cohort Study (SCCS), an ongoing prospective follow-up study focused on investigating health disparities, including those in cancer care, within predominantly low-income populations. Detailed information on the SCCS has been published.15 16 Briefly, enrolment for the SCCS took place between 2002 and 2009, during which 84 508 participants aged 40–79 years were eligible to join the cohort. In addition to baseline data collection at enrolment, participants underwent two consecutive follow-up assessments, with the most recent follow-up interview conducted in 2018. About 85% of these participants were recruited from community health centres, with the remaining 15% recruited by mail. The SCCS received ethical approval from the institutional review boards (IRBs) at Vanderbilt University and Meharry Medical College (IRB# 010345). All participants provided written informed consent prior to their involvement. Additionally, this analysis of SCCS data was reviewed by the IRB of the University of Tennessee Health Science Center, which granted a waiver for analysis as investigators did not have access to personal identifiers (23-09249 NHSR).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct or dissemination of the results of this study.

Individual-level factors

Individual-level factors associated with obesity or CRC at baseline or follow-up assessments included: age; gender (male or female); race (White or Black); employment status (yes, no or unknown); marital status (married, separated/divorced/widowed or single); BMI (underweight: <18.5 kg/m², normal weight: 18.5–24.9 kg/m², overweight: 25–29.9 kg/m², obese: ≥30 kg/m²); smoking status/pack-years (never smoker, former smoker/<20 pack-years, former smoker/20+pack years, former smoker/pack-years unknown, current smoker/<20 pack-years, current smoker/20+pack years, current smoker/pack-years unknown); household income (less than US$15 000, US$15 000–24 999, US$25 000–49 999, US$50 000–99 999, US$100 000 or more, or unknown); educational status; history of diabetes mellitus (yes, no or unknown); family history of CRC (parents or siblings) (yes, no or unknown); regular use of any nutritional supplement (yes, no or unknown); and use of supplements, including multivitamins, vitamin A, vitamin C, vitamin D, vitamin E, folic acid, calcium and aspirin (yes, no or unknown).

Neighbourhood obesogenic attributes

We extracted the neighbourhood-level factors from three sources. First, sociodemographic data were obtained from the 2010 Census at the block group level (www.census.gov). These sociodemographic data included median gross rent, education levels, unemployment rates, household income, poverty and home value indices. Second, the built environment data were extracted from OpenStreetMap (https://overpass-turbo.eu/), a collaborative global mapping platform containing details on roads, retail outlets and eateries. Using the Overpass API and ArcGIS Pro V.2.5, we extracted information on restaurants, food retailers, businesses and recreational facilities within a 1-mile walking distance of each block group’s centroid. Finally, the Walkability Index was sourced from the National Walkability Index (areas into four levels (lowest to highest walkable) based on intersection density, transit access and land use mix (https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/walkability-index).

The neighbourhood socioeconomic status (nSES) was constructed as a composite measure using principal component analysis of census block data on housing (median rent and median house value), occupation (proportion with a blue-collar job and proportion older than 16 in a workforce without a job), education (percentage of high school graduates by the year needed to complete high school), employment and income (median income and percentage of living below the poverty level).19 20 The nSES was divided into five equal groups based on its distribution, where the first quintile (Q1) corresponded to the most economically disadvantaged areas and the fifth quintile (Q5) represented the most affluent neighbourhoods.

The neighbourhood-built attributes included the number of recreational facilities, parks, and businesses, the Retail Food Environment Index and the Restaurant Environment Index. Each of the recreational facilities, parks and businesses was categorised as none (absent) and some (present). The Retail Food Environment Index was calculated as the ratio of the number of convenience stores, liquor stores and fast-food restaurants to supermarkets and farmers’ markets. This index was categorised as: no low-quality retail food, Tertile 1, Tertile 2 or Tertile 3, or no retail food outlets in the area. Similarly, the Restaurant Environment Index was the ratio of a fast-food restaurant to other restaurants and was classified as: no fast food restaurants, Tertile 1, Tertile 2 or Tertile 3, or no other restaurants.16 20 Higher values in both indices indicated less healthy food environments. The Walkability Index was categorised as least walkable, below average walkable, above average walkable and most walkable environment.21

Outcome variables

The outcome variables in this study were CRC incidence and CRC mortality. The SCCS participants were followed from the time of enrolment until the occurrence of a CRC diagnosis, death, emigration or the end of the follow-up period (2018), whichever came first. CRC cases and deaths were identified through linkage to state cancer registries International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3) C18, C199, C209, C260, excluding specific histologies (9590-9989, 9050-9055, 9140+) and considering invasive behaviour, the National Death Registry and follow-up surveys, which were confirmed through examination of medical records.

Sample

Out of the original 84 508 SCCS participants, we excluded 2377 individuals due to missing BMI information, 3725 individuals with unknown race or who did not self-identify as either White or Black/African-American, 649 individuals with unknown marital status, and 457 individuals with missing smoking status or smoking history. Since our primary focus was on evaluating the risk of developing CRC, we also excluded 380 participants who had been diagnosed with CRC before SCCS enrolment. Additionally, 6401 participants were excluded due to missing data for at least one attribute of nSES or inability to link to individual-level data. Consequently, our analysis included a total of 70 519 participants (figure 1).

Statistical analysis

Both descriptive and analytical analyses were conducted. Frequencies, percentages, and measures of central tendency and dispersion were used to describe the data. The median follow-up time with the IQR was calculated. The association between neighbourhood obesogenic factors and CRC risk was examined using a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model using age as a time-varying factor, with corresponding 95% CIs. Additional race-specific models (for White and Black participants) were run to account for heterogeneity in CRC risks between these groups. A stratified analysis based on participants’ BMI categories and race groups was also conducted, excluding those with a BMI less than 18.5 kg/m² due to insufficient sample size. Each model was controlled for individual-level variables. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version V.17.0 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA), with a two-sided probability, and a p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the total 70 519 participants included in the analysis, 69.49% were Black, while the remaining 30.51% were White. The majority (59.32%) were female. The follow-up time ranged from 1 to 196 months, with a median follow-up time of 134 months (IQR 106–152). The mean age of the participants at enrolment was 52.15 years (53.85 years for White individuals and 51.4 years for Black individuals), 37.82% of White and 48.19% of Black participants were in their 40s when enrolled in the study.

Table 1 summarises the study population characteristics. Many participants were either overweight (29.77%) or obese (44.52%), while only 24.45% had a BMI in the normal range. 60% of the participants had insurance coverage, including 63.18% of White participants and 58.19% of Black participants. The prevalence of a past digital rectal examination was 28.22%, while the prevalence of prior colonoscopy was 51.70%. Among those who underwent colonoscopy, 58.40% were White and 48.75% were Black. Approximately 39% of both Black and White participants were unemployed. A majority of participants had smoked at some point (64.45%), and about 23% were former smokers. White participants were more likely to be current heavy smokers (20 or more pack years: 27.50% (White) vs 14.92% (Black)), while a higher percentage of Black participants smoked less than 20 pack years compared with White participants (27.51% vs 10.97%, respectively). Household income below US$15 000 per year was prevalent among both Black (59.09%) and White (47.08%) participants. Only 23.67% of Black participants and 16.11% of White participants had completed 12 years of schooling, while 3.11% of Black participants and 7.97% of White participants had pursued graduate-level studies (online supplemental table 1).

Influence of neighbourhood obesogenic factors on CRC risk

A total of 927 participants (1.31%) were diagnosed with CRC during follow-up, including 257 White participants (1.19%) and 670 Black participants (1.37%). After adjusting for individual-level CRC risk factors as well as neighbourhood-level attributes, in general, neighbourhood obesogenic factors were not statistically significantly associated with risk of CRC overall or within racial strata. The exception is that nSES demonstrated a significant association with CRC risk (online supplemental table 2). Participants residing in neighbourhoods within the fourth quintile of nSES had a 27% higher risk of CRC compared with those residing in the fifth (wealthiest) nSES quintile (adjusted HR (aHR) 1.27 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.57)). This association was similar within Black or White participants but was no longer statistically significant when the sample size was reduced with stratification by race.

Table 3 examines the nSES–CRC relationship further stratifying by BMI as well as race. Overweight participants living in the second quintile of nSES had a 48% higher risk of CRC compared with those in the highest (wealthiest) quintile of nSES (aHR 1.48 (95% CI 1.01 to 2.19), p=0.049). Interestingly, obese participants living in the fourth (wealthier) quintile of nSES had a 38% higher risk of CRC compared with those in the fifth (wealthiest) quintile of nSES (aHR 1.38 (95% CI 1.02 to 1.90)). This increased risk in the fourth quintile of nSES was evident among Black or White participants but was no longer statistically significant when the analysis was stratified by race.

Online supplemental table 3 examines the relationships between an obesogenic environment across nSES categories and in combination with race. Residing in the least walkable neighbourhoods was associated with an increased risk of CRC (aHR 2.42 (95% CI 1.09 to 3.56)) among participants living in neighbourhoods in the second-lowest nSES quintile. This association was primarily driven by Black participants living in neighbourhoods in the second-lowest nSES quintile (aHR 3.41 (95% CI 1.29 to 9.02)).

The Retail Food Environment Index was associated with an elevated CRC risk among participants residing in higher-SES neighbourhoods (fourth and fifth quintiles). Among those living in the fourth nSES quintile, participants residing in neighbourhoods without restaurants were more likely to develop CRC (aHR 1.94 (95% CI 1.01 to 3.76)). This association was particularly pronounced among Black participants in the same neighbourhood category (aHR 2.36 (95% CI 1.05 to 5.32)). Furthermore, the Retail Food Environment Index was associated with an increased CRC risk among participants residing in the fifth (wealthiest) nSES quintile (aHR 3.07 (95% CI 1.23 to 7.61) for Tertile 1 vs None; aHR 3.06 (95% CI 1.23 to 7.60)). Similar associations were observed among both Black participants (aHR 3.65 (95% CI 1.11 to 12.0) for Tertile 1 vs None) and White participants (aHR 5.20 (95% CI 1.29 to 20.97) for Tertile 2 vs None) living in the same nSES, although subgroup estimates were imprecise due to wide CIs (online supplemental file 3).

Influence of neighbourhood obesogenic factors on CRC mortality

There were 255 CRC fatalities identified during follow-up, or 27.5% of incident CRC cases. After controlling individual-level covariates, the only neighbourhood obesogenic factor significantly associated with CRC mortality was the Walkability Index (table 4). Those living in the least walkable areas had a 2.23 times higher risk of dying from CRC (aHR 2.23 (95% CI 1.10 to 4.54), p=0.021). Participants in below-average walkable areas had a 2.10 times higher risk (aHR 2.10 (95% CI 1.08 to 4.07), p=0.023). Those in above-average walkable areas had a 2.33 times higher risk (aHR 2.33 (95% CI 1.24 to 4.39), p=0.007) (table 4).

Discussion

Using the large-scale prospective data from the SCCS, we are able to investigate the potential association of neighbourhood obesogenic environments on CRC risk and mortality. In our analysis, we considered race as a social, not biological, construct and were able to control for individual income level while investigating the impact of nSES on CRC. Participants residing in neighbourhoods with lower nSES quintiles were observed to have a greater risk of CRC compared with those in the highest nSES quintile. This association was stronger among overweight and obese individuals. Additionally, the Retail Food Environment Index was associated with an increased risk of CRC among overweight participants, with this association being particularly pronounced among overweight Black participants. However, only the Walkability Index was associated with CRC mortality, where individuals residing in less walkable areas exhibited a higher risk of CRC mortality.

Our study found that nSES influenced the risk of CRC, with effects varying based on participants’ BMI and race. Among both Black and White participants with a normal BMI, those residing in higher nSES neighbourhoods had a lower risk of CRC, but this finding was statistically insignificant. Previous studies showed that residing in neighbourhoods with lower socioeconomic levels had a significantly higher risk of CRC, with the risk varying across different geographic regions.17 In addition, a recent study using the SCCS data found that participants residing in lower-nSES areas had reduced odds of ever undergoing colonoscopy or stool-based CRC testing.22 Such screening gaps may lead to later-stage diagnoses, thereby increasing CRC risk and mortality and worsening overall outcomes in these communities.

The Retail Food Environment Index showed an association with an elevated risk of CRC among overweight participants. Individuals with high BMI, who are already at an increased metabolic risk, may be more susceptible to the effects of built environment exposures on CRC risk. Moreover, the higher value of the Retail Food Environment Index demonstrated an association with an elevated risk of CRC among participants residing in higher-SES neighbourhoods (fourth and fifth quintiles).

Higher retail food environment scores were associated with higher CRC risk, particularly among overweight Black participants, suggesting that unhealthy food environments may contribute to observed disparities. A previous study found that better access to a healthy food environment (as measured by the Food Environment Index scores) was associated with reduced CRC incidence, particularly in areas with larger Black populations.23 In contrast, an analysis of the Multiethnic Cohort in California showed that less healthy retail food environments were linked to elevated CRC risk among White males.24 These inconsistent findings suggest that the impact of unhealthy food environments on CRC risk may vary by racial/ethnic group, highlighting the need for further investigation.

Lack of physical activity is a consistent risk factor for CRC,25 and previous studies suggest that neighbourhood walkability is associated with lower BMI26 27 and increased physical activity.28 Our findings suggest that residing in less walkable environments may be associated with increased CRC risk, although this association was not statistically significant. However, a statistically significant relationship was observed between lower walkability and higher CRC mortality, although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Walkable neighbourhoods can foster walking, reduce car dependency and encourage physical activity, which may help prevent obesity-related diseases. Walking has broad benefits, including improved metabolism, enhanced insulin sensitivity, reduced body fat, better mental well-being, lower stress levels and improved emotional health. Conversely, limited walking opportunities may contribute to a poorer quality of life and adverse outcomes for individuals diagnosed with CRC. CRC mortality did not show a statistically significant association with other neighbourhood obesogenic attributes; however, it was not feasible to stratify mortality data by BMI and race as we did with incidence data due to the limited number of death events.

This study has several notable strengths. The SCCS is a prospective cohort study with significant enrolment of low-income Black and White participants across 12 southeastern US states to enable the evaluation of race differences across a similar range of nSES categories. Individual-level data on candidate CRC risk factors such as BMI, dietary supplementations, SES, MET values for sitting and walking and other CRC risk factors were available for summary analyses and to include in models of neighbourhood factors. Additionally, multiple neighbourhood-level indices related to the built environment, which may influence obesity, were calculated.

Limitations of this analysis included a relatively small number of mortality outcomes and the absence of tumour pathology data. Because SCCS participants were enrolled between 2002 and 2009 while neighbourhood-level measures were primarily derived from 2010 datasets, temporal mismatch may have introduced exposure misclassification. In addition, all neighbourhood obesogenic factors were determined using participants’ baseline addresses; therefore, some individuals may have relocated to areas with higher or lower nSES during follow-up, which could have further contributed to misclassification bias. Furthermore, we were unable to assess certain neighbourhood characteristics, such as street connectivity, traffic density, participants’ access to transportation for reaching fast-food vendors and changes in the neighbourhood’s obesogenic environment over time. These factors could have provided further insights into the observed race-specific associations and may be developed in future analyses. Causality cannot be established in a single observational study such as this, and residual confounding by unmeasured factors remains possible.

Conclusion

Higher nSES was associated with increased CRC risk among White and Black participants, particularly among those who were overweight or obese. Additionally, residing in a less walkable environment was linked to higher CRC mortality risk. These associations underscore the need for further studies to clarify and explain causal pathways and inform actionable public health interventions29 aimed at reducing disparities in vulnerable communities. Furthermore, our findings highlight the role of neighbourhood-level factors in contributing to racial disparities in CRC risk beyond individual-level risk factors and emphasising the urgent need for public health policies that address neighbourhood-level deprivation in low-income populations in the Southeastern USA.
