IL7-Receptor–Targeted CAR T-Cell Therapy for T-Cell Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia
Authors: Hocine R Hocine, Undrakh Ganbaatar, Alfredo Amador-Molina, Srijita Banerjee, Kyohei Misawa, Viviana Rubino, Pratiti Ankola, Christina Fong, Yuquan Xiong, William-Ray Vista, Rebecca Y Bellis, Navin K Chintala, Kevin J Curran, Maria Luisa Sulis, Jae H Park, Andrew L Kung, Prasad S Adusumilli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOn the basis that T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) cells overexpress IL7 receptor (IL7R), which promotes resistance to chemotherapy and disease relapse, here we develop IL7Rα (CD127)–targeted chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells with low- and high-affinity single-chain variable fragments. We establish the antitumor efficacy of CD127 CAR against T-ALL cells in vitro, in female mouse models of T-ALL, and against blasts from patients with T-ALL using the patients’ own T cells transduced with CD127 CAR. Antitumor efficacy is higher with low-affinity CAR T cells than high-affinity CAR T cells, albeit with fratricide of CAR T cells following eradication of CD127-overexpressing blasts. CRISPR-Cas9 knockout of CD127 eliminates fratricide at the risk of prolonged lymphopenia in vivo. To overcome fratricide, we investigate short-term ( < 7 days) co-culture with or without dasatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, versus a natural selection method (10 days) and demonstrate that co-culturing with dasatinib facilitates higher CAR T-cell yield, improved fitness, and preserved functionality. In vivo, dasatinib can be used to temporarily and reversibly suppress CAR T-cell activity. With this supporting translational data, we are initiating a trial with low-affinity CD127 CAR T cells for adult and pediatric patients with relapsed or refractory T-ALL.Subject terms: Cancer immunotherapy, Tumour immunology, Leukaemia

Introduction

T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL) is a rare and aggressive form of leukemia with few effective therapeutic options for relapsed disease. Prognoses for adults with T-ALL continue to be poor because adults are not able to tolerate the high intensity of multiagent chemotherapy used in children and because targeted immunotherapy options, such as those that revolutionized outcomes for B-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (B-ALL), are lacking for T-ALL. Adult patients with relapsed T-ALL have a 5-year disease-free survival of 30%, even with allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant1–3. No drug has been approved for relapsed T-ALL since 2005, when nelarabine was approved with complete response rates of only 20% to 30%. Pediatric patients with relapsed or refractory T-ALL also have dismal prognoses, with few treatment options currently available4,5. The development of effective targeted small-molecule drugs for T-ALL has been challenging because of an absence of common driver mutations.

T-ALL overexpresses IL7 receptor (IL7R) and mutations in IL7R pathway promotes resistance to chemotherapy and disease relapse, expression of IL7R is associated with poor clinical outcomes6–8. Approximately 70% of leukemic blasts from patients with T-ALL and 30% to 40% of blasts from patients with B-ALL express IL7R, although expression levels vary across disease subtypes9. Oncogenic mutations in NOTCH1 are observed in >65% of cases of T-ALL; IL7R signaling plays a crucial role in the NOTCH1-driven pathogenesis of T-ALL10,11. There is considerable evidence that IL7 and its receptor, IL7R, which is formed by IL7Rα (CD127) chain and common gamma (CD132) chain, may play a role in the development of T-ALL12. IL7 promotes cell viability, facilitates cell cycle progression, and activates the JAK/STAT5 and PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathways in both normal and malignant T cells13,14. Of note, gain-of-function oncogenic mutations in IL7R are present in 10% of cases of T-ALL and 2% of cases of B-ALL15,16.

Building on this biological relevance, IL7Rα (CD127) has been identified as a potential immunotherapy target in T-ALL17,18. Its functional importance in leukemic cell survival supports its value as a biologically relevant therapeutic target, providing a rationale for exploring IL7R-directed strategies alongside other T-lineage antigens. Although CD127 is broadly expressed on normal T cells and its expression in T-ALL can be heterogeneous, its role in IL-7–mediated leukemic cell survival provides a rationale for therapeutic targeting on the basis of pathway dependence, rather than lineage restriction.

Recently, following in the footsteps of the development of drugs for B-ALL, engineered cellular therapies such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells targeting T-cell lineage antigens such as CD5 and CD7 have entered clinical trials, with encouraging preliminary results19–22. The use of CAR T-cell therapy to treat T-cell malignancies presents unique challenges, including CAR T-cell fratricide and on-target/off-tumor cytotoxicity, owing to the shared expression of target antigens between normal and leukemic T cells. CD5 and CD7—shared antigens targeted by CAR T-cell therapy—are expressed on normal T cells and can potentially induce prolonged or permanent T-cell aplasia if CD5- or CD7-specific CAR T cells persist long term23. Unlike CD19 CAR–associated B-cell aplasia, which can be managed with infusions of immunoglobulin, T-cell aplasia leads to severe immunodeficiency—similar to levels seen in HIV-infected patients—and, thus, is associated with more-serious health consequences than B-cell aplasia24. Furthermore, because these potential side effects affect the regeneration of normal T cells and immune reconstitution, CD5 and CD7 CAR T-cell therapies are considered to be only a bridge to allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant25,26. Therefore, there is a clear unmet need to develop effective and safe targeted cellular immunotherapies that spare normal hematopoietic cells and that can serve as a definitive, stand-alone treatment for patients with T-ALL.

In this study, we investigate low- and high-affinity CD127-targeted CAR T-cell therapy in clinically relevant T-ALL models, including in a T-ALL patient–derived xenograft model, to investigate the potential of CD127–targeted CAR T-cell therapy for the treatment of T-ALL. Unlike other antigen targets expressed on T cells, CD127 is downregulated following T-cell activation. Nevertheless, with a goal to translate to clinical trial, avoiding potential CAR T-cell fratricide during manufacturing, we investigate the use of dasatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, during cell manufacturing versus the natural selection method versus the genetic depletion of CD127 by use of CRISPR-Cas9 in CAR T cells.

Results

Low-affinity anti-CD127 CAR T cells perform better than high-affinity anti-CD127 CAR T cells in vitro

Second-generation anti-CD127 CARs with CD28-transmembrane, intracellular CD28-costimulatory, and CD3z-signaling domains targeting human CD127 were generated with a c-myc epitope tag incorporated into constructs to allow tracking of CAR T cells (Fig. 1a). On the basis of the distinct binding affinity to CD127 of the single-chain variable fragment (scFv) used in each CAR, the constructs are hereafter referred to as LOW CAR (low-affinity anti-CD127 CAR) and HIGH CAR (high-affinity anti-CD127 CAR) (Supplementary Fig. S1a–c). Human donor T cells were successfully transduced with both LOW and HIGH CARs, with equivalent transduction efficiency (Fig. 1b). We then characterized the expression of CD127 on multiple T-ALL target cells and assessed the in vitro cytolytic activity of LOW and HIGH CAR T cells against target cells with varying levels of antigen expression (Fig. 1c), at multiple effector to target (E:T) ratios. LOW and HIGH CAR T cells had no cytolytic activity against very low-antigen-expressing target cells (Loucy and MOLT4), but both had higher levels of cytolytic activity against moderate-antigen-expressing (Jurkat) and high-antigen-expressing (HPB-ALL and DND.41) target cells, compared with untransduced (UT) T cells (Fig. 1d). At higher E:T ratios against high-antigen-expressing targets, LOW CAR T cells had significantly better cytolytic activity than HIGH CAR T cells (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01 at the higher E:T ratios in both HPB-ALL and DND.41 tumor cells). In addition, LOW CAR T cells expressed significantly higher levels of early activation (CD25) and degranulation (CD107a) markers and higher levels of immune checkpoint receptors (PD-1 and LAG3), compared with HIGH CAR T cells (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively). The percentage of CD8+ T cells and the percentage of cells with expression of CD127 were similar between LOW and HIGH CAR T cells (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. S1d). Both CD4+ and CD8+ LOW CAR T cells had a significantly higher frequency of effector memory cell phenotype (Fig. 1f) and higher levels of secretion of proinflammatory cytokines (IFNγ, TNFα) (Fig. 1g), compared with HIGH CAR or UT T cells after 24 h of coculture with DND.41 tumor cells. Overall, these data indicate the presence of antigen-specific cytotoxicity and the absence of fratricide in short-term cocultures (51Cr-release assay).

When CAR T cells were cultured alone (i.e., in the absence of cancer target cells) on day 4 after transduction, the yields (absolute counts) of LOW and HIGH CAR T cells were lower than the yield of UT T cells; however, the percentages of CD4+ and CD8+ CAR T cells were not statistically different between UT, LOW CAR, and HIGH CAR T cells (Supplementary Fig. S2a). In addition, compared with UT T cells, both LOW and HIGH CAR T cells had higher expression of activation markers (PD-1, LAG3, CD25) and lower expression of CD127. Expression of activation markers (PD-1, LAG3) was significantly higher and expression of CD127 was significantly lower in LOW CAR T cells than in HIGH CAR T cells, which indicates fratricidal activity in LOW CAR T cells—perhaps against T cells that retained expression of CD127 (Supplementary Fig. S2b, c).

We then questioned whether fratricide would compromise the proliferation and accumulation of anti-CD127 CAR T cells in the presence of target-antigen-expressing cancer cells. We performed repeated antigen stimulation every 72 h with CD127-expressing T-ALL cancer cells DND.41, HPB-ALL, and Jurkat (Supplementary Fig. S2d). After 3 rounds of stimulation on day 9, LOW CAR T-cell numbers were consistently higher ( ~ 3-fold increase, p < 0.001 in DND.41 and HPB-ALL and p < 0.05 in Jurkat tumor cells) than HIGH CAR T-cell numbers, in the presence of all target cells tested. However, the percentages of CD8+, PD-1+, central memory cell, and effector memory cell phenotypes were not statistically different between LOW and HIGH CAR T cells (Supplementary Figs. S1e and S2e). We then increased the level of antigen stress by adding antigen-expressing targets to the culture every 24 h (Supplementary Fig. S2f). LOW CAR T cells again had 2- to 3-fold higher expansion than HIGH CAR T cells (p < 0.01 from day 4 to day 8), and the percentages of MYC+, CD8+, and PD-1+ CAR T cells and central memory cell and effector memory cell phenotypes were not statistically different between LOW and HIGH CAR T cells (Supplementary Fig. S2g). Together, these data show that, in the presence of antigen-expressing target cells, LOW CAR T cells have relatively higher levels of activation and proliferation and, consequently, a greater potential for accumulation, compared with HIGH CAR T cells.

Low-affinity anti-CD127 CAR T cells have better antitumor efficacy in vivo than high-affinity CAR T cells, but fratricide after reduction of tumor burden allows tumor relapse

To test the in vivo efficacy of LOW and HIGH CAR T cells, NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mice were intravenously administered 3 × 106 DND.41-effLuc cells; tumor engraftment was confirmed by bioluminescence imaging (BLI). Four days later, on day 0, mice were treated with intravenous administration of 3 × 106 UT, LOW CAR, or HIGH CAR T cells; tumor progression was monitored by weekly BLI (Fig. 2a). LOW CAR T cells were associated with longer survival than UT or HIGH CAR T cells (median survival: 35 vs. 73 vs. 47 days for UT, LOW, and HIGH, respectively; p < 0.001 for LOW vs HIGH; p < 0.0001 for UT vs HIGH and UT vs LOW) (Fig. 2b); however, tumor relapse was observed by BLI imaging starting on day 19 (Fig. 2c,d). To investigate the mechanisms underlying tumor relapse, the in vivo study was repeated, mice were euthanized on day 17, bone marrow was harvested (to evaluate CAR T-cell count, phenotype, and functional status at the time of tumor relapse in the LOW CAR T-cell group) (Fig. 2e). Flow cytometric analysis of bone marrow revealed that the numbers of total tumor cells and total CAR T cells and the percentage of CD4+ CAR T cells were lower in LOW CAR T cell–treated mice than in HIGH CAR T cell–treated mice. Although the differentiation status of CAR T cells (the percentage of central memory and effector memory cell phenotypes) was not statistically different between LOW and HIGH CAR T cells, a higher percentage of LOW CAR T cells expressed CD127; the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) signal was not statistically different between LOW and HIGH CAR T cells. In contrast, expression of the exhaustion markers PD-1, TIM3, and LAG3 was higher in HIGH CAR T cells than in LOW CAR T cells (Fig. 2f), and a higher proportion of HIGH CAR T cells coexpressed all 3 markers (TRIPLE POS), which indicated a relatively higher exhaustive state in HIGH CAR T cells (Fig. 2g). Taken together, these results suggest that LOW CAR T cells may be prone to fratricide because of their higher expression of CD127 after reduction of tumor burden and because of their less-exhausted state, compared with HIGH CAR T cells, at the day-17 time point.

To further our understanding of the dynamics of fratricide, we next conducted a series of in vivo studies involving intravenous administration of cell trace violet (CTV)–labeled CAR T cells in mice without tumor, DND.41 tumor–bearing mice, and autologous peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)–recipient mice. Administration of CAR T cells was followed by early sacrifice (day 5 to 6) and bone marrow harvest to evaluate proliferation by analyzing the MFI of CTV, CAR T-cell count, expression of CD127, and expression of PD-1 for LOW and HIGH CAR T cells (Supplementary Fig. S3a). In NSG mice without tumor, although proliferation was not statistically different between LOW and HIGH CAR T cells (MFI of CTV), T-cell counts and the percentage of cells with expression of CD127 on the cell surface were significantly lower for LOW CAR T cells than for HIGH CAR T cells, suggesting the presence of fratricide among LOW CAR T cells. The percentage of CAR T cells with expression of PD-1 was similar between constructs (Supplementary Fig. S4a, b). In tumor-bearing NSG mice, which received intravenous administration of DND.41 cells 4 days before administration of CAR T cells, LOW CAR T cells achieved higher proliferation (i.e., a significantly lower MFI of CTV) and similar accumulation, percentage of cells expressing CD127, and expression of PD-1 on the cell surface, compared with HIGH CAR T cells (Supplementary Fig. S4c, d). This finding suggests that fratricide among LOW CAR T cells was reduced in the presence of high-antigen-expressing tumor targets. Next, to assess fratricide in the presence of CD127-expressing nontarget bystander PBMCs, NSG mice (pretreated with cyclophosphamide to eliminate murine immune cells) were inoculated with autologous human PBMCs before intravenous administration of CTV-labeled LOW and HIGH CAR T cells at an E:T ratio of 1:10 (Supplementary Figs. S3b,c and S4e). Similar to our observations above, in the presence of autologous CD127–expressing PBMCs, proliferation of LOW CAR T cells was higher than that of HIGH CAR T cells ( ~ 3-fold increase, p < 0.001), resulting in higher accumulation of LOW CAR T cells than HIGH CAR T cells. Furthermore, whereas the percentage of CAR T cells with expression of CD127 and PD-1 was similar between groups (Supplementary Fig. S4f), the percentage of CD127-expressing PBMC target cells (CTV-) was lower for LOW CAR T cells than for HIGH CAR T cells or control CAR T cells (M28Z) (Supplementary Fig. S4g)27,28. These data show that LOW CAR T cells have more-potent cytotoxic effects against antigen-expressing target cells or autologous bystander PBMCs, compared with HIGH CAR T cells.

Collectively, the data from these in vivo studies suggest that LOW CAR T cells are better at controlling tumor burden and prolonging survival of mice than HIGH CAR T cells, although, in the absence of tumor with overexpression of the target antigen, fratricide can reduce the long-term persistence of LOW CAR T cells. HIGH CAR T cells have poor antitumor efficacy because of high levels of exhaustion.

T-ALL patient–derived low-affinity anti-CD127 CAR T cells are efficient at eradicating autologous primary blasts in a patient-derived xenograft model

To evaluate the feasibility of producing CAR T cells from patients with T-ALL, we first characterized the expression of CD127 on primary blasts and normal T cells isolated from PBMCs collected from 21 patients with pediatric or adult T-ALL (Supplementary Table 1). Primary blasts from 14 of 21 patients (66%) had overexpression of CD127; normal T cells from all patients (except patient 8, whose normal T cells were undetectable) had expression of CD127 (Supplementary Fig. S5a). We then performed fluorescence-activated cell sorting to isolate normal T cells (CD45hi CD3+ CD19- CD7+) from malignant T cells (CD45dim CD3- CD19- CD7+) in peripheral blood and bone marrow aspirates from patients with T-ALL (Supplementary Fig. S5b). Next, we transduced low-affinity anti-CD127 CAR into T-ALL patient–derived CD45hi CD3+ T cells obtained from at least 3 patient donors. We then evaluated in vitro phenotypic characteristics and functional activity by evaluating cytokine secretion and 51Cr-release, after coculture with DND.41 cells. Transduction of low-affinity anti-CD127 (LOW) CAR was successful in patient-derived normal T cells. In vitro phenotypic characteristics, antigen-activation-specific secretion of IFNγ, IL2, and TNFα, and cytolytic activity against DND.41 target cells were similar to those for CAR T cells derived from healthy donors (Fig. 3a–c). In addition, when patient-derived LOW CAR T cells were cocultured with autologous primary blasts ex vivo, expression of CD69, PD-1, CD25, and CD107α on effector cells was consistent with our previous observations. Measurement of DAPI+ cell frequency in LOW CAR and UT T cells revealed that LOW CAR T cells were associated with higher percentages of antigen-specific lysis of target primary blasts, compared with UT T cells (Fig. 3d). To facilitate the testing of efficacy in vivo, we cultured the sorted primary blasts (CD45dim CD3- CD7+) ex vivo in the presence of IL7 and IL15 to expand the number of blasts and administered them to NSG mice; to evaluate engraftment, their presence in spleen and bone marrow was assessed 30 days later (Supplementary Fig. S5c, d). After confirmation of the engraftment of CD127+ T-ALL blasts in NSG mice, we established a patient-derived xenograft mouse model by intravenously inoculating mice with 1 × 106 primary blasts followed by no treatment (blasts only) or intravenous administration of 5 × 105 autologous UT or autologous LOW CAR T cells on day 30 (Fig. 3e). Tumor burden was monitored by twice-weekly measurement of body weights, and survival was assessed. Although primary blasts were not as aggressive as the T-ALL cancer cells DND.41, median survival of untreated (blasts only) or autologous UT T cell–treated mice ranged from 66 to 80 days; however, all autologous LOW CAR T cell–treated mice had significantly longer survival—until day 120, when the experiment was terminated (median survival: 66 vs. 80 vs. not reached days for blasts only, autologous UT, and autologous LOW, respectively; p < 0.05 for autologous LOW vs autologous UT; p < 0.0001 for autologous LOW vs blasts only) (Fig. 3f, g). Analysis of spleen, liver, and bone marrow harvested from mice revealed that autologous LOW CAR T cell–treated mice had a higher percentage of T cells and a lower percentage of residual blasts, compared with mice treated with control CAR T cells (Fig. 3h). Expression of CD127 was lower in both proportion (%) and intensity (MFI) in residual tumor cells retrieved from spleen and liver from LOW CAR T cell–treated mice, compared with UT T cell–treated mice, although this relationship was not observed in residual tumor cells from bone marrow (Fig. 3i). Overall, these data demonstrate that the production of LOW CAR T cells from patients with T-ALL is feasible and that these CAR T cells are effective at reducing tumor burden in a patient-derived xenograft mouse model, both of which support their development as a potential therapeutic option for patients with T-ALL.

CD127 gene knockout in anti-CD127 CAR T cells prevents fratricide without affecting T-cell phenotype and function

To eliminate fratricide among anti-CD127 CAR T cells, we first performed CD127 gene knockout in donor T cells using CRISPR-Cas9 technology. Multiple single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting CD127 had equivalent knockout efficiency at depleting expression of surface antigen, which was as much as 3-fold lower than the original expression in healthy donor–derived T cells (Supplementary Fig. S6a). The knockout efficiency of the sgRNA of choice was validated in multiple donor-derived T cells (Fig. 4a), followed by CAR transduction the next day. 51Cr-release cytotoxicity assay showed that cytotoxicity against DND.41 and HPB-ALL target cells was similar among wild-type and knockout formats of UT, LOW CAR, and HIGH CAR T cells (Fig. 4b). Next, we performed an in vitro fratricide assay where CFSE-labeled autologous T cells with or without CD127 gene knockout were cocultured with CAR T cells for 5 days (Fig. 4c). Flow cytometric analysis of CFSE⁺ target cells revealed increased DAPI⁺ population in CD127⁺ cells co-cultured with LOW and HIGH CAR T cells, indicating antigen-dependent fratricide (Fig. 4d, left). DAPI⁺ population was minimal in UT controls. In contrast, CD127⁻ target cells exhibited limited cell death across all groups, confirming the specificity of CD127 CAR-mediated killing. Moreover, residual CD127⁺ target cells displayed reduced surface CD127 following co-culture with LOW CAR T cells (Fig. 4d, right). Together, these results demonstrate that CD127 CAR T cells selectively eliminate autologous T cells CD127⁺ and that CD127 gene knockout hinders cytotoxicity/fratricide.

Consistent with our previous observations, both wild-type LOW and wild-type HIGH CAR T cells had lower accumulation than wild-type UT T cells. In contrast, cell counts of knockout LOW and knockout HIGH CAR T cells were significantly higher than cell counts of their wild-type counterparts and were also 1.5-fold higher (p < 0.001 for WT LOW vs KO LOW; p < 0.01 for WT LOW vs WT HIGH; p < 0.05 for WT HIGH vs KO HIGH) than cell counts of knockout UT T cells. The percentage of cells with expression of CD127 was also statistically different between wild-type and knockout LOW and HIGH CAR T cells, although there was no statistical difference in CD8+ or PD-1+ cell frequency or memory cell phenotype signatures, which suggests that any fratricidal effects were eliminated by CD127 gene knockout (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. S6b). Finally, proliferation and accumulation were significantly higher (p < 0.05 for WT LOW vs KO LOW in HPB-ALL cells) in knockout LOW CAR T cells than in wild-type LOW CAR T cells upon repeated exposure of antigen to HPB-ALL and DND.41 target cells (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. S6c). These data suggest that CD127 gene knockout in anti-CD127 CAR T cells by use of CRISPR-Cas9 can prevent fratricide and thus improve accumulation of CAR T cells in vitro without affecting cell phenotype or cytotoxic function.

CD127 gene knockout enhances in vivo antitumor activity of low-affinity anti-CD127 CAR T cells but not high-affinity anti-CD127 CAR T cells

To investigate the in vivo efficacy of CD127 gene knockout anti-CD127 CAR T cells, we established a xenograft mouse model by inoculating NSG mice with 3 × 106 DND.41-effLuc cells via tail vein injection; tumor engraftment was confirmed by BLI. Four days later, 3 × 106 wild-type or knockout CAR T cells were administered via tail vein injection; tumor progression was monitored by weekly BLI (Fig. 5a). Both wild-type LOW and knockout LOW CAR T cells were associated with significantly longer survival than wild-type HIGH and knockout HIGH CAR T cells (median survival: 26 vs. 26.5 vs. 45 vs. not reached days for UT WT, UT KO, HIGH WT, HIGH KO, LOW WT and LOW KO respectively; p < 0.05 for HIGH WT vs HIGH KO; p < 0.001 for HIGH WT vs LOW WT); however, only knockout LOW CAR T cells eliminated tumor cells without allowing tumors to relapse. In contrast, although knockout HIGH CAR T cells were associated with significantly longer survival than wild-type HIGH CAR T cells, they did not prevent tumor relapse (Fig. 5b, c and Supplementary Fig. S7a). To confirm the efficacy of knockout LOW CAR T cells, we repeated the in vivo study with a lower dose of CAR T cells: 1 × 106 wild-type LOW or knockout LOW CAR T cells were administered via tail vein injection 4 days after 3 × 106 DND.41 tumor cells were established in mice (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. S8a, b). In this reduced-dose setting, knockout LOW CAR T cells were associated with significantly longer survival than wild-type LOW CAR T cells (median survival, 95 vs. 61 days, p < 0.05) (Fig. 5e). Although both wild-type LOW and knockout LOW CAR T cells eliminated tumor burden by day 5 to 6 after administration of CAR T cells, only knockout LOW CAR T cells were able to maintain tumor eradication, without tumor relapse, up to day 80 after treatment. In contrast, wild-type LOW CAR T cells were associated with tumor relapse at approximately day 19 (Fig. 5f). Further assessment of the presence of cancer cells, total T cells, and CAR T cells in various organs, including peripheral blood, bone marrow, spleen, liver, and brain, harvested at the time of sacrifice of mice that survived to day 53 to 126 revealed that knockout LOW CAR T cells were present in significantly higher numbers in peripheral blood and spleen compared with wild-type LOW CAR T cells ( ~ 7-fold increase in blood and ~14-fold in spleen; p < 0.05) (Fig. 5g, h, Supplementary Figs. S7b, c and S8c). An intriguing observation is the substantial reduction in the expression of CD127 on residual tumor cells (range, 4% to 44%) collected from wild-type LOW CAR T cell–treated mice at the experimental endpoint (day 76). These residual tumor cells exhibited a markedly decreased MFI of 300, compared with before CAR treatment, when the MFI was 2,362. In instances where tumor was completely eradicated, expression of CD127 was undetectable. In contrast, knockout LOW CAR T cell–treated mice that were euthanized at day 126 had a detectable population of residual tumor cells (range, 5% to 28%). Furthermore, loss of CD127 antigen on these tumor cells appeared to be organ dependent, as tumor cells in the intestine retained expression of CD127 (MFI = 331) (Fig. 5g). These findings indicate that tumor relapse in wild-type LOW CAR T cell–treated mice is primarily driven by CAR T-cell fratricide, a challenge that has been addressed in knockout LOW CAR T cells through the knockout of the CD127. In contrast, the delayed tumor relapse observed in knockout LOW CAR T cell–treated mice can be attributed to antigen escape by tumor cells, highlighting a distinct mechanism of resistance that requires further investigation and intervention.

To evaluate the performance of anti-CD127 CAR T cells in a clinically relevant scenario—expression of CD127 may vary significantly among patients—we assessed the efficacy of wild-type and knockout LOW and HIGH CAR T cells, with similar phenotypic characteristics, against HPB-ALL leukemic cells that express CD127 at a similar percentage as DND.41 cells but that have a lower antigen density, as demonstrated by the difference in MFI signal (3-fold lower than that of DND.41 cells) (Fig. 1c). NSG mice were intravenously administered 3 × 106 HPB-ALL-effLuc cells on day -4, followed by intravenous administration of 3 × 106 CAR T cells 4 days later (Supplementary Fig. S8d, e). Assessment of cancer cells (CD3+ CD45lo) and effector cells (CD3+ CD45hi) in spleen showed that knockout LOW CAR T cells had higher accumulation of total T cells and CAR T cells, fewer residual cancer cells, and a significantly lower percentage of CD127-expressing residual cancer cells ( ~ 2-fold increase in KO HIGH vs KO LOW, p < 0.05), compared with knockout HIGH CAR T cells (Supplementary Fig. S8f, g). These data suggest that, despite heterogenous expression of antigen on target cells or the low dose of effector cells, knockout LOW CAR T cells, which resist fratricide and accumulate at high levels, can eradicate CD127+ T-ALL cancer cells and prolong survival. Although CD127 knockout did not impair T-cell activity in our experimental settings, the role of IL7R in long-term T-cell maintenance warrants further evaluation in extended models to fully define its impact on memory formation and persistence.

Naturally selected CD127-negative/low anti-CD127 LOW CAR T cells without gene knockout have equivalent antitumor efficacy as LOW CAR T cells

Gene knockout in donor T cells using CRISPR-Cas9 technology is a costly step in manufacturing CAR T cells. In addition, the efficiency of gene knockout varies by patient. Natural selection of CAR T cells by means of prolonged culture is proposed as an alternative approach to manufacturing CAR T cells with fratricidal effect. The production of naturally selected CD7-negative anti-CD7–targeted CAR T cells has been shown to be feasible, with superior antitumor efficacy, compared with nonselected CAR T cells29,30. Although CD7 is a pan–T-cell marker, in contrast to CD7, CD127 plays a crucial role in the proliferation and survival of T cells13,14; therefore, avoiding complete knockout of CD127 in CAR T cells is desirable. In a natural selection approach optimized protocol, by extending coculturing up to day 10 (instead of day 7) after CAR transduction in the presence of IL2, we achieved naturally selected CD127-negative anti-CD127 CAR T cells (LOW NS) (Fig. 6a). LOW NS CAR T cells had higher accumulation, with up to 20-fold expansion, and were viable, even after 10 to 14 days of culture (Fig. 6b). Because fratricide in culture results in stimulation of CD127, NS CAR T cells have low expression of CD127, a high proportion of the CD8+ cytotoxic phenotype, and low expression of PD-1+ (Supplementary Fig. S9a, b). On investigation, LOW wild-type and LOW NS CAR T cells had similar percentages of CAR T-cell memory cell phenotypes (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Fig. S9b) and similar antitumor efficacy in a 51Cr-release cytotoxicity assay against CD127+-expressing DND.41 G cells (Fig. 6c). In vivo in NSG mice established with DND.41 G tumor, NS LOW CAR T cells had similar anti-tumor efficacy as wild-type LOW CAR T cells (Fig. 6d–f). Flow cytometric analysis of CAR T cells isolated from bone marrow and spleen revealed lower frequencies of highly exhausted phenotypes (co-expressing PD1, CD39, LAG3, and TIM3) in the NS group (Fig. 6g) (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis of wild-type, naturally selected and knockout LOW CAR T cells differentiate phenotypic characterization

We conducted single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis to elucidate the phenotypic characterization of CD127-depleted CD127-targeted fratricidal CAR T cells (wild-type, naturally selected, and knockout) and compared these with control (nonfratricidal) mesothelin-targeted CAR M28Z wild-type cells as control CAR T cells (Supplementary Fig. S11b)31. By the use of a uniform manifold approximation and projection plot, CD127-negative CAR T cells were clustered into 10 subpopulations on the basis of cluster-specific highly differential gene expression and T-cell markers (Supplementary Fig. S12a, b). For instance, cluster 8 comprises cells specifically expressing CCL4, CCL3, CCL4L2, CSF2, IFNG, GZMB, and IL2RA—the genes characteristic of cytotoxic T lymphocytes. This cluster is present across all 3 effector groups: wild-type, naturally selected, and knockout. In contrast, cluster 6 is characterized by the expression of TSHZ2, PDE7B, TP63, LDLRAD4, AHI1, and LINC01934, which are predominantly found in exhausted T cells. This cluster was highly enriched in both wild-type and naturally selected CAR T cells, compared with knockout and control CAR T cells, which suggests that fratricidal activity and sustained T-cell activation likely contributed to exhaustion at the time of harvesting. Cluster 3 was present in wild-type and naturally selected CAR T cells but was absent in knockout CAR T cells and was defined by the expression of CCL1, TCF7, XCL1, MT2A, CRTAM, ISG15, and XCL2. These genes are characteristic of memory CD8+ T cells and naive-like T cells; this was confirmed by flow cytometric analysis (Supplementary Fig. S7c) from in vivo studies, which revealed a higher proportion of naive CAR T cells in wild-type CAR T cells than in knockout CAR T cells.

In addition, cluster 4, defined by ADAM19 and ATP8B4, represents activated T cells and was present in all 3 effector groups. Cluster 5, characterized by GAPDH, LDHA, TUBA1B, ENO1, TPI1, and MIF, represents proliferating T cells and was exclusively found in knockout CAR T cells (Supplementary Fig. S12c).

Bulk RNA-sequencing analysis revealed similar gene expression between wild-type and naturally selected CAR T cells, and the IFNα and IFNγ gene sets specifically were enriched in wild-type CAR T cells, compared with naturally selected CAR T cells (Supplementary Fig. S12d). Although there were minor differences between wild-type and knockout CAR T cells, no genes were significantly enriched (fold change >1) (Supplementary Fig. S12e). Naturally selected and knockout CAR T cells had the most pronounced differences on bulk RNA-sequencing analysis, with 7 effector-specific genes significantly upregulated (Supplementary Fig. S12e). Downstream pathways associated with IL7R signaling, including JAK/STAT, mTOR, and IL2/STAT5, remained intact and were enriched in knockout CAR T cells, compared with wild-type and naturally selected CAR T cells (Supplementary Fig. S12f). RNA-seq analysis revealed no major transcriptional changes in canonical IL7R-associated genes (JAK/STAT/PI3K/mTOR) after CD127 knockout, suggesting that IL7R deletion did not markedly alter these pathways at the RNA level under the culture conditions used.

Dasatinib boosts CAR T-cell fitness and efficacy and provides reversible in vivo control of LOW CAR T-cell activity

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as dasatinib can reversibly suppress TCR/CAR signaling, reducing activation-induced cell death and exhaustion during CAR T-cell manufacturing32,33. dasatinib improved CAR T-cell yield, resulting in a ~ 120-fold expansion compared to CAR T cells manufactured without dasatinib ( ~ 20-fold), after 10 days co-culture (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Fig. S13). To investigate whether dasatinib treatment during CAR T-cell production can enhance CAR T-cell fitness and functionality, we compared expansion, cytotoxicity, phenotype, and exhaustion profiles of CAR T cells manufactured with or without co-culture with dasatinib. Following withdrawal of dasatinib (LOW+Dasa WD), LOW CAR T cells cytotoxicity is preserved across a wide range of effector to target ratios (Fig. 7b). Co-culturing with dasatinib maintained relatively higher proportion of CAR T cells in naïve phenotype (76% in LOW + Dasa vs 28% in LOW), suggesting improved preservation of a less differentiated state (Fig. 7c). In addition, the proportion of memory phenotype CAR T cells remained similar to the unstimulated UT control(76% in LOW + Dasa vs 67% in UT), suggesting that dasatinib mitigates fratricide-mediated differentiation to effector phenotype (Fig. 7d), also evident by substantially lower expression of CD39, PD-1, LAG-3, and TIM-3 exhaustion markers when CAR T cells are co-cultured with dasatinib, relative to culturing CAR T cells alone (Fig. 7e) (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Collectively, these results indicate that dasatinib treatment during CAR T-cell manufacturing enhances expansion, preserves a naïve/memory phenotype, and reduces exhaustion marker expression, leading to improved in vitro fitness of LOW CAR T cells. Next, we investigated whether this manufacturing protocol could improve the antitumor efficacy of LOW CAR T cells in vivo. NSG mice were engrafted with DND.41 G leukemia cells and treated with a single dose of 1 × 106 UT, LOW, LOW NS, or LOW+Dasa CAR T cells, (Fig. 7f). Mice treated with LOW+Dasa CAR T cells showed significantly reduced tumor growth compared with other groups (p < 0.0001 vs NS and UT; p < 0.05 vs LOW), as evidenced by lower bioluminescent signals over time (Fig. 7g). This improved tumor control likely reflects preservation of naïve and less exhausted CAR T-cell phenotype compared to manufacturing with IL-2 alone. Correspondingly, Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed prolonged survival in the LOW+Dasa group (median survival: 30, 44, 55 and 70 days for UT, LOW, LOW NS, and LOW+Dasa; p < 0.001 vs UT and NS; 0.05 vs LOW) (Fig. 7h). These results demonstrate that dasatinib treatment during CAR T-cell manufacturing enhances in vivo antitumor efficacy and prolongs survival.

Building on our findings that dasatinib during manufacturing mitigates fratricide and reduces activation, we next evaluated its potential to temporarily and reversibly suppress CAR T-cell activity in vivo. To this end, we established a xenograft model by injecting NSG mice with DND.41-effLuc leukemia cells, followed by administration of LOW CAR T cells on day 4 via tail vein injection. Mice were treated with oral dasatinib (50 mg/kg) twice daily for up to 8 days post–CAR T-cell infusion (Fig. 7i). Assessment of tumor burden by serial BLI showed that treatment with dasatinib arrested LOW CAR T-cell function, reflected by tumor burden progression. In contrast, treatment with LOW CAR T cells and vehicle was associated with a significant reduction in tumor burden by day 8 ( ~ 1 log₁₀ difference; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 7j). After discontinuation of dasatinib treatment, LOW CAR T-cell activity was restored, and tumor burden began to gradually reduce, reaching a level similar to the CAR T-cell (vehicle)–treated group by day 15 (Fig. 7j). Necropsy, performed on day 9, revealed that dasatinib treatment and vehicle were associated with a similar number of CAR T cells in harvested spleens of mice (Fig. 7k), confirming that dasatinib did not affect accumulation of CAR T cells in vivo. These results collectively support use of dasatinib during manufacturing and potential use in vivo as a safety agent.

In addition, we evaluated the effect of dexamethasone on CD127 CAR T cells in vitro. Exposure to dexamethasone (500 nM) resulted in reduced CAR⁺ T-cell numbers, compared with untreated controls, at 72 h (Supplementary Fig. S13d). These observations are consistent with the known effects of corticosteroids on CAR T cells34,35.

Discussion

The primary limitations of CAR T-cell therapy for T-cell malignancies include the restricted availability of target antigens because of shared antigens between blasts and T cells, associated CAR T-cell fratricide from on-target/off-tumor toxicity, and ensuing compromised functional persistence of CAR T cells, resulting in tumor relapse. We investigated CD127 as a target for T-ALL and to develop an CD127-directed CAR T-cell therapy. Furthermore, we systematically compared different approaches (CAR affinity, wild-type, knockout, natural selection, and dasatinib co-culture) to determine the benefits and limitations of each method for manufacturing CD127-targeted CAR T-cell therapy.

Our decision to select CD127 as a target antigen was based on the following observations: (a) gain-of-function mutations in the gene encoding the CD127 subunit drive leukemogenesis10,15,16,36; (b) whereas IL7R signaling is induced by IL7-mediated heterodimerization of CD127 and common gamma chain, mutant IL7R can signal independently of IL7 and gamma chain, owing to disulfide bond formation and homodimerization of 2 mutant CD127 chains, which leads to cell transformation and leukemogenesis13,37,38; and (c) given the dynamic regulation of expression of IL7R on healthy T cells, which is upregulated in resting cells and downregulated upon activation, we postulated that both manufacturing of CAR T cells and establishment of persistent antitumor activity are feasible without major limitations from fratricide. Targeting an antigen that is critical for tumor aggressiveness and tumor progression limits the potential for loss of antigen, rendering the tumor less capable of growth and dissemination. We and others have reported that tumor cell expression of CD127 plays a role in the aggressiveness of solid tumors; thus, CD127-targeted CAR T-cell therapy can be used to treat other cancers, in addition to T-ALL39.

With the knowledge that CD127 is expressed on normal T cells and T-cell blasts, we investigated low-affinity and high-affinity CD127-targeted CAR T cells in vitro and in vivo. Low-affinity CARs preferentially recognize tumor cells with higher antigen expression while sparing normal cells with lower antigen expression. In contrast, high-affinity CARs may indiscriminately target both malignant and healthy T cells, leading to toxicity, specifically after eradication of or reduction in tumor burden wherein normal tissue can be exposed to CAR T cells at a high E:T ratio. Studies have shown that low-affinity CARs targeting EGFR, CD123, and CD38 provide discrimination between tumor and normal tissues that express these antigens at lower levels40–42. Because of their lower on-rate, low-affinity CARs require bivalent interactions to stably bind target cells, a condition more readily met in cells with a high antigen density. Our observations demonstrate that LOW CAR T cells had better cytotoxicity against cancer cells with high antigen expression, such as DND.41 and HPB-ALL, compared with HIGH CAR T cells. Although the differences in cytotoxicity between high- and low-affinity CAR T cells were modest in vitro, in vivo experiments revealed that low-affinity CAR T cells provided greater tumor control and prolonged survival, confirming that affinity tuning confers a meaningful therapeutic advantage in a disease-relevant setting.

The better targeting of high-antigen-expressing cells by LOW CAR T cells was further reflected by the higher levels of release of early activation markers and cytokine secretion upon coculture with DND.41 cells and the higher proliferation rate, corresponding to the antigen intensity of target cells, compared with HIGH CAR T cells, and reduced expression of exhaustion markers. Other studies have reported that low-affinity CARs targeting ErbB2 and ICAM-1 elicit equivalent or superior antitumor responses, compared with high-affinity CARs, in vivo41,43,44. Building on this concept, we applied affinity modulation in the context of T-ALL, where fratricide remains a major challenge. Here, we show that a low-affinity CAR design enhances efficacy by reducing self-recognition due to downregulation of CD127 after activation, and combining this strategy with transient dasatinib modulation provides additive benefits. This integrated approach mitigates off-tumor activity while improving CAR persistence and performance.

In the context of CAR T-cell therapy for hematologic malignancies, an antigen density of <3000 molecules per cell is generally classified as low antigen expression45, which is a key factor that contributes to tumor immune escape after treatment with CAR T cells. Although only a limited number of studies have quantified CD127 antigen density in T-ALL, the available reports suggest that >10,000 molecules per cell indicates high antigen expression; normal T cells have <5000 molecules per cell6,46. The CD127 expression cutoff percentage and intensity on T-ALL blasts in patients to be recruited for a trial of anti-CD127 CAR T cells are yet to be determined. However, approximately 70% of patients with T-ALL exhibit significantly elevated expression of CD127 on blast cells, compared with their counterparts with healthy T cells9. The shorter receptor-ligand interaction duration associated with low-affinity binding may enhance serial triggering, leading to better signaling through proliferative pathways. In contrast, high-affinity CARs and T-cell receptors may impair T-cell function by prolonging interactions, thereby limiting serial triggering, which is essential for an optimal T-cell response47,48, and this may, in turn, result in T-cell exhaustion, functional impairment, and tumor relapse. In our in vivo models, although both LOW and HIGH CAR T cell–treated mice eventually experienced tumor relapse, LOW CAR T cells were associated with better antitumor efficacy, lower tumor burden, and longer survival, by an additional 26 days, compared with HIGH CAR T cells.

To reduce the influence of fratricide, prolong the functional persistence of CAR T cells, and thereby reduce the incidence of antigen escape, we developed fratricide-resistant knockout LOW CAR T cells by genetically depleting CD127 by use of CRISPR-Cas9 and by retrovirally transducing CARs into bulk T cells without sorting for CD127-negative cells. Given that IL7 is critical for normal development, viability, and proliferation of T cells13,14, we anticipated functional consequences of depletion of CD127. Surprisingly, we did not observe any detrimental effects on T cells due to CD127 depletion. Furthermore, our single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis revealed that key downstream pathways, including JAK/STAT5 and PI3K/mTOR, remained intact even after CD127 knockout. As expected, CD127 naturally selected CAR T cells had similarities to CD127 wild-type CAR T cells; manufacturing, proliferation, and accumulation of CAR T cells are highly feasible by any method adopted. As JAK/STAT and PI3K/mTOR signaling are largely regulated posttranslationally and as our assays were performed in the presence of IL2, the RNA-seq profiles likely reflect transcriptional steadiness, rather than pathway activity. Analyses under resting conditions could further delineate the impact of IL7R deletion on downstream signaling and long-term T-cell maintenance. In addition, our in vivo data demonstrate that knockout LOW CAR T cells successfully achieved long-term persistence by sustaining a relapse-free state for >80 days after a low-dose infusion of 1 × 106 CAR T cells. In contrast, wild-type LOW CAR T cells were associated with early relapse, after 15 days, and a median survival that was approximately 30 days shorter than for knockout LOW CAR T cells. Although knockout LOW CAR T cells are efficient at killing cancer cells, variable achievement of knockout and the cost of producing autologous knockout LOW CAR T cells are potential limitations. Our investigation of CAR T-cell manufacturing with dasatinib, a method already in practice33,49, reduced fratricide and improved CAR T-cell yield, fitness, and function. Furthermore, we provide evidence supporting the use of dasatinib as a temporarily and reversibly acting pharmacologic agent to suppress CAR T-cell activity in vivo. Our in vitro data confirm that dexamethasone reduces CAR T-cell viability, consistent with its clinical use to manage CAR T-cell–associated toxicities. Together, these findings support pharmacologic strategies to modulate CAR T-cell activity when clinically indicated.

To investigate the mechanisms of tumor relapse, we assessed CD127 antigen density (MFI) on tumor cells before and after treatment with CAR T cells. Both knockout LOW and wild-type LOW CAR T cells effectively eliminated CD127-expressing tumor cells; however, low-antigen-expressing tumor cells persisted within the bone marrow at early stages and within the central nervous system at later time points ( > 120 days after a single low dose of CAR T-cell treatment). These protected niches likely served as reservoirs for relapse, allowing tumor cells to reemerge.

Previous reports have described the natural selection of CD7-negative anti-CD7 CAR T cells with enhanced efficacy29,30. However, unlike CD7, CD127 plays a critical role in the proliferation and survival of T cells13,14, making its complete depletion undesirable, with regard to preservation of CAR T-cell function. By extending in vitro coculturing up to 10 days after CAR transduction in the presence of IL2, we successfully generated LOW NS (naturally selected CD127-negative) anti-CD127 CAR T cells. Our investigation and optimization of a protocol to produce naturally selected LOW CAR T cells demonstrated the feasibility of this approach and established the higher accumulation of CAR T cells and equivalent antitumor efficacy, both in vitro and in vivo, compared with knockout LOW CAR T cells. Our in vitro data demonstrate that LOW NS CAR T cells have better proliferation and better cytotoxicity against tumor cells, compared with conventional LOW wild-type CAR constructs. Although prolonged in vitro expansion raises concerns about potential T-cell exhaustion, antitumor efficacy was unaffected. Single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis revealed enrichment of CD8+ effector memory cell subtypes during the generation of naturally selected CAR T cells; this finding was corroborated by flow cytometric analysis, which revealed a higher population of CD45RA+ CD62L− effector MYC+ (CAR + ) T cells, compared with the other groups.

The most clinically advanced CAR T-cell target for T-ALL is CD7, with 20 ongoing clinical trials targeting CD7 worldwide. CD7 is a pan–T-cell marker that is expressed on 90% of relapsed or refractory T-ALL blasts and >99% of normal T and natural killer lymphocytes. Gene-editing technologies, including CRISPR-Cas9–mediated knockouts and base-editing tools, have significantly enhanced CAR T-cell engineering by improving the survival, functionality, and tumor specificity of CAR T cells50. Clinical trials that incorporate these innovations—such as protein expression blocker anti-CD7 CAR T cells and CRISPR-engineered constructs—have demonstrated high rates of remission, long-term persistence, and manageable toxicity profiles51,52.

Another clinically tested CAR T-cell target for T-ALL is CD5, which is expressed on >70% of relapsed or refractory T-ALL blasts and 85% of normal T cells. CD5 CAR T cells evade fratricide by sparing normal T cells because of CD5 degradation53. In contrast, other T-cell markers, such as CD2, pose additional challenges in CAR T-cell therapy because of their shared expression on both malignant and normal T cells. To overcome this challenge, researchers developed a universal CAR (UCAR2) by introducing biallelic deletions of CD2 and TRAC, which prevents fratricide and graft-versus-host disease. However, deletion of CD2 reduced the efficacy of UCAR2 T cells, leading to reduced T-cell activation and cytotoxicity54. In addition, engineering CARs in alternative immune cell types, such as natural killer cells or macrophages, eliminates the risk of self-targeting, as these cells lack shared antigens with T cells55–57. Furthermore, targeting tumor-associated antigens with low expression on normal T cells, such as CD99, CCR9, and CD1a (<10% expression on T cells), has been reported to enhance selectivity, spare normal T cells, and minimize on-target/off-tumor effects58–61.

In the manufacture of autologous CAR T cells, T cells are isolated from peripheral blood via apheresis; however, because of overlapping immunophenotypes, residual T-ALL blasts may be co-collected alongside healthy T cells. On the basis of our experience, we successfully enriched healthy T cells using fluorescence-activated cell sorting, leveraging their higher expression of CD3hi and CD45hi; T-ALL blasts exhibited downregulated expression of CD3neg and reduced expression of CD45dim. In addition, T-ALL blasts had poor viability under CAR T-cell culture conditions, particularly in the presence of high concentrations of cytokines, further limiting their persistence during the manufacturing of CAR T cells.

Lymphopenia is a frequently observed adverse event during CAR T-cell therapy that necessitated the development of effective prevention strategies to enhance the safety of treatment. Dasatinib, a potent inhibitor of lymphocyte-specific protein tyrosine kinase, disrupts the phosphorylation of CD3ζ and effectively abrogates signaling in CAR constructs that incorporate the CD28-CD3ζ activation domain62. Our investigations indicate that dasatinib functions as a reversible ON/OFF switch for CD127-targeted CAR T cells in T-ALL without compromising viability, proliferation, or effector cell accumulation. Given its reversible nature, dasatinib represents a promising safety agent that can be used in clinical settings, even after tumor clearance, to mitigate the risk of on-target/off-tumor toxicity. Dasatinib has been reported to temporarily and reversibly suppress CAR T-cell activity, acting as a pharmacologic on/off switch32,62 and as a manufacturing modulator that enhances product quality49. In this study, we leveraged these complementary properties in the context of a fratricide-prone CAR targeting T-ALL, integrating transient pharmacologic control in vivo with an optimized manufacturing approach. Postinfusion dasatinib is envisioned as a short, event-driven intervention to transiently pause CAR signaling; chronic use at CAR-inhibitory levels is not intended, given potential suppression of TCR. Prior studies have documented rapid and reversible inhibition of CAR activity in vivo with brief exposure, with full recovery upon drug withdrawal32,62.

In conclusion, CD127 is a promising therapeutic target for T-ALL. Low-affinity CD127-targeted CAR T cells demonstrated strong translational potential as a treatment option for patients with relapsed or refractory T-ALL. We are currently conducting Investigational New Drug studies, with the goal of initiating a phase I clinical trial in the near term.

Methods

Animal model

All animal studies were conducted under protocols reviewed and approved by the Memorial Sloan Kettering Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval number: 08-01-001). Mice were housed in a temperature-controlled room (22 ± 1 °C) under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle (08:00–20:00) with unrestricted access to food and water. To develop the xenograft mouse model of T-ALL, 6- to 8-week-old female NSG mice (NOD-SCID-Il2rg-/-) (The Jackson Laboratory) were used in all in vivo studies. All procedures were performed under approved Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocols at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center. Mice were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation, followed by cervical dislocation either upon reaching approved humane endpoints (e.g., signs of distress in survival studies) or at predefined experimental endpoints for tissue collection and necropsy (days 5, 9, 16, and 17, as indicated for each experiment).

Cell lines and cell culture

Human T-ALL cell lines such as DND.41 (obtained from DSMZ, Braunschweig, Germany, ACC525), HPB-ALL (DSMZ, ACC483), Jurkat (ATCC, TIB-152), MOLT4 (ATCC, CRL-1582), and Loucy (ATCC, CRL-2629) were cultured in T175 flasks for 2–3 days before passaging using 48–50 mL of RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS), 2mM L-glutamine, penicillin, and streptomycin. DND.41 and HPB-ALL cells were retrovirally transduced to express the enhanced firefly luciferase fusion protein (DND.41-effLuc and HPB-ALL-effLuc). All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and cell-surface expression of target antigens.

Cell retrieval from tissue

Mouse-derived peripheral blood, spleen, and bone marrow were collected using a syringe plug and a 40-micron filter mesh to isolate cell suspensions, were rinsed with unsupplemented RPMI 1640 and lysed with 2 to 3 mL of ACK lysing buffer (Gibco, lot 2451175) for 5 min, and were washed twice with 0.5% FCS/phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Other organs, such as lung, kidney, brain, and liver, were harvested and filtered through 40-micron filter mesh using a syringe plug and resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium. These suspensions were washed once with RPMI and then enzymatically digested using an enzyme cocktail consisting of 25 units/mL DNase (Roche, 11284932001), 2 mg/mL Collagenase D (Sigma-Aldrich, 64311323), and 0.5% Trypsin EDTA (Gibco, 2323415) for 45 to 60 min, depending on the tissue type, with shaking at 180 to 200 rpm at 37 °C. After digestion, cell debris removal solution (Miltenyi Biotec, lot 5221108249) was used to isolate live cells, which were later used for flow cytometric analysis.

BLI analysis

To quantify the T-ALL tumor burden, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane before administration of a single intraperitoneal dose of D-luciferin 150 mg/kg (GoldBio LUCK-100) for the firefly or effLuc reporter gene (provided by P. Hwu, Moffitt Cancer Center, Florida, USA). BLI data were acquired weekly using an In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) and analyzed using Living Image software (version 2.60, PerkinElmer). The BLI signal was reported as the average total flux (photons per second) of both the ventral and the dorsal BLI signal, calculated using Microsoft Excel and analyzed using Prism software (GraphPad)63.

Gammaretroviral vector construction and plasmid production

The nucleotide sequences encoding scFvs targeting CD127 with high affinity (OSE703-H) and low affinity (OSE703-L) were obtained from Ose Immunotherapeutics. These nucleotide sequences were modified to link human CD28 and human CD3z domains to encode second-generation CARs: OSE703LH-28Z (HIGH) and OSE703HL-28Z (LOW), respectively. Similarly, second-generation mesothelin-specific CAR T cells (M28Z) were generated to use as controls64. A c-myc epitope tag sequence was also included in the CAR vector construct to allow tracking. The synthesis and insertion of the resulting CAR vector sequences into SFG γ-retroviral vector backbone (provided by I. Rivière, Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center) was performed in accordance with standard molecular biology protocols65. The resulting SFG-OSE703LH-28Z, SFG-OSE703HL-28Z, and SFG-M28Z encoding plasmids were transfected into 293 T H29 and 293 T RD114 packaging cell lines to produce retroviral supernatant66.

T-cell isolation and CAR T-cell generation

PBMCs were isolated by density-gradient centrifugation from buffy coats of healthy anonymous blood donors purchased from New York Blood Center whose sex was unknown to the investigators and who were all aged >18 years. This work was deemed not to be human subjects research by the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center Institutional Review Board. Donor T cells were enriched from PBMCs by activation using phytohemagglutinin (2 µg mL–1, Remel) or Dynabeads human T-CD3/CD28 (Gibco, lot 2697276) at a ratio of 1:1 for 2 days, followed by magnetic enrichment using anti-CD3 microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-097-043) and LS columns (Miltenyi Biotec, 5201113029). Enriched T cells were transduced with retroviral supernatants encoding the indicated CAR constructs by centrifugation (1800 × g for 1 h) on retronectin-coated plates (15 μg ml⁻¹; Takara). Following transduction, CAR T cells and untransduced control T cells were maintained in culture in the presence of IL-2 (200 IU ml⁻¹)27. CAR T cells thus produced were either used immediately in experiments, while maintained in culture with RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with low-dose recombinant human IL2 (Miltenyi Biotec), 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin, or frozen in freezing medium (heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum plus 10% DMSO) and stored at -80 °C until use. High-affinity and low-affinity CD127 CAR T cells were manufactured for up to 5 days before downstream experiments were performed, whereas selective depletion of self-reactive CAR T cells was performed for up to 10 days with IL2 (100 IU/mL) to allow fratricide to enrich the nonfratricidal-dominant population. Dasatinib (BMS-354825; Selleck Chemicals) was added at a final concentration of 100 nM 1 day after transduction only in the indicated experimental conditions. Control CAR T cells were generated in parallel without dasatinib.

Cytotoxicity assays

The cytotoxicity of T cells transduced with a CAR or vector control was determined using standard 51Cr-release assays67. To perform the luciferase cytotoxicity assays, CAR T cells were incubated for 4 to 8 h with tumor cell lines expressing effLuc, at different E:T ratios, in triplicate. The viable tumor quantity was determined by BLI using IVIS Spectrum CT after the addition of 100 µL of D-luciferin (5–15 mg/mL) per well and was compared with the signal of tumor cells incubated with untransduced T cells or tumor cells alone. Cytotoxicity was determined as (1 − (RLUsample)/(RLUmax)) × 100.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspension was resuspended in 100 µL of 0.5% FCS/PBS, incubated with human and mouse Fc receptor–blocking reagents for 15 min, and stained with antihuman monoclonal antibodies (Supplementary Table 2) for 20 min. After washing twice with 0.5% FCS/PBS, cells were resuspended in 1 ug/mL DAPI (Thermo Fisher) (0.5% FCS/PBS) solution, 7AAD (Biolegend) (0.5% FCS/PBS), or eFluor 780 Fixable Viability Dye (Thermo Fisher) according to the experimental design before acquisition. Data acquisition was performed using an Attune NxT or Attune NxT CytPix flow cytometer (Invitrogen), and the results were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.9.0).

Cell-accumulation test

A total of 1 × 106 CTV (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C34557)–labeled CAR T cells were seeded with 1 × 106 cancer cells, such as DND.41-effLuc, HPB-ALL-effLuc, or Jurkat-effLuc cells, in 12-well plates in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FCS and IL2 (20 IU/mL). Cell cultures were replenished with the same cancer cell every 72 h until day 9; CAR T-cell characteristics (cell count, proliferation rate, and phenotype) were assessed by flow cytometric analysis.

Repeated antigen-stress test

A total of 1 × 106 CAR T cells were seeded with 1 × 106 cancer cells, such as DND.41-effLuc or HPB-ALL-effLuc cells, at an E:T ratio of 1:1 in 12-well plates with RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FCS and IL2 (20 UI/mL). Cell cultures were replenished with the same cancer cells every 24 h until day 8; CAR T-cell characteristics (cell count, proliferation rate, and phenotype) were assessed by flow cytometric analysis.

Luminex cytokine-detection assay

Cytokine-release assays were performed by coculturing 5 × 105 to 5 × 103 CAR T cells or UT T cells with (or without) 5 × 103 cancer cells in 200 µL of RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FCS and IL2 (20 UI/mL) in 96-well round-bottom plates in triplicate. After 4 to 18 h of coculture, supernatant was collected, and cytokine levels were determined using a multiplex bead Human Cytokine Detection kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (MilliporeSigma).

PBMC-based assessment of CAR T-cell cytotoxicity in NSG mice

NSG mice 6 to 8 weeks old were intraperitoneally administered 100 mg/kg cyclophosphamide (Athenex, lot 21048), followed 2 days later by intravenous administration of 1 × 107 healthy donor–derived PBMCs. Two days after that, mice were administered 1 × 106 CTV-labeled autologous donor-derived M28Z or LOW or HIGH CAR T cells intravenously via tail vein injection. On day 6, mice were euthanized, and spleen, bone marrow, and peripheral blood were harvested for flow cytometric analysis. Tissues were digested (when necessary), homogenized by mechanical disintegration using a 5-mL syringe plunger, and passed through a 40-micron mesh filter, with washing with warm RPMI 1640 medium. Splenocytes and bone marrow–derived cells were lysed in 2 to 3 mL of ACK red blood cell lysing buffer (Gibco, 2451175) for 5 min and washed twice in 0.5% FCS/PBS. Single-cell suspension was incubated with 5 µL of undiluted human and mouse Fc receptor–blocking reagents for 15 min and then stained with cell type–specific antibodies (Supplementary Table 2) for 20 min. After staining, cells were washed twice in 0.5% FCS/PBS and stained with DAPI (1 µg/mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific, WI3371931) before acquisition. Stained cells were analyzed on an Attune NxT or Attune NxT CytPix flow cytometer (Invitrogen). The frequency of residual target cells was normalized to that of the PBMC only–treated control group.

Synthetic sgRNA

sgRNAs targeting IL7R exons 4 and 6 were designed and synthesized by Synthego. The targeted sequences in exon 4 (AGACUUGGAAGAUGCAGAAC, UGCAUCUUCCAAGUCUCCUG) are located within the coding region that contributes to the extracellular domain of the IL7R protein. Similarly, the sequences targeted in exon 6 (UUGUUCCUCCCCAGGAGACU, CUGCAUCUUCCAAGUCUCCU) are part of the region encoding the transmembrane domain of IL7R.

CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing

Donor T cells were enriched from PBMCs after incubation with Dynabeads (human T CD3/CD28, Invitrogen) for 2 days at a ratio of 1:1. Cells were then washed in warm medium after magnetic bead separation. A total of 2 × 106 harvested cells were resuspended in 20 µL of electroporation medium (Lonza) with 1 µg/µL Cas9 mRNA (TriLink BioTechnologies NC1472164) and 1 µg/µL IL7R sgRNA (Synthego) and were electroporated in a cassette (Lonza). The next day, after resting cells in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with IL2 (20 IU/mL) overnight at a concentration of 2 × 106 cells/mL, CAR transduction was performed as described in the T-cell isolation and CAR T-cell generation section27.

Samples from patients with T-ALL and the patient-derived xenograft model

Primary blasts (CD45dim CD3- CD7+) isolated from the bone marrow and peripheral blood from an adult patient with T-ALL (patient 2; 58% blast presence, 59% of cells with expression of CD127) were expanded in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with recombinant human IL2 (20 IU/mL). NSG mice 6 to 8 weeks old were inoculated with 1 × 106 primary T-ALL blasts intravenously via tail vein injection. After 30 days, bone marrow and spleen were harvested, and the presence and phenotype of malignant cells were assessed by flow cytometric analysis. Mice were treated with 5 × 105 autologous CAR T cells intravenously, and tumor burden was monitored by twice-weekly measurement of body weights. To generate CAR T cells from T-ALL patient–derived healthy T cells (CD45hi CD3+ CD7+), fluorescence-activated cell sorting was performed before retroviral transduction. Primary T-ALL samples used for the generation of patient-derived xenograft models were obtained from patients after written informed consent, under a protocol approved by the MSK Institutional Review Board (#06-107).

Single cell RNA-sequencing sample preparation, library construction, and sequencing

CD127-negative, flow-sorted low-affinity anti-CD127 LOW CAR T cells from WT, NS, and KO groups, along with control CD127-negative and CD127-positive M28z CAR T cells (nonfratricidal), were subjected to single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) using the 10x Genomics platform at the Integrated Genomics Operation (IGO) Core Facility at MSK. Sorted and frozen cells were thawed, washed in RPMI medium, and cell pellets were resuspended in 1× PBS containing 0.04% BSA at a concentration of 700–1200 cells/μL with >75% viability prior to submission for 10x 3′ scRNA-seq. Library construction and raw data generation were performed by the IGO Core Facility following their standard workflow procedures.

Single cell RNA-sequencing data analysis

Raw sequencing data analysis was processed at Bioinformatics core (BIC) facility at MSK following standard Seurat workflow pipeline (version 4). Briefly, performing QC/Cell level filtering of raw data resulted in 68-80% of the unique features (genes) detected from all the samples (2705 genes on average per sample) qualifying for downstream analysis. Ribosomal genes were excluded and PCA transformation was performed on the scaled data using the top variable features determined in the previous step. The top N PCA coordinates are then used to both cluster the data and compute the 2D projection (UMAP) at resolution 0.2 with FDR ≤ 0.05 and average Log2 FC > 1. Finally, the genes that were differentially expressed between the clusters were identified, and the distribution of the marker gene expression within each cluster was graphically represented. Next, sample-level differential gene expression analysis was performed with FDR ≤ 0.05 and Log2 FC > 0.25, and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted to identify biological pathways (Hallmark and KEGG) associated with gene expression signatures for each sample.

Statistics

Data analysis was performed using Prism software (version 9, GraphPad). Statistical methods included the 2-tailed Student’s t test and 2-way analysis of variance, with the Sidak-Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons where appropriate. For in vivo experiments, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to represent overall survival, and differences in survival between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Statistical significance was determined as p < 0.05, p < 0.01, p < 0.001, and p < 0.0001.
