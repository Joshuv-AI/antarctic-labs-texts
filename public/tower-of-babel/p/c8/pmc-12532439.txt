Habitat conditions during winter explain movement among subpopulations of a declining migratory bird
Authors: Alexander R Schindler, Anthony D Fox, Alyn J Walsh, Larry R Griffin, Seán B A Kelly, Lei Cao, Mitch D Weegman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding animal movement patterns among subpopulations is crucial for identifying spatiotemporal patterns in animal abundance. Quantifying such movement rates enables a better understanding of population dynamics and how animals decide to move between habitats throughout their range. However, detailed assessments of animal movements among subpopulations are often difficult to obtain with typical capture-mark-recapture methods, limiting our ability to incorporate movement information into conservation planning.MethodsWe used six years of high-resolution Global Positioning System (GPS) tracking data in a Bayesian multistate model to quantify habitat drivers of monthly intra- (i.e., from one month to the next within a winter) and inter-winter (i.e., from the last month of one winter to the first month of the following winter) movements among eight different subpopulations of a declining migratory bird, the Greenland white-fronted goose (Anser albifrons flavirostris).ResultsWe found that while Greenland white-fronted geese were highly philopatric to geographically distinct wintering subpopulations, individuals made intra- and inter-winter movements based on locally available foraging habitat. These decisions changed within and among winters; geese were more likely to make intra-winter movements to areas with fewer croplands and boglands, potentially in response to local food depletion, and more likely to make inter-winter movements to areas with more boglands and “greener” grasslands.ConclusionsWe demonstrated a framework for using high-frequency GPS tracking data to discover movement patterns and test hypotheses about environmental drivers of movements, which could be linked with population dynamics and applied to other species of concern for an improved understanding of metapopulations across space and time. Implementing habitat management strategies that optimize foraging conditions throughout winter, including rewetting degraded peatlands to provide bogland food plants, provision of cereal stubbles in early winter and high-quality grasslands throughout winter may help improve conservation outcomes for Greenland white-fronted geese.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40462-025-00600-2.Keywords: Anser albifrons flavirostris, Bio-logging, Conservation, Metapopulation dynamics, Movement ecology, Multistate model, Non-breeding season, Philopatry

Background

Quantifying animal movement patterns is crucial for understanding animal population structure and spatiotemporal patterns in abundance, especially in heterogeneous landscapes [1]. Habitat fragmentation causes animals to aggregate into patchily-distributed local subpopulations, interlinked by movement within a larger network of subpopulations, called a metapopulation [2–4]. While changes in overall metapopulation abundance over time are driven by the balance of births and deaths, movement among subpopulations can substantially affect local abundance patterns in a variety of animal species [5]. For example, uneven immigration and emigration rates among subpopulations may affect population growth rates, providing stability to subpopulations with low reproductive success or survival [6–8]. With land-use change increasing habitat fragmentation [9, 10] and influencing animal populations, it is increasingly important to consider animal movement patterns among subpopulations when assessing population dynamics [5]. However, detailed assessments of animal movement among subpopulations are difficult to obtain using typical capture-mark-recapture methods, especially for migratory species which use broad geographic ranges and have wide dispersal capabilities [11].

The subpopulation-metapopulation framework for studying population dynamics has traditionally been applied primarily to non-migratory animals. However, many migratory animal populations also form demographically independent subpopulations with limited exchange during at least part of the annual cycle [12]. For example, some migratory bird species like wood thrushes (Hylocichla mustelina) [13] and lesser snow geese (Anser caerulescens caerulescens) [14] use geographically distinct breeding areas despite co-occurring in shared wintering areas. Other migratory birds, like bristle-thighed curlews (Numenius tahitiensis) [15] and Kirtland’s warblers (Dendroica kirtlandii) [16] share breeding areas but are highly philopatric to distinct wintering (i.e., non-breeding) subpopulations. Applying a subpopulation-metapopulation framework to one or more stages of the annual cycle can therefore help us better understand spatiotemporal patterns in migratory bird abundance despite co-occurrence and mixing during other phases of their annual or life cycle [12].

Greenland white-fronted geese (Anser albifrons flavirostris) are long-lived migratory birds that breed in west Greenland, stage in south and west Iceland during spring and autumn, and winter in Great Britain and Ireland at up to 68 geographically distinct locations (i.e., winter subpopulations) [17–20]. Greenland white-fronted geese are highly philopatric to wintering subpopulations; previous studies of individuals marked with alphanumeric neck collars found most adults returned to wintering subpopulations where they were marked, with 14–15% of marked individuals observed changing wintering subpopulations each year and 22% observed changing wintering subpopulations at least once during their lifetimes [21–23]. While more mixing likely occurs on staging and breeding areas, marked geese from the southern wintering range (i.e., Ireland) tend to stage in western Iceland and breed in the northern part of the breeding range, while marked geese wintering in the northern wintering range (i.e., Scotland) tend to stage in southern Iceland and breed in the southern part of the breeding range [24, 25]. Previous demographic analyses identified heterogeneity in survival and reproductive rates among geese at different wintering locations [26]. Studying Greenland white-fronted geese using a subpopulation-metapopulation framework during winter provides structure to estimate and compare population dynamics within and among units, including the connectedness of subpopulations.

The Greenland white-fronted goose metapopulation declined by more than 50% from 35,000 individuals in 1999 to 15,000 in 2024, and only 52 of the 68 subpopulations present in the late 1990s were observed in their winter ranges between winter 2017/2018 and winter 2023/2024 [20, 27]. Abundance trends vary among subpopulations [28], and while some of this variation is due to heterogeneity in reproductive rate, and to a lesser extent survival, movement seems to play a key role in regulating growth rates in Greenland white-fronted goose subpopulations. For example, population census data indicated that abundance at one of the largest subpopulations, Wexford, was until very recently stable [27]. However, demographic studies found that Wexford functions as a cryptic sink, with mortality exceeding recruitment and population size only sustained through high rates of immigration from other subpopulations [26]. Due to logistical constraints and effort required to obtain long-term capture-recapture data, Greenland white-fronted goose movement information is limited to a few wintering subpopulations. We also know very little about the habitat and habitat management factors that may be driving Greenland white-fronted goose movements within and among winters. Improving our understanding of movement rates among a wider network of wintering subpopulations and the habitat conditions driving these movements will help us understand why some subpopulations are declining faster than others and inform management for maintaining the full extent of wintering subpopulations, which is a key objective of the International Single Species Action Plan for the Conservation of Greenland White-fronted Geese [29].

Many long-lived migratory birds exhibit high fidelity to wintering areas [30–32], with inter-site movements uncommon among adults [33]. Individuals likely experience benefits to survival and reproduction from returning to the same wintering areas, including knowledge of predictable food sources, roost sites, and predation risks [34–36]. However, changes in habitat may cause individuals to disperse to new areas if they perceive more favourable conditions elsewhere. For example, the North American midcontinent population of greater white-fronted geese (Anser albifrons frontalis) have shifted wintering distribution in response to large-scale landscape modification that altered location-dependable food sources (e.g., increased rice, corn and soybean production) [37]. Greenland white-fronted geese have increasingly shifted from feeding on natural vegetation (e.g., common cottongrass Eriophorum angustifolium found in traditional peat bog habitats) to intensively managed agricultural grasslands and cereal crops [38, 39]. Habitat use also likely varies seasonally; a previous assessment of Greenland white-fronted goose habitat use from count data found that geese were more likely to feed on bogland plants and cereal crops in late autumn and early winter, and more likely to use dry grasslands in early spring [40]. However, we do not know how shifts in habitat use affect movement among subpopulations.

Greenland white-fronted geese may also make movement decisions based on anthropogenic disturbance and habitat quality [40, 41]. For example, other Arctic-nesting geese select grass based on nutritional quality (i.e., selecting “greener” early-growth grass containing higher nutrient content) [42–44]. In response to increased use of agricultural habitats by geese, the National Parks and Wildlife Service (Ireland) and NatureScot (Scotland) began implementing goose management plans with farmers in 2006 and 2017, respectively. These programs involved incentivizing private landowners to allow undisturbed feeding areas on agricultural grasslands and crops used by geese. However, the extent to which these habitat and management conditions affect goose movement rates among subpopulations remains uncertain.

In this study, we used six years of high-frequency movement data from Global Positioning System (GPS) tracking devices deployed on Greenland white-fronted geese to better understand the habitat drivers of movement among subpopulations. Our objectives were to (1) quantify monthly intra- (i.e., from one month to the next within a winter) and inter-winter (i.e., from the last month of one winter to the first month of the following winter) movement probabilities among eight Greenland white-fronted goose subpopulations (comprising ~ 71% of the metapopulation) and “Elsewhere” (combination of smaller subpopulation groups containing ~ 29% of the metapopulation), and (2) test hypotheses about how differences in habitat area, quality, and management extent among subpopulations affected movement probabilities. We predicted movement among subpopulations would generally be low, though movement probabilities would be greatest from smaller subpopulations to larger ones, following observed population-level patterns [26]. We predicted that geese would be more likely to move to areas with high proportions of agricultural habitats (i.e., grasslands and crops) and low proportions of bog habitats, representing a shift from more natural to agricultural habitat use. We also predicted that geese would be more likely to move to areas with high grass “greenness” and high proportion of land enrolled in goose management plans, as these represent habitats with high nutritional quality and low disturbance, respectively. In this paper, we provide a framework for using GPS tracking information to test hypotheses about environmental drivers of animal movement among subpopulations, which is an important step in understanding metapopulation dynamics and informing conservation planning for species of concern. Applying this framework to studying environmental drivers of Greenland white-fronted goose movement patterns will help conservation practitioners maintain high-quality habitat throughout the wintering range, which is crucial to increasing resiliency of this declining metapopulation.

Methods

Goose capture and deployment of GPS tracking devices

Greenland white-fronted geese currently winter in ~ 52 subpopulations in Great Britain and Ireland [27]. From 2017 to 2022, we captured 83 Greenland white-fronted geese among four wintering subpopulations in Ireland (Lough Iron 53°36´ N 07°28´ W, Sheskinmore 54°48´ N 08°27´ W, Lough Swilly 54°58´ N 07°38´ W, Wexford Slobs 52°21´ N 06°24´ W) and three subpopulations in Scotland (Loch Ken 55°00´ N 04°01´ W, Islay 55°47´ N 06°15´ W, West Freugh 54°50´ N 04°56´ W) at feeding sites using standard cannon-netting techniques. We additionally caught six Greenland white-fronted geese at one staging site in Iceland (Hvanneyri 64°33´ N 21°45´ W) during autumn 2017, all of which spent the following winter at Wexford. We sexed captured geese by cloacal examination [21], distinguished juvenile (i.e., first winter) geese from adults (i.e., second winter and older) based on plumage characteristics [45], and fitted adults with neck collar GPS tracking devices (OrniTrack-N38, 38 g, www.ornitela.com and Ecotone GPS-GSM, 24 g, www.ecotone-telemetry.com). The devices were solar powered, collected a GPS point every 15 (Ornitela devices) or 30 (Ecotone devices) min, and uploaded data daily via the Global System for Mobile Communication. Greenland white-fronted geese maintain long-term family associations, and offspring typically closely associate with parents for 2–3 years [46, 47]. To maximize independence in our data, we visually identified family groups (i.e., small groups of closely associated birds) prior to capture and attempted to fit devices to only one (typically adult female) individual per family group. One goose captured at Lough Iron immigrated to the Connemara subpopulation (53°19´ N 09°25´ W) early in our study period; thus, we used GPS information from winter 2017/2018 to winter 2022/2023 to estimate movement probabilities among eight focal populations (seven catch locations and Connemara; Table 1).

Winter capture history development

We defined annual wintering periods for each individual Greenland white-fronted goose based on the first/last GPS points over land in Great Britain and Ireland each year. Greenland white-fronted geese in each wintering subpopulation share a unique roosting location and typically forage within 15 km of this roost site [23] (i.e., core wintering areas for each subpopulation typically do not overlap even when foraging; verified by our GPS data). We subset GPS locations to one location per day that was closest to midnight to identify daily roosting coordinates and matched to known roosting locations of Greenland white-fronted goose subpopulations [29, 48]. We developed daily capture histories by assigning “states” according to subpopulation: “1” – observed at Lough Swilly, “2” – observed at Sheskinmore, “3” – observed at Connemara, “4” - observed at Lough Iron, “5” – observed at Wexford, “6” – observed at Islay, “7” – observed at West Freugh, “8” – observed at Loch Ken, “9” – observed Elsewhere, “10” – not observed (Fig. 1). We combined information from the 44 other extant subpopulations into the Elsewhere category as we did not have enough observations for these to estimate subpopulation-specific transition probabilities. While it was necessary to include the Elsewhere category to encompass all possible movements, we focused inference on focal subpopulations, as Elsewhere included a broad range of subpopulations with varied habitat conditions, and we did not specifically tag geese in these subpopulations. Due to the high subpopulation fidelity (i.e., infrequent transitions among subpopulations), we summarized daily capture histories into monthly capture histories (i.e., one state per 31 days per individual) and assigned a single state to each month from mid-October to mid-April (to encompass earliest and latest observations on wintering areas). If a transition occurred within a month, the capture history reflected the transitioned-to state even if the individual returned to the original state during the same month. If two or more state transitions occurred during a month, the final transition was assigned for the month. Only 9% of bird-months contained a transition within a month, and < 1% of bird-months contained two transitions within a month. We defined intra-winter transitions as occurring from one month to the next within a winter and inter-winter transitions as transitions from the last month of one winter to the first month of the following winter.

Habitat and habitat management data

With help from local biologists, we surveyed all known Greenland white-fronted goose feeding areas for Lough Swilly, Sheskinmore, Connemara, Lough Iron, Wexford, Islay, West Freugh, and Loch Ken subpopulations and recorded field-level land cover type as grass, cropland, or bog (comprising the dominant Greenland white-fronted goose winter foraging habitats [39]). We recorded all other land cover types as “other”. For all areas not surveyed, we used land cover data from the 2018 National Land Cover Map of Ireland (0.1-ha resolution) [49] and the UKCEH Land Cover Map for 2019 (0.04-ha resolution) [50] for subpopulations in Ireland and Scotland, respectively. We reclassified land cover in both these data sets to match land cover categories from our habitat surveys (see Additional File 1 for details). We then combined GPS points among all individuals and created one 95% minimum convex polygon per subpopulation and month, which represented the typical foraging range used by geese around the roosting location. For each minimum convex polygon, we calculated the proportion classified as grass, cropland, or bog.

To assess the effects of grassland quality on movement, we used the enhanced vegetation index from MODIS MOD13C1 version 6.1 data (0.05-degree resolution) [51]. We used the maximum enhanced vegetation index value for all land classified as grass within each minimum convex polygon during the corresponding month; thus, this index represented the maximum “greenness” of grass available to geese for the corresponding subpopulation and month. To test the effects of private lands management on movement, we also obtained spatial information for goose farm plan schemes in Ireland and Scotland from the National Parks and Wildlife Service and NatureScot, respectively. Both these programs pay private landowners to allow geese to feed undisturbed on enrolled agricultural croplands and grasslands (i.e., farmers must delay all agricultural activities until after geese depart for spring migration). We calculated the proportion of the corresponding minimum convex polygon managed through a farm plan program.

Results

Among all winters, 41.6% (n = 37 of 89) of tagged Greenland white-fronted geese made at least one monthly movement among subpopulations. Of these, 43.2% (n = 16 of 37) only temporarily immigrated, later returning to the initial subpopulation. Monthly intra-winter fidelity (i.e., probability of staying at the same subpopulation from one month to the next) was high for all subpopulations, and highest at Sheskinmore (median = 0.99, 95% CRI = 0.93–1), followed by Islay (0.99, 0.97–1), Lough Swilly (0.98, 0.90–1), Connemara (0.96, 0.81–1), Loch Ken (0.94, 0.85–0.98), Wexford (0.92, 0.87–0.95), West Freugh (0.91, 0.81–0.97), Lough Iron (0.89, 0.75–0.96), and Elsewhere (0.84, 0.72–0.93; Fig. 2). Intra-winter movements with the highest probabilities were from West Freugh to Elsewhere (0.08, 0.02–0.17), Lough Iron to Elsewhere (0.06, 0.01–0.18), Wexford to Elsewhere (0.06, 0.03–0.11), Elsewhere to Sheskinmore (0.06, 0.02–0.16), and Loch Ken to Islay (0.04, 0.01–0.11; Fig. 2). Among all subpopulations, percent cover of cropland and percent cover of bog had negative effects on intra-winter movement/fidelity probabilities (median = -3.39, = 0.85 and = -1.42, = 0.75, respectively; Fig. 3). Percent cover of bog and enhanced vegetation index value of grass had positive effects on inter-winter movement/fidelity probabilities (median = 2.59, = 0.83 and = 2.81, = 0.83, respectively; Fig. 3). Thus, within a winter Greenland white-fronted geese were more likely to move to or stay in subpopulations where habitat contained low proportions of cropland and bog. Among winters (i.e., from the last month of a winter to the first month of the following winter), Greenland white-fronted geese were more likely to move to or stay in subpopulations where habitat contained high proportions of bog and grasslands with high enhanced vegetation index values (i.e., “greener” grass). No other habitat or habitat management covariates explained substantial variation in movement probabilities.

Discussion

Using GPS tracking data with high spatial and temporal resolution, we found that most Greenland white-fronted geese were highly philopatric to wintering subpopulations. Yet, there were differences in movement rates among subpopulations between and within winters, explained by prevailing foraging habitat conditions. Individuals were more likely to move between winters to areas with more boglands and “greener” grasslands, then move within a winter to areas with fewer croplands and boglands, likely in response to local depletion of these finite food sources [40, 57]. The wintering range for Greenland white-fronted geese is characterized by a mild climate, and managed grass can grow throughout winter, which provides geese with continuous plant regrowth for forage once available bog plants and cereal waste grain (which are finite, and non-renewable resources within winter) are consumed.

Greenland white-fronted geese traditionally used peat bog habitats in winter, primarily feeding on common cottongrass, but have increasingly fed on agricultural grasslands and croplands since the 1950s [38, 39]. In early winter, waste cereal leftover from harvest and the underground storage organs of common cottongrass both provide lipid- and carbohydrate-rich forage which allow geese to replenish depleted fat stores from autumn migration and prepare for food shortages and thermoregulatory requirements of winter [57–59]. However, these food sources are limited (as neither are produced during winter), so geese must switch to grass when these food sources are depleted. There is ample evidence that many goose species select foraging habitats based on perceived nutritional quality (e.g., selecting early-growth grasses which contain low fiber, high protein, and high energy content [42, 44, 60]) and intake rates (e.g., selecting large fields with high biomass of live grass [61–63]). Greenland white-fronted geese likely select grassland habitats based on similar factors; geese favoured “greener” fields which likely represented managed pastures that are reseeded and fertilized, providing birds with extended periods of high-density swards that are nutrient-rich [57, 64, 65].

Disturbance from anthropogenic activities (e.g., agricultural machinery, cattle grazing) likely also affected Greenland white-fronted goose movement among wintering subpopulations. We expected our movement probabilities would indicate a shift from bogs to croplands. Although geese receive nutritional benefits from exploiting agricultural food types [66], these benefits come at an energetic cost from increased agricultural-related disturbances (resulting in more time spent flying and less time spent feeding) [67]. Norriss and Wilson [41] found a negative effect of agricultural-related disturbances on Greenland white-fronted goose subpopulation abundance trends. These disturbances were highest on intensively farmed landscapes and lowest on boglands and flooded grasslands. Thus, bogland habitats are still likely important for Greenland white-fronted geese, as these offer a safe refuge from agricultural disturbance until bog plants are depleted and geese must switch to feeding on grasses.

Differences in movement rates among Greenland white-fronted goose subpopulations likely affect observed abundance patterns, as geese differentially emigrate away from areas of poor habitat conditions and immigrate to areas with better habitat. Previous demographic analyses found that subpopulation stability at Wexford was only maintained by high immigration rates from other subpopulations [26]. We found high inter-winter movement from Lough Swilly to Wexford, indicating Lough Swilly may be acting as a “source” as birds perceive superior habitat at Wexford and move there. Wexford also had high inter-winter fidelity, supporting previous evidence of net immigration (i.e., high immigration rate to Wexford, low emigration rate away from Wexford) [26].

Overall, we found heterogeneity in overwintering strategies among Greenland white-fronted goose subpopulations. Six of our eight subpopulations exhibited high inter- and intra-winter site fidelity; these individuals typically spent the entire winter in the same subpopulation. However, tagged birds from Sheskinmore and Lough Iron characteristically spent early winters with the Machrihanish subpopulation (part of the Elsewhere group, 55°30´ N 05°38´ W) or Islay subpopulation, respectively, before moving mid-winter back to their primary wintering subpopulation (e.g., birds departing Sheskinmore for spring migration, breeding, and subsequent autumn migration first returned to wintering areas at Machrihanish [inter-winter movement from Sheskinmore to Elsewhere], before moving mid-winter back to Sheskinmore [intra-winter movement from Elsewhere to Sheskinmore]). These wintering strategies may help geese exploit seasonal changes in food availability. Movement within the non-breeding season in response to resource availability is common among migratory birds [68]. For example, tree swallows (Tachycineta bicolor) and Montagu’s harriers (Circus pygargus) make large-scale movements during winter in response to seasonal changes in food availability [69–71]. Understanding these movement patterns and changes in habitat use are important for informing management practices that optimize food availability throughout winter.

Other factors in addition to habitat may affect movement among Greenland white-fronted goose subpopulations. For example, movement rates vary by age class, with highest rates of movement observed in younger birds [72]. Pair bonding in Greenland white-fronted geese primarily occurs during the second and third years of life and may result in inter-winter movements to the wintering subpopulation of the new mate [21, 73]. We only tracked movements of adults and therefore did not quantify age-specific movement probabilities. Greenland white-fronted geese also have prolonged family associations lasting up to 13 years, which is uncommon in birds [46, 47]. Larger family units of geese are better able to defend resources [74–76]. Thus, social structure may explain variation in movement rates through competition for resources, with family groups excluding unrelated pairs or individuals from preferred habitat. However, more research is needed to fully understand the effects of social structure on movement probabilities.

Using GPS devices to develop capture histories allowed us to quantify movement patterns at finer spatial (i.e., larger number of subpopulations) and temporal (i.e., distinguishing inter- and intra-winter movements) scales than possible with previous studies using traditional capture-mark-recapture methods [26], which was a crucial component of our study. However, financial and logistical constraints associated with deploying GPS tracking devices resulted in a smaller sample size of marked birds and shorter study duration than previous capture-mark-recapture data sets. As a result, some of our movement estimates had high levels of uncertainty, especially for inter-winter movements. Sheskinmore and Connemara sample sizes were particularly limited due to constraints with marking birds in small subpopulations. We recommend caution when interpreting specific movement probabilities from these two subpopulations as decision-making in a small sample of birds may not be representative of the overall subpopulation. Nevertheless, we believe our movement estimates indicate patterns in the data, particularly for instances of high movement/low fidelity relative to cases of low movement/high fidelity and were adequate to detect environmental drivers of movements. We recommend researchers consider potential trade-offs in obtaining spatially and temporally rich information per marked individual and obtaining a larger sample size of marked individuals when designing similar studies.

We also acknowledge that our sample of geese in the Elsewhere category contained biases, as these individuals all originated from one of our focal areas. We therefore recommend caution when interpreting movement probabilities from Elsewhere to each of our focal areas as many of these movements consisted of temporary emigrants returning to their original subpopulation. However, including an Elsewhere category in our model was necessary to account for all possible transitions.

Conclusions

Understanding movements among wintering subpopulations and the factors driving these movements are especially important because of links between overwintering habitats and survival and reproductive success in migratory birds [77–79]. Quantifying movement rates helps us better understand spatiotemporal patterns in population dynamics [6–8] and how migrants track resources across space and time throughout their range [68]. However, quantifying animal movement among subpopulations can be difficult, often limiting our ability to incorporate movement information into conservation planning [11]. We demonstrated how high-frequency GPS information can be used in a Bayesian multistate model to estimate intra- and inter-winter movement probabilities and habitat drivers of these movements. This information improves our understanding of animal population structure in space and time and can help practitioners provide appropriate habitat resources for species in areas of greatest need.

The Greenland white-fronted goose metapopulation has declined by ~ 50% over the past 25 years, though individual wintering subpopulations have varied trends; some have declined (~ 16 of which towards eventual extinction), while others appear stable [27]. We found that geese made intra- and inter-winter movements based on locally available foraging habitat. Thus, habitat management strategies could optimize foraging conditions by increasing access to favoured bogland plants (e.g. by rewetting degraded peatlands [80]) and stubble cereal grains (e.g., through sacrificial crops, delayed ploughing and by decreasing agricultural-related disturbances) in early winter, and high-quality grasslands throughout winter. Maintaining high-quality habitat throughout the wintering range is a key objective of the International Single Species Action Plan for the Conservation of Greenland White-fronted Geese [29], and maintaining the extent of wintering subpopulations is important for increasing resiliency of this declining metapopulation to future environmental changes.
