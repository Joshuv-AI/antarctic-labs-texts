Association of ABO blood group and coronary heart disease in blood donors—Results from a cohort study in Australia
Authors: Surendra Karki, Katy Bell, David O Irving, Marijke Welvaert, Rena Hirani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundABO blood groups may be associated with increased risk for several diseases including cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), particularly coronary heart disease (CHD). The role of other significant blood group systems, such as RhD and RhCE, has not been extensively investigated.MethodsWe linked The Sax Institute's 45 and Up Study to blood donation records and other health data to examine the association between ABO and Rh blood groups with CVD, CHD, and Cerebrovascular disease (CeVD). We used ICD10 codes from hospital admission data to ascertain the study outcomes. Cox‐proportional hazards regression models were performed to assess the association.ResultsA total of 32,510 blood donors were included. Over an average follow‐up period of just over 10 years, 8.3%, 5.4%, and 2.3% developed CVD, CHD, and CeVD, respectively. CHD incidence was highest among individuals with group AB (6.6/1000 person‐years). Compared to group O, fully adjusted hazard ratios for CHD were: A, 1.05 (95% confidence intervals [CI]: 0.95–1.16); B, 0.99 (0.84–1.16); AB, 1.27 (1.01–1.59). RhD or RhCE blood groups were not associated with CHD. For CeVD, 738 cases (2.3%) occurred, with no evidence of associations found for ABO, RhD, or RhCE blood groups. No significant association was observed between ABO blood group and all CVDs considered together as an outcome.ConclusionThere was a borderline association of higher CHD risk in individuals with blood group AB compared with group O, whereas no association was observed for groups A or B individually.Keywords: ABO, blood donors, coronary heart disease, RhCE, RhD

INTRODUCTION

Human ABO blood group antigens are glycoproteins and glycolipids located on the surface of red blood cells (RBC) and the cells found in several other human tissues. 1 , 2 ABO blood groups have been reported to be associated with increased risk for several diseases including cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), COVID‐19, gestational diabetes, cancer, and infections. However, these associations are not always consistent across studies. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7

Coronary heart disease (CHD) and cerebrovascular disease (CeVD) are both types of CVD. Whilst they are distinct clinical entities affecting different organ systems and having diverse clinical manifestations, they do share some important atherosclerotic and thrombotic mechanisms but may have different factors affecting them, thus requiring independent analyses. A small number of studies have investigated the role of ABO blood groups in CHD and CeVD risk, with some conflicting outcomes. 5 , 8 , 9 , 10 While overall there was no consensus on a relationship between blood group and CHD, studies including a systematic review and meta‐analysis of published literature found increased risk for non‐group O individuals, particularly group AB. 5 , 8 , 9 , 10 Other studies reported increased risk of ischaemic stroke (a type of CeVD) for non‐group O individuals, particularly group AB. 11 , 12 These studies tended to study associations for group O and non‐group O, rather than studying associations for each specific blood group, O, A, B, and AB. 9 , 11 , 13 , 14 A Canadian study of a blood donor population where risk was reported separately by specific blood group found a higher CVD risk in group AB compared with group O donors. 15

The causal pathways, if any, for how ABO type might be linked to CVD, CHD, and CeVD are not well described but are likely to include determinants of the atherosclerosis that underlies most CVD. 16 The pathogenesis of atherosclerotic plaque is thought to develop over time from maladaptive endothelial function and intimal thickening with inflammation. 17 ABO blood groups have been associated with inflammatory markers, plasma lipid levels, and the function of the factor VIII‐von Willebrand factor (VWF) complex, with each of these factors implicated in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 In particular, VWF is involved with hemostasis and arterial thrombus formation and elevated levels of VWF are a well‐characterized marker for CVD, CHD and ischemic stroke. 21 Additionally, VWF levels in plasma are 25% lower in healthy group O individuals compared with healthy non‐group O individuals. 22 , 23

Beyond associations between ABO blood group and CHD, few studies have also explored the potential role of the RhD blood group. One study reported worse outcomes for patients with ischemic cardiomyopathy who were RhD negative (RhD−) compared with those who were RhD positive (RhD+). Another reported higher survival following cardiac resynchronization therapy for RhD− patients compared with those who were RhD+. 24 , 25 A possible mechanism for how RhD− blood may affect CVD risk has been reported through its association with hyperlipidemia. 26 , 27 , 28 No studies have examined the influence of the RhCE blood group system in CVD risk. RhCE is more challenging to analyze since RhD is more commonly recorded for patients as part of routine blood typing prior to transfusion whereas the RhCE phenotype is less likely to be investigated unless the patient requires frequent transfusion events. In Australian blood donors, RhCE typing has become more common because of automated phenotyping instrumentation implemented since March 2019 allowing this group system to be examined. 29

In this study, we examined the risk of CVD, CHD, and CeVD by ABO and the Rh blood group systems, in blood donors identified within a large Australian population‐based cohort study, The Sax Institute's 45 and Up Study (hereby referred to as ‘The 45 and Up Study’). 30

METHODS

Study data

We used The 45 and Up Study survey data linked to Australian Red Cross Lifeblood (Lifeblood) donation data, and administrative data from the New South Wales Admitted Patients Data Collection (NSWAPDC), the New South Wales (NSW) Registry of Births, Deaths and Marriages (RBDM), and the NSW Cancer Registry data (NSWCR). A detailed description of the datasets and variables used in the analyses is shown in Figure 1.

Datasets and variables used in the analyses.

The 45 and Up Study data

The 45 and Up Study is the largest cohort study in Australia to date. 30 This study recruited 267,357 participants from the Australian state of New South Wales at a baseline timepoint between 2005 and 2009. At the time of recruitment, participants were aged ≥45 years. 30 , 31 Briefly, adults ≥45 years were randomly sampled from the Services Australia Medicare enrolment database, which provides near complete coverage of the population residing in NSW, and were invited to participate in the study. People 80+ years of age and residents of rural and remote areas were oversampled. Of those invited, 19.2% participated. 30 Participants also agreed for their data to be used for research through record linkage of their information to a wide range of administrative data sets. 31 The survey questionnaire that was completed by the participants at recruitment included information on socio‐demographics, health, lifestyle, and behavioral questions. Further details on the questions used in this study from The 45 and Up Study questionnaires and variables categorization/derivation is provided in Supplementary information (Table S1). 32 At the time of the data linkage for this study, data from 266,734 participants were available for data linkage (excluding those withdrawn from the study).

Lifeblood donor data

Lifeblood is the national provider of blood and blood products in Australia. Information regarding all blood donors and donations in Australia is recorded in Lifeblood's National Blood Management System (NBMS). NSW donation data was incorporated into the NBMS in June 2004 with data migration commencing from 1 January 2002. These data were used to ascertain the blood groups in donors included in this study.

The NSWAPDC data

The dataset includes every hospital admission in NSW, recording admission/discharge dates, a primary diagnosis, and up to 49 secondary diagnoses. We used NSWAPDC data (available July 2001–December 2018) to identify events of CVD, CHD, and CeVD. 33

The NSWCR data

The NSWCR, established in 1972, systematically records all cancer diagnoses in NSW, including demographics, diagnosis dates, and mortality. We used NSWCR data (complete to December 2015) to identify and exclude participants diagnosed with cancer before the start of follow‐up of study outcomes. 34

The RBDM data

This dataset contains records of the dates of all births, deaths, and marriages of NSW residents. We used RBDM deaths data to ascertain overall number of deaths and date of death. 35 The RBDM dataset was complete up to December 2018.

The 45 and Up Study, Lifeblood, NSWAPDC, NSWCR, and RBDM data were linked by the NSW Centre for Health Record Linkage (CHeReL) using a probabilistic matching method using name, date of birth, and street address. Data analysis was conducted within the Sax Institute's Secure Unified Research Environment (SURE). Previous quality assurance audit for linkages done by CHeReL shows a false‐positive rate of 0.5%. 36

Study population

Of 266,734 participants in The 45 and Up Study, ABO blood group data were available for 35,587 blood donors. After excluding 3077 with CVD or cancer at baseline, 32,510 participants remained for analysis. Similar exclusion criteria were used by He et al. in the Nurses' Health Study, and Health Professionals Follow‐Up Studies examining ABO blood group and CHD. 5

Ascertainment of blood groups

The main exposure variables in our analyses are the ABO, RhD, and RhCE blood group systems. Phenotyping of these blood groups was performed at the time of blood donation as previously described. 29 , 37

Ascertainment of outcome variables

CHD was identified when hospitalization records listed a primary ICD‐10 diagnosis of I20‐I25. CeVD was similarly ascertained using ICD‐10 codes I60‐I69, G45, and G46. Peripheral vascular disease (PVD) admissions (I70‐I74) were few (n = 242) and excluded from further separate analyses due to potential sparse data bias. Heart failure was ascertained using ICD‐10 code I50. CVD outcome included CHD, CeVD, PVD, and heart failure.

Potential confounder variables

We included demographic, lifestyle, and health‐related variables as potential confounders, defined in Table S1. Demographic variables were age, sex, location, country of birth, education, and household income. Health and behavior variables included body‐mass index, self‐rated health, smoking, alcohol intake, and vigorous activity at recruitment. Additional variables included RhD blood group, treatment for high cholesterol, treatment for hypertension, family history of heart disease or stroke, personal history of hypertension, and personal history of diabetes at The 45 and Up Study baseline. 32 Variables with missing data were treated as a separate category.

Data analysis

Person‐years of follow‐up were calculated from the date of enrolment into the 45 and Up Study until the date of whichever of the following incidents occurred first: CVD event, death, or administrative censoring date (30 December 2018). The risk of CVD by ABO blood group was modeled using Cox regression with age‐ and sex‐adjustment, and an extended model including additional confounders defined a priori from existing literature. We report age‐ and sex‐adjusted and extended variables adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) as effect measures. The analyses were initially stratified by sex to investigate if there were differences in risk ratios. Further, effect modification by sex was assessed by including an ABO blood group and sex interaction term in the Cox model. The relationship between ABO blood group and CHD and CeVD was similarly modeled. Participants with a history of CVD or cancer prior to baseline were excluded from all of the Cox regression models except in one of the sensitivity analyses.

We also report the univariable association between RhD and RhCE blood group and risk of CHD and CeVD. An a priori decision was made to run separate adjusted regression models with Rh groups as the exposure only if a statistically significant association was identified in the univariable analysis as there are no direct identified relationships between Rh groups and CVDs in the published literature.

As part of the sensitivity analyses, we also conducted an age‐ and sex‐adjusted logistic regression model to examine the association between ABO blood groups and CHD events that were observed in the NSWAPDC dataset (from 2002 to 2018) regardless of the recruitment date into The 45 and Up Study. This was done to analyze the events of CHD that occurred before and after participants' recruitment into The 45 and Up Study and would be relevant in our context where the main exposure of interest, the blood groups, is a genetically determined risk and theoretically may exert its effect from the start of fetal development. Further, we also ran the extended model for CHD by keeping participants in the analysis who had a cancer diagnosis prior to recruitment and treating this variable as one of the potential confounders.

We used t‐tests to compare the distribution of continuous variables, and chi‐square tests to compare the distribution of categorical variables across the ABO blood group. From regression models, we report point estimates of HR along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. We used Stata 19 software (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA) to conduct all statistical analyses.

Ethics approval

The 45 and Up Study received ethics approval from the University of New South Wales Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC). Additionally, this study was approved by the NSW Population & Health Services Research Ethics Committee (2016/02/633), and the Australian Red Cross Lifeblood Ethics Committee (2015#13).

The baseline characteristics of participants differed across ABO blood groups for: country of birth, body‐mass index, alcohol consumption, and RhD and RhCE blood groups. For other characteristics, the groups did not differ significantly. ABO blood group and risk of CVD

During a mean follow‐up duration of 10.2 years (sd, 2.1), maximum 13.4 years, CVD occurred in 2698 (8.3%) participants. We did not observe a statistically significant association between ABO blood group and CVD in either of the unadjusted, age‐ and sex‐adjusted, and extended models (Table 2) when analyzed together or stratified by sex. The fully adjusted HR from the extended model for blood group A, B, and AB compared to group O were 1.03 (0.95, 1.12), 0.95 (0.83, 1.09), and 1.11 (0.92, 1.36), respectively.

ABO blood group and risk of CHD

During a mean follow‐up duration of 10.3 years (sd, 1.9), maximum 13.4 years, CHD occurred in 1808 (5.6%) participants. The incident rates of CHD per 1000 person‐years were 5.5 (95% CI 5.1, 5.8) for group O, 5.6 (95% CI 5.2, 6.0) for group A, 5.3 (95% CI 4.6, 6.1) for group B, and 6.5 (95% CI 5.2, 8.0) for group AB.

Sex‐stratified results showed that for males, compared with group O donors, the age‐adjusted HRs were 1.05 (95% CI 0.93, 1.18) for group A, 1.04 (0.86, 1.27) for group B and 1.22 (0.92, 1.62) for group AB. For females these were 1.06 (0.89, 1.27) for group A, 0.93 (0.70, 1.24) for group B and 1.32 (0.89, 1.95) for group AB. There was no effect modification by sex (p interaction = 0.89, Chi‐square value = 0.65). Further analyses were combined after finding no significant evidence of effect modification by sex, while still adjusting for sex as a confounder in all adjusted models.

In the combined analysis, there was a suggestive higher risk of CHD in group AB individuals compared with group O. Table 2 shows unadjusted, age‐ and sex‐adjusted, and fully adjusted HRs. Compared with blood group O, the aHRs in the extended model were 1.05 (0.95, 1.16) for group A, 0.99 (0.84, 1.16) for group B, and 1.27 (1.01, 1.59) for group AB (p = 0.045; see Tables 2 and S2).

We also tested the univariable association between RhD blood group system and CHD and observed a HR of 0.97 (0.86, 1.09) in individuals who were RhD− compared to those who were RhD+, with a similar estimate in the age‐ and sex‐adjusted model. Similarly, we tested the univariable association between RhCE blood groups and CHD and found no evidence for any significant differences between these groups (Table S3). As there was no statistical association observed in the univariable analyses for either RhD or RhCE blood types, we did not undertake further adjusted analyses.

ABO blood group and risk of CeVD

After excluding for history of CVD or cancer, a total of 32,510 participants were included in this analysis. During follow‐up, CeVD occurred in 738 (2.3%) of participants. We observed no significant association between ABO blood groups and the risk of CeVD in univariable, age‐ and sex‐adjusted, and extended models (Table 2), which all gave nearly identical results. When compared with blood group O, the fully adjusted HRs were: 0.98 (0.84, 1.14) for group A, 0.79 (0.60, 1.03) for group B and 0.94 (0.63, 1.40) for group AB (Table 2). Univariable models also found no significant associations between RhD or RhCE blood types and CeVD (Table S4).

Sensitivity analyses

An age‐ and sex‐adjusted logistic regression analysis using all CHD events observed in the data (from 2002 to 2018) showed the direction of results was similar to that of the cox‐proportional hazards regression model examining the relationship between blood groups and incident events of CHD. Compared with group O, age‐ and sex‐adjusted odds ratio (OR) was 1.08 (0.99, 1.16) for group A, 1.01 (0.88, 1.15) for group B, and 1.27 (1.05, 1.54) for group AB (p = 0.012).

When participants with a history of cancer at baseline were treated as one of the confounders, rather than being excluded from the extended model while examining the relationship between ABO blood groups and CHD, the HRs were again similar to that of the main analysis. In this sensitivity analysis, compared with group O, aHRs were 1.04 (0.94, 1.14) for group A, 0.99 (0.84, 1.15) for group B, and 1.25 (1.00, 1.56) for group AB (p = 0.053).

DISCUSSION

In this study, which is the first to investigate whether there are any associations between ABO and Rh blood group systems and CVD in an Australian population, we observed a borderline association between blood group AB and higher risk of CHD, whereas no significant associations were observed for group A or B individually. No significant association was observed between ABO blood group and risk of CVD (that included CHD, CeVD, PVD, and heart failure together). Also, we did not find an association when ABO blood group and risk of CeVD was analyzed separately. Our study adds important data to the handful of previous studies from elsewhere in the world that have investigated associations of groups A, B, and AB individually compared to group O, rather than grouping all non‐group O individuals together. 5 , 11 , 15 Furthermore, this is one of the first studies to report the risk for RhD and RhCE blood group systems with CHD and CeVD, where no association for either indication was found.

The findings of our study partially align with those reported by He et al., where they analyzed two large cohort datasets from the Nurses' Health Study (NHS, n = 62,073) and the Health Professionals Follow‐Up Study (HPFS, n = 27,428) in the United States. 5 Similar to our analyses, they had also excluded people with prior CHD, cancers, and stroke, and started following up for incident CHD from the time of enrolment into the studies. 5 Consistent with their results, we observed a higher risk of CHD in individuals with group AB compared with those with group O. In contrast to the US study, we did not find any increased risk for individuals with group A or B. While the reasons for the difference in findings are unclear, it is worth noting that in the US study the relative risk ratios for group A and group B were much lower than for group AB. Moreover, when He et al. analyzed the NHS and HPFS cohorts separately, they also did not observe statistically significant associations for group A and B, suggesting that limited statistical power may have hindered the detection of smaller effect sizes in our study. 5 Additionally, our study population, composed exclusively of blood donors, appears to be healthier overall than the cohorts studied by He et al., as indicated by a substantially lower prevalence of current smoking. 5 Our findings are in agreement with a Canadian study involving a blood donor population which also found a significant association between group AB and combined cardiovascular risk, but not for group A or B. 15

Our study has several strengths, including comprehensive data on key CHD confounders and laboratory‐confirmed blood group data. Extensive analyses, including sensitivity tests, further support the robustness of our findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. As blood donors are typically healthier than the general population, generalizability may be limited. Participants aged ≥45 years (mean 57) were recruited during 2006–2009; thus, comparisons with younger cohorts should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, for outcomes that are related to genetically determined risk factors, recruiting at mid–late adulthood and following up for incident cases may introduce survivor (depletion‐of‐susceptibles) bias since individuals who developed CHD before the start of follow‐up are excluded. If a blood group (e.g. AB) is associated with earlier onset of CHD, fewer high‐risk individuals from that group will remain event‐free and eligible for follow‐up for incident disease, biasing effect estimates toward the null and attenuating the observed association. We have addressed this partly through logistic regression modeling of all prevalent cases of CHD, in which risk ratios were not markedly different. Also, it should be noted that, while NSWAPDC captures hospitalized CHD cases, milder or fatal events without hospital admission may be missed. Further, although data linkage reduces subjective bias and manual errors, it remains susceptible to inaccuracies. However, CHeReL audits using the Master Linkage Key report a false‐positive rate of 0.05%. 36

In conclusion, we observed a borderline association between blood group AB and CHD risk compared with group O. This trend is consistent with reports from some large prospective cohort studies in other countries. Thus, ABO blood groups appear to be a possible risk factor for CHD. Future mechanistic studies could elucidate possible causal pathways. Since ABO is a genetically determined risk factor, our focus remains to advise all blood donors and nondonors, regardless of blood type, to follow healthy lifestyle interventions, including not smoking, participating in regular physical activity, having healthy diets, and making regular general practitioner visits to reduce CVD risk. 38 , 39 , 40
