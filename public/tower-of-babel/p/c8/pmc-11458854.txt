Restoring social deficits in IRSp53-deleted mice: chemogenetic inhibition of ventral dentate gyrus Emx1-expressing cells
Authors: Su Hyun Kim, Bomee Lee, Seong Mi Lee, Yangsik Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIRSp53 is a synaptic scaffold protein reported to be involved in schizophrenia, autism spectrum disorders, and social deficits in knockout mice. Identifying critical brain regions and cells related to IRSp53 deletion is expected to be of great help in the treatment of psychiatric problems. In this study, we performed chemogenetic inhibition within the ventral dentate gyrus (vDG) of mice with IRSp53 deletion in Emx1-expressing cells (Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox). We observed the recovery of social deficits after chemogenetic inhibition within vDG of Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice. Additionally, chemogenetic activation induced social deficits in Emx1-Cre mice. CRHR1 expression increased in the hippocampus of Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice, and CRHR1 was reduced by chemogenetic inhibition. Htd2, Ccn1, and Atp61l were decreased in bulk RNA sequencing, and Eya1 and Ecrg4 were decreased in single-cell RNA sequencing of the hippocampus in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice compared to control mice. This study determined that the vDG is a critical brain region for social deficits caused by IRSp53 deletion. Social deficits in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice were recovered through chemogenetic inhibition, providing clues for new treatment methods for psychiatric disorders accompanied by social deficits.Subject terms: Schizophrenia, Psychiatric disorders, Molecular neuroscience

Introduction

IRSp53, also known as BAIAP2, is a synaptic scaffold protein that affects actin polymerization and synaptic transmission [1–5]. Mice lacking IRSp53 in Emx1-expressing dorsal telencephalic glutamatergic neurons displayed social deficits, hyperactivity, and decreased prepulse inhibition [6, 7]. Furthermore, re-expression of IRSp53 in adult mice restores social deficits without changes in hyperactivity [8]. The function of IRSp53 in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus is suggested to be important for synaptic functions and various behaviors including social behavior, thus further research is needed to identify brain regions for social deficits.

In our previous studies, we focused on the promoter Emx1 (Empty spiracles homeobox 1), a gene expressed in neural stem cells, progenitor cells, differentiated neurons, and glial cells [9, 10]. Emx1-expressing neural stem cells develop and differentiate into neurons and glial cells in the subventricular zone (SVZ) and subgranular zone (SGZ), where neurogenesis occurs from early E9.5 embryos to adulthood. Adult neurogenesis occurs in the SVZ, which differentiates into inhibitory granule cell neurons in the olfactory bulb, and in the SGZ, which differentiates into excitatory granule cell neurons in the dentate gyrus. Dysregulated adult neurogenesis is associated with psychiatric disorders, including cognitive decline and mood disorders [11–15]. In this study, we attempted to restore social deficits by regulating Emx1-expressing cells in the SGZ, which contributes to adult neurogenesis.

The ventral DG (vDG) is associated with mood disorders, such as major depressive disorder, and the action of antidepressants is associated with the restoration of neurogenesis in the vDG via 5HT1 and TrkB receptors [13, 16–23]. A study by Anacker et al. using Cre-induced chemogenetic modulation and apoptosis in Nestin-expressing cells in the vDG showed that chemogenetic inhibition and Cre-induced apoptosis resulted in reduced social behavior and locomotion [24]. A difference from previous studies showing social deficits through chemogenetic inhibition in Nestin-Cre WT mice is that Nestin expression was restricted to early-stage neural stem and progenitor cells [25]. In this study, we investigated the recovery of social deficits through chemogenetic modulation of Emx1-expressing cells in the vDG of IRSp53 deleted mice.

The present study was conducted to identify brain regions and cellular/molecular changes related to social deficits and their recovery in Emx1-Cre conditional IRSp53 knockout mice (Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox). Recovery from social deficits via chemogenetic modulation has been attempted in Emx1-expressing cells in the vDG. In addition, protein and transcriptomic changes were explored at the tissue and single-cell levels in the hippocampi of control and Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice. Thus, we aimed to restore social deficits by regulating Emx1-expressing cells in the SGZ of the vDG through chemogenetic modulation. By employing these methods, we sought to influence the activity and function of these cells, thereby contributing to adult neurogenesis and potentially mitigating the associated psychiatric symptoms.

Methods and materials

Animals and experimental design

C57BL/6 J background mice had ad libitum access to food and water and were housed with 4–5 animals per cage under a 12 h light/dark cycle. We included only male mice in this study because previous studies have shown that only male mice exhibit social deficits. All mice were bred and maintained in accordance with the requirements of the National Center for Mental Health (NCMH) Animal Research. All procedures and methods were approved by the NCMH Animal Research Committee (IACUC No: NCMH-2005-001-003-01). Mice were identified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) genotyping using the following PCR primers: IRSp53 flox AGGAGGTGTTTCTGCTCTGG/AATAGCAGTCTGGGGTCTGG and Cre CGTACTGACGGTGGGAGAAT/TGCATGATCTCCGGTATTGA. Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice was a gift from Eunjoon Kim, KAIST (Daejeon, South Korea) [26].

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Data normality was determined using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test. Normally distributed data were analyzed using Student’s t test and analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post hoc tests. Data that failed the normality test were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Further detailed information on methods is available in Supplementary Information.

Results

Chemical inhibition of Emx1-expressing cells in the ventral dentate gyrus restores social deficits in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice

In previous studies, we observed an increase in the intrinsic excitability of pyramidal neurons in layers V and VI of the medial prefrontal cortex with the deletion of IRSp53 [6, 7]. Additionally, electrophysiological measurements of synaptic transmission and excitability were performed in granule cells of the DG, and a decrease in mIPSC frequency was observed in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice mice (Supplementary Fig. 1). In this study, chemogenetic modulation was used to induce an increase in excitability in the vDG of control mice and suppress excitability in the vDG of Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice to examine whether each affected social behavior.

First, AAV9-hSyn-DIO-hM3Dq-mCherry chemogenetic activation was performed in the vDG of Emx1-Cre mice as a control. Because we used an AAV vector with the human Synapsin I (hSyn) promoter, it was expected to be expressed mainly in neurons [27]. A small dose of clozapine (0.5 mg/kg) induced chemogenetic activation, and there was no difference in locomotion between the vehicle and clozapine groups. However, mice subjected to chemogenetic activation showed decreased exploration of a stranger mouse compared to that of an object (Fig. 1a, b).

Behavioral experiments showed that chemical inhibition of Emx1-expressing cells in the ventral dentate gyrus restored social deficits in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice.

a Normal locomotor activities after chemogenetic activation of Emx1-Cre wild-type mice compared to before chemogenetic activation in the open-field test. n = 7 mice, ns, not significant, Mann–Whitney U test. b Decreased sociability after Emx1-Cre wild-type mice compared to before chemogenetic activation in the three-chambered social interaction test. n = 7, **p < 0.01, ns, not significant, two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test. c Decreased locomotor activities after chemogenetic inhibition of Emx1 cKO (Emx1-Cre;Irsp53fl/fl) mice compared to before chemogenetic inhibition in the three-chambered social interaction test. n = 9 mice, ns, not significant, Student’s t test. d Improved sociability after Emx1 cKO (Emx1-Cre;Irsp53fl/fl) mice compared to before chemogenetic inhibition in the open-field test. n = 9, **p < 0.01, ns, not significant, two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test. e Cre-selective DIO-hM4Di-mCherry expression was observed in the right ventral dentate gyrus: scale bar, 200 μm.

In Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice, chemogenetic inhibition was conducted using AAV-hSyn-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry. There was decreased locomotion in the clozapine group compared to the control group, and also social deficits were improved (Fig. 1c, d).

Further experiments investigated whether fluoxetine improves social deficits in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice. Instead, social deficits were induced in control (IRSp53 flox/flox) mice, and social deficits did not improve in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b).

Behavioral experiments showed that chemogenetic activation of Emx1-expressing cells in the vDG of Emx1-Cre mice reduced social behavior, and chemical inhibition of Emx1-expressing cells in the vDG of Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice helped ameliorate social deficits.

Increased CRHR1 expression in the hippocampus in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice is restored by chemogenetic inhibition

To observe the changes caused by IRSp53 deletion, we examined protein expression in the hippocampus of the mice (Fig. 2a–h). Compared with control (IRSp53 flox/flox) mice, Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice showed increased levels of CRHR1, a corticotropin-releasing hormone receptor on the stress-related HPA axis, SOX2, a neural stem cell marker, and NeuN, a mature neuronal marker. Additionally, the deletion of IRSp53/BAIAP2 in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice was confirmed.

Protein expression of the hippocampus showed increased CRHR1 in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice is restored by chemogenetic inhibition.

CRHR1 (a), SOX2 (d), and NeuN (e) were increased in Emx1 cKO mice, but BAIAP2 was not expressed in Emx1 cKO (Emx1-Cre;Irsp53fl/fl) mice compared to control (Irsp53fl/fl) mice (g). n = 4 hippocampi each group. *p < 0.05, ns, not significant, Student’s t test (a–d, f, g) and Mann–Whitney U test (e). h An image of immunoblotting results between control and Emx1 cKO mice. i–n CRHR1 (a) were decreased after chemogenetic inhibition in vDG of Emx1 cKO mice compared to before chemogenetic inhibition, but SOX2 showed no difference before and after chemogenetic inhibition in vDG of Emx1 cKO. n = 3 hippocampi each group. *p < 0.05, ns, not significant, Student’s t test. o An image of immunoblotting results before and after chemogenetic inhibition in vDG of Emx1 cKO mice.

Regarding hippocampal protein expression before and after chemogenetic inhibition of Emx1-expressing cells in the vDG of Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice (Fig. 2i–o), CRHR1 decreased after chemogenetic inhibition, and SOX2 and NeuN did not differ between the control and cKO groups.

In addition, changes in hippocampal protein expression before and after chemogenetic activation of Emx1-expressing cells in the vDG of Emx1-Cre mice (Supplementary Fig. 3) showed a decrease in doublecortin (DCX), a neural progenitor marker, without changes in CRHR1.

Taken together, we observed increased CRHR1, SOX2, and NeuN protein expression in the hippocampus of Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice compared to that in control mice, decreased CRHR1 expression after chemogenetic inhibition of Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice, and decreased DCX expression due to chemogenetic activation of Emx1-Cre mice.

Increased Htd2, Ccn1 and Atp6ap1l expression and altered biological pathways in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice are revealed in hippocampal transcriptome analysis

Bulk RNA sequencing was performed for hippocampal transcriptome analysis of the control and Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice (Fig. 3). RNA expression in control (flox1-3) and Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox (cko1-3) mice was relatively well-differentiated (Fig. 3a). In gene ontology (GO) terms, the regulation of multicellular organismal processes and vitamin binding had the greatest influence on biological processes and molecular function, respectively.

Among the differentially expressed genes (DEG) between groups (Fig. 3d, e), Htd2, Ccn1, and Atp6ap1l showed significant differences, and the results of Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice showed fold-changes of 3.07, 2.14, and 2.66, respectively, compared to control mice.

Through bulk RNA sequencing of the hippocampus, changes in GO terms, such as the regulation of multicellular organism processes and vitamin binding, were revealed in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice, and differences in Htd2, Ccn1, and Atp6ap1l were identified.

Eya1 in astrocytes and Ecrg4 in microglial cells and perivascular macrophages in the hippocampus of Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice are showed in single cell transcriptomic analysis

Fresh hippocampal tissue was isolated and dissociated to investigate the effects of IRSp53 deletion in Emx1-expressing cells, and single-cell RNA sequencing was performed (Fig. 4). Cell distribution was as follows: oligodendrocytes (33.4%), microglia (32.5%), vascular endothelial cells (13.5%), and choroidal epithelial cells (7.9%) (Fig. 4a–c).

The most obvious difference between the groups was a decrease in Eya1 expression in astrocytes and an increase in Ecrg4 expression in perivascular macrophages, vascular smooth muscle cells, microglia, and vascular endothelial cells in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice compared to control mice.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that social deficits in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice could be effectively recovered through chemogenetic inhibition of Emx1-expressing cells in the ventral dentate gyrus (vDG). Behavioral experiments confirmed that chemogenetic activation of these vDG cells in Emx1-Cre mice led to a reduction in social behavior, while inhibition in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice resulted in significant improvements in social deficits. At the molecular level, our protein analysis revealed increased expression of CRHR1, SOX2, and NeuN in the hippocampus of Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice compared to controls, with a notable decrease in CRHR1 following chemogenetic inhibition. These changes indicate a potential link between CRHR1 modulation and the observed behavioral outcomes. Furthermore, transcriptomic analyses provided additional insights, highlighting alterations in gene expression related to multicellular organism processes and vitamin binding, with specific differences in Htd2, Ccn1, and Atp6ap1l. Single-cell RNA sequencing further identified distinct changes in glial cell populations, particularly decreased Eya1 expression in astrocytes and increased Ecrg4 expression in microglia, which may contribute to the observed behavioral and molecular phenotypes.

Role of vDG neurons in social behavior

In this study, we successfully demonstrated the rescue of social deficits in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice through chemogenetic inhibition of the ventral dentate gyrus (vDG). This suggests that the inhibition of Emx1-expressing neurons plays a crucial role in restoring sociability. Using an AAV vector driven by the hSyn promoter, our approach provided neuron-specific chemical modulation, which may differ in its effects compared to other models [9, 10, 13, 24, 27–31]. Electrophysiological measurements in this study revealed a decrease in mIPSC frequency in the granule cells of the dentate gyrus (DG) in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice, suggesting reduced inhibitory synaptic transmission relative to excitatory transmission. We observed that chemogenetic inhibition effectively suppressed this heightened excitatory tendency, which was associated with the recovery of social behavior in these mice. Conversely, chemogenetic activation of vDG neurons increased neuronal excitability and induced social deficits in Emx1-Cre mice, reinforcing the critical role of neuronal excitability in regulating social behaviors.

Additionally, our recent exploration of the lateral hypothalamus (LH) within the prefrontal-LH-ventral tegmental area (VTA) circuit [26], which is implicated in social behavior, revealed that chemogenetic inhibition of LH-projecting Emx1-expressing vDG granule cells also led to a decrease in locomotion and the recovery of social deficits (Supplementary Fig. 4). This points to a complex interaction between the LH and vDG regions, which warrants further investigation to deepen our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying sociability.

While these findings are promising, our study also attempted to connect IRSp53 deletion and chemogenetic manipulations with alterations in neurogenesis in vDG. However, this study do not include experiments on neurodevelopment, and the lack of specific neurogenesis markers limits conclusions of this association. Future research should incorporate targeted markers to explore the potential link between neurogenesis and social behavior more definitively.

Implication of CRHR1 on social behavior in psychiatric disorders

Stress-vulnerable individuals often exhibit stronger activation of the HPA axis, including CRHR1, and struggle to adapt to even mild external stressors. Genome-wide association studies have linked genes associated with stress vulnerability to HPA axis activation [32–36]. This stress vulnerability is relevant to clinical conditions like PTSD and mood disorders [37–39]. Notably, psychiatric risk genes are not confined to a single diagnostic category but span multiple conditions including schizophrenia, mood disorders, and autism spectrum disorders. Key stress-related genes include serotonin transporter, brain-derived neurotrophic factor, bromodomain 1, Cacna1a, Catechol-O-methyltransferase, Disrupted-in-schizophrenia-1, Estrogen receptor alpha, Fkbp5, Glutamate decarboxylase1, and Neuregulin1 [40–45].

In this study, we observed that the deletion of IRSp53 results in a stress vulnerability pattern characterized by HPA axis activation, increased CRHR1 expression, and the presence of social deficits. We attempted to restore CRHR1 levels and improve social behavior through chemogenetic modulation of the ventral dentate gyrus (vDG). CRHR activation, influenced by both environmental stressors and genetic factors [46, 47], can enhance synaptic function in the hippocampus, particularly in the dentate gyrus, when activated briefly [48]. However, prolonged activation may lead to synaptic deterioration, such as decreased synapse numbers [48]. Moreover, CRHR1 activation has been linked to increased excitability of hippocampal CA1 glutamatergic neurons, a mechanism implicated in stress-related epileptic disorders [49–54].

Deletion of CRHR1 in telencephalic glutamatergic neurons has been associated with cognitive deficits, including memory impairment, and CRHR1 overexpression has been linked to heightened stress vulnerability and increased alcohol consumption under stress [55–58]. The IRSp53 deleted mice used in this study likely resulted in genetic susceptibilities without prominent stress triggers. Our findings suggest that social abnormalities can be addressed by correcting the increased excitability in the vDG caused by genetic or environmental factors, using chemogenetic modulation.

Broader neurobiological changes related to psychiatric disorders in IRSp53 deleted mice

Bulk RNA sequencing analysis identified several key genes with potential implications for brain function and psychiatric disorders. Htd2, a crucial enzyme in mitochondrial fatty acid synthesis (mtFAS) found in adipocytes, has been linked to neurodegeneration when mtFAS is disrupted [59–62]. The extracellular matrix protein Ccn1 is involved in repair mechanisms and has been associated with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) due to its role in extracellular matrix and cell cycle-related gene expression [63], particularly in Shank3 deficiency [64]. Increased Ccn1 secretion has also been implicated in glioblastoma [65], contributing to macrophage infiltration, and has been associated with the age of onset in schizophrenia [66]

Atp6apl1, another gene of interest, was found to be decreased in patients with PTSD compared to controls [67–69]. This gene, a paralog of vesicular ATPase, is thought to play a role in stress-related brain functions. Although no direct correlation was found with changes such as CRHR1 found in this study, these genes are associated with brain functions and psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia, neurodegeneration, and stress-related conditions like PTSD.

Single-cell RNA sequencing further highlighted Eya1, which is crucial for cranial placode neurogenesis and is implicated in BOR syndrome, a condition linked to both autism and neurogenesis-related brain regions [70–73]. Ecrg4, identified as a tumor suppressor gene in various cancers [74, 75], is also involved in brain development, cerebrospinal fluid homeostasis, and neural aging [74, 76–78]. A variant of Ecrg4 has been associated with Alzheimer’s disease [79, 80].

Although our study did not establish a direct connection between these findings and CRHR1, the identified genes suggest broader neurobiological changes relevant to various psychiatric disorders. Future studies should explore these pathways in more detail, using targeted analyses to clarify their roles in brain function and disorder susceptibility.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, the chemogenetic modulation was restricted to Emx1-expressing cells in the ventral dentate gyrus (vDG), limiting the scope of our findings. We focused on the expression of specific proteins, such as CRHR1, making it challenging to directly link protein expression with transcriptomic data. Although the protein and transcriptomic changes observed are not exclusively associated with psychiatric disorders like schizophrenia and autism spectrum disorder, they are linked to pathways implicated in these conditions, such as CRHR1 upregulation and neurogenesis disruption. These findings may offer insights into shared mechanisms underlying psychiatric disorders and social deficits.

Additionally, we were unable to validate the transcriptomic analyses, such as through qPCR, or compare transcriptomic changes before and after chemogenetic modulation. Another limitation is the fragility of adult neurons, which likely led to neuronal loss during hippocampal dissociation for single-cell RNA sequencing. Therefore, more meticulous protocols and experience are necessary to improve the accuracy of hippocampal dissociation and subsequent analyses [81–84].

Conclusion

Chemogenetic inhibition of vDG significantly affected the recovery of social deficits in Emx1-Cre;IRSp53 flox/flox mice, and the restoration of CRHR1 reduction in the hippocampus was observed as a mechanism. While the current study provides compelling evidence for the role of vDG neurons, future experiments should include additional controls to confirm the specificity of the observed effects, particularly those related to the molecular mechanisms in social deficits. Further studies are needed to determine whether regulating excitability in Emx1-expressing cells of the vDG helps mice with genetic susceptibility to stress or prominent environmental stress to recover from social abnormalities. If this approach helps adult mice with stress vulnerability and social deficits recover, it may provide clues for the treatment of psychiatric disorders in the future.
