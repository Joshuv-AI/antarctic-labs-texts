Integrated transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analyses reveal molecular mechanisms underlying clutch length differences in goose ovaries
Authors: Hechuan Wang, Yunuo Liu, Ke Jiang, Jiaxin Yin, Kexin Cong, Xinyi Miao, Weiran Yang, Ying Zhang, Shengjun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clutch length is a key determinant of reproductive efficiency in geese and strongly positively correlates with egg production. We recorded daily egg production in 280 individually housed Zi geese, calculated clutch-related indices, and selected 12 geese to form long-clutch (LC) and short-clutch (SC) groups for ovarian transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analyses. The results showed that egg number, large clutch length, large clutch number, average clutch length, and average clutch number were significantly higher in LC than in SC groups (P < 0.0001). Transcriptomic analysis identified 885 differentially expressed genes enriched in oocyte development and ovarian steroidogenesis, with APOB, PLA2G4C, MMP2, MMP9, and NOBOX as key genes; proteomic analysis identified 437 differentially abundant proteins enriched in arachidonic acid metabolism and mitophagy, with CXCL12, RARB, and MAD2L1 as key proteins; and metabolomic analysis identified 35 differentially abundant metabolites enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, with lactic acid, guanidinoacetic acid, and 3-hydroxybutyrylcarnitine as key metabolites. Integration of multi-omics datasets highlighted a lactate-associated cross-omics signature supported by YWHAZ at the protein level and by the lactate transporter SLC16A3. Collectively, these findings deepen our understanding of the molecular basis underlying clutch-length variation in goose ovaries and highlight candidate genes, proteins, and metabolites for future functional validation.

Introduction

Egg-laying performance in poultry is determined by both clutch length and the inter-clutch interval; birds with superior laying ability typically have longer clutches and shorter intervals between clutches (Bao et al., 2022). In geese, a clutch is a sustained period of oviposition in which the interval between consecutive eggs does not exceed 48 h; an inter-egg interval exceeding 48 h constitutes a clutch pause (Brun et al., 2003). Clutch length is a key indicator of sustained ovulatory activity and is significantly positively associated with total egg number (Wang et al., 2023).

Our previous work (Liu et al., 2025) suggested that several ovarian genes, including AVPR1A, FGF14, and LHCGR, together with key metabolites from the tricarboxylic acid cycle and glycolysis (e.g., pyruvate, isocitric acid, D/L‑serine, 3-phospho-d-glycerate, succinate, glycine, and glutamic acid) may be critical determinants of clutch performance in geese. We further identified the gene–metabolite pair ACSL4–phosphoenolpyruvate as a potentially important regulatory axis influencing clutch traits. However, that study integrated only transcriptomics and metabolomics; incorporating proteomics is needed to bridge this evidentiary gap and strengthen mechanistic inference.

Accordingly, this study presents an integrative transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analysis of the goose ovary. Our objectives are to construct core ovarian regulatory networks underlying clutch performance and to identify biomarkers, thereby providing a theoretical basis and candidate targets for molecular breeding and marker-assisted selection in geese.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The Ethics Committee of Science and Technology of the Heilongjiang Bayi Agricultural University approved this study (License No.: DWKJXY2025069). All experimental operations were carried out under the relevant guidelines and supervision. Written informed consent was obtained from all animal owners prior to their animals’ participation in this study.

Animals, clutch length assessment, and sample collection

To investigate phenotypic divergence in clutch length in geese, we established a controlled cohort from March to June 2025 at the Branch of Animal Husbandry and Veterinary of Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences (47.26°N, 123.68°E). We individually housed 280 healthy, 7-month-old female Zi geese (Anser cygnoides) in cages within naturally ventilated poultry houses, fed them a uniform diet, and provided water ad libitum. The basal diet was identical to that used in our previous study (Liu et al., 2025). For each goose, egg number (EN) and egg weight (EW) were recorded twice daily (09:00 and 16:00). However, because checking each goose’s laying status twice daily still did not allow determination of the exact oviposition time at an hourly resolution, clutch laying and clutch pause were defined using the 48-h threshold established in previous studies (Bao et al., 2022), and clutch-related indices were calculated from day-level records. At the end, we calculated four clutch-related indices for each bird using the Poultry Breeding Information Intelligent Management and Analysis System (Hand Era Technology, Beijing, China), included large clutch length (LCL), large clutch number (LCN), average clutch length (ACL), and average clutch number (ACN). Definitions of LCL, LCN, ACL, and ACN followed those described in previous studies (Bao et al., 2022).

Candidate geese were first defined using the upper (top 25%) and lower (bottom 25%) quartiles of ACL. The long-clutch (LC) and short-clutch (SC) groups were then selected from these candidates (n = 6 per group). ACN, LCL, and LCN were subsequently reported as supplementary descriptive indices for the selected individuals, rather than being used for group assignment. Prior to final selection, extreme outliers were removed to avoid undue influence of atypical records. Selected geese were fasted beginning at 20:00 on the day before sampling and were euthanized at a uniform time (08:00) the following morning to standardize pre-sampling conditions and minimize time-of-day effects. Then, geese were moved to the dissection facility, anesthetized with inhaled 1.5–3.0% isoflurane, and humanely euthanized by carotid transection. Immediately after collection, each ovary was divided into two portions. One portion was transferred to sterile cryovials, immersed in RNAlater Stabilization Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and stored at −20°C (as recommended by the manufacturer) until subsequent transcriptomic analyses. The remaining portion was placed in sterile cryovials, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until proteomic and metabolomic analyses.

RNA Sequencing, data acquisition and processing

Total RNA was extracted from ovarian tissue samples using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The quality and purity of the extracted RNA were assessed using the Agilent® 2100 Bioanalyzer Instrument (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) in conjunction with the Agilent® RNA 6000 Nano kit (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). RNA samples exhibiting an RNA Integrity Number > 7.0 were deemed suitable for subsequent library construction. mRNA was purified from the total RNA using Dynabeads Oligo(dT)25 magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The purified mRNA was then fragmented and reverse-transcribed into first-strand cDNA using SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Second-strand cDNA synthesis was performed incorporating dUTP (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), DNA Polymerase I (E. coli) (New England Biolabs, MA, USA), and RNase H (New England Biolabs, MA, USA), to generate U-labeled double-stranded cDNA. Following end repair, 'A'-tailing, and adapter ligation, the U-labeled second strand was excised using Uracil-DNA Glycosylase (New England Biolabs, MA, USA). The resulting libraries were amplified via PCR, purified, and validated to have average insert sizes of 300±50 bp. Finally, paired-end sequencing (PE150) was conducted on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, CA, USA). Raw sequencing reads were processed with Cutadapt (version 5.1) (Martin, 2011) to remove adapters and low-quality sequences, yielding clean reads. Quality control of these clean reads was performed using FastQC (version 0.12.0).

The clean reads were then aligned to the goose reference genome (GCF_040182565.1) (Zhao et al., 2024) using HISAT2 (version 2.2.1) (Kim et al., 2019). Mapped reads were assembled into transcripts using StringTie (version 3.0.0) (Shumate et al., 2022), and these assemblies were merged across samples using GffCompare (version 0.12.6) (Pertea and Pertea, 2020). Transcript abundance was estimated with StringTie and ballgown package (version 2.40.0) (Frazee et al., 2015), and mRNA expression levels were quantified based on Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package (version 1.48.2) (Love et al., 2014).

Proteomic profiling, data acquisition and processing

The samples were combined with a composite lysis buffer (composed of 8 mmol/L urea and 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl) and 1 × cOmplete, Mini Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), followed by ice-bathing for 5 min. After adding steel beads, the samples were lysed for 2 min using a cryogenic grinder (Jingxin Industrial Development, Shanghai, China). The lysate was then centrifuged at 20,000 × g and 4 °C for 15 min in a refrigerated centrifuge (Eppendorf AG, Hamburg, Germany), and the supernatant was collected. Dithiothreitol was added to a final concentration of 10 mmol/L, and the mixture was incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 1 h. Subsequently, iodoacetamide was introduced to a final concentration of 20 mmol/L and allowed to react in the dark for 30 min. Protein concentration was first determined using the Bradford method, followed by assessment of protein integrity and molecular weight distribution via SDS-PAGE. For each sample, 150 μg of quality-controlled protein was digested with 3 μg of Trypsin (Promega, Beijing, China) at 37 °C for 15 h.

The resulting peptides were desalted using Oasis solid-phase extraction cartridges (Waters, MA, USA) and dried under vacuum. Equal amounts of peptides from all samples were pooled and separated by high-pH reverse-phase liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) using an UltiMate 3000 binary rapid separation system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) equipped with a ZORBAX Extend 300 C18 column (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). The collected peptide fractions were lyophilized. The peptides were then reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid, centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was subjected to nano LC-MS/MS analysis on a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) equipped with an EASY-Spray HPLC column (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The separated peptides were analyzed by Data Independent Acquisition (DIA) on an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). DIA mass spectrometry data were processed with DIA-NN (version 2.0) (Demichev et al., 2020) for protein identification and quantification. The protein–protein interaction (PPI) network for the proteomics dataset was constructed using the STRING database (Szklarczyk et al., 2023) and visualized in Cytoscape (version 3.10.4) (Shannon et al., 2003), isolated nodes were excluded.

Metabolomic profiling, data acquisition and processing

Samples were placed in ice-cold 80% methanol containing stainless-steel beads and homogenized using a cryogenic grinder (Jingxin Industrial Development, Shanghai, China). The homogenates were incubated at −20°C for 30 min, then centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 15 min and the supernatants were collected. The collected extracts were lyophilized and reconstituted in ice-cold 50% methanol; after reconstitution the samples were centrifuged again at 20,000 × g for 15 min and the supernatants were recovered for analysis.

Ultra-performance liquid chromatography–high-resolution mass spectrometry (UPLC—HRMS) analysis was performed using a Vanquish Flex UHPLC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC T3 column (Waters, MA, USA) coupled to a Q Exactive Plus Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). For each sample, data were acquired in both positive and negative ionization modes. Raw data were processed with XCMS (v4.6.4) (Benton et al., 2010) for peak picking, peak grouping and retention-time correction, and with metaX (v1.4.2) (Wen et al., 2017) for peak processing, metabolite identification and downstream analyses.

Integration of transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data

All transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data were first log2(X + 1) transformed and then z-score normalized across samples. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment was performed separately for the transcriptome and the proteome, and the overlap between enriched pathways was defined as the shared transcriptome–proteome KEGG pathway. Genes and proteins annotated to these shared pathways were extracted from the transcriptomic and proteomic datasets, and the intersection of these lists defined the final set of genes with measurements at both the mRNA and protein levels within the shared KEGG pathways. For each gene in each sample, an integrated gene score was computed as the arithmetic mean of its z-score normalized RNA sequencing expression and protein abundance.

Statistical analysis

Independent-samples t tests for EN, EW, LCL, LCN, ACL, and ACN were conducted in SPSS (version 27.0). Data are presented as mean ±SD. Group comparisons were specified as the LC group versus the SC group. For omics layers, differential features were defined using nominal P-values together with effect-size thresholds to generate candidate sets for downstream integrative multi-omics analyses. Given the exploratory design and sample size, feature-level false discovery rate (FDR) filtering was not used as the primary gatekeeper; instead, biological interpretation was supported by pathway/gene set–level analyses with FDR control and by cross-omics consistency. In transcriptomic, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 and | log2 (fold change (FC)) | ≥ 1. In proteomic, differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) were identified with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 and | log2 (FC) | ≥ 0.263. In metabolomic, differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) were identified with a significance threshold of P < 0.05, | log2 (FC) | ≥ 0.263, and variable importance in projection (VIP) ≥ 1. The cutoff |log2(FC)| ≥ 0.263 (i.e., a 1.2-fold change) was applied, as a moderate effect-size threshold commonly used in quantitative MS-based proteomics (Chen et al., 2024b) and metabolomics (Zhang et al., 2022) to balance analytical variability with biological relevance. Pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated among DAMs to generate a correlation matrix. Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway analyses, was performed using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.16.0) (Wu et al., 2021), with a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Furthermore, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was implemented using the GSEA (version 4.4.0) to evaluate the enrichment of GO terms and KEGG pathways, with significant enrichment defined by |Normalized Enrichment Score (NES)| > 1, a nominal P < 0.05, and a false discovery rate (FDR) q < 0.05. In the multi-omics integration analysis, pairwise Spearman correlation coefficients were computed between integrated gene scores and metabolite abundances. Statistical significance was defined as follows: P < 0.05, significant; P < 0.01, highly significant; P < 0.001, very highly significant; and P < 0.0001, extremely highly significant. All figures were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1) and R (version 4.5.0).

Integrated transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analyses reveal clutch length–associated candidate mechanisms in goose ovaries

KEGG enrichment analyses of the transcriptomic and proteomic datasets identified 28 pathways enriched in both layers, including Oocyte meiosis, Progesterone-mediated oocyte maturation, ECM–receptor interaction, Cell cycle, and the FoxO signaling pathway (Fig. 6A). Cross-omics comparison further indicated limited concordance between the metabolome and the gene/protein layers: 10 pathways overlapped between the transcriptome and metabolome, and only 1 pathway overlapped between the proteome and metabolome; importantly, no pathway was simultaneously enriched across all three omics layers (Fig. 6B). To prioritize regulatory programs supported by both mRNA and protein evidence, we mapped the 28 shared pathways back to the expression matrices, yielding 2,610 pathway-mapped genes and 402 pathway-mapped proteins, including 373 molecules quantified at both transcript and protein levels (Fig. 6C). Within this jointly supported set, 71 genes met the DEG criteria and 22 proteins met the DAP criteria when comparing LC and SC ovaries. Among the 28 shared pathways, ECM–receptor interaction, Cell cycle, and Oocyte meiosis showed stronger enrichment signals and encompassed larger numbers of mapped genes and proteins (Fig. 6D). Finally, metabolite–gene association analysis added an additional layer of integration by linking differential metabolites to the jointly supported molecular set. Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant positive associations between lactate and YWHAZ, MYO1D, and HDAC2, alongside significant negative associations with SOS2, PPP2R5E, and FZD1. In addition, LysoPS(18:2(9Z,12Z)/0:0) was positively correlated with MKNK1 and RAB9B, but negatively correlated with CAPN11, HDAC2, and PRKCD (P < 0.05; Fig. 6E). To enhance cross-layer interpretability beyond simple pathway overlap, we constructed metabolite-centered evidence matrices that integrate metabolite-level changes with their KEGG pathway context and representative DEG/DAP support. Discussion

In this study, integrated analyses of transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data indicate that enhanced lipid and nutrient supply, reprogrammed energy metabolism, and dynamic extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling may jointly sustain rapid follicular turnover and thereby promote the occurrence of long clutch length. Notably, egg weight did not differ between LC and SC despite the markedly higher laying output in LC geese, suggesting no apparent trade-off in egg mass under prolonged consecutive laying and potentially reflecting a greater capacity to sustain reproductive output.

In transcriptomic analysis of ovaries with differing clutch lengths, genes such as ALOX5AP, APOB, MMP2, PLA2G4C, MMP9, and NOBOX may represent key DEGs. ALOX5AP (arachidonate 5-lipoxygenase activating protein) is responsible for anchoring 5-lipoxygenase to the cell membrane, a process that is essential for leukotriene biosynthesis (Elnageeb et al., 2025). In hen ovarian follicles, arachidonic-acid–derived prostaglandins (PGs) function as pivotal local mediators that coordinate follicular growth and peri‑ovulatory processes. Consistent with this view, granulosa cells (GCs) from the largest preovulatory follicle are a major source of prostaglandins (Etches et al., 1990), and PGE2 signaling has been shown to facilitate somatic-cell proliferation and follicular growth during prehierarchical follicle development in laying hens (Lin et al., 2018). Moreover, pharmacological inhibition of cyclooxygenase-dependent prostaglandin synthesis, such as with indomethacin, can disrupt mid-sequence oviposition and ovulation, underscoring the essential role of prostaglandins in the hen ovulatory cycle (Shimada et al., 1986). ALOX5AP has been implicated in mammalian reproductive processes (Li et al., 2025); however, functional evidence in avian species remains scarce, highlighting the need for targeted validation in poultry. The increased expression of ALOX5AP in the LC group may therefore indicate enhanced leukotriene-mediated inflammatory signaling that facilitates follicular rupture and supports the establishment and maintenance of long clutch length. PLA2G4C (phospholipase A2 group IVC) mediates the release of arachidonic acid from membrane phospholipids, and this fatty acid is an important precursor for PG synthesis(Godkin et al., 2008). In laying hens, cytosolic phospholipase A2 in GCs is regulated by intraovarian cues and likely acts upstream to govern arachidonic acid release and, consequently, prostaglandin production during follicular development (Li et al., 1997). Therefore, the increased expression of PLA2G4C in the LC group may contribute to enhanced prostaglandin synthesis, thereby facilitating orderly and sustained ovulation that supports long clutch length. APOB (encodes apolipoprotein B) is a major structural protein required for hepatic assembly of very low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) (Chen et al., 2024a). In poultry, yolk formation depends on liver-derived VLDLy that targets the ovary and mediates the bulk transport of lipids to oocytes through the circulation (Walzem et al., 1999; Li et al., 2020). Recent studies have shown that the avian ovary itself can express ApoB and the lipid transfer protein MTTP, which allows in situ assembly and secretion of VLDL within ovarian tissue (Chen et al., 2025). The high expression of APOB in the LC group therefore suggests an adaptive upregulation of yolk precursors, whereby enhanced VLDL output may enable the ovary to deliver triacylglycerols and cholesterol more rapidly to multiple follicles and support the continuous establishment of the follicular hierarchy. These findings suggest that high-producing geese may not rely exclusively on hepatic yolk precursor supply; instead, they may augment local ovarian lipid processing to meet the demands imposed by multiple rapidly growing follicles. Such local upregulation could facilitate more rapid delivery of triacylglycerols and cholesterol to multiple follicles within the follicular hierarchy, thereby supporting sustained follicular recruitment and growth. However, because our evidence is derived from transcriptomic differences, additional studies are needed to validate this hypothesis at the protein and functional levels. MMP2 (matrix metallopeptidase 2) and MMP9 (matrix metallopeptidase 9) play critical roles in remodeling of the ovarian ECM (Hrabia, 2021). Normal ovarian function depends on cyclic remodeling of the ECM, a process that directly influences follicular development, atresia, and ovulation (Smith et al., 1999). A study in chickens showed that the expression and activity of MMP2 and MMP9 in preovulatory follicles increase sharply as ovulation approaches (Hrabia et al., 2019). Another report reached a similar conclusion and highlighted the importance of MMP9 for improving laying performance in hens (Zhu and Jiang, 2014). The upregulation of MMP2 and MMP9 in the LC group may therefore indicate intense remodeling of the ovarian ECM that sustains the rapid follicular turnover required for long clutch length. NOBOX (NOBOX oogenesis homeobox) is a homeobox transcription factor that is uniquely expressed in oocytes and is essential for early folliculogenesis as well as oocyte specific gene expression (Rajkovic et al., 2004). In geese, NOBOX expression is reduced during broodiness compared with the egg-laying stage, coinciding with marked shifts in the abundance of developing follicles, which supports a role for NOBOX-linked oocyte programs in regulating follicular development and reproductive state in birds (Yu et al., 2016). In light of the critical role of NOBOX in primordial and early-stage oocytes, its downregulation in the LC group is particularly intriguing. One possible explanation is that in the present study ovarian samples were collected near the end of the peak laying period, when individuals in the LC group would be expected to recruit and develop a larger number of follicles than those in the SC group. Consistent with this, sustained high output in LC geese may entail a higher follicle recruitment rate and potentially a faster depletion of the primordial follicle reserve; however, direct evaluation of follicle reserve will be required to confirm this possibility. This intensified recruitment may reduce or even exhaust the follicular reserve pool and consequently lower the overall level of NOBOX, which is predominantly expressed in primordial and early follicles.

In proteomic analysis of ovaries with differing clutch lengths, proteins such as CXCL12, RARB, TGFB3, and COL6A3, may represent key DAPs. CXCL12 (C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 12) plays a critical role in oocyte maturation. In sheep, supplementation with recombinant CXCL12 significantly increases the proportion of oocytes that reach metaphase I (Zhang et al., 2023). Moreover, CXCL12 exerts a pronounced anti-apoptotic effect on GCs, as indicated by a significant inverse association between its ovarian expression level and the granulosa-cell apoptosis rate. In goose follicles, granulosa-cell apoptosis is tightly linked to the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway, as reflected by shifts in the BCL-2/BAX balance. Notably, experimental perturbations can markedly alter the BCL-2/BAX ratio and apoptotic rate in gooseGCs (Jiang et al., 2023). Thus, upregulation of CXCL12 in the LC group may help maintain a more favorable ovarian microenvironment by promoting oocyte maturation and preventing granulosa-cell apoptosis, thereby supporting the growth and survival of multiple follicles. RARB (retinoic acid receptor beta) is a nuclear receptor for retinoic acid, the principal biologically active metabolite of retinol (vitamin A) in animals (Theodosiou and Laudet, 2010). Retinol and its derivatives, including retinyl esters, retinal, and retinoic acid, are collectively referred to as retinoids (Mukherjee et al., 2006). In the chicken ovary, retinoids can directly modulate follicular steroidogenesis. For instance, 9-cis retinoic acid alters LH-stimulated progesterone secretion in GCs, and transcripts encoding retinoid X receptor are detectable across follicles (Pawłowska et al., 2008). Supporting an important role for the vitamin A/retinoid axis in avian folliculogenesis, vitamin A deficiency in chickens increases follicular atresia and is associated with hemorrhagic lesions in follicles (Bermudez et al., 1993). Moreover, dietary vitamin A supplementation in broiler breeders has been reported to modulate ovarian expression of hormone receptors and apoptosis-related regulators (Chen et al., 2016). Thus, upregulation of RARB in the LC group may indicate enhanced activity of the retinoid pathway, thereby increasing ovarian sensitivity to follicle-stimulating hormone and promoting follicular growth. TGFB3 (transforming growth factor beta 3) is a member of the TGF-β family, which comprises three ligands that collectively modulate cellular proliferation and differentiation via the SMAD signaling pathway (Schmierer and Hill, 2007). In the hen ovary, multiple TGF-β isoforms are endogenously expressed. Notably, TGF-β1 mRNA is readily detected in GCs, whereas TGF-β2 and TGF-β3 are expressed at comparatively lower levels in GCs than in thecal tissue (Law et al., 1995). Functionally, studies in cultured hen GCs indicate that TGF-β signaling can modulate the steroidogenic program, including suppression of LH-induced StAR expression and progesterone production (Fang et al., 2014). In addition, TGF-β1 upregulates connective tissue growth factor, CTGF/CCN2, in hen GCs, further linking TGF-β signaling to extracellular matrix–associated regulatory programs (Cheng et al., 2015). Thus, upregulation of TGFB3 in the LC group may promote granulosa-cell proliferation and survival, thereby providing enhanced support for oocyte development. COL6A3 (collagen type VI alpha 3 chain) encodes a major component of type VI collagen, an important ECM element that forms microfibrillar networks in multiple tissues, including the ovarian stroma and follicular wall, thereby linking cells to the interstitial collagen matrix and modulating tissue rigidity and cell behavior (Williams et al., 2022; Gregory et al., 2024). In avian ovarian follicles, the follicular wall and basal lamina are enriched in extracellular-matrix components, including collagens and proteoglycans. These matrices undergo dynamic remodeling during follicle growth and peri‑ovulatory processes (Hummel et al., 2004; Asem et al., 2002). Thus, COL6A3 downregulation in the LC group may reflect a concomitant reduction in type VI collagen, thereby decreasing the biomechanical resistance surrounding developing follicles and facilitating more rapid follicular expansion to sustain prolonged clutching.

In metabolomic analysis of ovaries with differing clutch lengths, metabolites such as lactic acid, guanidinoacetic acid, and 3-hydroxybutyrylcarnitine may represent key DAMs. Lactic acid is the terminal product of glycolysis and, within ovarian follicles, is predominantly generated by cumulus cells (Bao et al., 2024). These cells take up glucose from the follicular fluid, convert it via glycolysis to pyruvate and lactic acid, and subsequently transfer these metabolites through gap junctions to the maturing oocyte (Zhang et al., 2025). Notably, in chickens, glucose uptake increases with follicle development. Concordantly, transcripts involved in glucose transport and downstream carbohydrate utilization are robustly expressed during follicular growth, supporting pronounced engagement of glycolysis-linked energy metabolism in rapidly developing follicles and within the oocyte compartment (Seol et al., 2006). Thus, the upregulation of lactic acid observed in the LC group may indicate an increase in ovarian glycolytic capacity, which could help satisfy the sustained energy demand required for the concurrent development and ovulation of multiple follicles during continuous clutch production. Guanidinoacetic acid is an amino-acid–derived intermediate and the direct precursor of creatine synthesis, and exogenous guanidinoacetic acid supplementation can substantially increase creatine stores in animal tissues and enhance cellular energy metabolism (Asiriwardhana and Bertolo, 2022). In poultry, the creatine axis appears to be nutritionally modifiable. Dietary guanidinoacetic acid increases creatine availability, as evidenced by elevated serum creatine in laying hens and enhanced deposition of guanidinoacetic acid and creatine into hatching eggs in broiler breeders, supporting a role for the creatine–phosphocreatine system in meeting the high ATP demand of the developing egg (Azizollahi et al., 2024). Dietary supplementation with guanidinoacetic acid has been reported to improve broiler breeder hens fertility-related outcomes at later production stages (Sharideh et al., 2016). Similarly, inclusion of guanidinoacetic acid in diets with low metabolizable energy improves egg production and egg quality in laying hens, an effect attributed to efficient creatine synthesis that optimizes energy utilization (García-Gómora et al., 2024). Therefore, the upregulation of guanidinoacetic acid in the LC group may reflect enhanced creatine biosynthesis in ovarian tissues, which could facilitate rapid ATP turnover in oocytes and thereby support the continuous recruitment of follicles necessary for sustained clutch production. 3-Hydroxybutyrylcarnitine is a short-chain acylcarnitine, acylcarnitines are transport carriers formed when the acyl group of acyl-CoA is conjugated to carnitine, enabling shuttling of the acyl group into the mitochondrial matrix, where it is reconverted to acyl-CoA (Houten and Wanders, 2010). Acylcarnitines constitute the sole carrier system for mitochondrial β-oxidation and represent a key rate-limiting step in this process, and their accumulation is often regarded as a biomarker of disturbances in mitochondrial energy metabolism (McCann et al., 2021). Both carbohydrate and lipid catabolic programs are engaged during chicken follicle development. Expression profiling across follicular stages indicates coordinated regulation of genes involved in glucose transport and glycolysis as well as fatty-acid β-oxidation. Moreover, AMPK activation in hen GCs enhances fatty-acid oxidation, underscoring the importance of metabolic flexibility for sustaining follicular growth (Seol et al., 2006; Tosca). l-carnitine is widely recognized as a key facilitator of mitochondrial β-oxidation and energy utilization. Dietary supplementation in breeder layers has been reported to improve hatchability and reduce the proportion of unfertilized eggs, consistent with the notion that carnitine-linked fatty-acid oxidation contributes to sustaining reproductive output (Corduk and Sarica, 2008). Accordingly, the lower level of 3-hydroxybutyrylcarnitine in the LC group may indicate reduced accumulation of short-chain acylcarnitine intermediates in ovarian tissues, which is consistent with elevated rates of fatty acid oxidation coupled to ATP production, as required for sustained follicular development.

In integrated three-omics analysis of ovaries with differing clutch lengths, YWHAZ, SLC16A3, and lactate emerged as a candidate cross-omics signature. Lactate was significantly increased in LC ovaries and was positively associated with the integrated gene score of YWHAZ. Notably, the metabolite-centered evidence matrix further connected lactate to the upstream transporter SLC16A3, annotated as monocarboxylate transporter 4, which showed increased protein abundance accompanied by a concordant upward trend at the mRNA level. Importantly, avian studies support a role for MCT4 in lactate handling during hypoxia-associated metabolic remodeling. In broiler chickens with wooden breast myopathy, both MCT4 (SLC16A3) mRNA and protein are increased in parallel with altered lactate metabolism. Similarly, in sudden death syndrome, elevated cardiac lactate is accompanied by increased MCT4 transcript levels in hypoxic myocardium (Zhao et al., 2020; Safaei et al., 2021). Moreover, 14-3-3 proteins participate in gonadotropin-responsive signaling in hen GCs. For example, FSH induces PI3K/Akt-dependent phosphorylation and nuclear exclusion of FOXO1, in which 14-3-3 proteins act as nucleocytoplasmic transport factors, thereby promoting granulosa-cell proliferation and suppressing apoptosis during follicle selection and growth (Yan et al., 2025). Consistently, in chicken GCs, repression of YWHAB perturbs the 14-3-3/FoxO axis, thereby promoting apoptosis and reducing progesterone and estrogen secretion. Together, these findings support an important role for 14-3-3 family members in maintaining follicular cell survival and steroidogenic function (Ning et al., 2023). Moreover, lactate-linked glycolysis appears to be tightly coupled to follicular cell function and ovulation in the domestic chicken. Integrated proteo-transcriptomic profiling of preovulatory follicles highlights glycolysis and pyruvate metabolism, with LDHA emerging as a key module associated with ovulatory dynamics. Consistently, pharmacological inhibition of LDHA in primary GCs decreases lactate dehydrogenase activity, reduces cell viability, and increases apoptosis in vitro (Nie et al., 2024). Taken together, we propose that lactate-associated metabolic remodeling, together with elevated 14-3-3ζ–related signaling at the protein level, may contribute to sustained clutch production by promoting granulosa-cell survival and differentiation. Whether this combined lactate/14-3-3ζ signature can serve as an early biomarker of high-producing geese remains to be determined and will require longitudinal validation across the laying cycle.

Overall, relative to our previous report (Liu et al., 2025), the present study increased the sample size while keeping the species and husbandry conditions constant, and implemented more stringent phenotypic selection criteria and group stratification across clutch-length categories. These refinements increased statistical power and enabled the detection of factors that were not previously identified but appear to have more direct biological relevance. At the transcriptomic level, we again confirmed that FGF14 is significantly differentially expressed across clutch-length groups and additionally identified new key genes that are indispensable for sustained ovulation and oocyte formation, including those mediating yolk lipid deposition, ovulatory inflammatory signaling, ovarian ECM remodeling during follicular rupture, and maintenance of the ovarian reserve. At the metabolomic level, we further validated molecular markers directly associated with ovarian capacity for continuous ovulation, including metabolites related to oocyte energy substrates and intracellular energy-buffering systems. Given that ovarian ECM dynamics, lipid transport, and the follicular microenvironment are now recognized as central determinants of ovarian reserve, oocyte quality, and ovulatory competence (Wei et al., 2024), our findings further suggest that clutch length, an important and complex reproductive trait in geese, is governed by at least two partially distinct regulatory pathways. Nevertheless, this study has limitations. Because laying was recorded only twice daily, the inter-egg interval for each goose could not be quantified at an hourly resolution; therefore, future studies using fully automated egg-laying recording systems will be necessary.

Conclusion

This study preliminarily identified APOB, PLA2G4C, MMP2, MMP9, and NOBOX as key genes associated with clutch length in geese; CXCL12, RARB, and MAD2L1 as key proteins; lactic acid, guanidinoacetic acid, and 3-Hydroxybutyrylcarnitine as key metabolites; and a lactate-centered cross-omics chain supported by SLC16A3 and YWHAZ. Overall, these findings provide new insights into the regulation of clutch length in geese and offer a scientific basis for improving reproductive efficiency and reducing reproductive disorders.
