Late diagnosis of RAPSN mutation–associated congenital myasthenic syndrome with obstructive sleep apnea in a 5-year-old girl
Authors: Grace S. Kurian, Thomas Rio Frio, Isabelle Ruchonnet-Métrailler, Joel Fluss
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCongenital myasthenic syndromes represent a heterogenous group of rare genetic disorders that affect the neuromuscular junction. They present with variable combinations of extraocular, facial, bulbar, and limb muscle weakness with typical diurnal fluctuations of symptoms, with increased risk of sleep-related disorders. Due to the variable course, clinical diagnosis can be challenging and delayed, sometimes for decades.Case presentationWe present the case of a 5-year-old girl presumed to have benign infantile hypotonia, who exhibited head drop during intercurrent illnesses and increased evening fatigue. Intermittent bilateral ptosis, initially unreported by the family and not observed during early morning examinations, became evident during a late afternoon visit. Given the clinical suspicion of a congenital myasthenic syndrome, genetic testing was performed and identified a homozygous pathogenic variant in the RAPSN gene responsible for a post-synaptic form of congenital myasthenia. Due to the complaints of excessive night-time snoring, overnight polygraphies (PG) were performed, revealing obstructive sleep-apnoea secondary to oropharyngeal muscle weakness. Symptomatic treatment with pyridostigmine, including a night-time dose, was followed by drastic improvement and near-complete resolution of nocturnal symptoms.ConclusionCongenital myasthenia may be more prevalent than recognised, with milder phenotypes still frequently overlooked or misdiagnosed. Sleep apnea is an important comorbidity that should be actively investigated. Optimised pyridostigmine therapy, including nocturnal dosing, can significantly alleviate symptoms and improve quality of life.

Background

Congenital myasthenic syndromes (CMS) are a heterogenous group of rare genetic disorders that impair the neuromuscular junction. They are responsible for various presentations of extraocular, facial, bulbar, as well as limb muscle weakness with typical diurnal fluctuations of symptoms. Patients are also at increased risk of sleep-related breathing disorders. Due to the variable phenotypic presentation, clinical diagnosis can be challenging and is often delayed up to several decades. The persistence of such diagnostic delays highlights the need for greater clinical awareness and consideration of CMS in patients with unexplained muscle weakness.

Case description

This 5-year-old girl is the first child of a non-consanguineous couple of European origin, with no remarkable family history. Pregnancy was unremarkable. The infant was born at 41 weeks’ gestation via spontaneous delivery, with a birth weight of 2740 g, a length of 50 cm, and a head circumference of 35.5 cm. Apgar scores were 9, 10, and 10. Following birth, generalized hypotonia and head-lag were noted. Muscle strength appeared normal, with spontaneous antigravity movements. There were no respiratory distress and no feeding difficulty. There were no signs of arthrogryposis. She was admitted in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) for 8 days, where initial metabolic and genetic workup—including testing for Prader-Willi syndrome by MS-MLPA (Salsa kit ME028-B2), was unremarkable. Following spontaneous and rapid improvement of the hypotonia, the baby was discharged without further investigations. At a neurology follow-up visit at the age of 12 month, scheduled in the morning, persisting low tone in the extremities with normal muscle strength and reflexes was observed. Gross motor milestone acquisition occurred toward the later end of the accepted physiologic range. No ptosis or head-drop were reported. Independent walking was achieved at 17 months. At the subsequent neurology consultation at the age of 24 months, again early morning, her examination apart from mild hypotonia was normal, and a presumptive diagnosis of benign infantile hypotonia was made. There were no parental concerns of daytime fatigue or snoring, and regular general paediatric consultations did not prompt further referral. Early cognitive, fine motor and language skills were fully normal. In this context, no further genetic workup was carried out at this stage.

At the age of 5 years, the child was seen by a paediatric ophthalmologist, for adjustment of her spectacles prescribed for refractive error. As this consultation took place in the afternoon, a fluctuating bilateral ocular ptosis was recognised for the first time, prompting referral to the paediatric neurology clinic. This time, the medical history was remarkable for evening fatigue, intermittent eyelid ptosis and head drop during intercurrent illnesses. Neurological examination conducted in the late afternoon confirmed bilateral ptosis with a positive Simpson test. Auto-immune forms of myasthenia were first excluded with negative anti-acetylcholine receptor (AchR) and anti-MuSK antibodies. Suspecting a congenital myasthenic syndrome, further genetic testing was pursued. Whole-exome sequencing was performed on patient blood DNA, with bioinformatic analysis restricted to a 27-gene congenital myasthenic syndrome (CMS) panel. A homozygous missense variant, c.264C > A p.(Asn88Lys), was identified in the RAPSN gene (NM_005055.5). Targeted Sanger sequencing of both parents confirmed their heterozygous carrier status. According to ACMG guidelines for variant classification [1], the variant was classified as pathogenic (criteria applied: PS1, PS3, PM1, PM2_supporting, PM3, PP1). Thus, a homozygous pathogenic variant in the RAPSN gene was identified, confirming a postsynaptic congenital myasthenic syndrome. Both parents were found to be healthy heterozygous carriers. The genetic results were communicated to the parents with the risk of recurrence of 25% in future siblings, with the possibility of specific genetic counselling in case of family planning. While electromyographic examination with repeated nerve stimulation is a diagnostic standard, the test was not carried out as the diagnosis was well-established through genetic testing. Symptomatic treatment with pyridostigmine (Mestinon®), a reversible anticholinesterase inhibitor, starting at 1.0 mg/kg/dose (20 mg) three times daily was initiated, yielding significant clinical improvement. The medication was well tolerated. Due to the additional complaint of excessive night-time snoring in the absence of daytime somnolence, overnight home polygraphy (PG) was carried out to assess obstructive sleep-apnoea enhanced by oropharyngeal muscle weakness. This test demonstrated a pathological apnoea/hypopnoea index (AHI 7.5/h), corresponding to a mild degree. Discussion and conclusion

Congenital myasthenic syndromes (CMS) are rare disorders encompassing a spectrum of diseases arising from a variety of mutations affecting the neuromuscular junction. These mutations are subclassified as presynaptic defects, synaptic defects associated with the basal lamina, acetylcholine receptor defects, endplate development and maintenance defects, as well as congenital defects of glycosylation or associated to other syndromes [2].

The endplate development and maintenance mutations account for 25% of cases, of which mutations in the protein Rapsyn accounting for approximatively 15% [3]. Mutations on the RAPSN gene, located on chromosome 11 are prevalent in the European population possibly related to a possible founder effect [4, 5]. RAPSN gene encodes the rapsyn protein that enables the anchoring of the acetylcholine receptor in the post-synaptic membrane [6]. The resulting phenotypic changes caused by the RAPSN mutation vary widely, from severe neonatal presentations with pronounced hypotonia and feeding difficulty at birth, infants with respiratory distress or even arthrogryposes in a third of cases, to milder forms described in adults with mild bilateral ptosis [7]. As with other CMS types, respiratory control may be impaired due to reduced input to the respiratory centers in the medulla and pons, resulting in less effective signaling to the respiratory muscles responsible for maintaining upper airway patency and regulating ventilation [8]. Patients with RAPSN mutations have been described as having an early (before age 5) or a late- onset (13 to 48 years of age) phenotype, and symptoms can be confused with auto-immune myasthenia gravis [9]. In our patient, bilateral ptosis became evident only at the age of 5 years, without diplopia.

Diagnosing congenital myasthenic syndromes remains challenging. A study published in 2008 involving a cohort of 47 children found that 80% of CMS cases had been misdiagnosed [10]. Another study involving 34 adult patients reported that 94% of these patients had also been misdiagnosed [3, 11]. This diagnostic delay, sometimes lasting up to three decades, highlights the importance of considering CMS as an essential differential diagnosis for symptoms of muscle weakness, hypotonia, or fatigue resembling muscular dystrophies or autoimmune myasthenia gravis [11]. Moreover, benign congenital hypotonia should be questioned and viewed as a diagnosis of exclusion. Studies suggest this term is valid only in case of complete symptoms resolution, warranting further investigation conversely [12].

The diagnosis of CMS typically relies on suggestive clinical features supported by electrophysiological studies. It is increasingly confirmed by molecular genetic testing, with specific CMS gene panels now widely available. Given the strong suspicion for congenital myasthenia in our patient, and the genetic confirmation of homozygous RAPSN, electromyography examination that typically demonstrate a decrement of the compound muscle action potential (CMAP) was not performed [13, 14].

Respiratory complications represent a major cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with CMS; however, data on their long-term natural progression remain limited. A descriptive study reported that one-third of patients presented with respiratory difficulties and stridor at birth. Furthermore, approximately 40% required admission to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) before the age of two [15]. Although the overall need for surgical interventions such as tracheotomy or adenotonsillectomy is low; 35% of patients required long-term ventilatory support due to abnormal cardiorespiratory findings during sleep. Importantly, mutation subtype appears to strongly influence clinical presentations and outcomes. Neonatal stridor and respiratory distress at birth are typically seen in patients harboring DOK7 mutations, while RASPN mutations are often associated with episodic apnea and respiratory decompensation in early childhood, frequently necessitating Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) admission [15]. Independently of the genotype, many CMS patients showed a possible decline in lung function during adolescence, with reduced Forced Vital Capacity and Peak Expiratory Flow. These findings highlight the importance of performing pulmonary function tests during adolescence to monitor disease progression. In this case, lung function testing was not performed due to the child’s young age and inability to produce reproducible results [15].

Sleep-disordered breathing is also increasingly recognized in various congenital myasthenic syndromes. In a small cohort of patients of 11 children with CMS, 18% presented with documented sleep disorders with obstructive sleep apnea [16]. These results were confirmed by another study showing abnormal apnea/hypopnea index in 5 children with CMS [17]. Altogether, this suggests that overnight polygraphy should be integrated in the routine diagnostic work-up of CMS as oropharyngeal muscle weakness may be present in affected patients. This was confirmed in our patient who has nighttime snoring, with a pathological polygraphic recording due to oropharyngeal weakness. The addition of tailored night-time pyridostigmine dose was able to normalise the polygraphic findings, emphasizing the importance of screening for sleep-related symptoms and adjusting the therapy accordingly.

Pharmacological treatment depends on the CMS subtypes. Treatment with pyridostigmine, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, generally provide a favourable outcome in patients with RAPSN mutations, although half the patients experience exacerbations during infections or fever, when typical head-drop can be noticed [18, 19]. Some patients may also benefit from the use of 3–4 diaminopyridine (3–4 DAP or amifampridine), a presynaptic potassium channel blocker that subsequently prolongs the action potential, or beta-agonists such as salbutamol [4].

While the cardinal features of most congenital myasthenic syndromes (CMS) are generally stable over time, long-term prognosis is strongly influenced by the underlying genotype. Most patients remain ambulant and do not require ventilatory support, despite intermittent clinical worsening. Acknowledging the variable disease course across the lifespan, as well as mutation-specific trajectory patterns, is crucial for appropriate follow-up and monitoring [20].

In conclusion, congenital myasthenia may be more prevalent than currently recognised, with mild forms often misdiagnosed. It should be considered as a differential diagnosis, when symptoms are partially improving like self-resolving neonatal hypotonia but with persisting motor delay and signs of fatigability. Paediatricians should maintain a high index of suspicion, in particular the need for clinical monitoring to detect certain subtle symptoms (delayed motor milestones, fatigability, varying degrees of palpebral ptosis, facial and bulbar weakness). The timing of the neurological assessment may be crucial, as symptoms can be missed in the mornings, contributing to delayed diagnosis. Stressing a detailed history and video documenting of the child in the evening time could be an option to mitigate such bias. The genetic evaluation plays an essential role in accurately identifying the subtype, ultimately enabling targeted pharmacological treatment with generally favourable results. Because pyridostigmine may exacerbate specific CMS subtypes, such as slow-channel syndrome and DOK7-related CMS, genetic characterization is highly recommended before treatment. Respiratory comorbidities should be routinely screened for and appropriately managed.
