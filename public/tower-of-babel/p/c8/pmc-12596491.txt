Theoretical perspectives on submandibular gland preservation in head and neck cancer surgery: a pre-research review
Authors: Jing-Yi Liu, Yuan-Hao Wen, Zi-Zhan Li, Xuan-Hao Liu, Lei-Ming Cao, Guang-Rui Wang, Bing Liu, Xiao-Long Wang, Jun Jia, Lin-Lin Bu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the neck dissection procedures for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), the submandibular gland (SMG) is frequently and routinely removed. However, is the sacrifice of the SMG truly indispensable? We have analyzed this issue from multiple viewpoints. Anatomically, there are no lymph nodes (LNs) within the gland, thus eliminating the risk of cancer metastasizing to the SMG via LNs. From a pathological perspective, apart from cases in which the primary tumor tissue directly invades or adjacent LNs have extracapsular invasion, the probability of the SMG being affected by cancer is extremely low. In terms of patient survival rate, preserving the SMG during surgery does not exert a substantial impact on this critical metric. Preserving the SMG can assist in maintaining its secretory function. Nevertheless, the precise preservation of the SMG presents challenges. In addition to requiring greater data assistance, this idea may lengthen the procedure and raise the bar for surgeon skill. Although difficulties persisting, the significance of this perspective is far-reaching. The preservation of the SMG represents further exploration by clinicians in the realm of functional neck dissection. On the premise of ensuring safety, we endeavor to preserve more functional structures and reduce the resection scope, focusing not only on enabling patients with HNSCC to survive but also on guaranteeing their quality of life in the long term after treatment. Finally, we have proposed development directions and future prospects for this field, hoping to inspire fellow scholars. This study aims to analyze the necessity of performing SMG resection in head and neck cancer surgery, evaluate the evidence for SMG preservation (encompassing anatomical, pathological, survival, and functional aspects), discuss relevant controversies, and propose future research directions to provide guidance for clinical practice.Keywords: Submandibular gland preservation (SMG preservation), neck dissection, head and neck cancer, oral cancer, preservative functional surgery

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), with an estimated 930,000 new cases and 460,000 cancer-related deaths occurring in 2020, is the seventh leading cancer worldwide (1-4). HNSCC first infiltrates the primary site locally and then metastasizes to regional cervical lymph nodes (LNs) (5). The spread to regional lymphatics significantly reduces overall survival, for example, the rate of 5-year survival for oral cancer patients decreases from 71.4% (localized) to 45.8% (regional) (3). Therefore, appropriate management of the regional lymphatics through neck dissection is critical to the treatment and prognosis of HNSCC patients.

The philosophy hidden in the development of neck dissection can be generalized as “less is more”, i.e., less surgical excision to achieve more functional preservation and better post-operative quality of life. The submandibular gland (SMG), however, is routinely excised in the level IB neck dissection in spite of rare involvement (6). The American Head and Neck Society surveyed its surgeons and statistically found that 43.1% of surgeons preserved the SMG during the level 1b portion of a neck dissection (7). The biggest impact of removing the SMG on patients is the occurrence of xerostomia after surgery, which severely reduces their quality of life. Some scholars also emphasized that for patients receiving postoperative radiotherapy, preserving the SMG is expected to reduce xerostomia (dry mouth) after head and neck irradiation (8). In recent studies, there was also evidence of the feasibility of preserving the SMG (9). The potential benefits of preservation of SMG (Figure 1) in neck dissection include lower incidence of xerostomia and dental caries (10), avoiding injury to the lingual nerve and the marginal mandibular nerve and fewer demand for donor site tissues. Therefore, from the perspective of “less is more”, it is necessary to summarize and evaluate the available evidence to determine whether SMG can be preserved in neck dissection without compromising oncological safety. Besides, the indications for SMG-preserved neck dissection need to be assessed in order to help surgeons make the best clinical decisions.

History and development of neck dissection

Neck dissection, developed in the past century, is an essential tool in the treatment of patients with HNSCC, but remains a work in progress. From radical to selective, the scope of neck dissection has become more limited, and the indications have been defined more precisely (7). This principle, conservative functional surgery, can be summarized as follows: to conserve the functional organs or tissues as much as possible while ensuring the eradication of the tumor (11).

Regarding neck dissection for HNSCC, several significant transformations have taken place with the accumulation of clinical experience and the changes in people’s concepts of surgical operations. In the 18th century, people began some early attempts at neck dissection (12-16). Later, in 1905, the concept of radical neck dissection was first formulated by Crile (17). In 1951, Martin summarized the experience of 559 cases of neck dissection, further emphasizing the en bloc resection of the cervical LNs and soft tissues, which led to the further popularization and standardization of neck dissection (18).

In the 1960s, Suarez, Bocca, and others put forward the concept of functional neck dissection, that is, while removing the lymphatic tissues, preserving the internal jugular vein, sternocleidomastoid muscle, accessory nerve, etc., to reduce surgical trauma (19-21). Subsequently, selective neck dissection emerged. Finally, in 1991, the American Academy of Otolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery revised the naming rules for the regions of the cervical LNs, classifying neck dissection into four types: radical neck dissection, modified radical neck dissection, selective neck dissection, and extended radical neck dissection, making the classification of neck dissection more scientific and standardized (22). In 1993, Alex and Krag first described the application of sentinel LN biopsy for staging of HNSCC (23).

The critical role of LNs in HNSCC has now been further developed. Huang et al. discovered the presence of specific memory T cells in tumor-draining LNs, which exhibit excellent potential anti-tumor therapeutic effects (24). This implies that when the risk of cancer metastasis in LNs is low, preserving the LNs can play a promoting role in enhancing the efficacy of immunotherapy. However, there is evidence indicating that LN colonization can induce tumor immune tolerance, thereby promoting distant tumor metastasis (25). Therefore, the preservation of LNs still requires a further exploration of the mechanisms and individualized indications.

Nowadays, neck dissection has become an indispensable surgical method in the treatment of HNSCC, and it is still continuously developing and improving. Preservation of SMG: current evidence

Assessment of the feasibility of preserving the SMG involves four aspects: anatomical basis, tumor pathology, patient survival, and functional outcome. In this section, the available research evidence supporting the preservation of the SMG is summarized and discussed.

Anatomical evidence

The SMG along with its rich lymphatic system resides within the submandibular triangle (26). As the second largest salivary gland, it is a major contributor to mucinous saliva, secreting 60% to 65% of unstimulated saliva.

In terms of anatomical location, the SMG is located in the submandibular triangle and has a close relationship with the surrounding blood vessels and nerves. They receive blood from the submental, sublingual (facial artery branches) and lingual (external carotid artery branch) arteries, with the facial artery winding through the gland capsule; venous drainage relies on the sublingual and common facial veins (emptying into the internal jugular vein). These adjacent vessels, along with nearby nerves, are at high risk of injury during SMG excision (27).

Considering oncology, the LN distribution of SMG is quite distinctive. The LNs associated with the SMG are located adjacent to the submandibular triangle. The periphery of the SMG and the facial space between the SMG and the mandible are the common distribution sites. These LNs are usually distributed along the surface of the SMG and the surrounding blood vessels. Specifically, they are mostly distributed around the facial artery and facial vein. The facial artery is often surrounded by multiple LNs attached to it as it travels through the deep surface of the SMG, and the facial vein is accompanied by some of these LNs as it travels through the region. The number of submandibular LNs is usually 3–6, but there is individual variation in the exact number. The submandibular LN mainly collects lymphatic fluid from the face, oral cavity and SMG and other areas, which is important in immune defense and disease diagnosis, and is one of the important LN groups in the head and neck region (28).

It is worth emphasizing that the LNs associated with the SMG do not occur within the capsule of the gland. Although there is no clear conclusion, developmental biology also tends to suggest that SMG does not contain LNs (29,30). Yang et al. collected 116 samples of the SMG removed from 95 patients with HNSCC, including a variety of conditions such as involvement of the floor of the mouth, negative LNs, intranodal LN metastasis without peritumoral invasion, and extranodal spread of LNs. About 5,000 H&E-stained sections were produced and evaluated, none of which detected the presence of LNs in the SMG (31). This study provides evidence supporting the hypothesis that SMG may not contain LNs.

Pathological evidence

The possibility of preserving the SMG was proposed as early as 1977 (32). Considering that only 3 out of the 51 neck-dissection specimens presented metastasis to LNs in the submandibular triangle, the author suggested the preservation of the contents of the submandibular triangle region in radical neck dissections for patients with laryngeal cancer.

A systematic review of 17 articles has drawn a conclusion that the rate of SMG involvement in OSCC is significantly low (33). Among the 2,306 patients with 2,792 SMG resections, only 58 resections (2%) had tumor involvement. Within the tumor-involved SMG, the most and second most common invasion pathways were direct SMG invasion by primary tumor (43 of 58, 74.1%) and from adjacent LNs (10 of 58, 17.2%). Isolated metastatic invasion mode was rare (3 of 2,792, 0.1%). Subsequent studies have reported similar findings.

A multicenter retrospective study conducted across five European academic centers confirmed the rarity of SMG LN involvement: only 1.8% of the 4,458 patients analyzed exhibited such involvement (34). Similarly, a prospective study of 379 neck dissections for OSSC over a course of 7 years showed only 3 (0.79%) specimens had SMG involvement (6). A total of 131 patients had positive LNs, and only 3 patients had SMG involvement. A retrospective study of 149 patients of oral carcinoma with level I nodal metastasis over a period of 1 year showed SMG was involved in 7 (4.7%) cases (35). Another retrospective study of Zhou et al. of 145 patients receiving neck dissection for OSCC between January 2018 and October 2020 showed only 1 (0.7%) patient was involved in SMG by direct invasion from primary tumor (36). In 2020, Yang et al. analyzed the data of 302 patients with positive LNs in OSCC between January 2002 and December 2012. Results showed only 1 of 302 (0.3%) patients had SMG involvement (37). In 2021, Javadi et al. analyzed 131 patients with oral tongue SCC from 2001 to 2017. Results showed that there was only 1 (0.76%) patient with SMG involvement (38). In 2021, Jakhetiya et al. reported that among 303 patients with OSCC, only 4 (1.3%) patients had SMG involvement (39). Another retrospective study of Hamdy et al. of 105 patients with tongue cancer who received neck dissection showed only 1 (0.95%) patient had direct infiltration of SMG by primary tumor (40). In 2022, Joshi et al. reported that among 555 patients with OSCC, only 2 (0.36%) cases had SMG involvement (41).

The studies with results deviating largely from the mean are as follows. A retrospective study in 2018 analyzed 586 neck dissection specimens from 2005 to 2014. Among the 226 nodal positive patients, 21 (9.3%) had direct gland invasion (42). Another retrospective study in 2022 included 60 patients from 2015 to 2020 and reported that none of the patients had SMG involvement (43). Histopathological slides were retrospectively reviewed in 2024, 5 patients had SMG involvement in 171 (2.92%), and they came to the conclusion that in low-risk patients, SMG may be spared and the level IB LNs are dissected. High-risk patients may be chosen as candidates for SMG transfer or excision based on the extent of LN involvement (44).

Survival evidence

We cited some relevant clinical trials and clinical retrospective analyses, and conducted an analysis based on Table 1. According to the research results, most studies showed that there was no significant difference in 5-year local regional control (LRC) and disease-specific survival (DSS) between the group with SMG preservation and the group with SMG resection. For example, in the study by Gu et al., the 5-year LRC rates were 74% and 75% respectively (P=0.970), and the 5-year DSS rates were 74% and 69% respectively (P=0.709). The study by Vetrivel et al. (45) indicated that the technique of SMG preservation had no significant difference in the number of LNs harvested [LN yield (LNY)] and LN density compared with the traditional en bloc resection technique (P=0.543 and P=1.000), but operation time was significantly longer in the group with gland preservation (P<0.001). The recurrence situations varied in different studies. For instance, the study by Lanzer et al. showed that SMG resection had a significant impact on the disease-free survival of LN recurrence (P<0.001).

Overall, for specific types of HNSCCs such as early-stage buccal cancer and oral cavity floor cancer, under appropriate T staging (such as cT1–2N0), neck dissection with SMG preservation may be feasible. It can avoid problems such as postoperative appearance asymmetry and xerostomia, and may be oncologically safe. However, for cancers in tissues close to the SMG (such as the oral cavity floor or the tongue), it is not recommended to preserve the ipsilateral SMG.

When analyzing relevant studies, we found that there are problems in aspects such as sample size, staging and grouping, and follow-up in the clinical trials in this field. In the future, it can be developed in the directions of expanding the sample size, refining the staging and grouping, and extending the follow-up, so as to improve the clinical application value of this surgical procedure.

The sample size of some studies is small. For example, in the study by Gu et al. [2020], there were only 31 cases in the SMG preservation group, 29 cases in the same group in the study by Du et al. [2020], and 33 cases in the SMG preservation group in the study by Chen et al. [2011] (46-48). A small sample size may not be able to accurately reflect the overall situation, resulting in limited reliability of the research results, increasing the risk of bias in the conclusions, and making it difficult to comprehensively and accurately evaluate the efficacy and safety of this surgical procedure.

In relevant clinical trials, there are differences in the methods of staging and grouping patients among researchers: there are differences in the tumor staging and grouping methods in different studies. For example, in the study by Lanzer et al. [2014], it was classified according to oropharyngeal cancer and OSCC, and the specific staging of the preservation and resection groups was not clearly defined, while most other studies were staged according to tumor (T), node (N), and metastasis (M) (49). The non-uniform standards for staging and grouping make it difficult to directly compare different studies, and it is impossible to effectively integrate the research results, which hinders the comprehensive evaluation of the surgical procedure of SMG preservation under different tumor characteristics. There are also significant differences in the follow-up time and content for patients after surgery: the follow-up time varies, ranging from 1 to 115 months. For example, the follow-up period in the study by Chen et al. [2011] has a large span, and different studies have different focuses in the follow-up content (48). The differences in follow-up time and content affect the accurate judgment of key indicators such as the long-term curative effect, recurrence rate, and survival rate after surgery, and it is difficult to comprehensively understand the long-term impact of this surgical procedure.

In the future, we should also conduct large-scale, multi-center clinical studies, increasing the number of samples and covering a more extensive patient population, including patients from different regions, ethnic groups, and with diverse tumor characteristics. It is also of vital importance to standardize the staging and grouping criteria. We should attempt to clarify the applicable scope and efficacy evaluation indicators of the surgical procedure for SMG preservation under different staging conditions. We should also extend the follow-up period, continuously tracking the patients’ conditions for 5 years or even longer after the operation, and paying attention to long-term complications, recurrence and metastasis situations, as well as the long-term impact on their quality of life. At the same time, unify the follow-up content, including regular imaging examinations, functional evaluations, and patients’ subjective feelings, etc., so as to conduct a more comprehensive assessment of the long-term effects of the surgical procedure for SMG preservation.

Functional evidence

Patients who undergo SMG resections are likely to suffer from reduced salivary output and even xerostomia. Jacob et al. enrolled 29 patients with unilateral SMG removed and 8 patients with bilateral SMG removed, compared with 29 noncancer control subjects (50). Results showed that both unstimulated and stimulated salivary flow rates were lower in the experimental group than in the control group. One-third of the resection subjects suffered from xerostomia.

A 1-year-follow-up prospective study by Gu et al. assessed 36 SMG-preserved patients and 131 SMG-dissected patients (51). The salivary flow rates in both groups were significantly lower at 7 days after surgery than those at the preoperative stage. However, in the SMG-preserved group, the salivary flow rate gradually returned to baseline at 9 months post-operation and remained a steady level at 12 months after surgery. In contrast to the SMG-preserved group, the salivary flow rate in the SMG-dissected group had not returned to baseline after 12 months. In addition, the study compared the University of Washington Quality of Life (UWQOL) index between the two groups. Results showed that patients who had undergone SMG-preserved neck dissection had better swallowing and chewing function.

Some studies reported that SMG-excision only had an impact on unstimulated but not stimulated salivary flow rate. A prospective study performed by Jaguar et al. included 80 patients with head and neck tumors to assess whether neck dissection (with SMG excision) would influence salivary gland function. Salivary gland function was assessed via salivary flow rate and scintigraphy. The surgical group (37 patients) showed a lower mean unstimulated salivary flow rate (0.60 vs. 0.94 mL/min, P=0.008) than the non-surgical control group (43 patients), though stimulated flow rates did not differ significantly (P=0.26). Notably, xerostomia was reported in 21% of surgical patients versus 7% of controls (52). Another retrospective study of Altuntaş et al. supported the conclusion about the stimulated salivary flow rate (53). The study included 32 head and neck cancer patients (stage I) receiving unilateral or bilateral neck dissection (with SMG excision) and 23 patients (stage II–IV) as control group. The mean stimulated salivary output (SSO) between the two groups had no significant difference, which was 3.41 and 3.86 g. Nevertheless, the mean SSO of SMG-excised patients with a history of adjuvant radiotherapy was 2.61 g, which was significantly lower compared with the control group of patients without radiotherapy (4.07 g).

Shih et al. assessed functional outcomes in patients who underwent SMG-sparing neck dissection, finding that unilateral SMG excision was linked to a significantly higher relative risk of dry mouth versus complete SMG preservation (P<0.001) (54).

Preservation of SMG: contentions

In addition to the evidence supporting the preservation of the SMG, the potential risks of the procedure should also be considered. Inconsistencies in studies of preserved SMG postoperative function will also be discussed. Besides, there is no definitive conclusion as to which cases are suitable for neck dissection with SMG preservation. The various proposed indications will be reviewed and discussed.

Potential risks

The studies of SMG involvement rate are hugely based on histopathology. Therefore, the reliability of these studies is limited by the accuracy of the histopathologic assessment, inherent sampling error, and differences in reporting. Warshavsk et al. quantified the error rate in reporting the location and number of pathologic LNs in neck dissection specimens. The findings claimed at least 7.5% of neck dissection reports were inaccurate (55). There were also issues with the completeness of operative reports for neck dissections. Nocon et al. assessed 49 operative reports provided by 10 surgeons from 7 academic institutions. Results showed that the overall completeness of all neck dissection operative reports was 64% (40–79%, with a standard deviation of 9%). Sixty percent of reports described the removed LNs but did not share the same terminology or definitions (56).

The SMG has no true capsule but is enclosed within a sheath formed by the investing fascia of the neck that is attached superiorly to the mandible (57). Therefore, stripping the investing fascia requires additional effort on the part of surgeons to completely remove the peri-glandular fibroadipose tissue while preserving the integrity of SMG. Moreover, due to the obscuration of SMG, the surgical field of view of the post-glandular nodes is inadequate, which increases the difficulty of the procedure and the risk of iatrogenic injury. Preservation of the SMG increases the complexity of the neck dissection and prolongs the operative durations. Prior studies have shown that prolonged operative time is associated with an increased risk of post-operative complications and surgical morbidity, including, but not limited to septic shock, surgical site infection, wound disruption, thromboembolic events, extended anesthesia exposure, and longer hospital stay (58,59).

Functional outcome

SMG secretes the majority of the unstimulated saliva, which accounts for about 75% of the whole saliva (26). Therefore, the greatest purpose of preserving the SMG is to reduce the incidence of postoperative xerostomia. Although some studies have provided supporting evidence, others have shown that preservation of the SMG plays a minor role in salivary secretion. A retrospective study compared the presence and degree of postoperative xerostomia in 40 patients with OSCC (pT1–2N0) who received neck dissection with SMG preservation or not (60). Xerostomia presence and severity were assessed using three instruments: the University of Michigan Xerostomia Quality of Life (XeQoL), Short Form-8 (SF-8), and Xerostomia Severity Scale (XSS)—all of which are questionnaires. Results indicated no differences in XeQoL, SF-8, and XSS questionnaire scores between the SMG-preserved and SMG-excised groups (P=0.96, 0.87, and 0.7, respectively). In addition, Marunick et al. assessed the effects of unilateral SMG excision on whole salivary flow of patients with head and neck cancer (61). No significant difference was observed in salivary flow between the preoperative and postoperative periods. However, considering the limited number of subjects (only 5 patients), the results of the study seemed to be unreliable.

Compared to the questionnaire-based study, studies applying whole saliva collection and scintigraphy analysis are more convincing (50-53). A conclusion can be drawn that the SMG excision decreases the unstimulated salivary volume and is associated with postoperative xerostomia.

Discussion on the indications for SMG-preserved neck dissection

Only 3 clinical trials (Table 2) regarding SMG preservation in neck dissection could be retrieved, and none reported results yet (62-64). Due to the lack of clinical trials, there is no definitive conclusion on the indications for SMG-preserved neck dissection. Some studies have reached a consensus that the practice of SMG-preserved neck dissection should be conservative, with researchers limiting the indications for the SMG-preserved neck dissection to T1–2N0 patients. It was commonly agreed that the SMG should not be preserved in cases with positive LNs at level I and in tumor sites with a high risk of level I occult metastasis (46-48,65-69). Subramaniam et al. suggested SMG-preserved neck dissection is at least safe in early tumors (T1N0–1) (42).

On the contrary, there are also some radical views that the SMG can be preserved as long as it is not invaded, or no close positive LNs or metastasis are present (35,70-74). In addition, many studies have highlighted that floor of mouth and tongue tumors directly invaded the SMG most frequently. Preservation of the SMG should be more cautious with the tumors in these two areas (40,49,75). For early-stage OSCC, a systematic review concluded that elective neck dissection was associated with lower recurrence rates and higher OS and DSS compared to conservative observation approach (76). A prospective study suggested perineural invasion, lymphovascular invasion, and extra-capsular extension as the strongest predictors of SMG involvement (77).

Future perspectives

With the rapid development of multiple technologies and the emergence of numerous research findings, neck dissection with SMG preservation holds great promise in many aspects (Figure 3).

The neck dissection for HNSCC can achieve a higher level of precision and personalization. With the advancements in gene testing technologies and molecular markers, in the future, it is expected that by analyzing the genetic characteristics and molecular expression profiles of tumor patients, we can more accurately predict the risk of cervical LN metastasis in HNSCC. Some scholars have studied patient-derived tumor organoids. Through the research on 198 tumor tissue samples from 186 patients, they found that 77% of HNSCC patients could successfully undergo patient-derived organoids (PDOs) screening (78). In precision medicine, based on the sensitivity of PDOs to radiotherapy and drugs, we can determine patients’ responses to different treatment regimens. Thus, we can select the treatment method that has the least impact on the function of the SMG while effectively controlling the tumor, avoiding unnecessary SMG resection and achieving personalized treatment. At the same time, by taking into account patients’ individual differences, such as age, underlying diseases, and lifestyle, we can develop fully individualized treatment plans, making the decision-making regarding SMG preservation in neck dissection more scientific and reasonable.

We can also develop robotic surgery and minimally invasive surgery. Ma et al. used the oral and maxillofacial surgical robotic platform KD-SR-01 to perform neck dissection on porcine animal models. Compared with the standard endoscopic system, there was no significant difference in operation time, and it performed better in intraoperative bleeding control (79). We can achieve trauma reduction by precisely separating the tissue around the SMG with the help of robotic surgery and reducing the incision with the help of endoscopic surgery. We should also strengthen the interdisciplinary integration of medicine and engineering to develop new medical devices specifically designed for head and neck dissection. For example, the delicate surgical instruments can reduce the pulling and damage to the tissues, achieve effective hemostasis, and reduce the adverse thermal effect on the peripheral neurovascular. Robotic surgery enables the widespread implementation of clinical trials that preserve the parotid glands, thereby facilitating further exploration of its potential advantages over conventional surgical methods.

Real-time monitoring during surgery can significantly enhance the precision of the operation. Boehm et al. utilized hyperspectral imaging (HSI) combined with artificial intelligence. The HSI technology can capture spectral details beyond the reach of human vision, obtaining rich tissue information. In neck dissection surgeries aiming to preserve the SMG, HSI can be employed to conduct real-time detection of the surgical area, accurately differentiating between tumor tissues and normal tissues (80). This helps surgeons more clearly determine whether there is tumor infiltration in the SMG and its surrounding tissues. Some scholars have utilized drug delivery systems to deliver imaging agents, aiming to improve the accuracy of imaging for metastatic LNs. This is of great significance for precisely grasping the progression of patients’ conditions (81). In addition to this, fluorescence imaging technology, near-infrared spectroscopy technology, and others can assist surgeons in real-time identifying the lymphatic vessels, blood vessels, and nerve structures around the SMG during the operation, further improving the surgical accuracy.

We can implement multidisciplinary collaboration to help patients achieve better treatment outcomes. For example, in the case of recurrent or metastatic HNSCC, patients may experience adverse reactions such as adrenal insufficiency and gastritis after receiving a combined immunotherapy and chemotherapy regimen, which are managed by a multidisciplinary team (MDT) (82). Neoadjuvant immunotherapy offers various possibilities for the de-escalation of HNSCC treatment, which will provide an opportunity to help broaden the indications for SMG preservation (83). Regarding the preservation of the SMG, a MDT can comprehensively consider the timing and methods of combined applications of various treatment modalities, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy. If a patient is in poor physical condition and cannot tolerate surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy, an internist can first conduct systemic treatment to control the tumor. After the tumor shrinks, a surgeon can then assess the feasibility of preserving the SMG and develop a personalized treatment plan to balance tumor control and the preservation of SMG function.

It is also crucial to conduct long-term follow-up and quality of life studies on patients. Establish a comprehensive long-term follow-up system to conduct long-term tracking and observation of patients with HNSCC who have undergone neck dissection and retained the SMG. Not only should attention be paid to survival indicators such as tumor recurrence and metastasis, but also the impact of SMG preservation on the long-term quality of life of patients should be further investigated, including oral functions (saliva secretion, chewing, swallowing, taste, etc.), neck appearance, psychological state, and social functions. Besides, a research team conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis on the frequencies of LN metastasis at different cervical levels in patients with HNSCC (5). Moreover, whether the SMG can be preserved is significantly influenced by the location of the tumor. Research findings of this kind will help to clarify the indications for the preservation of the SMG. Through the accumulation and analysis of long-term follow-up data, continuously summarize experience and optimize the neck dissection procedure with SMG preservation, so that surgical treatment can truly achieve the dual goals of tumor eradication and improvement of quality of life.

Our predecessors have saved the paracervical nerve, sternocleidomastoid muscle and internal jugular vein from the scalpel in countless patients. Now we continue the spirit of our predecessors and propose the preservation of SMG, trying to take a new step in the development of functional neck clearance in HNSCC.

Our scholars have successfully utilized autologous microvascular SMG transplantation for severe dry eye disease. Follow-up results showed successful transplantation in 180 of 200 eyes (success rate: 90%), resulting in marked symptomatic relief of xerophthalmia (84). In the future SMG has the potential to be used for the restoration of other glands and is a potential donor of glandular tissue.

The molecular mechanisms at the cellular level will serve as the foundation for the development of surgical techniques. In our previous work, we explored the mechanisms of LN metastasis, proposed the PUMP+ (Preparation, Unleash, Migration, and Planting) principle, and delved into the close relationships among its molecular metastasis mechanisms, immunotherapy, and the precise diagnosis and treatment of LN metastasis. As we further clarify the molecular mechanisms, the logic behind whether the SMG can be preserved, and even the preservation of more related tissues, will become increasingly clear (85). Our thinking about SMG preservation will greatly deepen the concept of precision resection and provide a template for measuring whether the surrounding tissues can be preserved or not. The need for tissue preservation will be comprehensively analyzed from anatomical, pathological, survival and functional perspectives while ensuring patient safety. Although this puts higher demands on surgeons, we should actively respond to the challenges of precision surgery by advancing the development of clinical techniques and skillfully combining technological achievements in multiple fields. In the future, we will move in this direction to further explore the preservation of more functional structures and make continuous efforts to minimize patient trauma and improve patient quality of life.

Conclusions

With the widespread acceptance of the concept of preservative functional surgery, surgeons have been exploring the possibility of preserving more organs in neck dissection. Since the removal of the SMG in neck dissection can cause patients to suffer from post-operative xerostomia, the feasibility of preserving the SMG in neck dissection has attracted research interest in recent years. We summarized and evaluated the anatomical, pathological, survival, and functional evidence for the feasibility of preserving the SMG. In conclusion, the involvement of SMG in HNSCC is rare due to the absence of intraglandular LNs in SMG. Preservation of the SMG has no impact on patient survival and can significantly improve salivary function after surgery. The surgical approach to preserve the SMG in neck dissection is oncologically safe and is recommended for T1–2N0 patients.

However, several concerns remain to be taken into consideration. First of all, it is not clear whether the SMG can be preserved in patients with advanced stages, and the biological behavior of different types and sites of tumors varies. Therefore, the decision to preserve the SMG should not be planned preoperatively, but preferably made intraoperatively with the help of visual examination and frozen sections instead of preoperatively planned resection (86). However, the application of sentinel LN biopsy may be of little help in determine whether to preserve the SMG. Because there is no intraglandular LN in SMG, sentinel LNs of SMG cannot be defined. Second, some concerns have indicated that post-operative radiation therapy may render efforts to preserve the SMG futile, but salivary gland transfer (SGT) may offer a solution. Studies show that SGT is very effective in preventing xerostomia in patients undergoing definitive head and neck radiation therapy (87-91). Third, as the current grade of evidence on SMG preserved neck dissection is generally low, it is not sufficient to form a guideline. Currently, only 3 randomized controlled trial studies regarding SMG preservation in neck dissection are available in the Cochrane library, and neither has reported results yet (62-64). In addition to these, some studies mention that retaining the SMG increases the duration of the surgery, which may increase the burden on the patient’s prognosis (45). So better surgical techniques are also needed, and current surgical robots for head and neck surgery offer new ideas (79). Therefore, more high evidence-graded studies need to be conducted. Revolutionary innovation always comes from technological breakthroughs. We envision an enhanced technique of sialography that can determine preoperatively the presence or absence of tumor invasion. Non-invasive examination and accurate diagnosis are the blueprints for the future of SMG preservation.
