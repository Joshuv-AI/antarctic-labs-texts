Design of cold asphalt concrete with experimental emulsion by magnetic induction
Authors: Christopher Delafuente-Navarro, Manuel Lagos-Varas, Pedro Lastra-González, Irune Indacoechea-Vega, Daniel Castro-Fresno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this research, a cold asphalt concrete with asphalt emulsion has been developed, which has a structure and mechanical performance equivalent to hot asphalt concrete. The novel asphalt concrete is composed of a new asphalt emulsion and a magnetic industrial by-product, mixed at room temperature, without the need to pre-heat the binder or aggregates. The key to this technology is the use of magnetic induction, which allows the water present in the emulsions to evaporate quickly, facilitating the compaction and curing of the mixture. In order to make a holistic evaluation of the performance of this new “cold” asphalt concrete, rheological and mechanical tests were carried out. The rheological evaluation included tests such as Dynamic Shear Rheometer (DSR), Multiple Stress Creep Recovery (MSCR), Linear Amplitude Sweep (LAS), Binder Yield Energy Test and DSR-Elastic Recovery. The mechanical evaluation included tests such as Marshall Test, Water sensitivity test, Wheel Tracking Test and particle loss test in dry and wet conditions. Regarding the results, cold asphalt concrete presents a mechanical behaviour equivalent to traditional hot asphalt concrete with conventional B50/70, eliminating the need to heat aggregates or binders during the mixing. This represents a significant optimisation of resources and production times.

Introduction

The asphalt industry is one of the most energy-consuming activities in the world1. In this industry, the most commonly used method is the production of hot asphalt (HMA)2. The production process of HMA requires an average consumption of 356 MJ3 and generates approximately 60.7 kg of CO2 equivalent per tonne4. In addition, other greenhouse gases, such as nitrous oxide and methane, are emitted during their manufacture3. Therefore, it is essential to develop more environmentally friendly alternatives to mitigate the impact of this activity.

In this respect, cold mixtures have the advantage of requiring less energy and generating lower greenhouse gas emissions during their manufacture3,5. However, their use has been relegated to the background due to certain limitations: they require long curing times6–8, delaying the opening of new roads, and have lower mechanical performance3,5,7, the latter characteristic implies the need for more frequent repairs, resulting in higher costs and higher emissions than hot mixtures, when considered over the entire life cycle9.

Several researchers have worked on solving the limitations associated with cold mixtures, making significant progress. For example, modification with rubber improves the low-temperature properties of emulsified asphalt10, hot rubber offers better cohesion resistance11 or the possible use of RAP in cold mixtures12. However, these investigations have not resolved the long curing times. For this reason, other researchers have employed the promising technology of magnetic induction, which has led to a significant reduction in curing time13–16. In addition, this technology has been successfully applied for self-healing cold mixtures13,17, which could increase its durability and decrease the need for repairs. However, these mixtures still have inferior mechanical performance compared to hot mixtures, which represents a remaining challenge to optimise their properties and encourage their adoption as a potentially more sustainable option.

The design of cold asphalt concretes has significant limitations due to the water content present in the asphalt emulsions, water that cannot be compacted, which makes it impossible to achieve a low percentage of voids18. Traditional compaction methods, such as the Marshall method or the gyratory compactor, have failed to achieve adequate void content for asphalt concrete7,19. To address this problem, researchers have explored strategies such as prolonged heating of the mixture20–22 or the implementation of double compaction processes23,24. However, none of these techniques has been able to achieve the optimum void ratio for an asphalt concrete, is usually around 4% and 6% approximately.

In a previous investigation25, an innovative method combining the use of magnetic induction with traditional compaction techniques was proposed, achieving the design of an asphalt concrete with asphalt emulsion without the need to pre-heat the aggregates or binder. Although this approach solved the problem of achieving the required void ratio, the mechanical performance of the resulting concrete was inferior to that of hot mixed asphalt concretes because of the emulsion type: the residual binder was excessively soft.

For all the above reasons, the objective of this research is to develop an experimental cold asphalt concrete with emulsion capable of achieving mechanical performance comparable to that of hot asphalt concretes. The central hypothesis is that the use of an emulsion with a harder residual binder, combined with magnetic induction-assisted compaction, will allow the design of cold mixtures with rheological and mechanical properties equivalent to those of hot mixtures.

With the aim of achieving the objective of this research, an experimental asphalt emulsion was developed using a harder residual binder. In order to comprehensively evaluate the proposed technology, a rheological analysis of the mastic of the experimental asphalt concrete was carried out. In addition, a mechanical evaluation of the experimental asphalt concrete was carried out, comparing it to the asphalt concrete with commercial asphalt emulsion, designed in previous research of this same project25 and with a conventional hot asphalt concrete, made with a 50–70 penetration grade binder, typically used in Europe.

Concrete asphalt manufacture

Three types of asphalt concrete have been manufactured. Although hot asphalt concrete does not need it for its manufacturing process, they all had a magnetic industrial by-product in order to rule out mechanical differences in behaviour due to the SSB. In this respect, all samples had the same dosage, as shown in Fig. 4.

The first reference asphalt concrete (AC1), with binder type 50–70, has been manufactured according to the Spanish standard. That is, the binder has been heated for 3 h at 150 °C and the aggregates for 6 h at 170 °C. Then, the coarse and fine fraction were mixed with the binder. Rheological evaluation

The rheological study and analysis on asphalt mastics has been carried out to gain a comprehensive understanding of the performance of the new asphalt concrete. Firstly, the Linear Viscoelastic Properties (LVE) have been evaluated in order to know the stiffness |G*|, phase angle δ and complex viscosity η∗ of the asphalt mastic. Secondly, tests have been carried out to analyse the continuous damage of viscoelastic samples, obtaining plastic deformation properties, damage intensity, toughness, creep energy and elastic recovery. Three samples of each mastic have been carried out in each test.

Master curve

The master curves allow the analysis of the relationship between temperature and loading frequency in 2-D space. To obtain both settings, the dynamic shear rheometer (DSR) test is performed to measure the linear rheological properties (LVE). The temperature sweep in the test was from 10 to 70 °C. The oscillatory test was performed at 10 frequencies, ranging from 0.1 to 30 Hz, with a sinusoidal displacement of 0.1% strain. Binder yield energy test (BYE)

The BYE test is performed in accordance with AASHTO TP-123, using specimens at a temperature of 20 °C in the dynamic shear rheometer (DSR) with 8 mm diameter plates and 2 mm spacing. During the test, a monotonic load is applied with a constant strain rate of 2.315% s−1, measuring stress and strain as a function of time. Two key parameters related to fatigue behaviour are obtained from this test: the creep energy and the strain at the point of maximum shear stress. The creep energy, Er, is calculated as the area under the stress-strain curve until the maximum shear stress is reached (see Eq. 12). Dynamic shear rheometer-elastic recovery test (DSR-ER)

The DSR-ER test is used to quantify the elasticity of the tested samples. The test temperatures are 20 °C. Initially, a load with a constant shear strain gradient of 2.315% s−1 is applied until a shear strain of 277.78% is reached. Subsequently, a recovery stage of 30 min is carried out, during which the recovery of the deformation resulting from the creep process is measured.

Mechanical evaluation

A mechanical study and analysis were carried out to test the mechanical feasibility of the new experimental cold-mixed asphalt concrete. Density and voids were measured, followed by Marshall, water sensitivity, wheel tracking, modulus of rigidity and Cantabro tests.

All results of the mechanical assessment were analysed by statistical analysis using Minitab software with a 95% confidence interval. Specifically, the Student’s t-test was used when the results were parametric. Otherwise, the U-Mann Whitney test was used.

Multiple stress creep recovery test (MSCR)

The results of the MSCR test are shown in Fig. 6; Table 5. In this respect, M3 has a relatively stable recovery, with values of 12.34% at 64 °C and 12.12% at 70 °C, although lower than M2. Under the 3.2 kPa load, M3 experiences a decrease in recovery similar to M1. These results indicate that M2 has a good recovery capability, although it is affected at very high temperature conditions, possibly because M2 has a softer binder. At the same time, the mixture M1 shows elastic recovery (R) values that decrease with increasing temperature, reaching its lowest point at 64 °C (5.37%) and recovering slightly at 70 °C (13.42%).

Under a load of 3.2 kPa, the elastic recovery is even lower, especially at elevated temperatures, recording an R of only 0.77% at 70 °C. These results suggest that M1 has limited recovery capability, particularly under high loads and temperatures, what is to be expected because this binder is not modified with polymers. The mixture M2, on the other hand, exhibits relatively high elastic recovery at 0.1 kPa, remaining at 89.94% up to 64 °C, although it decreases drastically to 14.35% at 70 °C. At a load of 3.2 kPa, M2 also shows a decrease in R, but retains better performance than M1 at most temperatures.

As for the Jnr values, clear and distinct trends can be seen between the three mixtures under study. M1 shows a progressive increase in non-recoverable deformations as temperature and load increase. At low temperatures (52–58 °C), the Jnr values remain in the range of 0.23–0.63 at 1 kPa and 0.22–0.68 at 3.2 kPa, which is acceptable. However, at 64 °C, this slope increases, reaching a maximum of 2.88 at 70 °C, which indicates a high transition of susceptibility to creep. Therefore, an exponential loss of stiffness is evident, which is consistent with the nature of a modified binder.

In comparison, M2 shows a much more favourable and stable response. Specifically, at 52 °C, Jnr is minimal (see Table 5), demonstrating high resistance to deformation. Between 58 and 64 °C, the evolution is moderate with values around 0.18–0.81 for the minimum load, confirming greater creep resistance compared to M1 and M3. Despite this, at 70 °C this trend is reversed due to the Jnr values, increasing to 0.82 and 0.13 (1/kPa). This scenario shows us a stabilising effect of the modified binder, capable of maintaining good resistance and preventing possible rutting, but with performance losses at low loads and extreme temperatures. Finally, M3 shows intermediate behaviour, with relatively low and stable Jnr values up to 64 °C, with a pronounced increase at 70 °C. This does not indicate that polymer modification offers advantages at moderate temperatures, although it loses effectiveness in critical conditions.

In relation to recovery R, M1 behaves reasonably as the temperature increases, going from 14.98% to 5.37% from 52 to 64 °C, respectively. M2, on the other hand, behaves atypically, with a maximum recovery of 89.94% at 64 °C, which then drops to 14.35% at 70 °C. M3 maintains more consistent values across the temperature range, reflecting relative stability, although with lower recovery than M2 under favourable conditions. On the other hand, if we look at the Rdiff and Jnrdiff indicators, they reinforce these trends. For M1, the high Rdiff values, for example 94.28% at 70 °C and 26.41 in Jnrdiff, reflect high sensitivity to load increase, implying possible poor performance under heavy traffic. M3 exhibits a similar pattern, with high Rdiff across the range (42.91–84.44%) and a Jnrdiff peak of 53.48 at 58 °C, revealing instability in response to load changes. In contrast, M2 shows more robust behaviour, with reduced Jnrdiff values (maximum of 5.29 at 58 °C) and a drastic drop to 0.50 at 70 °C, indicating better adaptability to load variations and field conditions.

Linear amplitude sweep test (LAS)

The results of the LAS test (Table 6; Fig. 7) show clear differences in the fatigue mechanisms for the three emulsions. Firstly, the parameter obtained in the low strain sweep discriminates the rheological behaviour of each material. In this regard, M1 has the highest value of 1.470, indicating a more elastic response, while M2 and M3 reach values of 0.684 and 0.728 respectively, which generate a more viscous character, especially when the deformation demand increases. This initial difference conditions the rheological behaviour of subsequent damage.

In the second stage of the test (constant frequency of 10 Hz), the instantaneous resistance in terms of maximum stress is clearly higher in M3, reaching a value of smax of 820,380 Pa, followed by M2 with 643,353 Pa and M1 with 438,220 Pa. However, as we know, maximum stress does not translate into an indicator of pavement durability. Therefore, if we analyse the curve as the deformation amplitude increases, we find that M3 shows the shortest fatigue life across the entire range, while M1 maintains the longest life and M2 occupies an intermediate position. This trend indicates that M3 better withstands high stress peaks but quickly exhausts its integrity (Fig. 7b) under cyclic loads.

Sensitivity to life fatigue is largely determined by the fatigue slope (B). The values for M1, M2, and M3 are − 2.94, − 2.92, and − 2.75, respectively, indicating that M1 and M2 have steeper slopes, i.e., greater penalties for increased deformation, while M3 is slightly less sensitive. The damage parameters further reinforce this interpretation. Damage to failure Df is greater in M1, intermediate in M2 and lower in M3, which shows greater tolerance to accumulated damage in M1 before localisation and failure. Furthermore, this feature is consistent with the value of a (elastic range) and with the damage-integrity curves. Where M1 shows a more moderate accumulation of damage, without early acceleration, M2 concentrates its damage in the final stages and M3 in early damage peaks, typical of materials that dissipate energy via viscous means. The parameter k (M1 = 2.01; M2 = 2.11; M3 = 1.84), together with A and B, also suggests differences in the curvature of the damage law: M3 combines high σmax with lower k and Df, which translates into a rapid transition from the quasi-elastic regime to the damage-dominated regime, while M1 needs to accumulate greater effective damage to reach failure, prolonging its life under equivalent amplitudes.

Taken together, the interpretation of α, σmax, B, k and Df, together with the evolution of Nf as a function of deformation, allows the rheological mechanism to be linked to fatigue durability: M1 exhibits the best compromise between elastic storage and damage tolerance (higher α and Df, higher Nf), M2 presents intermediate performance with some vulnerability to initial stages of damage, and M3 shows high instantaneous resistance but low fatigue life due to its lower ability to maintain integrity as amplitude increases. This integrative reading goes beyond a mere comparison of Nf. It provides a mechanistic basis for explaining why materials with high σmax may nevertheless exhibit earlier fatigue. This occurs when their rheological response is more viscous and their tolerance to damage accumulation is limited.

Binder yield energy test (BYE)

Figure 8; Table 7 show the results of the BYE test to evaluate the ability of a material to withstand cyclic loading. M1 presents a maximum stress of 0.0639 MPa, the lowest among the three samples, M2 and M3 have a significantly higher maximum stress of 0.1461 MPa and 0.1436 MPa respectively, more than twice that of M1. This result tells us that both M2 and M3 have higher resistance to applied loads compared to M1. The difference in the maximum stress values suggests that the M2 and M3 samples have a behaviour strongly influenced by the action of the polymers composing the emulsions. In contrast, M1, with a low maximum stress, could indicate a structure that is less resistant to loading, making it more susceptible to failure under high loading conditions.

Regarding the deformation reached at the moment of maximum stress, M1 has a deformation of 148.739%, the lowest among the samples. M2 has a deformation of 663.989%, significantly higher than M1. M3 has the highest strain of all, 693.138%, indicating a greater ability to deform under applied loads. These differences suggest that M2 and M3 have a greater ability to experience large deformations without failure, which is characteristic of more flexible and ductile materials. This behaviour may be beneficial in applications where the material must be able to absorb large deformations without suffering irreversible damage, such as in systems where high flexibility or good energy absorption capacity is required.

Creep energy measures the ability of the material to recover its original shape after being deformed. In this case, the high Er values for M2 and M3 indicate that these materials are better able to recover their shape after deformation, making them more suitable for applications where the material is expected to undergo repetitive load cycling, such as in components subjected to bending or impact. This resilience is especially relevant in applications where durability is a key factor. In contrast, M1 has a much lower recovery modulus, suggesting that after deformation, this material is less able to recover its original shape.

Dynamic shear rheometer-elastic recovery test (DSR-ER)

The results of the DSR-ER test are visualized in Fig. 9. The results indicate that after the maximum strain value has been reached (about 2 min), the specimens begin to show a gradual elastic recovery, i.e., the strain decreases with time. This behaviour reflects how the material tends to return to its original shape once the load is removed. Regarding the elastic recovery ER of the samples, we obtain that sample M1 (ER = 6.80%) shows a relatively low elastic recovery. This suggests that the material is not very capable of recovering its original shape after deformation, what may be due to the lack of polymers in its structure that contribute to recover its original shape. M2 (ER = 16.45%) shows a significantly higher elastic recovery than M1, indicating a better ability to recover its shape after deformation. Sample M3 (ER = 8.72) shows intermediate recovery, suggesting that the material has a moderate recovery capability. Although it is better than M1, its performance in terms of elastic recovery is lower than M2. This result can be explained by the fact that M3 has a residual binder equal to M1 and is modified with polymers, so it is natural that it recovers more of its shape, but presents more difficulties than M2, which has a very soft binder modified with polymers.

Wheel tracking test

Table 12 shows the results of the wheel tracking test. In this respect, AC1 and ACexp show the best results respect to depth and wheel tracking slope, with no statistically significant differences between them. As in the previous tests, AC2 shows severe plastic deformations, which is statistically significantly upper than the rest of the mixtures (Table 13). Therefore, AC1 and ACexp have high resistance to permanent deformation, being much more stable and able to withstand loads without excessive deformations. These are consistent with the observations made in the MSCR test, where AC1 and ACexp, associated with binders with moderate Jnr values and more stable elastic recovery (R), showed less non-recoverable deformation under different load levels. In particular, the low Jnrdiff and Rdiff values of these mixtures reflect lower sensitivity to increases in applied stresses, which translates into a more consistent response and better performance under repeated loads. As discussed in WST, AC1 and ACexp are more suitable for use in pavement wearing courses, where the risk of rutting is high. On the other hand, AC2 would have limited performance and would require adjustments in its design, since the asphalt emulsion commonly used is inadequate.

The results at plastic deformation show that the mixtures made with the experimental emulsion are shown to be a potentially efficient option to replace the use of hot mix asphalt concretes made with the traditional 50–70 grade binder.

In contrast, mixture AC2, characterised in MSCR by Jnr values increasing with temperature and very limited elastic recovery, as well as high Rdiff and Jnrdiff values at high loads, showed a marked inability to maintain elastic recovery when the stress was increased, which explains the accumulated deformations and high slopes recorded in the wheel tracking test. Thus, the integration of the results of both tests confirms the existence of a direct relationship between the rheological indices and the mechanical behaviour of the mixtures: binders with low non-recoverable deformation and high stability against load variation produce mixtures with better rutting resistance, while binders with lower recovery capacity and high sensitivity to load tend to produce mixtures with significant plastic deformation under traffic and temperature conditions.

Overall, the results of the MSCR and wheel tracking tests highlight the ability of the experimental emulsion used in ACexp to provide performance comparable to that of AC1, made with a conventional 50/70 binder, confirming its viability as an effective alternative in wearing courses where the risk of rutting is high.

Conclusions

A novel asphalt concrete manufactured by magnetic induction with a new asphalt emulsion has been studied. In this regard, it is possible to conclude that:

ACexp has equivalent or even superior mechanical performance to the hot asphalt concrete usually used in northern Spain. Specifically, ACexp and AC1 do not present statistically significant differences in terms of deformation and stability. In addition, both asphalt mixtures show a high resistance to plastic deformation. In addition, ACexp has higher WTS values than AC1 and AC2 in both dry and wet conditions. At the same time, in terms of particle loss, all asphalt concretes present a high resistance, with AC2 showing the best performance, although without significant statistical differences with ACexp. Therefore, the mechanical performance of ACexp is positioned as an efficient option to replace conventional hot AC mixtures.The experimental mastic (M3) shows characteristics that make it suitable for asphalt pavement applications that require a balance between stiffness and deformation capacity. Firstly, the DSR indicates that M3 is more suitable for pavement conditions where a higher complex modulus is needed, reflecting its high stiffness. In addition, in the LAS test, the M3 specimens showed the highest maximum stress value (820,380 Pa), which is evidence of its high strength. However, it showed fewer cycles, so further work is needed in this area in the future. On the other hand, the BYE test results reveal that M3 withstands higher applied loads and has the highest deformation capacity (693.138%), which allows it to deform significantly without failure. Finally, the intermediate elastic recovery (ER = 8.72) observed in the DSR-Elastic recovery test suggests that, although its recovery capacity is not extreme, it is adequate for its expected performance in pavements.The commercial asphalt emulsions typically used are not adequate to achieve the equivalent mechanical strength of commonly manufactured hot mix asphalts. This research has shown that the possibility of working with emulsions with a harder residual binder gives positive results in terms of rheology and mechanics.

Future issues

The ACexp has a mechanical performance equivalent to the conventional AC1. However, this study has certain limitations related to the experimental scope and the conditions evaluated, so it is recommended that future research address the following lines of work. In this regard, in the future, an environmental and economic impact analysis will justify the use of magnetic induction technology and experimental emulsion. In addition, more studies should be made regarding its performance at low temperatures and at medium scale before projecting a test section with this type of asphalt concrete.

In addition, it is essential to study in the future the fatigue resistance of the asphalt concrete designed with the experimental emulsion by means of magnetic induction.

Furthermore, although self-healing was not directly evaluated in this study, it is important to note that the inclusion of magnetic particles in the ACexp mixture lays the foundation for possible self-healing through magnetic induction reheating cycles. This suggests that, beyond the equivalent mechanical performance achieved, these mixtures could extend their service life if their self-healing capacity is confirmed in future research.
