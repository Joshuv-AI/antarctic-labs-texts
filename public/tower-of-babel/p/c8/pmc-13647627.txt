Chronic kidney disease burden attributable to heat waves amid climate change and related hospital accessibility: a health impact assessment study based on two nationally representative surveys in China
Authors: Wanzhou Wang, Fulin Wang, Xiao Zhang, Ze Liang, Shaoqing Wei, Yingying Qin, Jinwei Wang, Feifei Zhang, Pengfei Li, Ying Zhou, Limin Wang, Chao Yang, Luxia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeat waves are increasingly recognized as an environmental determinant of chronic kidney disease (CKD). However, the CKD burden attributable to heat waves and associated healthcare inequalities, particularly under future climate change, remains insufficiently characterized.MethodsBased on two nationally representative cross-sectional surveys in China, we established climate region-specific exposure-response functions between heat waves and CKD. Then, we conducted 1-km grid-level health impact assessments integrating the most up-to-date nationally representative CKD prevalence data, temperature projections, population estimates, and socioeconomic indicators. The analyses focused on stages 4–5 CKD (advanced CKD), given its progressive nature and requirements for continuous medical treatment. Heat wave-attributable CKD burden was quantified as attributable cases (ACs), attributable fractions (AFs), and population attributable fractions (PAFs). Projections for 2030–2090 were generated under multiple Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs). Hospital accessibility was evaluated using AC-weighted driving times and accessibility scores, with inequality assessed via Gini indices and Lorenz curves.ResultsIn 2020, an estimated 491,362 (227,772–694,191) stages 4–5 CKD cases (about 30.06% of all cases and 45.31 per 100,000 adults) were attributable to heat waves, disproportionately affecting rural areas (PAF: 47.08 per 100,000) and subtropical and tropical regions (PAF: 52.43 per 100,000) compared to their counterparts. Projections indicated increasing trends in PAFs under high-emission scenarios (e.g., SSP5-8.5 showing > 2-fold increase in 2090). The average driving time to the nearest hospital for ACs was 15.8 (7.6–22.3) minutes, with rural areas showing significantly longer times (20.4 min) and lower accessibility. The national Gini index for hospital accessibility was 0.45, indicating high inequality, and the inequality might persist across all future climate scenarios.ConclusionsHeat waves are associated with a substantial and spatially uneven burden of advanced CKD in China, compounded by significant inequalities in hospital accessibility. These inequalities may persist under future climate change given current hospital accessibility settings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12916-026-05067-5.

Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major global public health concern [1]. It is estimated that there were about 674 million individuals worldwide living with CKD, and 1.5 million deaths were attributed to CKD in 2021 [2]. In China, the estimated prevalence of CKD is 8.2% ~ 10.8%, indicating that more than 100 million people are affected [3, 4]. In addition to traditional risk factors such as obesity, diabetes, and hypertension, climate change has been identified as one major environmental determinant of CKD [5–7]. High temperatures may impact kidney health through multiple mechanisms, including inducing dehydration, intracellular volume depletion, and chronic heat stress, which collectively elevate renal filtration workload and trigger cumulative renal damage, thereby contributing to the progression of CKD [8, 9]. Epidemiological studies from various countries have also confirmed a significant association between high temperatures and an increased risk of CKD [8, 10–13]. For instance, a meta-analysis estimated a 7.7% increase in kidney-related morbidity associated with high-intensity heatwaves [10]. A study in Adelaide, South Australia, also observed significantly elevated admissions for CKD associated with the increase in temperature [8]. In China, although several nationwide studies have reported exposure-response relationships between heat waves and CKD, showing that short-term heat exposures increase the risk of CKD hospitalizations and annual heatwave durations increase the odds of CKD prevalence [11–13], there remains a lack of studies estimating the burden of CKD attributable to heat waves under various future climate change scenarios.

Hospital accessibility is considered a factor closely associated with disparities in health outcomes, which can lead to health inequality issues [14–16]. Enhancing the equity of hospital accessibility is essential for promoting human health and social welfare, and has been identified as a key objective of the United Nations’ 2030 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [17]. Previous studies have suggested that greater accessibility to healthcare resources (such as the number of hospitals, timely access to care, and hospital bed availability) might be associated with lower heat wave-related health outcomes, thereby mitigating the human health impacts of heat exposure in the context of climate change [18–20].

In China, the substantial population size and persistent regional development disparities have increasingly highlighted the critical issue of inequality in hospital accessibility [21–23]. Given the substantial disease burden of CKD and its high susceptibility to extreme heat, analyzing the coupled inequality between heat wave-related CKD burden and hospital accessibility is of great significance for optimizing the allocation of medical resources and enhancing health adaptation capacity in underdeveloped regions within the context of climate change. This is particularly crucial for patients with advanced (stages 4–5) CKD, as it is irreversible and inevitably progresses to requiring renal replacement therapies such as dialysis or transplantation [4, 24, 25]. For these patients, continuous and standardized care under specialized nephrology services is essential [25]. This makes hospital accessibility a critical and life-saving factor for preventing complications and ensuring better outcomes.

To address these research gaps, we aimed to provide evidence on the burden of advanced CKD attributable to heat waves and the inequalities caused by hospital accessibility amid climate change in China. This study was structured based on the following hypotheses: (a) exposure to heat waves is positively associated with prevalence of advanced CKD; (b) the advanced CKD burden attributable to heat waves will progressively escalate amid climate change; and (c) the heat wave-attributable CKD burden and related hospital accessibility have significant spatial inequalities. In this study, first, based on all available nationally representative data on CKD, we established the exposure-response functions between heat waves and advanced CKD. Second, we estimated the spatial distribution characteristics of heat wave-related CKD in the current time and up to 2090 under different climate change scenarios based on temperature projections, population estimates, and socioeconomic indicators. Third, we further evaluated the hospital accessibility and inequity associated with heat wave-related CKD cases.

Methods

Study design

In this health impact assessment study, we followed a two-stage approach. In the first stage, we established the exposure-response functions for heat waves and CKD based on all available nationally representative, population-based data on CKD in China. Using all available nationwide evidence aligns with the core principle of the “best available evidence” suggested by health impact assessment frameworks, thereby minimizing the risk of selection bias in the exposure-response functions and enhancing the accuracy of the subsequent burden estimates [26–28]. In the second stage, we generated grid cells with a spatial resolution of 1-km × 1-km covering mainland China as the basic study units, and linked each grid to the most recent nationally representative statistics on CKD prevalence, exposure-response functions, high-resolution daily ambient temperature data, gridded population estimates, socioeconomic data, and hospital accessibility data. These inputs were used to estimate the burden of CKD attributable to heat waves and associated inequalities in hospital accessibility across China in the current period (2020) and under future climate change scenarios. Our study focused on advanced CKD populations, considering the severity, irreversibility, and the demand for continuous specialized medical treatment in hospitals [4, 24, 25].

Study population

We included all available nationally representative individual-level data on CKD to date, including the China National Survey of Chronic Kidney Disease (CNSCKD) covering 47,086 participants from 13 provincial-level divisions in mainland China conducted between 2007 and 2010, and the Sixth China Chronic Disease and Risk Factor Surveillance (CCDRFS) covering 176,874 participants from all 31 provincial-level divisions in mainland China conducted between 2018 and 2019 [11, 12]. The two surveys used multistage stratified sampling methods to ensure population representativeness (flow charts for sampling methods are shown in Fig. 1).

In the two surveys, information on participants’ demographic characteristics, medical histories, and health status was collected using questionnaires, physical measurements, and standardized laboratory examinations. Questionnaires captured sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and medical histories. Physical measurements included height, weight, and blood pressure measurements following a strict protocol. Fasting blood and morning urine samples were sampled and analyzed in standardized laboratories with quality control. More detailed information is provided in Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods) and our previous publications [11, 12]. The CNSCKD and the Six CCDRFS were approved by the ethics review committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-20030) and the National Center for Chronic and Noncommunicable Disease Control and Prevention, respectively. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before the study began.

CKD definition and prevalence

CKD was defined by impaired kidney function or albuminuria, as examined by laboratories that had completed standardization and certification programs [11, 12]. The CKD-EPI formula was used to calculate the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and advanced CKD was defined as an eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 [29]. Advanced 4–5 CKD prevalence by province and urban/rural areas was calculated based on the sixth CCDRFS (2018–2019), the most up-to-date representative cross-sectional survey covering all 31 provincial-level divisions in mainland China [4]. The sixth CCDRFS adopted a multistage, stratified, clustered sampling design based on the national Disease Surveillance Point (DSP) system, ensuring representativeness at both provincial and urban-rural levels. This design allowed for reliable estimation of region-specific CKD prevalence [4].

Characterizations of heat waves

Historical daily mean temperature data at 1-km spatial resolution from 2000 to 2020 used in the first stage were obtained from one high-accuracy gridded meteorological dataset in China [30]. This dataset was developed using a hierarchical reconstruction framework that integrates observations from more than 2,300 meteorological stations with topographic and geographical predictors, and has demonstrated high accuracy and spatial continuity across diverse climatic and terrain conditions (the median correlation coefficient = 0.99) [30, 31]. Information on residence at the community level was collected for all participants and geocoded, allowing assignment of residence-specific daily mean temperature time series [11, 12].

Projected daily mean temperature data from 2030 to 2090 used in the second stage were obtained from Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP6) under four shared socioeconomic pathways (SSPs)-representative concentration pathways (RCPs) scenarios, including SSP1‑2.6, SSP2‑4.5, SSP3‑7.0, and SSP5‑8.5 [32, 33]. These scenarios couple broad socioeconomic trajectories, which outline possible future social and economic developments, with radiative forcing pathways indicating greenhouse gas concentrations by 2100. In brief, SSP1‑2.6 represents a sustainable future with low emissions, SSP2‑4.5 represents a middle-of-the-road scenario, SSP3‑7.0 represents regional rivalry with higher emissions, and SSP5‑8.5 represents a fossil-fuel-intensive pathway. Outputs from eight different General Circulation Models (GCMs), including ACCESS-CM2, BCC-CSM2-MR, CMCC-ESM2, CNRM-ESM2-1, GFDL-ESM4, IITM-ESM, MIROC6, and MRI-ESM2-0, were selected based on their coverage of the four SSP-RCP scenarios and their documented performance over China [27, 34, 35]. To ensure consistency between historical and future exposure assessment, projected temperatures were bias-corrected using the aforementioned high-resolution observed historical dataset as the reference [30], following a distributional calibration approach detailed in a previous study [26].

To account for spatial heterogeneity in background climate and local temperature adaptation, heat waves were defined using a residence-specific, percentile-based approach rather than absolute temperature thresholds, in line with previous studies [11, 12, 36]. A heat wave day was identified when the daily mean temperature exceeded the 95th percentile of the location-specific historical (2000–2020) daily mean temperature distribution for at least three consecutive days [13, 36, 37]. In the first stage, based on the geocoded coordinates of participants’ residences in the CNSCKD and CCDRFS studies, we extracted and assigned residence-specific heat wave days of the year preceding each participant’s respective survey time to represent individual exposure levels. For the second stage, heat waves in the current (2020) and future (2030–2090) periods were identified and assigned to each 1-km × 1-km grid cell.

Datasets used in the health impact assessment study

Hospital accessibility

We obtained hospital accessibility data from a high-resolution (1-km × 1-km) national dataset, which provided both estimated travel times and accessibility scores across mainland China [23, 38]. This dataset included five key indicators at the grid cell level: estimated driving time to the nearest primary, secondary, tertiary, and all hospitals, as well as a hospital accessibility score. Travel times were calculated based on OpenStreetMap road network data using the Contraction Hierarchies algorithm, which enables efficient computation of the shortest driving routes across complex, large-scale road systems. Driving time was used as the primary measure of spatial access, aligning with healthcare system planning in China. For the hospital accessibility score, the dataset applied the Gaussian Two-Step Floating Catchment Area method, which considers both hospital capacity and population demand within variable catchment thresholds [23]. In the first step, the supply-to-demand ratio for each hospital was computed by dividing its bed capacity by the Gaussian-weighted service population. In the second step, the spatial accessibility score for each grid was derived by aggregating the weighted ratios of all accessible hospitals within their respective travel thresholds. This approach more accurately reflects realistic healthcare service coverage. The validity of the modeled travel times was supported by strong agreement with real-time estimates from three widely used Chinese map services (Baidu, Gaode, and Tencent), with provincial-level R2 values ranging from 0.82 to 0.87 [23].

Population data

We obtained current population distributions from the LandScan 1-km resolution dataset [39, 40]. LandScan applies a multi-factor modeling approach that synthesizes diverse geospatial inputs to allocate population counts within census-defined boundaries. This ensures consistency between aggregated cell-level estimates and official census totals. Previous validation studies reported moderate to high agreement (> 75%) of LandScan population values at the grid-cell level [40–42].

For future population estimates, we used 1-km gridded projections aligned with SSP1‑2.6, SSP2‑4.5, SSP3‑7.0, and SSP5‑8.5 developed using a region-specific Random Forest (RF) model [43, 44]. This modeling approach incorporated spatial autocorrelation and a wide range of covariates reflecting environmental, geographical, and urban development characteristics. Validation against WorldPop 2020 benchmarks showed grid-level mean absolute errors ranging from 3.76 to 55.57 people/km2, demonstrating strong comparative performance relative to existing projection models.

To enhance accuracy for this study, all population estimates were calibrated against official demographic statistics from the National Bureau of Statistics of China (https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/) [45]. Given that advanced CKD burden estimates focused on adults, we calculated adult population counts (aged ≥ 20 years) at the grid-cell level for further health impact assessments.

Land urbanization, climate zones, and geographical regions

The 1-km land urbanization data were derived from the Global Human Settlement- Settlement Model grid (GHS-SMOD) [46, 47], which classified settlement typologies via a logic of population size, population, and built-up area densities as a refinement of the “degree of urbanization” method described by the Statistical Office of the European Union (EUROSTAT). According to the GHS-SMOD, urban regions were defined as “an urban center consists of contiguous grid cells (4-connectivity cluster) with a density of at least 1,500 inhabitants per km2 of permanent land, and has at least 50,000 inhabitants and <15 km2 holes filled”. The GHS-SMOD urban region grids were classified as “urban”, and non-urban region grids were classified as “rural” in our study [47].

We divided the grid cells into Mid temperate (encompassing Mid-Temperate Arid, Mid-Temperate Semi-Arid, Mid-Temperate Semi-Humid), Warm temperate (encompassing Warm Temperate Semi-Humid), and Subtropical and tropical (encompassing Marginal Tropical Humid and North Subtropical Humid) climate zone subgroups [48]. Considering the insufficient population counts in the Plateau Temperate Semi-Arid climate zone in the CNSCKD and CCDRFS surveys, we used the exposure-response functions of the Mid temperate climate zone instead, and added estimates of the Plateau Temperate Semi-Arid into the Mid temperate climate zone.

In addition, the grid cells were also categorized into Eastern, Central, Western, or Northeastern regions, according to the administrative classification. Geographical distributions of land urbanization, climate zones, and geographical regions are shown in Fig. 2. More detailed descriptions of the input datasets are provided in the Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods.

Statistical analyses

Exposure-response functions between heat waves and advanced CKD based on individual data

To establish the exposure-response functions, we utilized individual participant data from both nationally representative surveys to integrate all available data. In the two cross-sectional studies, we separately applied generalized linear models with a logit link function (logistic regression). The dependent variable was the binary status of whether an individual participant had advanced CKD (yes/no) at the time of the survey, and the primary independent variable was defined as the annual average number of heat wave days at the geocoded residence during the year preceding the investigation for each participant. The models were adjusted for demographic, medical, and meteorologic covariates, including administrative region (categorical, rural or urban), sex (categorical, female or male), age (continuous), body mass index (BMI, continuous), income (categorical, < 500; 500–1000; or > 1000 CNY per capita month), education (categorical, primary school or lower, secondary school, high school, or college or above), smoking status (categorical, yes or no), alcohol drinking (categorical, yes or no), physical activity level (categorical, < 150 or ≥ 150 min per week), hypertension (categorical, yes or no), diabetes mellitus (categorical, yes or no), hyperuricemia (categorical, yes or no), history of CVDs (categorical, yes or no), annual ambient temperature (continuous), and relative humidity (continuous) [11, 12]. The ambient relative humidity data were generated based on the 1-km High-resolution Atmospheric Moisture Index Collection over China (HiMIC-Monthly) [49], showing a good performance, with a mean R2 of 0.996 compared to monitoring data [49]. Results are presented as the odds ratio (OR) of advanced CKD prevalence along with one-day increase in heat wave days.

Considering potential heterogeneity in the estimates, we further examined exposure-response functions stratified by urban or rural status, climate zone, and geographical region. Heterogeneity was observed across climate zones, whereas no significant differences were identified by urban or rural classification or by geographical region. Therefore, climate zone-specific exposure-response functions were adopted in the subsequent analyses. To estimate the overall exposure-response functions, the survey-specific ORs from the two independent surveys were synthesized using the random-effect inverse-variance-weighting (IVW) method to obtain pooled exposure-response functions [50]. This method assigned statistical weights to each survey inversely proportional to its variance of effect estimates, thereby ensuring that the estimate with higher precision contributed more to the pooled functions [50]. The exposure-response functions were subsequently used to estimate advanced CKD burden attributable to heat waves in the current and future.

In addition, we calculated the Gini index to quantify the inequality in hospital accessibility among CKD cases attributable to heat waves. The Gini index is based on pairwise differences in hospital accessibility, weighted by the spatial distribution of attributable cases. A higher Gini index indicates more unequal distributions of hospital accessibility [52]. In addition, we generated Lorenz curve plots to visually represent these distributions, illustrating the extent to which hospital accessibility is disproportionately distributed. The Lorenz curve plots the cumulative proportion of ACs (x-axis) against the cumulative proportion of hospital accessibility (y-axis). A Lorenz curve that deviates further from the 45-degree line of equality represents a more unequal distribution, and thus corresponds to a higher Gini index [53]. Sensitivity analyses

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of our findings. First, we provided results at the city and county levels to examine the geographical consistency of the results. Second, we employed a nationwide exposure-response function approach, instead of stratifying by climate regions, to evaluate the potential influence of spatial heterogeneity.

Uncertainty was quantified using a Monte Carlo simulation approach [26]. To consider uncertainty from both the exposure-response function and different climate model projections, we generated 500 random samples of the coefficients from the exposure-response function, assuming a normal distribution. The burden estimation was repeated for each climate model under a given SSP-RCP scenario. The final point estimate was calculated by the mean across all these runs, and the empirical 95% confidence intervals were derived from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the resulting distribution [26, 35]. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.3 and Python version 3.12. Maps were drawn using R version 4.3 and ArcGIS version 10.8. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Detailed baseline characteristics of the participants from the two national surveys are presented in Table 1. The study included a total of 176,874 individuals from the CCDRFS and 47,086 from the CNSCKD. In the CCDRFS, the majority of participants resided in rural areas (59.1%), with a mean age of 55.2 years. Hospital accessibility and inequality

Nationally, the average travel time to the nearest hospital was 15.8 min (7.6–22.3), as shown in Table 4. Rural areas had significantly longer travel times (20.4 min, 9.8–28.7) compared to urban areas (4.6 min, 2.2–6.4). Consistently, hospital accessibility was lower in rural areas (35.5, 15.8–50.9) than in urban areas (57.5, 26.6–81.3). By climate zone, the mid-temperate zone shows the longest travel time (21.7 min, 8.7–33.0), followed by the subtropical and tropical zone (16.1 min, 10.0–21.2) and the warm-temperate zone (13.4 min, 0.3–22.5). By geographical region, the Western region shows the longest travel time (21.4 min, 11.0–29.7), while the Eastern region shows the shortest travel time (11.6 min, 4.7–16.9). The national-level Gini index was 0.45, exceeding the inequality threshold of 0.4. Rural areas had greater inequality (0.50) than urban areas (0.29). By climate zone, mid- and warm-temperate zones had higher inequality (0.48; 0.49), compared to 0.44 in the subtropical and tropical zone.

City- and county-specific CKD burden, hospital accessibility, and inequality maps exhibited more detailed yet consistent geographical distributions compared to provincial maps, as shown in Additional file 1 (Figs. Sensitivity analyses using universal exposure-response functions estimated a total of 510,227 (424,187–589,202) ACs, which were slightly higher than the current estimates (491,362 [227,772–694,191]) derived from climate zone-specific exposure-response functions (Additional file 1: Table S2). Discussion

This study presents the first nationwide assessment of CKD burden attributable to heat waves and inequalities driven by hospital accessibility in China, with detailed spatial analyses and projections under future climate change SSPs-RCPs. Our findings indicate a substantial and geographically uneven burden of advanced CKD attributable to heat waves, accompanied by high levels of inequality in hospital accessibility. Rural areas, the subtropical and tropical climate zone, and Central regions experience both a higher heat-related CKD burden and poorer hospital accessibility. Meanwhile, rural, Eastern, and Western regions experience significant inequalities in hospital accessibility. Future projections suggest a growing challenge under high-emission pathways (e.g., > 2-fold increase in PAF nationally and in most regions under SSP5-8.5), with persistent inequalities in hospital accessibility. These findings highlight the need to improve hospital accessibility and reduce inequalities for heat wave-attributable advanced CKD cases requiring specialized care to prevent complications and improve outcomes.

Research evidence on the burden of heat wave-related CKD cases remains limited. Our study established the exposure-response functions based on nationally representative data, identifying significant positive associations between heat wave exposure and advanced CKD, and for the first time estimated the CKD burden attributable to heat waves in China. We estimated that about 0.5 million advanced CKD cases in 2020 were attributable to heat waves, accounting for about 30% of the total cases and about 45 cases per 100,000 adult population. Some previous studies have estimated the risk of CKD hospital admissions attributable to short-term exposure to high temperatures [13, 54]. For example, our previous study estimated that the AF due to short-term exposure to high temperatures was 5.50% for CKD hospitalizations in 261 cities in China [13]. Our estimations may also support the idea that sustained or repeated exposure to high temperatures over time may cause chronic damage to the kidneys [9, 12]. Repeated exposure to heat can lead to dehydration, increased workload on the kidneys, and chronic heat stress, all of which may increase the risk of developing CKD [9, 55]. This may emphasize the need for public health strategies that focus on long-term heat protection rather than just immediate responses to extreme heat events.

We observed significant regional disparities in the heat wave-related CKD burden, demonstrating that the burden is highly heterogeneous across different socioeconomic and geographical regions. For instance, rural areas showed both higher ACs and PAFs compared to urban areas. This might partially be attributed to the relatively higher prevalence of CKD and higher heat wave exposure inequality in rural regions [4, 56]. Similarly, subtropical and tropical climate zones and Central China experienced higher burdens than other regions. Subtropical and tropical and Central regions have longer and more intense heat wave periods [13], which may amplify related CKD burden. Moreover, there were notable spatial variations, with substantial differences across different provinces (e.g., particularly higher burden is observed in Anhui), which might be attributed to variations in the CKD disease spectrum, socioeconomic conditions, and population structures across administrative regions [35, 57]. These results suggest that national or provincial-level strategies may not be enough. More localized and targeted interventions are needed, especially for rural communities and regions with more heat wave exposures.

Another key finding is that hospital accessibility of CKD cases attributable to heat waves is limited and unequally distributed. On average, the estimated driving time to the nearest hospital was 15.8 min nationally, and CKD cases in rural areas had to drive more than 20 min on average, indicating a relatively long and inconvenient status [58]. Rural and less developed Western regions consistently showed longer driving times and lower hospital accessibility scores. These gaps may be driven by factors such as inadequate transportation networks and limited healthcare infrastructure compared to more developed urban areas [59]. Notably, rural populations not only experience a higher burden of heat wave-attributable CKD but also face greater challenges in hospital accessibility, which might delay diagnosis and worsen disease outcomes.

The national Gini index of hospital accessibility weighted by ACs was 0.45, exceeding the commonly accepted inequality threshold of 0.4 [60]. Inequality was more severe in rural areas and in several less developed provinces such as Tibet, Inner Mongolia, and Guizhou. Lorenz curve analysis also showed that a small percentage of the population enjoyed a disproportionately high share of hospital accessibility, especially in rural, Eastern, and Western regions. The findings highlight the significant inequality in hospital accessibility among CKD cases attributable to heat waves, indicating a mismatch between the distributions of healthcare resources and disease burden [61, 62]. Addressing this inequality is essential to improving the equity and effectiveness of kidney disease prevention and treatment.

Our projections under various SSPs-RCPs suggest that the burden of CKD attributable to heat waves will grow over the 21st century, particularly under high-emission scenarios like SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5 as climate change progresses. While the number of ACs may remain relatively stable due to population decline, the PAF nationally and in most regions could more than double by 2090. The variations across different SSPs-RCPs are consistent with projections of heat-related deaths in China [35, 51]. Assuming unchanged future medical resources, our results suggest that high levels of hospital accessibility inequality and regional disparities may persist across all climate change scenarios, including the more favorable scenarios (e.g., SSP1-2.6).

Notably, previous studies have shown that healthcare accessibility can modify the health risks associated with heat waves [18–20]. For instance, a study from South Korea found that timely access to medical services was linked to a reduced risk of heat wave-related mortality [20]. Our findings provide a scientific basis for the allocation of healthcare resources and the improvement of their accessibility, particularly in high-burden and underserved areas. Enhancing healthcare accessibility through investments in transportation infrastructure and the expansion of community-based services may help to reduce disease burden and promote the overall health system capacity to adapt to the growing challenges posed by climate change. These efforts may be particularly important for patients with advanced CKD, who require continuous, specialized management and are highly reliant on timely access to hospital-based nephrology services to avoid complications and maintain effective care [24, 25].

This study has several strengths. It is a nationwide assessment conducted at a high spatial resolution, providing granular insights into geographical disparities. The use of the most recent nationally representative CKD prevalence data and established exposure-response functions enhances the robustness of our estimates. Furthermore, the integration of climate projections and detailed hospital accessibility analyses provides a more comprehensive understanding of kidney health and related healthcare challenges under the climate change background.

However, there are several limitations to consider. First, in our future projections, we assumed that hospital infrastructure remains unchanged over time, which may not reflect future developments. Nevertheless, this approach enables a straightforward comparison of current and future conditions by isolating the effects of climate and population changes. Second, the study does not fully account for changes in population adaptability to climate. For example, as climate change progresses, people’s tolerance to heat waves may improve, which might potentially result in an overestimation of the projected CKD burden [57, 63]. Third, according to the study design and sampling process of the sixth CCDRFS survey, the representativeness at both provincial and urban-rural levels could not further include age- and gender-specific estimates, which limited our ability to conduct a more detailed analysis of vulnerable subpopulations. Fourth, due to the absence of nationally representative cohort data on CKD in China, the exposure-response functions based on the two cross-sectional studies may result in overestimation of the effects of long-term exposure to heat waves on CKD. This highlights the need for more prospective data to refine our estimates.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study highlights a significant association between heat wave exposure and advanced CKD, with this association corresponding to a substantial and spatially uneven burden of advanced CKD in China. Rural areas, the subtropical and tropical climate zone, and Central regions experience both a higher heat-related CKD burden and poorer hospital accessibility. Meanwhile, the burden is accompanied by significant inequalities in hospital accessibility, particularly in rural, Eastern, and Western regions, suggesting a spatial mismatch between the advanced CKD burden attributable to heat waves and hospital infrastructure. Under the current CKD prevalence and hospital infrastructure settings, the heat wave-related CKD burden is projected to increase gradually over time as climate change progresses, especially under high-emission SSPs-RCPs, and inequality in hospital accessibility may persist. Given the demand for continuous specialized medical treatment of advanced CKD, improving hospital accessibility, especially in rural and high-burden areas, is essential to advancing kidney health equity in a warming climate.
