Rank-Dependent Amphiphilicity of Coal: Insights into Combustion and Beneficiation Mechanisms
Authors: Manish Kumar Srivastava, Kunwar Vikram, Kaushal Kishor, Alok K. Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the amphiphilic nature of coal through a detailed spectroscopic analysis, employing FT-IR and XPS to examine the chemical and microstructural transformations from lignite to semianthracite rank of coals. By analyzing hydrophilic and hydrophobic functional groups across varying coal ranks, this research provides insights into how compositional differences impact coal’s surface properties, which ultimately impact the industrial utilization of these coals. Findings demonstrate that hydrophobicity increases with coal rank, correlating to rising C–H and C–C areas, while hydrophilic groups such as CO, C–O, and COO– decline gradually. These results offer a basis for developing screening criteria based on the moisture content and functional group distribution, facilitating the identification of high-quality coal with optimal combustion characteristics. It also provides a comprehensive understanding of the role of surface amphiphilic characterization for coal beneficiation processes across different ranks of coal. Additionally, the paper presents a new formula to represent the hydrophobicity of the sample based on the contribution (%) of C–C bonds.

Introduction

Coal is a combustible sedimentary rock of organic origin, composed of maceral and mineral matter. , As a key fossil fuel, it has served as a major energy source throughout the past century. , Its complex nature appears from the diverse plant materials that contribute to its formation, the specific sedimentary environments where its precursor, peat, accumulated, and the transformational processes of coalification that it undergoes over time. The end product of the aforementioned material and processes is coal, which is a composition of organic material known as maceral and inorganic materials known as mineral matter. , The various proportions of these two (maceral and mineral matter) present in coal decide its appropriate utilization. Ensuring consistency and efficiency is necessary for coal quality assessment for any industrial process where coal is being utilized. Proximate analysis is a fundamental method used to determine the basic composition of coal, including its moisture content, ash yield, volatile matter, and fixed carbon. This analysis provides quick insights into coal’s fuel quality and combustion characteristics. Micropetrography is a key method for analyzing the maceral composition, essential for identifying coal type and evaluating its suitability for various applications.

Additionally, vitrinite reflectance analysis provides insight into coal maturation, aiding in the comprehensive assessment of its utilization potential. As coal matures from peat and lignite up to anthracite, its rank increases, reflecting a steady reduction in moisture content and volatile components, while the concentration of carbon increases correspondingly. Just as the bulk properties of coal change with rank, the functional group composition, surface properties, and chemical structure of coal also change with increasing rank.

Indian Permian coals generally contain a high amount of mineral matter and primarily belong to the bituminous rank. These coals are considered the most suitable for combustion in coal-fired power plants or utilization as industrial furnaces or other uses after coal beneficiation. Moisture content affects both processes. Hence, it is vital to understand the surface characteristics of coal, which are responsible for the water retention capacity. Coal is amphiphilic in nature, containing both hydrophilic and hydrophobic functional groups on its surface. As coal matures, along with all of the chemical changes, the surface amphiphilic characteristics of coal also change. To understand these changes in depth, a comprehensive study was conducted on coal samples belonging to diverse geological and geographical backgrounds. After the determination of bulk composition, coal rank, and type, through proximate analysis, vitrinite reflectance study, and organic petrography, respectively, the coal samples were subjected to detailed surface geochemical characterization using Fourier transform-infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). XPS is a powerful, quantitative technique that provides surface-sensitive insights into coal capable of analyzing chemical bonds as small as just 10 nm. This method reveals crucial information about the material’s electronic binding energy configuration and chemical architecture. Meanwhile, FTIR spectroscopy has been employed to identify both inorganic and organic functional groups present in the coal samples. FTIR detects absorption at specific wavenumbers, a result of unique chemical interactions between the sample and infrared radiation. −

Materials and Methods

Sampling

For the present study, coal samples from different ranks (Lignite to Semianthracite) were collected from different coal-bearing horizons of India. These samples represent a range of geological ages, basins, coal ranks, and geographic regions. This diversity in the coal samples enhances our understanding of the characteristics and nature of coal. Specifically, the collection included 3 coal samples sourced from the Kalakot mines in Jammu and Kashmir, 24 samples from the Bokaro coal field in Jharkhand, 17 samples from the Jaintia Hills in Meghalaya, and 12 lignite samples from the Gurha mines in Rajasthan (Table ).

Sample Preparation

The samples were subjected to a series of preparation steps, including crushing, grinding, and sieving, to achieve specific particle sizes: −18 mesh (−1 mm) for petrographic analysis, −72 mesh for proximate analysis, and −100 mesh for spectroscopic examinations. Subsequently, particulate coal mounts were formed from the samples sized to −18 mesh by using a cold-setting binder, without the application of pressure.

Proximate Analysis

Proximate analysis, following the guidelines established by BIS (IS:1350, 1984) and utilizing a Leco Proximate Analyzer, was performed to determine the moisture content (M), ash yield (A), volatile matter (VM), and fixed carbon (FC) on an air-dry basis.

Micropetrography

Petrographic analysis was performed using a state-of-the-art petrological microscope equipped with MSP-200, fluorescent attachment, along with specialized software for model analysis (LASv4.6) and an advanced imaging system. The immersion oil (refractive index: 1.518 at 23 °C) was employed as the medium between the coal pellet and the objective lens. The classification of macerals was carried out in accordance with the ICCP standards for the identification and nomenclature of coal macerals. − The measurements of vitrinite reflectance provided crucial information regarding the rank of the coal. The analysis was conducted following the guidelines outlined in ISO-7404.

Spectroscopy

A Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy analysis was performed to identify different inorganic and organic functional groups within the coal samples. The spectra were recorded on one homogeneous sample created by mixing equal portions of all the samples belonging to each sample group. To create coal pellets, potassium bromide (KBr) was utilized. The spectral data were collected over a range of 400 to 4000 cm–1 in transmittance mode. The detailed methodology for the analysis is described in the previous literature. ,

Several researchers utilized X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to analyze the elemental composition and surface characteristics of coal samples. − XPS was chosen for surface characterization to predict the material’s amphiphilic character. XPS enables the identification of surface elements and functional groups governing amphiphilic behavior through hydrophilic and hydrophobic interactions. Spectra were acquired from a homogeneous composite sample at room temperature under vacuum and calibrated using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. Data were processed via Shirley baseline correction and Voigt peak fitting using Origin Pro 2023 Student version.

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

Infrared (IR) spectroscopy is a widely employed method for the analysis of carbon-rich materials. This technique provides valuable indirect information about bulk properties through the nature of bond formation and offers direct insights into surface properties, including functional groups attached at the boundaries of sp2/sp3 clusters. The vibrational modes associated with these functional groups usually display significantly high extinction coefficients. This characteristic makes IR spectroscopy particularly useful for studying surface properties in a range of carbon-based materials, such as activated carbon, carbon blacks, char, coal, graphite, carbon films, carbon fibers, nanocarbons, and other similar systems.

For the current investigation, the FTIR spectra were recorded to identify functional groups as a molecular system attached to coal samples, revealing that organic groups consist of aromatic and aliphatic structures, while inorganic groups include carbonate, silicate, and hydroxyl elements. The spectra of all samples are recorded in transmission mode in the range of 400 to 4000 cm–1 and are represented in Figure . The spectra of all samples show several peaks in the fingerprint region, clearly revealing that organic groups consist of aromatic and aliphatic structures. In contrast, inorganic groups, including carbonate, silicate, and hydroxyl elements, are present in all of the coal samples. This indicates diverse functional groups and mineral associations. Moreover, the X–H region (>3000 cm–1) also shows several bands in all spectra, but the relative intensities of the peaks in the X–H region with the fingerprint region were observed to be different in each spectrum (see Figure ). The assignment of peaks appearing in the spectra was done on the basis of a previous IR spectroscopic study of coal and carbon samples. Peaks in the 3600–3700 cm–1 range suggest kaolinite and –OH groups through –OH stretching. The 3010 cm–1 peak marks aromatic CH presence, while peaks between 2850 and 2950 cm–1 indicate various aliphatic groups (CH, CH2, and CH3). ,,,, Peaks at 1690–1720 cm–1 correspond to CO, ketone, aldehyde, and –COOH, and highly conjugated CO is observed in the 1650–1630 cm–1 range. Additionally, peaks around 1490, 1450, 1375, and 1000–1100 cm–1 represent aromatic rings, CH2 and CH3 bends, and aliphatic ethers, respectively. Peaks between 1032 and 1070 cm–1 confirm Si–O stretching in clay minerals, supported by XPS studies. The peaks observed at 1075, 790, and 452 cm–1 indicate the presence of SiO2, a finding that is further validated by XPS analysis. In addition, peaks at 689 and 528 cm–1 signify the presence of kaolinite mineral. ,,,, The prominent peaks in the range of 2850 to 2950 cm–1 reveal various aliphatic CH, CH2, and CH3 groups, which are also corroborated by the XPS analysis of the coal samples. Furthermore, the broad absorption between 3500 and 3000 cm–1 indicates the presence of –OH groups within the coal samples.

FT-IR spectra of semianthracite (Sample 1), bituminous (Sample 2), and sub-bituminous (Sample 3) coal and lignite (Sample 4).

The comparative FT-IR analysis supports the idea that as the rank increases, the diversity of the functional groups decreases. The FT-IR spectra of lignite samples have several peaks representing different functional groups, ranging from C–C, C–H, C–O, CO, OH, COOH, etc., whereas the semianthracite is composed primarily of C–C, C–H, C–O, etc., groups (Figure ). As C–C and C–H groups are more hydrophobic than other O-containing groups, it can be concluded that as the rank of the coal increases, the hydrophobic characteristics increase.

Peak Intensity (Height) and Integrated Intensity (Area) Analysis

The absolute peak height and peak area represent the maximum count and total count of binding energy corresponding to a particular peak, whereas a relative peak height or peak area represents the percent contribution of an element with respect to the other element (O and C only in this case). Discussion of XPS Results

Analysis of the XPS data indicates that the coal samples contain various carbon-based groups, including C–C, C–H, C–O, COO-, and CO. Previous studies (Dwivedi et al., 2023; Xia and Zhang, 2017) suggest that groups like C–O, CO, and COO- exhibit hydrophilic properties, whereas C–C and C–H groups are hydrophobic. This implies that the studied coal sample is amphiphilic in nature, containing a mixture of both hydrophilic and hydrophobic carbon compounds. Its overall tendency toward hydrophilicity or hydrophobicity will depend on which type of carbon compound is more prevalent. As coalification progresses, lignite, which is primarily composed of amorphous carbon, gradually transforms into anthracite, characterized by a graphitic carbon structure. This transformation occurs over time under increasing pressure and temperature conditions. The XPS of the C 1s level can also be utilized for the identification of sp2 and sp3 hybrid forms of carbon. Amorphous carbon tends to attract more surface functional groups compared to graphitic carbon due to several factors. It contains a mixture of sp2- and sp3-hybridized carbon atoms, with sp3 carbon being more reactive and readily bonding with hydrogen, oxygen, or nitrogen groups.

Additionally, lignite, which often contains a higher proportion of amorphous carbon, is characterized by a high density of defects, dangling bonds, and exposed edges. These features create numerous reactive sites that facilitate the attachment of functional groups. Hence, a high density of surface functional groups such as hydroxyl (−OH), carboxyl (−COOH), and other polar groups in lignite can form hydrogen bonds with water molecules, enhancing hydrophilicity. In contrast, semianthracite is predominantly composed of graphitic carbon. The surface of graphitic carbon is made up of highly ordered, sp2-hybridized carbon atoms arranged in hexagonal lattices. This highly stable structure has fewer defects, resulting in limited sites for the attachment of functional groups and minimal bonding with water and a more hydrophobic nature.

Random vitrinite reflectance (% Ro) is a key indicator of coal maturation during coalification, reflected by the coal rank. A comparison of C 1s and O 1s peaks reveals an overall increase in carbon content and a decrease in oxygen content as coalification progresses. Notably, the contribution of C–C bonds increases with a rising coal rank, suggesting that coal becomes more hydrophobic as its rank advances. Concurrently, the areas corresponding to C–O, CO, and COO– bonds diminish, indicating a decrease in hydrophilicity with increasing coal maturity.

The authors propose a formula to represent the hydrophobicity of the sample based on the contribution (%) of C–C bonds. The hydrophobicity can be calculated by using the following equation: hydrophobicity=(PAC−CPAC−C+PACO+PAC−O+PACOO−)×100 where PAC–C = peak area of C–C bonds, PACC = peak area of CO bonds PAC–O = peak area of C–O bonds, and PACOO– = peak area of COO– bonds.

A positive trend is observed, with a linear regression equation of y = 17.598x + 16 and R 2 = 0.82, indicating a strong positive correlation. Potential Industrial Applicability

Combustion

Moisture content can significantly alter the combustion properties of coal. High moisture levels in coal decrease its calorific value and may create challenges during combustion. To ensure effective burning, a surface amphiphilic characterization of coal might be helpful. Sample 1, classified as semianthracite, exhibits the highest hydrophobicity due to its abundance of C–C and C–H functional groups, followed by Samples 2 and 3. Conversely, lower-ranked coals such as lignite (Sample 4) contain a greater proportion of oxygen-rich functional groups, including hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, which enhance their hydrophilicity and their ability to hold and attract surface moisture. Ultimately, the findings indicate that as the coal rank increases, hydrophobicity also escalates, resulting in enhanced combustibility.

Coal Preparation

Flotation is a multifaceted process influenced by numerous chemical and hydrodynamic variables. However, the surface properties of the material are paramount as they provide an essential foundation for achieving effective separation. Coal flotation is a standard method used to enhance the quality of fine coal particles that result from abrasion and breakdown during mining. The floatability of coal largely depends on its degree of coalification as this process influences the nonpolar properties of its surface, which are key to successful separation. , The more hydrophobic the surface of the coal, the better its floatability. Understanding this relationship is key for efficient coal separation and beneficiation, especially in preparing clean coal for energy and industrial use. Semianthracite (Sample 1), containing more C–C and C–H functional groups, is the most hydrophobic, followed by Samples 2 and 3.

In contrast, lower-rank coals, like lignite (Sample 4), have more oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., hydroxyl and carboxyl groups) that increase the hydrophilicity and reduce the floatability behavior of coal. In conclusion, as the rank of coal increases, its hydrophobicity also rises, leading to improved floatability. Enhanced floatability is advantageous for coal beneficiation, facilitating more effective separation and processing.
