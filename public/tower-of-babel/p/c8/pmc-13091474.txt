Reply to “Stimulation selectivity in transcranial motor evoked potentials for monitoring during surgery for supratentorial lesions”
Authors: Fares Komboz, Tammam Abboud
Published: 2026

We thank the authors for their interest in our article, “Stimulation selectivity in transcranial motor evoked potentials for monitoring during surgery for supratentorial lesions.” Their letter raises several methodological and physiological considerations, and we welcome the opportunity to clarify the scope and interpretation of our findings. Our study prospectively compared three TES montages and found that C3/4→Cz showed the greatest selectivity in eliciting contralateral responses before ipsilateral responses emerged (Abboud et al., 2026).

First, the suggestion that electrode positions may have been substantially altered by craniotomy placement, Mayfield fixation, or sterile draping does not reflect our study protocol. As described in the Methods, corkscrew electrodes were installed on the scalp according to the international 10–20 system at C1, C2, C3, C4, and Cz, and TES testing was performed before skin incision. Accordingly, the montage comparison was conducted under standardized preincision conditions, before the operative stage at which surgical exposure might meaningfully affect electrode positioning. Thus, while minor practical variation is inherent to any clinical setup, the protocol itself did not rely on montage placement modified by the craniotomy or surgical corridor (Abboud et al., 2026).

Second, the concern regarding sequential testing and possible facilitation effects deserves acknowledgment but should not be overstated. Repeated TES may influence TcMEP response characteristics; however, the facilitation literature primarily addresses augmentation of response amplitude, whereas our study examined stimulation threshold. These are related but not interchangeable physiological endpoints. To our knowledge, there is no established evidence-based intertrial interval that has been shown to abolish facilitation effects in intraoperative threshold testing, and current guideline and review literature do not define such a preferred interval (Dulfer et al., 2023, Legatt et al., 2016). The montage testing in our study was performed under a standardized protocol at a frequency consistent with routine intraoperative practice. Accordingly, while a carryover effect cannot be excluded completely, the concern remains theoretical in the present context and should not be interpreted as evidence of a systematic bias in the principal comparative threshold finding (Dulfer et al., 2023, Legatt et al., 2016). Moreover, although Train-of-Four monitoring was not performed and as acknowledged in the article, a residual neuromuscular blockade therefore cannot be excluded completely, any such effect would be expected to influence absolute thresholds across all tested configurations within the same patient rather than selectively favor one montage over another. Accordingly, residual neuromuscular blockade is more appropriately regarded as a potential source of overall threshold variability than as a likely explanation for the principal between-montage differences observed in our study.

Third, with regard to ipsilateral responses, we agree that their precise physiological origin cannot be reduced to a single mechanism. In our article, the interpretation that ipsilateral responses may reflect recruitment of deeper corticospinal pathways at higher stimulation intensities was intended as a plausible physiological explanation, not as definitive proof of anatomical origin (Abboud et al., 2026). Alternative mechanisms, including bilateral cortical activation, cathodal excitation, and transcallosal recruitment, are also possible and have been discussed in the literature (Holdefer et al., 2006, Wilkinson et al., 2023). This complexity, however, does not undermine the principal observation of our study: that the tested montages differed in the degree to which contralateral responses could be elicited before ipsilateral responses appeared (Abboud et al., 2026).

We also agree that recordings limited to the abductor pollicis brevis provide a focused rather than comprehensive representation of corticospinal activation and do not directly address proximal or lower-extremity pathways. However, this reflects the scope of the study, which was designed to compare montage selectivity using a standardized threshold endpoint. While tumor-related displacement or infiltration may contribute to overall threshold variability, the montage comparison was performed within the same patients across all tested configurations, and stimulation values did not differ significantly between the affected and non-affected hemispheres for any montage. Accordingly, lesion-related heterogeneity does not provide an obvious explanation for the consistent montage-dependent differences observed in this within-subject analysis.

Finally, we agree that the clinical implications of the selectivity ratio should not be overstated. Our study was designed to compare stimulation thresholds and selectivity characteristics across montages, not to determine superiority with respect to sensitivity, specificity, or prediction of postoperative motor deficits (Abboud et al., 2026). These questions require separate outcome-based investigation. Within the scope of the present study, however, the data support C3/4 → Cz as the most selective of the tested configurations under standardized preincision conditions (Abboud et al., 2026).

In summary, we appreciate the authors’ comments and agree that electrode localization, stimulation sequencing, and physiological interpretation warrant further study. At the same time, we believe the main conclusion of our article remains unchanged: among the tested configurations, C3/4 → Cz demonstrated the highest selectivity ratio under the conditions studied (Abboud et al., 2026).
