Barriers to primary health care: perspectives of marginalized Roma women and healthcare professionals
Authors: Jana Marosnikova Plavnicka, Zuzana Dankulincova Veselska, Daniela Filakovska Bobakova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMarginalized Roma communities (MRCs) in Slovakia experience longstanding exclusion from essential services, including healthcare. Roma women, in particular, face compounded vulnerabilities that contribute to unequal access and poorer health outcomes. Despite increasing attention to these issues, a deeper understanding of the lived experiences that shape healthcare access in MRCs remains necessary.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews with 13 Roma mothers living in MRCs and 13 professionals working in healthcare, public health, or policy, including six of Roma origin. Data were analyzed using consensual qualitative research and thematic analysis to identify significant access barriers.ResultsRoma women face multiple, often interconnected barriers to accessing healthcare, many of which are rooted in longstanding structural inequalities. These include distrust of the health system stemming from prior discrimination, difficulties in understanding health-related information and navigating the system, and financial hardship. On the side of healthcare providers, barriers involve shortages in the healthcare workforce, poor care coordination, and discriminatory attitudes.ConclusionsImproving access to healthcare for Roma women requires a comprehensive, multi-level strategy. Efforts should focus on building trust, improving communication, addressing financial and systemic obstacles, and investing in culturally sensitive primary care. Health promotion assistants play a crucial role in bridging the gaps between communities and healthcare providers. Culturally sensitive healthcare interventions and inclusive policies are essential to reducing health disparities and promoting equitable access.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13482-2.Keywords: Marginalized Roma communities, Roma women, Healthcare access, Discrimination, Health literacy, Financial vulnerability, Healthcare workforce, Responsiveness of healthcare system

Introduction

The Roma represent one of the largest disadvantaged populations in Europe, experiencing persistent structural inequalities across multiple sectors of society [1]. A significant factor contributing to their marginalization is anti-Roma racism, which remains deeply embedded in social and institutional structures [2]. Discriminatory practices continue to manifest in education, employment, housing, healthcare, and access to public and private services, further exacerbating their social exclusion [1, 3–6]. Reliable and disaggregated data on health and health inequalities between the Roma and majority population remain scarce, complicating efforts to assess disparities accurately [7]. Given that unmet needs among Roma are an important indicator of access to healthcare and its quality, it is essential to focus on identifying them [8]. In Slovakia, the Roma population is estimated at approximately 440,000, with more than half residing in marginalized Roma communities (MRCs). These communities, often situated on the outskirts of municipalities or entirely outside their boundaries, are characterized by spatial and social segregation, resulting in severe material and financial hardship [9].

Roma in Europe, especially those residing in MRCs, encounter substantial challenges in accessing healthcare, with obstacles such as discrimination in medical facilities, the significant distances between settlements and healthcare centers [10], financial constraints limiting transportation, and communication difficulties with medical professionals [7]. Additional barriers such as language difficulties, limited health literacy, bureaucratic hurdles in transferring medical services, and deep-seated mistrust toward healthcare providers further exacerbate the issue [11]. A major challenge in accessing care is the lack of healthcare professionals with cultural competence and the insufficient responsiveness of healthcare systems [12]. Research indicates that both Roma individuals and healthcare professionals face challenges that are structural, economic, cognitive, or psychological in nature [13]. In Slovakia, anti-Roma racism fuels mutual mistrust between Roma and healthcare providers, often leading to delayed care and misinterpreted behaviors as non-compliance [14]. Low health literacy further limits understanding of prevention and timely treatment [15, 16], while physical distance remains a barrier [17].

Roma women, as a distinct subpopulation, encounter disproportionately greater barriers in accessing healthcare services. This position makes them particularly vulnerable to receiving lower-quality healthcare [18]. Moreover, in MRCs, it is overwhelmingly women who accompany children to healthcare appointments and navigate health-related responsibilities within the household. In Slovakia, Roma women continue to face discrimination, neglect, and violations of dignity in reproductive healthcare, including during childbirth [19]. Although healthcare use increases during the childbearing years, it declines afterwards and resurfaces only with the onset of chronic illness in later life [20]. Gender roles and caregiving duties further limit their engagement with healthcare services [21]. Structural and psychosocial barriers, such as mistrust of providers, low health literacy, poverty, inadequate infrastructure, distance, lack of transportation or childcare, and financial hardship, further hinder timely access to care [22].

Therefore, the aim of this study is to explore and identify the key barriers to primary healthcare access experienced and perceived by marginalized Roma women, with the goal of informing targeted interventions and policy strategies. Unlike most of the previous studies, this research incorporates the perspectives of both Roma women and healthcare professionals.

Methods

Given the complex, deeply rooted, and multidimensional nature of the barriers faced by marginalized Roma women, a qualitative research design was deemed the most appropriate methodological choice. This design allows for an in-depth exploration of both Roma women’s and healthcare professionals’ perspectives, providing rich, contextualized insights that would not be captured through quantitative methods alone [23]. To ensure conceptual clarity and consistency in interpretation, we define key terms used in this study in accordance with established frameworks and widely recognized public health literature. Access to healthcare is understood in line with Levesque, Harris, and Russell [24] as the opportunity to identify healthcare needs, seek services, access healthcare resources, obtain or utilize healthcare services, and be offered services appropriate to one’s care needs. Barriers to access refer to individual, structural, systemic, or institutional factors that hinder people from receiving timely and appropriate healthcare. These may include logistical, financial, linguistic, cultural, administrative, or discriminatory obstacles [24, 25]. Primary healthcare is the first point of contact for individuals, families, and communities within the healthcare system, providing essential, accessible, and coordinated services focused on prevention, early detection, treatment of common conditions, and person-centered care across the life course [26]. Marginalized Roma communities (MRCs) refer to geographically and socially excluded groups characterized by concentrated poverty, limited infrastructure, high unemployment, and longstanding systemic discrimination [27].

The recruitment of Roma community members was guided by the primary focus of the RIVER-EU study, which aimed to explore barriers to HPV (human papillomavirus) vaccination in children eligible for the national free-of-charge vaccination program [28]. This study aims to provide insights into this topic through secondary data analysis. Participants were selected among parents, predominantly mothers, who are typically the primary caregivers responsible not only for their children’s healthcare but often for the health of the entire family. As a result, the sample includes only mothers with children and does not cover perspectives of women without caregiving responsibilities or of men, whose involvement in child health and preventive care is generally limited in this context.

Procedure

The interview guides were developed for the RIVER-EU study, focusing on HPV vaccination [28]. The initial part of the interview covered topics related to general access to primary healthcare. Two topic guides were created: one for community members (see Supplementary Material 1) and one for healthcare providers (see Supplementary Material 2).

Semi-structured interviews were conducted in the Slovak language by two of the authors, who had no prior relationship with the participants, and lasted approximately from 30 min to two hours. Participants were given the freedom to select the location for the interview. Roma mothers were interviewed at community centers or in the comfort of their homes about their experiences in seeking and using healthcare, their trust in healthcare providers, and the obstacles they encountered in accessing consistent medical care for themselves and their children. Health promotion assistants (also known as Roma health mediators) assisted in building rapport and facilitating communication. The researchers were aware of the power asymmetries and took care to approach the interviews with respect, openness, and cultural sensitivity. This included creating a safe, non-judgmental space for sharing personal experiences.

Healthcare providers were interviewed both in person and online via Teams or Zoom platform according to their preference. In-person interviews took place at the research department, community centers, or respondents’ offices. Healthcare providers were asked to discuss the specific challenges faced by patients from MRCs, the resources available to deliver appropriate care, and the presence of outreach or collaborative services.

Analyses

The data were analyzed using a combination of Consensual Qualitative Research (CQR) methodology [29] and thematic analysis [30]. CQR was chosen for its focus on consensus-building and systematic coding among researchers, which strengthens analytical rigor and minimizes individual bias.

Analyses were performed by a team of three female researchers with backgrounds in health psychology, public health, and social work, and varying levels of experience in conducting qualitative research. Before starting the analysis, the team members discussed their personal backgrounds, professional assumptions, and potential biases that could influence their interpretations. This reflexive process aimed to make their subjectivities explicit and support a more balanced and transparent analysis. The interview transcripts were reviewed, and each researcher independently coded the transcripts, followed by team discussions to reach consensus on the coding framework and interpretation of themes. In case of disagreement, discussions were held until a consensus was reached. The coding and analysis process was facilitated by MAXQDA software.

Following the initial coding phase, a thematic analysis was conducted. Codes pertaining to barriers to accessing primary health care were grouped into subthemes based on their thematic content. These subthemes were then synthesized to identify overarching concepts, culminating in the creation of thematic categories. While the initial coding was inductive and not guided by a predefined theory, the interpretation of the findings was informed by the Levesque, Harris, and Russell [24] conceptual framework of access to healthcare. This model enabled a comprehensive understanding of how structural and individual-level factors interact to influence healthcare access in underserved populations. Furthermore, our approach was shaped by existing literature on Roma health inequities and ethnic discrimination in healthcare, which helped situate the identified barriers within broader social and institutional contexts.

Ethical considerations

All procedures were designed and implemented in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national regulations. The study was approved by the relevant ethics committees (see Declarations). All participants provided informed consent after receiving written information about the study. For Roma mothers, the consent form was also explained verbally to ensure full understanding, especially in cases of limited literacy. Participation was voluntary, with the right to withdraw at any time. Interviews were conducted with no additional individuals present in the room whenever feasible. Anonymity and confidentiality were ensured by using coded identifiers and securely storing all data. No personal or identifying information appears in the results.

Results

The sample consisted of two groups of respondents with different perceptions of the topic under study. We interviewed 13 mothers and 13 professionals with diverse expertise at various levels of the professional hierarchy, including healthcare (N = 4), health mediation (N = 7), public health (N = 1), and policy (N = 1). All mothers were residents of MRCs. Also, six respondents from the group of professionals were of Roma origin. The basic characteristics of the sample are presented in Table 1.

This section presents findings related to barriers in primary health care from the perspectives of both Roma women and healthcare providers. Reported barriers are not exclusive to Roma women. Rather, we report on barriers as described from the perspective of Roma women, reflecting their lived experiences when seeking healthcare for themselves or their family members. These include both their direct encounters with the healthcare system and observations made in their role as primary caregivers within their households. Barriers on the side of healthcare users

Education and health literacy

Roma women did not directly reflect the theme of low education and low functional and health literacy. However, their responses suggest that they often do not understand health-related information provided by doctors and are less familiar with the healthcare system. These difficulties were not explicitly named by the women themselves as issues of low literacy or education, but were described through examples of confusion or lack of understanding.

Some words are sometimes hard for me to understand because they’re said differently — they sound like foreign words. I don’t know them and don’t know what they mean.

(mother from an MRC)

Low levels of education and health literacy were more explicitly mentioned by healthcare professionals, who also noted that such limitations contribute to difficulties in assessing the severity of health problems, navigating unfamiliar locations, or reaching health facilities, especially when lacking private transport or struggling with public transit.

So, we are rather referring to the fact that they (health promotion assistants) have a problem getting them to the doctor when the doctor needs them for a preventive checkup. And then, when it comes to acute examinations, getting them to the clinic when they need to. […] they may not come with children who should be seen [by the doctor]. They have a bad estimate of when they should go to the doctor.

(public health authority)

Where is she supposed to leave her seven children when she needs to travel 40 km or more to get to the nearest workplace? What is she supposed to do with them, and where is she supposed to get the money? And then there’s the fact that she has to get off at a station in a town she’s never been to in her life, figure out where she is, know how to get on the right train or bus — and she has no idea how any of that works. She doesn’t even know where to buy a ticket. These are all completely legitimate concerns.

(public health authority)

Low education and low health literacy are also supported by the language barrier. The language barrier was mentioned by both Roma women and healthcare professionals. Roma women themselves did not explicitly identify the language barrier, but their responses suggest that complex Slovak terminology and technical language can be difficult to understand.

Some women can’t understand, for example, a doctor saying this, this, this in Slovak, and some women just look at him because they can’t communicate and can’t understand him, but if it was in Romani language […] Some women understand the Slovak language, but it would be good if somebody could explain it to them in Romani.

(mother from an MRC)

Competing priorities and health deprioritization

According to healthcare professionals, health is often not perceived as a priority in MRCs. Some mothers reported focusing on more immediate concerns and seeking care only when problems become serious. In the context of MRCs, where daily challenges such as food insecurity, poor housing, and financial instability dominate, this focus is understandable. Healthcare professionals also noted a low uptake of preventive checkups, which are often not seen as important or necessary.

With our Roma, who are the worst off, those social classes that are the worst off, there is only one problem: these people lack everything, and therefore, health is put in the last, very last place and is only addressed when they are sick. […] It is only addressed when they have enough food, if they have enough money, if they have enough warmth, if they have enough clothing, and if they have a good roof over their heads.

(health promotion assistant)

Relationship with doctors

Trust in healthcare was a recurring theme across most interviews. Both Roma mothers, Health promotion assistants, and public health experts described mistrust toward doctors, often rooted in experiences of discrimination, poor-quality care, and disrespectful treatment. Healthcare providers themselves did not mention these issues. Mistrust manifested as reluctance to seek medical help, negative perceptions of doctors, and dissatisfaction with the care received. Such experiences sometimes escalated into conflicts. In addition, several respondents expressed a lack of confidence in the domestic healthcare system as a whole. Additionally, healthcare professionals noted that family patterns — such as a lack of emphasis on regular care or past negative experiences — contribute to reduced healthcare utilization and may be passed on to younger generations.

People from the socially weaker strata have very bad experiences with doctors because those doctors are sometimes literally arrogant; they are not at all interested in the person who has a problem; they also have bad experiences with nurses who beat them back very quickly, forcefully, and literally brutally.

(health promotion assistant)

This mistrust and avoidance of healthcare also contribute to non-adherence – a pattern of behavior that healthcare professionals have described. Preventive checkups are often underutilized; some patients fail to attend scheduled visits, and others do not follow doctors’ recommendations. These forms of non-adherence are linked to earlier negative experiences and communication problems.

There are also those who really need the help; the doctor is willing to help them, but they don’t want it. […] They have bad experiences with doctors. If the approach towards them was so unfair, inhuman, hm, then they have a problem that they don’t have a specialist doctor, or they don’t follow what the doctor recommended.

(health promotion assistant)

Financial vulnerability

The absence of personal documentation and health insurance constitutes a barrier rooted in the circumstances and vulnerabilities of the healthcare users. It often results from a combination of poverty, unstable housing, and exclusion from the formal labor market, which makes it difficult for individuals to obtain or maintain the necessary documents. Complex administrative procedures further exacerbate these problems. Without valid insurance, people fear unreimbursed care and cannot afford postponed treatment.

But you know how it often is with them and insurance cards. Sometimes the whole family shares just one card.

(policymaker)

How can you fix your teeth if you don’t have money? You can’t. So people suffer for half a year, even three quarters of a year, with painful teeth, not knowing what to do. Then various infections develop, and people literally die from illnesses that are otherwise banal.

(health promotion assistant)

High financial barriers mean that people from MRCs are unable to cover even relatively small expenses related to travel to the city and additional payments for medicines or hygiene needs, and these financial constraints are closely intertwined with spatial and infrastructural challenges. Physical distance from health facilities and cities, combined with poor mobility, significantly increases the difficulty of accessing health facilities, a barrier further exacerbated by poverty, inadequate public transportation links, and the scarcity of doctors and clinics in the region.

When they live on the outskirts or far from the clinic they’re assigned to, and it’s 10 km away, they won’t take a bus into town with a child if they don’t have a car. And if they don’t have money — or if they have to spend that money on something else — they just won’t go.

(gynecologist)

When my children were sick, and I didn’t have money, I had to go around asking family to help me pay for the medicine. That happened to me often, and it still happens even now.

(mother from an MRC)

Barriers on the side of healthcare system

Unresponsiveness and passivity of the healthcare system

This theme, including all its subcategories, was reflected exclusively by public health professional, who critically assessed the system’s inability to respond to the specific needs of marginalized patients. One of the primary issues is the way healthcare is organized, which often fails to consider the individual needs of patients, is slow to respond, and is inadequate or unprepared to adapt to patients’ needs. Moreover, the lack of mechanisms to address the abovementioned financial vulnerability of patients, closely intertwined with spatial and infrastructural challenges and poor mobility, significantly increases the difficulty of accessing healthcare.

On the system side, there is a deep conviction that it is enough if the system is technically built. That is, there will be some network of buildings, which is filled with clinics, there are doctors in those clinics, those doctors have opening hours, those opening hours are posted on the doors, and you just have to come there, and things will happen. […] The system is built, and that seems to be the end of it. And everything else is already on the side of the recipient of that “service” because, on the provider side, it is as if the matter is settled. If the prevailing norm is that healthcare should primarily be provided in the clinic, then the burden of overcoming the barrier lies entirely on the person who is expected to show up.

(public health authority)

Healthcare capacity and continuity

Were concerns shared by both healthcare professionals and respondents from MRCs, who described similar limitations in the healthcare system, especially in primary care. A key issue was insufficient capacity, due in part to the ageing workforce, which exacerbates the shortage of healthcare personnel and high patient loads and limits the ability to provide individualized care. Healthcare professionals have highlighted that administrative burdens reduce the time available for patients and increase stress, thereby contributing to burnout. Together, these factors create serious barriers to the delivery of quality healthcare, reducing efficiency and patient satisfaction. Respondents from MRCs and professionals alike noted that limited doctor availability and long waiting times make access more difficult. For healthcare users, waiting in overcrowded waiting rooms was described as a barrier to care. Professionals also pointed out the lack of outreach services, which limits the system’s ability to respond to patients’ needs in their home settings. Finally, both groups identified problems with the lack of continuity in ambulance handovers when doctors change, leading to fragmented care and uncertainty about patients’ health needs.

Perhaps it’s also because they’re really overworked, have large patient lists, and many of them are of retirement age. They’re just doing their best to manage the basics. With the current age structure, older pediatricians may no longer have the time or energy to keep up with new knowledge, and some of them don’t even run outreach or community clinics. If we had more – and younger – pediatricians, the outcomes in this area would probably be much better.

(gynecologist)

We don’t have it like others, a regular doctor where we can go to the office, sit down, and talk about how the children are doing. For us it’s always rushed, because there’s no doctor in our village, so we have to go to [name of the town district]… But even there, she doesn’t lift a finger beyond just checking what’s wrong. She has no time to communicate with us. She always says she has too many patients.

(mother from an MRC)

Position of the primary care physician

The analysis revealed several systemic issues affecting physicians’ roles, effectiveness, and ability to deliver quality care. Both public health professionals and healthcare providers pointed to structural limitations in primary care. They emphasized the restricted competencies of general practitioners, which undermine their capacity to coordinate care. Additionally, limited support for young doctors — who often face demanding conditions without sufficient mentoring — hinders their ability to adapt to practice demands. A lack of financial incentives, especially for pediatricians, further impacts motivation and job satisfaction.

When I think about what these patients might be facing… It’s the fact that their doctor often functions more like a dispatcher — someone who can’t prescribe specialized medication, can’t offer them any specific tests, and doesn’t have access to basic diagnostic tools like X-rays or ultrasounds. They lack the essential equipment that could save patients’ time and money and would also increase the doctor’s competence. And if they don’t have that, their role is reduced to just referring people elsewhere. I believe that helps no one — and it certainly harms marginalized groups. For them, this is often one of the main barriers to even seeking care in the first place.

(public health authority)

Prejudice and racism

The attitudes and behaviors of primary care providers directly impact the quality of care. Respondents from MRCs, public health expert, and health promotion assistants described various forms of prejudice and unequal treatment in routine practice. Reports included impersonal or inhumane treatment, neglect, rejection, or even unintentional removal from patient registers—experiences that erode trust in the system. Communication was another concern: doctors were said to dismiss patients’ views, fail to explain decisions clearly, and use language that left patients confused or ignored. Additionally, participants described experiences of prejudice, racism, insolvency bias, and double standards. A lack of empathy or interpersonal skills among some doctors and nurses was also observed to diminish care quality and harm patient-provider relationships.

People who are extremely biased — those with strong personal barriers — should simply never be allowed to work with this community, for everyone’s sake. Because I believe that once someone finishes medical school at 25, their personality is already formed. You can’t change that. And in my opinion, we can torture ourselves trying to think of ways to remove barriers, but it won’t work, because some of these people should never have entered the profession in the first place.

(public health authority)

It shouldn’t be like: this is a white child, and this is a Roma child. They should treat all children equally and work with them together, whether they’re white or Roma. That’s all I’d want — that they show respect for our children too.

(mother from an MRC)

The complexity of working in MRCs

Healthcare professionals, as well as public health expert described a range of challenges related to the complexity of providing care in MRCs. Doctors often face uncomfortable situations and safety concerns that impact their ability to deliver quality care. Providing basic healthcare in MRCs is overwhelming for care providers due to poor working conditions and an overload of responsibilities. Many doctors do not show interest in working in these communities, resulting in a reluctance to work directly in the MRC. In addition, care providers often do not speak up about problems in the delivery of care, which reduces the likelihood of finding solutions and improving conditions in these areas.

He’s probably the only doctor who actually stayed there [catchment area of MRC]. So I don’t need to tell you, if you’ve spoken with him, you probably know what it takes to do this work. No one else stayed — not the dentist, not the general practitioner, not the gynecologist. And there was a fully equipped health center set up there, with everything ready. So apparently, no one else was willing or motivated enough to do this kind of work with those people.

(gynecologist)

Discussion

Our findings identified two main categories of barriers: (1) barriers on the side of healthcare users, including subcategories such as education and health literacy, competing priorities, health deprioritization, relationship with doctors, and financial vulnerability; and (2) barriers on the side of the healthcare system, with subcategories such as unresponsiveness and passivity of the healthcare system, healthcare capacity and continuity, the position of the primary care physician, prejudice and racism, and the complexity of working in MRCs. While these categories reflect the perspectives of both patients and providers, several of the identified barriers are closely interconnected and mutually reinforcing. Therefore, while the results section followed this structure consistent with the conceptual model of healthcare access by Levesque, Harris, and Russell [24], in the discussion, we re-examine the barriers through a complementary framework that groups them into three interrelated levels: individual, systemic, and interactional. This approach enables us to capture the dynamic interplay between personal experiences, institutional factors, and relational processes within the healthcare setting more effectively.

One of the key barriers, highlighted in participant interviews and explicitly emphasized by healthcare professionals, is low educational attainment and health literacy. Although Roma women did not explicitly articulate these as barriers, their statements reveal significant difficulties in understanding medical explanations and navigating the healthcare system. In this context, health literacy refers to an individual’s ability to access, understand, evaluate, and use health-related information to make informed decisions about health and healthcare. This aligns with Sørensen, who describes low health literacy and uncertainty in navigating the health system as a cycle that exacerbates social exclusion and undermines the right to health and well-being [31]. This phenomenon is supported by the findings of other studies that point to the low level of health awareness among Roma and its impact on their health behavior [32, 33]. Persistent educational disparities, shaped by broader social and policy-related inequalities, are among the major factors affecting health literacy among MRCs [34]. Thus, improving access to quality education represents a crucial opportunity for strengthening health literacy in these communities.

An important aspect that emerged in our research based on participants’ accounts is that in the context of daily survival challenges, preventive healthcare often becomes deprioritized for marginalized Roma women, despite its long-term importance, and visits to the doctor are often made only in acute cases. This also relates to non-adherence, which in our study refers to situations where patients may not follow recommendations, attend appointments, adhere to treatment plans, or participate in preventive care. It is essential to recognize that such behavior does not stem from individual disinterest, but rather from structural constraints, such as poverty, housing insecurity, and social exclusion, that limit people’s ability to prioritize their health and reflect user-side manifestations of a deeper systemic problem. This phenomenon aligns with findings that, despite reporting more barriers, Roma tend to use some segments of healthcare, particularly emergency care, more frequently than the majority population [32, 33]. However, basic preventive care, especially for children, is often neglected [33], and overall healthcare utilization remains significantly lower among people in MRCs—up to 36% less compared to the general Slovak population [7]. Structural barriers, including limited access to education, further constrain health-related knowledge and decision-making [35].

On the system level, participants (particularly healthcare professionals) identified key barriers such as staff shortages, high workloads among physicians, and limited care coordination competencies of GPs. The shortage of healthcare workers is a long-term problem in Slovakia [36]. These systemic factors lead to overloaded outpatient clinics, long waiting times and reduced quality of care. The inadequate network of primary health care providers, also highlighted by health professionals in our research, was identified as a problem in the Health Expenditure Analysis [37], which revealed that primary care is underfunded and underdeveloped at the expense of specialized care. Underfunding of primary health care and limited capacity put pressure on outpatient departments, which can limit access to healthcare. This universal problem is more pronounced in rural areas and can indirectly worsen the situation of MRCs [37]. While it is true that many of the system-level barriers identified, such as time constraints, poor coordination, and limited accessibility, affect all users of the healthcare system, their impact is not evenly distributed. Structural weaknesses and a lack of responsiveness within healthcare systems disproportionately burden the most vulnerable [38], making these barriers particularly consequential for marginalized Roma women, who are less equipped to navigate or compensate for such shortcomings.

Distrust of doctors was consistently described by Roma women and health promotion assistants as a central barrier, rooted in prior experiences of mistreatment and discrimination. In our analysis, prejudice and racism encompass both interpersonal discrimination (such as disrespectful or hostile behavior) and structural bias (such as differential treatment or lower-quality care based on ethnicity or perceived social status). Such accounts illustrate the persistence of discriminatory practices and the erosion of trust, which are well-documented in previous studies [32, 39–42]. For example, Belák et al. [32] reported that one-third of households in MRCs experienced discrimination when seeking medical care. Medical assistance is frequently refused or delayed solely due to their address [39]. Segregated waiting rooms, overt racism or hostile behavior of healthcare professionals [40] combined with the language barrier of healthcare users contribute to damaged relationships between patients and healthcare providers, discourage healthcare-seeking behavior and adherence, reduce the effectiveness of the healthcare services provided, and deepen health inequalities [41, 42].

Given the context described above, health promotion assistants appear to be a key bridge between healthcare providers and MRCs. Their presence fosters trust and improves communication. Health promotion assistants significantly contribute to equipping Roma communities with valid health information and enabling Roma women to make informed decisions about their health [43] and navigate the healthcare system [44]. These workers serve as intermediaries between health professionals and Roma communities, helping to build trust, increase health literacy, and overcome cultural barriers to healthcare provision [45]. Their presence in healthcare facilities contributes to reducing the language barrier, as the use of interpreters improves access to healthcare [46] and helps eliminate discriminatory practices while creating a more inclusive environment for patients from MRCs.

Although there are various reasons for unmet health needs, such as distance, waiting lists, and limited human resources [47], financial constraints are one of the main barriers to undergoing medical examinations and treatment [8, 48]. In our study, financial vulnerability refers to the everyday economic hardship that prevents individuals from covering both direct and indirect healthcare-related costs, such as transport to a clinic, co-payments for medications, or basic hygiene needs. Financial barriers play an exclusionary role in access to health care, with their impact being particularly pronounced for Roma women. Women without health insurance cannot access healthcare on time to undergo medical screening [49], further exacerbating their vulnerability. The findings of our research showed that respondents face difficulties with reimbursement of healthcare fees, the cost of transportation to the doctor, and concerns about unreimbursed medical procedures. Access to healthcare is formed by the intersection of beneficiaries’ financial constraints and the health system’s ability to provide affordable services [24].

Our findings align with the conceptual model of healthcare access proposed by Levesque, Harris, and Russell [24], which emphasizes that access is shaped by the dynamic interaction between the health system’s characteristics (approachability, availability, affordability, acceptability, and appropriateness) and users’ abilities to perceive, seek, reach, pay for, and engage with healthcare. Barriers reported in our study, such as difficulties navigating the system, financial constraints, and distrust rooted in prior discrimination, map onto several of these dimensions, especially users’ ability to perceive and seek care, and the system’s acceptability and appropriateness.

Moreover, our findings are in line with the theoretically informed framework developed by Belák et al. [14], which articulates four ethnographically grounded mechanisms that contribute to poor health outcomes in MRCs: (1) social disintegration, (2) ethnically framed institutional neglect, (3) behavioral adaptations to uncertainty, and (4) reduced autonomy in managing health and life circumstances. Authors also pointed out that what we identified as competing priorities and health deprioritization can be understood through the lens of constrained autonomy and behavioral adaptations to institutional unpredictability. Similarly, system-level shortcomings, such as gaps in the primary care network and limited responsiveness, may contribute to a pattern of institutional unresponsiveness that inadequately addresses the specific needs of Roma communities. Our findings reflect a perceived resistance of the healthcare system to change, marked by a lack of mechanisms to address patient vulnerability and a tendency to place the burden of overcoming access barriers on individuals. System unresponsiveness refers to its inability or unwillingness to adapt to the needs of vulnerable groups, including rigid structures, limited outreach, inadequate support, and inflexible administrative or financial procedures.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in its direct engagement with women from MRCs, a group that remains underrepresented in health research [50] despite a growing number of studies. Capturing perspectives from within MRCs remains methodologically and practically challenging, which adds to the value of this work. The study thereby contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of healthcare utilization among minority populations. The reliability of the results, their interpretation, and the elimination of the subjectivity of the researchers were achieved by following the principles of the consensual qualitative research methodology [29]. By shedding light on the healthcare disparities experienced by marginalized Roma women, the survey has the potential to raise awareness and advocate for improved healthcare access and equity for this population.

A limitation of the research is that, apart from covering the perspectives of healthcare professionals, it focuses predominantly on the experiences of healthcare utilization from the perspective of Roma mothers residing in a specific region of Slovakia. While this focus was appropriate given the primary aim of the broader RIVER-EU project to investigate HPV vaccination in eligible children, it may have excluded important perspectives. Specifically, the exclusion of men and of women without caregiving responsibilities reflects the gendered nature of health-seeking in MRCs, where women typically bear the primary responsibility for accessing care for their families. However, this focus may have led to the underrepresentation of male experiences or broader community views on healthcare access. An additional constraint of this research is its execution within MRCs, which are characterized by significant heterogeneity. Therefore, caution is advised when generalizing the findings. The chosen region represents the area with the most densely populated Roma community. Nevertheless, in other regions with varying demographic compositions and healthcare provisions, disparities in accessing primary healthcare services may manifest to varying extents.

Implications and conclusions

The findings of this study have several critical implications for healthcare practice, policy, and future research. First, improving health literacy within MRCs must become a strategic priority, as higher levels of health literacy provide clear benefits to society, while lower levels are a risk factor for poor health and lead to higher healthcare costs [34]. Targeted, culturally sensitive, linguistically accessible, and community-based educational programs are needed to enhance health knowledge and system navigation. Healthcare professionals also require training in communication techniques tailored to patients with low health literacy.

Second, building trust between MRCs and healthcare providers is essential. Open dialogue helps ensure that patients feel heard and their needs are addressed, especially given the lasting impact of discrimination on health-seeking behavior. Key steps include anti-discrimination training, the development of structural competence for healthcare professionals, and integrating health promotion assistants as trusted liaisons who bridge communication, build trust, and mediate conflicts.

Third, investments should be made to expand the primary care workforce, especially in underserved regions. Policies should include incentives for young doctors to practice in rural and marginalized areas, as well as support structures for professional development and supervision.

Finally, financial and infrastructural barriers must be systematically addressed. Coverage of transportation costs, free preventive services, and streamlined documentation processes could significantly reduce barriers to access. Broader social policies aimed at reducing poverty and improving access to housing and education will also have a positive impact on health outcomes. To improve accountability and healthcare delivery, it is essential to eliminate discrimination and strengthen oversight through effective complaint mechanisms. This may include establishing independent bodies to investigate and sanction cases of discrimination and substandard care. These steps can lead to a more inclusive and equitable health system that serves all residents equally. Taken together, these recommendations highlight the need for a comprehensive, multi-sectoral response. Addressing the interconnected individual, systemic, and cultural barriers requires not only changes within the health system but also long-term investment in social equity.

Our findings, supported by existing literature, demonstrate that many of the barriers identified are not merely the result of communication gaps or cultural misunderstanding, but reflect deeper, systemic failures to ensure equitable access. Strengthening health literacy, investing in inclusive and community-based care, and fostering institutional accountability are not just practical steps but necessary conditions for systemic change. Frontline strategies such as structural competence training or the use of health promotion assistants, while effective in mitigating some relational and informational barriers, cannot by themselves resolve issues like institutional neglect, chronic underfunding, or embedded discrimination. To effectively reduce these persistent inequities, interventions must extend beyond the healthcare system alone. Therefore, we advocate for the implementation of these interventions as part of a broader structural transformation, including governance reform, resource reallocation, and accountability mechanisms, to ensure that health systems respond to the needs of people living in MRCs.
