Efficacy of probiotic-supplemented formula in treating chronic diarrhea in children aged 6–43 months: evidence based on a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Feng Ji, Yifan Wang, Changying Lu, Qiuling Tang, Shuo Geng, Caidi Jiang, Jia Xiang, Zhong Li, Guanghuan Wang, Lin Zhang, Songwen Tan, Hui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChronic diarrhea is a common gastrointestinal disorder in young children, leading to nutritional deficiencies, growth retardation, and increased healthcare burdens. Gut microbiota dysbiosis is closely associated with the pathogenesis of chronic diarrhea, but the efficacy of probiotic-supplemented formula tailored to the intestinal microenvironment of Chinese children remains unclear. This study aims to evaluate the therapeutic effect of probiotic-supplemented formula on chronic diarrhea in children aged 6–43 months and explore its potential mechanism through gut microbiota analysis.MethodsThis randomized, open-label controlled trial was conducted from September 1, 2024, to September 1, 2025, at two hospitals in Shantou City. A total of 160 children aged 6–43 months with chronic diarrhea were enrolled and randomized to the intervention group (n = 80) or control group (n = 80), with 129 participants ultimately included in the efficacy analysis. The intervention group received formula supplemented with Bifidobacterium breve (10 billion CFU/day) as the sole variable, while the control group received the identical formula without Bifidobacterium breve, with both groups consuming the formula for 1 month. The primary outcome was the 1-week cure rate, defined as cure (BSFS = 3,4) or ineffective (BSFS = 5,6,7); efficacy was quantified as the difference in cure rates between the intervention and control groups. Secondary outcomes included time to symptom resolution (diarrhea cessation, stool normalization), changes in fecal microbiota composition, and adverse events.ResultsThe cure rate in the intervention group was significantly higher than in the control group (37.7% vs. 19.7%, relative risk [RR] = 1.91, 95% confidence interval [CI]:1.08–3.38, P = 0.013). Multivariable logistic regression showed the intervention group had significantly higher odds of efficacy after adjusting for confounders (adjusted odds ratio [OR] = 3.40, 95% CI:1.41–8.22, P = 0.006). Cox proportional hazards modeling indicated a 66% reduction in the risk of persistent diarrhea in the intervention group (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.34, 95% CI:0.21–0.53, P < 0.001). Gut microbiota analysis revealed the intervention group had enriched specific beneficial taxa without the reduction of pre-existing pathobionts.ConclusionProbiotic-supplemented formula significantly accelerates the resolution of pediatric chronic diarrhea. Mechanistically, this efficacy is driven by specific commensal engraftment rather than mere pathobiont eradication, overcoming the persistent ‘dysbiotic lock’ to improve intestinal clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13099-026-00822-8.Keywords: Probiotics, Chronic diarrhea, Gut microbiota, Pediatric population

Background

Diarrhea remains a prevalent gastrointestinal syndrome in pediatrics, predominantly affecting children under 5 years of age. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), diarrhea is clinically classified based on symptom duration: acute diarrhea, which lasts several hours to days; and persistent diarrhea, defined as episodes lasting 14 days or longer [1]. While acute diarrhea is typically self-limiting and often of infectious origin, prolonged symptoms pose greater risks of malnutrition and growth retardation.

Clinically, chronic diarrhea is defined as the persistence of loose or liquid bowel movements (≥ 3 times daily) for more than 4 weeks [2, 3]. Unlike acute diarrhea, which is often self-limiting, this prolonged course suggests a complex pathology involving post-infectious mucosal injury, secondary malabsorption, and, critically, gut microbiota dysbiosis [2, 4]. In the context of this report, we define ‘dysbiosis’ as a structural and functional imbalance of the gut ecosystem, characterized typically by a reduction in microbial diversity, a depletion of beneficial commensals (e.g., Bifidobacterium), and an expansion of potential pathobionts (e.g., Escherichia-Shigella). In toddlers aged 6–43 months, chronic diarrhea is frequently functional or a sequela of acute gastroenteritis (post-infectious enteropathy), characterized by a persistent imbalance in the gut ecosystem rather than a primary organic disease like celiac disease or cystic fibrosis [3, 5]. This ‘dysbiotic lock’ perpetuates mucosal inflammation and gut barrier dysfunction. Therefore, this study specifically targets this dysbiosis-associated chronic diarrhea phenotype. Although dysbiosis was not verified via sequencing as an inclusion criterion prior to enrollment, it is clinically presumed to be a key driver in these patients after excluding primary organic causes (e.g., celiac disease, infection).

Epidemiologically, chronic diarrhea affects children across all age groups and geographic regions, with notable variations in etiology distribution by setting. In developing and transitional regions, infectious agents remain the predominant drivers. For example, the landmark Global Enteric Multicenter Study (GEMS) and recent systematic reviews identify pathogens such as Rotavirus, Cryptosporidium, and Shigella as major causes of moderate-to-severe diarrhea in low-income settings [6, 7]. This contrasts with high-income regions, where infectious burden has declined, and non-infectious etiologies such as food protein allergy, congenital diarrhea syndromes, and immunodeficiency-related gut inflammation are increasingly recognized as the primary causes [2, 3].

The most studied intervention for pediatric diarrhea is the administration of probiotics, defined as “live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host [8]. " Clinical studies have shown that specific probiotic strains, such as Lactobacillus acidophilus NCFM and Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis Bb-12, can reduce the incidence and duration of diarrhea in children [4]. In previous studies, probiotics derived from breast milk (e.g., Lactobacillus fermentum CECT5716) have shown potential in improving gastrointestinal health in infants [9, 10].

However, most probiotic strains approved for infant foods in China are foreign-patented, isolated from non-Chinese populations, raising concerns about their suitability for Chinese children due to differences in gut microbiota composition across ethnicities [10]. Among existing studies, only a few randomized clinical trials (RCTs) have focused on probiotic-fortified formulas, with limited data on children aged 6–43 months with chronic diarrhea. Thus, the effect of probiotic-supplemented formula on pediatric diarrhea in this specific pediatric population remains to be clarified [11]. In the current study, we aimed to assess the efficacy of a probiotic-supplemented formula in the management of pediatric diarrhea in children aged 6–43 months.

Methods

Design

This randomized, open-label, controlled trial was conducted at two centers in Shantou, Guangdong Province, China: the Second Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College (a tertiary university teaching hospital) and Chenghai District People’s Hospital of Shantou City (a regional general hospital). These centers serve a diverse catchment population ranging from urban to suburban residents with mixed socio-economic backgrounds, representative of the coastal Special Economic Zone in Southern China. The trial aimed to evaluate the efficacy of probiotic supplementation in improving pediatric diarrhea and was prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400094508). The study adhered to standard protocols for interventional trials, with data collection and analysis following predefined procedures to ensure scientific rigor and reproducibility. The study protocol and reporting follow the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 guidelines. The completed CONSORT checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Randomization and blinding

Eligible subjects were randomly assigned to the intervention or control group (1:1 ratio) using a stratified block randomization method to ensure balanced allocation across the two study sites. Separate randomization sequences were generated for each hospital by independent statistical personnel (YFW) from Southeast University, ensuring no involvement from the commercial sponsor. Within each stratum, participants were assigned consecutively according to the pre-generated sequence, which was concealed from enrolling clinicians until the moment of assignment to prevent selection bias.

Regarding blinding, the study was open-label for participants, caregivers, and site investigators due to the nature of the intervention. However, to minimize detection bias for objective endpoints, laboratory personnel processing stool samples for 16 S rRNA sequencing were blinded to treatment allocation (using alphanumeric codes). Furthermore, the final statistical analysis was conducted by an independent researcher on coded datasets to ensure objectivity.

Intervention

Following randomization, participants were allocated to two groups with the primary variable being the intake of Bifidobacterium breve. All other dietary and environmental conditions were strictly standardized. Both study groups received formulas based on the same standard cow’s milk-based powder (Stage 3 toddler formula) manufactured by Hunan Siji Nanshan Nutritional Food Co., Ltd. (Changsha, China).

Intervention Group: Children received the study formula fortified with Bifidobacterium breve (supplied by Jiangsu New-Bio Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) at a concentration of 10 billion CFU per daily serving.

Control Group: Children received the identical base formula without the addition of Bifidobacterium breve. The control formula was matched for protein, energy, and micronutrient content and possessed indistinguishable organoleptic properties (appearance, texture, and taste) to the intervention formula to maintain blinding validity.

Protocol and Compliance: Participants were instructed to consume the allocated formula as their main milk source for 1 month. Compliance was monitored via caregiver daily logs (recording consumption volume) and by counting unused sachets/powder. Satisfactory compliance was defined as consuming ≥ 80% of the prescribed daily volume (≥ 320 mL/day), ensuring a minimum effective probiotic dose. Dietary pattern and clinical symptoms were recorded at baseline and 1 month after-intervention.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome: The primary outcome was the cure rate at 1-week follow-up. Clinical cure was defined as the normalization of stool consistency (BSFS type 3–4) recorded on Day 7 of the intervention, representing the point-prevalence of symptom resolution at the first weekly assessment window. Efficacy was defined as the comparative difference in cure rates between the intervention and control groups.

Secondary outcome: The secondary outcome measures included time to symptom resolution, defined as the duration from the first dose of the intervention to the passage of the last loose or liquid stool (Bristol Stool Form Scale type 6 or 7), changes in fecal microbiota composition (assessed by 16 S rRNA sequencing at baseline and 1 month after-intervention), and adverse events.

Data collection

Data collection included baseline and 1-month after-intervention recordings of clinical symptoms, dietary pattern, and stool consistency. Weekly follow-up sessions (conducted in-person or via phone) were scheduled to monitor adherence, symptom changes, and adverse events. Information extracted from medical records included demographic data (age, sex), anthropometric measurements (height, weight), monthly household income, current physical condition, types of drugs taken within 3 months prior to enrollment, daily defecation frequency, stool consistency, presence of loss of appetite or reduced milk intake, dietary pattern, presence of nausea and vomiting, and regular probiotic use in the past 3 months.

Specimen collection

Fecal specimens were collected at baseline and 1 month after-intervention for 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Caregivers were strictly instructed to collect fresh stools using sterile, nucleic acid-free containers, ensuring no contamination from urine, water, or other exogenous liquids. To preserve the microbial community integrity and prevent artifactual shifts in taxa abundance, caregivers immediately stored the fresh samples (approximately 5–10 g from the mid-section) in their home freezers at approximately − 20 °C. Within 24 h of collection, the frozen specimens were transported to the clinical study center in insulated coolers equipped with pre-frozen ice packs to maintain a continuous cold chain. Upon arrival, all samples were immediately aliquoted and transferred to a − 80 °C ultra-low temperature freezer for long-term storage until DNA extraction.

Adverse event monitoring

Adverse events were primarily monitored through weekly follow - up sessions. As per the study protocol, starting from the initiation of the intervention, caregivers and participants were scheduled to have a follow - up assessment every week. During these weekly follow - up visits, researchers actively inquired about any changes in the participants’ health status that could potentially be classified as adverse events. This included symptoms such as increased abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, rash, fever, altered feeding behavior, or any other unusual manifestations. Caregivers were also encouraged to report any concerns or new symptoms that had emerged since the last visit. The information gathered during these weekly sessions was systematically recorded for further analysis. Adverse events were classified into three levels of severity: (1) Mild: Symptoms were transient or mild, did not interfere with the child’s daily activities, and required no medical intervention. (2) Moderate: Symptoms caused discomfort or disruption to daily activities, necessitating basic medical management. (3) Severe: Symptoms were severe, persistent, or potentially life - threatening. These events required emergency medical care, hospitalization, or discontinuation of the study intervention if deemed necessary. Caregivers were contacted on a weekly basis for safety evaluation. Each week, they were either met in person during the scheduled follow - up visits at the study site or reached via phone if in - person meetings were not possible. These regular contacts ensured that any emerging adverse events were promptly detected and addressed. The weekly frequency provided a consistent and timely approach to monitoring the well - being of the participants throughout the course of the study.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on predefined assumptions for the primary outcome (clinical cure of diarrhea): an absolute 20% difference in treatment efficacy between the probiotic and control groups, with the control group expected to have a 45% efficacy rate and the probiotic group a 65% efficacy rate (consistent with preliminary clinical observations and relevant pediatric research). The calculation employed a significance level (α) of 0.05 (two-sided) and 80% statistical power, using the formula for comparing two independent proportions via G*Power 3.1 software. Anticipating a dropout rate , the study aimed to achieve a final analyzable sample of 129 participants. To account for potential attrition, a total of 160 participants were initially enrolled, ensuring a sample size deemed sufficient to detect clinically meaningful differences in the primary outcome while accounting for potential attrition, ensuring methodological reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ baseline characteristics: continuous variables were presented as median (min-max), and categorical variables as counts (percentages). For dichotomous outcomes, crude relative risk (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was calculated to explore the unadjusted association between probiotic intervention and treatment outcomes. The χ² test was used for categorical variables with sufficient cell counts, and Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell frequencies were less than 5. Multivariable logistic regression was applied to assess the independent effect of probiotics on pediatric diarrhea after controlling for prespecified confounding variables (age, sex, HAZ, WAZ, monthly household income, current physical condition, daily defecation frequency, stool consistency, presence of loss of appetite or reduced milk intake, and dietary pattern. For time-to-event outcomes (e.g., time to cessation of diarrhea, time to normalization of stool consistency), Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate whether probiotic intervention shortened the time to symptom resolution, with adjustment for confounding variables.

A modified intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was conducted on all enrolled participants; due to the completeness of baseline data, no data imputation methods were used in the modified ITT analysis. Sensitivity analyses were performed to address potential bias from participants lost to follow-up. Additionally, a per-protocol analysis was performed on participants who ingested at least 75% of the study formula (based on caregivers’ reports and counting of unused sachets). For gut microbiota analysis based on 16 S rRNA sequencing data of fecal samples, bioinformatics tools were used to conduct core species identification via Venn diagram, α-diversity analysis (Shannon and Chao1 indices) with intergroup differences compared using box plots and appropriate non-parametric tests, β-diversity analysis via Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and Analysis of Similarities (ANOSIM) to test intergroup differences, differential taxon screening at phylum, genus, and species levels using Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) with a predefined LDA score threshold, and comparison of relative abundance of dominant microbial taxa across groups via one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA).All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at P < 0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between December 31, 2024, and February 28, 2025, 160 children aged 6–43 months with chronic diarrhea were consecutively enrolled from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Shantou University Medical College and Chenghai District People’s Hospital of Shantou City. Participants were assigned to the probiotic intervention group (n = 80) or the control group (n = 80). During the study, 27 participants in the intervention group were excluded from the final analysis: 17 discontinued the intervention due to poor compliance and 10 were lost to follow-up. In the control group, 4 participants were excluded (2 discontinued, 2 lost to follow-up). Consequently, a total of 129 participants (n = 53 in intervention, n = 76 in control) were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were generally balanced between the two study arms (Table 1). However, a chance imbalance was observed in the history of probiotic use, with a higher prevalence in the intervention group (18.8%) compared to the control group (3.9%). Given the limited sample size, this variable was not included in the multivariable model to prevent overfitting. Instead, potential residual effects were minimized by the mandatory 2-week washout period required for all participants prior to enrollment.

Characteristics of participants

Primary outcome

For the primary outcome of cure rate at week 1 follow-up (BSFS = 3,4), 20 of 53 participants in the intervention group achieved effectiveness (37.7%), compared with 15 of 76 participants in the control group (19.7%). In the modified ITT analysis, the intervention group had a significantly higher cure rate (relative risk RR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.08–3.38; P = 0.013). The absolute risk reduction (ARR) was 0.18, corresponding to a number needed to treat for benefit (NNTB) of approximately 6. Multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for baseline confounders (age, sex, HAZ, WAZ, income, health status, defecation frequency, appetite changes, and diet), revealed the intervention group had 3.4-fold higher odds of achieving efficacy (OR = 3.40, 95% CI: 1.41–8.22; P = 0.006; Table 2).

Secondary outcomes

For the time-to-event outcome of diarrhea cessation, the median time to resolution was significantly shorter in the intervention group (14.0 days) compared with the control group (21.0 days). Per-protocol analysis yielded effect sizes identical to the primary analysis, as detailed in Supplementary Table S1, confirming the robustness of the findings.

Gut microbiota diversity analysis

Prior to the intervention, we verified the baseline comparability of the gut microbiota between the Intervention (BI) and Control (BC) groups. Alpha diversity analysis showed no significant differences in Shannon (P = 0.84) or Chao1 (P = 0.20) indices between the two groups. Similarly, Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity revealed indistinguishable clustering patterns (ANOSIM R = 0.018, P = 0.136), confirming that the randomization process successfully established balanced baseline microbial profiles (Supplementary Figure S1).

Microbiome diversity assessment revealed that a dynamic abundance Venn diagram identified 1,346 core species shared among the pre-intervention group (BI), after-intervention group (AI), and after-control group (AC). Notably, 818 species were exclusively shared between BI and AI, while 842 species were shared between AI and AC (Fig. 2). The AI group exhibited a significant reduction in unique microbial species compared to the BI group, whereas the AC group retained a higher number of shared species with the BI group.

LEfSe analysis identified significant differences in microbial community structure between the AI and BI groups (Fig. 5A). The BI group (Pre-intervention) was enriched in several Bacteroides species, including Bacteroides ovatus, Bacteroides plebeius, and Bacteroides stercoris, as well as Prevotella species. The AI group (After-intervention) showed significantly higher abundance of Bifidobacterium, Clostridium species (e.g., Clostridium butyricum), and Streptococcus species.

Comparisons between the AI and AC groups (Fig. 5B) revealed that the AI group was enriched in beneficial taxa [8, 12] such as Bifidobacterium breve (LDA score > 4.0, p < 0.05), Bacteroides eggerthii, Parabacteroides distasonis, and Enterococcus faecalis. Conversely, the AC group was dominated by Ruminococcus (including Ruminococcus gnavus), Blautia, Roseburia, Lachnospira, and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii. LEfSe analysis consistently identified Bifidobacterium breve as the most discriminant taxon in the AI group, directly reflecting the effect of the intervention.

Distinct microbial profiles were observed at the phylum, genus, and species levels across groups (Fig. 6A–C). At the phylum level (Fig. 6A), Proteobacteria was the dominant phylum in the AI group, while Bacteroidetes remained abundant and relatively stable across all groups, particularly in the BI and AC groups. The combined abundance of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria was observed in all groups but appeared lower in relative proportion compared to Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes in the AI group. At the genus level (Fig. 6B), Escherichia-Shigella was highly prevalent in the AI group, consistent with the dysbiotic baseline of the enrolled subjects. The BI (Pre-intervention) group exhibited a more diverse profile with notable proportions of Bacteroides, Prevotella, and Citrobacter, distinguishing it from the after-intervention profiles. The AC group maintained a mixed profile with presence of Escherichia-Shigella and Bacteroides, differing from the dominance seen in the AI group. At the species level (Fig. 6C), the AI group was characterized by a high relative abundance of Escherichia-Shigella flexneri. Most notably, the AI group showed a specific enrichment of Bifidobacterium breve (indicated by the unique yellow band), which was virtually absent in the BI and AC groups. In contrast, the AC and BI groups showed higher proportions of other commensals (e.g., Citrobacter freundii and Bacteroides species).

Discussion

In this randomized clinical trial, probiotic-supplemented formula significantly improved outcomes in children aged 6–43 months with chronic diarrhea. At 1-week follow-up, the intervention group achieved a 37.7% cure rate, which was significantly higher than the 19.7% rate in the control group (RR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.08–3.38; P = 0.013). The number needed to treat for benefit (NNTB) was approximately 6, indicating a meaningful clinical impact. After adjusting for confounding factors via multivariable logistic regression, the intervention group had a 3.40-fold higher odds of efficacy (OR = 3.40, 95% CI: 1.41–8.22; P = 0.006). Additionally, Cox proportional hazards modeling revealed that the intervention group had a significant 66% reduction in the risk of persistent diarrhea (HR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.21–0.53; P < 0.001), confirming that probiotics not only enhanced efficacy but also accelerated the resolution of diarrhea.

Our findings align with previous research indicating that probiotics can ameliorate pediatric diarrhea through mechanisms such as gut microbiota modulation and intestinal barrier enhancement [13–15]. However, it is crucial to interpret these results within the specific etiological context of chronic diarrhea. Previous research has largely focused on acute or antibiotic-associated diarrhea (AAD). For instance, a recent systematic review by Minaz et al. (2024) reported that the overall certainty of evidence for probiotics in reducing the duration of acute and persistent diarrhea remains low, with significant heterogeneity [16]. Similarly, Lukasik et al. (2022) found no significant effect of a multispecies probiotic on the incidence of AAD in a strictly defined pediatric cohort [17].

We argue that the discrepancy between our positive results and the variable efficacy seen in acute or AAD trials stems from the distinct underlying pathologies. Acute diarrhea is often self-limiting and driven by transient pathogen overload, while AAD results from acute microbial depletion. In contrast, the chronic diarrhea observed in our cohort (duration ≥ 4 weeks) is characterized by a ‘dysbiotic lock’—a stable state of pathobiont enrichment (Escherichia-Shigella) and mucosal inflammation [18, 19]. Our data suggest that Bifidobacterium breve-supplemented formula is particularly effective in this specific chronic context because it targets the established dysbiosis through ecological restoration, a mechanism that may be less relevant in the rapid course of acute diarrhea. Thus, while references to acute diarrhea establish the general safety and immunomodulatory potential of probiotics [20, 21], our study highlights that clinical efficacy is strictly dependent on the specific disease phenotype and the duration of intervention.

The observed clinical benefits of probiotic-supplemented formula are supported by specific changes in the gut microbiome. LEfSe analysis revealed that probiotic intervention enriched beneficial taxa in the AI group, such as Bifidobacterium breve, Bacteroides species (B. eggerthii, B. dorei, B. uniformis), and Parabacteroides distasonis [8]. These genera are known to contribute to intestinal homeostasis through carbohydrate metabolism, short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production [12], and modulation of immune responses—mechanisms that may directly alleviate diarrhea symptoms by enhancing intestinal barrier function and reducing inflammation [15].

Notably, regarding the pathobiont Escherichia-Shigella, our data showed a relatively high relative abundance in the AI group compared to the baseline BI group. While this indicates that the specific probiotic intervention did not eliminate the dysbiotic signature characterized by Escherichia-Shigella expansion [22], the clinical outcomes were nonetheless significantly improved. This suggests that the therapeutic mechanism of Bifidobacterium breve may not rely on the direct exclusion or reduction of Escherichia-Shigella, but rather on re-establishing functional homeostasis, for instance, by enhancing SCFA production or modulating host immune responses to counteract the pathogenic effects despite the pathogen’s persistence [12, 15]. The specific enrichment of Bifidobacterium breve in the AI group (Fig. 6C) supports this hypothesis of functional restitution over structural normalization.

The decrease in microbial diversity (Shannon and Chao1 indices) in the AI group after-intervention may reflect a targeted restructuring of the gut microbiota, with a shift toward a more stable, beneficial community [14]. This is consistent with the observed enrichment of Bacteroides and Prevotella in the AI group, which are associated with anti-inflammatory properties and efficient nutrient metabolism—key functions for restoring gut health in children with chronic diarrhea [23].

Notably, the AC group showed higher abundance of Ruminococcus, Roseburia, and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii—genera involved in fiber degradation and SCFA production—yet this did not translate to clinical improvement [24]. This discrepancy suggests that the specific probiotic strain used in our intervention may have exerted a more targeted effect on pathobiont suppression and beneficial taxon enrichment, highlighting the importance of strain-specificity in probiotic efficacy [15].

Overall, the microbiome data provide mechanistic support for the clinical findings: probiotic-supplemented formula likely improves chronic diarrhea by correcting gut dysbiosis, restoring beneficial commensals to counteract the functional impact of persistent pathobionts [12, 15], and promoting the growth of beneficial bacteria involved in maintaining intestinal barrier function and immune homeostasis [23].

Strengths and limitations

Several strengths enhance the reliability of our findings. First, we rigorously adjusted for a comprehensive set of confounding factors, including baseline stool consistency, and dietary patterns, which strengthens causal inference regarding the independent effect of probiotics. Second, consistent results across multiple statistical methods (crude RR, multivariable logistic regression, and Cox proportional hazards models) reinforce the robustness of the associations observed. Third, by employing a strict inclusion protocol and a standardized single-strain intervention, this study minimizes the confounding factors often responsible for heterogeneity in broader systematic reviews, thereby providing high-quality, phenotype-specific evidence for this pediatric population.

Our study has several limitations. First, the trial was conducted as an open-label study. Although the intervention and control formulas possessed identical appearance and taste, a double-blind design could not be implemented due to logistical constraints regarding the repackaging and coding of study supplies in this pragmatic clinical setting. This introduces potential performance bias; however, we attempted to mitigate this by relying on objective biological endpoints, specifically the quantitative analysis of gut microbiota changes (e.g., Escherichia-Shigella abundance), which are less susceptible to subjective reporting bias than clinical symptoms alone. Caregivers in the intervention group may have paid closer attention to symptom changes or reported outcomes more favorably, while researchers may have interpreted results with differential stringency. However, this bias was mitigated by the use of objective outcome criteria (e.g., stool frequency, consistency) and standardized data collection forms with predefined efficacy thresholds [25]. We also acknowledge the notable imbalance in withdrawal rates between the groups (33.8% in the intervention group vs. 5.0% in the control group). We attribute this primarily to the open-label nature of the trial. Caregivers aware that their children were consuming a ‘live bacterial’ supplement may have exhibited heightened vigilance, attributing unrelated symptom fluctuations or behavioral fussiness to the intervention, leading to preemptive discontinuation. Additionally, despite our efforts to match the organoleptic properties of the formulas, subtle differences in taste or texture introduced by the high-concentration probiotic powder may have been detected by sensitive toddlers, contributing to the higher rate of refusal (poor compliance) observed in the intervention arm.

Second, the follow-up frequency (once weekly) was insufficient to capture fine-grained outcomes such as short-term symptom fluctuations or early recurrence of diarrhea. Third, the sample size was relatively small (n = 129), limiting the generalizability of findings to broader pediatric populations and reducing the power of subgroup analyses by age. Finally, while we analyzed the taxonomic changes in the gut microbiota, we did not perform metabolomic profiling (e.g., direct measurement of fecal short-chain fatty acids) or assess host inflammatory biomarkers, which limited our ability to fully elucidate the functional mechanisms underlying the observed clinical improvements.

Regarding safety assessment, while no serious adverse events were documented through weekly standardized monitoring (including symptoms such as increased abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, or altered feeding behavior), the sample size of 129 is insufficient to detect rare adverse outcomes. Additionally, the 1-month follow-up duration prevents us from evaluating long-term safety profiles. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts powered for safety endpoints and extended follow-up are needed to further confirm the safety of this intervention.

Future studies should address these limitations by: (1) Employing blinded designs with placebo formulas of identical appearance to minimize bias; (2) Enrolling larger, multicenter cohorts to enhance generalizability and enable robust subgroup analyses by age and other clinical factors; (3) Increasing follow-up frequency (e.g., twice weekly) to capture short-term symptom dynamics and recurrence; (4) Measuring serum biomarkers (e.g., intestinal permeability markers, inflammatory cytokines) to elucidate the mechanistic links between probiotic intervention, gut function, and symptom resolution. These efforts will refine probiotic-based strategies for managing pediatric chronic diarrhea.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that the single-strain probiotic intervention is a highly efficacious strategy for resolving pediatric chronic diarrhea. Beyond clinical symptom alleviation, our paired microbiome analysis reveals that this efficacy is driven by targeted commensal enrichment rather than the complete eradication of pathobionts. By successfully engrafting beneficial commensals such as Bifidobacterium, the intervention overcomes the persistent ‘dysbiotic lock’ and drives clinical recovery. These findings underscore a critical paradigm shift in managing chronic infantile diarrhea: transitioning from primarily targeting pathogens to actively fostering specific beneficial microbes within the gut.
