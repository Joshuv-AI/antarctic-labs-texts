Understanding the roles of equine veterinary nursing teams in UK practices: A cross‐sectional study
Authors: Anna Saul, Phillippa Pritchard, Julia Shrubb, Natalie Robinson, Jenny Stavisky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFeeling undervalued, having limited opportunities to utilise clinical skills and lack of career progression have been identified as factors negatively affecting the job satisfaction and retention of small animal registered veterinary nurses (RVNs). The aim of this study was to explore the roles and activities of equine veterinary nurses, and the barriers and facilitators associated with their professional progression.MethodsAn anonymous, cross‐sectional online questionnaire was distributed to all RVNs and student veterinary nurses working with equids in VetPartners practices. The questions explored respondents’ current roles and responsibilities, including Schedule 3 procedures and ambulatory nursing, and how their skills could be better utilised.ResultsMost of the 89 respondents reported wanting more opportunities to carry out clinical tasks within the workplace, including wound management (57/76, 75%) and intramuscular injections (51/76, 67.1%). The most common barrier reported was lack of delegation (78.7%, 48/61). The most‐identified factors that would make respondents feel more valued at work were more opportunities to utilise professional skills (22/53, 41.5%) and improved salary/benefits (13/53, 24.5%).LimitationsThis study was conducted in a single corporate group, so generalisability may be limited. Social acceptability bias could also have influenced respondents' answers, despite the anonymity of the questionnaire.ConclusionsMost respondents reported that they would highly value more opportunities for clinical progression and greater responsibilities, including within ambulatory services, with increased delegation of Schedule 3 procedures.

BACKGROUND

The recent RCVS Workforce Action Plan, which aims to tackle the current challenges around recruitment and retention in the veterinary professions, highlighted greater responsibility for veterinary nurses as a key ambition. 1 Retention among veterinary nurses is a profession‐wide concern, with one‐quarter of respondents in a recent survey of veterinary nurses indicating that they planned to leave the profession within 5 years. 2 Both job satisfaction and retention have been shown to be impacted by feeling undervalued, having insufficient opportunities to practice a full range of professional skills, a lack of career progression and poor financial reward. 3 , 4 , 5 These components are likely to be intertwined, as when nurses are confined to supporting roles with limited opportunities for direct revenue generation, their work may be perceived to be of lower value.

Better understanding of the working activities and roles of equine registered veterinary nurses (RVNs) has been hampered by the fact that most of the data gathered so far either explicitly or implicitly focuses on companion animal nurses. In the recent RCVS survey of the nursing profession, only 1.7% of respondents were in equine practice, and a further 7.7% were in mixed practice. 2 Similarly, in a survey of veterinary nurses, only one respondent in a sample of 205 worked in equine practice. 6 It is therefore difficult to know how far previous research findings can be applied to nurses within equine practice.

Equine veterinary nurses typically provide far fewer client‐facing services than their colleagues in small animal nursing, and so there is a particular need to facilitate career progression, and better utilisation of skills, in this group of nurses. Although Schedule 3 of the Veterinary Surgeons Act allows specific types of medical treatment and minor surgery to be delegated to veterinary nurses, until recently, most of the relevant guidance for applying this was aimed at small animal practice. In recognition of this, the British Equine Veterinary Association (BEVA) has provided guidance detailing which procedures can be delegated to RVNs under Schedule 3 within equine contexts. 7 However, there remains the question of how this guidance is actually translated into practice. VetPartners is a large veterinary group with over 500 veterinary practices in the UK. Of these, 14 are equine only, and a further 25 include equids within a mixed caseload. Project NURSE was instigated by members of the VetPartners Equine Nursing Clinical Interest Group to further explore these issues. The first phase involved a survey of horse owners to explore their perceptions of equine nursing roles. Relatively few respondents had experienced a nurse providing ambulatory care, with 28% having experienced such care with a veterinary surgeon also present, and 6% having experienced a nurse providing care alone. However, 87% of owners reported a willingness to have care provided by an equine veterinary nurse. Over 80% of respondents reported they would be somewhat or extremely comfortable with nurses performing procedures such as dressing changes, worming advice, weight and nutrition advice, administering medications and blood sampling, 8 suggesting a high level of acceptability to clients for more nurse‐led services.

In this exploratory study, we aimed to complement our findings on owners’ perspectives by investigating the viewpoints of those working in equine veterinary nursing within VetPartners regarding their current roles and activities and how they envisioned developing these further in the future. Quality improvement‐based activity remains a useful source of meaningful insights into current practice. 9 , 10 , 11 However, when findings are shared outside their original workplace environment with the purpose of eliciting wider discussion and change, this arguably constitutes research. Since this project straddles the boundaries of research and quality improvement, ethical review was obtained from the RCVS Ethical Review Panel (#24‐055‐Robinson).

This study aimed to answer the following three questions: What are the current roles and activities of members of the equine veterinary nursing team at VetPartners?How would those working in equine nursing like to progress their roles going forward, particularly in relation to client‐facing services?What are the barriers to and facilitators for career progression for equine veterinary nurses, particularly relating to expanding nurse‐led client‐facing services?

METHODS

Study design

An anonymous cross‐sectional online questionnaire was designed in SurveyMonkey, with a mixture of multiple choice, matrix, scoring and free‐text questions. These questions requested information about respondents’ current roles and activities (developed by discussion using the RCVS survey of nurses’ activities and internal guidance around Schedule 3 for nurses 2 ), how they would like these to develop and change in the future, and their perceived barriers to career progression. It was piloted by members of the VetPartners Equine Nursing Clinical Interest Group and Equine Clinical Board (professional discussion groups open to veterinary surgeons and nurses within VetPartners) and disseminated via internal email lists and social media within VetPartners between 22 July and 31 October 2024. A copy of the questionnaire is provided in Appendix 1. Although no personally identifiable information was collected in the questionnaire, participants were invited to submit their email at a separate link to enter a draw for one of two £50 vouchers as an incentive. Only the questions about consent and job role (to ensure eligibility) were compulsory; provided these were completed, all subsequent responses were included even if the questionnaire was not fully completed. The number of respondents for each question is provided in the results.

Data analysis

Data were exported into Microsoft Excel. Descriptive statistics were compiled in Excel and Jeffrey's Amazing Stats Programme. 12 Answers are expressed as a percentage of the respondents answering each question. Content analysis was used to deductively code the free‐text responses.

RESULTS

Eighty‐nine equine nurses responded, of whom 61 (68.5%) were qualified and 28 (31.5%) were students. An exact response rate cannot be calculated; while our data suggested a total of 76 RVNs and 24 SVNs working with equids within VetPartners practices at the start of the project, the exact number varied slightly over the study period due to staff changes, new students enrolling/qualifying and acquisition of new practices. Respondents had worked in equine veterinary nursing for a median of 6 years (range 0‒32 years). Respondents were invited to describe their role and could select multiple options if appropriate. Fifty (56.2%) were clinical nurses, 31 (34.8%) were head/senior nurses and 17 (19.1%) were clinical coaches. Other descriptors included teacher, advanced nursing assistant and imaging technician. Most respondents (71/89, 79.8%) had completed some qualifications at the time of responding. A diploma (44/89, 49.4%) and/or degree (33/89, 37.1%) were the most commonly reported qualifications achieved, followed by suitably qualified person certification (12/89, 13.5%), an advanced veterinary nursing certificate (6/89, 6.7%) and a Masters degree (3/89, 3.4%). Other qualifications included British Horse Society stages and National Vocational Qualifications. Most respondents (76/89, 85.4%) worked a full‐time pattern and were either regularly (48/89, 53.9%) or occasionally (16/89, 18%) on‐call.

When respondents were asked if there were any tasks that they disliked or would like to do less, the most common answer was ‘no’ (15/35, 42.9%), followed by horse holding/trotting up (10/35, 28.6%) and cleaning (5/35, 14.3%). When asked if they would prefer to do more or less of certain specified clinical tasks during ambulatory work, most respondents indicated, for most tasks, that they would like to do more (Figure 2). Respondents were also given the opportunity to provide free text to describe other tasks they would like to do more frequently; of 51 responses, the most popular were minor surgery/suturing (29/51, 56.9%) and nurse clinics (11/51, 21.6%).

Summary of equine registered veterinary nurses'/student veterinary nurses' responses to the question ‘Which of the following describes how you feel about carrying out each of the following while out on ambulatory work (i.e., visiting horses at their yards), assuming appropriate training was provided?’.

Thirty‐nine respondents provided an answer to the question ‘Are there any tasks which you wouldn't want to do, even if delegated and appropriate training provided?’. Of these, the majority said there were no such tasks (24/39, 61.5%), with a small number 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 of respondents each saying they would prefer not to carry out anaesthesia, dentistry, minor surgery, nerve blocks, nasogastric tubing and euthanasia.

Respondents were asked what they understood by the term ‘Schedule 3 procedure’. Both RVNs and SVNs agreed on the most popular feature: treatment under veterinary supervision or delegation (overall 38/58 [65.5%], including 27/44 [61.4%] of responding RVNs and 11/14 [78.6%] of SVNs). Other responses included surgery not in a body cavity (12/58, 20.7%), procedures that can be carried out by veterinary nurses (17.2%) and minor surgery (7/58, 12.1%). Two responses indicated a lack of clear understanding, describing Schedule 3 as ‘Procedures qualified nurses are able to perform without a surgeon’ and ‘Performing certain tasks under the direction of their employer to animals under their care’.

Respondents most commonly carried out Schedule 3 procedures either several times daily (18/61, 29.5%) or 1‒5 times per week (11/61, 18%), although a substantial proportion reported they did so either less than monthly (8/61, 13.1%) or never (11/61, 18%). The most frequently carried out Schedule 3 procedures reported were intravenous injections and intravenous catheter placement. Most respondents (55/60, 91.7%) stated that doing Schedule 3 procedures was important for their job satisfaction.

Respondents reported the most enjoyable parts of their jobs to be patient care (18/54, 33.3%; example quote: ‘Providing the highest quality care to the patients’), a busy/variable caseload (12/54, 20.7%; example quote: ‘I love a busy day full of different patients, especially medical cases and those that allow me to fully utilise my skills’) and seeing the process through/patients getting better (8/54, 14.8%; example quote: ‘Seeing patients arrive, being involved in the whole process from admission to discharge and making a difference to their and their owners experience’). Other aspects commonly identified as enjoyable included clinical work, client contact, Schedule 3 procedures and teaching.

Of the 61 respondents who answered the question, 37 (60.7%) were responsible for giving direct advice to clients. This most commonly included advice about bandage/wound care, rehabilitation or aftercare, feeding, weight and medication administration.

Respondents were asked to score to what extent different factors held them back from carrying out more clinical tasks in practice, from ‘1—not at all’ through ‘3—partly/sometimes’ to ‘5—completely’. Lack of delegation had the greatest impact, with 78.7% (48/61) respondents scoring this from 3 to 5, followed by lack of training, with 66.7% (40/60) scoring this from 3 to 5 (Figure 3). Other responses included having a lot of students and a lack of change.

DISCUSSION

To the authors’ knowledge, this study represents the first exploration of workplace factors specific to veterinary nurses working in equine practice. This work complements the previous study that revealed that many horse owners reported being open to receiving more services, including ambulatory care, from veterinary nurses. 8

The equine nurses surveyed in this study showed a strong appetite for more opportunities to engage in clinical work, including aspects of patient care and client‐facing activities such as nurse clinics. This agrees with previous findings; for example, in a study analysing the 2014 and 2019 surveys of the profession, job satisfaction, having opportunities for career progression and running nurse clinics were identified as important factors in retention. While the area of practice was not specified in these surveys, it is likely that these responses largely or wholly represented companion animal nurses, as nurses in this area greatly outnumber equine nurses. 4 Autonomy, reward and feeling part of a workplace community have all been identified as potentially protective factors for burnout in veterinary nurses. 13 There may also be parallels in medical nursing, where having varied opportunities for self‐efficacy and career progression have been identified as important for retention and job satisfaction. 14 , 15 Additionally, since 60.5% (46/76) of respondents in the present study reported training veterinary surgeons or students, and 63.2% (48/76) reported teaching SVNs, maintaining clinical skills necessary to provide these teaching opportunities is an essential part of ensuring nurses can continue to support less‐experienced team members in their clinical progression.

This study highlighted substantial diversity in the amount of responsibility offered to nurses across practices. For example, when asked about activities carried out on ambulatory visits, a high proportion of respondents reported taking blood samples (52/74, 70.3%), performing intravenous injections (46/74, 62.1%) and performing intramuscular injections (45/74, 60.8%). However, comparatively few led behaviour clinics (10/71, 14.1%) or carried out asthma (15/74, 20.3%) or weight (18/74, 24.3%) management. This suggests that some practices are more successful than others in providing delegation and clinical autonomy to nurses, and also that some activities may be more readily devolved to nurses than others. Barriers to increased delegation and enabling veterinary nurses to more fully utilise their professional skill set have rarely been explored. In one study of small animal veterinary surgeons and nurses in New Zealand, respondents were in strong agreement that it would be desirable for veterinary nurses to perform clinical tasks such as placing intravenous catheters, but only 78% of nursing respondents reported actually carrying out these activities 16 ; the study did not determine the reasons for this. Anecdotally, teams have been concerned that nurse‐led procedures would not be well accepted by clients; however, the findings of our previous survey of horse owners suggest that, on the contrary, many owners would receive the option of nurse‐led services positively. 8 For example, while over 80% of owners reported being comfortable with nurses providing weight advice, fewer than a quarter of nurses reported providing this in the present study. Respondents reported that the main thing holding them back was a lack of delegation from other team members. Ensuring all team members are aware of the scope of nurses’ abilities may help, and the recent guidance clarifying Schedule 3 procedures, both in general and specifically within equine practice, will be invaluable in this regard. 7 , 17 Creation of equine resources that parallel existing guides to implementing small animal nurse‐led consultations may be helpful in facilitating changes in practice 17 , 18 as well as increasing awareness and usage of the existing guidance. 7 Campaigns such as Vet Nurse Futures and Protect the Title may additionally assist in providing advocacy around the roles of nurses within practice teams. 19 , 20

Around a quarter of respondents reported that a salary increase would be important in making them feel more valued at work. While this does not appear to be the sole or primary factor influencing job satisfaction, it is clearly important. This is consistent with prior evidence showing that satisfaction with salary was associated with a reduced risk of wishing to leave veterinary nursing, and that having a second job was associated with increased intention to leave the profession. 4 Additionally, a recent report commissioned by the RCVS showed veterinary nurses to be largely unsatisfied with their salary, with two‐thirds of respondents intending to leave the profession citing it as a cause. 2

LIMITATIONS

This study was only open to colleagues within VetPartners practices. Included practices represent a range of activities (mixed and equine‐only; primary care and referral; hospital and ambulatory) and, although part of a corporate organisation, retain a high degree of clinical autonomy, so should reflect a diverse range of practices. More of the responses than initially expected were from SVNs; this likely represents slightly fluctuating numbers as colleagues began and completed nursing courses, as well as the acquisition of practices by VetPartners over the course of the study. A high proportion of respondents reported carrying out activities such as blood sampling while on ambulatory work; however, not all VetPartners equine practices have an ambulatory caseload. Due to the high response rate, this may indicate that respondents were describing how often they carried out the listed activities within a hospital rather than an ambulatory setting. While we envisioned the option ‘teaching veterinary surgeons/students/student VNs’ to apply to providing support in a practice setting, and ‘teaching/lecturing’ to apply to a more formal educational environment, the lack of distinction may have resulted in some overlap in responses to these options.

This project spans quality improvement and research, as it was initially conceived in order to progress practice within our specific organisation, but the findings may have wider implications. For those wishing to compare the findings more closely in other organisations, a copy of the original survey is provided in Appendix 1. While the response rate was high and responses were anonymous, it is still possible that responses may have been subject to factors such as social acceptability bias.

CONCLUSIONS

RVNs and SVNs working in equine practice within VetPartners reported a strong desire for more frequent and varied opportunities to practice their clinical skills and take on more responsibilities within their work. While some practices provide diverse opportunities for veterinary nurses to offer clinical services and take on case responsibility, in others, nurses’ roles appear much more curtailed. Expanding equine nursing services can be supported by improving veterinary professionals’ understanding of what can be delegated under Schedule 3, which is now clearly laid out by the BEVA guidance. 7 It will also be important to examine other potential barriers to increasing the appropriate delegation of clinical responsibilities. In order to improve equine nurses’ job satisfaction and retention, and allow more flexible distribution of workloads to benefit the whole practice team, practices should consider their nurses' skill sets and ways in which these can be better utilised and expanded.
